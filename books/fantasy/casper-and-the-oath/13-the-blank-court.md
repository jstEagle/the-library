## 13. The Blank Court

They did not bind his hands.

That was the first thing Casper noticed, being marched through the dusk streets of Vell between two Blanks whose faces were smooth as the inside of a glove — they didn't need to bind him, because there was nothing left of his hands' work to protect. No ledger to steal back to. No lock to pick. They had taken the satchel with the second ledger's pages, taken the scrap of codex vellum he'd kept against his ribs like a talisman, taken even the stub of graphite he used for marginalia. What remained was a man in a stolen coat walking through a country that was forgetting itself, escorted by clerks who had forgotten themselves entirely.

The streets bent toward the archive-cathedral as if the whole city were a page curling inward. Casper counted his steps out of habit — a thief counts, always; doors, guards, heartbeats — and found that the numbers came wrong. Between one lamppost and the next there were eleven steps going and thirteen coming, though the distance hadn't changed. Vell's arithmetic was softening at the edges. He was running out of time in a place where time had already been spent.

*Don't think about Yara,* he told himself, and thought about her immediately, the way a tongue finds the broken tooth. She would have seen them take him. She would be doing something reckless and brilliant about it, or something reckless and catastrophic, or — worst possibility, the one that sat coldest in his chest — she would be doing the math she always did, the cold guildmaster's arithmetic, and deciding that one thief was an acceptable loss against a whole country.

He hoped she was doing the arithmetic. He hoped it even as he hated it. Because if Yara Ashgrove could write off Casper Vale without flinching, then she'd survive what came next, whatever it was.

* * *

The Blank Court convened in the cathedral's transept, where the shelves gave way to a semicircle of desks arranged like a jury box carved from one piece of black wood. Nine Blanks sat behind the desks. Their faces were not empty exactly — emptiness implies something was removed, and these faces seemed never to have held anything at all, smooth as river stones, featureless except where a mouth should be, and even the mouths were only suggestions, seams in the stone.

Above them hung the Oath itself, written out on vellum so old it had gone the color of tea, its letters still faintly wet after a century. Ink bled down the parchment in slow black tears and pooled along the bottom edge without ever dripping. Casper, who had spent his life reading ledgers, read the hanging document the way other men read scripture: looking for the loophole.

"Keeper who counts," said the center Blank. Its voice was neither male nor female, young nor old. It sounded like a page turning.

"I prefer *thief*," Casper said. "It's more honest about my intentions."

"You are accused of theft."

"I'm accused of everything. It's the family business." His mouth ran ahead of his fear, as it always did. "Though I'll note for the record that I've stolen nothing from Vell. Yet. I've given, actually. One day. To a baker named Maren Ostrow. You can check your books."

The nine Blanks did not move, but Casper felt something move through them anyway, a ripple passing through still water. Checking their books. He wondered what Vell's books looked like now. He wondered if his name appeared in them yet, growing longer by the hour, each line another hour of his life converted into somebody else's history.

"We do not keep books," said the center Blank. "We are what remains when the books are finished with a person."

The cold that went through Casper then had nothing to do with the cathedral's perpetual dusk. He thought of the memory he had stolen from the Blank in the wheat field's shadow — the horrifying truth he had carried for days like a coal in his pocket and told no one, not even Yara. *Former keepers. Stayed too long. Emptied.* And here they all were, nine of them, arranged with terrible neatness, the way a guild arranges its most senior members.

"Then let's skip the formalities," Casper said. "You know why I'm here. I know why I'm here. Let's talk terms."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive wearing horns. It arrives wearing mercy. It arrives speaking softly, in reasonable tones, with paperwork in order, and it offers you exactly the thing you were too ashamed to ask for.

"The Court offers clemency," said the center Blank. "You may leave Vell tonight. Through the Door. Unharmed, unemptied, unaltered — with one amendment." A pause, precise as a ledger line. "You will forget this country entirely. Every street. Every citizen. The baker and her daughter. The wheat field. The woman who guided you."

"And Yara," Casper said. "You forgot to list her."

"She is not forgettable," said the Blank, and for one strange moment the seam of its mouth seemed almost to curve. "She is the exception written into the Oath. But you will not remember that either. You will walk out of a door in a wheat field in a country you cannot name, carrying a debt you cannot source, and within a season the Ashgrove Guild will find you at some border crossing with no memory of the last month and a fine new respect for the word *enough*."

"That's not clemency," Casper said quietly. "That's amputation."

"It is survival." The Blank folded its hands on the desk — hands with too many joints, or perhaps the right number and simply wrong arrangement. "Consider what staying costs. We have read your ledger page, keeper. It grows longer every hour. When it reaches its final line, you will become what we are. You will stand in this court and hear some future intruder make jokes at you, and you will not remember making them yourself, a hundred years before."

Casper looked at the nine of them. Really looked. At the smooth faces, the patient hands, the terrible tidiness of them. Clerks. That was the horror under the horror — they weren't monsters, they were *clerks*, they had been keepers once, men and women who had done precisely what the prophecy demanded, stayed precisely as long as required, and the requirement had eaten them to the bone and then past it.

"How long?" he asked. "Before I end up like you?"

"The last keeper lasted eleven years," said the Blank to his left. "He was diligent. Diligence shortens the term."

"He loved the work," said the one beside it. "Love shortens it further."

"And you?" Casper asked the center Blank. "How long did you last?"

The seam of its mouth opened, and closed, and opened again, and for the first time since his capture, Casper felt something come off the creature that wasn't procedure. Something that had been buried so deep under the blankness it had nearly composted into nothing.

"I don't remember," it said. "That is the mercy we are offering you. Not to die. To stop remembering that you lived."

* * *

And here is the confession Casper would carry — the one honest entry in the ledger of himself: **he almost took it.**

Not because he was afraid of becoming a Blank. Well. Also because of that. But underneath the fear lay something quieter and more damning, something he'd been hauling around since the vault in Ashgrove, since the first night he'd found tonight's date written in a book a century old: the exhaustion of being a man made entirely of other people's property. Every memory in Casper's head worth keeping belonged to someone else. His childhood was a purchase. His mother's face — he'd already spent that, in the rite, feeding it into Vell's restoration, and what remained when he reached for it was a warm shape and a smell of bread and no features at all. He was a ledger pretending to be a man. The Court was offering to let him put the ledger down.

Forget Vell. Forget the dusk that never lifted. Forget Maren Ostrow's face when her daughter's day returned to her, the way she'd gripped his wrist and said *you gave me this, whoever you are, you gave me this*. Forget the weight of thousands of names settling into his chest like ballast. Forget the Door standing open in a burned field, and the fine print rising up its frame like frost, and the lie he'd told Yara with a straight face while she watched him with those gray eyes that missed nothing —

No. Not her. Never her. Except the bargain required exactly that. Total forgetting. There was no carve-out clause for the person you—

There was. The Blank had said it. *She is the exception written into the Oath.*

But he wouldn't remember the exception. He'd forget her along with everything else, and the forgetting wouldn't hurt, because he'd never know there was anything to hurt over, and that was the truly obscene part. The Court wasn't offering to wound him. It was offering to make the wound impossible.

"Say I accept," Casper heard himself say, and hated how steady his voice came out. Thieves' voices are always steadiest when they're betraying someone. Usually the someone is themselves. "What's the process? Do I sign something?"

"You are already signed," said the center Blank. "Your name is in the Codex. We need only strike it through. One line. Then the Door opens, and you walk through, and Vell closes over the place where you stood like water."

"One line," Casper repeated.

"One line," the Court said, all nine together, and the sound was a sound he knew.

* * *

It took his mind three full seconds to identify what he was hearing, because the impossible always takes a moment to file itself. Nine voices speaking in perfect unison should have sounded like one voice multiplied, flattened, choral. Instead it sounded like *one voice*, singular, specific, worn at particular edges — and Casper had spent his life memorizing voices the way other men memorized faces, because voices were what people sold him, voices were the goods.

He knew this voice. He had heard it twice: once in the second ledger's margins, where a predecessor's handwriting deteriorated across forty years of entries, and once in the memory he'd torn out of a Blank in the dark, where a man had whispered a message he'd never delivered.

*"Tell her."*

The last keeper. Aldous Wren, the ledgers called him. Guild partner to Edmun Ashgrove. Eleven years of diligence, a lifetime of love for the work, emptied to nine smooth stones in a row.

"No," Casper said aloud, mostly to himself. "Say it again."

"Would you accept the Court's—"

"Not that. Anything else. Say anything else." He stepped forward, and the two escorting Blanks tensed, and the Court let him, which was answer enough. "Count. Counters count. It's in the prophecy — *the keeper who counts* — you must still count. Count backward from ten."

Silence in the transept. The ink above them wept down the Oath's face.

"Ten," said the center Blank, alone. "Nine. Eight." And there — God help him, there it was, unmistakable now that Casper knew to listen: a slight catch between *seven* and *six*, the hesitation of a man counting something that mattered, the cadence of someone who had once counted the days of a little girl's life in a ledger because her father was too frightened of loving her to do it himself. "Five. Four. Three. Two." The voice frayed at the end, thinned back toward the chorus, the stone closing over the water. "One."

Casper stood very still in the dusk-soaked transept of a dead country's cathedral, and understood, with the terrible clarity of his profession, exactly what he was looking at.

Not nine Blanks. Nine *layers*. The Court was one keeper, worn thin and spread across nine chairs, the way a candle's last wax spreads across a holder. Aldous Wren had not died. Aldous Wren had been *administratively redistributed*. And somewhere in the deep strata of that distributed man, beneath a century of erasure, a single undelivered message still sat on the books like an unpaid invoice:

*Tell her.*

Yara. It had always been Yara. Her father's partner, the second hand on the Oath, the man who had helped trade a country's existence and then stayed behind to hold what was traded — and his last living act had been trying to send a message to a girl, and he had failed, and the failure had outlived the man, fossilized in nine throats, waiting a hundred years for a thief with good ears to walk into the transept and hear it.

"What was her name?" Casper asked softly. "The one you couldn't tell. Say her name. Just once. For the record."

The nine Blanks did not move. The cathedral held its breath the way cathedrals do, in stone and dust and centuries.

And then, from the Blank at the far left end of the curve — the smallest one, the one whose hands trembled faintly, the only tremor Casper had seen in any of them — came a sound so small it might have been the building settling.

"*Yara,*" it said.

Just the once. Just the name. And then the seam of its mouth sealed, and the tremor stopped, and the nine sat smooth and silent as stones again, the window closed, the clerk returned to his files.

* * *

Casper turned away from the Court before his face could betray him, and found that his face was betraying him anyway, to an audience of creatures who no longer read faces. Good. He walked back toward the center of the transept with his heart slamming like a fist on a locked door.

"So," he said. "About your offer."

"The terms stand," said the center Blank. "One line struck. The Door opens. You live, and forget, and are free."

Free. The word sat in the air like a coin on a counter, and Casper looked at it the way he'd looked at a thousand objects in a thousand vaults, assessing weight, provenance, the true cost beneath the asking price.

A month ago — a lifetime ago, in a world of real mornings — he would have taken it. He knew that about himself now with uncomfortable precision. The old Casper Vale, the one who kept ledgers because ledgers didn't leave, the one whose competence was just loneliness wearing a nice coat, would have struck the line himself, would have sharpened the pen.

But the old Casper had never heard a hollowed-out man spend his last syllable on a girl's name. Had never watched mercy get offered in a voice that used to belong to someone who loved the work and was eaten by it. Had never stood in a country of borrowed dusk and understood that the difference between a keeper and a Blank was not courage or cleverness but simply *whether anyone outside remembered you existed*.

Yara remembered Aldous Wren. She just didn't know she did — didn't know the trembling-handed clerk at the end of the curve was the answer to a question she'd carried for a century. *Tell her.* The message was sitting right there, a hundred years late, and Casper Vale — liar, thief, keeper-elect, coward in at least four categories — was the only courier left.

If he struck the line, the message died with him. If he stayed, the message lived, and so did the lie he'd told her at the Door, and so did every hour of his ledger page ticking toward its final line. Staying meant becoming this. Sitting in one of these chairs someday, smooth-faced, hearing some future fool crack jokes, unable to remember the joke or the fool or the gray-eyed woman who had once — briefly, furiously, impossibly — made him want to be worth remembering.

The Court waited. It was very good at waiting. It had practiced for a hundred years.

"No," Casper said.

The word echoed strangely in the transept, and he realized the echo was wrong, doubled, as if the cathedral itself had repeated it — or as if something in the rafters, something that had followed him from the wheat field with a knife and a plan and no intention whatsoever of letting him face the Court alone, had breathed it with him.

Above him, in the shadowed gallery where the archive shelves began, a loose tile clicked softly against stone.

The Court's nine heads turned upward in perfect unison, and Casper started calculating — fast, thief-fast — how much trouble one exiled Ashgrove could cause in the four seconds before the Blanks finished looking up.
