## 14. Tell Her

The Blank Court kept its prisoners in the space behind its voice.

Yara understood that now, as she moved through the cathedral's undercrofts with a lantern she had no right to carry and a knife she had never once used for cutting. The Court did not have cells; it had *sentences*. It spoke a person into a room and the room obeyed. Casper had been spoken into one three hours ago, somewhere beneath the archive's oldest stacks, and every hour since, Yara had felt him slipping — not dying, nothing so clean as dying, but being *edited*, the way a scribe trims a page to fit a margin.

She found the door because it was trying not to exist.

That was the Blank way of building: walls that declined to be noticed, hinges that forgot themselves, a threshold worn smooth by feet that had been erased from every record of walking through it. A hundred years ago she would have passed it without a glance. A hundred years of exile had taught her to look for exactly this — the absence shaped like a presence, the silence with a heartbeat in it.

Yara pressed her palm flat against the wood and said, in her father's formal cadence, the words he had used to open every ledger in Vell:

"Ashgrove keeps what others lose."

The door remembered itself. Remembered *her*. It opened with a sound like a held breath released, and inside, in a room with no furniture and no shadows and no time, Casper Vale sat on the floor with his back against nothing at all, and he was counting.

"—forty-one, forty-two," he said, hoarse, eyes shut. "Forty-three—"

"Casper."

His eyes opened. For one unguarded instant — the thief's face stripped to the wall beneath the wallpaper — he looked at her like a man looking at land after drowning. Then the wall went back up, brick by practiced brick, and he smiled the smile she had already learned to distrust and could not seem to stop trusting anyway.

"Yara Ashgrove," he croaked. "Come to rob me? I'm afraid you'll find I've already been robbed."

"Up." She pulled him to his feet. His arm was cold through his sleeve. "Can you walk?"

"I can lie convincingly about walking. Close enough." He took two steps, wavered, and put his hand against her shoulder like it was an accident. It was not an accident. She let it stay.

They climbed. The undercroft stairs were stone gone soft as chalk, and above them the archive-cathedral rose in its terrible beauty — shelves taller than trees, books breathing their faint paper warmth into air that smelled of ink and dust and, underneath it all, faintly, of wheat. Somewhere far off, something faceless turned a page. Yara counted the turns the way other people count heartbeats. Three. Four. Moving away.

When they reached the gallery between the history stacks — the ones about wars Vell had never fought, because Vell had been excised from every war before it could lose one — Casper stopped walking, and she knew, from the sudden stillness of him, that whatever he had carried out of that room was heavier than fear.

"You heard something," she said.

He looked at the shelf instead of at her. "You first. How'd you find me?"

"The Court speaks people into rooms. Rooms built by Blanks still answer to the Oath's grammar, and the Oath's grammar is my father's handwriting. I've spent a century learning to read my father's handwriting." She said it flatly, because there was no other way left to say it. "Now you."

* * *

He told it badly at first. That was how she knew it was true; liars tell things beautifully, and Casper Vale, when it mattered, told things the way a man coughs up water — in pieces, wrong order, hands shaking.

"They offered me a bargain," he said. They had sat down on the gallery floor, backs against the shelf, knees up, the way children sit when the story is too big to stand under. "Leave tonight. Forget all of it — Vell, you, the codex, everything — and live. A whole life, wiped clean. And some part of me—" he laughed, and it came out broken, "—some part of me wanted it, Yara. You don't understand what forgetting sounds like when you're tired. It sounds like mercy."

"I understand exactly what it sounds like," she said quietly. "I invented the market for it."

Something moved across his face — not quite anger, not quite gratitude — and he went on. "I almost said yes. I had the word in my mouth. And then the Court leaned in — all of them, all those faces-that-weren't-faces, they leaned in together like a single creature — and it spoke again, and it wasn't the Court's voice anymore."

"What was it?"

"It was old. It was tired. It sounded like a man who'd been reading aloud to himself for so long he'd forgotten anyone was listening." Casper's hands had stopped shaking now, which frightened her more than the shaking had. "And it said — it said, *tell her the door was never locked from her side. Tell her I stayed because staying was the only way to keep her name written anywhere at all.*"

The gallery went very quiet. Somewhere below them the cathedral ticked and settled, the sound a country makes when it dreams.

"And then?" Yara asked. Her own voice seemed to come from very far away, from someone else's mouth, someone else's century.

"Then it said the rest of the sentence." He finally looked at her. "It said: *tell her I'm sorry about the garden. Tell her—*" and here his voice changed, dropped into a cadence that was not his own, older and rougher, a borrowed coat of a voice, "*tell her that Yara's name is the last word on every page I ever wrote.*"

* * *

Afterward — she would never know how long afterward — Yara found that she was standing. She had no memory of standing. Her body had simply decided, the way bodies decide to flinch, and now she stood in the middle of the gallery with her hand pressed over her mouth and a hundred years rearranging themselves behind her eyes.

Because she knew that voice.

Not from the Court. Not from the ledgers. From *before* — from the summer she was nine, from the garden behind her father's house where two men bent over a table of contracts while she stole plums off the low branch and pretended not to listen. Her father's partner in the writing of the Oath. The second signature. The man who had argued — she remembered this suddenly, horribly, with a child's perfect clarity — who had argued that the price was too high, that erasure should fall on the country and not the countrymen, and who had been overruled by her father's logic and her own family's desperation and the plague coming down out of the hills like weather.

Aldous. His name was Aldous Wren, and she had not thought it in ninety years because thinking it had been forbidden by the same law that forbade thinking of Vell, and when the Oath took hold it had taken him too — or she had believed it had. Everyone had believed it. The second keeper of the second signature, gone the day the borders closed, presumed consumed, mourned in the private way her father mourned things: by never speaking of them again.

"He was your father's partner," Casper said softly. He was standing too now. "The predecessor. The last keeper. I saw his entry in your second ledger — *tell her* — and I didn't know who *her* was. But the voice in the Court, Yara, the Court isn't a court. It's keepers. It's what keepers become. He's been down there a hundred years, wearing the shape they emptied him into, and he used the only moment of leverage he's had in a century to send a message to—"

"To me." The word came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh. "To me."

* * *

Here is what Yara Ashgrove had believed for one hundred years, and had built her entire self upon, the way a cathedral is built on a foundation no one inspects:

She believed her exile had been chosen. She believed that when the Oath closed around Vell, she alone had slipped its grip — half-inside, half-out, able to walk the country's dusk but never leave it, never re-enter the world — and that this had been her punishment, her penance, the fair wage of a daughter whose family had sold a nation to save it. She had worn her exile like a hair shirt. She had polished it. She had made it mean something. *I stay because someone must,* she had told herself ten thousand times in ten thousand dusks. *I stay because guilt is the only inheritance I have.*

And underneath it, in the cellar of her heart, in the place she never lit a lamp: *and no one came for me. My father wrote the Oath, my father knew every clause of it, and in one hundred years my father never once found a loophole wide enough to send me a single word.*

She had hated him for that. Quietly. The way you hate a dead man, which is the safest hatred there is, because it can never embarrass you by answering.

Now Casper stood in front of her with the Court's bargain still wet on his skin and Aldous Wren's message still hanging in the air, and the foundation cracked straight down the middle.

"Say it again," she whispered. "All of it. Exactly."

"*Tell her the door was never locked from her side.*" Casper repeated it slowly, watching her face the way he watched a lock he intended to pick. "Yara. What does that mean?"

"It means—" She stopped. Started again. Her voice was doing something shameful. "There are doors in the Oath. Of course there are doors; an oath is a cage and every cage has a hinge. My father designed them. One for the founders, sealed. One for the plague, sealed. And one—" her breath caught on the shape of a memory she had carried for a century without ever turning it over, the way you can carry a key for years and never wonder what it opens, "—one left *open*. A maintenance door. A way for the keeper to pass memory in and out, to keep Vell fed with recollection so it wouldn't starve inside its own forgetting. I knew it existed. I never used it."

"Why?"

"Because I tested it once, in my first year. I pushed a letter through — a confession, everything, the whole truth of what we'd done — addressed to the Guild, to anyone. It came back unopened. So I assumed the door was barred from the other side. I assumed—" and here the whole architecture of her century collapsed inward, floor by floor, cell by cell, "—I assumed the world had chosen to forget us completely, and that reaching out was just another way of begging."

Casper was very still. Thieves go still when they see something worth stealing, and what was in her face right then was worth more than anything in the Ashgrove vault.

"Your letter came back unopened," he said carefully, "because the last keeper was already inside. Already emptying himself into the Court. Already becoming a Blank — a thing with no hands to open letters, no eyes to read them. He couldn't reach you, Yara. He couldn't even remember why he wanted to. All he could do was stay, and hold your name on every page, and wait a hundred years for a thief stupid enough to get caught and stubborn enough to listen."

"But he *knew*," she said. The words tore coming out. "He knew the door worked. He must have known — he helped write it — he knew the door opened from my side, and he let me believe—"

"No." Casper crossed the space between them and took her shoulders, and it was the first time he had ever touched her without pretending it was an accident. "Think. You're the archivist. Think like the ledger, not like the wound. A man writes a door into a covenant. A man watches his partner seal it. A man gets trapped inside the seal before he can tell anyone the hinge works. What's the last thing such a man does with his human mind, before the Court takes it?"

Yara closed her eyes.

And saw it — the second ledger, the hidden one, the list of everyone the Oath had consumed. The final entry in a hand going loose and uncertain, the letters drifting the way handwriting drifts when the writer is being unwritten even as he writes. *Tell her.* Two words. He'd had time for two words. He had spent his last coherent hour not on escape, not on rage, not on cursing her father's name — but on a message he could not finish, to a girl he could not name, about a door she would spend a century believing was locked.

"He didn't let me believe anything," she said. "He died trying to correct a mistake he never got to make. And I—" 

She pulled away from Casper's hands and walked to the window at the end of the gallery, where Vell lay under its perpetual dusk, the rooftops of a town that had lived the same day for a hundred years, the baker's chimney already smoking tomorrow's identical bread.

"I chose it," she said. "Do you understand? All of it. The exile, the guilt, the century of standing at windows. I had a door, Casper. I had one working door out of this place, and I never opened it, because I preferred a prison with meaning to a freedom with risk. I made my abandonment into a vocation. I made it *noble*." Her laugh came out jagged. "My father sold a country and I sold myself the same way — wrapped the transaction up in duty so no one would ever ask what it cost, least of all me."

"That's not—"

"Don't." She raised a hand. "Don't comfort me. You're a thief; you know better than anyone that the cruelest thefts are the ones the victim performed willingly." She pressed her forehead against the cool glass. "Aldous Wren stayed a hundred years in that dark so that my name would exist somewhere in writing. And I repaid him by never once saying his name aloud, because remembering him hurt, and I had decided hurting was my profession."

Behind her, Casper said nothing for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had lost all its cleverness, and she understood that he was giving her something rare: the truth, ungilded, from a man who gilded everything.

"My mother used to say that regret is just love with nowhere to go," he said. "You've got a hundred years of love in you with nowhere to go. So we're going to give it somewhere to go."

"How."

"We finish his sentence." She turned, and found him holding the stolen fragment of the Court's voice the way he held everything precious — loosely, like it might be taken back. "The Court speaks in keepers. Keepers are the Oath's engine. Which means the Court knows every clause, every hinge, every flaw in your father's work — including the flaw Aldous tried to warn you about. Including, maybe, the reason the Oath needs a keeper at all." His eyes had gone bright and hard and certain, the look of a man casing a vault he has already decided to enter. "We're going back into the archive-cathedral. We're going to read the original Oath, every line, every margin, every smudge. And we're going to find what your father hid and Aldous died pointing at."

Yara wiped her face with the heel of her hand — an unladylike gesture she had not performed since girlhood, in a garden, over stolen plums — and felt something shift in her chest that was not quite hope and no longer grief. Something with weight. Something usable.

"The Oath is in the Founders' Stack," she said. "Sealed by bloodline. No one has opened it in a hundred years."

Casper smiled, and for once it reached all the way up to his eyes, and she realized with a small, fatal jolt that she was smiling back.

"Lucky for you," he said, "breaking into places is the only honest work I've ever done."

Far below them, in the dark where the Court kept its sentences, something old began — very softly, in a dead man's borrowed voice — to count.
