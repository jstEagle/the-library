## 15. Rewriting the Oath

The archive-cathedral had been built to make a person feel small.

Yara Ashgrove had known that since she was nine years old, when her father first brought her through its doors and told her to look up. The vaulted ceiling rose into a darkness no lamp could reach, and from that darkness hung shelves — ten thousand of them, iron and ashwood, cantilevered off pillars that had been grown, not carved, their surfaces whorled like fingerprints. Every shelf held books. Every book held a life. And somewhere in all of it, in a case of black glass at the crossing of the nave, lay the Oath itself: the founding document of Vell, the contract her father had signed with a book instead of a god.

She had spent a hundred years avoiding this place.

Now she walked down the central aisle with Casper Vale two steps behind her, and the cathedral seemed to lean in around them, as if the building remembered her name and was not pleased about it.

"You said you'd never come back here," Casper said quietly. He had a thief's way of speaking — low, close, as though every sentence might be stolen by someone else if he raised his voice.

"I said I couldn't." Yara's boots rang on the stone. "Coming back is different. Coming back is a choice."

"Is there a difference? In this country?"

That was the trouble with thieves. They asked the questions honest people were too polite to ask. She didn't answer him. Ahead, the crossing opened up beneath the great rose window — dusk-colored glass, because everything in Vell was dusk-colored now, even the light — and beneath the window stood the case.

The Oath rested on an iron lectern inside it. It did not look like much. A single sheet of parchment, heavy as lead, dense with script so fine it looked less like writing than like weather — the record of a storm that had happened once, long ago, and never stopped happening.

Yara lifted the black glass away. Her hands shook. She hated that they shook.

*You wrote half of this,* she reminded herself. *You were fourteen years old, and your handwriting is on the second page.*

"Read it with me," she said. "Not for what it says. For what it doesn't."

Casper came to stand beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him, which still startled her every time. Everything in Vell was cool. Everything in Vell was preserved, pickled, held at the exact temperature of a memory. He was the only warm thing in the country, and some traitorous part of her had begun orienting toward that warmth the way flowers oriented toward a sun that no longer existed here.

He leaned over the parchment. His eyes moved the way they always moved when he read ledgers — fast, hungry, cataloguing. She watched him find the structure of it, the architecture of clauses, and felt something loosen slightly in her chest. It was good, having someone else read it. For a hundred years the Oath had been a weight only she carried. Weights carried alone became indistinguishable from the self.

"'Let it be recorded,'" he read aloud, "'that the people of Vell, facing the Plague of Unmaking, do swear themselves out of the world's remembering. Let maps forget them. Let tongues lose them. Let grief pass over their houses as a tide passes over sand, leaving nothing.'"

"My father's hand," Yara said. "He dictated it to me while the plague ate the eastern provinces. Three days. That's how long it took to write. People were forgetting their own children's faces mid-sentence, and we sat here with candles and ink."

Casper's jaw tightened, but he kept reading. "'And let there be a keeper.'" He stopped. Read it again silently. "There it is. The keeper clause."

"There it is," Yara agreed. "Say the rest."

"'And let there be a keeper, one soul appointed to hold the whole of Vell against the forgetting, that when the plague has passed the country may be restored from that single faithful memory. The keeper shall remember from within, and shall not leave, and shall not be forgotten by the Oath itself, though all else forgets.'"

Silence filled the nave the way water fills a hull.

"That last line," Yara said slowly. "*Though all else forgets.* Do you understand what that means?"

"The Oath protects the keeper's memory of Vell. Nothing else does." Casper straightened. "Which is why your predecessor—" He caught himself.

"Say it."

"Which is why the last keeper stayed until the Blanks took him. He couldn't leave without breaking the clause. He couldn't stay without being emptied." Casper's voice had gone flat, careful, the way it went flat and careful whenever he touched something that hurt him. She had noticed that about him. Thieves, she was learning, were full of locked drawers, and she was learning it the way she learned most things lately: too late, and at a cost.

"He stayed forty years," Yara said. "Longer than my father intended anyone to. Longer than any sane person would have. And then the keeping hollowed him out, and the Oath needed a new keeper, and there wasn't one, and so Vell began to eat itself. The loops. The repetitions. The baker who buries her daughter every day." She pressed her palm flat against the lectern, against the cold iron, grounding herself. "I thought the flaw was in us. In our courage, or our cleverness. But you're right — read it again. What exactly does it demand?"

Casper bent over the parchment once more, and this time he read like a thief reads a lock: not admiring the mechanism but hunting for the tolerance in it, the millimeter of give that every honest craftsman leaves because perfect things shatter.

"It demands a keeper," he said. "One soul. Appointed. Who holds the whole of Vell. Who remembers *from within.*" He paused on the phrase. Turned his head. "Wait."

"Don't wait. Say it."

"*From within.*" He tapped the parchment, once, twice, the way he tapped ledger pages when an entry didn't balance. "It says the keeper must remember from within. It doesn't say *within what.*"

The words went into Yara like a key turning.

She heard them, and then she heard everything underneath them — her father's voice dictating in the candle-smoke, her own fourteen-year-old hand copying clause after clause, the plague screaming beyond the walls, the terrible haste of people trying to save a nation before breakfast. Haste made gaps. Grief made gaps. She had spent a hundred years assuming the document was seamless because it had cost so much, because surely anything bought at that price would be flawless. But contracts weren't prayers. Contracts were made of language, and language was made of doors, and she of all people should have known that a door left unwatched stays open forever.

"Casper." Her voice came out strange to her own ears. "When the Oath was written, there was only one world that mattered. Inside and outside meant Vell and everywhere else, and everyone assumed — my father assumed, I assumed, the Codex assumed — that *within* meant inside Vell. That the keeper had to live here, breathe this air, walk these loops, hold every street and face and name in a skull that would eventually crack under the weight of them. That's why keepers empty. That's why the Blanks exist. A single mind cannot carry a country indefinitely. The clause asks for the impossible."

"But the clause doesn't actually say impossible," Casper said. His eyes had gone bright — that particular brightness she'd seen in him in the wheat field, in the loop-stutter after the baker, whenever the machinery of the world showed him a seam. "It says *within.* And 'within' is doing all the work, isn't it? Within Vell. Within the Oath. Within..." He turned fully to face her, and the dusk-light through the rose window fell across his face in colors that didn't exist anywhere in this country anymore. "Within a memory. If the keeper remembers from within — if the whole of Vell lives inside someone — then the geography doesn't matter. The clause is satisfied by the containing, not the location."

"Yes," Yara breathed. "Yes. The keeper must hold Vell. The clause never says the keeper must be *in* Vell to do it."

"Someone outside." Casper was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the restless geometry of a mind working. "Someone beyond the Oath's reach. Someone the forgetting can't touch because they were never part of the bargain. If a person outside Vell willingly takes in its memory — all of it, names, streets, songs, debts, the baker's daughter's laugh — then the keeper clause is fulfilled. Vell is held. Faithfully. From within that person. And the country—"

"—is free to restore itself," Yara finished. "The plague passed a century ago. My father built the Oath to be reversible — *when the plague has passed the country may be restored.* He just tied restoration to the keeper, and the keeper to this soil, because he couldn't imagine any other shape for faithfulness." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, because under it was a hundred years of grief reorganizing itself into fury. "It was a drafting error. All of it. The loops, the Blanks, the keeper who stayed until there was nothing left of him but the word *her* — it was a *drafting error*, and no one caught it for a hundred years because no one outside existed to read it."

"No one outside," Casper repeated softly.

They looked at each other.

Because there was someone outside. There was exactly one person standing in this cathedral who had crossed the Door Out of Vell with both directions of his memory intact, whose name was written in the guild's ledgers in another world, whom the Codex itself had pulled across because he was recorded in both places. There was exactly one person who could leave, and remember, and return the memory to a world where it could take root.

"You," Yara said.

"Me," Casper said.

The hope that rose in her was enormous and immediately terrifying, the way floodwater is terrifying — it did not rise gently, it rose all at once, breaking over the levees she had built out of a century of discipline. She made herself look at the practical shape of it instead, because hope unexamined was just another kind of theft; it stole the future from you in advance.

"It won't be simple." She turned back to the lectern, forcing her voice into the register she used for negotiations, the heir's voice, though there was no guild left to inherit here except the ghost of one. "Taking in a country isn't reading a book. The Oath will want proof of holding. Every name, every story, every debt in the ledgers — they'll have to go into him truly, not copied. And memory displaces memory, Casper. You know that better than anyone. You've spent your whole life watching what it costs other people to lose a single afternoon."

"I steal memories for a living, Yara. I know the arithmetic." He said it lightly, but she saw the flinch under it, quick as a card sharp hiding a tell. "What's the alternative? Leave Vell looping until the loops decay and the Blanks finish their inventory? Watch the baker bury her daughter until there's no daughter left to grieve?" He shook his head. "There's no arithmetic where this costs more than that."

"There might be." She gripped the edge of the lectern. "Listen to me. I need you to hear the worst version before you agree to the best one. If you take Vell in — if you become the vessel — the Oath will treat you as its keeper. Its protections will extend to you. *Though all else forgets.* But its demands will too. Keepers don't get to put the memory down. The clause says *shall not leave* — and if the Oath interprets *leave* the old way, if some clerk-minded fragment of it insists the keeper remains within—"

"Then we amend the interpretation now. While we hold the pen." He came back to stand beside her, shoulder almost touching hers. "Contracts are renegotiable. You taught me that. Or rather, your guild did, back when I was stealing from it."

Despite everything, despite the dread coiling under her ribs, she huffed something that was almost a laugh. "The Ashgrove Guild taught you to rob it. We were negligent employers."

"You were excellent ones. You taught me that every binding thing ever written was written by a person, and persons are fallible, and fallibility is a door." He looked down at the Oath, at the storm-fine script, and his voice dropped into something quieter and more dangerous than ambition. "Your father wrote this in three days, terrified, saving what he could. He left a door open without knowing it. I'm going to walk through it."

Yara studied him in the failing light — the thief who had fallen into her country out of a prophecy, who gave away memories he couldn't afford to give, who lied beautifully and apologized badly and looked at the citizens of Vell as if their griefs were his own unpaid debts. She had exiled herself from two worlds. She had erased her own name from her own homeland. She had spent a hundred years believing that the only honest thing left to her was endurance.

And now a stranger with a thief's hands was telling her the cage had been unlocked the entire time.

"If this works," she said, "the rite happens at the Door. The Oath will want to witness the transfer where the boundary between worlds is thinnest. And the transfer will take — hours, maybe. Maybe longer. Taking in thousands of lives isn't a sip, it's a drowning done slowly." She pulled the second ledger from her coat — the hidden one, the list of everyone the Oath had consumed — and laid it on the lectern beside the founding document, old grief beside older grief. "We'll need the full inventory. Every entry. And we'll need to start tonight, before the Blanks regroup and before the Codex notices its loophole has been found."

"Then we start tonight." Casper set his hand flat on the second ledger, over the final entry, over the two small words that had unraveled her century: *tell her.* He didn't say anything about them. He had stopped saying anything about them since the Blank Court, and the silence had a texture she didn't like, a weight she couldn't place. Another locked drawer. Later, she promised herself. After.

"After," she said aloud anyway.

He glanced up. "After what?"

"After we save a country." She closed the black glass case behind them with a sound like a held breath released. "Come on. We have an Oath to rewrite, and for once in a hundred years, I'm not afraid of what it will cost."

But as they turned down the aisle together, the rose window above them flickered — all its dusk-glass paling for half a heartbeat toward something that looked horribly, impossibly like dawn — and far off in the dark of the shelves, ten thousand books rustled at once, as if the archive had just realized what they intended.

As if it had started counting.

* * *

They worked through what passed for night.

Yara fetched volumes; Casper balanced them. That was the shape of it, and she marveled at the shape — the exiled heir and the thief, passing armloads of lives down the cathedral aisles while the lamps burned oil that hadn't been made in a century. He read entries the way no keeper before him ever had: not with reverence, but with recognition. *A ferryman. Two sisters who ran a bell foundry. A boy who collected river stones shaped like teeth.* He said the names aloud as he catalogued them, deliberately, practicing, and each name landed in the nave and echoed and did not fade, and Yara understood that he was already beginning the holding — that the rite had started the moment he chose it, and every hour of preparation was also an hour of taking.

"How many?" he asked, near what would have been midnight in any honest country.

"In the second ledger? Eleven thousand four hundred and six." She didn't soften it. He hadn't asked her to.

"And the ledgers here — the ones the Oath holds directly?"

"More." She hesitated. "Some lives were never written down anywhere, Casper. Some people in Vell were illiterate, or undocumented, or simply poor. The Codex keeps them the way silt keeps footprints. We'll have to draw them out of the archive itself."

"Then the archive comes with us." He said it easily, terribly, as if eleven thousand lives were a delivery route. Then he paused, one volume open in his hands, and frowned. "Yara. This entry."

She crossed to him. He turned the book so she could see: an ordinary record, a cooper's wife named Maren Ostrow, born, married, dead. Ordinary except for the date of death, which was dated ninety-nine years ago — and except for the marginal note in a hand Yara knew better than her own, because it was her own, aged and rewritten a thousand times by the Codex's slow revisions:

*First to wake. First to speak the keeper's name.*

"Maren Ostrow," Yara whispered. "The baker."

The woman who buried her daughter every day. The woman Casper had broken his own rules to save. The woman who had woken, briefly, when the loop stuttered — and who had looked at Casper in her doorway with wet, astonished eyes and called him by a name no one in Vell should have known.

"She spoke my name," Casper said quietly. "Before I ever gave it to her."

Above them, in the unreachable dark, the shelves groaned — a long, settling sound, like a house deciding whether to stand or fall. Somewhere in the deep stacks, pages began turning on their own, faster and faster, a wind moving through a forest of paper.

And at the far end of the nave, where the great doors stood shut against the dusk, a thin line of light appeared beneath them.

Not dusk-light.

Morning.
