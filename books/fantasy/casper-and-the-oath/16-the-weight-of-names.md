## 16. The Weight of Names

The archive-cathedral smelled of candle smoke and old paper and, beneath it all, something faintly sweet — the scent Yara said was Vell itself, the smell a country makes when it is only remembered and never lived in. Casper stood at the center of the nave where they had drawn the rite's circle in ink mixed from his own blood and the Codex's margins, and he tried not to think of it as an altar.

Thieves didn't use altars. Thieves used ledgers.

"This is how it works," Yara said for the third time, because saying it aloud seemed to steady her more than him. "The Oath took Vell out of the world. To carry it back out through the Door, someone has to hold all of it — every name, every story, every grief the country has been looping through for a hundred years. The Codex can't do it. Paper forgets. It needs a mind."

"It needs a ledger," Casper said. "A living one."

"Yes."

"And ledgers get written on. And writing means erasing what was there before." He flexed his fingers. They were steady. He'd spent twenty years training them to be steady over other people's most precious things; it seemed obscene that they should shake now over his own. "How much of me?"

Yara looked away. That was answer enough.

"The flaw in the Oath," she said instead, "is real. I've checked it eleven times. A keeper inside Vell anchors the memory to the country. But if someone *outside* willingly holds Vell's memory — carries it through the Door as a living record — then the Oath is satisfied twice over, and it can't argue with its own loophole. Vell wakes up whole. The citizens keep their names. The Blanks dissolve."

"And the carrier pays in himself."

"Casper—"

"How much, Yara?"

She met his eyes. Hers were gray-green, the color of dusk over wheat, the color of everything in this country, and he thought: *I will lose this too, eventually. Whatever part of me keeps her face.* He made himself listen anyway.

"Thousands of memories," she said quietly. "Every citizen. The baker and her daughter. The ferryman who drowns every evening and doesn't know it. The children. The dogs. The names carved on the cenotaphs. Each one you take in will displace something of yours to make room. That's the arithmetic of it. Nothing is held for free."

"Nothing is held for free," he repeated. It was the first lesson the Guild ever taught him, back when he'd been a boy with quick hands and nothing worth stealing. *The vault gives nothing without taking something. Neither does the world.* He'd thought he understood it. He'd understood nothing.

"Once the rite begins," Yara said, "it can't be paused. Not cleanly. If you stop halfway, you'll be half-full of Vell and half-empty of yourself, and neither half will fit back together." Her voice did something small and terrible at the edges. "So if you're going to refuse, refuse now. I'll find another way. There's always another way. It just costs more than you have."

Casper almost laughed. "That's what my guildmaster said about joining the Ashgrove. Word for word."

"Was he right?"

"He was right about everything except the parts that mattered." He stepped into the circle. "Begin."

* * *

The rite was not dramatic. That was the first cruelty of it.

He had expected fire, or voices, or the cathedral's thousand-year windows bleeding light the way the Codex bled ink. Instead there was only a book — the second ledger, open on a stand before him — and a needle-thin stylus of bone that Yara pressed into his hand, and her instructions, spoken low and even like a woman defusing something.

"Read each entry aloud. Then press the stylus to your palm. The memory will come in like water finding a crack. Don't fight it. Don't chase it. Just let it settle and move to the next line."

"That's it? No incantation?"

"The Oath is bureaucracy, Casper. It always was. My father wrote it like a contract because he believed contracts were kinder than wars." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "He was wrong, but the paperwork still works."

Casper looked down at the first line.

*Marta Ellery Voss, baker of Lowfield Row. Born under the plum tree behind her mother's oven. Married Tomas twice, once for love and once because the first time no one came.*

He read it aloud. His voice sounded strange in the vast nave — small, secular, a clerk's voice. He pressed the stylus to his palm.

It came in like cold water poured down the back of his collar.

Not a summary. Not the neat paragraph the ledger gave. The *whole* of her — the weight of flour sacks on young shoulders, the specific grief-shaped silence of a house after a child dies, the way Marta hummed off-key when she kneaded because her mother had done it and she'd never once noticed. A life, entire, arriving in the space of a breath.

And leaving — something else, to make room.

He didn't notice what went at first. That was the second cruelty. Displacement wasn't theft; it was erosion. You only saw it afterward, looking at the bank of yourself and finding the shoreline moved.

"Marta Voss," he said again, testing. She was there, whole and humming. He reached for his mother —

and found a shape where she should be. Warmth, yes. A kitchen. A hand smoothing his hair back from his forehead. But the face above him was smooth as an egg, a blank where features should be, and when he tried to summon her eyes they wouldn't come, and her voice was a murmur heard through a wall.

His mother's face was gone.

He stood very still. The stylus trembled against his palm. Somewhere above, the cathedral windows held their perpetual dusk, and Yara watched him with the expression of a woman who had counted this cost already, a hundred times, in the dark.

"You knew," he said. "You knew what the first one would take."

"I didn't know *which*. Only that it takes what's nearest the surface. What you hold most lightly." She hesitated. "Or what you've trained yourself to hold lightly. Thieves are good at that, aren't they? Keeping the precious things buried deep so they can't be stolen."

"My mother's face isn't—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It's buried. That's all. It's buried deep, and it'll hold."

It was a lie and they both knew it. Buried things were exactly what got displaced first — the sediment at the top of the grave washed away before anyone noticed the ground sinking.

"Second line," Yara said gently.

Casper looked down at the ledger. His hands had gone cold, but they were steady. Of course they were. Twenty years of practice.

*Tomas Voss, wheelwright. Afraid of rivers since the age of six.*

He read. He pressed. Tomas arrived with his fear of rivers and his stubborn, unremarkable love for his wife, and Casper felt something else go quiet somewhere behind his ribs — he couldn't say what. A room in the house of himself, dark now, door closed. He would inventory the losses later. If there was a later with enough of him left to do inventorying.

*Ilse Brandt, born on the night the plague came. Never sick. Never forgiven herself for it.*

In. Out. Gone: the smell of rain on the Guild's slate roof, which had meant *home* to him for twenty years and now meant nothing at all.

*Piet Alsen, ferryman. Drowns every evening at the loop's turning and wakes on the far bank believing it was a dream.*

In. Out. Gone: the taste of the spiced wine he stole a sip of every solstice from the guildmaster's private cask. He hadn't even liked it. He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, hollow as a struck bell.

"Don't count yet," Yara said. She had moved closer, standing just outside the circle's edge. "Don't start counting what you've lost until it's finished. Counting mid-rite breaks concentration, and a broken rite breaks *you*."

"I'm a thief, Yara. Counting is the only thing I'm good at." He pressed the stylus down harder than he needed to. "Besides. Someone has to count the keeper. Isn't that what the codex says?"

"That's chapter twenty-two of a story we haven't gotten to yet." Her mouth twitched — the ghost of humor, offered like a hand across a chasm. "Line five."

*Ansel and Wren Hollis, twins. One of them invented a game called Doors that the whole town plays now without knowing who invented it.*

He read it. He pressed it. And the twins came in laughing, and their game came in — the chalked grids on cobblestones, the shouting, the way an entire town could play a children's game for a century without knowing why — and Casper felt it slot into place beside Marta's humming and Tomas's river-fear, thousands of lives beginning to stack inside him like books shelved spine-to-spine.

And something of his own went out to make shelf-room, and this time he caught it leaving.

His mother's voice. Not the face — that was already gone — but the voice, the particular low alto that had sung to him through a fever when he was seven. He felt it lift out of him the way smoke lifts off a wick, and he grabbed for it with everything he had, and it went anyway, because you cannot steal back what is being given away by contract.

"No," he said aloud. Stupidly. As if the rite were a burglar and might be shamed into dropping its prize.

"Casper." Yara's voice, sharp now. "Breathe. Let it go. Fighting the displacement tears the new memories loose with it — you'll lose both."

"They're *mine*—"

"They're the payment." She said it flatly, and he hated her for a full second, hated her clean gray-green certainty, and then he saw her hands. They were shaking. She had known — she must have known, planning this for a hundred years — that the man who finally carried Vell out would pay in the currency of everyone he loved, and she had brought him here anyway because there was no one else, and she would carry that knowledge the way she carried everything: upright, armored, alone.

The hate went out of him like heat off iron.

"Line five," he repeated. His mother's singing voice was gone. Marta Voss hummed off-key in the space where it had been. "Let's keep going."

* * *

They worked through the night that wasn't a night, because Vell had no nights anymore, only the long bruised dusk and the loop's slow turning beneath it.

By the two-hundredth name Casper had lost the color of the sky over the city where he'd grown up. By the four-hundredth, he'd lost the name of the street he'd lived on — he knew a street existed, knew a boy had run down it with stolen apples in his coat, but the boy might as well have lived in a story someone told him. By the six-hundredth, he sat down in the circle without meaning to, legs simply declining further argument, and Yara knelt at the edge with water he couldn't drink (nothing crossed the circle once begun) and fed him the next lines herself, making him repeat them, her voice and his braided together in the empty nave.

*Greta Lund, midwife, who delivered half of Vell and attended the deaths of a quarter of it.*

*Old Samm, who claimed to remember the plague years and lied about all of it except the part that mattered.*

*A dog named Turnip, beloved of three generations of children on Cooper's Lane.*

Each one came in whole. Each one pushed something out. He stopped trying to track the losses around the nine-hundredth mark, because tracking was counting and counting hurt worse than losing, and because — this was the thought that surfaced, cold and clear as lake water — because some part of him, the professional part, the thief who had audited ten thousand entries in the Guild's vaults, had begun doing the arithmetic anyway, silently, automatically:

*Six hundred memories in. Perhaps eight hundred of mine displaced, given the overhead. At roughly four thousand citizens remaining, plus the dead, plus the buildings' memories, plus the land itself... I will run out of Casper Vale somewhere around dawn.*

He wondered what would be left standing at the end. A body that spoke ten thousand names. An empty room with a ledger in it.

He wondered if that was so different from what he'd been before. He had come to the Guild at nine years old, traded his childhood in installments for bread and training, and built a self out of other people's cast-off memories the way a bird builds a nest from stolen string. Maybe the rite was only finishing the job. Maybe Casper Vale had always been mostly vacancy with excellent posture.

"Stop," Yara said.

"I'm fine."

"You're crying."

He touched his cheek. Wet. Interesting. He hadn't authorized that. "Must be Greta Lund. She attended a lot of deaths. Something of hers is settling." He wiped his face with his sleeve and picked up the stylus. "Next line."

"Casper."

"*Next line*, Yara." He heard his own voice crack down the middle and hated it. "Because if I stop, I think about what I've lost, and if I think about what I've lost, I stop forever. And if I stop forever, then Marta Voss hums off-key in a loop until the end of the world, and the twins never learn who invented their game, and your father's contract wins. So. Next. Line."

She looked at him for a long moment — the look of a woman memorizing a face, and he recognized it because he'd worn it himself over other people's faces a thousand times, the thief's farewell, the *this may be the last time I see this* — and then she bent to the ledger.

*Edda Voss,* the next line read. *Daughter of Marta and Tomas. Died at nine years old, on the day the Oath was sworn.*

And the grief came in — oh, it came in like a flood through a broken door, the whole town's grief for one small girl, the funeral that restarted every single morning in the bakery on Lowfield Row — and Casper held it and held it and did not fight it, because this one he wanted. This one he would carry gladly, all the way through the Door, all the way out of Vell, so that somewhere in the waking world a little girl who died inside a sealed country would be remembered by at least one living soul outside it.

He pressed the stylus to his palm and whispered, "For Edda," and felt something go out of him that made him gasp — something large, load-bearing, a beam from the center of the house.

He didn't know what it was yet. That was the worst part. He wouldn't know until he reached for it later and found air.

"Yara." His voice was thin. "When this is done. When we get to the Door. There's something I need to tell you. About the fine print. About what the Codex—"

The windows exploded inward.

Not glass — *ink*. A black tide pouring through the colored panes like the sea coming through a cathedral's eyes, and out of it rose shapes in clerks' gray, faceless, featureless, the Blank converging at last on the one thing they could not allow: a ledger filling itself faster than they could erase.

Yara was on her feet, knife already drawn. "The rite — can it survive being moved?"

Casper looked down at the circle glowing faintly around him, at the ledger, at the stylus sunk into his bleeding palm. Sixteen hundred names deep. Three thousand to go.

"No," he said. "But I don't think we're being asked."

The Blanks began to walk down the nave, unhurried as clerks at the end of a shift, and the last thing Casper saw before the dark took the windows was the ledger page fluttering — turning itself, eagerly, toward whatever came next.
