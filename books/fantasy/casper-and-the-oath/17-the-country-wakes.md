## 17. The Country Wakes

Yara felt it before she saw it — a shudder running through the floor of the archive-cathedral, as though the building itself had been asleep for a century and had just been shaken by the shoulder.

She was standing at the base of the Oath's original text, one hand pressed flat against the stone where the founders' names were carved in letters worn nearly smooth, when the first bell rang.

Vell had no bells. That was the point of Vell. A country locked in dusk, locked in loop, needed no bells to mark the hours because the hours never changed. The baker pulled her loaves at the same gray minute every gray day; the lamplighter walked his same circuit through streets that never fully darkened and never fully dawned. Yara had lived inside that sameness so long she had stopped hearing its silence.

Now the silence broke like ice on a river.

The bell — if it was a bell — came from everywhere at once, from the stones and the air and the marrow of her bones. It was the sound of a ledger being opened after a hundred years, of a page turning that no one had turned since her father signed his name in ink made of everything they were willing to lose.

"Casper," she said to the empty nave.

He was three arches away, kneeling in the circle they had chalked from the Oath's own geometry, his hands flat on the open second ledger, and he did not answer her. He could not. The rite had him now. She watched the light move under his skin — not the gold of guild-magic but something older, wheat-colored, dusk-colored, the color of Vell itself — pouring into him the way water pours into cracked ground. His lips moved without sound. He was counting. Of course he was counting. The codex had promised a keeper who counts, and Casper Vale had been counting his whole life: debts, entries, names in columns, years stolen and years returned. Now he counted a country.

Yara ran to the cathedral doors and threw them open onto a street that was screaming.

* * *

The loop did not end gently. It ended the way a held breath ends.

She stood on the steps and watched the dusk split. Above the rooftops of Lower Ashen Street — a street that had not existed for anyone, including its own residents, an hour ago — the perpetual twilight tore along a seam only she seemed able to see, and beyond the tear was not night and not day but *color*, real color, the bruised violet of genuine evening arriving for the first time in a century. People stopped mid-stride. A man carrying a bolt of blue cloth dropped it in the road. A woman at a window let her watering can fall and didn't hear it land.

Then they remembered.

Yara had imagined this moment a thousand times across a hundred years, and in every imagining it looked like joy. She had pictured doors flying open, strangers embracing in the streets, the whole country exhaling at once — *we are here, we were always here, we are Vell.* She had built the fantasy carefully, the way you build anything that has to last a long time in the dark.

What actually happened was worse and better and nothing like it.

A scream started somewhere near the well square and spread the way fire spreads through dry grass. It was not one voice. It was hundreds, thousands, the entire population of a nation waking mid-day with a century of stolen days landing on them all at once — every funeral they'd half-mourned, every child they'd half-raised, every grief they'd been forced to feel fresh each morning because the loop reset before mourning could finish. The baker on Coriander Row had buried her daughter eleven hundred times. Now she remembered all eleven hundred funerals in a single breath.

Yara went down the steps two at a time.

"Her name is Sefa," she heard herself saying, pushing through the crowd toward the bakery with the crooked chimney. "Sefa Ilvet. Her daughter's name was Miren. Miren loved—"

But she didn't know what Miren loved. That wasn't hers to know anymore. It was in Casper now, all of it, every name and story and small beloved detail of a country she had erased, and she could only stand in the street outside the bakery and listen to a woman howl her daughter's name at a ceiling, and understand that she had done this. Not the Oath alone. Not her father alone. *She* had helped write it. She had held the pen while her father dictated the terms, and she had told herself for a hundred years that it was mercy, that forgetting was kinder than the plague of hollowing that would have eaten these people alive, and now the bill had come due and it was being paid in screams.

An old man grabbed her arm. His eyes were wild and wet and terribly awake.

"You," he said. "I know your face. I don't know why I know your face."

"Yara," she said. "My name is Yara Ashgrove."

"Ashgrove." He said it like a curse word, and then he said it like a prayer, and then he began to cry, and she understood that both things were true at once and always had been. "You're the founder's girl. You're the one who wrote us out." His grip tightened until it hurt. "Why? Why would you — my wife, I couldn't remember my wife, I set two places at the table for thirty years and I couldn't remember *why*—"

"I'm sorry," Yara said, which was the smallest, truest thing she owned. "We're taking it back. All of it. Right now. It's coming back to you."

"It's *too much*," the old man whispered, and let go of her, and sat down in the middle of the street like a puppet with cut strings, and began to rock.

Around her, Vell was tearing itself into factions faster than she could turn. Some people wept with gratitude — a young woman clutching a stranger who turned out to be her brother, both of them shouting each other's names over and over like proof. Some cursed. A butcher came out of his shop with a cleaver still in his hand, eyes flat with fury, looking for someone to blame, and Yara met his gaze and did not look away because she deserved whatever he saw in her face. And some — this was the part that frightened her most, more than the cleaver, more than the screaming — some begged.

"Forget me again," a woman pleaded, kneeling in front of a neighbor who had just remembered losing her. "Please, please, I can't have you remember that, take it back, take it *back*—"

Grief without anesthesia, delivered all at once, to everyone. Liberation that felt like catastrophe. Yara stood in the middle of her country's first true hour in a century and understood, finally, completely, what her father must have felt standing in this same square with the plague creeping up the valley and the Codex open on his knees: that there are choices where every branch is a wound, and you simply pick the wound you can live beside.

She had picked forgetting. Casper was picking remembering. And neither choice, she saw now, had ever been clean.

* * *

By the time she fought her way back up the cathedral steps, the sky above Vell had gone properly dark for the first time, and stars were coming out.

Stars. She stopped dead on the top step. There should not have been stars. Vell's sky was painted — not literally, but effectively, sealed under the Oath like a lid on a jar — and yet there they were, sharp and indifferent and ancient, wheeling slowly into place overhead as if they'd been waiting a hundred years for permission. Somewhere below, a child was shrieking with what might have been terror or delight, and an adult voice kept repeating, dazedly, *"Look, look, look."*

Inside, the light around Casper had gone blinding.

He was still kneeling, but he was translucent at the edges now, his outline flickering like a candle flame in a draft. The second ledger lay open beneath his hands and its pages were moving, turning themselves, drinking. She could see names rising off the pages and sinking into him — Sefa Ilvet, Miren, the lamplighter Dov Harn, the old man in the street whose name she realized with a lurch she had never learned — and with each name he grew fainter and the country around them grew *firmer*, colors deepening, sounds sharpening, the very stones of the cathedral regaining a grain and weight they hadn't possessed since the Oath closed over everything.

It was working. It was actually working. He was becoming the living ledger, the anchor the loophole required, memory held willingly by someone standing inside the border of the thing remembered —

— and the Blanks were coming.

She heard them before she saw them: the particular silence of their approach, a hole in the noise of the waking city, like a footprint in sound. Through the great west window she saw them crossing the well square, six of them, then ten, then more, flowing out of the administrative quarter in their clerk-gray coats with their smooth blank faces tilted up toward the cathedral. They moved without hurry. They had never hurried. They had a hundred years of practice at being patient, and tonight, for the first time, they had a reason not to be.

The Blanks had been keepers once. Casper knew that — he'd told her nothing, but she'd read it in the way he looked at them afterward, in the new flinch behind his eyes — and Yara had pieced it together herself long ago, in the worst of her century, staring at those faceless clerks and wondering why their movements felt familiar. They were what happened when a keeper stayed too long and the memories stayed instead of the man. They were the Oath's immune system, and Casper Vale was, by every law written into the Codex, an infection.

They reached the bottom of the steps and stopped, all of them at once, heads tilting in unison toward the doors.

Yara put her back to the nave and faced them, arms out, the way you stand between a wolf and something smaller than you.

"He's almost finished," she called down. "And when he finishes, the Oath is rewritten. Your bargain is void. Whatever you were, whoever you were — it ends tonight. Let it end."

The foremost Blank took a single step forward. Where its foot fell, the starlight on the cobblestones went gray and dead, and did not come back.

From inside the nave, faintly, Casper's voice — thin, stretched, counting: *"—four thousand one hundred and twelve, four thousand one hundred and thirteen—"*

So many names. So much of him, spent. Yara thought of the room in her own house scrubbed empty of her, of the price she'd paid to walk freely through a country that could not see her, and she understood exactly what Casper was buying with what he was paying, and she hated it, and she had never admired anyone more.

The Blanks began to climb the steps.

Yara wrenched the cathedral doors shut and dropped the crossbar, knowing it would hold about as long as a promise. Behind her, the light swelled. Outside, the first fist struck the wood — a soundless impact, because the Blanks made no noise, even in fury, even perhaps especially in fury — and she felt the bar jump in its brackets.

"Casper." She ran down the aisle. "How many left?"

His eyes opened. They were the strangest thing she had ever seen: full of Vell, every street and grave and kitchen garden, and underneath all of it, barely holding on, still recognizably himself. He smiled. Even now, the thief smiled.

"Four thousand one hundred and forty," he said. "Give me a minute."

The crossbar splintered.

Yara spun, raised both hands, and did the only thing she had left: she spoke the Oath aloud — her father's words, her own handwriting, the terms she had helped draft in a smoke-filled study while the plague howled at the windows — and the words came out changed, amended by everything they'd learned, by the flaw they'd found, by the loophole bought with Casper's mother's face and four thousand names besides:

*"Let the keeper hold what was given, from within, willingly, until the count is done—"*

The doors burst inward.

Gray coats poured through the wreckage like smoke poured through a crack, faceless and silent and numberless, and Yara planted herself in front of the chalked circle with nothing between the Blanks and Casper but her body and her name.

The lead Blank raised its hand toward her. And then — it hesitated. Its smooth face tilted, the way a dog's head tilts at a familiar whistle. Around it, the others slowed, faltered, their advance stuttering like the loop had stuttered.

Yara stared at them, and understanding arrived cold and enormous.

They remembered her. Under everything, under the erasure, some sediment of who they had been still stirred — keepers, all of them, men and women who had once stood where Casper knelt — and the Oath they served was dissolving around them with every name Casper swallowed, and they could feel the ground going out from under the only existence they had left.

The lead Blank's hand trembled in the air between them.

"Whoever you were," Yara said softly, to all of them, "it's almost over. I'm sorry it took this long."

Behind her, Casper's voice rose toward the final count, and outside, over a country seeing stars for the first time in a hundred years, the last of the dusk finally, mercifully, began to break.
