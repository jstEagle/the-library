## 19. The Oath Kept

Yara had spent a hundred years learning to read lies the way other people read weather.

She could feel this one coming the moment they left the archive-cathedral — a pressure in the air, like the hush before ink dries. Casper walked ahead of her through Vell's streets with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders set at an angle she'd come to know as *performing calm*. He was good at it. He was very, very good at it. That was the whole trouble with thieves: they stole so well that eventually they forgot what belonged to them.

The wheat field opened before them gold under a dusk that no longer moved. The Door stood where it had always stood — alone, patient, its frame of black iron furred with frost that never melted and was never cold. Behind it, the horizon held the color of old paper.

And above the Door, hanging in the air like a signature at the bottom of a contract, the Codex's fine print had surfaced.

Yara read it once. Then again. Her heart did not break; she had learned long ago to keep her hearts in separate rooms, and this one she simply closed the door on.

*The keeper must remain within Vell, holding what was taken, until the count is complete.*

Casper was watching her watch it. She saw him decide — saw the flicker behind his eyes, quick as a pickpocket's hand — and then he smiled, easy and warm, and said, "Well. Looks like paperwork. There's always paperwork."

"No," Yara said quietly. "There isn't."

"There usually is. Guild law runs on triplicate." He gestured at the fine print as if brushing away a fly. "It says the anchor has to be someone recorded in both worlds. Fine. We'll sort out which one of us that is back at the cathedral. I've been meaning to file a complaint about the Codex anyway — century-old prophecy, terrible margins—"

"Stop."

He stopped. He was still smiling, but the smile had gone thin at the edges, stretched over something else.

"You lied beautifully," Yara said. "I want you to know that. The cadence was perfect. You even gave me the joke about filing a complaint, which you've been saving since the Blank Court, waiting for exactly the moment I'd be too frightened to notice you were hiding something." She stepped closer. "I know because I wrote the same lie. Word for word, almost. A hundred years ago, standing in front of this same Door, telling my father there was nothing in the fine print worth reading."

The dusk pressed close. Somewhere far off, a bell rang the hour it had rung every hour for a hundred years, and no one heard it.

"Casper," she said. "You're not staying here."

* * *

He didn't deny it. That was the thing about Casper Vale — he had a thief's honesty, which meant he only ever lied when lying was cheaper than truth, and right now the truth had become the better bargain.

"It's done," he said simply. "The rite is finished. Your country's names are inside me. All of them. Every baker and blacksmith and child who ever fell asleep mid-sentence. If I walk through that Door, Vell comes apart like wet paper. You know that. I know that. So the arithmetic is already done, and the only question left is who gets to be sentimental about it."

"I'm not being sentimental. I'm being practical." Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she let it. "You can't hold a country, Casper. You're a ledger-keeper, yes, the best the guild ever trained — but a keeper needs roots. A keeper needs to have been *counted* somewhere. You were counted nowhere. You told me yourself — your mother sold your name before you could walk. You are the one person in two worlds with nothing anchoring him, and you think that makes you the obvious choice. It doesn't. It makes you the cheapest."

Something moved behind his eyes at the mention of his mother — the memory he'd traded first, the face he no longer had. He covered it fast, but not fast enough. Thieves never could, when you stole from their real inventory.

"And you?" he said. "You're rooted? Yara, you erased yourself from this country so thoroughly that your own house has a room with no you in it. The ledgers don't trust you either. They don't even remember you exist."

"The ledgers don't need to remember me." She pulled the second ledger from her coat — the hidden one, the book of everyone the Oath had consumed — and held it up between them like a shield or a verdict. "I wrote my name into this book myself, the day I helped bind the Oath. My father thought I was signing as a witness. I signed as collateral. Look."

She turned it toward him. There, on the oldest page, in her own younger handwriting: *Yara Ashgrove — given freely, against the day of counting.* And beneath it, in the Codex's slow copperplate, a line that had grown since the rite began:

*Collateral accepted. Collateral sufficient.*

"Sufficient," Casper repeated. His voice had gone quiet and careful, the way it went when he was doing sums he didn't like. "Sufficient for what?"

"For the anchor." She met his eyes. "The fine print doesn't say the keeper stays. It says *something* stays — something the Oath holds claim on. It's been holding claim on me for a hundred years. I just didn't read my own contract closely enough until tonight." She laughed, short and bitter. "I wrote a loophole for everyone in Vell and none for myself. Poetic. My father would have hated it."

"No."

"Casper—"

"*No.*" He said it louder than she'd ever heard him say anything, and the wheat field seemed to lean in around the sound. "You don't get to do this. You spent a century locked out of your own homeland — you stood outside the Door every year, I've seen how you look at it — and now that it finally opens, you want to give it away? To me? To a man who can't remember his own mother's face?" He shook his head slowly. "You go through. That's not a negotiation. That's the whole point of everything we did. The loophole was *yours*, Yara. Someone outside Vell willingly holds its memory. You're outside. You hold it. I'm just the courier."

"A courier who swallowed a nation," she said. "You think the guild will take you back like that? You'll walk through that Door carrying thousands of lives in your head, and the Ashgrove ledgers will open for you because they'll have no choice — because for once, the impossible thing will already be in our vault, wearing a tired man's face." She stepped forward, close enough to see the dusk reflected in his eyes. "But if I stay, I stay as collateral, and collateral is not a life. I've done a hundred years of almost-life, Casper. I know exactly what it costs. Don't you dare price it for me like I haven't paid it before."

* * *

They fought the way people fight who love each other badly — sideways, in arguments shaped like logistics.

He said the Blanks would empty her within a decade; she said the Blanks were dissolving already, released by the rite, and he knew it. He said the citizens waking would need someone who remembered Vell from *inside*; she said they needed someone alive more. He said a keeper must be someone the ledgers already trust, and she threw the second ledger at his feet and shouted that trust was a fiction the guild invented to make debt feel like devotion.

And then he said the thing that stopped them both.

"If you stay," he said, "then I carried all of this for nothing. Every name. Every stolen-feeling memory I took into myself while pieces of me went dark." He touched his own temple, briefly, like a man checking a wound. "I lost my mother's face for this country. I lost my tenth summer. I lost the smell of the vault — do you understand? The place I was loneliest, and I lost even that. I did it so that *you* could walk out. Not Vell. Vell doesn't get to walk anywhere. *You.* Because someone should leave this place carrying something other than grief, and you're the only person I've met in two worlds worth the freight."

The wheat whispered though there was no wind. Above the Door, the fine print hung patient as a creditor.

"That's not an argument," Yara said at last, unsteadily. "That's a confession."

"I'm a thief. Everything I own is stolen, including my arguments." He tried to smile. It nearly worked. "Take the memory, Yara. Take all of them. Rebuild the guild into something that gives instead of takes. Find a way back, if you can — you're the only person stubborn enough. And when you write me into the ledgers, spell my name properly. I never got to see it spelled properly anywhere."

She looked at him for a long time. And then, because she had spent a century learning to read lies, she read the truest one he'd ever told her — that he believed this was fair, that he had made peace with it, that he would stand in this field until the dusk ended and call it a good bargain.

And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that arguing with him would cost more than losing him. He had built this ending brick by brick across twenty-two chapters of theft and mercy, and if she tore it down he would simply build another, worse, alone.

So she did the only thing left. She changed the shape of the argument without changing its outcome.

"Then hear my terms," she said. "Not yours. Mine. You stay — but you stay as *my* keeper, sworn to me personally, entered in the second ledger under my authority, and I am your counter-signature for as long as I draw breath. Which means every year, on the night the Door stands open, I come to this field and I read to you from whatever the ledgers have become. Every year, Casper. Until one of us finds the flaw in this contract too. And there is *always* a flaw. I should know. I've written three of them."

His breath caught. "That's not how the Oath works."

"The Oath," Yara said, "is whatever we say it is. We rewrote it once already. Did you think I came this far to obey it twice?"

* * *

They sealed it the way the guild sealed everything — with words spoken plainly and witnessed by a book. Casper pressed his palm to the second ledger and gave his oath in a voice that did not shake until the last line, which was her name. Yara signed beneath him, and the Codex's copperplate, after a long considering pause, added a single grudging word beneath the fine print:

*Witnessed.*

Then there was nothing left but the Door, standing open onto the ordinary dark of an ordinary world, and the two of them standing before it with a farewell folded between them like a letter neither would finish reading aloud.

"You'll forget the small things first," Casper said. "The way Vell smelled. What dusk sounded like here. Keep those somewhere safe. Those are the ones worth stealing."

"I'm the heir to the Ashgrove Guild," Yara said. "Stealing is the family business."

He laughed — really laughed, once — and then he took her hand, pressed something small into it, and stepped back before she could see what it was. She opened her fingers. A slip of ledger paper, torn neat, with a single line in his cramped clerk's hand:

*Tell her I counted her among my kept things.*

Her throat closed. She looked up to say something — anything — but he was already turning toward the wheat, toward the long gold light, toward the country he had chosen the way some men choose a wound.

"One more thing," he called back, not turning around. "When you find the way back — and you will, because you argue like my mother probably did, gods rest whichever face she had — knock first. I'll be counting. I always count."

The dusk began, at last, to move.

Yara Ashgrove walked through the Door carrying a nation's memory and one torn line of paper, and behind her she heard it begin: soft, steady, unhurried, the voice of a man starting a count he intended never to finish.

*One,* said Casper Vale. *Two.*

The Door closed on *three*.

And far away, in a vault full of impossible things, a ledger opened itself to a fresh page and waited for her hand.
