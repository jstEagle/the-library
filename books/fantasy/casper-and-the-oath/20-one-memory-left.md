## 20. One Memory Left

The cathedral had gone quiet in the way that only very old buildings can — not silence, but a listening. The archive shelves rose around Casper like the ribs of something that had once breathed, and between them the last light of Vell's long dusk pooled on the floor in amber sheets, as if the country were already practicing for morning.

He stood at the center of the transference circle Yara had chalked from the Oath's own margins, and he tried to think of it as a ledger.

That was the trick. That had always been the trick. A memory was an entry; a life was a book; and a thief, above all things, was honest with his arithmetic. What came in must be counted. What went out must be recorded. He had spent twenty years keeping other people's sums balanced, and now he stood in the middle of the largest entry he would ever make, and the sum of it was himself.

Yara watched him from the edge of the circle. She had stopped arguing somewhere past midnight, which frightened him more than any argument would have. Arguing meant she still believed there was a version of this where they both walked out. Silence meant she had done her own arithmetic and arrived at his.

"You don't have to say anything clever," she said finally. "I know how you get before a job."

"I wasn't going to say anything clever." He checked the chalk line with his toe. "I was going to say something accurate. There's a difference."

"Gods forbid."

"The difference," Casper said, "is that clever is what you use when you want someone to stop asking questions, and accurate is what you owe someone who's about to carry you home in their head."

Her jaw tightened. She knew — she had to know; she'd written the same lie a century ago, worn the same smile across the same farewell — but knowing a thing and being able to bear it were different ledgers entirely, and he would not make her audit this one twice.

So he lied beautifully, one last time, with his whole face: "When the transfer's done, I'll walk through behind you. The Door stays open while the keeper holds. That's what the margins said. I read them three times."

"You read them four," Yara said quietly. "You always read things four times when you're checking whether I'll catch you lying."

He laughed, because there was nothing else to do with the sound in his chest, and stepped into the center of the circle.

* * *

The rite began, as all the Oath's workings did, with a name.

Not his. The Codex did not care for the names of thieves. It began with *Vell* — the whole word of it, spoken into the amber air, and the word opened like a door inside a door. Casper felt the first citizen arrive before he saw anything: a pressure behind his eyes, a warmth in the mouth, the taste of rye bread and river water and woodsmoke that belonged to no single day.

Then the names came.

They came the way rain comes at the end of a dry season — first one, tentative, testing the ground; then ten; then the sky simply giving up on holding any of it back. Every person Vell had ever held. Every name the plague of forgetting had eaten and the Oath had swallowed whole to keep safe. They poured into him along channels of ink he could feel threading through his blood, and each one brought its cargo.

A ferryman named Odell who sang flat and knew it. Twin sisters who had invented a language of whistles to talk past their mother. A stonemason whose hands remembered every wall in the capital better than his eyes did. A child's drawing of a horse with too many legs, kept in a drawer for forty years because the mother couldn't decide which leg to grieve.

Casper took them the way he had been trained to take everything: fully, without flinching, counting as he went.

But taking was not the same as giving, and the rite demanded both directions. For every name he absorbed, something of his own had to flow out to fill the space — that was the flaw Yara had found, the loophole turned inside out: a vessel cannot hold a country and remain itself. The Oath had never specified *what* the keeper gave up. So the ledger of Casper Vale began, entry by entry, to empty.

His mother's face went first.

He had known it would — he had chosen the order himself, in the small hours, alone with the Codex — and knowing did nothing, nothing at all, against the actual moment of it. One instant she was there: flour on her knuckles, the crooked tooth, the way she hummed when she thought he was asleep. The next instant there was a shape where she had been, warm and beloved and utterly blank, like a window fogged from the inside.

He did not cry out. He counted. *One.* He wrote it in the air with the thief's habit of his fingers, an invisible tick against nothing, and moved to the next.

The smell of the guild vault's iron door. Gone. *Two.*

His first theft — the taste of someone else's wedding day, stolen at nine years old from a bride too happy to guard herself. Gone. *Three.*

Winter in the capital, the specific gray of it. The sound his boots made on the guild stairs. The face of the guildmaster who had dismissed the prophecy over cold tea. The name of the street he'd grown up on. The feeling of being proud of himself — all of it, every instance, drained out through his fingertips into the soil of Vell like water into cracked earth, and the earth drank, and somewhere far away a wheat field that remembered burning began, faintly, to remember growing instead.

He could feel the country knitting itself around his losses. Each surrendered memory became a stitch. The baker's street unburned. The cathedral's broken spire rose. Somewhere a bell that hadn't rung in a hundred years gathered its note in its throat and waited.

It hurt less than he expected. That was the horror of it — it felt like rest, like setting down loads he'd forgotten he carried, and he understood suddenly why the Blanks never fought. Emptiness was gentle. Emptiness was a warm room with no doors. He gripped the edges of the pain deliberately, cataloguing it, hoarding even agony the way a miser hoards copper, because the alternative was to drift.

And beneath the drifting, at the very bottom of him, one thing sat anchored like a nail driven through the hull of a sinking ship.

*Yara.*

Not a memory of her — those were going, going, going; her voice at the vault door, her fury in the wheat field, her face lit by the Door's white seam, all of it bleeding out of him in its turn, and he let each one go because he had to, because the arithmetic did not negotiate. But under the memories, deeper than memories, was the thing that had made them matter, and that thing was a single syllable-shaped fact:

She had a name, and he had been trusted to say it.

* * *

Somewhere past the loss of his tenth year — he could no longer remember whether he'd liked his tenth year; the space where the answer lived was smooth and warm and vacant — the transfer changed key.

The citizens' waking memories were done. What remained was Vell itself: the land's own recollection, older and slower, moving through him like groundwater. He felt the founding. He felt the plague arrive like a tide of static, eating names off gravestones, unwriting children mid-sentence. He felt the founders gather in this very cathedral, young and terrified and brilliant, and he felt — wearing Yara's father's memory like a coat two sizes too large — the moment the Oath was drafted, the moment the price was named, the moment everyone in that room decided, silently, together, that survival was worth becoming a secret.

And he felt Yara, seventeen years old, standing in the doorway of this same circle, holding the pen her father handed her.

He felt her write her own name into the clause of exile.

The memory was hers, and receiving it felt like trespass more than theft, and he held it as carefully as he had ever held anything. This was the room scrubbed bare. This was the empty house. This was the hundred years. He finally understood her completely, in the last hour he had a self to understand with, and the understanding was so heavy and so simple that he almost laughed: she had erased herself from her own country so that she could move freely enough to save it, and it had worked, and it had cost exactly everything, and she had never once complained, because complaining required a witness and she had made sure there were none.

Well, Casper thought. There's one now. For another few minutes.

*Four hundred and six,* he counted. *Four hundred and seven.* Names, days, mercies. His hands had stopped being able to feel the counting gesture, so he did it in his mind, ticking away against the dark like a clerk in a vault that was slowly filling with water.

The wheat field outside the city was turning gold. He could feel it turning, feel the burned memory composting into growth, feel the Door at its center humming like a struck bell that refused to fade. The Door was open. It had been open since the rite began. It would stay open as long as the keeper held — and the keeper held, the fine print said, *from within.*

He had read it four times.

* * *

Near the end, the Codex spoke to him.

Not in words. In the way a ledger speaks: by showing him the balance. He saw himself laid out in columns — everything taken in, everything given away — and the columns were nearly equal now, nearly closed, a book almost finished. At the bottom, in the margin where anomalies lived, one final line waited to be written.

*The keeper may retain one (1) item of personal significance.*

The mercy of it was obscene. The Codex had consumed keepers for a century and offered, at the very end, a single crumb of self, as if that made the arithmetic kind. He hated it. He examined the hatred and found it useful, and kept it — no, he couldn't keep it, hatred weighed something, hatred was an entry, and the columns were closing—

He chose fast, the way thieves choose, with the hand before the heart can file paperwork.

He reached down past the fogged windows where his mother had been. Past the vanished streets and the stolen tastes and the gray particular winters. Down to the nail through the hull. And he took the one thing the rules had never listed among his possessions because it had never been his to take:

*A name, freely given.*

She had spoken it once — only once, weeks ago, in the dark of her preserved house with its one scrubbed-empty room, after he'd found what she'd done to herself and she'd found him sitting in the wreckage of her privacy. She had been furious, and then exhausted, and then, in the smallest voice he'd ever heard her use, she had said it: her full name, the one no living person carried anymore. *Yara Ashgrove.* Given, not stolen. The only gift in Vell anyone had ever handed him without a debt attached.

He wrapped the Codex's single permitted retention around that name like a man wrapping his last coin in oilcloth before a flood. Not the memory of the night — that drained away with all the others, and he let it, and it went gently, the way a tide goes out. Just the name remained. Two words. Weightless and enormous.

*Yara Ashgrove.*

Everything else emptied.

* * *

He came back to himself — to the shape where himself used to be — kneeling in the circle with dawn coming through windows that had been broken for a century and now were not.

Morning. Real morning, Vell's first in a hundred years, pouring through the restored glass in bars of clean gold, and the dust motes in it were not dust motes but names, settling, finding their places, the country breathing itself back together around the hollow that was Casper Vale.

He stood. His body worked. That was in the terms somehow — he supposed the Oath needed hands to hold the ledger.

At the edge of the circle, Yara stood absolutely still, watching him with an expression he recognized from the inside: the look of a person doing arithmetic and hating the total.

"Casper," she said.

The name landed in the hollow of him and rang, and there was nothing in him that answered to it — no childhood, no guild, no mother, no crimes, no pride — and yet some structure deep in what remained knew the word was his, the way a bell knows its own strike.

"That's mine," he heard himself say. His voice sounded like a stranger's. "The name. You keep using it like it's still attached to something."

"It's attached to you."

"It's attached to a hole." He looked down at his hands. Thief's hands. He no longer knew whose habits had trained them, only that they were steady, and that steadiness felt inherited rather than earned. "There's a lot of Vell in here now. Whole streets. I can hear a bell deciding whether to ring. I think it's waiting for me to count it as ready."

"Casper—"

"The Door's open." He turned toward the cathedral doors, and beyond them, far across the gold field, the white seam of it stood in the morning haze like a held breath. "It'll hold until you're through. After that—" He shrugged, and the shrug was light, effortless, beautiful, the best performance of his career, delivered to an audience of one by a performer who had forgotten his own name but not, apparently, his craft. "After that it closes. Rules are rules. You taught me that in the wheat field, remember?"

"No," Yara said. Her voice had gone very quiet and very level, the levelness of a drawn blade. "I remember what the fine print says. I remember reading it four times myself, a hundred years ago, standing exactly where you're standing, telling my father the exact lie you just told me."

For a moment the hollow in him ached with something that was not quite grief and not quite relief.

"Then you know," Casper said, "how convincing I am."

"Yes." She didn't move toward the Door. She didn't move at all. "And I know what I did about it. And I have spent a century learning exactly how much it costs to let someone stand in your place because staying is easier than fighting them." Her eyes were wet and furious and utterly clear. "So we are going to have this argument, Casper Vale, right now, in the morning light, in front of your bell and your country and whatever's left of God. Because I have done the arithmetic, and I am not finished with you."

Behind him, in the deep places of the restored land, the bell finally rang — one clear note across the gold field — and Casper, keeper of Vell, holder of ten thousand names and one, discovered that a hollow man could still be afraid.

"Yara Ashgrove," he said, and gave her back the only thing he had left. "Let's argue, then."

She crossed the circle toward him, and the Door, far off in the wheat, waited — patient as a page — holding open for her alone.
