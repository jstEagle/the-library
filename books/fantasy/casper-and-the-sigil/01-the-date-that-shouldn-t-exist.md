## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Dominic Fairweather found the date that shouldn't exist.

He knew it was late because his eyes had begun to ache in that particular way they did after six hours of columns — the numbers swimming, doubling, insisting on being wrong. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts the way it kept everything else: badly, lovingly, and with an unreasonable number of exceptions. Tonight's ledger was worse than most. Three pages of tallow purchases that made no sense, a debt owed by someone recorded only as *the woman who isn't here anymore*, and an entry, in Brannoc's brutal hand, reading simply: *one miracle, used, will return it Tuesday.*

Dominic dipped his pen, squared the page against the desk's worn edge, and reached for the second ledger beneath his elbow — the one he hadn't asked for.

It had arrived that morning with no courier's mark, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age. He'd assumed it was another of the guild's strays; things found their way to them the way lost cats find their way to kind women. He'd stacked it under his work and forgotten it until now, when shifting his elbow dislodged it and sent it sliding across the desk with a whisper like something exhaling.

The binding was black leather cracked into a mosaic of tiny islands. No title. A sigil pressed into the cover — not stamped, but *grown*, as though the leather itself had been persuaded to rise into those looping, interlocking lines. Wax sealed the fore-edge, old wax the color of dried blood, unbroken.

A sealed book. In a guild full of people who could not leave a locked door alone, it had survived the entire day untouched only because it had arrived addressed, faintly, in ink gone brown as old tea: *For the ledger-keeper.*

Him. There were three people in the guild who could be called keepers of anything, but there was only one ledger-keeper.

Dominic told himself he would simply note its arrival in the acquisitions column and set it aside for Maren, whose business this properly was. Royal historians got the dangerous books. He balanced sums. That was the whole of his ambition, and he clung to it the way other men clung to religion.

But the wax was already cracked.

He stared at it. He was almost certain — nearly certain — it had been whole when it came off the cart. The break ran clean down the center, and from inside the fissure rose a smell he had no name for: not incense, not rot, but the smell of a room where someone has just finished crying and opened a window.

*Note it in acquisitions,* he told himself again, and did exactly that, because procedure was a kind of courage available even to cowards. *One book, sealed, provenance unknown.* His pen hovered over the entry. Then, because the ledger demanded particulars and the ledger always won:

*Contents: unknown. Condition: broken open.*

The moment the words dried, the candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

Dominic opened the book.

The first hundred leaves were blank. Not empty the way paper is usually empty — blank the way snow is, before anything has crossed it. He turned pages faster, and the cold coming off them raised the hair on his arms. Then, two-thirds through, the pages began to fill. Not all at once. Ink crawled up out of the fibers as he watched, letters assembling themselves letter by letter, like men gathering in a square.

*In the reign of the king who will not die,*

He read on. The script was old-fashioned, formal, the hand of someone trained to copy scripture. Prophecy, then. Maren would want —

*on the night the bells lie,*

*the unmaker comes to Vaelmoor.*

*His name is written in the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things.*

Dominic laughed. It came out thin and stupid in the empty room. Every crackpot in the realm wrote prophecies about the guild; they were famous enough to attract lunatics and poor enough to deserve them. He turned the page to find the author's name, the seal, some scrap of evidence to carry upstairs to Maren so they could laugh about it together over breakfast.

Instead he found the date.

Tonight's date. Not a year, not a season — the exact day, rendered in the guild's own house style, the style he himself had invented four years ago because the old format wasted margin space. No one outside these walls dated anything that way. No one alive, at least.

And beneath it, in ink still wet enough to shine:

*Dominic Fairweather, keeper of accounts.*

*He balances what cannot be balanced.*

*He begins the unbinding.*

The candle went out.

Darkness took the room whole, and in the darkness the page began to glow — a soft, sourceless luminance, as if the ink remembered light and missed it. Dominic stood so fast his chair went over backward. He did not remember deciding to run for the stairs. He did remember that every lamp in the guildhall slept behind two floors of stone, and that shouting would bring Brannoc, and Brannoc was exactly who he needed, and his legs would not move toward the door.

They moved toward the desk.

Not his legs' decision. He understood that with the strange clarity of a man watching his own hands do carpentry while his mind is elsewhere. Something had hold of him the way a current holds a swimmer — not cruelly, not even firmly, but absolutely. He gripped the desk edge hard enough to splinter a nail. The glow brightened. The letters on the page were moving now, rearranging, and he caught fragments between blinks: *the bells lie* — *Vaelmoor* — *unbinding* —

"I don't consent," he said aloud, which was such a ledger-keeper's thing to say that he almost laughed again, hysterically. "I don't — I am not a party to this transaction—"

The ink rose off the page.

It came up like smoke given weight, black ribbons winding around his wrists, his forearms, cool as river water and smelling of that same window-opened-after-tears air. It did not drag him. That was the worst part. It simply *pulled*, gently, the way sleep pulls, and his grip on the desk broke one finger at a time, and the last thing he saw before the page swallowed the room was his own name, glowing, patient, *waiting for him the way a grave waits.*

Then there was no floor.

Falling without wind. Darkness without depth. Somewhere below him — or above, or inside his own chest — a bell began to toll, one great bronze stroke that he felt in his teeth before he heard it. Another followed. Another. Bells, many bells, all of them ringing the same slow rhythm, and Dominic thought absurdly, as the dark poured past him like a river in flood: *someone is ringing those for me,* and then he thought: *no. Someone is ringing them for a king.*

He hit cobblestones with his knees and palms, rolled, and lay gasping in a lane that smelled of woodsmoke and roasting meat and, underneath both, something faintly wrong — a sweetness like flowers left too long in a closed room.

Vaelmoor. He knew it from maps. He had drawn it, in fact — the royal city, twice surveyed, once for the guild and once for a merchant consortium that had never paid its invoice. But maps are flat lies, and the city rising above him now was no lie at all: spires needled against a violet sky, banners streaming from every rooftop, gold and white, the king's colors. Lanterns everywhere. Music somewhere, pipes and drums. A festival, by the density of it.

He got upright. His knees bled through his trousers. His satchel was still on his shoulder — the small mercy of a man whose whole life fit in leather and string — and inside it, when he checked with shaking hands, the black book was gone. Of course it was. It had delivered him. Books like that didn't travel with their cargo; they *were* the road.

"Bells," he said to no one, because saying things aloud steadied him, and because the bells had not stopped. They tolled on and on across the city, that same slow funereal count, and yet the streets below him streamed with celebrants, garlands, roasted chestnuts, children with paper crowns. Grief and festival, braided together, each making the other obscene.

Then he looked closer at the faces, and stopped cataloguing, and simply stood very still.

The people smiled. All of them smiled. But the smiles sat on their faces like masks hung on hooks, and behind their eyes there was a dimness, a fogged-glass quality, as though each person were trying to recall something important and had just decided it wasn't worth the effort. A woman near him held a memorial candle — wax running down her wrist unnoticed, flame guttering — and when Dominic asked her, gently, *who is the mourning for?*, she looked at the candle with genuine surprise, as though finding it in her own hand for the first time.

"For the king," she said. "Long live the king." And she smiled her hung-on-a-hook smile and drifted on into the crowd, leaving a thread of smoke behind her.

The king. Weeks ago — Dominic was almost sure it was weeks, gossip traveled even to the guild — word had come down the trade roads that the old king was dead. Fever, they said. There had been a funeral; Maren had worn black for a month. And now bells for a king who should have been ash, and a city celebrating a reign that had ended, and no one, no one at all, finding any of it strange except the stranger bleeding on the cobbles.

Another bell stroke rolled over the rooftops, and this time Dominic heard what his ears had refused before: the tolling was wrong. Off-rhythm by a fraction, like a heart missing every fourth beat. Like something imitating a bell the way the smiles imitated joy.

*On the night the bells lie.*

Dread arrived then, whole and cold, settling into him like water into the hold of a ship. He was not a brave man. He had never claimed to be. But he was a man who reconciled accounts, and every instinct he owned now screamed that something in this city was deeply, catastrophically *out of balance* — a debt accruing somewhere unseen, interest compounding on the one currency he'd never thought to fear.

Memory. The city was spending memory. He could see it happening, if he looked — the fogged eyes, the abandoned candles, a man walking past a shrine to his own father without a flicker of recognition. And somewhere above, in the palace with its borrowed light, whatever wore the crown was eating.

Dominic pressed himself back against a wall as boots rang up the lane — armored boots, six pairs, marching in perfect unison, wardens in white-and-gold livery sweeping the crowd with lanterns that burned a color fire should not burn. He pulled his collar up and became, with the skill of a lifetime's practice, a man not worth noticing.

It almost worked.

"Down here," said a voice at his elbow, low and sharp. "Left side, don't look, *don't look*." A small hand closed around his wrist — quick fingers, a pickpocket's calluses, a girl half-hidden behind a rain barrel with eyes like struck flint. She glanced up at him once, and her expression did something extraordinary: it shifted from calculation to recognition, from recognition to disbelief.

"It's you," she breathed. "You're actually — grandmother said you'd come. Grandmother *swore* it."

Behind the rain barrel, the warden-lanterns swept closer, burning their wrong-colored light, and the lying bells tolled on.
