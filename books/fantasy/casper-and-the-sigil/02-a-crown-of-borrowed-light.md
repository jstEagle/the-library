## 2. A Crown of Borrowed Light

The bells had not stopped.

Dominic Fairweather had been a keeper of ledgers for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned one iron rule: numbers do not lie, but dates can. Tonight's date had been inked into a sealed prophetic text a century before he was born, followed by his own name in a hand that felt uncomfortably like his own. He had not yet finished the thought *this is impossible* when the page had pulled him through — a sensation like being read aloud — and set him down in the royal city of Vaelmoor with his ledger still clutched to his chest and his boots ringing on cobblestone that smelled of rain and roasting chestnuts.

The bells tolled again. Not the bright, staggered peal of celebration, but a slow, even cadence, like a heart beating in an empty room.

He climbed the nearest stair — a broad flight of granite descending from a merchant quarter toward the river terraces — and stopped halfway, because the city opened below him like a book he had drawn maps of but never seen.

Vaelmoor. He knew its shape intimately. He had inked its districts a hundred times for the Guild of Impossible Things: the crescent of the Lanternbridge, the spiral of the Old Market, the crown of the palace rising above the terraced gardens like a fist closed around a jewel. But maps were made of distances, and distances did not tell you that the city *glowed*. Every window burned with festival light. Every balcony dripped with garlands of white flowers. Lanterns hung on wires strung from spire to spire, thousands of them, so that the sky itself seemed netted with stars that had been caught and made obedient.

And everywhere, the bells.

*For a king who died weeks ago,* he thought. The ledger in his arms felt suddenly heavy. The Guild's records were clear on this point — King Aldric Vael had died in the autumn, and the crown had passed in silence, and no one at the Guild had thought much about it, because dead kings were not impossible things. Dead kings were the most ordinary things in the world.

So who was the city celebrating?

He descended into the crowd.

* * *

The festival filled the Old Market in a river of bodies and light. Dominic was a tall man with ink-stained fingers and the permanent apologetic posture of someone who spent his life apologizing to furniture, and he had never in his life been comfortable in a crowd. He pressed himself against a baker's stall and watched.

They were cheering. That was the first thing. The whole square, thousands strong, was cheering with a fervor that should have warmed him — and did not, because something about the sound was wrong. It took him a moment, the way a wrong number in a ledger takes a moment, and then he had it: the cheering did not *vary*. It was a wall of sound with no texture in it, no laughter breaking through, no whistles, no whoops, no children shrieking. Just the cheer, uniform as printed text, rising and falling together as if the whole crowd were one throat.

Then the king appeared.

He came out onto the balcony of the customs house — not the palace, Dominic noted, filing that away, *why not the palace?* — and the crowd's cheer redoubled. King Aldric Vael stood in the lantern light wearing a crown that seemed to hold its own weather. It was gold, certainly, but the gold moved, light sliding across it like water over a stone, and where that light touched the crowd below, faces lifted toward it the way faces lift toward the sun.

Dominic had drawn this man's profile once, from a portrait engraving, for the margins of a trade atlas. The face on the balcony was that face. Exactly that face. Not aged a day. Not changed by the illness the records said had eaten him alive over three years.

The king raised a hand, and the crowd fell silent as one.

"My people," the king said, and his voice carried impossibly, reaching every ear at once, gentle as a hand on the head of a sleeping child. "You are remembered. You are *kept*."

The crowd sighed. All of them. At once. A single exhalation of bliss that moved through the square like wind through wheat, and Dominic felt the hair on his arms stand up, because he had heard that sound before. He had heard it from congregations. He had heard it from mourners. He had never heard it from an entire city at once.

And then he saw what the light was doing.

It was hard to see at first — the eye resists its own evidence — but a woman stood near him, an old woman in a good shawl, her face upturned, her eyes full of the crown's borrowed glow. And as he watched, the light *took* something from her. There was no other word for it. A warmth went out of her face the way a coal goes gray. Her mouth kept smiling. Her eyes kept shining. But something behind them dimmed, the way a room dims when a lamp is carried out of it, and she swayed slightly, and the man beside her — her son, perhaps, there was a family resemblance — steadied her without looking at her, because he was dimming too.

"Grandmother," the man said, in a tone of mild puzzlement, "do I know you?"

"I expect so, dear," the woman said pleasantly. "I expect I'm someone."

Dominic's stomach turned over. He looked wildly around the square and saw it everywhere now that he knew to look: the light moving through the crowd like a slow tide, and in its wake, faces going smooth and pleasant and vacant. A man laughing at a joke he had already forgotten. A child asking her mother *what was Papa's name again?* and the mother answering brightly, *I'm sure it will come back to us.* A flower-seller staring at her own hands as though she had never seen them before.

The king on the balcony smiled his borrowed smile. *You are remembered. You are kept.* Kept, Dominic thought, the way a larder keeps things. The way a ledger keeps debts. The way a cage keeps a bird.

The bells tolled again, and he understood them now. They were not celebrating. They were counting. Each toll was a memory leaving the city, and the city had learned to make music of its own devouring.

He turned to go — to find the Guild's local chapter house, to find *anyone* — and walked directly into the chest of a sigil-warden.

* * *

The wardens wore gray, the gray of ash and old ice, and their masks were smooth porcelain with a single sigil burned into the brow — the same sigil, Dominic realized with a lurch, that had been inked in the margin of the prophecy, beneath his name. There were three of them, and they had formed a triangle around him with the unhurried precision of men who had done this a thousand times.

"Citizen," the nearest one said. The voice came from behind the mask, flat and doubled, as if two people spoke in unison. "You are not cheering."

"I — " Dominic's mind, so reliable with numbers, offered him nothing. "I was just leaving."

"You were watching the king."

"I was watching the *festival.*"

"You were watching the king," the warden repeated, and there was no anger in it, which was somehow worse. "Unwitnessed joy is hoarded joy. Hoarded joy is theft from the realm. You will come with us to be reconciled."

Reconciled. The word went through him like a nail. He took a step back and found the second warden there, porcelain face tilted with something like curiosity, and Dominic thought with absurd clarity, *I am going to die in a city I have mapped a hundred times, and no one at the Guild will know to look for me here, because I haven't written the report yet—*

A hand closed around his wrist and yanked.

The world spun. He was pulled sideways through a gap between two stalls so narrow he should not have fit, shouldering through canvas and hanging herbs and a wall of hanging sausages, and then he was running, dragged by the wrist through the labyrinth behind the market — alleys no wider than his shoulders, staircases that went up and then inexplicably down, a low arch where he had to fold nearly double — while somewhere behind them the doubled voice called *citizen, citizen, citizen* in a tone that never rose and never fell.

The hand on his wrist was small and strong and grimy. Its owner was a girl of perhaps sixteen, sharp-faced, dark-haired, with a scarf pulled up over her chin and eyes the color of wet slate. She moved through the alleys the way water moves through cracks — without hesitation, without wasted motion — and Dominic, who could navigate the Guild's archive blindfolded, had never felt so lost in his life.

She stopped at last in the shadow of a collapsed aqueduct, listened for a long ten seconds, and let him go.

"You," she said, "are the worst pickpocket bait I have ever seen. You walk like a tax collector and you stare at people like you're counting them."

"I *was* counting them," Dominic said, before he could stop himself. "The crowd. The cheering had no variance. Statistically, a crowd that size should have—"

The girl stared at him. Then she laughed — a short, startled bark, quickly smothered, as if laughter were also something that could be confiscated. "You're real," she said. "Gran said you'd be real."

"Gran?"

"My gran." The girl pulled the scarf down. Under it her jaw was set stubbornly, but her eyes had gone uncertain, the way eyes do when a story they've stopped believing knocks on the door. "She used to tell stories. Before they took her to the Keeping Houses. About the Guild of Impossible Things. About a man who kept the ledgers, who wrote down everything the world forgot so it could be found again." Her slate eyes went over him slowly — the ink stains, the ledger still clutched to his chest like a shield, the look of a man who had seen something impossible and was already, furiously, taking notes. "She said if the city ever needed saving, it wouldn't be a knight. It'd be the man with the books."

Dominic looked down at the ledger. Its cover was warm. He had not noticed that before. Under his fingers he could feel, faintly, like a pulse through paper, the ink of tonight's date still drying.

"I'm not a savior," he said. "I'm a cartographer. I keep accounts. I — " He stopped. The image rose in him unbidden: the old woman in the good shawl, smiling her emptied smile. *I expect I'm someone.* "Something is eating this city. It's eating what people are. And I think — I think a book told me I'm supposed to do something about it."

The girl was quiet for a moment. Somewhere above them the bells tolled on, patient as a tide.

"Wren," she said at last, and stuck out a hand that was missing the top joint of one finger. "And if a book told you that, then you'd better come with me. Because there are people you need to meet, and they are going to *not* believe you, and I want to watch."

"Who?"

She was already moving, ducking under the broken arch, glancing back with the first real grin he'd seen on her face.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," she said. "The real one. The one that's been hiding under this city for a hundred years, waiting for the man with the books." Her grin flickered, sharpened. "Gran also said you'd be late."

The bells tolled again — and this time, Dominic could have sworn one bell rang out of rhythm, off by a single beat, like a voice clearing its throat.

He followed her into the dark.
