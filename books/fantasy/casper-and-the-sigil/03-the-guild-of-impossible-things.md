## 3. The Guild of Impossible Things

Wren had three rules for moving through Vaelmoor unseen, and she broke all of them in the first hour of knowing Dominic Fairweather.

Rule one: never take a stranger through the Understairs. But the wardens were sweeping the Lanternmarket with nets of humming bronze, and the rooftops were watched, and the boy — man, rather, though he clutched his ledger book like a child clutching a blanket — moved like someone who had never once needed to be invisible. So she popped the grate behind the chandler's on Rope Lane, pulled him down into the dark, and told him to breathe quietly or she'd leave him for the wardens to find.

Rule two: don't talk. He talked.

"Is this a sewer?" he whispered, hunched under a ceiling of old beams furred with dust. "It smells like— actually, it smells like my grandmother's root cellar. That's not comforting. I don't know why I said that."

"It's the Understairs," Wren said. "The spaces the city forgot when it started forgetting everything else. Nobody remembers them, so nobody guards them." She glanced back at him. In the grey light leaking through grates overhead, he looked less like a prophet's instrument and more like exactly what her gran's stories had promised: a thin, ink-stained man with worried eyes who carried the world's accounts in a leather book. "You really came out of a page?"

"I really came out of a page."

"Huh." She ducked under a low lintel. "Gran used to say the ledger-keeper would come when the bells went wrong. She'd say it every night before she forgot how to say anything at all. I thought it was just one of her stories. Like the fish that swallowed the moon."

"That one's true as well," Dominic said faintly. "It's in the guild annals. 1143. Very unfortunate for the moon."

Wren stopped walking. Turned. Stared at him.

"You're telling me," she said slowly, "there are records. Of the things people forget. Written down somewhere."

"The Guild of Impossible Things keeps ledgers of—" He paused, and she watched him realize what he'd said, watch the color drain from his face by lamplight. "You don't know the guild."

"I know the name." Everyone in Vaelmoor knew the name; it lived in half-songs and grandmothers' warnings, *the Guild of Impossible Things will take you if you tell lies* — except nobody could say where the guild was, or what it did, or whether it had ever existed at all. The sigil had eaten it, same as it ate everything. "I thought it was a story to frighten children."

Dominic smiled, and it was the first unguarded thing she'd seen him do. "Most days," he said, "so do we."

* * *

They came up out of the dark through a door disguised as the back of a mirror-maker's shop, and then down again, and then sideways, until Wren lost count of the turns and gave up keeping them — which, she suspected, was the point. Finally a wall of stacked crates swung inward on oiled hinges, and warm light and the smell of solder and old paper spilled out, and there it was.

The sanctuary was a collapsed cistern, vast and round, its domed roof pierced by a hundred tiny holes that let in daylight like constellations. Shelves climbed the walls to impossible heights. A workbench sprawled across the center, buried under broken things: a music box with its song visibly leaking out of it in silver wisps, a wedding ring split clean in two, a letter folded so many times it had become a small hard stone.

And three people turned to look at her, and at the stranger she'd brought in past every rule she owned.

"Oh," said the small round woman at the workbench, setting down her tinker's irons. "Oh, you're *late.*"

"Tallow," Wren said, "this is—"

"Dominic Fairweather," said the tall grey man rising from a nest of books in the corner, in a voice worn smooth by decades of lecturing. He crossed the room like a man in a dream, took Dominic's hands, and stared at them as though checking for ink stains. "Ledger-keeper, third chair, keeper of the Annals. You've been missing for ninety-one years."

"I've been—" Dominic's voice cracked. "I'm twenty-six."

"Yes," the old man said gently. "That would be the problem, wouldn't it."

"Maren," Tallow muttered, "you can't just *say* things like that to people," but the historian wasn't listening. He was already pulling Dominic toward the books, talking in a rush about dates and discrepancies, and Wren caught fragments: *the sealed texts*, *the ninth bell*, *we calculated it wrong by a century.*

In the corner by the stove, a huge man sat very still and watched. He had a warden's build and a warden's cropped hair, but no badge, and a scar ran from his ear to his collarbone like a signature he couldn't erase. When Wren caught his eye, he nodded once — Brannoc, the ex-warden, the one even Maren spoke of carefully — and went back to sharpening a knife that already looked sharp enough to shave with.

Wren exhaled. Somewhere between the Lanternmarket and here, without deciding to, she had stopped being alone in Vaelmoor. It felt dangerous. It felt like standing in open sunlight.

She hated how much she wanted to keep it.

* * *

They confirmed it around the stove that evening, with stew that tasted mostly of rosemary and hope.

Maren laid out the prophecy — not the sealed text Dominic had fallen through, but an older copy, water-stained, its margins crowded with a dead hand's annotations. "*When the bells go wrong and the king is twice-crowned,*" he read, "*the unbinding comes, written in the ledger-keeper's hand.*"

"And the date," Tallow said quietly. She'd gone still in a way Wren hadn't seen before, her tinker's hands flat on the table. "Tonight's date. It matches?"

"To the day," Dominic said. His voice had gone careful, the way people speak when they're holding something heavy and aren't sure of the floor beneath them. "And it names me. Not 'a ledger-keeper.' Me. *Dominic Fairweather, the unbinding.*"

Silence. The stove ticked. Above them, through the hundred pinpricks in the dome, the first stars came out over a city whose bells were still tolling wrong.

"So it's real," Wren said. "The prophecy. All of Gran's stories."

"Real," Brannoc rumbled, from his corner. He hadn't touched his stew. "Realer than any of you want it to be." He set down the knife at last and leaned forward, firelight carving deep lines in his face. "I helped bind the first wardens to the sigil. I stood in the crown vault. I heard the king's own voice give the order." His jaw worked. "He was dying. The court mages swore the sigil would only hold what remained of him — preserve it, keep the realm steady till an heir could be found. Instead it learned to feed. Memory, first. Then names. Then debts owed and wrongs done and promises made." He looked around the circle. "You've all felt it. Your gran forgetting. Your neighbors' faces going smooth. That's not grief. That's *harvest.*"

"And it can be unbound?" Dominic asked. "By me? I balance ledgers. I file things. Last month my greatest triumph was reorganizing the guild's collection of impossible maps."

Brannoc almost smiled. "The binding is a contract, ledger-keeper. Nine clauses, nine seals. Contracts have terms. Terms can end." He tapped the prophecy copy. "But contracts also have *parties*. The sigil chose its instrument when it pulled you through that page. Which means it's watching you now. Which means—" 

Which meant, Wren understood, that they were all in danger simply from sitting near him. She looked around the table at these strangers — a tinkerer, a traitor-historian, a warden without a badge, a man out of time — and waited to feel afraid.

Instead she felt something worse. She felt *responsible* for them.

"Well," Tallow said briskly, standing and gathering bowls, because Tallow always filled silences with motion, "he's here, the stew's hot, and nobody's unmaking anybody before breakfast. Wren — you get the cot by the shelves. Dominic can have the hammock, if he can manage a hammock, which frankly I doubt."

"I can manage a hammock," Dominic said with dignity, and immediately knocked his elbow on the table.

Maren laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a door opening after years. And Brannoc, collecting knives, paused beside Wren on his way past and said, low, so only she heard it:

"You brought him in safe. Through the Understairs. Alone." He studied her with those warden's eyes that missed nothing. "Gran raised you right, whatever the city's forgotten."

Wren didn't answer. Her throat had gone tight in a way she refused to examine.

Later, in the dark of the cistern, she lay on the cot listening to the sounds of the guild: Tallow's soft humming, Maren's pages turning, Dominic failing quietly and repeatedly at the hammock, and finally succeeding, with a small triumphant grunt. Strangers, all of them, hours ago. Now something with weight and warmth, something being built rather than found.

She was almost asleep when Maren's voice cut through the dark, sharp with a horror that made everyone sit up at once.

"The margins." Pages rustled frantically. "The annotations — I thought they were my predecessor's, but the hand — oh, gods, the hand is *mine.*" A beat of terrible silence. "There's something I need to tell you all. About how the sigil took the throne. And about what I did."

Above them, far off in the sleeping city, a bell tolled — the ninth bell, tolling wrong.

Nobody slept after that.
