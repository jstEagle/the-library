## 4. What the Sigil Eats

Brannoc had worn the sigil-warden's grey for eleven years before he burned it, and some nights he could still feel the wool against his neck like a hand that hadn't let go. Tonight was one of those nights. He sat in the guild's undercroft with his scarred hands flat on the table, watching the candle gutter, and waited for the cartographer to ask the question everyone else had been too frightened to say aloud.

Dominic Fairweather sat across from him, still smelling faintly of ink and of Vaelmoor's festival smoke. Wren perched on a barrel by the door, pretending not to listen, which in Wren's language meant listening very hard indeed. Maren stood at the far wall with her arms folded, her historian's face arranged into something neutral that fooled no one.

"So," Brannoc said. "You want to know what it eats."

"I want to know why nobody's stopped it," Dominic said. "The bells have been tolling for a dead king for weeks. Half the city cheered today like he'd never been ill at all. Someone must have noticed."

"Someone noticed." Brannoc turned his left hand over. The brand sat in the meat of his palm — the warden's mark, a closed eye inside a ring, faded now to a pale ghost of itself but never gone. "I noticed. That's why there's no more *someone*. Notice something in Vaelmoor these days and the noticing doesn't stay with you. You wake the next morning and it's gone, like breath off glass."

He reached beneath the table and brought up the thing he'd carried out of the wardens' archive the night he deserted: a slate, no bigger than a prayer book, its surface scored with hundreds of fine scratches. Tally marks. He set it down between them.

"When I was sworn, they taught us the sigil was mercy," he said. "The old king — Aldric Vael, the true one — was dying. Not quickly. Cruelly. A wasting sickness that took his mind first, then his body, a year at a time. His physicians came to the court with a proposal. An old working, pre-imperial, from before the city had laws. A sigil bound into the crown that would hold the king as he was. Preserve him. Keep the realm from tearing itself apart over succession while a proper heir was found."

"And instead?" Wren said from her barrel.

"Instead it kept him the way you keep meat in salt." Brannoc's voice stayed level, because level was all he had left. "The king died anyway, weeks ago. But the sigil didn't. It had been given one task — *preserve* — and no instruction for when preserving became impossible. So it did what hungry workings do. It found another way to keep him. It keeps the *idea* of him. And an idea needs feeding."

He tapped the slate. "Memory. That's the feed. The sigil drinks memory out of the city around it, and pours what it takes into the shape wearing the crown. Every day, Vaelmoor remembers less. First it took the small things — where people left their keys, the names of neighbors' children. Then it started on the weightier stuff." He looked at Dominic. "Tell me what you saw at the festival. The eyes."

"Hollow," Dominic said quietly. "They were cheering and their eyes were — empty. Like men applauding in a dream."

"That's mid-feed. The sigil takes deepest during ceremony, because ceremony is when a crowd's memories braid together. Ten thousand people remembering the same moment is a feast." He drew a breath. "Here's what it has already eaten, and I want each of you to understand this, because this is the part that will keep you awake."

He counted on his branded hand.

"It has eaten the city's dead. Ask anyone in Vaelmoor to name their grandmother who passed last winter. They'll smile at you kindly and change the subject, because the grief isn't there anymore. The graves are tended by no one who knows whose they are. Mourning clothes hang in closets and no one recalls buying them. The dead aren't just forgotten — they're *unremembered having existed*."

Maren's arms tightened across her chest.

"It has eaten debts. The moneylenders' books balance perfectly now because every debt owed to the crown has simply evaporated from every mind that held it. Land grants, pensions, wages owed — gone. The poor don't notice they've been robbed because the memory of being owed anything went with it."

"And wrongs?" Dominic asked. His voice had gone careful.

"Wrongs last." Brannoc let that sit in the candlelight. "Crimes dissolve. There are men walking free in Vaelmoor who committed atrocities under the old reign, because the witnesses can't recall witnessing. There are victims who feel a shadow of hurt and cannot attach it to any face or name. Justice is not being broken in Vaelmoor, cartographer. Justice is being *digested*."

The candle spat. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone and earth, the evening bells tolled again — that slow, wrong rhythm the city had learned to think of as normal. Nine strokes tonight. Brannoc watched Wren count them without seeming to.

"You said nine days," she said.

"I did." He turned the slate toward them so the tally marks caught the light. "This is my count of the feeds since the king died. One a night, at bell. Each feed is bigger than the last — the sigil is starving faster as it burns through what's easy. But it can't eat everything. There's a floor to how much memory a city holds, and beneath that floor is bedrock: the recorded memories. Written records. Ledgers, chronicles, registers, deeds. Ink is harder to drink than living minds, so the sigil saved it for last. It's been burning through Vaelmoor's archives for days now — the great library, the temple rolls, the Hall of Records. Maren can tell you what's left."

"Almost nothing." Maren's voice was dry as old vellum, but her knuckles were white. "I checked the Hall of Records yesterday. Shelves that held forty thousand volumes. I found ash and three hundred books, and I would swear to you on every oath I ever broke that three weeks ago there were forty thousand. The sigil is saving the crown vault for the end — the royal archives, the deep registry, the true chronicles of the realm. Everything that makes Vaelmoor *Vaelmoor*, and the realm a realm. When those burn, the sigil won't need to pretend anymore. There'll be nothing to compare the pretender against. No past at all. Just a forever-now with a king who never dies."

"And when does it reach the vault?" Dominic asked.

Brannoc met his eyes. "Nine nights. Nine feeds. At the final bell, the last recorded memories in this realm go into the fire, and after that—" he spread his hands— "there is no unbinding. A working like that needs an anchor, and its anchor is everything it hasn't eaten yet. Cut it loose now, while the anchor still holds, and it can be shattered. Wait past the ninth bell, and you'd be trying to break a chain with no wall to bolt it to. It would drift. It would keep feeding. Slowly, quietly, forever."

Silence. Wren slid off her barrel and came to the table, and for once she didn't look like she was calculating an exit. She looked down at the slate, at his tallies, and her jaw was set the way it got when someone mentioned her grandmother.

"The stories my gran told me," she said. "About the guild. About things worth doing that can't be done." She looked up. "Nine days to walk into the crown vault of a fortress full of wardens, past a king made of stolen memories, and break a binding older than the kingdom. That qualifies."

"It does," Brannoc said.

"How do we even get in? The vault's under the palace. Wardens on every door, and I'd wager the sigil knows we're coming. It ate your memories of the wards, didn't it? It knows what a defector looks like."

"It knows what a warden looks like." Brannoc touched the faded brand in his palm. "It doesn't know what I know anymore — I made sure of that the night I left. Took a knife to my own recollection of the vault's defenses rather than let it drink them out of me. Which means I'm the only man alive who half-remembers the way in, and only half is dangerous. But there's another way. Or there was."

He looked at Maren, and the historian went very still.

"Maren Ondrey," he said. "Royal Historian, first class, keeper of the sealed chronicles. Disgraced, stripped of office, erased from the rolls — but once upon a time, you had vault access that wardens would kill for. And you know the old ways through the palace better than the architects did, because you spent twenty years reading what the architects wrote."

"There is a way," Maren said slowly. "Through the Hall of Forgotten Names. The memorial gallery beneath the west wing, where the realm records its honored dead. If the sigil's been eating the city's dead first—" her breath caught, and Brannoc saw the thought land on her like a weight— "then the hall may be *empty*. Unguarded, because there's nothing left in it worth guarding. Nothing left to mourn."

"Then we go through the room where the realm forgot everyone it loved," Wren said, "and we do it before the ninth bell."

Brannoc nodded once. Then he picked up the slate, and with the edge of his knife scratched a fresh mark into it — not a tally this time, but a line, straight and deliberate, running through the whole column of feeds. Under it, he carved four words:

*Nine nights. Then never.*

"We grieve later," he said, and his voice cracked on it, just slightly, because he was thinking of his own mother's grave out in the Fenmark hills and whether he would remember her face long enough to reach it. "Tonight we plan."

But the candle had barely burned another finger's width when the trapdoor at the top of the stairs creaked — soft, careful, the sound of someone trying not to be heard — and Tallow's round, worried face appeared in the opening, pale in the lamplight from above.

"Begging all your pardons," the promise-tinkerer said, and his eyes went from face to face like a man counting exits, "but there's a warden patrol on Candlewick Row asking after strangers. Grey cloaks. Six of them. And Brannoc—" his voice dropped— "they're carrying your description. Word for word."

* * *

Brannoc stood, and the brand in his palm seemed to warm, the closed eye of the warden's mark almost itching, as if somewhere far above, the thing wearing the king's crown had just opened its own.
