## 5. The Historian's Confession

Maren Voss had spent forty years learning how lies were built, and she recognized the architecture of this one from the inside.

She stood at the guild's long table, the sealed prophecy open between them, and watched the others look at Dominic Fairweather as though he were a lit fuse. The tinkerer Tallow had gone quiet in the way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. Brannoc leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed and his old warden's sigil still inked faintly on his knuckles, faded but not forgotten — ink remembers, even when people don't. Wren perched on the windowsill with her boots swinging, a knife turning over her fingers in idle circles, watching Maren with the particular patience of someone who has already decided everyone in the room will eventually disappoint her.

And Dominic — the cartographer, the ledger-keeper, the unmaker, if you believed a book that was older than all of them put together — sat with his hands flat on the table as if steadying himself against weather only he could feel.

"You've read it," Maren said. "The whole thing."

"Twice," Dominic said. "It's not kind to any of us."

"No," Maren said. "Prophecies never are. They're written by people who need the future to have a shape, because the present is unbearable." She drew a breath. It went down like cold water. "I know who wrote this one. I helped her."

The room changed temperature. Even the candle flames seemed to lean in.

* * *

Maren had been the Royal Historian of Vaelmoor for eleven years before she became the disgraced Royal Historian of Vaelmoor, which is a distinction she liked to pretend mattered more than it did. She had been good at her work. That was the whole of the problem. She had been very, very good at deciding what deserved to be remembered.

"The king was dying," she said. "Not suddenly. Not poison, not treachery — just the slow failure of a body that had ruled for thirty years. And Vaelmoor was held together by him the way a wheel is held together by its hub. Three duchies ready to war over his succession. A grain debt to the Salt Coast that would have starved half the lower city when it came due. Two hundred years of treaties that existed only because everyone believed the king's word was iron."

"And so?" Brannoc said quietly. He knew. She could see it in his face. He had been a warden; wardens always knew more than historians admitted.

"And so the Queen's chamberlain came to me," Maren said, "with a woman I had never seen before or since. She carried a box, and in the box was a sigil — a crown-and-root, drawn in a gold that didn't tarnish under lamplight. She told me what it was meant to do. Preserve him. Hold the king's mind and memory intact past the death of his flesh, so the realm would have time. Time to name an heir, settle the debts, keep the treaties warm. A bridge, she called it. We were building a bridge over the worst year of Vaelmoor's history."

Wren's knife stopped turning. "And instead?"

"Instead we built a mouth." Maren heard her own voice go flat, the flatness she'd once used to dictate official records, and hated it. "I forged the succession papers. My hand wrote the decree that named the sigil regent-in-perpetuity until the king recovered. I certified the physicians' accounts. I am the reason every document in the royal archive says that His Majesty still reigns, hale and sound, in the high tower." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I did it beautifully. That's what I want you all to understand. There isn't a clerk in this city who could find the seam."

"There's no seam to find," Brannoc said. "Because the city forgot there was ever a king to lose. The sigil doesn't just wear his crown, historian. It eats the remembering of him. Every person who recalls the truth becomes a meal."

"Yes." Maren pressed her palms against the table. Under her hands, the prophecy's page felt warm, like something sleeping. "I understood that part too late. The night they crowned the thing in the king's body, I went home and tried to write the truth in my private codex. The words came out wrong. Not censored — *rewritten*. My own hand, my own ink, and the sentence about the king's death turned itself into a sentence about the festival preparations. I burned three pages before I understood: the sigil had read my records. Everything I'd catalogued, everything I'd indexed and cross-referenced and made findable — I hadn't preserved the realm's memory for the sigil. I'd prepared its pantry."

Dominic spoke for the first time in minutes. "Then why can we talk about it here? Why hasn't it eaten this conversation?"

Maren looked around the guildhall — the crooked shelves, the promise-tinkerer's bench strewn with broken oaths soldered back together, the smell of oil and dust and stubbornness. "Because this place is off the ledger. The Guild of Impossible Things keeps no records the archive can reach. Your sanctuary exists precisely because it was never written down." Her mouth twisted. "For forty years I thought that was a failing. An institution without a chronicle. Now I understand it's the only kind of fortress left."

"That," Wren said, "and nobody important knows we're here."

"Nobody *important*," Maren agreed, and then, because shame is a debt that compounds nightly whether or not anyone collects, she gave them what she had come to give. "But if we're going to reach the crown vault, you'll need more than my guilt. You'll need the entrance. And the entrance isn't where the archive says it is."

* * *

She unrolled a map across the table — her own work, decades old, drawn in the careful hand of a younger woman who had believed that accuracy was a form of loyalty. The royal quarter spread across it in precise ink: the palace, the vaults beneath, the ceremonial stair that every guard rotation in the city was built to watch.

"Every record places the crown vault behind the Ceremonial Stair," she said. "That's true, and that's the trap. The stair leads to a vault-shaped room with a vault-shaped door, and behind the door are forty feet of solid granite and whatever warden happens to be standing in the dark. I know because I helped certify those plans too. The real way down was never drawn. It was *forgotten on purpose*."

Brannoc straightened off the doorframe. "The Hall of Forgotten Names."

Maren nodded, and something in her chest cracked along an old seam. "Under the palace there's a chapel older than the dynasty. Before Vaelmoor kept ledgers, it kept walls — every name that mattered was carved into the stone, and when a name fell out of living memory, the masons chiseled it away and set the fragment in the floor. A graveyard for names. The kings used it to swear their coronation oaths, standing on the names of everyone who came before. It was supposed to be humility." She traced one line on her map with a fingernail, a corridor that appeared on no official plan because officially it did not exist. "The Hall connects to the crown vault through the Oath Gallery. The sigil can't watch it well. Do you understand why? The sigil feeds on memory — but that floor is made entirely of memories already surrendered. There's nothing there for it to eat. Walking that hall is like walking through a stomach that's already full. It barely notices."

"It barely notices," Tallow repeated, from his bench, in a voice with a hairline crack running through it. He had been polishing the same brass fitting for an hour without making it brighter. "You're certain."

"As certain as I am of anything I helped bury." Maren looked at him. He wouldn't look back. She filed that away, the way she filed everything, and hated herself a little for the habit.

"How do we get into the Hall?" Wren asked. "Palace chapels tend to have doors."

"One door, and it opens from inside the vault complex. Sealed since the last coronation." Maren met each of their eyes in turn. "But the masons' guild kept a key. One key, passed master to master, never recorded — because a recorded key is a copyable key. The last master mason died nine years ago. Before he did, he gave it to someone he trusted to remember things other people couldn't afford to."

The silence stretched. Brannoc raised his hand slowly, flexed the fingers with their faded warden's ink, and produced from around his neck, on a leather cord worn shiny with age, a small iron key shaped like a chisel's edge.

"He said I'd know when to use it," Brannoc said. "He said the city would forget something too big, someday, and whoever still remembered would have to go down and stand on the old names." He looked at Maren. "I thought he meant mourning. I didn't think he meant war."

"They're often the same thing," Maren said. "In my experience."

* * *

It was Wren who heard them first — she was always the first, the girl who had made a profession of listening to footsteps — and she came off the windowsill without a sound and put out two fingers toward the street door.

"Wardens," she breathed. "Three patrols. They're not sweeping, they're *searching*. Door to door. And they're coming up Candlemaker's Row, which means—"

"Which means they're coming here," Brannoc finished.

Maren's blood went cold in the old professional way, the way it did when a document contradicted itself. "They can't know. This place isn't in any record I ever touched—"

"No," Dominic said softly. He was looking at the prophecy, at the margins, where someone had annotated the text in a cramped brown hand. Maren followed his gaze and felt the floor tilt.

She knew that handwriting. She had taught herself to recognize it thirty years ago, when the chamberlain's letters started arriving with instructions she pretended not to understand.

The annotations weren't old. The ink was fresh. Whoever had written them had done so recently, carefully, and from memory — because memory was the one thing the sigil could not stop a guilty heart from carrying.

"Tallow," Dominic said, without accusation, without anything at all, "who else has seen this book?"

Tallow set down his brass fitting very gently, the way a man sets down something breakable when his hands are shaking. Outside, the first patrol's boots reached the corner of Candlemaker's Row, and began to count doors.

"I need to show you something first," Tallow said. "Before they arrive. Please. It's about my daughter."
