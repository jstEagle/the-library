## 6. The Traitor in the Margins

The betrayal, when it came, did not announce itself with trumpets or blood. It came the way rot comes to a beam — quietly, from inside, where no one thinks to look.

Dominic found it in the margins.

He had spent the morning doing what he always did when the world grew too large: keeping accounts. The guild's sanctuary beneath the old candlewright's warehouse was a warren of salvaged furniture and stubborn hope, and after three days of planning the assault on the Hall of Forgotten Names, everyone had scattered to their tasks — Brannoc sharpening warden-forged keys with a whetstone's patient hiss, Maren cross-referencing her forged records by candlelight, Wren out in the city listening at the seams of things. Dominic sat at the long table with the guild's ledgers spread before him, because someone had to know what they stood to lose, and that someone was always him.

That was when he noticed the ink.

The guild kept a route-map of their planned approach to the vault — a working copy, inked fresh each night and burned each dawn, so that no warden patrol could ever hold the whole of it in one piece. Dominic had drawn the dawn copy himself. He knew every line of it the way he knew his own handwriting. And there, in the lower margin, where he had sketched the drainage grate beneath the Hall of Forgotten Names, was a small notation in a hand that was not his.

*Grate rusted through. East passage clear. Wardens change watch at the ninth bell.*

Accurate. Terribly accurate. And written in the fine, careful script of a man who mended promises for a living — a hand trained to write small and true, so that a repaired vow would hold its shape.

Tallow.

Dominic sat very still. The candle guttered. Somewhere above, cart wheels rattled over the cobbles of a city that was forgetting itself one morning at a time, and here beneath it all, in a margin no bigger than a thumb, sat the end of everything they were trying to do.

He thought, absurdly, of arithmetic. Of how a ledger balances. A man gives something; a man receives something of equal weight. Tallow had given the route. What had Tallow received?

Dominic knew. He had known since the second night, when he'd woken past midnight and heard Tallow in the next room, speaking softly to someone who wasn't there — or worse, to someone who was there only when the sigil wanted him to believe she was. *Liss. Liss, I'm coming. I found a way.* The name of a daughter. A daughter none of them had ever seen, though Tallow's workbench held a child's carved wooden bird, worn smooth by years of a father's absent-minded thumb.

The sigil did not only eat the past. Dominic was learning that. It also *traded* in it. It kept certain memories — certain people — in its keeping, the way a moneylender keeps collateral. And it offered them back, piece by piece, at a price.

He closed the ledger gently, as though the betrayal were something sleeping, and went to find Tallow.

* * *

He found him at his workbench, as always, hunched over a broken promise like a surgeon over a wound. This one was a marriage vow, cracked down the middle, its original words gone gray with neglect. Tallow held it to the light, turning it, his lips moving soundlessly as he searched for the shape of what had been said.

"Tallow."

The tinkerer did not startle. That was the first wrongness. Tallow startled at everything — at doors, at voices, at his own shadow. But now he set down the vow with terrible calm and said, without turning, "You found it, then. The margin."

"Yes."

"I wondered if you would. You're the ledger-keeper. It's what you're *for*." He said it without bitterness. That was the second wrongness.

Dominic came around the bench and looked at him. Tallow looked, if anything, *lighter* than he had in days. The constant flinch had gone out of him. Guilt, Dominic thought, when it finally resolves into action, looks almost like peace.

"Tell me what they have," Dominic said.

Tallow's hands, which had been steady, began to tremble. "You know."

"I know the shape of it. I want the whole of the account."

So Tallow told him. His voice stayed level in the way of a man reciting something he had recited to himself ten thousand times. A daughter, Liss, born in the first year of the king's illness, when the sigil was young and hungry and still learning what it could take. Tallow had been a court artisan then, before his disgrace — before the guild — and the sigil's wardens had come to his door with an offer dressed as a kindness. The child was sick. The sigil could keep her. Not heal her — *keep* her, suspended, unchanging, in the amber of its own light, until such time as her father rendered service worthy of her return.

"They told me she doesn't suffer," Tallow said. "They told me she doesn't age. I've done the arithmetic, Fairweather. She's been in there eleven years, and she is still six years old, and every year I do not buy her back is a year she stays six forever, and I—" His voice cracked at last, one clean fracture. "I did the arithmetic. I'm not the only one in this guild who keeps accounts. I know what I sold. I sold the route. I sold Brannoc's plan. I sold *Wren*, probably, though I didn't mean to — they ask questions and I answer them, that's how it works, it works like bleeding."

"The ninth bell," Dominic said quietly. "The east passage. They'll be waiting there."

"They were always going to be waiting. The Hall has one way in that isn't a death sentence, and they've known it longer than we have." Tallow finally looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and dry, the eyes of a man who had finished crying sometime in the night. "I'm not asking your forgiveness. I'm telling you so you can change the plan. That's the only coin I have left, and I'm spending it here, with you, because you're the one who found the margin and you deserve to hear it from the hand that wrote it."

Dominic stood there a long moment. The candle burned. Above them, the city went on forgetting.

Then he went back to the long table, and he picked up the ledger, and he carried it to the workbench.

* * *

"What is this?" Tallow said, as Dominic laid the great book open before him.

"The guild's ledger. Every debt, every promise, every name that has ever passed through this sanctuary." Dominic turned the pages with the care of a man handling something holy, which, to him, he was. "You think I keep it out of habit. I don't. I keep it because in a city that forgets, someone has to remember *on purpose*. That's all a ledger is, Tallow. Memory with a spine."

He reached the page he wanted. He had known it was there — he had written it himself, years ago, in his first month with the guild, back when the entries were small and the world was smaller.

He turned the book toward Tallow.

There, in the careful columns of the guild's founding years, in Dominic's younger, rounder hand:

*Tallow, promise-tinkerer. Owes: nothing. Brings: a daughter, Liss, six years of age, whose name he asks us to hold until he can hold her himself. Held in trust. Not forgotten.*

Tallow made a sound that was not a word. His hand came out and hovered over the entry without touching it, the way a man hovers over a flame he is afraid will vanish.

"You wrote her down," he whispered.

"You asked. The night you came to the guild, half-frozen, with a carved bird in your pocket and no explanation. You asked us to remember her name, and I wrote it down, because that is what I do." Dominic kept his voice steady, though it cost him. "The sigil has her *body*, Tallow. It has kept her six years old for eleven years, and it will keep her six years old forever, because that is the only mercy a thing like that can offer — a mercy that is really a cage. But her name is here. In ink. In a book that the sigil cannot reach, because the guild built it that way, because we are, all of us, in the business of impossible things, and the most impossible thing we do every single day is refuse to forget."

"I sold you," Tallow said. His voice had come apart entirely now. "I sold all of you. Brannoc will — he was a warden, he knows what traitors get. And Wren trusted me, she *mended my tools*—"

"Brannoc will want to break your nose, and Wren will let him, and then they will both get over it, because that is what this guild *does*." Dominic closed the ledger, gently, and rested his hand on its cover. "I'm not offering you forgiveness, Tallow. I don't have the right to hand that out like bread, and you don't have the right to expect it. What I'm offering is this: the account is not closed. You sold the route — fine. Then help me sell them a *false* one. You're the only one of us the wardens trust. That makes you the only one of us who can lie to them convincingly. Give them the east passage. Give them the ninth bell. And we'll come through somewhere else entirely, with their watchmen staring at an empty grate."

Tallow stared at him. "Why? Why would you — after what I—"

"Because your daughter's name is in my book," Dominic said, "and I intend for it to come *out* of my book one day. Not struck through. Not marked paid in blood. Written into a new page — *Liss, returned to her father, debt discharged, all accounts balanced.* That's the only ending I'll accept. And I can't write it alone."

For a long moment Tallow did not move. Then he reached into his workbench drawer and took out the carved wooden bird, worn smooth as riverstone, and set it on top of the ledger, where it sat like a small, patient promise.

"The false route," he said, his voice raw but level. "I'll need ink. And paper. And you'll need to not watch me do it, because I have spent eleven years learning to lie to wardens and I can't do it with a witness." He paused. "Fairweather. The man they sent to collect me — the warden I've been talking to. He said something, at the end. He said the sigil knows about you. About the prophecy. It's not hunting the guild anymore, Dominic." His eyes came up, and they were frightened, and they were honest. "It's hunting *you*."

Outside, far above, the bells of Vaelmoor began to toll — not the ninth bell, not yet, but the first of the festival bells, the ones that would ring for nine days without stopping until the last recorded memories of the realm burned away in the crown vault.

Nine days. And now, perhaps, fewer than that.

Dominic picked up the carved bird and pressed it back into Tallow's hands. "Then we'd better give them a route worth hunting," he said, and went to wake the others.
