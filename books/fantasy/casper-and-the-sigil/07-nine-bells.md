## 7. Nine Bells

Wren had spent her whole life learning the shape of not being seen. She knew it like other children knew their mothers' faces — the slump of the shoulders, the softness of the step, the way you learned to move along walls and let eyes slide off you like rain off slate. Being unseen was a skill. Being unseen was *safety*.

So when Brannoc pressed a lantern into her hands and said, "You'll walk point," she laughed before she could stop herself.

"Me? I'm the one they don't notice."

"That's exactly why," he said. His grey eyes held no flattery, only arithmetic. "The wardens are watching for a cartographer with prophecy ink on his fingers, a disgraced historian, and an ex-warden whose face is still in their training manuals. Nobody is watching for a pickpocket."

"Nobody watches for me at all," Wren said, and heard how it came out — not proud, not bitter, just true, the way you'd report the weather.

Tallow, hunched over his workbench with a broken promise humming under his tools, looked up. For once he didn't make a joke. "That's not nothing, girl. Invisibility's a kind of armor. Just don't mistake it for a life."

She took the lantern.

* * *

They went down through the city on the night of the Festival of Unending Reign, and Vaelmoor had never looked so beautiful or so wrong.

Lanterns hung from every balcony, ten thousand flames in glass and paper, and the streets ran gold with light. Musicians played on corners. Children spun under the lamps with sparklers clutched in fists. And everywhere — everywhere — the cheering had that hollow ring Wren had learned to hear, the sound of joy with no one home inside it. She'd watched it happen over weeks: the baker who forgot which grave he wept at every year; the old men in the taverns who could no longer remember why they hated the tax collectors, only that they'd always been fond of them; her own grandmother, in her last lucid hour, gripping Wren's wrist and whispering, *Don't let them take my name, girl. It's all I ever owned.*

The bells began at moonrise. Nine of them, from nine towers, because there were nine days left and the sigil wanted the city to celebrate its own forgetting like a wedding.

"The Hall of Forgotten Names sits behind the Archive of Records," Maren murmured as they moved through the festival crowd, hooded, close. "There's a service door beneath the west colonnade. My key still works — if they haven't changed the locks since my disgrace."

"And if they have?" Dominic asked.

"Then we improvise," said Wren, "which is my favorite part."

Brannoc brought up the rear, and Wren kept catching him watching the wardens — pairs of them on every corner now, their silver-grey cloaks marked with the sigil's coiled line, their faces smooth as eggs. Once, those cloaks had meant law. She remembered being eight years old and hiding from a warden patrol after lifting bread, and how the fear had felt clean somehow, honest. Now the wardens' eyes were lamp-lit and empty, and the fear they inspired was the fear of deep water.

They reached the colonnade without trouble. Too little trouble. Wren noticed it the way she noticed everything — a beat too much silence between the musicians, a warden pair standing at parade rest rather than patrolling, positioned precisely where they'd see anyone approaching the service door. Not hunting. *Waiting.*

"We're expected," she breathed.

Maren's key turned in the lock anyway, because two days remained and there was no other way through, and the door swung open onto darkness — and then torches bloomed all around the colonnade, a dozen of them at once, and the wardens came off the corners like puppets whose strings had finally been pulled.

"Run!" Brannoc roared, and the night tore open into chaos.

Wren did what she was made to do: she vanished. Into the crowd, under a festival cart, along the wall where shadow pooled thick. She saw Maren dragged back by two wardens, saw Brannoc put three of them down with brutal economy before a fourth caught his arm, saw Dominic — pale, furious, ink-stained Dominic — fighting toward the door they'd already lost.

And then a hand closed on her ankle.

She kicked free, rolled, came up with her knife out — but the warden didn't strike. He only looked at her, and his empty eyes focused for the first time, sharpening like frost forming.

"There you are," he said softly. "The unseen girl. The king has been asking for you."

* * *

They didn't blindfold her. That was the first cruelty dressed as courtesy. They wanted her to see everything.

The palace rose above the burning festival like a cliff face carved by vanity, and they walked her up stairs that smelled of beeswax and something else underneath — something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a locked room. The sigil was stronger here. Wren could feel it pressing on her thoughts, a thumb against wet clay, trying to smooth her out.

They brought her to a hall lit by a thousand candles, and on a throne of black oak sat the thing wearing King Aldric's crown.

It looked almost right. That was the horror of it. The dead king's face, rendered lovingly, a portrait given breath — but the eyes were wrong, depthless, and when it smiled the smile arrived a half-second after the mouth began to move.

"Wren," it said. Her name in the dead king's voice, warm as a hearth. "Little shadow. Granddaughter of Sera of Tanner's Lane."

Her whole body went cold. "You don't know my grandmother."

"I remember her better than you do." The thing rose, and the crown caught candlelight like a net catching fish. "I remember her laugh. I remember the winter she sang to you when the fever came. I remember the name she begged a stranger not to forget." It tilted its head. "I can give her back to you. Whole. Every memory, every year, every word. You could sit with her again tomorrow morning and she would know your face."

"You're lying," Wren said. Her voice shook. She hated that it shook.

"I never lie. Lying requires a past worth protecting." The king-thing descended the dais steps slowly, robes whispering. "Let me tell you what I see, little shadow. I see a girl the city never learned to look at. I see a child who learned invisibility before she learned letters. I see someone who has stolen bread, coins, keys, and affection, and been thanked for none of it. I see a girl who stood tonight in a guildhall of strangers and called it family because she had nothing else to call it."

"Stop."

"They will use you and forget you," the thing went on, gentle as a lullaby. "That is what people do. But I do not forget. I am made of remembering. Walk away from this rebellion, Wren, and I will make you *seen*. A house with your name on the deed. A history no one can dispute. Your grandmother alive in your kitchen, scolding you for tracking mud. Every eye in Vaelmoor turning to you — not because you stole something, but because you *belong* somewhere. Isn't that the one theft you've never managed?"

Wren's knife hand trembled.

Because here was the poison in it: it wasn't offering her gold. Gold she could steal. It wasn't even offering her revenge. It was offering her the thing she'd wanted since she was small enough to fit inside a flour barrel — to matter to somebody, permanently, on purpose. To be written down. To be kept.

She thought of the guildhall. Of Tallow's terrible jokes and Maren's shaking hands and Brannoc calling her *point*, like she was essential, like she was trusted. She had known them six days. Six days. Against a whole lifetime of being overlooked.

But that was exactly it, she realized, and the thought came cold and clear as well-water: six days was real. The vision was a forgery. And Wren knew forgeries better than anyone in Vaelmoor — she'd spent her life spotting the gilt on the fake, the weight that wasn't quite right in a counterfeit coin. Love that was manufactured was just another con, and the mark was her.

"You can't give her back," Wren said quietly. "If you could, you'd have done it already. You keep her name because names are what you eat." She lifted her chin. "My grandmother told me not to let them take her name. She didn't say anything about trading mine."

The smile on the king-thing's face stopped pretending.

"Unfortunate," it said. "But useful. Do you know why I asked for you specifically, little shadow? Because your grandmother's stories mentioned more than you. They mentioned the cartographer. And stories, unlike people, cannot refuse to talk."

It raised one hand, and the candles guttered, and Wren understood with sickening clarity that she had not been captured despite being invisible.

She had been captured because she was the only one of them the sigil had never been able to read — until now.

"Guards," the king-thing said pleasantly. "Find Dominic Fairweather. Tell him I have something of his."
