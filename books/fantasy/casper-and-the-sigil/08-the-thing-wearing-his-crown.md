## 8. The Thing Wearing His Crown

The trade was simple, in the end. That was the obscenity of it.

They brought Wren out through the warden cordon with her wrists bound in pale cord that glowed faintly, like the veins of a leaf held up to light. She fought them the whole way — not uselessly, not like a child dragged to bed, but with the specific economy of someone who had spent her whole life being taken and had never once made it expensive. Two wardens limped. One carried his helm under his arm instead of on his head. She had cost them that much, at least.

Then she saw Dominic step past the guild's barricade of overturned carts and burning lantern-oil, hands raised, and she went still in a way that frightened him more than any screaming would have.

"No," she said. "No, you absolute ledger-keeping *fool*—"

"Brannoc," Dominic called over her, over the wardens, over the bells tolling their festival peal above the square. "Take her back. Tell Maren she has until dawn tomorrow. Nine days ends at midnight; I intend to be inconvenient about it."

"You're not—" Wren's voice cracked. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to *buy* me, I'm not a — I'm not a debt you settle—"

"I know," he said gently. "You're the opposite of a debt. You're an asset." He smiled, because it was the only joke they had between them, the one from her first night at the guild when she'd asked what he was good for and Brannoc had grunted: *"He counts things. He's an asset."* "And assets get protected."

The warden captain looked between them with the blank patience of a man who had long ago stopped understanding what people were for, and jerked his chin. The cords around Wren's wrists loosened. She stumbled forward one step — toward him, always toward him, that was the thing about Wren, she moved toward trouble like water finding its level — and then Brannoc's huge hand closed on her shoulder and held.

Her eyes over his shoulder as they dragged her back were the worst part. Not fury. Not even grief.

*Promise me*, they said. And Dominic could not promise. He could only turn and face the palace stairs.

* * *

They did not take him to a dungeon. They took him up.

Through corridors where the festival spilled gold and noise, past servants whose smiles arrived half a beat too late, up staircases lined with portraits whose faces had gone soft and vague, like wax left in sun. He passed a portrait of the old king — Aldous IV, stern, gray-bearded, the face from every coin Dominic had ever balanced against a merchant's palm — and stopped despite himself.

The portrait's eyes were closed. Painted shut. Someone had rendered, in loving detail, a sleeping man, though the plaque beneath still read ALDOUS IV, BY THE GRACE OF THE UNENDING REIGN. As if the painter had started truthfully and been corrected mid-stroke by something patient and hungry.

"He rests," said a voice beside him. A steward, gray-liveried, pleasant. "He reigns even so."

"Of course," said Dominic, who had read enough ledgers to know a cooked book when he stood inside one.

The throne room doors opened onto light.

* * *

It should have been dark. Every instinct of story and sense said a thing like this belonged in shadow, in cold, in the smell of rot beneath the gilt. Instead the hall blazed with festival candles, ten thousand of them, and the king sat on his throne in the full flood of their light, and he was beautiful.

That was the first horror. The dead king had been sixty-one and gouty and beloved in the way of kings who are mostly absent. The thing wearing his crown had kept the outline but improved everything else: the shoulders squared, the skin smooth as poured candlewax, the eyes a warm brown that caught the candlelight and gave it back doubled. It wore Aldous IV's face the way a tailor wears a fine coat.

"Dominic Fairweather," it said, and its voice was kind. Kindness, Dominic thought, is the most terrible sound in the world when you cannot verify its source. "Cartographer. Ledger-keeper. Keeper of small true things." It rose, descended the dais steps, and — worse than anything — offered its hand.

Dominic took it. The grip was warm. Warmth seemed obscene.

"You've come a long way from your little guild," the king-thing said. "From your borrowed rooms and your borrowed family and your ledgers no one will ever read. I've watched you. Do you know what I see, when I look at Dominic Fairweather?"

"A man who's about to ruin your accounting," Dominic said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

The king-thing laughed, delighted, human. "A man who has never once been *kept*," it said. "Come. Before we discuss terms, there's something you should see."

It gestured, and the candles went out — all ten thousand at once, without smoke — and the vision began.

* * *

He stood in Vaelmoor, but a Vaelmoor remade. Sunlight, real or convincingly counterfeit, lay warm on the cobbles. People filled the streets, and every single one of them turned as he passed, and every single face knew him.

*"Dominic!"* A woman selling pears waved him over and pressed one into his hand, no coin asked, because everyone knew how he liked pears, didn't they? Didn't everyone know? *"There he is. There's our Dominic."*

Children ran alongside him calling his name like a game. A scholar at the archive door bowed slightly and said, "Master Fairweather, we've shelved your maps exactly as you specified." His mother — dead eleven years, forgotten by everyone but him — sat outside a bakery with flour on her hands, and she looked up and said, "There you are, love. Sit. Tell me everything," and her voice was exactly right, exactly remembered, and Dominic's legs nearly went out from under him.

The Guild of Impossible Things stood in the square, whole and bright. Brannoc laughing — actually laughing, head back, unashamed. Maren honored, her name cleared, her histories taught in schools. Tallow holding his daughter's hand, the girl alive and whole and *remembered*. And Wren — Wren in a fine coat that fit, Wren with a name on a deed, Wren turning to him with an expression he had spent weeks pretending he hadn't learned to read, and saying:

"Took you long enough. We saved you a seat. We always save you a seat."

Everyone remembered him. Everyone loved him. No door in the world would ever again open onto a room where he was a stranger. He stood in the middle of it all with a pear going soft in his hand, and he wept, openly, the way he had never once permitted himself, and the vision held him like a mother holds a fevered child.

"Stay," said the king-thing, from everywhere, from inside his own heartbeat. "This is only the shape of it. Live, and it becomes real. Be mine, and you will never be unmade, unmapped, unnamed again. You have spent your whole life being a marginal note, Dominic Fairweather. I am offering you the page entire."

And here is the truth the chapter of his life would later hinge upon, though no ledger recorded it: Dominic Fairweather almost said yes.

His mouth was open. The word was forming. He could feel it sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal, *yes, yes, take me, keep me, I'll sign anything* —

And then he saw the seam.

It was small. A woman across the square, waving at him, smiling — and as she turned away, her smile stayed fixed a moment too long, like a mask hung on a hook before the face beneath remembered to move. And behind her eyes, where a person keeps the private weather of themselves — the small grudges, the secret kindnesses, the unsanctioned thoughts — there was nothing. A tidy, candlelit nothing.

The pears were always ripe here. The bread was always warm. Nobody ever disagreed, because disagreement requires a self, and these people had traded theirs for the pleasure of loving him.

His mother's laugh repeated itself. Exactly. The same laugh, word for word, breath for breath, when a child dropped a scone nearby — the same laugh she'd given him thirty seconds ago, recycled, a coin worn smooth from spending.

Manufactured love. Love as tribute. Love as fuel.

Wren's face in the vision said *we saved you a seat* — and the real Wren, bound and furious in a guardhouse somewhere, had said *you don't get to buy me.* The real one argued. The real one got angry at him, stole his pen, called him an asset, made him earn every inch of ground between stranger and friend. That was the difference. That was the whole difference. Real love was a thing you built joint by joint, load-bearing, that could collapse and be rebuilt; this was a painting of a bridge, gorgeous, flat, and bearing nothing.

"They're not real," Dominic said quietly. The square flickered, just once, like a lamp in wind. "None of them are. They can't refuse me. That's how I know none of it counts."

"It can be real," the king-thing said, and now the warmth thinned, and beneath it he heard it: the vast slow hunger, the patience of a thing that had eaten a kingdom's memory one grief at a time. "Give me the prophecy. Give me the counter-words Maren carries. Stay, and believe, and I will make it so real you'll never know the difference."

"That," said Dominic, "is the most honest sentence you've said all night. *Never know the difference.* That's your whole kingdom, isn't it? Not happiness. Just the absence of knowing."

He let the pear fall. It struck the cobbles and burst — and through the split fruit, through the seams of the world, he saw the truth of the place: darkness, and candles burned down to pools of wax, and a throne, and a thing.

"No," Dominic Fairweather said. Freely. With everything in him. "I choose the version where I have to be earned."

* * *

The vision tore away like wet paper.

He was on his knees on the marble of the real throne room, alone with the thing on its throne, the festival roaring distantly beyond the windows as if nothing at all were happening. The king-thing regarded him for a long moment. Its beautiful borrowed face did not change, but the candlelight around it guttered and leaned *toward* it, ten thousand flames bending like grass before wind.

"So," it said. The warmth was gone entirely now, and what remained was worse than anger: interest. The interest of a collector who has finally found the one piece worth keeping broken. "You would rather be forgotten truly than remembered falsely."

"I'd rather be real," Dominic said. "Even the parts that hurt."

The king-thing rose. When it spoke the next words, they were not spoken so much as *written*, each syllable landing on Dominic's skin like a brand, and he understood that he was hearing ink, hearing the language the sigil used to write its will into the world.

"Then hear your sentence, unmaker. You have refused the keeping. So you shall have the other thing. I mark you for *unmaking* — not death, which leaves a body, which leaves a grave, which leaves *grief*, and grief is memory, and memory is food. Unmaking. From every map. From every ledger. From every mind that ever held your name, until the last person who might have spoken of you forgets there was ever a silence where you stood."

Cold spread through him from the brand-point between his shoulder blades. Not pain. Absence, advancing.

"But know this, cartographer," the sigil-king said, descending the dais at last, standing over him, crown blazing with stolen light. "Your friends come for me tomorrow regardless. Maren carries my ending in her pocket. So I give you one mercy, since you refused all others: you may watch yourself be erased *first*. Line by line. Beginning—"

It smiled with Aldous IV's dead mouth.

"—with whatever they wrote of you tonight."

Far below, in a guardhouse cell, a girl who had spent her life unseen pressed a stolen pen to stolen paper and began, in a hand that shook, to write: *His name is Dominic Fairweather, and he came back for me.*

And in the vaults beneath the palace, something ancient felt the first stroke of that pen — and, for the first time in nine days, felt *hunger* answered with resistance, and did not understand it, and was afraid.
