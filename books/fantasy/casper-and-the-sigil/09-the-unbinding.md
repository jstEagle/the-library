## 9. The Unbinding

**Brannoc**

The vault door was not a door. It was a wound.

Brannoc had walked past the Hall of Forgotten Names a hundred times in his warden years, and never once seen the seam in the marble where a king's grief had been sealed shut with a sigil and called security. Now he pressed his scarred palm against it and felt the old binding answer — a slow, hungry pulse, like a heart that had forgotten what it was beating for.

"They'll know we're here," Maren whispered behind him. "The moment you touch it."

"I'm counting on it." He drew the warden's iron from his belt — the same iron he'd carried for nineteen years, the same iron he'd used to mark the forgetful and the debt-ridden and the dangerous. "Better they come to us than find Tallow's daughter first."

Above them, through the vaulted dark, the Festival of Unending Reign roared on. Ten thousand voices singing a dead man's praises. Brannoc thought of every festival he'd worked, every crowd with candlelight where their memories should have been, and his hand shook against the stone.

"You can still walk away," Dominic said quietly. The ledger-keeper stood with the counter-prophecy cradled against his chest like an infant, ink-stained fingers white around its edges. "You've done your penance. This part's ours."

Brannoc almost laughed. "Boy, I spent two decades holding the leash of that thing up there. You think I get to *walk away*?" He set his palm flat and spoke the old unsealing words, the ones wardens were never supposed to know existed. "I get to stand still while it comes for me."

The wound opened.

* * *

**Wren**

They came out of the dark like moths to a reversed flame.

Wren had fought wardens before — quick scuffles in alleys, a knife across a wrist, always running, always unseen. There was nothing unseen about this. The Hall of Forgotten Names blazed with sigil-light, every nameless niche glowing like a coal, and down the great stair came thirty wardens in lacquered black, iron staves humming, and Wren stood her ground on the ninth step because someone had to hold the line while Dominic read.

"Left side!" Brannoc bellowed, and then he was simply *gone* into them, a bear of a man moving like something twenty years younger, iron meeting iron with a sound like church bells falling downstairs.

Wren put a knife in the first warden's knee and took his stave out of the air before he finished screaming. She'd stolen harder things.

Maren fought like a woman doing arithmetic — precise, economical, each strike exactly as much as necessary, no more. And Tallow — gods, Tallow, who had sold them all three days ago and come back anyway with his daughter's name written on his forearm in his own blood — Tallow fought like a man with nothing left to lose except the one thing he'd gotten back, and so he fought *behind* them, shielding the stair, taking hits meant for other people and smiling through bloody teeth.

"HOW LONG?" Wren shouted up at Dominic.

He didn't answer. He was reading.

* * *

**Dominic**

The counter-prophecy did not want to be read aloud.

Dominic had known this since Vaelmoor, since the night the ink pulled him through the page. Words of unmaking resist being made sound; they snagged in his throat like fishhooks, and every syllable cost him something he couldn't name — warmth, color, the memory of his mother's kitchen, the smell of the guild's tallow candles. He read anyway.

*"That which was bound to preserve shall be bound no more—"*

The sigil-king turned from its throne.

Up close, wearing Aldric's face, it was worse than the festival had promised. The crown sat on a head that had stopped being a head weeks ago — light moved beneath the skin like water under ice, and where the eyes should have been there were only deep, patient reflections of everyone who had ever looked at them. Dominic saw himself in those eyes: small, ledger-bound, *recorded*. Catalogued. Kept.

It raised one hand, and the fighting below went silent — not stopped, *silenced*, as though sound itself had been added to the collection.

*"—and the realm will remember what it paid,"* Dominic read, voice cracking, *"and pay it gladly—"*

**Stop,** said a voice that came from everywhere, from the niches, from the walls of names, from inside Dominic's own skull. **You are reading my death warrant in a dead man's house.**

"It's not your house," Dominic said.

***No.*** Something vast shifted behind the king's face, something older and sadder. ***It is his. Let me show you whose.***

The world folded.

* * *

**Dominic**

He stood in a bedchamber he recognized from a hundred history lessons: the royal apartments, ninety days ago. King Aldric the Fourth lay dying, gray-faced, forty years old and ancient, and around the bed stood the men and women who had forged the records Maren had confessed to forging — and among them, faint as breath on glass, the sigil itself, waiting to be invited.

And Aldric opened his eyes, and looked directly at Dominic as if he'd been expecting him all along.

*"You're the one from the book,"* the dying king said. His voice was ash. *"The unmaker. Good. Listen, then, because I don't have the strength to say it twice.*

*"The realm is sick. Not my body — the realm. The ledgers are lies, the courts sell verdicts, the granaries are empty and the lords know it and the people will burn when they find out. They'll burn each other. I've seen the ledgers too, cartographer. I keep my own.* 

"*So here is my choice. I die, and Vaelmoor tears itself apart over my corpse and the empty treasury and every wrong anyone ever remembered. Or I let the sigil take me — take my face, my voice, my name — and buy the city time. A year. Five. Long enough to mend honestly what would have been mended in fire.*

*"I chose the second thing. I am choosing it now, while I still have a self to choose with. Whatever it becomes after — that is not my choice. But this one was mine. Do you understand? No one bound me. I* volunteered."

The vision held him there, in the dying room, in the terrible quiet of a good decision.

***Do you see?*** the sigil said, gentle now, almost pleading. ***I am keeping his last promise. Unmake me and you unmake the mercy he died to give.***

Dominic stood in the memory of a brave man's final act, and wept, and understood.

And kept reading.

* * *

**Wren**

When the silence broke, it broke all at once, and Wren was mid-fall off the eleventh step with a warden's gauntlet closing on her ankle — and then Brannoc caught her by the collar one-handed without even looking, the way her grandmother used to catch her off fences, and hauled her upright, and she had exactly one heartbeat to be astonished before the next wave hit.

But something had changed. She could feel it in the floor. The sigil-light in the niches was guttering.

"He's refusing it!" she screamed. "He's REFUSING — keep them OFF HIM—"

Tallow laughed, wild and wrecked, and threw himself bodily into three wardens at once. Maren tore the warrant-scrolls from her satchel — the forged records, her originals, the evidence of her own crime — and hurled them into the air, where the burning sigil-light ate them like moths. "Let it eat THESE! Let it choke on the truth!"

And Wren, who had spent her whole life learning how to be invisible, did the most visible thing of her life: she climbed onto the balustrade, balanced above thirty armed wardens, and shouted the name at the top of her lungs.

Not the sigil's name.

Aldric's. His true name, the one erased from every record, the one her grandmother's stories had kept alive in the only archive that couldn't be burned — the mouths of the poor.

"Aldric Vaelmoor the Fourth! ALDRIC! You died a MAN and we REMEMBER—"

Every niche in the Hall flared.

* * *

**Dominic**

*"—for a promise kept by erasure,"* Dominic read, and his voice was nearly gone now, and he could feel whole years going with it, birthdays, faces, the shape of his own handwriting, *"is not a promise but a prison—"*

***Please,*** the sigil said, and for the first time it sounded small. ***He asked me to save them.***

"And they're saving themselves," Dominic said. "Look."

Through the vision's thinning veil he showed it — or it showed itself — the hall full of people shouting a dead king home, a pickpocket standing on a railing giving his name back to the walls, a traitor bleeding for strangers, a historian feeding her shame to the flames. Not one of them bound. Not one of them *kept*.

Remembering freely, the way it was always meant to be done.

***...He would have liked them,*** the sigil said.

"Yes," Dominic said. "Finish it with me. One last line. His and ours together."

He read the final words of the counter-prophecy, and the sigil — the thing Aldric had volunteered to become — spoke them with him:

*"LET THE DEAD KING SLEEP. LET THE LIVING KEEP HIS NAME."*

The shattering was quiet. That was the worst part, Wren would say later — no thunder, no apocalypse, just a sound like a page turning in an empty room, and then the light beneath the king's skin going out gently, gently, until what collapsed on the throne was only what it had always been underneath: a tired man, ninety days dead, finally allowed to lie down.

* * *

**Brannoc**

The payment came due at dawn.

Brannoc smelled the smoke before he understood it — every ledger in the guild's sanctuary, every map, every record, every book Dominic Fairweather had ever touched or written or balanced, igniting at once in a fire that cast no heat and left no ash. The Guild of Impossible Things' entire memory, burning as the price of the unbinding, exactly as the prophecy's fine print had always intended.

They found Dominic in the Hall of Forgotten Names, kneeling beside the empty throne, watching his own hands go translucent at the edges like paper held to a window.

"No," Brannoc said, crossing the floor at a run. "No, boy, we won't—"

"It's fine," Dominic said, and smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has just finished balancing an impossible column and found it true. "It was in the terms. I read them. I *always* read the terms." He looked up at them — Wren crying without any dignity whatsoever, Maren gripping Tallow's arm, old Brannoc on his knees like a penitent. "Listen to me. The ledgers are gone, which means I'm gone from them. But you—" his voice was fraying now, letters dropping out of it like coins through a torn pocket, "you knew me. That's a different kind of record. That one's yours to keep or lose."

"We'll keep it," Wren said fiercely.

"Then write it down," Dominic Fairweather said. "Write it *yourself*. Don't trust the ink. Trust—"

And the sentence lost its ending, and the man lost his outline, and the greatest cartographer the guild had ever produced faded from the Hall of Forgotten Names like morning mist off a map that no longer bore his name.

For a long moment, nobody breathed.

Then Wren wiped her face with the back of her knife-hand, pulled a stub of charcoal from her pocket, and snatched a scrap of fallen parchment from the warden's scattered gear.

"Wren," she said aloud, writing as she spoke. "*Wren, thief, granddaughter of Marisol, met Dominic Fairweather the night the bells rang wrong.*" She looked up at the others, eyes red, jaw set. "Somebody who knew him better goes next. We're doing this properly. All of him. Every page."

Brannoc rose, joints cracking, and picked up a fallen warden's quill.

"Get me better paper," he said. "We start tonight."
