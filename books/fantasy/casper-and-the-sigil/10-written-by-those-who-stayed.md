## 10. Written By Those Who Stayed

The morning after the unbinding, Vaelmoor woke up screaming — and it was the most beautiful sound Dominic Fairweather had ever heard.

He stood at the guild's high window as the city below came apart and put itself back together. A woman in the fish market dropped her basket and began to weep over a name she'd said aloud for the first time in weeks: *Alder, Alder, my son.* A debt-collector on the corner unfurled his ledger with shaking hands and found the entries still there, every coin owed, every promise recorded, and people did not run from him — they gathered around him like men around a well. Somewhere a bell rang that had not rung since the king died, tolling the true count of days, and the whole city seemed to flinch at the arithmetic of what had been taken from it.

Justice returned the way floodwater returns to a dry channel: fast, muddy, unstoppable.

By noon there were trials. Not the sigil's pantomime tribunals where verdicts were written before the accused arrived, but real ones — ugly, slow, full of shouting and grief. Maren spent the day before the Commission of Reckoning, testifying to every record she had forged, every name she had bent to keep a dead man's crown warm. She did not soften it. Dominic watched her from the gallery as she read aloud from her own confession, voice steady, hands not, and when they asked her if she wanted mercy she said, "I want the ledger balanced. Mercy can come after."

Brannoc stood beside her as witness, one-time warden turned evidence, naming the orders he'd carried out, the doors he'd held shut while memory was fed into the vault. Some spat at him. One old woman walked the length of the chamber and took his scarred hand and said, "You remember my husband's name. Say it." And Brannoc said it — *Corvin Hale, cooper, died of the grey fever eleven years past* — and the woman sat down again, satisfied, as though a debt had been paid in full.

Reparations came too. The guild's ledgers — what remained of them, smoke-scorched and half-eaten by the fire that had been their price — became scripture of a kind. Wren organized a small army of scribes and pickpockets (the distinction was thinner than she admitted) to copy out every surviving entry and return what could be returned: coins, houses, inheritances, apologies. It would take years. Wren said so cheerfully, the way other people say *it will be fun.*

And through all of it, Dominic faded.

* * *

He noticed it first in the mirrors, or rather in the absence of himself from them — a smear where a face should be, a figure the glass kept forgetting to finish. Then in the streets. He'd lived in Vaelmoor six years; shopkeepers who had sold him ink a hundred times looked through him now, their eyes sliding off like rain off oiled cloth.

The ledgers burned as payment. That was the law beneath all laws, the old arithmetic of magic: nothing unbound without something bound in its place. Every map he had drawn, every record bearing his hand, every line in the Guild of Impossible Things' books that named him cartographer and ledger-keeper and friend — ash. The sigil had not merely been broken. It had been *paid for*, and the currency was him.

"It isn't death," Maren told him, the second evening, in the sanctuary's lamplit cellar where the tinkerer's bench still smelled faintly of Tallow's solder. "Death is being erased. This is worse and better both. You're unwritten. There is no record anywhere in the realm that says Dominic Fairweather exists. No deed, no diploma, no guild registry. Not even your own handwriting remembers you."

"Then why am I still here?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before — not the historian's detachment, not the penitent's shame. Something warmer and more frightened.

"Because ink isn't the only way a person gets written," she said.

* * *

Wren came to him on the third night.

She climbed through his window the way she did everything — as if doors were suggestions made for lesser people — and sat on the sill with her knees drawn up, watching him try and fail to catch his reflection in a copper pot.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I don't look like anything. That's the problem."

"Good. Easier to steal with." But she wasn't smiling. She reached into her coat and brought out a book — a cheap one, paper-bound, the kind sold three for a copper at the river markets. Its pages were blank except for the first, which was covered in her jagged, sideways-slanting script.

"What's this?"

"Sit down, Dom."

He sat. She opened the book across both their knees, and read aloud:

*"Dominic Fairweather. I met him four days ago when he fell out of a prophecy into the Fountain Square and nearly got trampled by the festival crowd because he was looking up instead of where he was going. He has ink on his fingers always, even after washing. He apologized to a warden while I was robbing him, which is the stupidest thing I have ever seen anyone do, and I once saw a man apologize to a bear. He listened to me. Nobody listens to me. People look *past* me — it's how I survive — but he looked *at* me, the very first minute, like I was somebody worth finding. My gran used to tell stories about a fairweather man who'd come when things were most impossible. I thought she meant the weather. I think maybe she meant him. I am writing this down so the world can't lose it. Signed, Wren, who was seen."*

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above them the city bells tolled nine, honestly, for nothing and no one.

"Wren," Dominic managed, "what is this?"

"It's testimony." She closed the book with a snap and shoved it against his chest. "Maren says the sigil ate memory because memory was only ever kept in one place — the vault, the records, the crown. Kept like treasure. Hoarded. But that's not how remembering works. Remembering is people telling each other things. It's a hundred little books, not one big one." Her chin came up, defiant, daring him to make it small. "So we're making a hundred little books."

* * *

Brannoc came next.

The ex-warden arrived at dawn, huge and awkward in the doorway of Dominic's room, holding his own volume like a man carrying an armed weapon. His page was written in the blunt block-hand of someone trained to record facts and nothing else.

*"Dominic Fairweather,"* Brannoc read, his gravel voice gentle in a way it never was in daylight, *"was the first man in twenty years to ask me a question I didn't already know the answer to. He asked me if I was sorry. Wardens are not permitted regret; it interferes with the binding. He made me permitted. For that alone the realm owes him. Also he cannot hold a sword correctly and I intend to fix this. Witnessed and sworn, Brannoc, formerly First Warden of the Sigil Guard, currently a man learning to be sorry properly."*

Brannoc cleared his throat. Set the book down on the table with exaggerated care, as though it might go off.

"Maren says each testimony anchors you. Says the old bindings needed seals and blood and vaults because they were built on *possession* — someone owning the record of you." He looked at Dominic, and his ruined eyes were bright. "This kind is built on something else. Doesn't need guarding. Can't be stolen. Only given away, and it grows when you do."

* * *

Maren's testimony was ten pages long. Of course it was.

She read it to him in the archive room she was rebuilding from ash, surrounded by empty shelves waiting to be filled with honest books. She wrote of the historian's trade and its corruptions; of the moment in the Hall of Forgotten Names when Dominic had taken her shaking hands and said, *then help us write it true now*; of how redemption was not a document but a practice, performed daily, witnessed by others. The final lines she read twice:

*"Let the record show that I forged the throne and that Dominic Fairweather refused to let me carry it alone. Let the record show he never once called me traitor, though I earned the word. Let the record show that history is not what kings preserve but what the living refuse to forget. I, Maren of the Royal College, sign this willingly, gladly, and last of all my confessions, because it is the only one that is entirely true."*

* * *

Tallow came on the ninth day.

Dominic almost didn't recognize him at first. The promise-tinkerer had shaved, combed his wild hair, put on a coat without burn holes. He stood in the doorway holding his daughter's hand — Liss, eight years old, solemn-eyed, released from the sigil's keeping when it shattered, returned to a father she barely remembered and who remembered everything about her.

"She asked where I'd been," Tallow said hoarsely. "I told her the truth. All of it. Even the betrayal. Even you, showing me her name in the ledger when I thought the sigil had eaten it forever." He swallowed. "You didn't turn me in. You showed me the page."

"You were already turning yourself in. I just handed you the pen."

Tallow laughed, wet and cracked, and set his book on the table. On the cover, in careful tinkerer's letters, he had stamped a small brass plate: *TESTIMONY OF DOMINIC FAIRWEATHER, VOL. IV.*

He didn't read it aloud. He simply turned it so Dominic could see the last line, written large enough for a child to copy out beneath it in wobbling pencil — Liss's contribution, her first act of memory, her first page of anything:

*dominic fairwether. he gave daddy back.*

"That's misspelled," Dominic said, and found he could not finish the sentence for a long time.

"Yeah," Tallow said. "We're keeping it."

* * *

On the tenth night, the guild gathered in the sanctuary cellar — the same room where a prophecy had once pulled him through a page and into the rest of his life. Wren sat on the table swinging her legs. Brannoc occupied two chairs. Maren presided over a growing shelf: five volumes now, spines labeled in her precise hand. Scribes from the city had begun sending copies. Strangers Dominic had never met were writing paragraphs about a man they knew only from rumor — the ledger-keeper who unmade a king. The hundred little books were multiplying on their own.

"So," Wren said, when the candles were lit and the wine poured. "No more ledgers. No maps with your name. No guild registry. By every official measure in Vaelmoor, you're nobody." She grinned. "How's it feel?"

Dominic looked around the cellar — at the thief who had written him into existence, the warden learning regret, the historian writing truth at last, the tinkerer and his daughter, the ragged impossible family the prophecy had never mentioned because prophecies only ever care about crowns.

He thought about belonging — how he'd spent his whole life believing it was granted, recorded, sealed in some vault somewhere, and could therefore be revoked. How wrong he'd been. Belonging wasn't ink. It was this: people who chose, daily, to say your name.

"It feels," he said, "like home."

Wren slid a fresh blank volume across the table toward him, along with a pen.

"Then get to work," she said. "Volume one's yours. And Dom? Write big. We've got a lot of remembering left to do."

He dipped the pen, and began.
