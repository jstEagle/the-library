## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Gilded Scale smelled of lamp oil, tallow smoke, and the particular sweetness of dried feverfew that Juniper hung above the kitchen door every autumn and swore was for headaches, though everyone knew she liked the way it looked against the beams.

Beatrix Ashgrove sat at the long counting table in the back room with three lamps lit around him — one for reading, one for the ledger, one because Ovist Sable insisted a man working numbers needed light enough to see his own mistakes — and dipped his pen in ink the color of a bruise.

The night's work was ordinary, which Beatrix loved in the way other men loved feasts or festivals. Ordinary meant the ledgers balanced. Ordinary meant no one had bled, no one had run, no one had come through the back door at midnight wearing a face like a held breath. He ruled a fresh line beneath the day's column of figures, blew gently across the wet ink, and permitted himself the small, private satisfaction of a page that added up.

*Twelve bolts of Vessian wool, mislabeled as sailcloth.* That went under *Textiles, misc.* The duty owed to the harbormaster's cousin — a bribe, though never called one — went under *Port fees, seasonal adjustment.* The two casks of Ashfall brandy that had technically belonged to a merchant who had technically sold them to a man who did not exist went under *Beverages, estate clearance.* And the payment received from Lady Merrow of the Salt Court for services rendered — services Beatrix did not ask about and did not want to know about, though he suspected a stolen portrait and an even more stolen husband — went under the catchall heading he had invented his first week at the Scale: *Miscellaneous, delicate.*

He had been a guild clerk for six years, and in six years he had learned that every smuggling operation in the mortal realms ran on exactly two things: fast horses and good bookkeeping. The horses were someone else's problem. The books were his, and they were beautiful.

"You're doing the face again."

Beatrix didn't look up. "What face?"

"The one where you're in love with the paper." Wren dropped into the chair across from him, upside-down boots hooked over its back, a heel of bread in one hand and her short dark hair still damp from the washhouse. She was the Scale's runner and, when the job required it, its most convincing orphan; she could cry on command from either eye independently, a talent she demonstrated at parties. "You do it every night. Marry the ledger if you love it so much."

"It would be a more faithful partner than most I've known."

"Low bar." She tore off a piece of bread and set it on the corner of his page, which was her way of affection — feeding people whether they asked or not. "Ovist wants you upstairs when you're done. We've got a client."

"At this hour?"

"Odd hours make odd prices," Wren said, reciting guild doctrine with the flat piety of someone quoting scripture she'd heard ten thousand times. She swung her legs down and leaned across the table, lowering her voice even though the back room was empty except for the two of them and the patient ticking of the mantel clock. "It's a strange one, Bee. Fen says the man came in off the coast road without a horse. On foot. In this cold. And he paid the door-fee in old coin."

"Old how?"

"Worn smooth. The kind of worn you can't fake." Wren shrugged, but her eyes stayed serious. "Fen checked them twice against the weight-book. They're real. They're just *old*."

Beatrix considered this while he finished the line he was writing. The Gilded Scale dealt in plenty of strange — that was the nature of the trade; anything valuable enough to smuggle was valuable enough to have a story attached — but old coin worn smooth was a specific flavor of strange. Coins wore smooth in pockets, in purses, in hands. A lot of hands took a very long time.

"I'll be up directly," he said. "Let me close out the day first."

"You always say that."

"Because there's always a day to close." He tapped the ledger with two fingers. "The books don't balance themselves."

"No," Wren agreed, standing and stretching until her spine popped. "That's why they keep you."

* * *

The common room of the Gilded Scale occupied the ground floor of what had once been a customs house, back before the border treaties made *customs* a word men died over and the building changed hands eleven times in a decade. Ovist Sable had bought it cheap from a widow who wanted nothing to remind her of her late husband, which is why the taproom still bore the carved scales-of-justice above the hearth, gilt flaking to bare wood on one pan. The guild had kept the name. It made the paperwork easier.

By the time Beatrix climbed the stairs with his second-best coat brushed over his shirt, the room was in its evening shape: Fen Marsh behind the bar polishing glasses that were already clean, because Fen thought better with his hands moving; Tomlin and Ada playing knucklebones by the window with the grim concentration of people who had real money riding on the outcome; Juniper curled on the bench nearest the fire with a lock mechanism half-disassembled in her lap and a screwdriver between her teeth like a rose stem. The fire itself burned low and gold, and above the mantel, in the place of honor where another house might hang a stag's head or a family crest, hung the framed first page of the guild's original ledger, in Ovist's young handwriting:

*Item the first: one debt forgiven. Paid forward.*

Beatrix had asked about it once, years ago, when he was new and still flinched at loud noises. Ovist had said only, "Every business needs founding documents. Ours needed to be honest." He had not elaborated, and Beatrix had learned not to ask Ovist Sable things twice.

The client sat at the corner table farthest from the fire.

He was a thin man wrapped in a traveling cloak gone gray with road dust, hood down despite the cold, revealing a face that might have been any age between forty and seventy — weathered, unremarkable, forgettable by design or by nature. His hands rested flat on the table, and they were wrong somehow, though Beatrix couldn't say why at first. Then he understood: the man held them *still*. Completely still. Most people's hands moved — tapped, fidgeted, adjusted. This man's hands were placed like objects on a shelf.

Ovist stood beside the table, arms folded, wearing his negotiating face, which looked almost identical to his disappointed-in-you face, a fact that had unsettled many counterparties over the years.

"There's our clerk," Ovist said, nodding Beatrix over. "Master Ellery, this is Beatrix, keeps our accounts. Anything agreed at this table goes through his book or it didn't happen."

"That's proper," the client said. His voice was dry as paper. "A thing should be written down."

Beatrix sat, opened the slim black ledger he carried for client meetings — separate from the master ledger, because Ovist was paranoid in a dozen useful ways — and uncapped his pen. "Terms?"

"A delivery," Master Ellery said. "Not goods. Not information." He paused, and Beatrix had the odd sensation of a man choosing words the way a sapper chooses footholds. "I want a star. Still warm."

The fire popped. Across the room, Juniper's screwdriver stopped turning.

Beatrix kept his eyes on the page. Six years of practice kept his hand level. Inside his chest, beneath the breastbone, in the place he did not look at and did not touch, something stirred — a faint pressure, like weather gathering behind glass. He breathed through it, slow and even, the way he'd taught himself, the way that had kept him alive through three inspections and one terrible night in a courtroom.

"Fanciful requests are common in this trade," he said mildly. "Usually they mean something else. A relic? A meteoric stone? Certain fallen-star iron carries value in—"

"A star," the man repeated, with the patience of someone correcting a child's arithmetic. "Still warm. From your sky. The ones that are up there now." His flat eyes lifted, briefly, toward the high window where the first evening stars were pricking through the blue-black over the rooftops. "There are fewer than there used to be. Did you know that? Astronomers write papers about it. Atmospheric interference, they say. Dimming epochs." Something moved at the corner of his mouth, not quite a smile. "They don't know what dimming epochs are."

Ovist shifted his weight. "Master Ellery, we deal in possible things. That's our whole discipline. We find the seam in the impossible and we drive a cart through it. But you'll need to tell me what this actually *is*, or this conversation ends with a drink on the house and my compliments."

The man reached into his cloak.

Every instinct in the room sharpened at once — Beatrix saw Fen's hand drift below the bar, saw Tomlin's knucklebones go quiet — but what Master Ellery produced was not a weapon. It was a purse. Heavy, old leather, tied with a cord that had been retied many times. He set it on the table with a click of metal that sounded wrong for coins, too resonant, too bright.

"Half now," he said. "Half on delivery. Name your price beyond it. I've waited a long time. I can wait for the right crew."

Ovist didn't reach for the purse. "And if we refuse?"

"Then I'll find someone worse than you," the man said simply, "and the difference will be your problem eventually. Things taken badly always come home badly." He looked at Beatrix then, directly, and Beatrix felt the look like a finger pressed against a bruise he'd forgotten he had. "You keep the accounts. So you'll understand: everything is owed to someone. I am owed warmth. That is all. Warmth, and light, before the long dark finishes falling."

Nobody spoke. Above the window, through the frost-feathered glass, the early stars burned on, indifferent and lovely.

Ovist exhaled through his nose. "Leave the purse. Leave us the night to think. Come back tomorrow at dusk, and you'll have an answer."

"I'll come back tomorrow at dusk," the man agreed, rising. He drew his hood up. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and said — to the room, or perhaps to no one — "Mind your books carefully, clerk. This year, everything balances." Then the cold swallowed him, and the door thumped shut, and the Gilded Scale exhaled all at once, four conversations starting up like birds after a hawk passes.

"Well," said Fen, coming around the bar with the brandy bottle and five glasses, which was one fewer than the number of people who needed one. "That was a new one."

"He was mad," said Tomlin.

"He was rich," said Ada.

"He was sad," said Juniper quietly, from her bench. She had reassembled the lock without looking at it. "Did anyone else think he was sad? Like — homesick-sad. For somewhere that isn't anywhere anymore."

Beatrix said nothing. He was looking at the purse on the table, at the cord, at the shape of it, and feeling the pressure behind his sternum rise and fall like a tide answering some moon he refused to name.

* * *

Midnight found him alone in the back room, as midnight usually did. The household slept in the loose, staggered way of smugglers — Fen snoring in the loft, Tomlin and Ada sharing the room over the stable because they'd given up pretending otherwise two winters ago, Juniper wherever Juniper decided to sleep, sometimes her cot, sometimes the window seat, once memorably the bread oven. Ovist kept late hours and earlier ones, sleeping in pieces like a cat.

Beatrix turned the day's entries into the master ledger in his careful clerk's hand. When he reached the client's request, he paused, pen hovering.

Guild law, unwritten but absolute: *the book records the trade as spoken.* Ovist had learned it from whoever taught him, and taught it to Beatrix in his first month: *Never prettify an entry. Never translate. If a man sells his shadow, you write shadow, and you date it, and you witness it.* Because the ledger wasn't just memory. Beatrix had come to suspect, in his private and unexamined hours, that the ledger was something closer to *law* — that the Gilded Scale's books, kept honestly for twenty years, had accumulated a weight of their own, the way a riverbed accumulates the shape of the water. You wrote a thing down truly, and the world had one more true thing in it. That mattered. He didn't know why he was sure of it. He was sure of very few things that deeply, and he'd learned not to interrogate the certainty.

So he wrote: *Received of Master Ellery, retainer against delivery: one star, still warm. Terms pending.*

His pen scratched. The fire settled. And outside the high little window, over the frozen rooftops of the quarter, the sky did what it had done every night of Beatrix's life.

Except tonight, as he blotted the entry and closed the ledger — tonight, one star in the eastern dark, a bright one, a familiar one he'd steered by on more than one illicit crossing — flickered.

Not twinkled. Stars twinkle; that is their nature and their charm. This was different. This was a guttering, the way a candle gutters when a door opens somewhere in the house and the draft finds it. The star shrank, shivered, went brown at the heart —

and went out.

Beatrix stood up so fast his chair skidded. Behind his ribs the pressure surged, sudden and enormous, storm-pressure, lightning-hunger, the old wildness waking in its cage and pressing its palms flat against the walls of him. Frost bloomed up the windowpane from the sill in a spreading fern-pattern, crackling, and he realized distantly that the temperature in the room had dropped, that his breath was fogging, that the hair on his forearms was lifting, that he was doing it, he was *leaking*, six years of control fraying in four seconds because a star had died overhead and some buried animal part of him had felt it the way a dog feels an earthquake coming.

He gripped the edge of the table. Breathed. Counted, the old courtroom trick: *four in, seven hold, eight out.* The pressure receded by degrees, sullen, unwilling, like a tide dragged backward off a shore. The frost on the window stopped growing. Slowly, slowly, his breath stopped showing.

In the glass, distorted by ice-ferns, the eastern sky lay open and black where the star had been.

Beatrix looked at the ledger. At the fresh entry, ink drying, dated tonight. *One star, still warm.*

"We didn't take the job," he whispered to the empty room. "We didn't say yes. We only *wrote it down*."

The house creaked around him, settling into cold. Somewhere above the frozen town, faintly, impossibly, he thought he heard — not with his ears, lower than ears, in the marrow-place where the weather lived — a sound like a note cut off mid-phrase. Then another, farther east. Then another.

Stars going out. One by one. Like lamps being shuttered along a street by someone walking away, and walking quickly, before anyone could ask them why.

Upstairs, a floorboard groaned — Ovist, awake, or Juniper, or only the cold. Beatrix pulled the ledger to him, held it against his chest the way Wren accused him of holding it, like a beloved, like a shield, and stared at the dark window with his heart hammering out the oldest rhythm he knew: *hide it, hide it, hide it, hide.*

Outside, the winter deepened. And somewhere far off past the border, past the wards, past the edge of every map the guild owned, something vast began — softly, patiently — to sing.
