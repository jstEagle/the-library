## 2. Wards Falling

The Gilded Scale kept its books in the old way, which was to say the way Beatrix liked: by candlelight, with a pot of mulled wine going cold at his elbow and the house settling into its bones around him. Midnight work suited him. The counting room sat above the guildhall's common room, close enough that he could hear the last of the evening's noise bleeding up through the floorboards — Ferren laughing too loudly at something that probably wasn't funny, Juniper's boots on the stair rail where she absolutely should not have been walking, Ovist's low voice smoothing over all of it like a hand over a rumpled bedsheet.

Beatrix dipped his pen and wrote, in his small precise hand: *One lantern, everburning, sold to Widow Marsh, paid in full, delivered Tuesday.*

An impossible thing, recorded as though it were ordinary. That was the trick of the ledger, the secret grammar of it. You did not write *everburning lantern* in a way that invited questions. You wrote it the way you'd write *six nails* or *a bolt of grey wool*, and the world, reading over your shoulder, believed you.

He had been keeping the Gilded Scale's accounts for four years now. Four years since the trial, since the stripping of his name from the rolls of the storm-callers' college, since the Warden-Captain had looked through him with those flat grey eyes and pronounced the sentence that was not quite death but wanted to be: *exiled from every city with walls, barred from the weather-rites, forbidden under sanction of law to call, shape, or soothe any storm.* Four years of ink stains instead of lightning scars.

His fingers remembered anyway. Some nights, when the wind came off the mountains in long gray ropes, his hands would move without his permission — the old gestures, thumb to second knuckle, palm turning like a door — and he would have to press them flat against the desk until the itch went out of them. Tonight they were quiet. Tonight there was only the ledger, and the candle, and the small warm sounds of a family that had never asked him what he'd done to deserve exile, because Ovist Sable had decided it didn't matter, and Ovist's decisions were the closest thing the Gilded Scale had to scripture.

Below him, someone began tuning a fiddle badly.

*One star, still warm,* the newest entry read. He'd written it three nights ago and still disliked looking at it. The client had been wrong in ways Beatrix couldn't name — wrong the way a portrait is wrong, everything in its proper place and yet no one home behind the eyes. He had worn grey traveling clothes of good cut and paid in coin that was neither minted nor cast, each piece smooth as a river stone and faintly warm. He had set the shard on the counter — the payment, the down-payment — and said, "For the balance, I'll return," in a voice like wind through an empty room.

And then he had walked out into the night, and Beatrix had entered the trade in the ledger because that was his job, and had told himself the crawling feeling along his arms was only the weather changing.

It was not the weather changing. He knew, now, in the way you know things in dreams — completely and uselessly — that it had never been the weather.

The shard sat in the vault two floors down, wrapped in wool inside an iron box inside a warded cabinet that Ferren had picked six different locks to build. Even through the floors, even through the ledger desk, Beatrix could feel it sometimes. Not warmth exactly. Not sound. A *hum* at the edge of hearing, the way a struck bell goes on ringing after the ear gives up on it.

He was reaching for the wine when the first ward went.

He felt it before he heard it — felt it in his teeth, in the scar tissue along his forearms where lightning had written its signature years ago. A high, thin note, like glass singing, coming from everywhere at once. From the north. From the border, forty miles off, where the wards ran in their ancient chained lines from mountain to mountain, older than any kingdom, older than the guilds, older than the quarrels that had made exiles necessary.

The note bent. Stretched. Broke.

Above the common room, the fiddle stopped mid-squeak.

Then the second ward went, and the third, and Beatrix was on his feet without remembering standing, because he could *see* it now — not with his eyes but with the sense he was forbidden to use, the weather-sense unrolling behind his eyes like a chart — and what he saw was the north going *white*. Not snow. Cold. Cold as a substance, cold as water behind a cracked dam, pouring through the broken line of the wards in a flood that had weight and hunger and direction.

"Beatrix?" Juniper's voice through the door, sharp with alarm. "Did you feel—"

"Get everyone up." His voice came out steadier than his hands. "Now. Cellars, not the yard."

"Why, what's—" But she was already moving; Juniper's feet were always faster than her questions.

The window iced over while he watched. Frost crawled across the glass in fern-patterns, beautiful and wrong, and beyond it the stars —

Beatrix had spent four years teaching himself not to look at the sky the way he used to. Storm-callers read clouds the way clerks read contracts; it was the first thing they taught you and the last thing they could take away. He looked now because he had no choice, and what he saw made his stomach drop through the floorboards, through the foundations, through the frozen earth itself.

The stars were going out.

Not dimming. Not hiding behind cloud — there was no cloud; the night was viciously clear. They were going out in patches, whole constellations snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers, and where they vanished the dark was not empty but *occupied*, a darkness with texture, a darkness that pressed. The cold flooding over the wards poured down from that occupied dark the way light pours from flame.

The ledger entry rose in his mind unbidden. *One star, still warm.*

Someone had bought a star. And tonight, it seemed, someone had come to collect the rest of them.

The house shuddered.

It was not an earthquake — Beatrix knew earthquakes, this land's faults were old and sleepy — it was the wards, the near ones, the city's own ring of protection drinking deep of whatever power fed them and finding the well dry, the covenant severed upstream. Wards were promises, at bottom. Promises made between realms so long ago that no living creature remembered the terms. And a promise with one party gone silent is not a promise anymore. It's a wall with nothing holding it up.

The Gilded Scale had stood for ninety years on the strength of those promises. It came apart in eleven seconds.

First the chimneys went — he heard them go, a soft collapsing crunch from either end of the roofline. Then the great hearth below blew its fire backward up the flue with a sound like a gasp, and the common room screamed, and then the eastern wall of the counting room folded inward like paper held over flame, and the world became falling timber and white dust and a cold that hit Beatrix's face like a slap from a giant.

He got his arms up. He got the desk between himself and the worst of it, some clerk's instinct — protect the ledgers, protect the *books*, four years of impossible trades balanced to the copper — and then a beam the size of a ship's mast came down across the desk and the ceiling came down across the beam, and the last thing he saw clearly was his candle, upright and undisturbed amid the ruin, still burning.

Still burning, in all that cold.

That was wrong. That was so deeply, cosmically wrong that his mind seized on it the way a drowning man seizes driftwood, and he was still staring at the little defiant flame when the weight above him shifted and the dark closed in—

—and opened again, torn wide by two enormous hands.

"Boy." Ovist Sable's voice, gravel and smoke and absolute calm, the voice of a man who had pulled people out of burning warehouses and sinking ferries and one memorable bear pit. "Boy, blink if you're breathing."

"I'm breathing," Beatrix said, and coughed dust. "The ledger—"

"Damn the ledger."

"The ledgers are the *guild*, Ovist."

Ovist's face appeared in the gap above him, broad and seamed, lit by the impossible candle. His eyes went to the little flame, and Beatrix watched him see it, watch the same wrongness land on him, watch him decide not to look at it directly. There were things Ovist Sable did not look at directly. It was one of his rules, and it had kept him alive through thirty years of smuggling things that bit back.

"Hold still." Ovist took the beam in both hands. He was sixty years old and built like a dock crane, and the beam moved maybe three inches, which was enough. "Ferren! Under here, now, shoulder to it — *now*, lad!"

Ferren came, grunting, swearing cheerfully, and the gap widened, and Beatrix rolled out of the trap of his own counting room into air that cut like a knife. The sky overhead was a black wound. Half the stars — half — simply gone. Snow was beginning to fall from that starless dark, fat flakes that fell too slowly, as though reluctant, as though each one were considering whether to land.

Around the guildyard, the Gilded Scale breathed out its ruin. The eastern range was down. The great chimney lay across the stable like a fallen tree. People were everywhere — the family, all of them, plus the three lodgers and the widow's boy who'd been sleeping in the hay — moving, counting each other, wrapping each other in blankets. Juniper had taken charge of the children with the fierce competence of someone who had once been one herself, abandoned at a gate somewhere, collateral in a trade nobody had ever explained to her.

Ovist gripped Beatrix's shoulder hard enough to bruise and looked him over head to foot, cataloguing damage. "Bleeding?"

"Scraped."

"Bones?"

"All present."

"Good." Ovist turned to survey his ruined house, and Beatrix saw something cross his face that he had never seen there in four years: fear, naked and unhidden, there and then deliberately put away, folded up and pocketed like a knife. "Wardens will come," Ovist said quietly. "When the wards fall, the wardens always come. Questions first, help later, that's the order of things. So we get the wounded inside what's left, we get the ledgers out of what isn't, and nobody—" his eye found Beatrix, held it, "—nobody does anything strange tonight."

*I called the weather-sense,* Beatrix thought. *I stood in the window and read the sky like a schoolboy reading letters.* If any warden had been within a mile — if Hale, if *Hale* — 

"I understand," he said.

"The vault," Ovist added, and his voice dropped further. "Come with me."

They went down together through the tilding corridors, past Ferren directing salvage with a crowbar and a vocabulary, past Juniper hauling blankets, down the stairs that still stood, into the vault's iron-bounded chill. The warded cabinet stood untouched amid the dust-sifted dark, and inside the iron box, inside the wool—

The humming had become a song.

Not loud. Barely audible. But unmistakable now, a thin sweet tone rising and falling, rising and falling, and with every fall of the melody somewhere overhead another star went out, Beatrix was sure of it, sure the way he was sure of storms. The shard of dead starlight — dead, they had all assumed, dead as the client who'd left it — was singing a dirge for its siblings.

Or a summons. He couldn't tell which. That was the worst of it. He pressed his palms flat against the iron box and felt the vibration run up his arms into the old lightning scars, and the scars *answered*, a faint heat kindling along them like coals breathed upon, and for one vertiginous moment he felt the storm that was coming — not weather, something larger than weather, a pressure system the size of grief rolling down out of the occupied dark — and knew, with a clerk's certainty, with a ledger's arithmetic, that the balance of the world had been broken on the debit side, and that winter was the interest accruing.

"Beatrix." Ovist's hand closed over his wrist, pulling it back from the box. "What is it? What's it doing?"

"It's mourning," Beatrix said. "Or calling. I don't know. Ovist — the wards didn't fail. They were *ended*. Something ended the covenant, and everything since midnight is just... consequence. Falling dominoes." He heard his own voice go flat and precise, the ledger-voice, the voice he used when the numbers frightened him. "And the client. Three nights ago. The one who bought the star."

"What about him?"

"He paid in advance." Beatrix looked at the humming box, and past it, at the frost climbing the vault's iron door, writing its fern-patterns over steel as if steel were glass. "Ovist — whoever he was, he wasn't buying a star. He was making a *withdrawal*. The whole sky is an account, and somebody just cleaned it out, and I entered the first transaction in my own book."

The humming swelled, one long note, and far above them, muffled by rubble and distance, they heard the last star over the guildhouse gutter and die, and the cold came down through the broken roof like a verdict.

Ovist Sable stood very still for a moment. Then he took off his coat, heavy and lined, and put it around Beatrix's shoulders the way he had done on the night four years ago when a hollow-eyed exile had turned up at his gate with no luggage and no story, and he said:

"Then we're going to need a bigger book. Come on. We've got wounded, we've got wardens coming, and you're going to tell me everything — *everything*, boy — before the sun comes up. If it comes up."

Outside, the snow fell slower and slower, each flake hanging in the dark like a question, and in the vault behind them the shard sang on into the silence between stars.

* * *

Dawn did not come.

At the hour when the east should have gone grey, the horizon stayed black, and the guild family stood in the ruins of their common room around a fire built from their own broken furniture, watching the sky refuse. Juniper counted heads. Ferren counted supplies. Ovist counted everything twice.

And Beatrix sat apart with the ledger open on his knees, pen poised over a fresh page, trying to find words for an entry no clerk had ever had to write. At last he wrote:

*Night of the Falling Wards. Assets lost: the sky.*

*Liabilities: unknown. Presumed total.*

He was still staring at the ink when the lookout in the tower — what remained of the tower — sounded the horn, twice, the signal for riders on the road.

Wardens. Coming fast, with torches against a dawn that would not break, and at their head a grey-cloaked figure whose silhouette Beatrix would have known anywhere in the world, in any darkness, even this one.

Hale.
