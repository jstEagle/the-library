## 5. The Road Through White Silence

The world beyond Gildenholt had been green when Beatrix was last exiled from it. Now it was the color of old bone.

He stood at the edge of the borderlands with the wind worrying at his coat hem and tried not to listen to what the air was telling him. That was the trouble, and it had always been the trouble: the weather *spoke* to him whether he willed it or no. Every storm-caller learned early that calling was only half the curse. The other half was hearing — the low mutter of pressure fronts like argumentative relatives, the whine of a cold snap gathering itself in the high passes, the long slow groan of ice deciding where to grow. He'd spent eleven years teaching himself deafness. Eleven years of pressing his palms flat against his thighs when a thunderhead rolled over the guildhouse, of biting the inside of his cheek until he tasted copper rather than answer the sky's questions.

Now there were no answers left to give. The sky above the borderlands had gone out entirely — not overcast but *absent*, a black ceiling pressed down close enough to touch, and beneath it the white country stretched away without horizon. Snow lay knee-deep and undrifted, because there was no wind to drift it. No wind at all. The stillness was worse than any gale. It was the silence of a held breath.

"Say something gloomy," Juniper said beside him. "You've got a face on."

"I'm admiring the view."

"It's a terrible view."

"It's an honest one." Beatrix pulled his scarf up over his chin. "Everything out here is exactly what it looks like. I find that restful after twenty years of ledgers."

Behind them, the last of the guild's caravan finished loading: two sledge-carts drawn by shaggy moor-ponies whose breath came out in plumes, Ovist Sable standing at the shafts of the first like a man taking up position at a bar he intended never to leave, and old Maren Voss checking the lashings for the fourth time because checking things was how Maren prayed. Six of them, all told. A smuggling crew dressed for a funeral, which Beatrix supposed was accurate. They were carrying the shard of dead starlight north, wrapped in three layers of wool and one of lead-lined silk, toward the Spire at the hinge of the world, on the theory — Ovist's theory, argued into being over three sleepless days — that the thing they'd unknowingly bought from their client was either the key to mending the broken covenant or the proof of who had broken it. Possibly both. The ledger suggested both. Ledgers usually did.

The client himself was gone. Had been gone since before midnight, since before the wards fell — vanished from the Gilded Scale's back room leaving behind a chair still warm, a payment too strange to refuse, and an entry in the book that read, in Beatrix's own careful hand, *one star, still warm, delivered*.

He thought about that entry now as the ponies stamped and the snow creaked underfoot. He had written thousands of entries in eleven years. Grain futures. Contraband lace. A shipment of singing eels for a bishop with more money than sense. Never once had his pen trembled until that night.

"All right," Ovist called, and his voice carried flat across the stillness, stripped of its usual barrel-deep warmth by the dead air. "North by the old drover road as far as it goes, then we make our own way. Nobody wanders. Nobody touches anything blue. And if anyone hears anything—" his eyes found Beatrix, brief and heavy, "—they tell me first. Move out."

They moved out.

* * *

The first day taught them the shape of the new winter.

Beatrix rode the second cart and kept his hands in his pockets and did not listen, and listening happened anyway, the way a scar aches before rain. There was nothing living in the weather anymore to speak to — that was what unsettled him most. Weather had always had a kind of animal quality, moods and appetites; even the cruelest blizzard wanted something, even if what it wanted was to bury you. This was different. The cold here wasn't doing anything. It was just *being*, vast and patient, spreading through the mortal realm like ink through water, and when Beatrix reached out along the edges of his senses the way he hadn't permitted himself in years, he felt nothing answering. Only absence. A room where the conversation had stopped.

Except once. Near dusk, as they made camp in the lee of a fallen waystone, he felt it — a thread of something, thin as spider silk, coming from the north. Not weather. Something older wearing weather's clothes, moving at the pace of continents. It passed overhead like the shadow of a bird too high to see, and every pony screamed.

Not neighed. Screamed.

Juniper had them calmed within minutes, murmuring into their ears the way she could calm locks, and afterward she stood shaking at the cart's rail with her arms wrapped around herself.

"It looked at us," she said. "I know it didn't have eyes. It looked anyway."

"The wards are down," Beatrix said. "Things that used to stay on their side of the fence can wander now." He heard his own voice go flat and clerkish, the tone he used for bad news about tariffs, and hated it. "Get some sleep. I'll take first watch."

He took first watch and second watch and would have taken third, but Ovist came and sat on the sledge beside him without asking, which was how Ovist did everything — arrived, settled, assumed welcome. For a long while neither spoke. The dead stars gave no light worth the name; they sat by feel.

"When you were exiled," Ovist said at last, "did they take your name?"

"They took my right to use it. Different thing. Crueler, actually." Beatrix turned his hands palm-up in the dark. "A storm-caller's name is how the storm knows you. Call yourself by your own true name and the sky comes when you whistle. So they bound it — a writ signed by three magistrates, sealed in wax and law. If I speak my own name aloud in calling, the words come out wrong. Slurred. The storm doesn't recognize me." He smiled, and knew it was a poor smile. "It's very elegant. I helped draft the precedent, back when I was young and certain the College of Storms was a force for order."

"Young and stupid."

"Those were the same word, then."

Ovist grunted. He was a large man built like a warehouse door, with a beard going iron-grey and hands that had strangled better men than him in the guild's rougher years, and he was looking at Beatrix now with an expression Beatrix had learned to read over eleven years and still found unbearable: the look of a man deciding how much weight a beam could carry.

"If it comes to it," Ovist said, "on this road — if the law finds us, or worse finds us, and you need to be loud—"

"No."

"I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you my ledger already carries the debt. You call, and whatever it costs, the Gilded Scale pays its share."

Beatrix looked north, where the nothing-thread had gone. "You don't understand what you're offering. It isn't a fine. The writ doesn't punish me for calling, Ovist. It unmakes me. Every time I force a slurred word past the binding, the binding takes something back. First it took my home village — I literally cannot remember the name of the street I grew up on. Then my mother's face. Last year I lost the taste of oranges." His voice stayed level, clerk-flat, which was the only way he could say such things. "I am being erased in installments. Slowly. By instalments of my own choosing, whenever I choose to be loud. So no. When it comes to it, I will be quiet, because quiet is the only currency I have left, and I am nearly spent."

Ovist said nothing for a long time. Then he put one enormous hand on Beatrix's shoulder, briefly, and went back to his bedroll, and Beatrix sat alone with the silence and understood that the hand had been an answer, though he couldn't have said to what question.

* * *

On the third day the drover road ended, as promised, and the making-their-own-way began, and it nearly killed them twice before noon.

The first time was the crevasse field — a stretch where the frozen river plain had cracked into blue-black fissures wide enough to swallow a cart, masked by crusts of snow that held until they didn't. Maren went through to her waist before Juniper's rope caught her, and they hauled her out blue-lipped and swearing with tremendous fluency, and after that they roped themselves together like mountaineers and crawled the mile-wide field in two hours that felt like two days.

The second time was worse, because it was Beatrix's fault.

There was a moment — he would replay it for weeks afterward — when the rope went slack under Maren and the snow gave way and Beatrix, reaching for purchase on the crust, felt the ground itself hesitate. Felt the frost deciding, in its slow mineral mind, whether to hold or break. And without thinking, without anything so dignified as a decision, he *leaned*. Pushed a whisper of will into the ice. *Hold.* One syllable, unspoken, unnamed — the smallest working a storm-caller could do, less than a breath.

The ice held. Maren lived.

And the frost remembered him.

It was small — a shimmer along the crevasse lip, a bloom of crystals that spread outward from where his glove had pressed, feathering into shapes too regular to be natural, spirals within spirals, the frost writing something in the only alphabet it knew. Juniper saw it. He saw her see it, saw her eyes go from the ice to his face and back, quick as a lockpick reading tumblers.

By noon she had told Ovist, because of course she had, because that was the covenant of the guild — everything shared, everything weighed together. Ovist didn't confront him directly. Ovist simply changed the marching order so that Beatrix walked point, ahead of the sledges, alone, where the others could watch his hands.

Beatrix understood. He even approved. It was sensible. It was also the oldest wound he had, reopening with a familiar wet tearing sound: the memory of the day the College of Storms had turned him out, the courtyard, the magistrates' faces arranged into sorrow, his colleagues standing in a careful row, none of them meeting his eye. *Exiled for the safety of the realm.* He had been twenty-six. He had believed in the sentence. That was the worst part — he had agreed with it, had walked out the gates half-convinced the realm was safer with him outside it, and he had spent eleven years building a life on that foundation and calling the building humility when it was really just shame with good joinery.

Now here it was again. Point position. Watching eyes. The frost blooming where he touched because the world could always tell what he was, even through wool and gloves and twenty years of ink stains.

At midday they halted in the shadow of a leaning menhir, some border-marker older than the border, and Ovist handed out hardtack and said, conversationally, to the group at large:

"We'll be crossing the old covenant-line tomorrow. After that, weather gets strange even by strange standards. Which is why—" and here his gaze rested on Beatrix, mild and immovable, "—I'm glad to have our clerk walking point. Good eyes for reading ground."

It was not absolution. It was something more practical and therefore, Beatrix thought, more valuable: a job. Ovist Sable did not forgive people for being dangerous. He employed them for it, and watched them, and paid their debts. Beatrix chewed his hardtack and found, to his surprise, that his throat worked fine.

* * *

It was near dusk on the fourth day that Beatrix broke his own rule.

They had come up onto a long white shoulder of land where the map simply gave up — the surveyors' blank space beyond the last named landmark, marked only with the cartographer's traditional formula: *here be the Spire's country, God keep the traveler.* The cold had deepened from bitter into something structural, a cold that lived in stone and bone alike, and the ponies were failing. The lead mare had stopped twice in the last hour, head hanging, breath shallow. Another night like this and they'd be hauling the sledges themselves, and the day after that they wouldn't be hauling anything.

Ahead the land split into a maze of frozen ridges and buried ravines, and Beatrix stood at the front of the column and let himself do the thing he had sworn off for eleven years.

He listened.

It cost him immediately. The binding woke like a dog rising from sleep, a tightening behind his breastbone, a sensation of words being weighed and taxed at the border of his mouth. But he wasn't calling. Listening wasn't calling. The writ forbade the voice, not the ear — the magistrates had been thorough but not omniscient, and Beatrix had spent eleven years learning exactly how thorough they'd been.

So he opened the deaf ear he'd kept shut for a decade, and the white country poured in.

Dead, mostly. Dead as it had been since the wards fell. But underneath the death, threading through it — patterns. Pressure and absence-of-pressure, the bones of the old winds still lying where they'd died, and among them, faint but unmistakable, a seam. A channel where the stillness ran thinner, where something beneath the ice breathed slow and warm as a banked hearth. Geothermal veins, the old texts would have called them, vents of the deep earth's heat — the same warmth, he realized with a chill that had nothing to do with cold, that had fed the border-forts and the waystone-inns in the covenant's day. The realms had been warmer where they touched. The door between worlds had been a hearth once.

The seam wound northeast, through ridges that would shelter them from whatever watched, over ground that would hold. It was a path. It was possibly the only path. And following it meant trusting his hearing, which meant trusting himself, which was the transaction eleven years of exile had made almost impossible.

"Beatrix." Ovist had come up beside him, breath pluming. "Talk to me. You've got the face of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

"There's a route." He pointed, tracing the seam's line against the dark. "Northeast, through the ridge country. Warm ground underneath — the old hearth-veins. It'll hold the ponies, and it bends around whatever's hunting us." He paused, and then, because the guild's covenant was everything shared: "I can't see it. I can *hear* it. The weather tells me things. It always has. That's the part they didn't bind, because binding an ear is harder than binding a tongue."

Silence behind him. He counted heartbeats — four, five — waiting for the row, for the fear, for someone to remember that the last storm-caller whose weather-sense had been trusted was the one whose betrayal had coincided with the wards falling.

It was Juniper who spoke. "Can it find us a way around Hale, too?"

"Hale?" Beatrix turned. She was watching him steadily, no fear in her, only calculation — the guild's calculation, debts and routes and odds.

"Warden-Captain Hale," Ovist rumbled. "Crossed the border two days behind us with a full company and a warrant. Our friend in Gildenholt keeps ears in the Wardenhouse — Hale's orders are to bring you in, clerk. Alive, preferably. The law believes the exile broke the wards."

"Then the law is spending its coin badly," Beatrix said, "because I was balancing a ledger when the sky went out." He looked at Juniper. "Yes. I can hear where a column of forty men disturbs the air. Give me an hour and I'll give you Hale's position, his bearing, and probably what he had for supper."

"Do it," Ovist said. Then, quieter, only for Beatrix: "Eleven years you've been deaf on purpose. Must be loud as a market square in there now."

"It is," Beatrix admitted. "And it's all wrong. Ovist — the weather isn't just dead. It's *listening back*. Everything I hear, I think something else hears. Something that's using the whole sky as an ear."

"Then let it listen." Ovist turned back toward the column, raising his voice to carry. "Guild of the Gilded Scale! We move in ten minutes, northeast, single file, ropes on! Anyone complains, complain to the weather!"

Laughter, thin and ragged but real. The ponies were harnessed. The sledges creaked. And Beatrix stood a moment longer at the head of the line, feeling the great dead silence of the sky attend him like a congregation, and made himself one promise, quietly, in the privacy of his skull: *when I finally speak my name again, it will be because I choose to, and it will cost them, not me.*

Then the column moved out onto the warm-boned path, into the ridge country, into the white.

An hour later, kneeling in the lee of a ridge with his bare palm pressed flat to the snow, Beatrix found Hale.

Forty-one men, six miles south-southeast, closing at forced march. He could pick out the captain by the rhythm of his stride — longer than the others, metronome-steady, a man who had marched the same beat for twenty years. He could feel, threaded through the column's collective churn of breath and bootfall, something else: a hum, low and organized, radiating from the center of the formation. Metal, many pieces, tuned. Wardens carried resonance-chains for binding rogue weather-workers, and Beatrix had spent one long night of his trial wearing those chains, and his body had never forgotten their frequency. His skin crawled with the memory of it.

But there was a third thing in the air south of them, and it stopped Beatrix's heart.

Behind Hale's column — miles behind, moving faster than any march, keeping perfect pace with them like something pacing a carriage — the absence-thread from the first night had returned. The thing that wore weather's clothes. It wasn't following the guild. It was following *Hale*, hanging off the Warden column's flank the way a remora hangs off a shark, patient and hungry, and it was getting closer, and neither the captain nor his forty-one men had any idea it was there.

Beatrix rose. His legs were unsteady. Down the column, torches were being lit against the false dusk, and the little ring of light looked impossibly small against the dark that pressed in from every side.

"Ovist," he called, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, and he found he didn't care. "Ovist, we have a problem, and it isn't the one I promised you."

He was already reaching for the ledger in his mind, the old habit, the safe place — *entry: one debt, incoming, interest accruing* — when the northern sky, starless and black, began very faintly, from somewhere beyond the ridges ahead, to sing.
