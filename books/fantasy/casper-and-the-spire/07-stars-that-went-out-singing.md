## 7. Stars That Went Out Singing

Juniper had never trusted silence. Silence was the sound a lock made right before it told you the truth.

She'd learned that at seven years old, crouched in a chimney flue with her ear pressed to cold brick, listening for the tumblers of a house she wasn't supposed to be in. Locks talked. They groaned, they ticked, they sighed when a mechanism settled into place — and if you listened long enough, you could hear them *thinking*. The best locks, the expensive ones, were almost musical. She used to imagine that somewhere there was a great vault whose lock sang whole songs, and that one day she'd pick it just to hear the melody all the way through.

Now she lay on her back on the frozen borderlands, in a hollow the guild party had dug out of a snowdrift with their hands and a shovel Ovist swore he hadn't stolen (he had; Juniper had checked his coat), and she heard singing coming up through the ice.

It was not a good night for it. Nothing about the borderlands was good for anything. The stars had gone out eleven days ago, all at once, like lamps snuffed by a passing hand, and since then the world had been dying by slow degrees — frost creeping down from the border wards' broken line, rivers freezing from the top, birds falling mid-flight with their feathers rimed white. The Gilded Scale's wagon creaked under its load of salvaged goods, and the six of them huddled around a fire fed with charcoal because wood wouldn't burn anymore, not properly, not out here where the cold drank heat like a drunk drinks wine.

Beatrix couldn't sleep. She could tell by his breathing — too even, too controlled, the breathing of someone pretending. He lay three feet away with his ink-stained fingers laced over his chest, staring up through the gap in the snow-shelter's hide covering at the black sky, and Juniper knew that look. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

Ovist slept like a bear, which was to say loudly and with total commitment. Marta and the twins were curled together under the wagon's tarp. And Juniper — Juniper was listening.

At first she thought it was wind. Wind did things like this, sometimes: found a crack in a cliff or a gap between stones and made music of it, low and moaning. But the wind here had been the same flat dead moan for days. This was different. This came *up*. Through the ice beneath her bedroll, through the packed snow, through the bones of the world itself, faint as a voice heard through a wall.

She sat up so fast her breath fogged the air in a startled cloud.

"Juniper." Beatrix's voice, quiet. Not asleep after all, then. "What is it?"

"Shh." She held up a hand, pressed her palm flat against the ice. "Listen."

He listened. She watched him listen — watched his face do something complicated, something that started as skepticism and ended somewhere near fear. "I don't hear anything."

"You would if you'd spent your childhood inside other people's walls." She kept her palm against the ice. "There's singing down there. Under the frost. It's coming from—" she frowned, tilting her head, working it the way she worked a stubborn pin, "—*above*, actually. Or it was. It's caught. It comes down through the ice like water through a crack."

"Stars," Beatrix said. Not a question. His voice had gone strange.

"The ones that went out." Juniper pulled her blanket around her shoulders and knelt, both palms flat now, head bowed like a penitent. "They're not dead, Beatrix. Dead things don't sing. They're *caged*. I can hear them trying to get out."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a glacier calved with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable now that she'd found the thread of it, the song wound on.

*

They woke Ovist, because you woke Ovist when something mattered, and because he'd once told her, drunk on plum brandy after a job in Corvale, that the difference between a family and a crew was whether you woke each other for the bad news. "A crew lets you sleep," he'd said. "Family doesn't have the mercy."

He came awake the way he always did — instantly, completely, one hand already finding the iron poker he kept by his bedroll — and then he heard the song, or rather he watched Juniper and Beatrix press their ears to the ground like a pair of farmers divining water, and his face went through several expressions before settling on grim wonder.

"Well," he said. "That's either very good news or very bad news, and given our luck lately, I'll let you two fight over which."

"It's the stars," Juniper said. "Or whatever the stars are. They're alive down there — no, wait, that's wrong." She struggled with it, this thing she understood in her fingers and not her words. "They're alive up there, or they *were*, and something's got them. The song leaks. That's all a cage ever does, in the end. Every lock has a seam."

Beatrix had gone pale in the firelight. "The ledger entry," he said. "The one Ovist showed me. Someone traded away the covenant. What if the covenant isn't a wall? What if it's—" He stopped, visibly wrestling with the shape of the thought.

"A song," Juniper said. Because it was obvious, suddenly, the way the answer to a lock is obvious the moment your pick finds the last pin. "You said the Spire sits at the hinge of the worlds. You said the wards were woven from it. Wards are just locks, Beatrix, and locks are just—" she tapped her temple, "—music you can hear if you know how to listen. A covenant between realms would be a duet. One side sings, the other side answers, and the harmony is what holds the door."

"And if one side stops singing," Ovist said slowly, settling onto his haunches by the fire, poker across his knees, "the song falls apart. And everything built on the song falls with it."

"But who'd stop singing?" Juniper asked. "Why would anyone—"

She never finished, because the song changed.

It happened all at once, the way a held note breaks. One moment it was a single voice threading up through the ice, thin and lonely and achingly beautiful, a sound like silver stretched to its limit — and the next it was many voices, dozens, hundreds, rising together in a chord that made the ice hum under her knees and set her teeth buzzing. The frozen plain lit faintly blue from below, seams of light racing away toward the horizon like veins filling. For one impossible moment the whole borderland glowed like the inside of a lantern, and Juniper felt her heart crack open along a fault line she hadn't known was there, because it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard, and it was the sound of prisoners calling to each other through the walls of a jail.

Then, beneath the chorus, she heard the other thing. The thing that curdled the wonder in her chest into something cold and heavy.

Beneath the singing, there was a silence moving.

Not stillness — *movement*. A silence with edges, a silence that traveled, pressing down on the song the way a hand presses down on a mouth. Where it passed, voices cut off mid-phrase. Where it passed, the blue light guttered and died. It moved among the singers like a debt collector moving through a tavern, and the song bent around it, thinned, grew desperate.

"They're being hunted," Juniper whispered. "In there. Whatever's got them — it's in there with them, and it's hunting them while they sing."

Marta had come awake; so had the twins. Nobody spoke. Above them the sky hung black and starless, and below them the light pulsed and dimmed and pulsed again, and the song fought on, thinner every hour.

Ovist broke the silence first, because he always did. "Sleep in shifts," he said gruffly, but his voice wasn't steady. "We move at first light. Faster."

*

Juniper didn't sleep. Instead she did what she always did when a problem wouldn't yield: she took it apart piece by piece, lying in the dark with her eyes closed, running picks through an invisible lock.

Here was what she knew. Here was what she could feel in her fingertips, in the place where knowing lived before words got to it.

Locks were honest. That was the whole secret of her trade — everyone thought locks were about keeping people out, but really they were about *telling the truth*. A lock recorded every hand that had touched it. A lock remembered its maker, its purpose, its history of openings and refusals. When she picked an old lock, she wasn't defeating it; she was reading it, a diary written in brass and tension.

And the covenant was a lock — the oldest one, the hinge-lock, the master mechanism of two worlds. Which meant it remembered. Which meant the song bleeding up through the ice wasn't random. It was the covenant's memory, still turning over and over, still trying to complete the phrase it had been made to hold.

A duet. She turned the idea like a key. Two realms, singing to each other across the hinge. Each verse an exchange — a gift given here, a gift answered there. Season answering season. Tide answering moon. The mortal realm giving its small warm mortal things; the other realm giving back its strange bright otherness. And the Spire standing between them like the body of an instrument, turning one song into the other.

So why did it break?

Her name surfaced then, unbidden — the name she'd found on that old ledger page, the one tied to the covenant, the one that said she had been *collateral*, traded before she could walk. She'd told Beatrix about it two nights ago, around a fire much like this one, and he'd held her hand and said nothing wise at all, which was exactly the right thing. Collateral in a trade. Her small self, weighed against something enormous.

Maybe that was how the duet worked too. Maybe every verse required something held in trust. Maybe the covenant wasn't just a song — maybe it was a *ledger*, the largest one ever kept, and every voice in it a line item, and somewhere a bookkeeper had failed to balance the accounts.

One side stopped singing first. The thought arrived with the certainty of a picked pin dropping into place. It hadn't broken all at once. Duets didn't fail symmetrically — one voice faltered, and the other, waiting for an answer that never came, sang on alone into the silence until the silence ate it too. The song under the ice wasn't dying naturally. It was dying the way abandoned things died: still performing its half of the pattern, still faithful, still waiting.

*Still waiting,* she thought, and grief rose in her throat like floodwater, because she knew about waiting. She'd been a ledger entry once. A held thing. A promise somebody else made with her name on it. And she had spent her whole life since then learning locks precisely because locks were the only promises that could be undone — patiently, honestly, with the right tools and enough time.

The stars were waiting for an answer. And whoever was supposed to give it had stopped, years ago, decades, centuries — and the hunters in the dark were simply what moved in when a debt went unpaid.

She sat up. The fire had burned low. Beatrix was on watch, wrapped in his cloak at the shelter's mouth, and he turned when she stirred, and in the coals' red light his face looked carved out of something older than a clerk's.

"You figured it out," he said quietly.

"Some of it." She crawled over to sit beside him, shoulder to shoulder against the cold. Below them, muffled now, the song went on. "It's a duet, Beatrix. The covenant. Two realms singing to each other, and the song is the lock that keeps the door between them — not shut, but *true*. Aligned. And one side stopped singing first. The other half's been holding the phrase alone ever since, waiting for an answer." She hugged her knees. "That's why they're caged instead of dead. Cages are what you build for things you're afraid will leave. Something caught them because they kept singing, and the singing kept reminding it of what it had done."

Beatrix was silent a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "I keep thinking about my trial. About the questions they asked me. They wanted to know if I'd spoken to anything on the other side of the wards. As if the other realm were an enemy." He shook his head. "But it wasn't a wall, was it. It was a conversation. And we're the ones who went quiet."

"Not all of us." Juniper nodded down, at the ice, at the imprisoned light. "*They're* still singing. Whoever they are — the other realm's half of the choir, or the stars themselves, I don't know. They've been holding the verse alone this whole time, hoping we'd come back in." Her breath caught. "Oh. Oh, Beatrix. They're not caged because they're dangerous. They're caged because they're *faithful*. Something couldn't stand hearing them call to us anymore."

The words hung in the frozen air between them, and neither of them could take them back or soften them, because they were true, and truth out here had a weight like iron.

Below the ice, the chorus swelled once more — a great rising phrase, hundreds of voices, and for a heartbeat Juniper could almost make out words in it, or the shape of words, the ghost of a language that meant *come home, come home, we kept the door, we kept the door* — and then the moving silence swept over that corner of the deep and the voices cut out, and the darkness under the world rolled on.

Juniper found she was crying, silently, the tears freezing at the corners of her eyes. She wiped them with the heel of her hand and laughed, a wet, cracked sound.

"I'm sorry," she said. "It's just — I know what it's like. Being held for someone else's bargain. Waiting for someone to come pick the lock." She looked up at the starless sky, then down at the glowing seams in the ice, and something in her chest set itself, hard and bright, like a pin seating in a tumbler. "Someone has to answer them, Beatrix. Someone has to climb down into whatever dark they're locked in and turn the key from the inside. And I think—" she exhaled, steadying, "—I think that's why the ledger put my name in it. Not as collateral. As a *key*. Things that are traded aren't owned, not really. They're *entrusted*. Somebody marked me for this before I could walk, and I think it's time I found out why."

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment — this girl the guild had fished out of a chimney nineteen years ago, grown now into someone who could hear prisons singing — and then he did the thing that made him family and not merely a colleague: he believed her, immediately and without reservation.

"Then we'd better reach the Spire," he said. "Before the song runs out."

Behind them, Ovist stirred in his sleep and muttered something about ledgers. Ahead, past the black horizon, the road ran on toward the hinge of the world. And beneath the ice, fainter now, hoarser, the faithful voices sang on into the dark —

— until, just before dawn, Juniper heard something new threaded through them. A second melody, thin and ragged and unmistakably mortal, rising from the far south like an answer finally given.

She sat bolt upright, heart hammering.

"Beatrix," she whispered. "Something down there just heard us. And it's singing *back*."
