## 8. Hale

The warrant was three years old, and Warden-Captain Hale had read it every day since the wards fell.

He read it now by lamplight in the command tent pitched on the frozen road outside Emberfall, his breath fogging the parchment, his gloved thumb resting — as it always rested — on the seal at the bottom. The wax was cracked down its length. It had been cracked when they issued it, which the clerks swore meant nothing, but Hale had come to think of it as an omen he'd been too proud to read.

*By order of the Border Court of the Nine Marches: Beatrix Ashgrove, storm-caller, exiled under decree 1147, is to be apprehended on sight and returned to the Spire for judgment. Signed, Warden-Commander Vess. Countersigned—*

Countersigned. That word again. His thumb moved past it the way a tongue moves past a broken tooth.

Three days out from Emberfall, following the trail of a guild caravan through country that had forgotten what season it owed allegiance to, and still he could not stop reading the same lines over and over like a man checking a sum that would not balance.

Outside, the wind came off the dead borderlands with a sound he had no clean name for. Not howling. *Singing*, almost — a thin, tuneless keening through the ice-crusted pines, the way a wet finger circles a glass rim. His wardens said it was only weather. Hale had stood enough watches in enough bad places to know when the wind was complaining and when it was *saying something*. This one was saying something. He did not know what.

"Captain." Corporan Dree lifted the tent flap, letting in a knife of cold and a flurry of snow that fell upward for a moment before deciding, grudgingly, to fall down. "Scouts are back."

"And?"

"They found the wagon ruts. Guild wagons — Gilded Scale markings burned off, but you can see where they were. Headed north along the old salt road." Dree hesitated. He was a solid man, Dree, honest as a hammer, and honesty made him bad at delivering news he thought his captain wouldn't like. "There's something else. You should see it yourself."

Hale took the lamp and followed him out into the white dark.

* * *

The scouts had stopped at a frozen stream crossing, and they were standing around something in the ice the way men stand around a corpse — at a respectful distance, hats off without quite knowing why.

It was a warden's gauntlet. Standard issue, Nine Marches forge-mark, the leather stiff with frost. And it was not merely abandoned. It was set into the stream bank, upright, fingers spread, as if someone had planted it there deliberately — and packed snow around it, carefully, the way a gardener mulches a rosebush.

"That's Ferrow's," said Scout Liss. She was the youngest of them and had the sharpest eyes, which was a burden to everyone including her. "Marked it myself at the armory count. But Captain — Ferrow's detachment went west. Toward Coldmere. Nowhere near here."

"Then who put it here?"

Liss's jaw worked. "Nobody. That's what I keep coming back to, sir. There's no tracks leading to it. None leading away. The snow around it isn't disturbed — except—" she crouched, brushed the drift beside the gauntlet, and Hale felt the back of his neck tighten — "except this."

Beneath the snow, pressed into the ice itself, was a line of characters. Not letters in any script Hale knew, though he had served eleven years on the border and fancied himself conversant with most things written there. They looked less carved than *grown*, ridges of ice rising like scar tissue, and they caught the lamplight wrong — the light slid off them rather than into them.

"Ward-script?" asked Dree.

"No," Hale said. Ward-script was human work; it sat *on* things. This came up from underneath, the way frost comes up from the ground. "This is older than us."

He should have ordered it catalogued, sketched, left alone. That was procedure. Instead he found himself crouching, pulling off one glove, laying his bare palm against the ice.

Cold went through him like a bell being struck. Not temperature — *sound*. He heard, or remembered hearing, a voice speaking words he couldn't hold onto, and beneath the voice a second voice answering, and the two of them weaving together into something so orderly and so vast that his eyes stung and his throat closed. A duet. The ice was full of a duet, and one part of it had gone silent, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was—

Hale snatched his hand back.

"Captain?" Dree's hand was on his shoulder.

"It's nothing. Frost-touch. Happens on the marches." He pulled the glove on, flexing fingers that did not feel like his own. He had lied to his corporan smoothly, without effort, and the smoothness frightened him more than the ice had. Since Emberfall, lying had been getting easier. That was a thing he would have to look at later. Later, after the arrest, after the trial, after the ledger of his duty balanced again.

Except the ledger wasn't balancing. That was the whole trouble.

"Pack it up," he said. "We follow the salt road."

* * *

That night, in the tent, with the singing wind worrying the guy-lines, Hale laid out everything he knew about Beatrix Ashgrove on the camp table like a card player showing his hand to himself.

Not the warrant version. The other version.

Beatrix Ashgrove, before the exile: a boy from the river towns with lightning in his blood and no sense whatsoever about when to keep it hidden. Hale had been two years senior, both of them posted to the ward-garrisons, and for one long summer they had been — Hale searched for the word the way a man searches his pockets — *friends*. There had been a night on the wall at Coldmere when a storm came in off the fens and young Ashgrove had stood up on the parapet with his arms out and turned the worst of it aside from the town below, laughing, hair standing on end, absolutely alight, and Hale had watched him and thought: *this is what power looks like when it likes people.* When it serves.

And then the trial. And then the decree. And then three years of a name Hale did not say aloud, because the decree forbade even that — *the exile shall be nameless within the Marches* — and a man can't hate properly what he isn't allowed to name, so Hale had hated the empty space instead, and filled it with duty.

The official record said Ashgrove had broken covenant-law, consorted across the border, traded in things that crossed. The record was sealed. Hale had signed the escort order and never once been shown what was actually in those papers, and he had told himself for three years that this was proper — wardens enforce, judges judge — but the truth, which he kept in the drawer of himself where he kept uncomfortable truths, was that he had never wanted to know. Because if the record was unjust, then his signature was unjust, and if his signature was unjust, then everything since was a house built on a cracked sill.

And now the wards were fallen, and the stars were going out one by one like lamps pinched by a careful hand, and winter had come in a season that owned no winter, and every instinct Hale had said: *the exile did this. Of course he did. Who else? Who else but a storm-caller with a grudge and three years to nurse it?*

Every instinct. Except.

Except the gauntlet planted in the ice like a grave-marker for a man who wasn't dead. Except the script grown from underneath the world. Except the fact — and here Hale pressed his palms flat against the table until the wood bit — that the first star to go out had gone out *before* any report placed Ashgrove north of Emberfall. Before. He had checked. Three times. He had gone to the signal-archive himself, alone, at night, like a thief in his own garrison, because some part of him had needed to know whether the timeline cleared his quarry or condemned him, and the timeline had cleared him, and Hale had sat with that knowledge for two full days doing nothing with it, because doing something with it meant the whole structure came down.

Righteous certainty, he was discovering, was like ice over a stream. It held you up beautifully right up until it didn't, and the water underneath had been waiting the entire time.

Dree ducked in with tea. Hale wrapped his hands around the tin cup and let the heat hurt.

"Question, sir," Dree said, settling onto the supply crate that served him for a chair. "If we take this storm-caller alive — and we will, I don't doubt it, you've never lost a man you went after — what happens at the Spire? Judgment, the warrant says. But judgment needs a court, and last I heard the Border Court evacuated to Vail when the cold came. So who judges?"

"The Spire judges itself, historically."

"You believe that? Old stories. The tower reads your sins and weighs you."

"I believe in the warrant," Hale said, and heard, in his own voice, exactly how much of a creed that had become, and exactly how hollow a creed sounds when it has to say itself out loud.

Dree drank his tea. Then, carefully, because he was honest as a hammer and hammers know where they strike: "You knew him. Didn't you, Captain. Before."

The wind sang. Hale looked at the warrant, at the cracked seal, at the countersignature he had spent three years sliding past with his thumb.

"Yes," he said.

"And you're sure it was him? The wards?"

What Hale said was: "The evidence points to him."

What Hale did not say was that evidence pointed the way a weathervane points — wherever the strongest wind pushes it — and that the strongest wind in the Nine Marches, these three years, had been the court's need for someone to blame. What he did not say was that a third party leaves no convenient trail, and whoever had done this had left a trail *convenient as a carpet*. What he did not say was that he had once known a boy on a wall at Coldmere who used his storm to shield a town, and that men do not change poles that completely, and that if they did, Hale would want — would *need* — more than a weathervane before he dragged that man back to a judgment seat that no longer existed.

"Get some sleep, Corporan," Hale said instead. "Hard march tomorrow."

* * *

They caught the guild's trail properly on the fourth day, at a place called the Shepherd's Stair, where the salt road climbed through a pass between shoulders of black stone. And it was there, in the trampled snow of a campsite abandoned hours before, that righteous certainty finally went through the ice.

The guild had covered their traces well — Ovist Sable's people always did; Hale had chased smugglers long enough to respect craft — but covering traces means moving fast, and moving fast means leaving the small leavings. A button. A twist of wire. And half-buried in the ash of their cookfire, a scrap of paper, frozen, charred at one corner.

Hale knelt and worked it free with a knife-tip.

Ledger-paper. Fine stock, guild-watermarked. On it, in a clerk's neat hand, a single column of figures and beside them words:

*...third party confirmed. The trade predates the fall. Someone sold the covenant before the wards ever shook — countersigned at trial, gods forgive me, countersigned under coercion — must reach the Spire before the Unlit finishes eating—*

The rest was burned away.

Hale read it four times. Around him the wardens stamped and blew on their hands and made ready to march, and none of them saw their captain go very still in the snow, holding a dead man's-weight of paper, watching three years of certainty dissolve like sugar in warm water.

*Countersigned at trial.*

His thumb remembered the warrant. Moved, slowly, past the crack in the seal, down to the countersignature at the bottom — the one signature on all Nine Marches paperwork that no one ever questioned because it belonged to the Spire's own registry, the Spire's own authority, the *keeper's office*.

The keeper's office. Which had gone silent a year ago. Which had sent no reports, answered no queries, sealed its doors — and the court had called it eccentricity, and Hale had called it nothing at all, because questioning the Spire was above his pay and below his courage.

The wind sang through the pass. And for the first time Hale heard it plainly, the way you hear your own name spoken across a crowded room: the wind was not singing. It was *calling roll*. And somewhere ahead, in a tower half in each realm, something that had eaten a covenant and worn its signature like a stolen coat was keeping the list.

"Captain?" Dree, at his shoulder. "Orders?"

Hale folded the scrap of ledger paper with great care and put it inside his breastplate, over his heart, where he had carried the warrant for three years. Then he took the warrant out, and looked at it a long moment — at the cracked seal, at the name he was finally permitted, by his own conscience at least, to say aloud.

"Beatrix Ashgrove," he said quietly, testing the weight of it after the long silence. It weighed less than the empty space had. That seemed important somehow.

"Sir?"

"New orders." Hale stood, joints cracking in the cold, and looked north up the pass toward the Spire, toward whatever waited there. "We follow the guild. Not to arrest." He paused, feeling the sentence break against his teeth like thin ice, and finished it anyway. "To warn them. Something's been hunting *all* of us — and it signs its work with our names."

Behind him, unseen, the wind picked a gauntlet out of the frozen stream and carried it, spinning, up into a sky where another star had just begun, very faintly, to flicker.
