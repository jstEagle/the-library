## 10. What Was Traded

The doors of the Spire closed behind them with the sound of a book shutting.

Beatrix felt it in his teeth—that soft, final *thump*, as though some vast librarian had finished with them and set them on a shelf to be dealt with later. The light inside was not light as he understood it. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a pale gold suffusion without source, without shadow, and it made the great entry hall look less like architecture and more like the inside of an idea: walls that curved when he wasn't looking at them, stairs that seemed to remember being climbed by other feet, floors of stone so old it had gone smooth as sea glass.

Juniper's hand found his sleeve. She didn't grip it—she just rested two fingers there, the way she did when she wanted him to know she was counting him among the living. Ovist Sable stood a pace behind, arms folded over his barrel chest, his one good eye tracking every corner of the hall the way he tracked the vault at the Gilded Scale: cataloguing exits, weights, liabilities. Hale said nothing at all. The Warden-Captain's presence still sat between them like a splinter not yet drawn, but he had torn up the warrant three days ago on the white road, and Beatrix had watched the wind take the pieces, and there was no undoing that. There was only forward.

"Memory-wards," Beatrix said quietly, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be survived. "The keeper warned of them. In the last entry." He touched the folded page in his breast pocket—the spire-keeper's final ledger line, the plan that required *a living voice from each realm, sung together*, and the terrible, patient postscript that suggested the dead man had intended Beatrix all along. "They'll show us what happened here. All of it."

"And you'll want us watching," Ovist rumbled, "when they show yours."

It was not a question. Ovist Sable had built a family out of broken people precisely because broken people did not lie to each other about their breaking. He had known Beatrix four years, had never once asked what the exile decree said, and had never needed to.

Beatrix looked at the hall ahead. At its center stood a dais of black stone, and above it hung a sphere of woven silver—a thing like a cage and a lantern both, its bars etched with script that crawled slowly, patiently, like insects settling for the winter. Covenant-script. He could read it. That was the wound of the whole affair: only a storm-caller could read it, which meant the Spire had been waiting, all these years, for exactly the wrong man to walk through its doors.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "I'll want you watching. If I'm going to be unmade in front of my family, I'd rather it be in good light."

* * *

The memory-wards took them gently, which was somehow worse than if they had taken them by force.

One moment Beatrix stood on the dais with Juniper's fingers on his sleeve; the next, the hall was gone and he stood in the same space four hundred years younger, and the Spire was *alive*. Light poured through windows that no longer existed. Voices moved in the corridors—the spire-keepers, dozens of them, robed in grey-and-gold, carrying ledgers of their own. And through it all ran the song.

He had heard it before, in fragments, bleeding up through the ice on the road: the stars' slow chorus, the deep answering hum of the mortal world beneath it. But here, at the hinge, the duet was whole. It was not music so much as *agreement*—two vast voices trading warmth across the border like merchants trading grain, each verse a covenant renewed, each silence a promise kept. Beatrix's eyes stung. Four years he had lived half-voiced, ink-stained, hiding weather in his bones, and he had forgotten that magic could sound like this: not seized, but *given*.

"There." Juniper's voice came strange and doubled, from beside him and from somewhere in the past. She pointed.

At the center of the vision, a single figure stood apart from the singing keepers. He wore the grey-and-gold too, but worn thin, frayed at the hem, and where the others' faces were lifted toward the song, his was bowed toward something cupped in his hands—a darkness like a held breath, like a coin of absolute night that drank the light around it and gave nothing back.

"The keeper," Beatrix breathed. "The last entry's author."

"No," said Hale, and his voice was flat with a soldier's recognition. "*A* keeper. Look at the others. They don't see him. They sing *through* him."

He was right. The singers' eyes passed over the bowed figure as though he were furniture, or worse—as though he were already half-forgotten, a name struck from a roster. And Beatrix, who had spent four years learning what it was to be unseen in a crowded room, felt his stomach turn with a recognition he did not want.

The hungry thing in the keeper's hands pulsed. And the keeper spoke—not aloud, but in the way memories let you hear hearts—and his voice was dry paper, was a well going down and down:

*I kept the door for two hundred years. I sang the verses no one thanked me for. And now the realms grow careless, and the song grows thin, and I am the only one who remembers what happens if it stops.* A pause, long as a winter. *You are hungry. I am tired. Perhaps we can trade.*

"No," Beatrix whispered. "No, don't—"

But the memory did not care what he wanted. The covenant-script on the walls began to dim, verse by verse, as the keeper carried his little coin of night through the halls, and the trade was written in the air itself: *the pact between realms, surrendered; the border wall, unmade; the long winter, invited in.* And at the bottom of that terrible contract, glowing like fresh ink, waited two lines.

*Signed:* the spire-keeper.

*Countersigned:*—

The hall shuddered. The vision tore like wet paper, and Beatrix was falling through four centuries, and the last thing he saw before the present reasserted itself was the countersignature forming stroke by stroke—and it was forming in *his own hand*, the small cramped letters he used for ledger entries, the *x* he crossed with a flourish because his mother had taught him flourishes meant pride—

Then the dais. The pale gold light. Juniper's hand gripping his sleeve now, hard, both hands. Ovist's eye on him. Hale's sword half-drawn on instinct and sheathed again just as fast.

And floating above the black stone, projected in the Spire's cold script for all of them to read, the memory-ward's final record:

*Covenant trade countersigned by:* **BEATRIX ASHGROVE, STORM-CALLER OF THE ASHGROVE LINE.** *Witnessed under compaction ward, trial of the Border Court, year of the Grey Frost.*

Dated eleven years ago. Three years before his exile. Dated to the week of his trial.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The pale light hummed. Somewhere far below, through the Spire's roots, something vast turned over in its sleep—or its hunger—and the floor carried the vibration up into Beatrix's knees.

"That's not possible," Juniper said finally, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Bea. That's not—you weren't even *here*. You told me about your trial. You were in the capital, in the court cells, you—"

"I was in the court cells," Beatrix said. His own voice sounded distant to him, clerk-flat, the voice he used for bad entries in good ledgers. "For nine days. They brought me documents to sign. Confessions, waivers, transfers of custody. Eleven documents." He had counted them every night for eleven years; he did not need to count now. "I signed everything they put in front of me. I was nineteen, and they told me that if I refused, my sister would answer for my crimes instead."

Ovist shifted his weight. "Beatrix."

"They put the papers in a stack," Beatrix went on, because if he stopped now he would never start again, "and they put a compaction ward over the table—I felt it, I thought it was just to stop me burning them, I thought—" His hands were shaking. Ink-stained fingers, trembling like a boy's. "I thought the wards were *for me*. To keep me honest. I never thought—" He laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow, a sound with no floor under it. "I countersigned the death of the sky. They made me do it and I *signed neatly*."

"You didn't know," Juniper said fiercely. She had let go of his sleeve and taken his hand instead, both her small chapped palms around his one. "Bea, look at me. You didn't know. Nobody knew. The whole realm thought the wards fell last month. The Wardens hunted *you* for it because they're blind, but nobody—not the court, not the guild, not the keeper's own order—nobody knew the covenant died eleven years ago. How could you have known?"

"Because it's *my name* on it!" The words tore out of him louder than he meant, echoing off walls that curved to listen. He pulled his hand free and pressed it over his mouth, and for a moment he simply breathed, in and out, the way he did before calling weather, except there was no weather to call, only shame, and shame was not a thing magic could carry. When he spoke again it was quiet. "Everything I've done since. The hiding. The ledgers. Flinching every time a Warden's boots hit cobblestone. Living half a life because I believed—because I *chose* to believe that what they exiled me for was real. That I had earned it. And the whole time, the truest crime I ever committed, I committed at nineteen years old with a ward on the table and my sister's freedom in the balance, and I didn't even know I was doing it."

"That's not shame," Hale said.

Everyone turned. The Warden-Captain stood at the edge of the dais, arms rigid at his sides, and his face had the look of a man reading a document he wished would rewrite itself.

"That's not shame," he repeated. "That's coercion. I've served the Border Court twenty-two years. I know how those trials were run—we *all* knew, we just called it procedure. Compaction wards on signing tables. Stacks of documents with the dangerous ones buried in the middle. You think you're the only one who signed something at nineteen that followed you?" His jaw worked. "I arrested a storm-child once. Twelve years old. Called lightning into a granary to save her brother from a fire. They had her sign a transfer of custody that put her in the Warden corps as an indenture. I stood witness. I thought it was mercy, because the alternative was the axe." He swallowed. "Her name was Ashgrove too."

The hall went very still.

"My sister joined the Wardens?" Beatrix said. "She—Hale, she *told* me she went south to service. She never said—"

"She never said because the indenture forbade it, and because she hoped you'd never learn your trial had bought her chains along with your exile." Hale's voice dropped. "She counted the years to her release the way you counted documents. She talked about you. In the barracks, when the officers weren't listening. She kept a drawing of a guildhouse with a star over it that she'd made from your description. She said if you ever got your name back, she'd come find you at the Gilded Scale and demand a room."

"*Kept*," Juniper said softly. "Past tense."

Hale looked at her, and then away, and did not answer, and the silence answered for him.

Beatrix sat down on the edge of the dais. Not dramatically—he simply discovered that his legs had resigned their commission. The black stone was cold through his trousers. Above him, the caged sphere of silver ticked and crawled with its patient script, and beyond the Spire's high sealed windows the long winter pressed its face against the glass, and eleven years of flinched shadows and hidden weather rearranged themselves in his chest into a new and stranger shape.

Not innocence. He could not claim innocence; the signature was his, the hand was his, the neat little flourishing *x* was his. But context—context was a kind of mercy he had never allowed himself, and receiving it now, unasked, from a former enemy, felt less like absolution than like being handed a heavy coat in weather he'd insisted for years he wasn't cold in.

"The Unlit," he said at last. "The thing the keeper fed. It has my countersignature. My name is written into its bargain. Which means when we go down into the roots to face it—"

"It can claim you," Ovist said. No comfort in it, no varnish. Just the truth laid on the table like a merchant laying out scales. "It holds a lien on your name, lad. Same as any contract. That's why the memory-wards showed us this first. That's why the Spire let us in." The guildmaster lowered himself onto the dais beside Beatrix with a groan of old knees and settled like a mountain deciding to sit. "Question is what we do about it."

"You can't void a countersignature," Beatrix said numbly. "I've spent four years in trade law. A signature is a signature. The only instruments that override one are—"

He stopped.

Juniper tilted her head. "Are?"

"—a superior instrument," Beatrix said slowly, and something in his chest, some muscle clenuted tight since a courtroom eleven years ago, began very slightly to loosen. "A newer covenant. A stronger pact, signed by greater authority, superseding and extinguishing all prior claims." He looked up at the silver cage, at the crawling script, at the keeper's last entry folded warm against his heart. *A living voice from each realm, sung together.* "The keeper left us a plan to forge exactly that. And the Unlit knows it. That's why it sent its herald onto the road. Why it offered Juniper her deepest want. It isn't hunting us because we trespass." He rose, legs steadier than he deserved. "It's hunting us because we're carrying the one instrument in existence that can void its collateral."

"Then we'd best not lose the collateral," Ovist said, and patted Beatrix's shoulder once, hard, the guildmaster's version of an embrace.

But as they turned toward the stair that spiraled down into the Spire's roots—down toward the dark, the herald, the hunger—Beatrix paused, and looked back once at the memory-ward's record still hanging in the cold gold air.

*Countersigned by Beatrix Ashgrove.*

Eleven years he had run from that name. Now he walked toward the thing that owned a piece of it.

The stair breathed warm air up out of the deep, and in the warmth, faint as a thread, he heard singing.
