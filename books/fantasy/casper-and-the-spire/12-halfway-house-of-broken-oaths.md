## 12. Halfway House of Broken Oaths

The waystation announced itself the way old sins do — quietly, and all at once.

One moment the guild party was crossing a plain of grey glass ice where the horizon bent like a bad ledger line; the next, Beatrix stopped mid-stride and said, "There," in the voice he used for numbers that didn't add up, and there it was: a squat stone house with a slate roof and one lit window, sitting on nothing at all. No road led to it. No ground held it up. It simply stood, patient as an unpaid debt, in the middle of the white.

Ovist Sable knew it before Juniper could read the sign above the door. He had slept under that roof twice in his younger years, back when his beard was black and his conscience was merely dented rather than broken. The sign read THE HALFWAY HOUSE in letters that rearranged themselves depending on which side of the border you were standing on. From the mortal side, he remembered, it promised *Rest Ahead*. From the other side, it promised *Rest Behind*. Neither promise had ever been entirely true.

"Waystations are for travelers," said Wren, the guild's quartermaster, squinting against the wind. "We're not traveling. We're fleeing."

"Fleeing is just traveling with worse manners," Ovist said, and pushed open the door.

Inside was warmth of the grudging kind — a hearth that burned without wood, flames the colour of old parchment, and air that smelled faintly of every fire that had ever been lit in it. Benches lined the walls, worn smooth by decades of backs. A bar stood at the far end with no bartender behind it, only a brass bell and a slate that read: *Pour your own. Pay what you owe.* Ovist had always found that last instruction deeply unfair, since no one had ever been able to determine what they owed in such places until after they'd left.

Juniper went to the window first, as she always did, checking sightlines with a lockpick's habit of thinking about exits. Beatrix set down the satchel holding the dead starlight shard, which hummed its low unhappy note against the floorboards. Hale — who still travelled a careful ten paces behind them all, warrant torn but habits intact — took up a position near the door with the particular stiffness of a man who has not yet been told whether he's a guest or a prisoner.

And then the hearth flared green, and Ovist felt the bottom drop out of his evening.

He knew that flare. Every guild master learns to read fires the way clerks read ink, and a green flare meant a registered arrival: someone stepping into a waystation from elsewhere along the between-roads. Someone with guild business. Someone whose name would appear on the slate above the bar in chalk that wrote itself.

The chalk began to move.

*Vess Coriander,* it wrote, in a hand Ovist would have recognized in his grave. Then: *Tam Broadfoot.* Then: *Merrin Ash.*

Ovist did not sit down so much as fail to remain standing. Wren caught his elbow. "Master?"

"That's not possible," he said, which was a lie, because he could feel in his knees that it was possible, that it was in fact happening, that the three names on the slate belonged to people who had walked out of the Gilded Scale eleven years ago with the guild's best wagon, its second-best horses, and its entire eastern route — and with Ovist's trust folded neatly inside the wagon seat like a letter never sent.

"They abandoned you," Wren said carefully. "You told us."

"I did tell us." Ovist straightened his waistcoat with both hands, a ritual older than most of his regrets. "Which means I cannot possibly be surprised when they arrive. Surprise would suggest I doubted my own testimony, and a guild master must never doubt his own testimony aloud." He looked at the green fire, and the fire looked back, and somewhere between realms three sets of footsteps were already approaching through weather that should have killed them. "Get everyone settled. Put the kettle on, if the kettle exists today. And Juniper—"

But Juniper was already watching the door, her small face unreadable in the parchment light. "They're here," she said. "I can hear them arguing about who knocks first."

* * *

They knocked first, as it happened. All three, at once, in a pattern Ovist had taught them himself twenty years ago: two quick, one slow, one soft. *Safe to open. We are who we claim.* His own code, coming back to him through a blizzard of unlight, carried on the knuckles of people who had once sworn never to use it against him.

He opened the door.

Vess Coriander had gone grey at the temples and sharp everywhere else; she still dressed like a woman mid-heist even when the heist was clearly over. Tam Broadfoot had grown enormous and gentle-looking, though Ovist noted the axe on his back was new and the hands around its haft were not gentle at all. Merrin Ash looked exactly as she had the morning she left — young, freckled, insufferably calm — which was somehow the worst of the three, as if betrayal were a preservative.

"Ovist," Vess said.

"Vess." He stepped aside, because hospitality is a muscle and his had never fully atrophied. "Come in before the dark does. You can explain yourselves where it's warm."

"We don't need to explain ourselves," Merrin said, stamping frost off boots that were wearing the wrong century. "We need your table, your fire, and whatever wards this place still honors. The storm out there isn't weather. It asked me my name twice."

"It asks everyone," said Beatrix quietly, from his corner. "Refuse politely. It takes refusal personally, but it respects it."

The three newcomers froze as one, taking in the room properly now: the clerk with ink-stained fingers, the child lockpick, the warden with his badge turned inward, the quartermaster with her hand already resting on something heavy in her coat. Vess's eyes narrowed.

"You've collected strays," she said.

"I've collected a guild," Ovist corrected. "The difference is that mine stayed."

The silence that followed had weight and edges. Tam set down his pack with exaggerated care, the way big men do when they're trying very hard not to break anything, including the moment. "That's not fair, Ovi," he rumbled.

"No," Ovist agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Fairness left this establishment some years ago, along with my best wagon. Do have a seat."

* * *

The kettle, it turned out, existed today, and Juniper made tea for eleven with the fierce competence of someone who has decided that if adults insist on being difficult, she will at least ensure they are difficult warm. Ovist watched her work and felt the old guilt stir — the specific guilt of a man who collects broken children and then wonders, at 3 a.m., whether collecting them is a kindness or a hoarding.

Because that was the truth of him, and he knew it, and Vess had once known it too, and had said it to his face the night she left: *You gather us, Ovist. You mend us. But you never let us finish mending, because a finished person might walk away, and then what would you be?*

He had thrown a tankard at her. He missed. She hadn't.

Now she sat across his own hearth from him, warming her hands, while outside the windows the Unlit storm pressed its darkness against the glass like a beggar at a bakery window. Inside the storm, occasionally, shapes moved — long, patient, curious shapes that were not hunting so much as browsing. The waystation's walls held. Waystation walls always hold. That is their whole purpose and their whole cruelty: they keep out everything except the past.

"The eastern route," Ovist said at last, because the silence had grown teeth. "Eleven years ago. Walk me through it as if I'm a client and you're confessing a smuggling job. Start with what you took."

"Ovist—" Tam began.

"Start with what you took, Tam."

Vess answered for all of them, because she had always been the one with the stomach for ledgers. "The wagon. The horses. Three months of contacts in the river towns. And the guild seal — the copy, not the true one, we weren't thieves." She met his eyes. "We were worse than thieves. Thieves take things. We took the version of you that believed we'd come back."

"Why?" It came out quieter than he intended. Across the room, Beatrix glanced up from the shard of dead starlight he'd been nursing, and Juniper stopped pretending to sleep, and even Hale's shoulders lost a degree of their official rigidity. Eleven years of composed guild-master indifference, and the question came out the size of a boy's.

Merrin Ash, who had been silent, finally spoke. "Because you asked us to do something impossible and we were too cowardly to say no to your face. So we said it to your back instead."

Ovist waited.

"The covenant," Merrin said. "The night the first ward-stones started singing wrong — this was years ago, before any of this, before anyone knew the words 'Unlit' or 'Spire' mattered — you called a guild meeting and told us you'd found evidence the border pacts were fraying, and that the Gilded Scale was going to spend its whole reserve shoring them up. Every coin. Every favor. Years of work, for something no client would ever pay for." Her freckled face twisted. "We thought you'd gone soft. We thought you'd gone mad. We thought — I thought — that you loved the idea of being the man who saved the world more than you loved us."

"I did love it," Ovist admitted. The fire popped. "Both things. In that order, some days. That was my sin, if we're assigning them. Not that I gathered broken people. That I gathered them and then spent them."

"And ours," Vess said, "was that we spent ourselves somewhere easier and called it freedom." She reached into her coat — Wren tensed, Hale half-rose — and drew out not a weapon but a ledger. Old, water-swollen, its cover stamped with a guild mark that predated Ovist's mastership. "We've been running the eastern route as our own house for eleven years. Small jobs. Good ones, mostly. And for eleven years, every time the wards cracked a little more, every time a town went cold, we told ourselves it wasn't ours to fix." She set the ledger on the table between them. "Three weeks ago the stars started going out, and we ran out of self-deception. So we went looking for the Spire. And the between-roads brought us here, to you, which tells you everything about how badly we want to make amends, because if we wanted forgiveness we'd have found literally anyone else."

Ovist looked at the ledger for a long time.

Then he laughed — a short, cracked sound, startled out of him. "Do you know what my clerk keeps?" he said, nodding at Beatrix. "A ledger of impossible things. Trades that shouldn't exist. Stars sold warm. And now the man who broke my heart sends me a ledger of his own, wanting it balanced." He rubbed his eyes beneath his spectacles. "This guild. This ridiculous trade. Everything comes back to the books eventually."

"Is that a yes?" Tam asked hopefully.

"It's a truce," Ovist said. "Truces are yeses with conditions. The condition is this: you'll carry what we tell you to carry, when we reach the Spire, and you won't ask why. And when this is done — if it gets done — we'll sit down with the true guild seal and the good whisky and settle accounts properly. Reconciliation, not absolution. I can reconcile numbers, Vess. Absolution isn't mine to give. It may not be anyone's."

Vess studied him, and something in her face eased by a single degree — the look of a woman setting down a load she'd forgotten she was carrying. "Reconciliation without absolution," she repeated. "That's the most honest thing you've ever offered anyone."

"I've been practicing honesty lately. It's going terribly."

Across the room, Juniper snorted into her tea, and the sound did more to warm the room than the hearth ever had.

* * *

The storm peaked at what the waystation insisted was midnight, though midnight in a place between realms is more of a suggestion than a schedule. The dark at the windows thickened until the glass seemed to be the only thing keeping the world from being unmade, and against that glass, shapes leaned close and whispered offers. Ovist heard fragments as he made his rounds: promises of wealth to Wren, of a father returned to Juniper, of absolution — actual absolution — to Vess, who sat nearest the window with her jaw set like a woman refusing dessert.

To Ovist himself, the dark offered nothing at all. It simply watched him, the way an auditor watches a man balance books he knows are crooked.

He ended his rounds at the window beside Beatrix, who had not slept either. The clerk held the shard of dead starlight cupped in both palms like a bird with a broken wing. Its hum had changed tonight — deeper, sadder, almost rhythmic.

"It's singing," Beatrix murmured. "Or trying to. The caged stars. When the storm gets loud, the shard answers. Like..." He searched for the word.

"Like a duet missing its partner," Ovist said.

Beatrix looked up. "Yes. Exactly that."

Ovist gazed out at the pressing dark, at the shapes within it, and let himself think the thought he'd been circling for days: *the Unlit doesn't want to destroy the song. It wants to be sung to.* He filed it away next to his other dangerous hopes.

Then the hearth flared green again — hard, urgent, a fourth arrival forcing its way along the between-roads against the storm's current, which should not be possible. The chalk scratched itself across the slate in jagged strokes:

*Hale. Warden-Captain. Plus six.*

Ovist turned slowly. Hale had risen to his feet, badge outward now, face draining pale.

"Those are my wardens," Hale said. "I left them at the border crossing. They were alive four days ago."

The door began to knock. Two quick, one slow, one soft — Hale's own signal, taught to him by the law, arriving at a smuggler's door in the middle of the end of the world.

"Well," said Ovist Sable, reaching for the latch, "it appears the family is growing whether we like it or not."
