## 22. Casper and the Spire

Spring came to Thornhollow the way forgiveness came — slowly, and then all at once.

Beatrix noticed it first in the gutters. For two years the eaves of the Gilded Scale had worn beards of ice so long they scraped the windows like old men's fingers, and now they were dripping. Dripping. A sound so ordinary it made him stop mid-stride in the courtyard with a crate of ledger-paper in his arms and simply listen, as though rain on stone were a language he'd forgotten he knew. The snow was going grey and rotten at the edges of the roofs. Somewhere down the lane a cart wheel squealed through slush instead of crunching over frost, and the sound was so absurdly, gloriously mundane that Beatrix laughed out loud, alone in the yard, and had to pretend he'd been laughing at the cat.

The cat — a scarred orange tom who had appointed himself guildmaster of the woodpile — regarded him with the contempt such laughter deserved.

"Go on, judge me," Beatrix told him. "Everyone else does."

It was true enough. Half the town still crossed the street when they saw the Gilded Scale's sign, because everyone in Thornhollow knew by now what lived above the counting-house: an exiled storm-caller, reinstated by decree; a smuggler-found-family whose amnesty negotiations had taken four months, three bribes, and one incident involving Warden-Captain Hale threatening to arrest a magistrate's clerk for *insubordinate penmanship*; and, hovering over the weathervane every night like a lantern that had decided against hanging from anything so crude as a hook, a star.

Not a metaphorical star. An actual one. Small, warm, gold-edged, about the size of a dinner plate if you judged by eye, though Ovist swore it changed depending on how honest you were being with yourself. It had followed them home from the Spire like a dog that had been fed once, and it had never left.

Tonight, Beatrix had business with it.

He climbed to the flat roof above his office where the guild kept its pigeon coop and its rain barrels, and where, in defiance of every law of astronomy and several laws he suspected hadn't been written yet, the light was already waiting. It drifted down to meet him as he settled onto the cold planks beside the coop, settling into the air at his shoulder height with the patient hover of a moth that has found its lamp.

"Casper," Beatrix said.

The light brightened. It always did that now, at the name — a little flare, pleased as a child hearing its own birthday called. Three weeks ago it had been nameless, or rather it had had ten thousand names in ten thousand dead languages and none of them fit. Then Juniper, sprawled on the roof with her boots against the chimney, had looked up at it and said, quite casually, "You know what you remind me of? Casper. My brother's old dog. He used to sleep on my feet even when I didn't want him to."

And the star had flared so hard it lit the whole rooftop, and that had been that. Casper it was. Juniper had gone pink and pretended not to be moved. Ovist had entered it in the ledger under *Personnel, Non-Humanoid, Luminous* and then quietly corrected the entry to just *Casper*, because some things shouldn't be filed.

"You're early," Beatrix said. "Sun's not even properly down."

Casper bobbed, which Beatrix had come to read as *the sun is irrelevant*. Fair enough. He dug into his satchel and brought out what he'd carried up here: a ledger. Not the guild's great book — that lived downstairs behind three locks and Juniper's best trapwork. This one was small, bound in blue-grey calfskin, unruled. Blank. He had bought it six days ago from a stationer who had looked at the storm-caller buying a notebook and charged him double out of pure nervousness, and Beatrix had paid without complaint, because there were worse taxes than fear, and he had spent years paying them.

He set the ledger on his knee. Casper drifted closer, curious, casting warm light across the blank page — the first real light to touch it, Beatrix thought, and a fitting christening.

"I've been putting this off," he admitted. The star said nothing, but it did the thing it did when it was listening: it steadied, its glow smoothing out until it threw no shadows at all. "Eleven days since the amnesty was signed. Eleven days of Ovist asking, very casually, over breakfast, whether I'd 'got round to it yet.' Which is guildmaster for *write the damn thing before I write it for you.*"

He uncapped his ink — storm-grey, the same ink he'd always used, because a man could change everything about himself and still have opinions about pens. His fingers, ink-stained as ever, hovered over the first line.

Here was the trouble. He had spent his whole life keeping other people's ledgers. Impossible trades, impossible debts, the Gilded Scale's beautiful criminal arithmetic. And before that, the covenant itself had been a kind of ledger — obligations on both sides, a door with duties written into its hinges. He understood ledgers the way some men understood rivers: you could drown in them, or you could learn their currents. But no one had ever taught him how to keep one for himself. What did a man owe himself? Where did you enter it? Was there a column for the years lost, and if so, was the debt owed to him or by him?

"You were there," he said to Casper. "At the reforging. You heard what I promised."

He had promised, with five voices joined in the dark while the Unlit came flooding up the Spire's throat like a tide of forgetting — he had promised to stop hiding. That had been his surrender, his line in the song. Not his name. Never his name; he'd fought for that, and won. Just the exile. Just the flinching. *I will stand openly as what I am,* he had sung, terrified, and the covenant had caught the words and made them true, and the stars had relit, and winter had broken like a fever breaking, all sweat and shivering and then, unbelievably, morning.

So: the ledger. A storm-caller's account, opened honestly, in his own hand, in his own book, in his own name — Beatrix Ashgrove, written plainly at the top of the first page, no guild cipher, no false client name, nothing hidden in the margins.

His hand shook only a little as he wrote:

*Ledger of Beatrix Ashgrove. Opened in the spring of the Thaw, in the town of Thornhollow, beneath the light of a friend.*

Casper flared again. Show-off.

*Entry the first,* Beatrix wrote, and then sat for a long time, because the entries mattered, and he wanted them right.

He thought about debts. About what was owed, and to whom, and — harder — about what had been paid, and whether payment was ever truly finished, or whether love was a debt that kept renewing itself, season after season, and whether that was a terrible thing or the entire point of being alive.

He began.

*To Master Ovist Sable:* he wrote. *Owed: one life, pulled from rubble; one family, given freely to a boy who believed himself ungiveable-to; one lie told well and often enough to keep Warden patrols off my trail for seven years. Paid: in full. Balance remaining: none — except that he will not accept payment in full, because that is not how he keeps books, and I have finally learned not to argue.*

That was true, and it deserved recording. Ovist Sable collected broken people the way other men collected compasses — not because he needed them, but because he couldn't bear the thought of a direction no one was following. Beatrix had spent years assuming there would be a bill. There never was. There was only soup appearing when he worked too late, and a heavy hand on his shoulder after nightmares, and the words *you're home, lad* spoken gruffly enough to be deniable. You could not settle that account. You could only pass it forward. He suspected Ovist had learned that from someone too, and that somewhere in the world was an old woman or a dead friend or a stranger on a road who had once pulled a young smuggler out of his own particular rubble. Debts like that didn't balance. They compounded, joyfully, forever.

*To Juniper Quill:* he wrote, smiling despite himself. *Owed: my courage. She found her own name in a dead man's ledger — collateral, traded before she could walk — and she carried that wound to me herself, held it out in both hands, and chose trust anyway. She refused the Unlit on behalf of every soul in that waystation, aged nineteen, with her hands shaking. Paid: I intend to spend the rest of my life trying. Current status: ongoing, gladly.*

She was downstairs right now, he knew, re-locking the vault with a new mechanism of her own design, humming. She hummed the covenant-song sometimes now, without noticing. The first bars of it. Beatrix had never told her she did it. Some things you let people keep.

*To Warden-Captain Hale:* — and here his pen paused longer. — *Owed: a warrant torn in half at the worst moment of my life, which was also, it turns out, one of the best. Owed further: patience, for a man learning that duty can be chosen rather than imposed. Paid: testimony given freely before the magistrates, at cost to his standing, to overturn my exile. Status: he says we are even. Hale is wrong about many things. He is wrong about this most stubbornly. I will buy him dinner until he surrenders.*

Hale had come to the amnesty signing in full dress uniform and stood at parade rest throughout, as though forgiving Beatrix Ashgrove were a formal military operation requiring precision and polish. Afterward, in the corridor, he had shaken Beatrix's hand exactly once, briefly, and said, "Your weather-sense saved my wardens' lives. It would have been *wasteful* to let them hang you." And then he had left, and Beatrix had stood there understanding that in Hale's country, that was a speech of tremendous warmth, practically an embrace, and he had laughed until Ovist came looking for him.

*To the realms themselves,* he wrote next, more slowly. *Owed: a betrayal, coerced, mine — my signature on the trade that broke the world. Paid: a new covenant, built on willing voices. Status: sealed. Witnessed. Growing.*

Because that was the thing about the door they had made, the thing the old spire-keeper had known and the Unlit had forgotten: a covenant was not a wall, and it was not finished. It was a living account between two realms, renewed at every crossing, every trade, every traveler who passed through with honest intent. The border wards were rebuilt now — softer than before, woven with give, with obligation on both sides instead of suspicion. Fae folk had already begun coming through at the crossings, tentative as birds after a hard winter. Last week a river-spirit had purchased, from the Gilded Scale, one (1) iron kettle, and paid in songs, and Juniper had recorded the exchange rate in the great ledger with a perfectly straight face while her eyes shone like coins.

Impossible things, becoming possible, one entry at a time.

Beatrix turned back to the beginning of the page, to the heading, and added one final line beneath his name — the line he had been circling all evening, the reason he'd climbed to the roof with a blank book and a bottle of good ink and a star for a witness:

*Debts owed to family: all marked paid. Note: family disputes this assessment. Note to note: family is correct. Ledger to remain open indefinitely.*

He blew on the ink. Casper leaned in over the page, warm light drying it faster, and for a moment Beatrix watched the words gleam — his words, his book, his name at the top of it, unhidden, unhunted, home.

"There," he said quietly. "First entry of my own. Only took me thirty-one years and one apocalypse."

Casper did a slow, delighted spiral around his head, scattering light like a shaken lantern, and Beatrix laughed and batted at him gently. "Yes, all right. Don't gloat."

* * *

Downstairs, the Gilded Scale was doing what it did best, which was pretending to be ordinary.

The common room smelled of stew and lamp oil and the particular sweetness of Ovist's pipe. Juniper had abandoned the vault and was teaching Merrit, the guild's newest hire — a gaunt borderlands boy they'd smuggled out of the dying country along with forty others during the worst of the winter — how to pick a lock using the practice set and a great deal of profanity. Merrit was hopeless and happy. Half the refugees they'd brought over had stayed in Thornhollow; a few had asked to work for the guild, and Ovist, who had never in his life turned away a useful pair of hands attached to a hungry heart, had hired all of them and complained about payroll with visible delight.

Ovist himself sat at the corner table with the great ledger open before him, and beside it — Beatrix saw with a jolt of something warm in his chest — a second book. Smaller. Newer. Its cover read, in Ovist's blocky, unapologetic handwriting: *Possible Things.*

"What's the first entry?" Beatrix asked, sliding onto the bench across from him.

Ovist tapped the page with one thick finger. "*One kettle, iron, sold to a river. Payment rendered in song.*" He looked up, and his craggy face did the thing it did instead of smiling, which was somehow better than smiling. "Market's expanding, lad. Somebody has to keep ahead of it."

"Somebody with a star over his roof," Juniper called from the lockpicking table, without looking up. "Which reminds me — Casper's been sitting over the east window again all evening. Blocking half the lamplight. Someone should tell him he doesn't pay rent."

"He pays rent," Beatrix said.

"In what?"

"Warmth." He said it simply, and Juniper stopped fiddling with the practice lock and looked at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her grin and go back to work.

Because it was true, and everyone in the room knew it was true, and no one found it strange anymore. The star stayed. Of all the lights that had gone out over the mortal realm and been freed again, Casper was the only one who had, upon regaining the whole singing sky, looked down at the small brown rooftops of Thornhollow and chosen a gutter-side guildhouse full of criminals and clerks. He hung over the east window on cold nights. He warmed the herb boxes on the sill so that Ovist could grow rosemary in defiance of both climate and botany. When Juniper had nightmares — and she still did, though fewer now — Casper pressed close to the glass near her room until the dark felt less like the Unlit and more like the ordinary dark, the honest kind, the kind you could sleep in.

A star, keeping watch over a counting-house. The scholars would write papers about it someday. Let them. Beatrix already knew the answer, the simple one, the one that would never make it into any academic folio: Casper had heard five voices sing together in the dark at the hinge of the world, and he had liked the song, and he wanted to stay near the singers. That was all. That was everything.

Later — much later, after the lamps were trimmed and Merrit had managed his first lock with a whoop and Juniper had gone up whistling and Ovist had banked the fire and clapped Beatrix once on the shoulder on his way past, wordless, sufficient — Beatrix climbed back up to the roof one last time.

The thaw was dripping everywhere, a hundred small silver sounds in the dark. To the north, past the hills, he could feel the border crossing breathing — the new door, the soft wards, the faint pulse of the other realm coming back to itself like blood returning to a numb limb. His weather-sense spread out over it all, unhidden now, unfrowned-upon, and the night air tasted of wet earth and green things deciding to try again.

Casper drifted up to meet him.

"Not tonight," Beatrix said. "Just wanted to look at you."

The star settled onto his shoulder — actually onto his shoulder, warm as a loaf, weightless as a rumor, which was new and which Beatrix elected not to examine too closely lest it stop. Together they looked out over Thornhollow: the crooked lanes, the smoking chimneys, the far lamplit window of the inn where Hale was staying, the dark bulk of the guildhouse below them holding its sleeping family like a cupped hand.

Beatrix took out the new ledger, opened it to the second page, and wrote one line more — not an entry, just a note to whoever came after, because ledgers outlived their keepers and he had learned that the hard way:

*If you are reading this: the world broke, and we mended it, and it can be done. It was not done by kings, or by bloodlines, or by anyone the histories would have picked. It was done by smugglers and lockpicks and lawmen who changed their minds, and one coward who stopped running. Start where you are. Sing with whoever answers.*

He closed the book. Above the east window, Casper glowed steady and gold, and somewhere in the Spire-far distance a caged star's sister sang back across the new-made door, and the ice went on melting, drop by drop by drop, like a long-held breath finally, finally let go.

Beatrix Ashgrove sat on the roof of his home, in the warmth of his friend's light, and stayed.
