## 3. What the Crown Pays For

The counting room smelled of tallow, iron filings, and the faint sweetness of old vellum—the smell of money that had been handled too long by too many careful hands. Kestrel Ashgrove sat at the third desk from the window, where the winter light came thin and gray through leaded glass, and did what she did best: she made impossible things look ordinary.

In the ledger before her, a fisherman in the Saltrow district had traded eleven years of his life for a son's recovery from fever. The entry read, in her own small, precise hand: *Received: 11 yrs., male, weathered. Disbursed: one convalescence, complete.* Beside it, a widow had sold the memory of her wedding day to cover her husband's debts, and somewhere in the vault below, that day sat folded into a stoppered glass like a firefly. Kestrel had stopped wondering, years ago, what became of such things after they were shelved. Wondering was not in her job description. Balancing was.

She drew a line beneath the column. The sums agreed. They always agreed. That was the horror of the Thorn Guild that no outsider ever understood—not cruelty, not chaos, but perfect, immaculate balance. Every debt entered. Every debt collected. Nothing lost, nothing gained. The guild did not steal; it only ever *traded*, and its books closed each night with the smug neatness of a mouth that had never once told the truth.

"Your margins are drifting again." Veyra's voice came from behind her left shoulder, where it always came from, soft as dust settling. "Third desk. You lean."

"I don't lean."

"You lean toward the window. As if you'd like to be elsewhere." Her mentor's hand appeared on the edge of the ledger—long fingers, ink-stained at the second knuckle of the right hand, a mark Kestrel had known since childhood and had never once asked about. Veyra turned back two pages without asking permission, because permission was a concept the guild extended to no one, least of all its own. "This fisherman. You recorded the years as 'weathered.' Write 'coarsened.' Weathered suggests loss by nature. Coarsened suggests loss by choice. We are not responsible for nature, Kestrel. We are only ever responsible for choice."

"Yes, mistress."

"And the child?"

Kestrel's pen paused. Down the hall, in the room they had given him—the small room off the ledger vault, warmest in the building, because even Veyra's mercy had a filing system—Casper was sleeping. He slept like something that had once run very far and intended to run farther when it woke. Three nights now. He ate what she gave him. He watched everything. And twice, half-dreaming, he had spoken in that language no scribe could place, the syllables falling out of his mouth like water finding old channels.

"He sleeps," she said.

"Mm." Veyra straightened. For a moment she stood at the window herself, looking down at the street, where the lamplighter was making his rounds and the snow was beginning again, fine as sifted flour. "Lock the strongbox at closing. And Kestrel—if he speaks in his sleep again, write down what he says. Exactly. Before you forget how it sounded."

"Why would I forget how it—"

But Veyra had already moved on, gray skirts whispering against the floorboards, and Kestrel sat with the unfinished question cooling in her throat like a swallowed stone. She looked down at the ledger. In the margin, unbidden, her pen had drawn a small spiral—a glyph she didn't know, in a script she shouldn't recognize, in a hand that was almost but not quite her own.

She scratched it out. The scratching took longer than it should have.

* * *

The knock came at the eleventh hour, which was to say: an hour after closing, when honest business had been locked away and dishonest business was welcome.

Kestrel answered it herself, because Veyra was upstairs and the night clerk was drunk and Casper was asleep, and because some part of her—the part that leaned toward windows—was curious about who knocked at the Thorn Guild's door in a snowstorm wearing a courtier's cloak worth more than the door itself.

Lord Aldous Renwick, Undersecretary to the Privy Seal, stood on the step with snow in his powdered hair and terror in his face, and he held a velvet bag against his chest the way a man holds a wounded bird.

"You're open," he said. It wasn't a question so much as a prayer.

"We're closed."

"I'll pay the closing fee. Whatever it is. Triple it." His eyes went past her shoulder, hunting the dark interior. "I need to sell something tonight. Tonight, do you understand? By morning it will be—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter, the way men speak when they have rehearsed a sentence until it has worn smooth. "I need to sell something before I can be made to forget I wanted to."

That, at least, was standard guild grammar. Kestrel stepped aside.

He sat where she put him, at the fourth desk, under the lamp, and he unwrapped the velvet bag with hands that shook so badly the drawstring fought him. Inside was a memory—that was how they were stored for sale, in a kind of seeing, a pressed flower of light and sensation that only the trained could handle without bruising. Even Kestrel, who had grown up among these, felt her breath catch. It was large. Larger than any single memory she'd seen outside the vault's deepest shelves. It shimmered with gold light and trumpets and the weight of a crowd's roar.

"What is it?" she asked, though she already knew. She could feel it. Some memories announce themselves.

"One memory of the king's coronation." Lord Renwick said it fast, like pulling a tooth. "Not mine. Given to me. I was—I was entrusted with certain things, and now certain people wish them returned, and I find I would rather sell them to strangers than return them to—" He stopped again. Looked at her with naked pleading. "You don't ask questions here. That's why I came here. Everyone knows the Thorn Guild doesn't ask questions."

"We don't," Kestrel said. "We only record answers."

She opened the intake ledger. Date. Seller. Description. Provenance—optional, rarely given, almost never this interesting. Her pen hovered over the provenance line.

"The coronation," she said. "Of King Aldric."

"Eleven years past, yes."

Eleven years. Something cold walked down Kestrel's spine on small feet. Eleven years ago. Ten years of purchases—all signed with the royal seal. The thought arrived whole, uninvited, the way the worst thoughts always did, and she had no idea where it had come from, except that it felt less like thinking and more like remembering something she'd never been told.

"Provenance?" she asked.

Renwick's laugh came out broken. "Girl, if I told you the provenance, you'd have me killed before I finished the sentence, and then you'd have the memory anyway. Sell it or don't. But weigh it fairly. It's—it's *heavy*. Can't you feel how heavy it is? There are pieces of him in there. Real pieces. The moment he knelt. The sound the crown made coming down. What he thought, in the instant before the anointing, when he still had a chance to refuse."

Kestrel weighed it. The scales sang. It was heavy—it was one of the heaviest things she had ever handled—and she wrote a figure beside it that made Renwick close his eyes and nod like a man accepting absolution.

Then she turned to the archive, because intake required cross-referencing, and cross-referencing required the master catalogue, and the master catalogue lived in the long cabinet against the north wall, ten years deep.

She began, innocently, with the seal.

* * *

The royal seal appeared in the purchase ledgers forty-one times.

Kestrel found the first one at the bottom of the oldest volume, dated nine days after the coronation: *Received: one capacity for grief, royal grade, unused. Payment: rendered in advance, terms perpetual.* Signed with the impress of the great seal of Aldric IV, pressed deep and clean, the wax still described in the margin as "warm."

Nine days after his crowning, the king had sold his capacity for grief.

She turned pages. The entries marched forward through the years with the terrible patience of erosion. A season of joy, sold in the third year. His mother's face, sold in the fifth—he had sold the memory of his own mother's face, and the buyer's line was blank, which meant internal acquisition, which meant the guild kept it, which meant it might still sit on a shelf downstairs wrapped in wool and darkness. His dreams, sold in the sixth year, *all future dreams, in perpetuity*. His fear of dying, seventh year. His love of the sea, eighth year, and Kestrel remembered reading, in a gazette, that the king had once been famous for sailing his own ketch in all weathers, and that he had not been to the coast in four years.

Forty-one entries. Forty-one pieces. Each one signed with the royal seal. Each one buying back a piece of Aldric himself.

And every payment line read the same way: *rendered in advance, terms perpetual.*

Which meant the king had never received anything for any of it. Which meant these weren't trades at all. Trades went both ways. This was—

This was harvest.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere above her, floorboards creaked under Veyra's measured steps. Down the hall, Casper slept, and Kestrel thought suddenly, violently, of the glyphs in the sealed volume, the ones she had copied by candlelight with her heart hammering, the phrase she had transcribed three times to be sure: *the king hollowed by installments.*

She had thought it was prophecy. Old books loved prophecy. But it wasn't prophecy. It was *bookkeeping*. It was a forecast, an audit, a projection of revenue across a ten-year term, written in the First Tongue before the First Tongue had walked through their door speaking it. The guild hadn't stumbled upon the king's dismantling. The guild had scheduled it.

Her small honest work—dates, descriptions, fair weights, tidy columns—her work was the mortar between the stones. Every entry she had ever balanced had helped make the theft invisible. You cannot rob a kingdom openly. But you can rob it in installments, in red-inked silence, one sold grief at a time, and no one will ever notice because no one audits a soul.

Kestrel gripped the edge of the cabinet and breathed.

The vertigo came anyway. It came the way she imagined drowning must come for a woman who had always believed she could swim: not as surprise but as betrayal. Ten years she had sat at the third desk from the window, proud of her precision, proud of her honesty, proud of being the one person in the guild whose columns always closed. And all that time she had been a clerk in the employ of a slow regicide, sharpening the pencil that signed the death warrant, keeping beautiful, immaculate records of a man being eaten alive in an office two miles away.

He was nearly whole now. She could see it in the arithmetic. The entries had accelerated in the last year—fourteen purchases in twelve months, where earlier years had held three or four. Something was finishing its meal. Something was gathering the last courses.

And the final entry, the forty-first, dated six weeks ago: *Received: one name, true, first-given. Payment: pending.*

Pending. The only pending line in ten years of perpetual prepayment. The guild owed the king something, and the king had not yet collected, and Kestrel stared at those two words until they blurred, because pending meant waiting, and waiting meant the transaction wasn't finished, and unfinished transactions were the only thing in the world that frightened the Thorn Guild.

Behind her, the door opened without a knock.

Veyra stood in the archive doorway, gray as the snow beyond the windows, and her eyes went from Kestrel's face to the open ledger, to the royal seal pressed forty-one times into the record like wounds, to the last line, *payment pending*, and something crossed her expression that Kestrel had never seen there in twenty years.

It looked almost like grief.

"You should have stopped at the coronation memory," Veyra said quietly.

"Mistress—"

"Close the book, Kestrel."

But Kestrel's hand did not move, and Veyra saw that it did not move, and in the humming silence of the archive, with the storm ticking against the glass and a child asleep down the hall who spoke the language of bindings, both women understood that the book, once truly opened, could never again be balanced.

"Who is the payment for?" Kestrel asked. "What does the crown owe, and to whom?"

Veyra closed her eyes. When she opened them, all the grief had been filed away, and there was only the counting room voice, gentle and terrible: "Go to bed, little bird. Some entries aren't yours to read."

Down the hall, in the warm dark of the vault room, Casper began to talk in his sleep—and this time, Kestrel heard it through the walls, and this time she recognized the rhythm of it, because she had copied those same syllables from the sealed volume not three nights past.

He was reciting the forty-first entry. Word for word.
