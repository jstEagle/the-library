## 5. The Hollow Court

*The boy's words come to you secondhand, and he knows it, and it does not trouble him. He has decided that Kestrel Ashgrove's voice is a coat he wears when he needs to be understood by the world; underneath, his own thoughts run in the old tongue like water under ice. What follows is what she made of him that day — her grammar laid over his bones.*

---

The palace smelled wrong to Casper.

Not bad. Wrong, the way a word is wrong when someone pronounces it with the accent of a country that no longer exists. He had lived three months among the scribes' ink and Veyra's cold counting rooms, and he had learned that stone could hold many smells: tallow, mouse, fear dried into corners. The Thorn Guild's halls smelled of all of those at once. But the palace smelled of *freshness over rot* — lilies set out every morning to cover something sweeter and darker beneath, honey cakes baked while the kitchens whispered, beeswax polished over wood that was slowly going grey from the inside.

He told Kestrel this in the old tongue, quietly, as they waited on a bench outside the audience chamber among six other petitioners who did not look at them because clerks were furniture.

Kestrel's hand stilled on the forged auditor's pass in her lap. "Freshness over rot," she repeated under her breath, translating it twice more before she seemed satisfied with the shape of it. "You have a poet's cruelty, little one."

*I have a nose,* he answered. *Poets are people with nowhere to put what they see.*

She laughed — a small, startled sound, quickly folded away, but real — and Casper counted it, as he counted everything now. Three laughs since the vault. Eleven times she had said his name and meant it. One night she had sat by his pallet until dawn copying glyphs from the sealed volume while he slept, and when he woke she had not pretended otherwise, which was its own kind of gift.

The chamber doors opened. A herald with a voice like wet slate announced: "The Office of Royal Audit."

They walked in, and Casper saw the king.

---

He had expected a monster. Kestrel had not told him to expect one, but the sealed book had told him things in his sleep — *hollowed by installments*, the glyphs said, and the glyphs had shown him pictures behind his eyelids: a man-shaped jar being emptied spoonful by spoonful, a crown like a lid screwed down tight. He had dreamed of teeth inside the throne. He had woken with the taste of iron and been ashamed of it, though he could not have said why.

But the man on the throne was only tired.

King Aldric sat slumped the way old men slump in chapels, his crown sitting slightly askew as if even it had grown bored of holding him together. He was broad once — Casper could see the ghost of the shoulders in the way the royal mantle hung — but now he looked poured rather than seated, a body kept upright by ceremony alone. His hands lay open on the arms of the throne, palms up, the way beggars hold them.

And when his eyes found Casper across the marble floor, they changed.

It was not recognition exactly. Casper knew recognition; he had seen it on Kestrel's face the first time he spoke aloud in the vault and she had dropped her pen. This was something older and stranger. It was the look of a man hearing, after ten years of silence, a song he had known before he had words for songs.

"Come here," the king said. And then, before the herald could bristle or Kestrel could bow deeply enough, Aldric spoke again — haltingly, horribly, beautifully — in the First Tongue.

*Little key. They let you through the door?*

Casper stopped walking.

The courtiers murmured. The queen, seated in a lesser chair beside the dais, went very still, her fan frozen half-open like a bird deciding whether to fly. Kestrel's hand pressed against Casper's back — gently, but he felt her heartbeat through her palm, fast as a trapped moth.

The king's pronunciation was terrible. It was the pronunciation of a man who had learned the language in dreams, where meaning arrives whole and the mouth must reconstruct it afterward. But it was the language. Unmistakably, impossibly, it was the language that lived in the sealed book and under Casper's tongue, the language the guild had spent a century pretending was only a merchant's cipher.

*Who taught you?* Casper asked him, in turn, and watched the whole room flinch at sounds it did not know.

"No one," Aldric said, and now he had slipped back into the common tongue as if the other burned, or as if speaking it too long might call something. He smiled, and the smile was warm and ruined at once. "No one taught me. I dreamed it. Before—" His hands closed, palms down now, gripping the throne's arms. "Before I was fitted. Before the coronation. There were dreams, and in the dreams there was a door, and the door was locked, and I knew the words that would open it. I have never known why. Until today." His eyes went to Casper again, and there was such naked hope in them that Casper had to look at the floor. "Until you walked in wearing my language like a coat."

Kestrel stepped forward, clerk-correct, voice pitched flat as ledger paper. "Your Majesty honors the Office. We've come regarding discrepancies in the tenth-year accounts—"

"Yes," the king said softly. "Yes, I expect you have." And then, quieter, in the old tongue, so that only Casper and Kestrel could possibly have caught it — though Casper saw the queen's knuckles whiten around her fan: *Ask her to stay. My wife will find you. Ask her nothing. She will tell you everything.*

The audience lasted eleven minutes more. Casper counted. It was theater — Kestrel reciting invented sums, ministers nodding at invented errors, the king agreeing to invented corrections with the pleasant vacancy of a man following a conversation through a wall. Twice his gaze drifted to the high windows, and twice it came back with visible effort, like a swimmer surfacing. Once, when a minister mentioned the harvest festival, the king began, quite sincerely, to weep — two clean lines down his cheeks, his expression unchanged, pleasant, attentive — and wiped them away without noticing he had done it.

Casper understood, then, what freshness-over-rot meant. The lilies, the honey cakes, the polish: a court performing wellness around a king who cried without knowing and forgot by the hour, and called the performance loyalty because calling it grief would require someone to be blamed.

*He is mostly gone,* Casper told Kestrel as they withdrew, bowing. *What is left is standing very straight so no one sees how little is behind it.*

"I know," Kestrel whispered back. Her face was pale. "Gods help me, I know. I balance his losses every quarter-day. I just never knew what column they came from."

---

The queen found them at dusk, in the clerks' annex where visiting auditors were housed — a narrow room smelling of lamp oil and other people's arithmetic. She came without guards, wrapped in a plain grey cloak that fooled no one, because queens cannot actually disguise themselves; the cloak simply announced that she wished to pretend, and courtesy required everyone to play along.

She dismissed the attendant at the door with a flick of two fingers. Then she stood a moment in the middle of the room, gathering herself the way one gathers spilled grain, and Casper saw that she was younger than her posture and more frightened than either.

"You're not an auditor," she said to Kestrel.

"No, Majesty."

"You're the guild's girl. Veyra Thorn's shadow. I've seen your name in margins." She said it without accusation, almost without interest, as if identity itself had become a minor administrative matter compared to the thing she carried. "I don't care. I'll pay whatever the guild charges and never speak of how I learned to ask. I only want to know—" Her voice cracked, and she mastered it the way she presumably mastered everything, publicly, at cost. "I want to know what's happening to my husband."

Kestrel was quiet a long moment. Casper watched her decide — he could see the scales working behind her eyes, the same scales that lived in every line of every ledger she'd ever touched. Duty said lie. Something else said—

"He is being emptied," Kestrel said finally. "In pieces. Bought and sold, piece by piece, for years. I don't yet know by whom. I know the purchases carry the royal seal, which means either the king consented to his own unmaking or someone has been signing with his hand. Either way, Majesty, the accounts are not discrepancies. They are the wound itself."

The queen sat down. Not gracefully — she simply sat, on the edge of the clerks' hard bench, cloak pooling, hands loose in her lap, and for a moment she was not a queen at all but a wife in a small room with bad light, and the transformation was more devastating than any crown.

"He weeps at nothing," she said. "At sunlight on the floor. At the smell of bread. Last week he wept for forty minutes over a sparrow that struck the window, and when I asked him what was wrong he said, 'I feel as if I'm grieving something I haven't lost yet.' And he forgets. Our anniversary. His mother's face. The names of rivers he sailed as a boy. But he remembers the dreams. He remembers the locked door. Sometimes at night he stands at our bedroom window and says the strange words over and over, softly, like a prayer, like a man practicing for something." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terribly clear. "Is he dying?"

"No," Casper said.

Both women turned to him. He had not spoken in their tongue; he had spoken in his own, and Kestrel translated it a heartbeat later, her voice roughened: "He says no. Your husband isn't dying. Dying takes all of you at once. This is—" she listened to him a moment longer, frowning, unwilling. "This is being harvested. Slowly enough that the field still looks green."

The queen made a sound Casper had never heard a person make — small, involuntary, like a hinge — and pressed her hand flat against her sternum as if holding a door shut from the inside.

"The guild can stop it," Kestrel said. "Whatever the guild started, the guild can end. That's the nature of ledgers, Majesty. Every entry can be reversed. It only costs."

"How much?"

"I don't know yet." Kestrel's jaw tightened. "But I intend to read to the bottom of the page."

The queen rose. Whatever she had come as — wife, supplicant, buyer — she left as something harder, and Casper understood that love, in this place, was learning to arm itself. At the door she paused, turned back, and looked directly at Casper for the first time.

"What is he saying?" she asked. "When he stands at the window at night. Do you know?"

Casper considered lying. He considered kindness. In the end he gave her the truth the way Kestrel gave it — plainly, because plainness was the only mercy that held.

*He is saying thank you,* he said. *To the door. For staying locked.*

Kestrel translated it. The queen stood very still, then nodded once, like a woman accepting terms, and was gone into the corridor dark.

---

That night Casper could not sleep. He lay on the narrow clerk's cot listening to the palace breathe — the guard rotations, the kitchen fires banked, somewhere far off a single sustained note of weeping that might have been wind.

And beneath it all, fainter than any of these, from deep below the floors, from below the cellars, from below the foundations themselves:

a rhythm.

Slow. Patient. Contented.

Like swallowing.

Casper pressed his palms against his own chest, feeling his heart answer with its quicker, smaller drum, and understood suddenly, wholly, with the certainty of the dream-language that needed no translation: the door the king dreamed of was not a door out.

It was a door *down*. And it was not locked to keep the king safe.

It was locked to keep something else fed.

In the morning, he would take Kestrel's hand and show her, in glyphs scratched into the frost on the window glass, exactly what he had heard — and watch her face go white as she recognized, from the sealed book, the binding script that wrote the lock.
