## 6. Entries in Red Ink

The master ledger was not kept in the vault.

Kestrel understood that now, in the way one understands a thing only after the understanding has already ruined her. For six years she had balanced the guild's accounts—years, names, borrowed luck, favors owed to the dead—and never once had she asked where the originals lived, because the copies were always enough. The Thorn Guild ran on the principle that no scribe ever needs to see the whole of a thing, only her small corner of it, and Kestrel had been content in her corner the way a bird is content in a cage it has never seen from outside.

It was Casper who found it. Not by searching—by listening.

He had taken to sleeping in the counting room now, curled on the bench beneath the high windows where the last light fell, and on the third night after their return from the palace he woke her with his hand pressed flat against the floorboards, saying something urgent and low in the First Tongue.

"What is it?" she whispered, kneeling beside him. "What do you hear?"

He looked at her with those too-old eyes and answered in her own language, which he was learning the way other children learned songs, piecemeal and sideways: *"Under. Old. Hungry."*

She had laughed at first—a thin, reflexive sound. Beneath the counting room there was nothing but the underlevels, flooded since before Veyra's time, black water and drowned staircases and the smell of rot that never quite left the air. Nothing lived down there. Nothing *could* live down there.

But Casper did not laugh, and his hand stayed on the boards, and when she laid her palm beside his she felt it too—not a sound exactly, not through her ears. A pull. Faint as the tug of a current beneath still water. Something down there turning over in its sleep, and turning toward them.

* * *

She went looking for the master ledger the next morning, while Veyra attended the quarterly reckoning upstairs and the other scribes bent over their columns like a field of bowed heads.

The counting room's floor was old oak, laid in a pattern of interlocking thorns that everyone assumed was decorative, because everyone assumes the oldest things in a building are its ornaments. Kestrel walked the room slowly, tracing the pattern in her mind until she found what she was looking for: at the room's exact center, beneath the great table where Veyra sat, a single plank whose grain ran against all the others.

It came up without protest. Of course it did. It had been waiting.

Below it was not water but a cavity, dry and lined with lead sheeting gone green with age, and inside the cavity lay a book bound in red leather so dark it was nearly black—the color of blood dried into cloth. No lock. No seal. The guild had never needed to lock this book away, Kestrel realized as she lifted it into the light; it had been hidden by the simple certainty that no one would ever think to look beneath the very heart of the guild's business.

*We hide our greatest treasure in plain sight,* Veyra had told her once, early, warm, teaching. *And our greatest shame we hide beneath it.*

She had thought it a figure of speech. Veyra was full of figures of speech, each one polished smooth by years of use, and Kestrel had loved them all, had collected them like coins.

The ledger opened to a page dated ten years ago, and the handwriting was Veyra's.

*Received: one soul, portioned. Seller: His Majesty Aldric, Third of His Name, by instrument of the Crown Seal. Consideration rendered per schedule. Buyer:* —and here the entry did not give a name but a glyph, a single character Kestrel recognized with a lurch that turned her stomach to ice, because she had spent three nights copying it from the sealed volume in the vault, the text even Veyra would not touch.

The binding script. The First Tongue. The word for *thorn.*

She read on. Page after page after page, ten years of entries in Veyra's immaculate hand, and every buyer was the same. Every consideration flowed one direction. The courtiers who came to the guild selling scraps of the king—the chamberlain who sold a memory of a hunt, the old nurse who sold his childhood fear of storms, the queen's own confessor who sold, gods forgive them all, the king's memory of his wedding day—had not been stealing from Aldric. They had been *selling him back.* Piece by piece, year by year, the crown itself purchasing its king out of existence, and every coin paid, every favor traded, every debt incurred in those transactions had flowed not outward to some rival power or foreign throne but downward. Into the floor. Into the thing beneath the floor.

The guild was not brokering the sale of King Aldric's soul.

The guild *was* the buyer. The guild had always been the buyer. And the seller—

Kestrel turned back to the first page and made herself read the seller's line again, though she already knew what it would say, because the world had spent ten years arranging itself around this single truth and she was only now being permitted to see the shape of it.

*Seller: His Majesty Aldric, Third of His Name.*

Signed with the royal seal. Freely given—or as freely as anything is given by a man who does not understand what he is signing away. Ten years ago, Aldric must have come here himself, or sent the seal with someone he trusted absolutely, and bargained for something worth more to him than his own wholeness. There was a note in the margin, cramped, older than the rest of the hand:

*His price: the kingdom survives the winter wars. He asked what else we would take and I told him nothing. A lie, but a kind one. He wept when he signed. They always weep.*

They always weep. As if kings were merely another commodity that passed through her hands, as if the dissolution of a living man were an entry like any other, notable only for its duration.

Kestrel closed the ledger and sat very still at the bottom of the empty counting room, listening to the silence above her and the silence below her and discovering they were not the same silence at all. Below was heavier. Below was *attentive.*

* * *

She should have stopped reading. Some part of her, some cold professional corner of her mind that had spent six years among impossible trades, knew precisely how much danger lived in knowledge, knew that the safest scribe was the one who copied numbers without asking what they numbered. That part of her begged her to put the book back, press the plank home, walk upstairs, and balance Tuesday's entries in borrowed luck until her hands stopped shaking.

But Casper slept beneath the floor's attention every night, and the queen had gripped Kestrel's wrist in a palace corridor and said, *he weeps at nothing, he remembers less each week,* and somewhere in the space between those two facts a question had taken root that could not be unasked: *what happens when the purchase is complete?*

So she read.

The entries changed over the years. In the beginning they were transactions—portions received, portions delivered, the dry arithmetic of a soul divided into installments. But by the fifth year the language had begun to shift. *The subject grows quiet. The subject forgets the eastern lords. The subject laughed today at nothing and did not know why.* Veyra was not recording sales anymore. She was recording symptoms. She was keeping a physician's notes on a patient being eaten.

And then, in the eighth year, a new word entered the ledger, and Kestrel felt it go through her like a blade finding bone.

*Fed.*

Not sold. Not stored. Not traded onward to whatever distant power she had once imagined might want the pieces of a king. Fed. The portions of Aldric's soul came down through the floors of the Thorn Guild the way grain comes down a mill's chute, and something below received them, and grew, and grew more *defined*, and the entries began to describe it with the wary precision of a woman describing a fire she is required to sit beside:

*It has begun to speak. Not in words. In wants. Last night it wanted the color blue and half the counting room's ink turned. I have given it the name it answers to, the old name, the one from the sealed book, because a thing with a name can be addressed, and a thing that can be addressed can be bargained with, and bargaining is all I have ever known how to do.*

The Thorn. The guild's own name, its sigil carved into every door and stamped in every seal of wax—and Kestrel had always assumed the guild had named itself after some heraldic fancy, some merchant's vanity. Instead the truth ran the other direction. The guild bore the creature's name the way a leash bears a dog's. Veyra had built an empire of impossible trade on top of an ancient hunger, and fed it, and called the feeding *business,* and called herself its keeper rather than its servant because the alternative was unbearable.

Why? Kestrel turned the pages faster, hunting. Why would Veyra do this—Veyra, who feared nothing Kestrel had ever seen, who negotiated with ghosts and won?

The answer was on the page dated the first winter, in handwriting shakier than any Kestrel had ever seen from her mentor's pen:

*It knows my name. It has always known my name. Every ledger I keep, it keeps a truer one. If I stop the feedings, it will enter me. I have seen what it enters. I will keep it fed. I will keep it sleeping. God forgive me, I will keep it fed.*

There it was, then. The whole architecture of Kestrel's life, exposed in one terrified paragraph. Veyra Thorne—who owned debts the way other women owned jewels, whose word moved armies of creditors, who had plucked a half-starved child off a debtor's block and raised her in ink and candlelight—served. Had always served. All the power, all the terrible elegant control, was rent paid to a landlord beneath the floorboards, and every kindness Veyra had ever shown Kestrel was a stone in the wall that kept the landlord asleep.

*Every kindness was an entry in a ledger.* She had thought she understood that sentence when it first formed in her mind, weeks ago. She had not understood it at all. She understood it now, sitting alone in the gutted center of the guild with a red book open on her knees: Veyra could afford tenderness the way she could afford anything—by spending someone else.

And Casper.

The realization arrived without drama, quietly, the way the truest horrors do. Casper spoke the binding script. The child who had wandered mud-streaked to their door spoke the language in which the Thorn was bound, the language of the sealed book, the language Veyra needed and did not have—for the entries of the past two years had grown desperate on just this point: *the bindings weaken. It stirs more often. I need a speaker of the old tongue. Where are they all dead? Where did they go?*

Where did they go. Kestrel thought of the feral boy found wandering with no history and no people and a language older than kingdoms on his tongue, arriving at the precise moment the guild's jailer needed a key—and the coincidence curdled in her chest into something far colder than chance. Either Casper had been sent. Or Casper had *come,* drawn like iron to a lodestone, the last free speaker walking unknowing toward the thing his ancestors had chained.

Either way, Veyra had not sheltered him. Veyra had *acquired* him. Received: one child. Owed: everything.

Above her, footsteps crossed the counting room floor. Slow. Unhurried. Stopping directly over the open cavity, directly over her.

Kestrel did not need to look up to know the tread. She had known it since she was seven years old; it had been the sound of safety her whole life, and it was only now, with the red ledger burning against her hands like a coal, that she heard what had always been underneath it—the particular care, the measured weight, of a woman who has spent decades learning to walk softly over something that sleeps.

"Come up, little wren," Veyra said, and her voice was gentle, and gentle had never sounded so much like a door closing. "Bring the book."

Beneath the floor, in the flooded dark, something turned over—and Kestrel felt it smile.
