## 7. The Price of Staying Invisible

The counting room smelled the same as it always had at this hour—tallow and old vellum and the faint copper tang of ink that had been mixed with things no apothecary would admit to. Kestrel had spent eleven years finding comfort in that smell. Tonight it tasted like a warning.

She had not gone back to her cot. She had not slept. She sat in her usual place at the third desk from the window, where the lamplight fell exactly as it always did, and she copied nothing, balanced nothing, entered nothing into nothing. Her hands lay flat on the ledger before her as if pinning it down—as if it might open itself and read *her*.

Across the room, the other scribes scratched and murmured. Someone coughed. The great case-clock ticked with the patience of a thing that had outlived every person who wound it. And beneath all of it, so faint that Kestrel could almost persuade herself she imagined it, came a sound from below the floor: a slow, wet shifting, like a body turning over in shallow water.

*It knows I know,* she thought. *That's what the whispering was last night. Not the pipes. It knows.*

The door at the far end of the counting room opened without a sound, which meant Veyra had oiled the hinges again, which meant Veyra wanted to arrive unannounced, which meant—Kestrel closed her eyes for half a heartbeat—everything.

"Kestrel."

Just her name. Veyra never wasted words; words were currency, and Veyra had never once in her life spent more than an entry required. She crossed the room in that unhurried glide she'd had for as long as Kestrel could remember, gray-streaked hair pinned back, gray wool dress buttoned to the throat, gray eyes that could price anything at a glance—including, Kestrel understood now, a king's soul, sold by installments over ten years and fed to something under the floorboards.

"You didn't sleep," Veyra said. It wasn't concern. It was observation, logged.

"No."

"And you've been down to the vault. Twice." Veyra stopped at the edge of Kestrel's desk. Up close, there were new lines around her mouth that hadn't been there a season ago—or perhaps Kestrel simply hadn't been able to see them before, the way one cannot read watermarks except by holding paper up to the light. "And you've been to the palace dressed as an auditor's clerk, which I will note you did competently, because I trained you, and I find I can't help being pleased even now."

Kestrel lifted her chin. "Then you know why I'm here."

"I know why you think you're here." Veyra drew out the chair across from her—the visitor's chair, the chair reserved for clients—and sat, folding her hands. For a moment she only looked at Kestrel, and the look was so familiar, so full of that terrible specific tenderness, that Kestrel's chest ached with it. Eleven years of that tenderness. Supper on cold nights. A scarf knotted by someone else's hands when fever took her at nine years old. The first pen she'd ever owned, mother-of-pearl, given wordlessly on her twelfth birthday.

Every kindness an entry. Every entry a chain link.

"The boy," Veyra said. "Casper. You've grown attached."

"He's a child."

"He's a key." Veyra said it gently, the way one corrects a student's arithmetic. "He speaks the binding script as his mother tongue. Do you understand what that means? There are perhaps four people alive who could perform an unbinding, and three of them are dead, and the fourth is eight years old and eats dirt when he thinks no one is watching." Something flickered behind her eyes—amusement, genuine and awful. "You found him. That was well done, actually. Better than you know."

"I'm not giving him to you."

Veyra sighed, and the sigh was the worst part, because it was patient. It was the sigh of a woman who had budgeted for this exact conversation years ago and found the cost acceptable.

"Kestrel. My dear girl. Let us not insult each other with heroics." She reached into her bodice and withdrew a slim black book—not one of the guild ledgers but something older, its cover scarred with a sigil Kestrel had seen only once before: on the sealed volume in the vault, the book Veyra herself refused to touch. "This is the master register. The true one. Every debt the Thorn Guild has ever held, including yours." She laid it on the desk between them, and the lamplight seemed to dim around it, the way light does around a deep hole. "Page forty-one. Would you like to hear your own entry? I have it by heart. 'Received: one child, female, approx. six years. Seller: the workhouse matron at Marsh End, payment rendered in remission of her own debts. Owed: service indefinite. Collateral: the child herself.'"

Kestrel's hands had gone cold on the desk. "I know what I am to this guild."

"Do you?" Veyra leaned forward. "Because I don't think you do. I think you believe you're a scribe with a long indenture. Kestrel—you were never the clerk. You were the collateral. The whole time. Your labor was incidental. What holds you here—" she tapped the black book once, softly, like knocking on a door— "is this. And what this can do to a person, you saw last night, or you guessed it. Body and will, Kestrel. If you are entered, you will not be punished. Punishment ends. You will be *kept*. You will stand in whatever corner I set you in and feel precisely as much distress about it as I permit, and you will thank me, because I will write that too."

The clock ticked. Beneath the floor, the wet thing shifted, listening.

"So," Veyra said, and her voice softened into something that was almost—almost—the voice that had soothed fevers. "Here is my offer, and it is the only one, and it is generous, and we both know it. Bring me the boy. Tonight. He goes into the vault, he serves his purpose, the king's account is settled, the Thorn is satisfied, and I burn page forty-one with my own hands. You walk out of this guild tomorrow owing nothing to anyone. Free. You could go anywhere. Open a shop. Mend books, if you still love that. Marry some dull, kind man who'll never understand you." A pause. "I would even write you a reference."

Kestrel laughed. It came out cracked and ugly, and she didn't try to make it pretty. "A reference."

"I told you we wouldn't insult each other."

"And Casper? What's his entry say?"

For the first time all evening, Veyra hesitated. It lasted less than a breath, but Kestrel had been trained by this woman to weigh hesitation the way a moneylender weighs coin, and she caught it, and she hated how much it hurt to catch it.

"He has no entry," Veyra said finally. "He isn't ours. He walked in off the street speaking the First Tongue. Whatever made him, made him for this. I'm not selling him, Kestrel. I'm *using* him, briefly, and then—" 

"—and then what's left of him?"

Neither of them answered. Below the floor, the shifting sound had stopped. The silence underneath was worse than the movement, the way a held breath is worse than snoring. Kestrel thought of Casper asleep in the vault among the ledger shelves, curled small against the oldest sealed volume as though it were a hearth, murmuring glyphs in his sleep that had unmade kings.

*He trusts me,* she thought. *That's the whole of it. That's the entire balance sheet. One child trusts me, and everything else is arithmetic.*

She stood up. Her knees felt strange, distant, like borrowed limbs—which, she realized, they very nearly were.

"No," she said.

Veyra closed her eyes. When she opened them, the tenderness was gone, folded away like a garment out of season, and what remained was the guild itself wearing a woman's face. "Then I'm sorry," she said, and sounded it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "Truly. You were the best thing I ever bought."

She opened the black book.

Kestrel ran.

* * *

The underlevels had flooded twice in Kestrel's lifetime—once when she was small enough to ride Veyra's shoulders through the waist-deep dark, and once three years ago when the river took the east sluice and the guild had simply *adjusted*, moving its deepest stores upstairs rather than admit to any authority that such stores existed. Since then the lower galleries had been abandoned to the water, which suited the guild's habits. The guild had always kept its truths somewhere damp and unlit, where they could rot slowly and bother no one.

Now Kestrel waded through them with a child on her back and terror climbing her spine like rising water.

"Hold tight," she whispered. Casper's arms were locked around her neck, his cheek pressed to her shoulder. He hadn't cried, hadn't asked questions—he had woken when she shook him, looked at her face, and understood everything at once, the way children understand catastrophes faster than adults do, having no investment in denial. Now he breathed fast and hot against her collarbone, and every few steps he murmured something in the First Tongue, soft as prayer, and the water around her ankles went strangely calm, as though the dark were listening politely.

The lantern she carried threw their shadows huge against the dripping brick. Ahead, the gallery branched: left toward the old sluice and the river, right toward the counting room's foundations—toward *it*. The water was knee-deep here and rising as they moved downstream, cold as guilt. Somewhere behind them, faint through stone, came voices. Lantern-light bobbed at the far end of the passage they'd descended. Veyra would not send many. Veyra never overspent.

But Veyra had the book. And the book, Kestrel was beginning to suspect, did not need guards.

The whispering started at the second junction.

At first it was only the water—the ordinary gossip of an underground river, gurgle and drip. Then it resolved, the way noise resolves into your own name spoken across a crowded room.

*"One child, female, approximately six years."*

Kestrel stopped. The lantern flame leaned sideways though there was no draft.

*"Received from the workhouse matron at Marsh End. Payment rendered: remission of debts owed by said matron. Owed: service indefinite. Collateral:"*

"Don't," Kestrel whispered.

*"Collateral: the child herself. Witnessed and entered by V. Blackthorn, keeper. Balance outstanding after eleven years of service: ALL OF IT."*

"It's lying," she said aloud, to Casper, to herself, to the dark. "Ledgers lie. That's the first thing you taught me, Veyra. Numbers lie when the hand that writes them wants them to."

But her feet had slowed. Because the voice was hers now—it had shifted, seamlessly, into her own voice, pitched low, reasonable, exhausted. The voice she used alone at night.

*"Think what it costs you to run. Wet boots. A hunted life. Exile from the only warmth you've ever known. And for whom? A boy you've known a fortnight. A king who never learned your name. No one in that palace knows you exist, Kestrel Ashgrove. No one in the world above is keeping an account of you at all."*

"That's not true," she breathed.

*"Isn't it? When did anyone last write your name anywhere but in a ledger? Surrender. Set down the lantern. Set down the boy. Be entered. It doesn't hurt—I promise you it doesn't hurt, I have entries who have thanked me for centuries—"*

Casper stirred on her back. He leaned forward and spoke into her ear—not to her, she understood, but past her, downward, into the water. His voice was small and clear and absolutely unafraid, and the language poured out of him like light into a flooded cellar, and the whisper *stopped*.

Stopped mid-word. Stopped like a debtor caught mid-lie.

In the ringing silence, Kestrel stood in knee-deep black water with a shaking eight-year-old on her back and felt something crystallize in her, cold and clean as a blade coming off a whetstone.

*It tempts because temptation works,* she thought. *It whispers my debts because my debts are real. But a debt is only power if I agree it must be paid.* She thought of Veyra's hands knotting the scarf. Of page forty-one. Of ten years of royal purchases, each one signed in good faith by honest clerks like her. *I have spent my whole life invisible, and I called it safety. It was never safety. Invisibility is just ownership with the paperwork filed somewhere else.*

"Casper," she said quietly. "Which way to the sluice?"

He pointed left, then—after a moment's listening to something Kestrel couldn't hear—corrected himself, pointing left-and-down, toward a maintenance crawl she'd forgotten existed, a gap in the foundation wall where the floodwaters drained toward the river.

They waded. The whispers did not return, but the feeling of being *counted* did not leave either—it walked beside them through the dark like a patient creditor, matching their pace, waiting for the next junction, the next weakness, the next entry.

Behind them, far back up the flooded gallery, a single lamp appeared. One pursuer. Veyra had come herself.

Kestrel saw the light bounce and sway—saw, with a lurch, that it wasn't moving like a woman wading. It was moving like something that knew exactly which stones rose above the waterline, gliding, unhurried, inevitable.

And it was carrying the black book open, and reading as it came, and wherever the light of that book touched the water, the water went still and smooth as glass, and began, very softly, to recite.
