## 8. Unbinding

The chapel had not been used for worship in years, and it knew it. Dust lay on the altar cloth like snow that no one believed in anymore, and the stained glass—saints whose names the kingdom had forgotten, if they'd ever been saints at all—threw colored shadows across the flagstones that looked less like blessing and more like bruises.

Kestrel knelt in one of those bruised pools of light, chalk in hand, drawing the first ring of the unbinding around the sleeping king.

Aldric had come to her easily enough. That was the worst part. When she'd told him, in careful courtly phrasing, that she was a guild auditor come to inspect the chapel's accounts of candle wax and votive offerings, he had simply looked at her with those washed-out eyes and said, *I dreamed you would come.* And then, quieter, in the First Tongue—the words falling from his mouth like stones dropped down a well: *the child who speaks before speech.*

Casper sat against the altar steps, wrapped in Kestrel's own cloak, watching her work with the solemn attention he gave to everything now. He was learning to be afraid, she realized. It was a terrible gift to give a child, fear. But he needed it tonight. Fear would keep him still when the chalk began to burn.

"Say it back to me," she said.

He said it back. The syllables of the First Tongue moved strangely in his small mouth—not awkwardly, but the way water moves through a pipe built for it, as though the language were older than his body and merely borrowing it. Each word he spoke made the chalk lines on the floor shiver faintly, like harp strings touched by breath.

The ritual was simple, as all truly dangerous things are simple. A circle to hold what is bound. A key to open what is locked. A blade to cut what should never have been joined.

Casper was the key. The First Tongue was the blade.

And the third thing—the thing the old text demanded in its cramped, furious marginal script, the line Kestrel had copied out three times because she could not make herself believe it the first two—

*No binding holds without consent. No unbinding holds without forgiveness. The debt must be freely forgiven, or freely paid.*

She set down the chalk and looked at her hands. Ink-stained, calloused, the hands of a scribe. They had written ten thousand entries in ten thousand ledgers, and every entry had been true, and every true entry had helped build the cage around this sleeping man's soul.

*You cannot unmake arithmetic,* Veyra had told her once, years ago, smoothing Kestrel's hair after her first day in the counting room. *You can only decide which column you stand in.*

Kestrel had chosen her column tonight. There was no going back from it. The queen knew—Kestrel had left the copied pages on her dressing table an hour ago, weighted with the royal signet she'd stolen from the audit office, so there could be no mistake about their provenance. Guards would be summoned. Veyra would be summoned. The Thorn Guild did not tolerate theft of its ledgers, and Kestrel had committed a theft so vast it would take them a generation to count it.

Which meant she had perhaps an hour. Perhaps less.

She began.

Her voice was poor instrument for the First Tongue, but Casper's was perfect, and they wove the verses together the way the sealed volume instructed—her speaking the binding-script backward, unspooling decade by decade, and Casper holding each unspooled thread with a word, a name, a small bright knot of language. With each verse, something left the king. She could see it leaving: thin grey wisps rising from his chest like smoke from a snuffed candle, coiling toward the chalk ring, dissolving where Casper's words struck them.

Ten years of purchases. Ten years of installments.

With the third verse, Aldric wept in his sleep. With the fifth, he began to speak—fragments, memories surfacing like drowned things: *a gray mare, my father's hands on the reins; the smell of ink; someone laughing in a garden I can't find.* With the seventh, the wisps stopped being grey and started being gold, and the stained-glass saints seemed to lean in.

And with the ninth verse, the doors of the chapel opened.

* * *

Veyra came alone down the aisle. The guards stayed at the threshold—they were palace guards, not guild, and whatever orders they carried, none of them wanted to walk into a room where the air tasted like copper and old promises. But Veyra walked the whole length of the nave in her grey traveling dress, unhurried, her hands folded, and stopped just outside the chalk ring as though it were a garden border she was too polite to trample.

"You've been busy," she said.

Kestrel kept chanting. Her voice shook and she let it shake; the First Tongue, Casper had shown her, did not require steadiness. Only truth.

Veyra watched the gold threads rise from the king's chest. "Do you know what happens when the Thorn starves, Kestrel? It doesn't die. Things like that don't die. It gets *hungry*. It starts taking collateral." Her eyes went to Casper. "It will start with him."

"I know," Kestrel said, between verses. "That's why I'm feeding it."

She reached into her coat and drew out the master ledger—the red-ink ledger from beneath the counting room floor, the one that named the buyer of King Aldric's soul as the Thorn itself, the one that named Veyra as its keeper, its servant, its *collateral*, bound by a contract signed before Kestrel was born.

Veyra went very still.

"I burned the others already," Kestrel said. The ninth verse completed itself; she began the tenth. "Every ledger in the vault. Years. Names. Favors owed to the dead. All of it, ash. This one I kept, because this one isn't the Thorn's contract. It's yours. And contracts can be forgiven, can't they? That's what you taught me. Any debt, freely forgiven—"

"Stop." Veyra's voice cracked like ice underfoot.

"—or freely paid. Those are the terms. You taught me the terms."

The tenth verse ended. The eleventh waited in her throat, and the eleventh was the hinge: the verse that required the forgiveness. The text had been explicit. *The unbinding must pass through a soul entangled with the binder's own. One who owes and is owed. One who loves and has been loved falsely. Let that soul speak release, or let the binding stand forever.*

There was only one person in the world entangled with Kestrel Ashgrove that deeply. There always had been.

"Read your own ledger, Veyra," Kestrel said softly. "Entry the first. *Received: one child, purchased from the river-docks, age six, fee: two favors owed and forgiven.* You bought me. You raised me. You sold me to the guild as collateral before I could write my own name, and then you spent fifteen years teaching me to love you so I'd never run." Her voice broke, and she let it break. "And you also pulled me out of the flood-water when I was dying. You fed me. You sat up three nights when I had marsh-fever. You gave me books. Every cruelty and every kindness, all of it entered, all of it *paid for*, and I have hated you and loved you in the same breath for fifteen years and I am done pretending those are separate columns."

Veyra's composure—the legendary composure, the face she wore into negotiations with things that wore crowns—crumpled. Just slightly. Just at the corners of her eyes.

"You don't understand what I saved you from," Veyra said.

"I know exactly what you saved me from. The river-docks. The slavers. I remember the water." Kestrel stepped forward, to the very edge of the chalk. "And I know what you sold me into. Me. Casper. Aldric. Everyone. You didn't save me, Veyra. You *acquired* me. But—" and here was the truth she had crossed a kingdom to say, the only currency she had left, "—you also loved me. Badly. In the only language you'd ever learned, which is the language of ownership. And I think some part of you has been trying to buy your way out of that language ever since. That's why you offered me freedom last week. That's why you flinched when the red ledger named you. That's why you're standing there right now instead of ordering the guards to cut me down."

Silence. The candles guttered. Somewhere far below them—beneath the palace, beneath the city, along whatever buried vein connected the counting room to this place—something stirred, and Kestrel felt it stirring, felt the Thorn turning its attention upward like a great eye opening.

"It knows," Veyra whispered. "It's calling in everything. All the debts at once. Kestrel, if you finish this, it will take—"

"Then forgive me," Kestrel said. "Or don't. But either way, say it. Say *something* true. For once in both our lives, say something that isn't an entry in a ledger."

Veyra looked at her for a long moment. Then at Casper, who had risen to his feet inside the ring, small and steady, his eyes ancient and six years old at once. Then at the king, whose breathing had changed—who was, impossibly, climbing toward wakefulness, gold light seeping between his fingers like dawn under a door.

And Kestrel saw the exact moment Veyra chose. Saw fifteen years of bookkeeping collapse into a single unrecorded act.

"The debt is forgiven," Veyra said, and her voice did not shake at all. "*All* debts. Mine, hers, the child's, the dead king's-before-him. Forgiven. Freely. Write nothing down. There is nothing left to write."

The words hit the chalk ring like a bell being struck.

Kestrel sang the eleventh verse.

The world went white and ringing, and far below, something vast began to scream.
