## 10. Marginalia

The shop smelled of glue and old paper, and Kestrel had come to think of the smell as the scent of safety.

It sat at the end of a lane in Marrowfen, three days' ride from the capital and a world away from it, between a chandler's and a cobbler who whistled off-key from dawn until dark. The sign above the door bore no name—only the painted image of an open book, its spine mended with a thread of gold. In the capital, that would have been read as a guild mark. Here, it was simply a bookbinder's vanity, and no one looked twice. That was the whole point. She had spent a decade learning how to be invisible, and for the first time in her life, invisibility felt like something she had chosen rather than something that had been done to her.

The mornings were the same, and she loved them for it. She rose before the light came over the fens, lit the stove, set water for tea, and opened the shutters onto the lane. Then she sat at her bench with the day's wounded books around her: a psalter with a cracked spine, a merchant's almanac swollen from damp, a child's primer chewed at one corner by a dog with opinions. She mended them the way she had once mended ledgers—carefully, entry by entry, page by page—except that now nothing she repaired was a record of anything owed. A book mended was simply a book mended. It asked nothing back.

Casper slept in the loft above the shop, in a bed she'd built herself from plans she'd found in a carpenter's manual, every joint cut twice because she could not bear the thought of it failing him. He was taller than he had been a year ago. His hair had been trimmed—by her, badly, twice, before the cobbler's wife took pity on them both—and his hands had lost the feral quickness they'd had when he first clawed his way out of the fen mud and into her counting room. He still woke before her sometimes, though. He still stood at the top of the loft ladder in the grey half-light and watched her work until she looked up and smiled, and only then would he come down, as if he needed the proof, every morning, that she was still there.

She understood that. She checked on him the same way.

The king, they said—she read it in the Marrowfen broadsheets, which were mostly gossip and fish prices—was much improved since his long illness. He wept at coronation-day ceremonies, which the court had decided was touching rather than alarming. The queen had sent no word. Kestrel hadn't expected her to. Some debts, once forgiven, are best left unmentioned by both parties, and the queen owed Kestrel a debt the size of a kingdom. Better for everyone that it never be entered anywhere.

* * *

Veyra's letters arrived on the first of each month, and they contained nothing at all.

That is not quite true. They contained paper—fine paper, heavy rag stock, the kind the guild had used for its master ledgers—and the paper was blank, and the envelopes bore no seal and no return address, only the Marrowfen postmark and a handwriting Kestrel would have known anywhere, because she had learned her letters from it, traced over Veyra's strokes a thousand times in a counting room that no longer existed.

The first blank page had terrified her. She had turned it over, held it to the light, run her fingers across the surface feeling for indentations, for hidden script, for the faint ghost-ridges of a stylus pressed through from some hidden second leaf. She had checked it against every cipher she knew. Nothing. Just paper, and the handwriting on the envelope, and no words.

It had taken her three months to understand.

Veyra had spent her whole life writing in ledgers. Every kindness, every cruelty, every gift had been an entry: *Received. Owed. Due.* A letter from Veyra would have been a transaction—a message that demanded a response, that created a thread, that bound two people together in the old familiar way. A blank page was the opposite. A blank page said: *I have nothing to claim from you. I am sending you emptiness, which is the only thing I have left that is truly mine to give.*

Or perhaps Kestrel was being sentimental, and Veyra simply had nothing to say. Both things could be true. Most things with Veyra could be two true things at once. That had been the hardest lesson of her childhood: that the woman who had bought her, raised her, used her, and—when it mattered, at the end, in the chapel with the guards at the doors and the Thorn screaming beneath the floor—had spoken her free, was not a monster and not a mother but a ledger-keeper to the last, weighing even love in columns of debit and credit.

Kestrel kept the blank pages in a drawer beneath her bench. There were eleven of them now. She did not write on them. She did not burn them. She simply kept them, the way one keeps a scar: not because it is beautiful, but because it is proof of what happened, and proof is its own kind of possession.

* * *

The traveler came on a Tuesday in the month of Thaws, when the fens were running with meltwater and the lane outside the shop was a shallow brown river that the neighborhood children sailed leaf-boats down.

He was a mapmaker, he said, or had been, before his eyes began to fail. He'd come up from the southern coast with a pack of manuscripts he could no longer read, hoping to sell them, and he'd heard—everyone in Marrowfen had heard, because the cobbler's wife had a voice that carried through stone—that the bookbinder at the end of the lane could read anything.

"Not anything," Kestrel said, sorting through his pack on the counter. "But I know someone who might."

Most of the manuscripts were ordinary: a shipping manifest, a book of tide tables, a romance with a broken binding and a plot she could guess from the first page. But at the bottom of the pack, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, was a thin volume bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, its pages dense with a script of angular, thorn-like glyphs that seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at them.

Her hands went still.

She knew that script. She had copied it by candlelight in a sealed vault while a child slept beneath her and spoke it in his sleep. She had watched it blaze across the floor of a palace chapel as a king came back to himself. It was the First Tongue, the binding script, the language older than kingdoms—the language that had unmade the Thorn, and in the unmaking, undone itself, fading from Casper's memory like breath from a mirror.

"Casper," she called up to the loft. "Come down. I need your eyes."

He came down the ladder the way he always did, fast, trusting the rungs the way he trusted almost nothing else in the world. He was thirteen now, or the fen-people had decided he was thirteen, which amounted to the same thing. He'd shot up that winter. His voice had begun, occasionally and to his own visible horror, to crack.

He took the manuscript and turned it in the light, and Kestrel watched his face.

She had watched that face for a year for any sign of what they'd lost—the language, the connection to whatever he had been before the mud and the fen and her. The healers said the First Tongue had been a tool his mind had carried for a purpose, and that when the purpose ended, the tool had been set down. Casper himself said he remembered it the way you remember a dream: shapes without meanings, a melody without words. Sometimes, in the first months, he had cried about it, furious at the loss of something he couldn't name.

Now he frowned at the glyphs. His lips moved. And then—he laughed.

"What?" Kestrel said. "What is it?"

"It's a recipe." He was grinning, that crooked, gap-toothed grin that had survived everything. "It's—Kestrel, this is a *recipe*. It's for bread. Some kind of festival bread, there's honey in it, and—" he squinted, tilting the page, "—I think crushed rowan berries? For luck. The last line says—" his brow furrowed, and the frown of effort smoothed into something like wonder, "—it says the dough must be kneaded by someone who owes nothing, or it won't rise."

The shop was very quiet. The meltwater chuckled in the lane. Somewhere down the street, the cobbler was whistling, off-key as ever.

"That can't be right," Kestrel said, but her voice wasn't steady. "The First Tongue doesn't write recipes. It's the language of binding. Of contracts. Of—"

"Of bread," Casper said, and laughed again, and pressed the book back into her hands. "Maybe it used to be the language of binding. Maybe that's what it was *for*, before people like your guild got hold of it. Maybe it just wants to be about ordinary things now. Honey. Luck. Bread that only rises if you're free."

He went back to the loft, whistling—off-key, in perfect imitation of the cobbler, and Kestrel laughed despite herself—and she stood at the counter holding the manuscript and felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight for a year.

The language older than kingdoms, the blade that had cut a king free and starved a god, and it had survived the burning of the ledgers, the collapse of the Thorn, the exile of everyone who'd ever spoken it—and the first thing it chose to say, to the only child alive who could still half-hear it, was a recipe for festival bread.

Maybe that was what peace was. Not the absence of old things, but their return, gentled. A blade remembering it had once been a plowshare.

* * *

That evening she opened the drawer beneath her bench and took out the twelve blank pages—there was a new one, arrived that morning, envelope in the familiar handwriting—and she laid them on the counter in a row, and beside them she set the grey manuscript, open to the recipe, and the child's primer she'd mended that week, and the psalter with the gold-threaded spine.

Casper came down for supper and stopped when he saw them. "What's all this?"

"An inventory," Kestrel said.

"Of what?"

She looked at the blank pages—Veyra's silence, Veyra's peace offering, Veyra's love in the only language left to her—and at the manuscript, and at the boy standing barefoot on her shop floor, growing like a weed, laughing at recipes.

"Of everything I own," she said.

He helped her carry them to the shelf above the stove, where the light from the window would reach them in the mornings, and that night, before he climbed the ladder to bed, he paused at the bottom and looked back at her with an expression she had learned to read without any language at all.

"Kestrel," he said. "The bread. The part about kneading it by someone who owes nothing."

"Yes?"

"I don't owe you anything. Right? That's not—" He hesitated, and she saw him searching, the way he still sometimes searched, for a word in the old tongue that the new tongue couldn't hold. "That's not how this works. Us. I stay because I stay. You keep me because you keep me. Nobody's keeping score."

She crossed the shop and put her hand on his head, on the badly-trimmed hair, and said, "Nobody's keeping score."

He grinned and went up, and she banked the stove and blew out the lamp, and in the dark she opened her shop's only ledger—the small one, the honest one, the one she'd bought for three coppers at a fen market—and she turned past the entries for glue and thread and paper to the first page, where a year ago, on the night they'd arrived with nothing, she had written the old formula out of habit, the way her hand had been trained to write it, the only prayer she knew:

*Received: one child. Owed: everything.*

And beneath it, in ink she had mixed herself from oak gall and rainwater, in a hand steadier than it had ever been in any counting room, she had added the only entry that mattered, the one she had waited her whole life to write, the one no guild had ever taught her because no guild had ever needed it:

*Paid in full.*
