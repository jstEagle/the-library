## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The wine was wrong before it touched his tongue.

Noor Yarrow knew it the way a sailor knows a storm coming — not by any single sign, but by a pressure in the air, a wrongness in the room's weather. The chancellor's cup sat gleaming on the silver tray, and the candlelight moved through its ruby depths a half-beat too slowly, the way light moves through something thicker than wine, something that has intentions.

He had been the crown's taster for eleven years. In that time he had swallowed nightshade distilled into honey, arsenic folded into marzipan, the ground root of widow's-lace steeped in a bride's wedding broth. His body had learned them all, one agonizing education at a time — the fevers, the convulsions, the weeks of sleeping with a bucket beside the bed while his blood argued with what he'd asked it to carry. He was not immune the way the court physicians claimed, with their talk of hardened humors and a providential constitution. He was immune the way a riverbed is immune to water: by having been carved, over and over, by everything that ever tried to pass through him.

So when the cup came to him, he did what he always did. He smiled the taster's smile — modest, grateful, slightly dull, a man whose only distinction was his willingness to die — and he lifted the wine, and he let it rest against his tongue for exactly three seconds.

Bitterness first, faint, like the ghost of a walnut skin. Then a coolness that wasn't cool at all but a numbness blooming at the root of the tongue, spreading backward toward the throat. The particular sweetness beneath it that hemlock wore like perfume over rot.

*Conium maculatum.* Prepared well, too. Whoever had distilled it had known to draw the alkaloids slowly, in the dark, over weeks — this was no market poison, no desperate wife's purchase. This had been made with patience, and patience in a poison was the signature of money.

Noor set the cup down. He did not spit — a taster who spat announced his findings to the whole hall and invited panic, investigation, and above all *attention*, and attention was the one thing Noor Yarrow had spent eleven years learning to live without. Instead he let his face do what it was trained to do: nothing. A small frown. The thoughtful look of a man deciding whether the vintage was to his liking.

Then he turned to the chancellor — Lord Aldous Merrin, seventy years old, gout-ridden, beloved by the grain merchants whose interests he shepherded through the crown's councils — and Noor let his elbow find the edge of the tray. It was a small motion, a stumble, the clumsiness of a servant who had stood too long at a banquet. The cup tipped. Red wine spread across the silver like a wound opening, and the hemlock went to the flagstones to join the spilled shadows of a hundred other cups.

"Clumsy," Noor said, and hated how easily the apology came. "A thousand pardons, my lord. Let me bring you a fresh pour myself."

The chancellor waved a ringed hand, already turning back to the ambassador from Vessany, who was saying something about tariffs. Lord Merrin would never know that he had, three seconds ago, been a dead man. He would eat his dessert, argue his tariffs, go home to his gout and his ledgers, and live another decade — and not one moment of it would he owe to the gray-eyed man in the plain servant's livery who was already walking back toward the wine steward with the tray held steady and his heart hammering a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear of the poison and everything to do with the older, quieter fear underneath it.

*Who?* Noor thought, moving through the candlelight. *Who has the patience, and who has the reason?*

The wine steward was a boy of sixteen named Pell, who had held his post for eight months and whose hands, Noor had observed across a hundred dinners, were too clean for the work. Not suspiciously clean. Just — a young man's clean, a young man who still believed his life would be something other than carrying cups in a palace until his back gave out. Noor watched him now, decanting a fresh bottle with careful, honest hands, and felt the familiar ache of not knowing. The poison had come from the cellars. That much was certain; hemlock did not wander into a decanter on its own. But the cellars were a warren of corridors and crates, and a patient person could have seeded a bottle weeks ago, and patience — patience was not a servant's vice. Patience belonged to people with plans.

He would have to look. Later. Quietly. The way he looked into everything, which was to say: alone.

"Fresh pour for the chancellor," Noor said, and his voice was steady, and Pell beamed at him because Pell still believed the taster was a friend, and Noor took the tray back into the light.

* * *

The banquet ended the way banquets always did — not with an ending but with a slow dissolution, the music thinning, the candles drowning in their own wax, the great and the near-great peeling away toward their carriages with the particular exhaustion of people who had spent four hours performing enjoyment. Noor cleared the chancellor's place himself. He collected the dessert plate, the napkin, the second cup of wine — untouched, because Merrin had grown cautious after the spill, and Noor had made sure the replacement bottle was one he'd sealed with his own thumbprint of wax.

In the servants' corridor, behind the screen where the used plate went, Noor tipped the untouched wine into the vial he kept in his sleeve. A taster kept evidence. A taster who wanted to survive his own competence kept evidence *and* kept it where no one would ever think to look — not in a locked box, not under a floorboard, but in plain sight among the clutter of a man too dull to have secrets.

Then he hung his livery on its hook, and he became the other Noor.

It was a transformation that required no magic at all, only a door. The palace's east service stair came out onto Cordwainer Lane, and Cordwainer Lane ran downhill through the tanners' quarter, and at the bottom of the tanners' quarter, wedged between a chandler's shop and a house that had been leaning for so long it had become a landmark, stood a doorway with no sign over it. Just a green door, paint peeling, with a brass knocker shaped like a moth.

Anyone could knock. Almost no one did.

Noor knocked twice, paused, then once more — and the door opened onto a corridor that was longer than the building it lived in, which was the first impossible thing, though by now he barely noticed it. The Veil Guild did not deal in the possible, and its architecture had stopped pretending years ago.

The back office was at the end of the corridor, past the receiving room where the guild's clients waited in upholstered chairs and never saw each other, past the vault door with its lock that required a key, a word, and a regret. The office itself was small and brown and lit by a lamp that burned without oil, and it contained a desk, two chairs, and the ledgers.

The ledgers were the reason Noor existed, in the way that a second moon might be said to exist — technically, obscurely, for purposes no one fully understood.

There were three of them, great folios bound in leather gone soft as cloth, and they recorded the guild's trade. Any clerk could have managed the coin. The coin was the least of it. What Noor balanced was everything else: the years, the voices, the memories, the griefs, the first loves, the last words, all the impossible inventory that passed through the Veil Guild's hands and left its mark on the world's arithmetic. Every transaction had a weight on both sides, and the weights had to match, and they almost never matched, and finding where the world had gone crooked was Noor's true work — the work he was, though he'd never dared say it aloud, better at than anyone the guild had employed in a century.

He sat, he trimmed the lamp, and he opened the second ledger to the week's entries.

*Sold: one borrowed year, quality good, to a merchant of Salt Row, against collateral of his daughter's singing voice, held in trust.* Noor's pen hovered. The daughter was nine. The merchant had borrowed the year to cover a shipping loss, and the guild's terms were gentle as such terms went — the voice held in trust, not spent, returnable. But Noor had learned to read what the ledger didn't say: that the man would come back for the year again next spring, and the spring after, and that trust was a loan that compounded. He made a note in the margin, in the small code only he and Guildmaster Vey could read: *watch this one. kindness now, or the ledger turns ugly later.*

*Purchased: three days of unspent courage, from a soldier, condition fair.* Noor remembered the soldier. He'd come in with his helmet under his arm like a hat and sold the courage he hadn't used at a battle whose name Noor had promised to forget. The guild had paid him fairly. The guild always paid fairly; that was its pride and its camouflage. What the ledger didn't record was where the courage went afterward, who bought it, and what a man might do with three days of borrowed bravery that was not his own.

And then, near the bottom of the page, an entry in the vault-keeper's hand, the handwriting like a spider that had learned calligraphy:

*Held in vault, pending valuation: one first love, donor deceased, provenance disputed.*

Noor read it three times. A first love was not rare — the guild traded in them the way a fishmonger traded in herring, because people parted with them constantly, usually in the years when the love had curdled and they wanted the weight of it gone. But *donor deceased* meant the love had been lifted from a dying man, which was against guild custom if not guild law, and *provenance disputed* meant someone had already argued about it, which meant it would land on Noor's desk eventually, because everything disputed landed on his desk eventually.

He wrote the entry into the balance column and felt the small, familiar click of the world settling straight, and it was the closest thing to peace he knew.

That was the truth of his two lives, though he'd never have phrased it so grandly. By day he was a gray man in gray livery whose one talent was dying slowly and surviving it, a man the courtiers called *the Yarrow* behind his back — after the plant, after the old country name for it: nosebleed, the wound-healer, the thing that grows in cracks. By night he was the guild's silent arithmetic, the one who made impossible sums come out even. And in the space between the two — in the walk down Cordwainer Lane, in the knock on the green door, in the hours when he was neither taster nor keeper but only a man alone with a lamp — in that space lived everything Noor Yarrow had instead of a life.

He was aware, in the way of a man aware of a draft he has never bothered to mend, that this was loneliness. He had simply decided, years ago, that it was the price of the work, and that the work mattered, and that a man who carried poisons in his blood and secrets in his pockets could not afford the luxury of being known.

The lamp hissed. Noor turned the page.

* * *

He was deep in the third ledger — reconciling a sale of returned voices, three of them, to a theater in the Shambles that had lost its lead singer to consumption — when the vault door at the end of the corridor opened, and someone came into the back office without knocking, which no one did, because no one came to the back office, ever.

He looked up expecting Vey. The guildmaster came at night sometimes, to check the balances, to stand over Noor's shoulder and hum through his nose at the margin notes. But it wasn't Vey.

It was a young woman he had never seen before, and Noor Yarrow, who noticed everything — who had built two entire lives on noticing what no one else noticed — realized with a small cold shock that he had *never seen her*, and that this was somehow impossible, because she moved through the corridor with the unhurried certainty of someone who had walked it ten thousand times. She carried a ledger of her own, small and black, and she had the particular stillness of a person who is used to being looked at and has decided not to mind.

"You're the new balance-keeper's clerk," Noor said. It was a guess dressed as a statement. The guild did not employ clerks.

"I'm the vault-keeper," she said. Her voice was even, warm, and completely unbothered by him. "You're Noor."

"I am."

"I know." She set the black ledger on his desk, next to the third folio, and tapped it once with two fingers. "You wrote that the provenance on the first love is disputed. It isn't. I disputed it, and then I resolved it. The donor sold it himself, three days before he died. He wanted it gone before the end, so it wouldn't be in the room. I've entered the correction."

Noor looked down at the black ledger, then up at her. "You came all the way back here at this hour to correct an entry?"

"I came back here," she said, "because you always work this late, and I wanted—" She stopped. Something crossed her face, quick as a fish under ice, there and gone before he could name it. "The entry needed correcting," she finished.

"What's your name?" Noor asked.

And he watched it happen — watched the stillness in her crack, just at one corner, the way ice cracks at a corner before the whole sheet goes. She looked at him for a long moment with an expression he would spend years trying to name, an expression that was grief and apology and something older and warmer than either, all folded small behind her eyes.

"Casper," she said.

"No," Noor said, patient, the way he was patient with everything. "Your *name*. What do people call you?"

"Casper," she said again, and now there was something raw under the evenness, and she picked up her ledger and turned for the door, and then stopped, with her back to him, and said, without turning around, in a voice carefully stripped of everything:

"Good night, Noor. Check the third folio, page forty. Someone's been skimming the borrowed years, and it isn't me."

Then she was gone down the corridor, and the vault door closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting, and Noor Yarrow sat alone in the lamplight with a hemlock vial in his sleeve, a corrected ledger under his hand, and the sudden, unwelcome certainty that page forty was the least of what was wrong with the world's arithmetic.

He turned to page forty. She was right. The sums were crooked — crooked in a way that had been hidden carefully, patiently, by a hand that knew exactly what it was doing.

Patience, Noor thought. Patience was not a servant's vice.

He reached for a fresh pen, and began to find where the world had gone crooked.
