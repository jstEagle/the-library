## 2. Goods That Should Not Exist

The inventory room sat beneath the guildhall like a held breath.

Noor had been down here a hundred times — to reconcile a shipment of bottled sleep, to verify the weight of a widow's grief against her receipt, to count the small glass reliquaries in which the Veil Guild kept its impossible stock. He knew the stair by feel: fourteen steps, the thirteenth one loose, the air turning cold and mineral at the bottom like the inside of a well. But he had never been down here with the master ledger open in his arms and Oren Vey's instruction ringing in his ears.

*Full audit. Everything. Even the shelves we don't show buyers.*

That last phrase had been delivered without looking up from his tea, which was how Vey said the things that mattered most.

Noor set three lamps burning at the corners of the long central table and unrolled the ledger beside them. The book was older than the guildhall above it; its spine had been re-stitched so many times it looked like something that had survived a war. The columns inside were written in four hands across two centuries, and every entry balanced against a corresponding entry in the covenant books Noor kept locked in his own chest — the ones that tracked not goods but *debts*. What was owed upward. What was owed down.

He began, as always, with the obvious shelves.

Row one: borrowed years, stoppered in blue glass, each vial faintly warm to the touch. He counted forty-one. The ledger said forty-one. He held each one briefly anyway, because warmth could be counterfeited with a warming charm and a warming charm cost less than a year, and cheaters always chose the cheap forgery. All genuine. All accounted.

Row two: returned voices, kept in clay flasks packed in damp sand. Thirty-three. Ledger said thirty-three. He uncorked none of them. A voice released indoors would fill the room and refuse to leave for days, and the last thing he needed was a stranger's laughter haunting the inventory.

Row three: memories sold by weight.

This was where the audit got difficult, because memory did not sit still on a shelf. It settled. It stratified, the way wine does, joy rising and sorrow sinking until an unshaken jar read falsely light or falsely heavy. The proper method was to hold each vessel level, close your eyes, and tip it once — a single slow tilt, no more — and listen to what moved inside. Noor had developed this technique himself, three years ago, after catching a broker who'd been skimming the bright sediment off the top of old memories and reselling it as fresh.

He worked down the row in silence. Nostalgia for a summer kitchen: intact. The smell of a mother's hands: intact, though faded, the sediment gone gray at the edges. A soldier's last morning before deployment: heavy, dark, still bleeding a little into the glass. Noor marked it for the vault-keeper's attention. Memories like that one leaked if you left them too long, and a leaked memory in an enclosed space could give a whole building nightmares.

Then he reached the shelf at the back wall, the one with no row number, and stopped.

There were only two vessels there, set apart on a square of white cloth like specimens under examination, though no specimen he knew of warranted white cloth. The first was a vial no longer than his thumb, blown from glass so clear it seemed less like a container than a gap in the air. Inside it floated a color Noor had no name for — not pink, not gold, some hue that existed only in the moment between noticing someone and being noticed back. The label, in a clerk's careful hand, read:

*First love. Unspent. Seller unknown. Buyer: none.*

And beneath it, smaller, in different ink:

*Do not decant. Do not dilute. Do not sell to anyone who asks casually.*

Noor stood very still.

He was a taster. He had trained his tongue and his blood to recognize every poison the world produced and several it had forgotten, and he liked to believe nothing surprised him anymore. But this — this small suspended hour of someone's life, bottled whole and never spent — made him feel the way he'd felt at nine years old, the first time he'd realized the guild's ledgers described a world larger than the city they lived in.

He should log it. Weight, provenance, condition. That was the job. His hand went to the pen.

It hovered.

Because the second vessel was worse.

It was a jar — an actual preserve jar, the kind a housewife might fill with damson plums — sealed with wax and wire, and inside it was not a color but a *weight*, a darkness so dense it seemed to bend the lamplight around itself. The label said simply:

*Grief. Unspent. Forty years' worth. The seller wished to die having never used it.*

Noor had tasted grief before, in trace amounts, when a client's payment came contaminated — sellers sometimes wept into their own vessels, deliberately or otherwise, and the residue soured everything nearby. He knew its flavor: iron and cold water and a sweetness underneath that was somehow the worst part. But forty years of it, never opened, never diluted by use? Carrying grief was supposed to lighten it. That was the whole logic of the trade. You gave your sorrow to the guild, the guild sold it to someone strong enough to bear it, and both parties walked away lighter. This jar had never been touched. It had simply been *kept*, growing heavier by the year, until its owner had run out of years entirely.

He wondered what had happened to the seller. The ledger didn't say. The ledger never said what became of people after they sold themselves piece by piece.

"Careful with that one."

Noor turned so fast his shoulder hit the shelf. He hadn't heard footsteps. Down here, you heard footsteps; the floor was stone and the acoustics were merciless. And yet a woman stood at the foot of the stairs with a lamp of her own, watching him with an expression of mild, unhurried curiosity, as though she'd been there all along and he'd simply failed to notice.

She was perhaps his own age, dressed in the undyed wool of vault staff, with a leather apron full of pockets and tags and little brass tools whose purposes he couldn't guess. Her hair was dark, cut short and practical, and she carried herself with the particular ease of someone who belonged underground. But it was her eyes that caught him — pale gray, almost silver, and completely, unnervingly *empty*. Not vacant. Attentive. But empty the way a guest room is empty: tidy, ready, waiting for someone to arrive.

"You're the taster," she said. "Yarrow."

"Noor Yarrow." He set the pen down. "You're the vault-keeper."

"Casper." She crossed the room without hurry and took the grief-jar from the shelf with both hands, cradling it the way a midwife holds a newborn, and set it back down precisely where it had been. "You were about to record its weight. Don't. It changes."

"Grief doesn't change."

"This grief does. It's been waiting forty years for somewhere to go. Some nights it's heavier because it's hopeful." She glanced at him sidelong, checking whether he'd laugh. He didn't. Something in her face eased. "Vey sent you for the full audit, then. Even this shelf."

"Even this shelf."

"He never sends anyone down here." She said it without accusation, more like noting the weather. "Which means either something's wrong upstairs, or something's wrong down here, and he wants to know which before anyone else does."

Noor considered lying to her. He was good at lying; a royal food-taster who survived by detecting falsehood in wine learned to produce it fluently in conversation. But there was something about her emptiness that made lies seem rude, the way you don't shout in a chapel.

"He didn't say why," Noor said. Which was true. "He said even the shelves we don't show buyers."

Casper nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something. She pulled a stool from beneath the table, sat, and began unpacking her apron onto the wood: brass calipers, a tuning fork, a spool of black thread, a small scale with pans of smoked glass. "Then I'll work with you. There are items on these shelves that will lie to you, and I know their tricks."

"Memories lie?"

"Everything down here lies a little. That's what makes it sellable." She picked up the vial of first love and held it to her lamp, tilting it so the unnamed color swam. "Look at this. Beautiful, isn't it. Every buyer who sees it wants it. Do you know why no one has bought it?"

"The price?"

"The price is fair. Half what it's worth." She set it down with a gentleness that contradicted her brisk voice. "No one buys it because everyone who comes close enough to examine it remembers their own first love while they're holding it. And then they can't go through with it. They put it back on the shelf and they leave, and half of them cry in the stairwell." She said it flatly, without cruelty. "We call it the honest vial. It's the only item in the inventory that does the buyer a kindness before taking their money."

Noor looked at the vial again, and this time he let himself feel it: the warmth starting behind his sternum, the sudden vivid image of a girl on a bridge eleven years ago, rain, the specific courage of saying her name out loud. He breathed through it. Put it away. It passed in ten seconds, leaving only the ache where it had been.

"That's remarkable," he said quietly.

"It's terrible," Casper said. "All of it is, if you look too long. That's the trouble with this trade. From upstairs it looks like charity. Grief offloaded, voices restored, years lent to people dying too soon. And it is those things. But come downstairs, stand here long enough, and you start asking the question nobody upstairs wants asked."

"Which is?"

She met his eyes. Her gray ones held nothing back, gave nothing back — a perfect mirror with no one behind it.

"*Where do the goods come from.*"

The lamps hissed. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone, the guildhall creaked through its evening settling, brokers locking drawers, clerks snuffing candles, the ordinary sounds of ordinary commerce. Down here the sound felt like another country.

"I know where they come from," Noor said. "Sellers. People come in off the street and trade—"

"People come in and trade *willingly*, yes. Sometimes." Casper began sorting tags into piles with quick, precise movements. "But do you know what a memory weighs when it comes off a desperate man? Less than when it came off a contented one. Desperation compresses. So the clever brokers learned that a man who's frightened sells more per visit than a man who's happy. And a woman who's been *made* frightened—" She stopped sorting. "I'm not telling you anything you can prove. I'm telling you what the shelves tell me. I've catalogued forty thousand items in nine years, taster. I can read provenance the way you read poison. And the provenance has been getting darker."

"How darker?"

"More fear in the sediment. More reluctance. Items arriving pre-cracked, like they were taken rather than given." She picked up the grief jar again, turned it slowly in the lamplight. "This one is the exception. This one was given freely, gladly, by a woman who wanted to die clean. But she told the broker something before she left, and the broker wrote it in his margin, and the margin got torn out, and I'm the only one who read it first."

Noor's mouth had gone dry. "What did she say?"

"She said the guild offered her money before she'd finished describing what she wanted to sell." Casper set the jar down. "As if they already knew."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Noor pulled the ledger toward himself, mostly to have something to do with his hands, and found his columns suddenly looked different — the neat balancing lines of a system he'd trusted with both his lives. He was the guild's creature twice over: raised in its kitchens, fed at its table, taught his letters from its shipping manifests. The guild had found him half-starved at nine years old with a taster's tongue and no family, and it had never once asked him what he was. He owed it everything.

He also, he realized, standing in its cellar with a jar of forty years' unspent grief six feet away, had never once audited it honestly until tonight.

"Tell me about yourself," he said, because the silence was becoming unbearable and because he genuinely wanted to know. "Nine years cataloguing. What were you before?"

Casper's hands went still over her tags.

It was a small stillness, quickly mastered, but Noor had spent his adult life detecting the exact moment a substance entered a drink — he knew stillness when it was hiding something.

"I'm the vault-keeper," she said.

"That's what you are now. What were you before?"

"I don't know."

"Do you mean you won't say?"

"I mean I don't *know*." She said it patiently, the way one explains rain to a child. "I have no past, taster. Whatever I was before the guild, I traded it. All of it. Before my first shift down here, before my first catalogue entry. I walked in with nothing but a name I don't remember choosing and a contract I signed in a hand that isn't mine anymore." She smiled, and the smile was genuine, which was the worst part. "It's not a tragedy. Everyone down here trades pieces of themselves eventually. I just did the whole transaction at once. Efficient."

"Nothing at all?" Noor heard his own voice drop. "Not one thing?"

"Not one—" She stopped.

Her head tilted, just slightly, the way a person tilts toward a sound only they can hear. For a moment her empty gray eyes focused on something very far away, or very deep, and Noor watched her search herself the way he searched wine: systematically, professionally, hoping to find nothing and dreading it.

"There's one thing," she said finally, and her voice had changed. Quieter. Almost puzzled. "One fragment. It survived the trade — I've never understood why. Everything else went clean, but this one piece stayed, like a splinter the body refuses to push out." She looked at him, and for the first time since she'd appeared at the foot of the stairs, she seemed uncertain.

"What is it?" Noor asked. "A face? A place?"

"A name." She hesitated, and then, as if setting down something fragile: "*Noor.*"

The lamps flickered. Or perhaps they didn't; perhaps it was only the cold walking up his spine.

"That's my name," he said stupidly.

"Yes." Casper studied him with new intensity, cataloguing him the way she catalogued her shelves, looking for provenance, for sediment, for the truth under the label. "I've known it for nine years. I assumed it was a debt from whoever I used to be — some stranger I owed, some name I'd sworn to remember. I've written it on the inside cover of every ledger I keep, in case the rest of me goes too. I never thought I'd meet the man it belonged to." She paused. "Have we met before tonight, taster? Think carefully. Because if we have, one of us has lost the memory, and I can promise you it wasn't me. I don't lose things. It's the only skill I have left."

"No," Noor said. "I'd remember."

He was almost certain that was true. He remembered everything — faces, debts, poisons, the taste of every betrayal he'd ever swallowed professionally. If they had met, some part of him would have kept her, the way his blood kept every poison it survived.

But she was still looking at him, and her emptiness had changed quality: it was no longer a guest room waiting. It was a locked door, and behind it, something was knocking.

"Well," Casper said at last, briskly, gathering her tools back into her apron as if the last minute had been a spill she'd wiped up. "The inventory won't audit itself. Start on row four; the borrowed years are due for weighing, and if you tilt them too fast they'll think you're Death and try to bargain." She was already moving toward the far shelves, lamp in hand, her shadow huge on the stone.

Noor watched her go, then looked down at the ledger, at the blank line waiting beside *first love, unspent*. Slowly, he dipped his pen.

And that was when he heard it — faint, from the top of the stairs, muffled by fourteen steps of stone: the front doors of the guildhall opening, boots on the entry tiles, and a voice he did not know asking Oren Vey, in the pleasant unhurried tone of a hunter who has all night,

"—about a memory sold in this district. Spring, two years ago. I understand you keep excellent records."

Above them, the guildhall went quiet the way a held breath goes quiet.

Casper appeared at his shoulder. Neither of them moved.

"We're closed," Vey's voice said upstairs, smooth as poured oil. "Though for the right inquiry, we're always open."

Noor looked at Casper. Casper looked at the grief jar, and the honest vial, and the forty thousand small containers of other people's lives, and then at the ledger with his name hidden inside hers.

"Whatever you were going to write," she murmured, "write it small."
