## 3. The Proclamation

The bell began before dawn.

Noor heard it from the guildhall's attic room where he kept a narrow bed he rarely slept in, and for three heartbeats he lay still in the gray light, cataloguing the sound the way he catalogued everything: not a fire bell, too slow; not the market bell, too solemn; not the chapel. This was the great bronze in the Chancery tower, rung only for coronations and declarations of war.

He dressed in the dark, out of habit, and was down the stairs and into the street before his mind finished forming the question it already knew the answer to.

Salt Row smelled of fish brine and wet cobbles. The lamplighters were snuffing their last flames, and the morning crowd had stopped moving — bakers with flour to their elbows, dockhands with rope over their shoulders, a milk cart abandoned at an angle, its horse dozing — all of them turned toward the Chancery steps, where a herald stood in scarlet beneath a canopy of the crown's black-and-gold.

Noor worked his way to the edge of the crowd without pushing. A taster learns to be unremarkable; it is the first skill they teach you, before you ever touch a cup. He folded himself into the shadow of an awning beside a woman selling chestnuts and listened.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Second," the herald called, his voice trained to carry across a battlefield, "and by counsel of the Privy Chamber: let it be known that within the realm there dwell persons bearing what is henceforth termed *the gift* — unnatural constitution against poison, unnatural memory beyond mortal span, unnatural speech with the departed, and all other workings of the Veil."

The words dropped into the street like stones into still water. No ripples yet. Just the held breath of four hundred people pretending they hadn't flinched.

"Let all such persons present themselves for registration at any Chancery office within thirty days." The herald unrolled a second parchment, as if the first hadn't been enough. "Registered persons shall be assessed, branded with the mark of the crown, and conscripted to royal service as their gifts allow. Those found unregistered after the thirtieth day shall be taken. Informants presenting verified names shall receive ten crowns per head."

Ten crowns. Enough to feed a family through winter. Enough to buy a neighbor.

The woman beside him went on roasting her chestnuts with the mechanical steadiness of someone who has decided not to hear anything. Down the row, a boy no older than eight looked up at his mother and asked, loudly, in the way children ask, "What's the Veil?" and the mother put her hand flat over his mouth and didn't answer, and that silence told Noor more than the herald's whole speech.

*Registration.* He turned the word over. It sounded gentle. It sounded like paperwork. But he had balanced ledgers long enough to know that every gentle word in a proclamation is paid for by an ugly one further down the page — *assessed*, *branded*, *taken*. The grammar of the thing moved from invitation to ownership to violence in three clauses, smooth as a recipe.

And underneath the grammar, colder: *unnatural constitution against poison.*

His own body. Named in a royal decree before breakfast.

---

He did not go straight back to the guildhall. That would have been the obvious move, and Noor had survived eleven years at court by never being obvious. Instead he walked his normal route — past the chandler's, past the fountain with the cracked mermaid, past the pastry stall where he bought two honey rolls he had no appetite for, because a man who suddenly stops buying his morning rolls is a man who has something to hide — and let the city tell him what it was becoming.

It became it quickly.

By the time he reached Tanner's Gate, a second herald had mounted the steps of the customs house, and this crowd was different. Not silent. *Interested*. He watched a shopkeeper copy the reward notice onto his slate with the careful strokes of a man doing sums. He watched two guardsmen who yesterday had waved people through now examining faces. He watched a young woman with ink-stained fingers — a scrivener, maybe, or one of the guild's letter-writers — turn sharply down an alley when the herald's eye swept near, walking fast, walking *wrong*, the way guilty people walk, and Noor felt his stomach tighten around the honey roll he'd eaten without tasting.

She wasn't gifted, probably. She was just frightened. That was enough. That was the design of it. You didn't need to catch the gifted if you could make everyone afraid of looking gifted.

At the corner of Candlemakers' Lane, a man Noor knew — old Piet, who sold eels off a cart and had done for twenty years — caught his sleeve.

"Noor." Piet's voice was low, his eyes flicking toward the customs house. "They're saying there's folk who can't be poisoned. That true?"

"Court gossip," Noor said, and smiled the smile he used at banquets, pleasant and empty as a clean plate. "Half the palace can't hold its wine."

Piet laughed. Piet always laughed. But he held on to Noor's sleeve a half-second longer than laughing required, and Noor understood, with the particular clarity that dread brings, that from today forward every conversation he had would have this half-second in it somewhere. The pause where a friend decided whether you were worth ten crowns.

He patted Piet's shoulder, said something about the quality of this morning's eels, and walked on, counting exits without meaning to. Alley behind the chandler's. Cellar door under the dyer's sign. The low arch that connected Candlemakers' to Salt Row. A taster's habit: always know how to leave the table.

---

The guildhall wore its ordinary face when he reached it: a counting-house frontage, respectable and dull, brass plaque reading VEY & ASSOCIATES, LEDGERS AND APPRAISALS. Inside, the smell of ink and candlewax and, faintly, underneath it all, the ozone-and-rosemary scent of things that were not entirely of this world. Noor signed the daybook like any clerk arriving late. The junior bookkeeper, Tomas, glanced up and away too quickly.

"You've heard," Noor said.

"Heard what?" Tomas said, which was itself an answer.

Noor climbed to the back office, and found Oren Vey standing at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the street the way a man watches weather he cannot stop.

Vey was seventy if he was fifty, silver-bearded, precise, a man built entirely of ledgers and patience. He had pulled Noor out of the gutter at fourteen, fed him, taught him numbers, taught him tongues, taught him how to taste a lie in a merchant's voice and hemlock in a chancellor's wine. Noor owed him a life. Some mornings that debt felt like a gift. Other mornings, like this one, it felt like a chain measured exactly to his neck.

"You're late," Vey said.

"There were proclamations on every corner. I read all of them."

"Then you'll want tea." Vey poured it himself from the pot on the sideboard — a courtesy so rare that Noor's skin prickled. "Sit down, Noor."

He sat. Vey did not. The guildmaster crossed to the iron cabinet in the corner — the one Noor had never been given a key to — and drew out three books.

Noor knew those books by sight even though he'd never been allowed to open them. Bound in gray leather, spines stamped with years going back decades. The deep ledgers. The ones that recorded not inventory but *origin*: whose borrowed years had been purchased from whom, whose voice had come back out of the Veil and at what price, and — the entries everyone whispered about and no one wrote plainly — what the guild had promised the crown in exchange for being left alone.

"What are you doing?" Noor asked, though the answer was already in Vey's hands, in the way he held them.

"The covenant is broken," Vey said. "Has been for a year, whatever we pretend. And now the crown has decided that broken means *huntable*. These—" he lifted the top volume, "—are the proof that we knew it would come to this. That we helped arrange the terms. If Serah Kolt or anyone like her reads these pages, the guild does not survive, and neither does anyone whose name appears in them. Which includes you."

"I keep the working ledgers. I know what's in—"

"Not these." Vey set the first book on the cold stove grate. "You balance what I show you. That has always been the arrangement. Some doors stay shut precisely so that you can stand in front of an inquisitor with a clean face."

The words landed harder than they should have. Noor heard the shape under them — *you are protected by your own ignorance*, which meant also *you are kept*, which meant also *you are not trusted*. Eleven years, and he was still the boy from the gutter whom Oren Vey decided what to know.

He said none of that. He said, "Burning them doesn't erase the debts. Every entry has a counterpart. Somewhere."

"Counterparts can be lost too." Vey struck a match. The flame trembled in the draft from the window, then steadied.

"Oren." Noor used the name deliberately, the way one uses a blade carefully. "There are people in those books who *sold their grief*. Who sold twenty years to feed children. If the record burns, they can never buy themselves back. Whatever the Veil holds of theirs stays held forever. Is that the guild's position? That we solve our danger by making sure the cheated stay cheated?"

Vey's hand paused. For a moment Noor thought he'd reached him. Then the match touched paper, and the gray cover caught, and twenty years of impossible commerce began to curl into black lace.

"It is the guild's position," Vey said quietly, "that dead clients cannot be reunited with anything. Including their property." He fed the second book to the flame. "The proclamation offers registration. Do you understand what assessment means? They will test each registered person to find the *limits* of their gift. And then they will use those limits. A man immune to poison becomes a weapon against poisoned enemies. A memory-keeper becomes an instrument of interrogation. We are not being invited into service, Noor. We are being inventoried."

The third book caught slower, its spine resisting, and Vey pressed it down with the poker until the fire took it properly. Smoke coiled up the flue and out into a morning that had no idea what was leaving it.

"Now," Vey said, dusting ash from his fingers with a handkerchief he then burned in the same grate, methodical to the last, "the practical matters. The guild closes to unusual trade until further notice. Ordinary appraisals only — furniture, estates, silver. Nothing that smells of rosemary. You continue at the palace. You are, as far as anyone knows, nothing but a food-taster with an excellent palate and a cautious constitution."

"And when someone asks why my constitution is so cautious?"

"Palates vary. Say you were raised on bitter greens." Vey finally sat, across the desk, and looked at Noor with an expression Noor had never seen on him before — not authority, not affection. Something older and wearier. Pity, maybe. Or apology. "But you should hear the rest of it, because you deserve the rest of it. There is a clause in the proclamation. Page three. Most won't read that far."

"I read page three on the Chancery steps while the herald cleared his throat."

"Then you know." Vey spread his hands. "*Persons whose immunity is known to the crown need not register, such immunity being deemed sufficient disclosure.*"

The sentence hung between them. Noor heard it again in his own head, in the herald's trained voice, in the language of the law: *sufficient disclosure*. The crown had not needed him to confess. It had needed only to write down what it already knew — that a taster who survives hemlock is a taster who survives hemlock, and no amount of bitter greens explains that. His immunity, the armor he had spent a lifetime building drop by drop, was itself the brand. He had been registered since the first banquet where he swallowed death and stayed upright. He simply hadn't been *counted* until this morning.

"They don't need to find me," Noor said. "I'm already on the list. I've been on the list for years. Every meal I've eaten for the king was a signature."

"Yes."

"And the conscription clause applies."

"If they choose to invoke it. Today they will leave the palace staff alone — the crown is not foolish enough to disarm its own table on announcement day. But Dame Serah Kolt has been appointed Inquisitor of Gifts, and she did not earn that reputation by leaving lists alone. She will come through the palace servants first. Then the merchants. Then the guilds." Vey met his eyes. "When she comes here — and she will come here, Noor, within the month — you will be a clerk. A good one, dull as dishwater, who tastes wine on the side. Can you be that man?"

Noor looked at the stove. The last of the third ledger was collapsing into flakes, gray-edged, glowing at the seams. Somewhere in that ash was the name of a widow who'd sold her sorrow by weight to afford her husband's burial, and the name of a sailor who'd pawned nine years of his life and never asked why the interest compounded, and the name of the person — he'd seen the entry once, years ago, in a working ledger, before Vey locked the deep books away — whose *first love* sat stoppered in a vial in the vault below, waiting for a buyer.

All of it gone to heat. All of them, now, unbuyable.

"I can be that man," he said.

"Good." Vey stood, and some of the old command returned to his spine, and he was once again the guildmaster who had never once hesitated in front of Noor. "Then go home, sleep, and be at the palace tomorrow looking bored. Boredom is the best disguise ever invented."

---

Noor left by the cellar door, because the front suddenly felt too exposed, and because the cellar was on his way to nowhere in particular and he wanted, just for a moment, to walk through the part of the guild that still made sense.

The lower corridor smelled of stone and salt. Along its walls stood the inventory racks — labeled jars, corked vials, small lacquered boxes, each tagged in the guild's neat script. *Borrowed years, 4, vintage unspecified. Returned voice, minor, slightly hoarse. Unspent grief, one measure, owner deceased.* He had audited these shelves a hundred times. Tonight the labels looked less like ledger entries and more like epitaphs.

Halfway down the row, he stopped.

A vial he knew — the first-love vial, pale gold, catching the lamp light like it held dawn instead of feeling. Beside it, new since his last audit, sat a small box with a tag in Casper's handwriting: *Received under bond. Contents: one childhood summer. Seller requests it back within the year. Flagged — may be beyond return.*

Beyond return. Because the covenant was broken. Because the thin place under the guild was gray static now, and nothing that went into the Veil came easily back out, and the crown was ringing bells about it in every square in the city.

Noor stood in the cold among other people's surrendered lives and realized his hands were shaking. Not from fear of the inquisitors. From something he couldn't yet name — the sense of standing inside a machine that had been running on stolen parts for longer than anyone admitted, hearing, at last, the sound of it beginning to seize.

Above him, muffled through floors of stone, the Chancery bell rang the hour. Thirty days. Ten crowns a head. One brand, one assessment, one inventory — and his name already written in the margin of the law in ink no fire could reach.

From the dark end of the corridor, soft as a closing book, someone spoke his name.

"Noor." It was Casper's voice, and it was wrong somehow — tight, urgent, stripped of its usual dreamy calm. "You need to come see what's happening to the vaults. Since this morning. Since the proclamation." A pause, and then, quieter: "Since they said the word *gift* out loud, the shelves have started remembering who owns them."

## End of Chapter 3
