## 4. What Casper Keeps

The vaults beneath the Veil Guild were cold in the way that old stone is cold — not sharp, not cruel, just patient. Casper liked that about them. The cold kept things honest.

She came down before dawn, as she always did, carrying a lamp with a shuttered flame and a ring of small brass keys that had once belonged to a woman whose name she could no longer remember. The keys fit locks that opened onto shelves, and the shelves held jars, and the jars held lives.

Not metaphorically. That was the thing people never understood until they saw it. A life could be decanted. Poured out of a person like wine from a cask, sealed under wax and glass, and shelved here in the dark where it would keep — or so they had all believed — for as long as anyone might want it back.

Casper walked the aisles slowly, trailing her fingers along the shelf edges the way a blind woman trails them along a wall. She knew this place by touch better than by sight. She had catalogued every jar herself, twice over: once when they arrived, once when the covenant broke and everything began to change.

The lamps along the aisle were supposed to burn a soft blue, the color of preserved memory, steady as starlight. This morning three of them were guttering. One had gone out entirely, its jar sitting dark on its shelf like an eye closed too long.

She stopped. Set down her lamp. Lifted the jar with both hands, careful, the way you lift something sleeping.

Inside, faintly, something moved. Not light exactly. More like the *idea* of light — a summer afternoon remembered by someone who was no longer sure it had happened to them. A child's birthday, maybe. A wedding held outdoors just before rain. It pressed against the glass like a moth against a window, and when Casper tilted the jar toward her own dim lamp, the little glow brightened hopefully, then sank again.

It was starving.

*They're all starving,* she thought. *Slowly. Quietly. Since the Veil tore.*

Memory needed somewhere to live. That was the whole secret of the trade, the thing Oren Vey explained to new brokers only after they'd already signed their names: memories were not goods. They were guests. You didn't store them; you *hosted* them. And hosting required a covenant — an agreement between the human realm and the Veil that said, in essence, *we will hold what you lend us, and you will lend us what we cannot make ourselves.* The anchor-stone had been the seal on that agreement, the handshake made permanent, the promise given weight enough to last centuries.

And the guild had sold it.

Casper set the jar back on its shelf with a tenderness that felt almost absurd. It was a jar. It was someone's joy, going gray at the edges. She touched the wax seal once, lightly, the way you touch a fevered forehead.

"I know," she whispered, though there was no one to hear, and nothing in the jar that could. "I know. I'm trying."

Her voice echoed strangely in the aisle, off stone that should have swallowed sound but lately seemed to want to talk back. Another sign of the failing. The vaults had begun, in small ways, to *answer*. A jar would rattle on its shelf with no hand near it. A label would blur overnight, ink crawling like frost. Once — only once, and she still thought about it — a whole shelf had hummed a few bars of a lullaby no one in the guild recognized, at midnight, in perfect tune, and then stopped as if embarrassed to have been caught.

The dead covenant, unraveling. The walls between *held* and *lost* growing thin.

Casper finished her rounds as the sky above the cellar grates went from black to iron-gray. She counted the failing lamps: eleven now, where last month there had been four. She wrote the number in her ledger in her small, exact hand, because even if the guild had stopped keeping honest books about other things, she refused to stop keeping honest books about this.

Then she climbed the stairs toward the workroom, where Noor Yarrow would be waiting with his ledgers, and where — though she did not let herself think of it as anything more than habit — she always arrived a little early, so that she would be there when he came in.

* * *

The workroom sat above the vaults, one floor up, its windows facing the alley rather than the street. It was the guild's quiet heart: two desks, a bank of candles, a wall of pigeonhole shelves where documents waited to be filed or burned or forgotten. In winter the chimney drew badly and the room smelled of tallow and cold tea. In spring it smelled of dust and, oddly, of the sea — a smell Casper had never been able to explain, since the coast was nine days' ride away, unless the sea itself was another thing the broken covenant had set loose to wander.

Noor arrived at his usual hour, which was to say late, which was to say precisely when he always arrived, because Noor Yarrow was a man whose lateness was more reliable than most men's punctuality. He came in smelling faintly of the palace — beeswax and roasted meat and, underneath it, something bitter he never spoke of. He hung his coat on the peg. He sat at his desk. He opened the ledger with the cracked green spine and began, without preamble, to work.

Casper watched him for a while from across the room. She did this often. She told herself it was professional interest — he balanced the guild's accounts, she kept its inventory, their work braided together like rope. But she knew, in the wordless way she knew most things about herself these days, that it was more than that, and less explainable.

Here was the strange arithmetic of her life: she had forgotten everything. Her childhood, her family, whatever face she'd worn before this one, whatever name her mother had called her through the years that mattered most. All of it traded away, poured into jars, sold or spent or sealed — she honestly couldn't recall which, since recalling was precisely the faculty she'd surrendered. She knew the shape of the loss the way you know the shape of a missing tooth: by the hole it leaves, by the way your tongue keeps returning to it.

But one fragment had survived. One fragment had refused, stubbornly, magnificently, to be traded or erased or shelved.

*Noor.*

Just the name. Not how she met him. Not when. Not why the name mattered, or what it had cost to keep it, or what she'd paid to save it — if she had paid anything, if saving it had even been a choice. Just the name itself, floating free in the empty house of her mind like a single piece of furniture left behind after every other room has been emptied.

She had tested it, over the years, the way you test a loose tooth. She would say it silently, feel its weight, try to hang a memory on it — *and then we*, *and then he*, *and that was the day* — and every time the hooks came away bare. The name carried nothing. It simply *was*, the way a stone is, the way north is.

And yet.

When she said it aloud — and she said it aloud rarely, hoarding it like the last coin of a currency that no longer circulated — something in her chest turned over. Not grief exactly. Not longing, either, though longing lived nearby. Something older than both. Recognition, maybe. The feeling of a key that fits a lock you can no longer find.

"Noor," she said now, quietly, testing it again as she did most mornings, watching for damage.

He looked up from his ledger. "Good morning to you too."

"You're late."

"I'm exactly on time. I'm always exactly this late." He turned a page. His pen moved in quick, certain strokes — columns of figures, dates, entries in his cramped upright hand. Watching him work was like watching water find its level: no wasted motion, no hesitation, everything flowing to where it belonged. "How are the vaults?"

"Worse."

He didn't look surprised. That was its own kind of wound. He never looked surprised anymore, not about the vaults, not about the covenant, not about any of the slow catastrophe the guild pretended wasn't happening. He absorbed bad news the way his body absorbed poison — completely, silently, without letting a drop show on his face.

"How much worse?" he asked.

"Eleven lamps failing. Fourteen jars gone dark altogether — I've moved them to the deep shelves where it's coldest, though I don't know if cold helps or only slows it. And the east aisle answered me again last night. Humming. Same lullaby."

"Same one?"

"The same eight bars. Perfect pitch." She paused. "I asked Oren if anyone recognized it. He said no. Then he said, very quickly, that it didn't matter. Which is how Oren says everything matters."

Noor set down his pen. For a moment he looked at her across the candlelight, and Casper felt the full strangeness of their situation settle over her like a coat: two people bound together by a name and a debt, neither of them able to say what the bond was or when it began.

"You should sleep more," he said. "You're down here before dawn every day. Whatever you're holding up, it'll hold better if you're not crushed underneath it."

"So will the vaults."

"That's not an argument against sleeping. That's an argument for hiring help."

"We can't afford help. We can barely afford candles." She gestured at the pigeonholes. "Half the brokers took their severance and left when the covenant broke. The ones who stayed are the ones with nowhere else to go, or something to hide. Guess which half Oren trusts."

"The second half," Noor said, "because the first half asks questions."

Casper laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised out of her, the kind she couldn't plan. She noticed him notice it. She noticed that noticing mattered to her, and decided, as she decided most mornings, not to examine why.

* * *

Later that morning, a delivery came up from the vaults that shouldn't have come at all.

One of the dark jars — the third one she'd moved to the deep shelves, a heavy jar with a chipped seal and a label so faded it read only *G—, 41 years* — had begun to sweat. Actual moisture on the outside of the glass, in a cellar dry as bone. When Casper wiped it, the droplets came back warm.

Warm. In the coldest room in the building. From inside a sealed jar.

She carried it up to the workroom because she didn't know what else to do with it, and because some part of her — the part that still trusted Noor with everything, though she couldn't have said when she'd learned to trust him, or why the trusting had outlasted every memory of earning it — wanted him to see it.

He held the jar up to the window light. Turned it. Frowned at the condensation.

"It's breathing," he said finally. "Or something is."

"Memories don't breathe."

"This one does." He tilted it gently. Inside, the grayed-out glow shifted — and for a heartbeat, only a heartbeat, it took a shape. Not a person's shape. More like the shape a person leaves: the dent in a pillow, the curve where a shoulder rested against a chair back. Then it collapsed back into fog.

Casper's hands went cold on the warm glass.

"What do we do?" she asked. "If they start waking up? If the stored lives start... coming back on their own, with nowhere to return to?"

Noor considered it with the same terrible calm he brought to everything. "We write it down," he said. "Everything. Dates, symptoms, counts. Because whatever's happening to the vaults is happening for a reason, and reasons leave records, and records are the only weapons this guild has ever actually owned."

"The ledgers," she said. "You and your ledgers."

"Somebody has to remember what happened," he said mildly. "Since you—" He caught himself. Stopped. The silence lasted two full seconds, which for Noor was a chasm.

"Since I can't," Casper finished for him. Flatly. Without flinching, because she had practiced not flinching at this for years, and practice works even when nothing else does.

"I wasn't going to say it."

"You were thinking it very loudly."

"Casper." He set the jar down and turned to face her fully, and there was something in his face she rarely saw — not pity, which she could not have borne, but a kind of fierce, unsentimental respect. "Do you want to tell me about it? What you gave up? Or would you rather I never ask?"

She looked at him for a long time. The candles had burned down to stubs; the morning light through the alley windows was thin and colorless. Somewhere below them, faintly, the eleventh failing lamp buzzed like a trapped fly.

"I can't tell you what I gave up," she said at last. "That's the whole point. I don't have it. Asking me what I traded is like asking a woman with no shadow what her shadow looked like."

"But you know *that* you traded."

"Yes." She wrapped both hands around her teacup, around its warmth, anchoring herself to something present. "I know the transaction happened. I know the year — roughly. I know it was to save the guild, because Oren told me so afterward, and because everyone who was here then treated me differently afterward, gentler, like people do around someone who has paid a price they can see the scar of but not the wound. And I know what I kept."

"And what you kept is—"

"A name." She said it carefully, placing each syllable like a foot on ice. "*Noor.*"

His teacup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Not a memory of you," she went on, because stopping now would be worse than finishing. "Not our first meeting. Not a conversation. Not a face — I know your face now, obviously, I see it every day, but I couldn't have picked it out of a crowd the year after I traded, and I want to be honest with you about that, because honesty is the only thing I have left that's worth anything. Just the name. Bare. Unattached. Like a single word torn out of a book, with the sentence it belonged to gone."

"Why that?" His voice had gone quiet in a way she'd never heard before. Not soft — quiet the way a room goes quiet. "Of everything. Why would one fragment survive a trade that took everything else?"

"I don't know."

"You must have a theory."

"I have several. None of them survive daylight." She counted them off on her fingers, dryly, because humor was armor and she wore hers even here. "Theory one: the broker botched the extraction, and I'm living proof of shoddy workmanship. Theory two: the name was too deep to reach — written somewhere in me that the craft doesn't touch. Theory three—" she hesitated, because theory three was the one she never said aloud, the one that sat in her chest like a stone at the bottom of a well, "—theory three is that I fought for it. That when they poured me out, some part of me grabbed one word and held on, and held, and held, past the point where holding should have been possible, and the part that held didn't care what it cost, because it understood something my mind has since forgotten: that the name mattered. That it was load-bearing. That losing everything else would be survivable if that one word stayed."

Noor set down his cup. He was silent for a long moment, and Casper watched him not speak, and understood that his silence was not emptiness but pressure — a man containing something too large for the room.

"When you say my name," he said finally, "does anything happen? In you. Honestly."

"Honestly: yes." She looked down at her hands. "Something turns over. I told you. It's not a memory. It's not a feeling I can name. It's closer to... hearing your own name called from another room. You don't know who's calling or why. But you turn."

"Every time?"

"Every time. Eleven years. Thousands of times. It has never once failed to turn."

The candles guttered in a draft from the stairwell. Below them, distantly, the vaults hummed — not the lullaby this time, just a low tone, like a struck bell heard through a wall. Casper felt it in her teeth before she heard it with her ears.

"The vaults are dying," she said. "I need you to hear me on this, Noor. Not failing. Dying. There's a difference. Failing means something's wrong with the mechanism. Dying means the thing that kept them alive is gone, and no repair will bring it back. The covenant was the host. The anchor-stone was the seal. Both gone — one sold, one shattered — and everything the guild ever held is now living on borrowed time in containers built for permanence."

"How long?"

"If I knew, I'd sleep better. Months, I think. Not years. The rate is accelerating — eleven lamps this month against four last month. It compounds. Whatever's leaking is carrying more of the seal away with it as it goes."

"And when they go?"

"When they go, everything comes out at once. Decades of traded lives, released into a city that mostly doesn't know they exist. Griefs without owners. First loves looking for faces that have grown old. Forty-one years of somebody's life in a chipped jar, waking up with nowhere to go home to." She stared into her cold tea. "I used to think that would be beautiful. A migration. Birds, released. Now I think it will be a flood."

Noor was quiet again. Then he said something she did not expect.

"Then we should decide what to save."

She looked up sharply. "Save? From a flood?"

"Every flood leaves things standing. The question is whether what stands is there by luck or by choice." He pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, squared it against the desk edge with two precise taps, and wrote a single line at the top in his cramped hand. He turned it so she could read it.

*If we can keep only one crate, what goes in it?*

Casper read the line twice. Something in her chest — the same something that turned over at his name, the same something she had never managed to name in eleven years of trying — went very still, and then very loud.

"You think it's coming to that," she said. "Already."

"I think a man who tastes poison for a living learns to read a table before the cup is poured." He tapped the paper once. "So. Answer the question. If it were your crate, Casper — yours alone, no committee, no Oren — what would you put in it?"

She meant to answer practically. She had a list ready, honestly, she'd been keeping one for months in the back of her inventory ledger: the oldest jars, the irreplaceable ones, the trades nobody had ever come back to reclaim. She opened her mouth to recite it.

What came out instead was quieter, and true, and it frightened her more than the humming vaults ever had.

"The terms," she said. "The original covenant. Whatever record exists of what was promised, and by whom, and what it cost. Because everything else in those vaults can be remade someday — new joys, new griefs, new borrowed years, if the trade survives. But the terms, the actual words of the old pact — if those burn, there's nothing to reforge. Nothing to hold anyone to. We'd be rebuilding a bridge with no blueprint, and the river doesn't forgive that."

Noor looked at her for a long moment. Then he wrote, beneath his question, in the same cramped hand:

*Casper: the terms.*

And beside it, smaller, as if the pen had decided something before its owner did:

*Noor: agrees.*

She almost laughed. Almost cried. Did both, a little, in the space of one breath, and covered it by reaching for the teapot.

"There's a crate in the east aisle," she said, aiming for briskness and landing somewhere near it. "Sealed oak, iron bands. Oren uses it for tax documents. It's empty. If we're doing this, we do it properly — inventory, manifest, seals. If the guild finds out we're preparing for a flood, Oren will call it defeatism. Let him. Defeatism is just prophecy with a bad reputation."

"Casper."

She looked up. He was watching her with that expression again — the one she had no memory of learning to read, and read anyway, fluently, effortlessly, as if the skill had survived the trade along with the name.

"The fragment," he said. "The name. Has it ever occurred to you that it might not be a leftover?"

"What else would it be?"

"A message." His voice was low and even, the voice he used for facts that cut. "Things that survive catastrophes intact usually survive for a reason. Ships keep one boat. Vaults keep one copy. Maybe whoever — or whatever — let you keep *Noor* wasn't being careless. Maybe it was being deliberate. Maybe the name isn't what's left of something lost. Maybe it's the seed of something that hasn't happened yet."

Casper stood very still, the teapot suspended, the steam rising past her face.

Below them, faint and patient, the vaults hummed their note into the stone. Above them, in the street, a crier's bell began to ring — early, off-schedule, urgent — and a voice rose up through the alley windows, cracking slightly with the effort of shouting something the whole city would need to hear.

Neither of them moved toward the window. Not yet. For one more breath, they stayed exactly where they were: a man with a pen, a woman with a teapot, and a single surviving word hanging in the air between them like a lamp that refused, against all odds, to gutter.
