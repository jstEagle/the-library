## 8. The Hunt Begins

Casper felt the hunt before she heard it — a wrongness in the vaults below the guildhall, the way a dog feels weather in its teeth.

She was elbow-deep in the third drawer of the sorrow cabinet, re-sealing a jar of unspent grief that had begun to leak — grief always leaked; it was the most restless thing they kept — when every memory on her shelves shivered at once. Not the jars themselves. The contents. She had learned to read the vaults the way Noor read ledgers: by feel, by weight, by the small telltale sounds of things that should be still. And this morning, forty years of borrowed childhoods were pressing against their glass like moths against a window.

"The covenant's wound is widening," she murmured to the empty aisle, because saying it aloud made it more bearable than thinking it. "Or something worse is coming."

The bells answered her.

Not the guild's soft bronze chimes, which marked the hours of trade. These were the crown's iron bells from the citadel tower, rung only twice in Casper's remembered lifetime — and both times, people had died afterward. The sound rolled down through the cellar stones and set the memory-jars ringing in sympathy, a faint glassy hum that followed her up the stairs like a chorus of small frightened voices.

She found Noor already in the front hall, dressed for the market, his tasting-kit slung across his chest with the careful neutrality of a man who wanted very much to look like no one at all.

"You heard them," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd stopped asking how she knew things months ago, which was its own kind of mercy, since she could never have answered him honestly without admitting how little she knew anything else.

"Everyone heard them." She took his sleeve before he reached the door. "Noor. Don't go out today."

"I have to. If I don't show my face at the fishmonger's, at the chandler's, if I miss a single errand I've run every week for six years—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. They both understood the arithmetic of hiding: a man survived scrutiny by being boringly, relentlessly predictable. "I'm just a taster buying salt."

"Today you're a taster buying salt while the crown rides through the square collecting anyone who glows." She kept hold of his sleeve. Under her fingers, through the wool, she could feel it — not see it, never quite see it, but *feel* it — the faint heat of him, the signature of a body that had drunk death so many times it had learned to greet it like an old friend. To ordinary eyes he was nothing. But the riders wouldn't have ordinary eyes. The proclamation had promised instruments. "They'll have a seeker with them. Someone who can smell the gift."

"They'll have a seeker looking for spellwork," Noor said gently, prying her fingers loose one by one, an old familiar ritual between them. "My immunity isn't magic, Casper. It's just a body that refused to die politely. There's nothing to smell."

"There's everything to smell," she said. "You're the most impossible thing in this city, and you keep walking around in daylight pretending to be a recipe."

He smiled at that — the crooked half-smile she had catalogued among the few possessions she could call her own — and kissed her forehead, and went out into the morning.

* * *

Casper lasted an hour in the vaults before the guilt drove her up after him.

It wasn't worry, exactly. Worry implied a future she could imagine, and imagining futures was not among her gifts. It was simpler and more shameful than that: she could not stand to be underground, safe, while he walked through whatever the iron bells had announced. So she climbed into the gray light, pulled her hood up, and joined the current of bodies flowing toward the market square, telling herself she would only look, only confirm he was alive, only then go back down to her jars.

The square was full in the way a throat is full before a scream.

Riders in crown black lined the east side, a dozen of them, mounted on pale horses that stood unnaturally still, as if even the animals had been warned. In front of them, roped off with red cord, stood a row of citizens — nine, ten, eleven of them, hands bound, faces blank with the particular shock of people whose secret has been read aloud in public. A crier stood on the fountain's rim reading the proclamation again, though everyone present had already heard it: registration, branding, conscription. *The gift is a debt owed to the crown,* the crier intoned, *and debts will be collected.*

And beside the riders, on foot, wrapped in a gray traveling cloak despite the mild morning, stood the seeker.

She was small and unremarkable, a woman of middle years with ink-stained fingers, and she moved along the line of prisoners touching each one lightly on the wrist, and where she touched, something happened that Casper's eyes could not follow but her vaults could: she was *reading* them. Skimming them, the way a broker skims a jar before pricing it. Casper had spent years around people who handled memories as trade goods. She knew the posture, the tilt of the head, the slight parting of lips. This woman was doing to strangers, without consent or contract, what the guild did for coin.

"No," Casper whispered, and pressed deeper into the crowd.

That was when she saw the child.

He couldn't have been more than eight, standing at a stall near the fountain with his mother, and he was crying — not over the arrests, Casper realized, but over a cup. A clay cup had fallen from the stall's edge and shattered at his feet, and the boy, in the reflexive way of children, in the innocent way, had knelt and gathered the pieces into his small hands, and the pieces had *come back together*. Seamlessly. The cup sat whole in his palms, wet with his tears, mended so perfectly that not even the glaze showed a scar.

For one heartbeat the whole square saw it. The mother snatched the cup and hurled it back down, deliberately, shattering it again, as if destruction could unmake what repair had revealed. Too late. The seeker's head came up like a hound's.

What followed, Casper would spend the rest of her life failing to forget — which was, given what she was about to do, a bitter irony she could not yet appreciate.

The riders didn't gallop; they walked, unhurried, closing around the stall like water around a stone. The mother screamed. The boy looked up at the horses with the bewildered calm of a child who has done nothing wrong and cannot conceive of the world disagreeing. When they took his arms, he finally understood, and the scream that came out of him was not a child's cry of fear but something older, something that sounded like it had been waiting in him all his life for exactly this moment: *"I only fixed it! I only fixed it! Mama, I only FIXED IT—"*

The crowd did nothing. That was the worst of it. Three hundred people, and the silence they made was a solid thing, a wall, and Casper stood inside it hating herself with a purity she hadn't known she was capable of. She thought of her vaults. She thought of the jarred griefs, the bottled first loves, the decades of other people's lives stacked in the dark below the guildhall — and she thought: *this is what we make possible. We taught this city that feeling is merchandise. We taught it to stand quietly while children are priced.*

The boy was thrown across a rider's saddle like a sack of grain. His mother lunged and was struck down by a flat of a gauntlet, and the crowd's wall of silence held, held, held—

—and broke, briefly, in one place. A man near the front shouted something — Casper never learned what — and two riders peeled off toward him, and in the churn and shove of the crowd compressing away from violence, Casper lost sight of everything except one detail that froze her blood:

Noor was no longer beside the chandler's stall where he had been standing.

* * *

She found him by feel, which was the only way left.

The square had begun to empty, the way water empties when the plug is pulled — slowly, then all at once, everyone suddenly remembering urgent business elsewhere. Riders were moving through the thinning crowd now, and behind them walked the seeker, touching wrists at random, skimming, reading, and Casper tracked Noor the way she tracked leaks in the vaults: by the faint wrongness in the air, the warmth, the *signature* he carried and could not see in himself.

He was twenty feet from the west archway, moving calmly, too calmly, carrying a paper sack of salt he had absolutely purchased as cover, and between him and the archway stood the seeker, turning, scanning, her ink-stained fingers flexing at her sides like a pianist deciding on a piece.

And Casper understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only to people who have forgotten everything else, what was going to happen. The seeker would touch him. Or worse, she wouldn't touch him at all — she would simply pass close enough, and the gift in his blood, the immunity worn so long it had become part of his architecture, would ring against her senses like a struck bell. He would be roped into the red cord with the others. He would be skimmed. And whatever they found in him — a food-taster who could not be poisoned, a man whose whole life was an impossibility — would lead them, within a day, to the guildhall, to the vaults, to everything.

To her.

There was no time to think, which was fortunate, because thinking would have stopped her.

Casper had spent seven years as the guild's memory-keeper, and in all that time she had followed one rule above every other rule, the rule Oren Vey had burned into her on her first day: *the stock is not yours.* Keepers who dipped into the inventory ended up like the brokers in the old stories, wearing other lives like stolen coats until their own shape was lost beneath them. She had never broken it. Not once. Not even the night she'd sat alone with a jar of someone's wedding day and wept for a happiness she couldn't remember ever having.

She broke it now.

She caught up to Noor at the mouth of the archway, spun him into the shadow of the stone pillar, and said, "Hold still. Don't ask."

"Casper—"

"*Don't ask.*"

She reached into the inner pocket of her cloak, where she had hidden — she could not now remember why, and the not-remembering was itself a kind of answer — one jar from the vaults. Small, clay-sealed, unmarked in the guild ledger because she had never entered it there. Her own private theft, committed years ago and never once opened: a memory she had saved from the shredding bin, a memory that had come to her hands on the night she'd arrived at the guild with nothing but a name in her mouth, and some instinct older than her missing past had whispered *keep this one, keep this one, you will need it.*

She cracked the seal.

The memory rose out of the jar like breath in winter — silver-gray, formless, humming — and Casper seized it with both hands the way she'd seen brokers seize escaping stock, and instead of shelving it, instead of sealing it, she pressed it into Noor's chest, into the space over his heart, and *wove*.

This was keeper's work, deep work, work she hadn't known she could do until her hands did it: she took the memory's thread and wrapped it around his signature, around the bright impossible heat of him, layer over layer, the way you wrap a lamp in cloth until no light escapes. The memory dissolved as she worked, spending itself, becoming camouflage, becoming shadow, becoming *nothing that could be seen* — and as it spent, it showed her, for three blinding seconds, what it had been.

A kitchen. Morning light. A woman's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists. A voice singing low and off-key. A child at the table — *herself*, she knew it with absolute certainty, herself, small, laughing at something — and the overwhelming, physical, unbearable texture of being loved by someone whose face Casper could not see because the memory belonged to the one person in the world she had been, and was no longer.

Her mother. It was her mother's memory of her. The single surviving fragment of the life she'd traded away, and she had been carrying it for seven years like a coal in her pocket, and now she was grinding it into dust to throw over Noor like concealing spice.

The last of it sank into him and went dark. The signature vanished. Where a moment before there had been the most impossible man in the city, there was now — nothing. A tired taster with a sack of salt. Boringly, blessedly human to any eye, mundane to any sense, sealed in the gray wool of an ordinary life.

Casper swayed. Noor caught her elbows.

"What did you do?" His voice had gone rough. "Casper. What did you just do?"

"Nothing," she said, and heard how thin it came out. "I spent some stock. Old stock. Spoiled, really. It was going to be written off anyway."

She was lying, and he knew she was lying, and she watched him decide — visibly, the muscle working in his jaw — not to press it, because the seeker had turned at the end of the arcade and was walking toward them, and there was no time for truth.

The woman in gray stopped three paces away. Up close she smelled of ink and cold iron, and her eyes did the thing Casper dreaded: they moved over Noor slowly, thoroughly, the way a buyer's eyes move over goods.

"Food-taster," she said. Not a question. "Yarrow. You're on the citadel's roster."

"I am." Noor's voice was perfect. Calm, slightly bored, the voice of a man with somewhere duller to be.

"Strange morning's work for your profession." The seeker glanced back toward the square, toward the red cord and the weeping mother being held back by two spears. "Watching all that. Most people run from it."

"Most people aren't paid to stay near danger," Noor said. "It's the whole job."

Something flickered in the seeker's face — amusement, or its taxidermied cousin. She extended her hand, ink-stained fingers loose. "Routine. Wrist."

"Of course." Noor offered his arm as though offering a coin to a beggar he pitied.

She touched him. Casper held her breath and felt, through the fading ghost of the memory she'd just destroyed, the seeker's attention slide over him like water over oiled stone — catching on nothing, finding nothing, finding *less* than nothing, finding a man so aggressively ordinary that suspicion itself slid off him. Ten seconds. Twenty. The seeker frowned faintly, as if her own senses had disappointed her, and let go.

"Hm," she said. Then, to Noor, in a tone that was almost pleasant: "Eat carefully, taster. Poison isn't the only thing in the city that kills."

She walked on, toward the next wrist, the next stranger.

Noor exhaled. Casper did not, because exhaling would have required letting go of the thing she was holding down inside herself, the vast hollowed-out ache where a mother's kitchen used to be. She had known it would hurt. She had not known it would feel like this — like a tooth pulled from the only jaw she had, like a room demolished out of the only house.

"You spent something irreplaceable," Noor said quietly. It wasn't a question either. He was watching her face with an intensity that made her want to look away, because he was seeing, she was sure, the exact shape of the loss.

"It was clutter," Casper said.

"Casper."

"Don't." She stepped back, out of his reach, and made her voice into the flat professional thing she wore like armor. "We need to get off the street. And Noor — the vaults. All those memories downstairs, forty years of traded lives, sitting under a guildhall with the crown's seekers walking the city." She was thinking aloud now, and the thinking frightened her more than the square had. "If they find the vaults, they don't just take our stock. They take *evidence*. Every name, every sale, every impossible thing this guild ever brokered — including the ones that broke the covenant. Vey needs to hear this. We need to move everything, or burn it, or—"

She stopped.

Because down the arcade, unhurried, gray cloak stirring in the wind off the square, the seeker had paused at the corner — and turned back. Not toward Noor. Toward *her*. Just for a moment, just a glance, ink-stained fingers resting on her own wrist as if steadying something. As if *she* had felt the echo of what Casper had done — a keeper's working, spent raw in open daylight, a signature flaring and dying like a struck match.

The seeker's eyes narrowed. Then she smiled — small, patient, terrible — and wrote something in the little book she drew from her cloak, and walked on.

Casper stood very still in the shadow of the pillar, holding what remained of herself together with both hands.

"Noor," she said. "She felt me."
