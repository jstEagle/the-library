## 9. Dame Serah's Offer

The summons came the way a blade comes: quietly, and already against your skin.

Noor found it folded beneath his door at dawn — not slipped under, but *placed*, weighted with a single dried rowan berry that had no business growing anywhere in the capital. He turned it between his fingers for a long moment before reading. The paper was crown stock, heavy as a confession.

*TASTER YARROW. THE PALACE KITCHENS REQUIRE YOUR TONGUE THIS EVENING. PRESENT YOURSELF TO THE WEST AUDIT CHAMBER AT THE SEVENTH BELL. — BY ORDER OF DAME S. KOLT, ROYAL INQUISITOR.*

There was no tasting scheduled. There were no kitchens in the west wing. And no one wrote *your tongue* in an official summons unless they wanted you to know they knew exactly what part of you was on trial.

He burned the note over his washbasin and ate breakfast anyway. Hunger did not care about dread, and neither did a body trained to survive anything. That was the joke of it, he thought, chewing bread that tasted of nothing at all. He could swallow hemlock and shrug. It was this — a slip of paper, a name in ink — that sat in his stomach like lead.

* * *

The West Audit Chamber had once been a chapel. Noor recognized the bones of it: the vaulted ceiling stripped of its saints, the alcoves where candles used to burn now fitted with iron cabinets full of files. Someone had decided that a room built for prayer made an excellent place for interrogation, and Noor suspected the choice had not been accidental. Confession lived in these walls like damp.

Dame Serah Kolt stood at the far end with her back to him, studying a map of the city pinned to a board. She did not turn when the door closed behind him. Two guards flanked the entrance, which was either theater or message, and Noor had learned long ago that with Serah it was always both.

"Taster Yarrow." Her voice was even, unhurried. "You're punctual."

"You said the seventh bell."

"I said nothing of the sort. I let a piece of paper say it." Now she turned, and her face was exactly what he remembered from the market square: patient, precise, a face that had never once needed to raise its voice. "I find people tell you more when they believe the instructions came from someone else. It keeps their resentment pointed at the messenger." A faint tilt of her head. "You understand misdirection. You practice it beautifully."

"Madam Inquisitor—"

"Sit down, Noor."

His first name landed on the floor between them like a dropped coin. He looked at it. He sat down.

She crossed to the table where a tea service waited — porcelain, palace-issue, steam rising from two cups — and poured with the unhurried grace of someone who owned every second in the room. "Chamomile and honey," she said, setting one cup before him. "From the stall on Copper Lane. You buy it every third morning. The vendor says you always ask for extra honey but never drink it while walking. You wait until you're somewhere private." She took her own seat across from him. "Why is that?"

"Because hot tea burns."

"Mm. Or because a man who tastes everything for a living wants to be sure no one watches him taste." She sipped. "I've been reading you for eleven days, Noor Yarrow. Not watching. *Reading*. Do you know the difference? Watching tells me what you do. Reading tells me what you are." She set the cup down without a sound. "You are a man who saves a chancellor from poison and says nothing, because thanks would mean attention. You are a man who walks the same route to work every day for nine years so precisely that the watch captains use you to check their clocks. And you are a man whose tongue has survived things that should have killed him four hundred times over."

"The crown employs me to survive poison. That's rather the point of the position."

"It is." She smiled, and there was something almost kind in it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "And I have read the employment rolls, and here is what interests me: tasters die. All the time. Mithradates build tolerance, yes, small doses, careful years — and still the arsenic gets them, or the new alkaloid from the southern trade, or the simple accumulation. But you—" she opened the little book from her cloak, the one she'd written in outside the guildhall, and Noor's mouth went dry, "—you have been poisoned forty-one times in documented incidents. Documented, mind; gods know how many undocumented. Belladonna twice. Hemlock, including last month, in wine you flagged *before the glass touched the chancellor's lips* — which means you tasted it in the decanting, through air, which no human tongue does." She closed the book gently, the way one closes a coffin lid. "Noor. What are you?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, a bell rang the half-hour, and Noor counted the strokes because counting was better than thinking about what came after answering.

He could lie. He was good at lying; he'd lied to queens' stewards and guildmasters and his own reflection. But Serah Kolt did not ask questions to hear answers. She asked questions to hear *how* you answered, and she had just spent eleven days calibrating herself to him like an instrument. Every lie now would be a wrong note played into a tuning fork.

So he said nothing. And she nodded slowly, as if he'd spoken volumes.

"There it is," she said softly. "That pause. That's the pause of a man deciding whether the truth costs less than the lie. Most men decide the lie. You're doing arithmetic." She leaned forward. "Let me save you the calculation. I don't need your confession. Confessions can be retracted. I need something better."

"Which is?"

"Your cooperation." She rose and went to the iron cabinets, drawing out a file — thin, tied with gray cord — and laid it open on the table between them, turning it to face him. Inside were sketches. Ledgers. A list of transactions written in the guild's own ciphered shorthand, and Noor felt the floor tilt under him, because he knew that hand. Some of those entries were *his* entries, balanced in his own neat figures.

"We seized this from a broker who operated out of Saltbridge," Serah said. "A middleman for the Veil Guild. Borrowed years. Returned voices. Memories by weight." She tapped the page. "Do you know what we found when we traced the buyers? A magistrate who purchased his dead daughter's laughter. A general who bought back the battle he lost, so he could stop dreaming of it. A widow who sold her grief because she couldn't afford to feel it and keep working." Her voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, deep, like current under ice. "Impossible things. Wonderful things. And every one of them torn out of the world's proper order, because your guild broke the covenant that kept such trade honest — or kept it *dead*, depending on which account you read."

"The guild trades fairly," Noor heard himself say. "Consent on both sides. Price agreed—"

"Price agreed," she echoed. "Yes. And when a starving man agrees to sell his memories of his mother for bread, is that consent, taster, or is it hunger wearing consent's coat?" She let that sit. Then, quieter: "But I'm not here to argue theology. I'm here because the crown has a problem, and you are uniquely positioned to solve it."

She drew a second document from her cloak. This one bore the seal of the Privy Council itself, wax unbroken, and she held it without offering it.

"Three weeks ago, the anchor-stone that sealed the covenant between the human realm and the Veil passed out of the Veil's keeping. The breach began. The gray wound under the city started spreading — you've seen the frost that doesn't melt on Marrow Street, the birds flying backward at dusk. The council believes the stone can be *controlled*. Held. Used." Her eyes fixed on his. "Not returned. Not restored. Controlled. Whoever holds the anchor holds the border between the realms — and the council intends that whoever holds it be the crown."

"That's madness," Noor said before he could stop himself. "The stone isn't a tool. It's a *keystone*. You pull it out of balance and—"

"And what?" she said, sharp and sudden, and for one heartbeat the patience cracked and he saw the woman beneath the office: tired, certain, carrying something heavy for a long time. "And the Veil comes back? Noor, I have read the old accounts. The Veil gave us the gift-touched and then demanded we brand them, cage them, register them — no, don't look surprised, I've read both sides. The Veil is not innocent. Whatever broke that covenant, both realms bled for it, and I am *done* bleeding for abstractions." She straightened. "The crown can hold the stone. Order can hold. That is my belief and my charge, and I will see it done."

"So what do you want from me?"

"What any sensible government wants from a man standing inside the enemy's house." She slid the unbroken-sealed document across the table until it rested under his hand. "Full immunity. For the entire Veil Guild. Every ledger-keeper, every broker, every memory-monger from the cellar to the signboard — pardoned, registered, licensed, *legalized*. The impossible trade continues, but under crown charter, with crown oversight, with prices that a starving man can't be starved into." Her voice softened into something that sounded, horribly, like reason. "No more hunts. No more branding irons. No more children taken out of market squares. The proclamation dies, and your people sleep soundly, and all it costs is one thing."

"Noor Yarrow finds the anchor-stone," he said quietly.

"And brings it to me instead of to Oren Vey." She watched him with those seeker's eyes that missed nothing. "Yes. I know Vey wants it back. I know he's sent someone hunting. I know his hidden books exist — a man balances three sets of ledgers for nine years, taster, and the ninth year's ink doesn't match the first. I know more than you think and less than you fear, which is exactly how an inquisitor survives." She folded her hands. "Help me, and the guild lives. Refuse me, and I do my duty: I take what I've read, I present it to the council, and the hunt begins in earnest — starting with the memory-keeper who stood behind a pillar in the market square wearing a signature she thought was masked."

The chamber went very cold. Noor kept his face still, but inside, everything lurched. *She felt Casper.* Of course she had. Of course.

"You'd burn her," he said. "For standing in a square."

"I'd arrest anyone the law names," Serah said, "and I would regret it, and I would do it anyway, because duty does not care what I regret. Unless." She tapped the sealed document once. "Unless the law changes. Unless the gifted become licensed instead of hunted. Which happens only if the council gets its stone." She rose, signaling the audience was ending, though her eyes stayed on him. "You have six days. The dark of the moon — yes, I know that matters to them too. Find the stone. Bring it to the west postern gate. Ask for me."

"And if I bring it to Vey instead?"

Serah paused at the door. When she turned, her expression was almost gentle, and it was the gentleness that made his blood run cold.

"Then I will come for the guildhall with writs and soldiers, and I will be right to do it, because you will have chosen a smuggler's mercy over a kingdom's order. I don't enjoy being right, Noor. But I am very often right." A beat. "The chamomile is from Copper Lane because I wanted you to know I could reach into your smallest habits. Consider it a courtesy. Next time, I won't announce myself."

The guards opened the doors. She left. The two cups of tea steamed side by side on the table, untouched, and Noor sat alone in a chapel that had forgotten how to pray, holding a pardon for everyone he loved in exchange for one betrayal.

* * *

He walked home the long way, past Marrow Street, where the frost still glittered on the cobbles at midday and an old woman swept it off her step with the grim rhythm of someone fighting a tide. Past the guildhall, where Oren Vey's silhouette moved behind the smoked-glass office window, pacing, pacing, a man sitting on a secret that was eating him alive.

*Vey sold the stone,* Noor reminded himself. *Vey started this. Vey's bargain bought the guild its safety with the realm's skin.*

And Serah — Serah offered to unmake the hunt, to legalize the gifted, to end the branding irons. It was a good offer. That was the trap of it. It was reasonable. It was *kind*, almost. A cage gilded so finely you'd have to touch the bars to know they were bars.

Because he had seen the ledgers. Not the forged ones — the true ones, the ones Vey didn't know he'd read. And whatever Serah believed about order and crowns and control, the accounts told a different story: a story with a cheated party, a stolen anchor, and a debt accruing interest in gray static under the city. Control the stone, and you didn't heal the wound. You pressed your thumb on it and called the pressure peace.

At his door, he stopped. Someone had chalked a small mark on the lintel at eye height: a single vertical stroke, fresh, deliberate. The mark of a seeker who had visited. *We know where you live. We know when you leave.*

Noor looked at it for a long moment. Then he licked his thumb and smeared it away, and went inside, and lit the lamp over the hidden desk where the guild's real ledgers slept behind a false panel.

Six days. Serah wanted the stone. Vey wanted the stone. The Veil, presumably, wanted its keystone home.

And somewhere in Vey's ciphered columns — in the numbers he'd balanced with his own careful hands, in the ink he'd mixed himself — Noor was suddenly certain the whole truth was waiting. Who had sold the anchor. To whom. And what price the Veil had named when it was betrayed.

He pulled the first volume toward him, uncapped his pen, and began to decode the broken year.
