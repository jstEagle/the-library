## 11. Casper's Fragment

The memory lived in a jar the size of a thumb, and Casper had spent eleven years walking past it.

She knew this the way she knew the vaults themselves — not with her mind, which was a house with every room emptied, but with her hands. Her hands remembered. They had catalogued forty thousand vessels of traded life: first loves stoppered in blue glass, griefs packed in wax like winter preserves, the last words of dying mothers folded into paper thin as moth wing. Her hands had shelved them all without trembling.

Except for the jar on the highest shelf of Vault Nine, third row from the window, behind the decoys of ordinary salt. Whenever she passed it — and she passed it every day, because Vault Nine was hers, because she was the keeper who could not be kept — her fingers curled inward, as if around something hot.

Tonight, with the guildhall half-burned above her and Noor asleep on a pallet in what remained of the scriptorium, she climbed for it.

* * *

The ladder rungs were cold. The vaults had survived the fire the way stone survives anything — by outlasting — but smoke had seeped down through the broken floors, and the air tasted of char and spilled memory, that faint sweetness like overripe plums where a crate had cracked open during Serah Kolt's raid. Freed memories still drifted through the streets above. Casper had seen them from the shattered window at dusk: pale motes rising off the cobbles, spiraling upward like birds deciding, all at once, to migrate. Some of them had paused at the glass and looked in at her. She would have sworn they looked in at her. And one — only one — had pressed close enough to fog the pane with a shape like a mouth, and whispered a name that was not Casper.

She had not told Noor. She did not yet know how to say *they called me something else* without also saying *I don't know what else I am.*

Now she stood on the top shelf's lip, one hand braced against a beam scorched black, and drew the little jar from its hiding place among the decoy salts. It weighed nothing. It had always weighed nothing; weight was a property of things that belonged to time, and this did not.

Inside, suspended in gray light that had no source, hung a single scene: a room, a table, a woman's hands flat upon the wood.

Her hands.

Casper carried the jar down the ladder slowly, cradling it against her sternum, feeling it hum there like a second heartbeat. She settled cross-legged on the floor between the shelves, in the aisle where she had slept some nights when the forgetting pressed too hard and she wanted to be surrounded by other people's pasts instead of her own absence. She set the jar before her knees.

There were rules for opening stored memory. Every broker learned them in their first year. Open in stillness. Open in darkness or near-darkness. Never open more than you can afford to carry back up.

And never — this rule was written on a card nailed above the decanting bench, in Vey's own cramped hand — *never open your own.*

She broke the seal anyway.

* * *

The gray light rose out of the jar like breath on a cold morning and wrapped her face, and Casper fell.

Not downward. Inward.

— The room is round, walled in living bark, and the table between her palms is grown, not built, its grain swirling in patterns that move when she isn't looking directly at them. Beyond the arched doorway there is no sky. There is the Veil itself: a horizon of silver-gray nothing, beautiful the way the space between lightning and thunder is beautiful, humming with the sound of everything held apart. She knows this place. She has known it for nine hundred years. She is —

— an envoy. The word arrives whole, like a stone dropped in water. Envoy of the Veil, voice of the anchor, sworn between. Her name is not Casper here. Her name is long, and it tastes of riverwater, and the freed thing at the window tonight had breathed the first syllable of it against the glass —

— and across the table sits a human man with ink-stained fingers and a taster's careful way of lifting his cup, and he is younger than she will ever remember him being, and he is afraid, and he has come to her because the covenant is already bleeding.

"Noor," she says in the memory, and hears her own voice with nine centuries in it, low and strange. "Tell me again. Slowly."

"The guildmaster sold it." His hands are steady; his eyes are not. This is years ago — decades — before the hunt, before the fire, before either of them became what they are now. "Oren Vey sold the anchor-stone to the crown. He told his brokers it was a trade for protection, but I balance his ledgers, and there's no protection in them. There's gold, and there's silence. The Veil wasn't consulted. The Veil wasn't even *told.*" He leans forward, and the lamplight catches the fine scar on his jaw that she will someday trace with her thumb without knowing why. "They're going to blame you for what happens next. I've read the drafts. The crown is already writing the story — *the Veil grew greedy, the Veil broke faith.* By the time the breach comes, no human will question it."

"And you came to tell me." Not a question. In the memory she is already standing, already moving toward the arch, already feeling the terrible pull of the threshold. "You came knowing what it would cost you to be seen here."

"I came because someone should owe you the truth," Noor says. "Even if you can't collect it."

* * *

Casper surfaced gasping, the empty jar rolling from her fingers across the floor.

The vault tilted around her. She pressed both palms flat to the cold stone and breathed, and breathed, and the scene replayed behind her eyelids in fragments: the moving grain of the table, the silver-gray horizon, her own voice with rivers in it. Nine hundred years. She had been nine hundred years old when she sat down at that table, and she had given up every year of them except the ones with his name in them.

Because now she saw the rest. Not in the jar — the jar held only the beginning, the moment before the choice — but in the shape of everything she'd felt since waking in this body with nothing but *Noor* written on the inside of her skull like a message smuggled out of a burning country. She had assumed it was sentiment. An accident of affection. A keeper's attachment to the one soul kind enough to talk to the empty vessel every day.

It was none of those things.

She had loved him first.

She had loved him across a table grown from living wood, in a language older than his kingdom, while the covenant cracked beneath both their feet — and then she had done the arithmetic that envoys do, the cold beautiful math of sacrifice, and understood that warning the humans would require becoming one. That exile meant the Veil would take her name, her years, her history, everything that made her *of the silver horizon* — and that if any fragment survived the stripping, it would not be duty that survived. Duty was the Veil's to keep. Only love was hers.

So she had chosen what to keep. She had reached into her own nine centuries and saved a single stone from the landslide: a man's name, spoken in lamplight, ink-stained fingers wrapped around a cup he tested out of habit even here, even in the presence of the impossible.

*Noor.*

Everything else went into the vault of her new mortal skull and was locked from the outside.

"Oh," Casper said aloud, to the shelves, to the dark, to the girl-shaped absence at the center of herself. "Oh, you fool. You magnificent, terrible fool."

She was crying. She noted it distantly, the way she catalogued inventory — tears, one vial's worth, unsold — because some part of her was still the keeper, and the keeper records. But most of her was simply a woman sitting on a cold floor learning that the hole where her life should be was not an accident, not a theft, not a broker's cruelty.

It was a door she had locked herself, from the far side, so that the warning could get through.

* * *

She found him where she'd left him, in the ruined scriptorium, sleeping badly on a pallet among the salvaged ledgers. Moonlight came through the burned-away roof in jagged pieces. He had the original covenant terms under one arm like a child holding a stolen bread loaf, and a bruise from the raid darkening his temple, and even asleep his breathing had the measured quality of a man who had trained himself never to taste the air too deeply.

He woke the way he always woke — instantly, silently, one hand finding the nearest object that could be thrown. When he saw it was her, the tension left him in stages, like a rope paid out link by link.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," he murmured.

"So are you."

"One of us should be competent about it." He shifted, making room on the pallet without appearing to decide to, and she sat on its edge because her legs wanted sitting more than standing. Up close, in the moonlight, she could see how tired he was — tired in the marrow, tired the way a wall is tired after a siege. "Bad dream?" he asked, because she must have looked like it.

"Worse. A good one." Her voice came out strange. She tried again. "Noor. Do you remember when we met?"

He blinked. "The inventory audit. Three years ago, spring. You were shelving grief in the wrong row and I corrected you and you threatened to sell my childhood by the ounce." The corner of his mouth moved. "I've thought about that threat a great deal since."

"That's when you think we met." She turned her hands over in her lap and studied the palms as if the answer might be written there, in lines she couldn't read. "I opened my jar tonight. The one I wasn't allowed to open. The one I've walked past every day for eleven years with my fingers burning."

He went very still. Tasters learn stillness the way soldiers learn marching; she watched him do it now, watched the whole man become a held breath.

"What was in it?"

"A room. A table made of living wood. A woman with nine hundred years in her voice." Casper kept her eyes on her palms because looking at him while she said the rest might kill her, or worse, might kill him. "And you. Across the table. Telling her that her guildmaster had sold the anchor-stone, and that her people would be blamed for the breaking. You'd crossed into the Veil to warn her. Alone. Knowing the cost."

The silence stretched so long she finally risked the look — and found him not disbelieving, which would have been bearable, but *recognizing,* which was not. Something moved behind his face like ice shifting on a river.

"I've dreamed that room," he said quietly. "For years. I told myself it was just the trade getting into my blood. Bark walls. A table that moved when you weren't watching. A voice I couldn't ever quite hear." His hand rose halfway toward her and stopped, hovering, the old taster's discipline fighting something older. "It was you. Before. It was always — "

"I was the envoy." The words fell out of her now like stones down a well, each one striking deeper. "I was the Veil's own, Noor. Sworn between. And I chose exile to warn your kind, because someone had to carry the truth across before the crown finished burying it. Exile means they strip you. Name, years, history — all of it. Everything that makes you theirs." She finally took his hovering hand, pressed it flat between both of hers, and felt him tremble. "But the stripping can't take what you refuse to release. So I chose one thing to keep. One stone out of nine hundred years."

His lips shaped it before his voice did. She watched him arrive there, watched the understanding break over him like dawn coming up too fast.

"My name."

"Your name." She laughed, and it came out cracked and wet and nothing like laughter. "Eleven years I've carried it like a coin sewn into my coat, and I didn't know why it was warm. You thought you were the stranger who was kind to the empty keeper. You weren't the stranger, Noor. You were the *reason.* Your name is the only thing I chose to survive the drowning. I loved you before I lost myself. I loved you first."

He made a sound she had never heard from him — small, wounded, undone — and then his forehead was against hers and his hands were in her hair and they stayed like that for a long time while the moon moved through the broken roof, two people holding the same grief from opposite sides.

When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "Can you get the rest back? The years? Is there a way?"

"The vaults are failing. You've seen it — the jars going dim, the seals rotting. Whatever anchored the memory-trade died with the covenant." She pulled back just enough to see his face. "And even if they weren't failing — even if every year of mine sat safe on a shelf — the terms are clear. Payment in kind. The Veil gave its memory when the pact broke; mortals have to give their armor to forge it again. Yours is your tongue, your immunity." She touched his jaw, the old scar. "Mine is already spent. I paid eleven years ago, at a table made of living wood, and I'd pay it again. That's the part that frightens me. Not the losing. The *willingness.*"

"Don't say that."

"It's true."

"It's true and I hate it," he said, and she heard in it everything he never said, three years of tenderness rationed out in tea orders and corrected ledger entries, and underneath it something newer and harder: guilt, the specific guilt of a man realizing the woman beside him had already destroyed herself once for his sake and was calmly prepared to do it again.

"Noor." She waited until his eyes focused. "There's something else. Tonight, at the window — one of the freed memories stopped. It looked at me. It said a name, and it wasn't Casper." Her hands tightened on his. "The first syllable of my old name. If the Veil's exiles know I'm walking around in borrowed skin, if my own people learn their envoy abandoned them for the humans who cheated them — "

"Then they'll want proof humans deserve a second covenant," Noor finished. He sat up fully now, and the sleepiness was gone from him entirely, replaced by the sharp competence she had fallen for twice — once across a living table, and once across a shelf of misfiled grief. "Then we don't bring them arguments. We bring them ledgers. We bring them the truth about who broke the pact, and we let them judge us honestly." A pause. "And if judgment goes badly?"

Casper looked out through the broken roof, at the sky going gray at its eastern edge, and felt the nine-hundred-year-old stranger inside her nod along.

"Then at least I'll be standing on the right side of the line when it falls."

Above the ruined guildhall, far off toward the palace district, a bell began to ring — wrong, arrhythmic, the alarm pattern she'd only heard described in old brokerage manuals. Noor was on his feet before the second stroke, snuffing the candle, gathering the covenant terms into his coat.

Through the gap in the walls, down the length of the smoking city, Casper could see riders. Many riders. And leading them at the head of the column, pale as the horizon she had been born under, rode a figure whose silhouette she knew the way her hands knew Vault Nine.

Her kin had come to find her.
