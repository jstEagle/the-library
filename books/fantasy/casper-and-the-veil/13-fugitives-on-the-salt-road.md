## 13. Fugitives on the Salt Road

The spice caravan left through the Salt Gate at dawn, and we left inside it.

Not smuggled — that would have been too neat. Vey's old factor, a woman named Bess with hands like cured leather and no love for the crown since they taxed her saffron into ruin, had simply written us onto her manifest as *consultants*. Spice merchants traveled with tasters; it was an old custom, older than the hunt. A man who could name poison by its shadow on the tongue was worth his salt on any road where rivals might slip nightshade into a rival's pepper bales.

So I rode out of Corvane in plain daylight, wearing a factor's gray coat, sitting beside Casper on a wagon stacked with cumin and cardamom, while two streets over the guildhall still smoked.

Beneath us, sewn into the false bottom of a crate marked *PEPPER — BLACK, WHOLE*, lay the covenant's original terms. Twelve pages of vellum in three hands — human script, Veil script, and a third alphabet that made my eyes water to look at directly, as though the letters were written slightly *behind* the page. The only surviving copy of the pact both realms had broken. The reason Serah Kolt had burned a building full of people to get it.

Casper sat with her knees drawn up, watching the city shrink behind us. She hadn't spoken since the gate.

"You should sleep," I said.

"So should you." Her voice was rough as the road. "You pulled me out of a fire."

"I pulled a crate out of a fire. You walked."

"Barely." She looked down at her hands. Soot still lived in the creases of her knuckles, and she kept rubbing at it as if she could scrub away more than ash. "Noor. The memories. When the vault went up — they came past us. Out into the street."

"I know. I saw."

"They knew me." She turned to face me fully then, and there was something in her expression I had never seen there before: fear not of what was chasing us, but of what was *inside* her, or near her, or waiting. "A dozen of them, maybe more. They poured past like birds, like smoke with faces, and one of them stopped. Right in front of me. It looked at me the way you look at someone you've been searching a crowd for." Her jaw tightened. "And it said, *Liraveth.*"

The word landed between us and stayed there.

"It's not my name," she said. "I checked. I've checked every ledger, every inventory list, every shipping record in that guildhall for six years. There is no Liraveth."

"Names change," I said carefully. "In trade, especially. People sell their old names along with everything else."

"Then someone sold mine." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged at the edges. "Do you understand what that means? Someone owned my name before I did. Someone remembers who I was, and I'm riding east on a wagon of cumin trying to outrun my own biography."

I didn't have an answer that wouldn't be a lie, so I reached over and took her soot-blackened hand. She let me hold it. That, at least, needed no memory at all — some things the body keeps even when the mind has been emptied and sold off by weight.

Behind us, Corvane's walls caught the sunrise and held it, gold-edged and beautiful, hiding the riders we both knew were coming.

* * *

We made thirty miles the first day, following the salt road east through country that grew steadily barer — hedgerows giving way to stone walls, stone walls to nothing at all, just gorse and wind and the long white scar of the road itself. The caravans used it because nothing grew here worth taxing and no magistrate bothered patrolling it. Which also meant, I thought grimly, nowhere to hide.

Bess ran a tight train. Six wagons, twenty mules, eleven hired guards who looked bored rather than dangerous, which was the most dangerous kind. She rode at the front under a faded awning and pretended not to notice that her new consultants never ate from the common pot and spoke to each other in half-sentences.

At the noon halt, she appeared beside our wagon with two bowls of barley stew and set them down without ceremony.

"Eat," she said. "You look like a pair of ghosts, and ghosts draw questions at the toll bridges."

"Thank you," I started.

"Don't thank me." Her eyes flicked toward the crate of black pepper, then back to the horizon behind us. "I carried Oren Vey's freight for nineteen years. He paid fair and asked little. Then this morning there's smoke over Corvane and royal riders asking after a spice shipment that left before dawn." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Whatever's in that crate, it cost him his life, didn't it."

It wasn't really a question. Vey had died on the guildhall steps with a ledger clutched to his chest, buying us the minutes we'd needed. I had seen it happen. I suspected I would keep seeing it happen, in the space between waking and sleeping, for a long time.

"Yes," I said. Because lying to her felt like one theft too many in a life already full of them.

Bess nodded once, slowly, the way people nod when a suspicion finally gets confirmed and they wish it hadn't. "Riders passed us an hour ago," she said. "Three of them. Crown livery. Going fast." She met my eyes. "They'll reach the crossroads at Duncarrow by dark. If they're smart, they'll wait there for whatever comes up the salt road. If they're *very* smart—" she glanced at the wagon again "—they'll already know what they're waiting for."

She walked back toward the head of the train, and Casper and I sat with our untouched stew and did the arithmetic neither of us wanted to do.

"Duncarrow," Casper said finally. "That's the fork. East stays the salt road to the coast. South goes down into the Veilmark."

"The Veilmark's crawling with exiles and thin places," I said. "Serah won't follow us in there. Her writ ends at the old boundary stones."

"And ours begins there." Casper looked south, toward hills I couldn't yet see but could feel, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. "Good. I want to know why the dead remember my name better than I do."

* * *

We drove through the afternoon ourselves, taking turns at the reins while the mule-train stretched out ahead and behind, and somewhere in the fourth hour of watching white road unspool beneath the wheels, Casper began to talk.

Not to me, exactly. More to the middle distance, in the low, level voice people use when they're setting down something heavy before it crushes them.

"When the vault burned, I wasn't just scared," she said. "I was *homesick*. Isn't that mad? Standing in a burning building, watching forty years of traded lives pour out the windows like starlings, and all I felt was homesickness. Like every one of those memories was a room in a house I used to live in, and the doors were all opening at once, and none of them were mine anymore, but I *knew* them. I could feel which ones had grief in them. Which ones were first kisses. There was one—" her hands flexed on the reins "—one that smelled like rain on hot stone, and I nearly climbed out the window after it. I nearly chased it into the street."

"Casper—"

"Liraveth," she said. She tried the name aloud for the first time, tasting it the way I tasted wine. Testing for poison. "It doesn't fit right. But it doesn't *not* fit either. It fits the way an old shoe fits — wrong, but shaped by somebody."

The mules clopped. The wind came off the flats carrying salt and the faint mineral smell of the distant sea.

"In the vaults," I said slowly, working it through as I said it, "you told me once that you can read any memory. Any of them. First time, last time, encrypted, sealed — doesn't matter. You open them like other people open books. I always assumed that was training."

"I assumed it was training too."

"What if it isn't?" I watched the road. "What if you can read them because you come from the same place they were minted? What if the vaults don't hold memories, exactly — they hold Veil-stuff, the raw material of remembering — and you handle it easily because you're *made* of it?"

She was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone very careful, the way mine went careful around an unknown poison.

"The freed ones called me Liraveth," she said. "And the envoy in the cellar — the kin of whoever I was — the message scratched by the empty cradle said *the price returns*." She shook her head. "You're suggesting I'm not a thief of the impossible, Noor. You're suggesting I'm stock."

"I'm suggesting," I said, "that whatever you traded six years ago to save this guild, you traded it from a position nobody else could have stood in. And I think the answer is south of here, past the boundary stones, in the place your own kind went when the covenant broke." I made myself look at her. "And I'm not going to pretend I'm not afraid of what we find. But I'd rather find it with you than have it find you without me."

Something in her shoulders came down half an inch. Not peace — she was a long way from peace — but the particular relief of being believed.

"That," she said quietly, "is the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"You don't know that."

"No." A ghost of a smile. "But I choose to."

* * *

Dusk found us short of Duncarrow, camped in the lee of a fallen waystone with the wagons drawn up and cookfires lit small and shielded. Bess posted double watch without being asked. Somewhere north, in the dark, three crown riders were waiting at a crossroads, and we all pretended not to count the hours until dawn.

Casper couldn't sleep. Neither could I, so we sat together against the warm flank of a wagon, wrapped in one blanket because the flats gave up their heat the moment the sun left, sharing it because there was only one and neither of us suggested otherwise.

"Tell me something true," she said. It had become a ritual of ours, these last days — one true story a night, hers or mine, small things mostly, the currency of two people rebuilding trust from salvage. "Anything. About before the fire."

I thought about it. Most of my true stories were ugly ones — the taste of arsenic described to a chancellor as *almonds*, the ledgers balanced in blood-ink, the years of living as a locked box inside a locked box.

"When I was nine," I said, "before the guild bought my debt, I lived above a pie shop on Tanner's Row. My mother made the pies. Every morning she'd give me the first burnt one, the tester, and tell me it was the chef's privilege. They were terrible. Charred crust, gristle, once a whole clove that bit like a toothache. I ate every one and told her it was delicious, every single morning, for four years."

"Why?"

"Because she needed to believe the shop was good. Because if I ever told the truth, she'd stop getting up before dawn to make them." I shrugged against the wheel. "Turns out that was training too. First lie I ever told was to protect someone. Everything since has just been variations."

Casper was quiet. Then she said, "Did it work? Did the shop survive?"

"For six years. Until she died of a fever I couldn't taste coming." The words came out flat, worn smooth from handling. "Poison I can beat. Ordinary dying, no."

She shifted, and her head came to rest against my shoulder — tentatively at first, then settling, as if her body remembered a shape her mind had sold. I went very still, the way you do when a wild thing lands on you.

"The freed memories," she murmured. "When they looked at me — at *her*, at Liraveth — they weren't afraid. They were glad. Like I was someone they'd been waiting for." Her breath fogged in the cold. "What kind of person does everyone remember loving?"

"One worth finding out about," I said.

Above us the stars came out hard and cold, and far to the north, at the crossroads of Duncarrow, three riders sat their horses in the dark and waited, and none of us slept well.

* * *

We skirted Duncarrow at first light, cutting off the salt road a mile early through Bess's cousin's turnip fields, which involved apologies, coin, and a great deal of trampled turnip. From the crest of the ridge we could see the crossroads below, and the three riders still there, scanning the empty highway — looking west to east, along the salt road, exactly where we were supposed to be.

Except one of them wasn't watching the road.

She was watching the ridge.

Even at that distance, even through the dawn haze, I knew the stillness of her. Dame Serah Kolt sat her horse like a drawn blade, and her face was turned toward our hilltop with the patience of a woman who had known, somehow, all along, that the salt road was a story we'd want her to believe.

She didn't spur toward us. She didn't signal the others. She simply raised one hand — not a threat, not quite a salute — and held it there, three heartbeats, four, while my blood turned to ice water in my veins.

Then she lowered it, turned her horse, and rode back toward the crossroads, leaving us the ridge, and the southern road, and the question of what a hunter does when she lets the quarry go — and what she wants in return.

"She saw us," Casper whispered.

"She let us see her seeing us." I pulled Casper down behind the ridge line, my heart hammering a rhythm I hadn't tasted since childhood. "There are two reasons a hunter shows you she's found you and doesn't close the trap."

Casper looked at me.

"Either she wants us to run somewhere specific," I said, turning south toward the gray line of the Veilmark hills, "or someone's already waiting for us there. And I'm no longer sure which frightens me more."

We took the south road at a trot, the covenant's pages pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and behind us the salt road lay empty and white and full of eyes.
