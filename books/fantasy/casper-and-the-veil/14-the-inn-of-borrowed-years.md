## 14. The Inn of Borrowed Years

The salt road gave out an hour past dusk, dissolving into a cart track that wound down through hills the color of old pewter. Casper walked with her hood up and her hand in Noor's pocket — not his hand, exactly, though his fingers found hers there anyway — because the freed memories that had followed them from the burning guildhall were drawn to warmth, and she had learned on the second night that they gathered around her like moths.

They called to her in voices she did not know and somehow knew.

*Ilsette,* one whispered as they crossed a frozen stream, a woman's voice thick with someone else's wedding day. *Ilsette, you kept mine safe. Ilsette, I remember you.*

She did not answer. She never answered. But Noor's fingers tightened around hers each time, and that was its own kind of reply.

The inn appeared where no inn should have been: a squat stone house crouched at the bottom of a hollow, windows lit amber, smoke rising straight up in the windless cold. Over its door hung no sign but a single key carved from gray wood, polished smooth by centuries of hands.

Casper stopped at the threshold. Something in her chest — some instinct older than her memory, which was not hard to be older than — told her she had stood here before.

"I know this place," she said.

"You said that about the last three farmhouses," Noor murmured. "And the sheep."

"The sheep looked familiar."

He smiled, tired and crooked, and knocked.

* * *

The man who opened the door was ancient in the way of trees — bent, weathered, and clearly built to outlast everyone who mocked him for it. He had a broker's ink stains on three fingers and a guild sigil worked into his scarf in thread so fine it was nearly invisible. His eyes went from Casper to Noor to the road behind them, cataloguing, appraising, deciding.

"Vey's people," he said. It wasn't a question. "I heard the guildhall burned. Heard Vey burned with it."

"He did," Noor said quietly. "Defending the ledgers."

The old man closed his eyes for a breath. When he opened them, the appraisal was gone and something rawer sat behind it. "Then come in before the road hears you're here. Names inside. On the road, you're nobody, and nobody is still alive this week."

Inside, the inn was warm in a way that felt deliberate, like a held breath. A fire in a hearth big enough to roast an ox. A kettle already singing. And along every wall, floor to ceiling, shelves — but not for bottles or plates. The shelves held jars, vials, boxes small as thimbles and large as coffers, each labeled in a cramped antique hand.

Casper stared. "This is a vault."

"This is an inn," the old man corrected. "Inn of Borrowed Years. The name is literal, girl; it usually is." He shuffled to the kettle. "Milo Ferren. Junior broker, retired sixty years, unretired forty. You'll take stew, both of you, and you'll eat all of it, and then we'll talk about why two fugitives are carrying covenant terms in a spice crate."

Noor went very still. "How—"

"You smell like cardamom and desperation," Milo said. "Sit."

* * *

The stew was lamb and barley and something sweet Casper couldn't identify, and she ate until her hands stopped shaking. Milo watched them eat without eating himself, turning a small brass weight over and over in his stained fingers — a memory-weight, she realized, the kind used to measure sold years by the ounce.

When the bowls were empty, he finally spoke.

"Serah Kolt passed through here eleven days ago. Alone. Asked if I'd seen anyone carrying guild marks." He tapped the nearly invisible sigil on his scarf. "I told her the last broker through here died before her mother was born. She wrote it down. They always write it down." He set the brass weight on the table between them. "You have perhaps five days before she retraces this road and checks my arithmetic."

"Five days," Noor repeated. He glanced at Casper, and she knew he was doing what he always did — counting, weighing, balancing a ledger only he could see. "The terms say the dark of the moon. Six days."

"Then you have one day of margin and none of luck." Milo leaned back. "Show me."

Noor fetched the crate from their packs and unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside lay the covenant's original terms — three sheets of vellum covered in paired columns, human script on the left, Veil-script on the right, and down the center a line of binding-glyphs that made Casper's teeth ache when she looked at them directly, like biting tinfoil.

Milo read for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a jar hummed softly to itself, the way stored memories did when the house was quiet.

"Mortal pays in kind," Milo read aloud at last. His voice had gone strange. "The Veil gave its memory to anchor the first covenant. So mortals must give—" He stopped.

"Give what?" Casper asked.

He looked at her, then at Noor, and Casper saw something cross his face that she had seen once on Oren Vey's face, in the last hour before the raid: the look of a man who has known a bill was coming and has just heard it called due.

"Noor," Milo said gently. "Read your own side."

* * *

Noor read it twice. Casper watched him do it — watched the candlelight move across his face, watched the moment the words stopped being words and became a shape, a price, a thing with his name already penciled faintly beside it.

"The Veil surrendered its memory," he said slowly. "All of it. Everything it was, poured into the anchor-stone so the border would hold." His voice stayed level, which was how Casper knew it cost him. "Payment in kind. Mortals must surrender their armor. Not steel. The thing each mortal carries instead of trust."

"And yours?" Milo asked, though Casper could see he already knew. Perhaps all brokers knew. Perhaps it was written somewhere in the way Noor held his cup, tested a hundred times against death and never once flinched.

"My immunity." Noor set the cup down. "Every poison I've ever survived. All of it. Surrendered." He laughed — one short, airless note. "Do you know how many that is? I stopped counting at four hundred. Hemlock. Arsenic. Belladonna. Things the guild brewed themselves that don't have names. My body is a ledger too, Milo. Every entry gets erased."

The fire crackled. Upstairs, a jar hummed a little louder, as if listening.

"No," Casper said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't planned to speak; the word had simply arrived, fully armed.

"There has to be another way. Another mortal with armor worth taking. There are thousands of gifted left, there's—"

"They're not paying, Casper." Noor's voice was soft. "That's what *in kind* means. It isn't a tax collected from whoever's standing nearby. It's a debt owed by whoever broke the faith. The guild sold the anchor-stone. The guild raised me. The guild taught my tongue its trade." He spread his hands. "There isn't another way. There's only whether I pretend otherwise for six days first."

Casper stood up. She didn't remember deciding to stand. Her chair scraped back and she was upright and her heart was hammering and she could not have said, honestly, why it hurt this much — except that some fragment buried under all her traded-away years apparently understood exactly what it would mean to watch him become mortal, and that fragment was screaming.

"You don't get to decide alone," she said. "Not this. We decide together or not at all."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, and under it, briefly, a grief so old it looked like relief. "Together," he agreed.

* * *

Later, Milo showed them to rooms under the eaves, narrow beds under sloped ceilings, the walls lined with more shelving. Before he left, the old broker paused in the doorway.

"A word, keeper," he said to Casper.

She followed him down to the landing, where a single jar sat on a shelf apart from the others, wrapped in black cloth. Milo didn't touch it. He rested his knuckles on the shelf beside it, the way a man rests his hand on the shoulder of an old friend.

"I've been keeping this for thirty years," he said. "A deposit. Left with instructions: *if the covenant terms ever surface again, give the keeper her choice back.*"

Casper's mouth went dry. "Whose memory?"

"Yours, Ilsette." He said the name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "One night of it. The night you chose exile. The vaults took everything else, but you paid one fragment out separately, to be held in escrow." His eyes were very kind and very old. "It's yours to open or leave sealed. But if you mean to stand at that threshold in six days, I'd want to walk in knowing who I am. Even if it costs."

She reached toward the black cloth and stopped, her fingers trembling an inch away.

From below, faintly, she heard Noor turn over in his bed. The floorboards announced it. Even exhausted, even facing his own erasure, he slept lightly — as if some part of him was always keeping watch over whatever room she was in.

"Not tonight," she said, and drew her hand back. "Tonight I'm just a woman in an inn. Tomorrow I can be whoever that jar says I am."

Milo nodded slowly. "That's the wisest thing a broker ever taught me," he said. "Bills keep. Warmth doesn't."

* * *

But sleep would not keep, and neither would she. Near midnight Casper found herself downstairs again, wrapped in a blanket, sitting by the low embers. The shelves breathed around her in the dark — decades of borrowed things, humming their separate songs.

Noor came down twenty minutes later. Neither of them pretended surprise.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he asked, settling beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"I was trying to remember what warm feels like from before," she admitted. "I don't have a before. I have a name other people whisper at me on roads, and a job I was apparently good at, and you." She turned to look at him. In the ember-light his face was all planes and shadows, and terribly easy to look at. "That's the whole inventory. And tomorrow I find out the rest costs a night I apparently couldn't bear to keep in the ordinary vaults."

"It might not be bad," he offered.

"It might be terrible. It might be the reason I paid extra to forget it." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Six days, Noor. Six days and then the dark of the moon, and then you pour four hundred survivals out of your body onto a stone, and I—" She stopped. Started again. "What happens to us? After? If the Veil seals and I'm whole again and you're just... a man who can die from a bad mussel?"

He was quiet for long enough that the fire settled twice.

"I don't know," he said finally. "I've been trying to balance that column for three days and it won't balance. Whatever I put on one side, the other side goes heavier." He turned his palm up, an old taster's habit, offering it flat and honest. "But I know what I'd choose. If I got to keep one thing out of the payment — one poison, one survival, anything — I'd keep the night you learned to laugh at my jokes. That one doesn't feel like armor. That one feels like the actual point."

Casper looked at his open hand. Then, before the fear or the mystery or the jar waiting upstairs could stop her, she leaned over and kissed him — clumsy, ember-warm, tasting of barley stew and woodsmoke and the specific terror of people running out of time.

When she pulled back, he was smiling, and she thought: *there. That's the entry. That's the one I'd save.*

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the wrapped jar on Milo's shelf began — very softly — to sing.
