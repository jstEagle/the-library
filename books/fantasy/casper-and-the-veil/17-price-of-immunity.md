## 17. Price of Immunity

The envoy's demand hung in the air like smoke that refused to rise.

Noor read the covenant terms a third time, his finger tracing the faded ink on vellum that had survived the burning of the guildhall, a week on the salt road, and Serah's hunters, and somehow still smelled faintly of the vault's cedar lining. The script was old — older than the guild, older than the crown — and it did not soften what it asked.

*The Veil gave its memory. Mortals shall give their armor. Payment in kind, or no covenant at all.*

"Read it aloud," the envoy said. Casper's kin, though the resemblance was a thing of angles rather than features — the same stillness in the shoulders, the same way of holding the head as if listening to a room no one else could hear. Where Casper was warmth, this one was winter. "Aloud, taster. The old law requires witness. What is signed in silence can be unwound in silence."

Noor's throat was dry. He had not eaten since dawn, and the gray wound behind the envoy's shoulder pulsed like a second heartbeat, fraying the edges of the world. Grass at the threshold's rim grew in spirals. A bird crossed the sky and crossed it again, the same bird, the same arc, three times before the envoy flicked one finger and let it go.

He read it aloud. His voice came out steadier than he expected. "The Veil gave its memory."

"Every year of it," the envoy said. "Every name, every face, every grief and gladness, poured into the stone that held your sky closed against our sky. We did not haggle. We did not ask for a lesser price. We forgot our children's names so that your children could keep theirs."

"Mortals shall give their armor." The word *armor* sat strangely on his tongue. He looked at Casper. She stood at the edge of the threshold, her hands folded, her face pale in the gray light — but she was watching him with an expression he had learned to read on the road, in inns, in the narrow hours before dawn. She had already understood. He could see it. She had understood before he did, and she was letting him arrive at it himself, because she knew him, and she knew that if she said it first he would spend the rest of his life wondering whether he'd chosen it or been led to it.

"Mortals shall give their armor," the envoy repeated. "What armor does a mortal wear that the Veil does not? Think, taster. You have worn it your whole life. You have worn it so long you mistake it for skin."

Noor thought of the banquet, three years ago, the hemlock in the chancellor's wine. He thought of the taste of it — bitter green, like the memory of a forest — and of how he had swallowed his mouthful and smiled at the duke and said nothing, and how the poison had slid through him and found nothing to hold. He thought of every poison since: the arsenic in a merchant's honey, the nightshade in a princess's perfume, the lead in a rival's paint, the dozen small deaths the guild had fed him as a boy in a cellar that smelled of tallow and ink, building him the way a smith builds a blade — folding him, quenching him, tempering him against everything meant to kill.

*Your armor,* the envoy said, in the silence of his own skull, though its mouth had not moved.

"No," Casper said.

It was the first word she had spoken since they'd reached the threshold, and it cracked across the gray air like a struck bell.

"No," she said again, stepping forward, and the envoy's gaze slid to her with something that might, in a softer creature, have been pity. "You don't get to ask that. He's not — the terms say *mortals*. It doesn't have to be him. It could be anyone. It could be—"

"It could be anyone who has armor to give," the envoy said. "Name one. Name a single soul in your realm who has spent a lifetime forging themselves against death, who has swallowed every poison their world could brew and lived, whose body is a ledger of survived deaths. Name one, and we will take them instead."

Casper's mouth opened. Closed. There was no one. There had never been anyone but Noor. The guild had made exactly one thing like him, in exactly one cellar, for exactly one purpose, and the purpose had never been this — or maybe it always had been, and no one had ever had the honesty to write it down.

"Noor," Casper said, and his name in her mouth was the oldest thing in either realm. "Don't."

* * *

He made her sit down first. That was the part she would remember, later, when she remembered nothing — that he had insisted on the small dignity of it, guiding her to a flat stone at the threshold's edge, pressing a skin of water into her hands, waiting until she drank. As if the signing were a meal and he were still, after everything, the taster.

"The envoy said payment in kind," he told her. He crouched so their eyes were level. The gray light made her look older and younger at once, the way candlelight did. "The Veil gave memory. We give armor. It's not a riddle, Cas. It's arithmetic. I'm the only one with the coin."

"You don't know what it will do to you."

"No," he admitted. "But I know what it did to you. You gave up everything you were, and the world kept turning, and you kept walking. I watched you do it for a year before I knew that's what I was watching." He smiled, and was surprised to find the smile was real. "I've had a good teacher."

"That's not — Noor, that's not the same. I chose to forget. You'd be choosing to be *killable*. Every poison you ever survived comes back. All of them. At once, or over a lifetime, or however the Veil decides to collect — you don't know. You'd be a man made of held breath, and you'd be letting go of all of it in one room."

"Yes," he said.

"That's your whole argument? *Yes?*"

"It's the only one that's true." He took her hands. They were cold, and he warmed them the way he'd learned to on the road, without thinking, the way he did everything for her now — without thinking, which was its own kind of covenant. "Cas. I spent my whole life tasting death so other people wouldn't have to. That was the job. Nobody thanked me, and I didn't need thanking, because the job was the point. Keep the chancellor alive. Keep the princess alive. Keep the kingdom breathing. And then I found the ledgers, and I learned that the kingdom I'd been keeping alive had broken its own covenant and blamed the ones who kept it, and I thought — I thought everything I'd swallowed had been for nothing." His voice caught, and he let it. "It wasn't for nothing. It was for *this*. Every hemlock, every nightshade, every year in that cellar — it was all leading here, to the one door that only I can open, because I'm the only one who paid the toll in advance. You don't throw away a life's work at the last step. You *spend* it. That's what it was for."

"The guild raised you to be a tool."

"The guild raised me to be a shield. There's a difference, and I finally know what it is." He squeezed her hands. "A tool gets used. A shield gets *chosen*. I'm choosing."

"You're choosing to die."

"I'm choosing to be mortal." He laughed — a small, unsteady sound. "Do you know what I felt when the riders took the cup-mending child in the square? Not fear. *Recognition.* I knew her. I've been her. The thing they take you for, the thing that makes the crowd step back — I've been carrying it since I was nine years old, and I've just been better at hiding it. Serah was right about me. The crown was right. I am marked. I've always been marked. The only question was what the mark was *for*." He looked past her, at the wound in the world, at the envoy waiting with a patience older than patience. "It was for this. It was always for this."

Casper was crying. She did it silently, the way she did everything, tears sliding down a face that had forgotten how to be anything but composed — and then, abruptly, not composed at all. She pulled him to her and held on with the desperation of someone who had already lost everything once and knew precisely what losing felt like from the inside.

"If it kills you," she said into his shoulder, "I will walk into that wound after you, and I will tell your kin exactly what I think of their arithmetic."

"Tell them," he said into her hair. "They seem to respect a good grievance."

She laughed through the tears, one broken note, and held him a moment longer. Then she let him go, because she was Casper, and Casper had never once stood between a person and the price they had decided to pay. She had only ever stood beside them, and written it down.

* * *

The signing was not what he expected. There was no table, no ink, no quill. The envoy drew a circle in the air and the gray light gathered into it, and the vellum of the old terms rose from Noor's pack and hung in the circle, turning slowly, and where his signature belonged there was a blank space shaped like nothing he had ever seen — a space that seemed to wait for a taste rather than a name.

"How does it work?" he asked. "The payment. What happens to me?"

"The poisons return to you," the envoy said. "Not as death. As *weight*. Every death you refused will live in you from this hour forward, as mortality lives in every mortal — as the possibility of it. You will taste hemlock and it may kill you. You will breathe nightshade and it may kill you. You will be, for the first time since you were a child, exactly as fragile as everyone you ever protected." The envoy's winter eyes held his. "The Veil does not lie in its contracts, taster. I will tell you the cost plainly, because your guild never did: you will spend the rest of a shorter life afraid in a way you have never been afraid. Every meal. Every cup. Every breath of unfamiliar air. That is the armor you are surrendering. Not a coat. A *certainty*."

Noor thought about it. He was surprised to find that he could think about it calmly, the way he thought about a dish — weighing, measuring, tasting the shape of the thing.

He had been afraid his whole life, actually. Everyone assumed the taster feared nothing, and everyone was wrong. He had feared the ledgers being found. He had feared Casper's empty eyes. He had feared, every single day, that the two lives he lived would collide and destroy each other. Fear had never needed poison to reach him. All the immunity in the world had never kept it out.

"You're selling me a thing I already own," he said. "Fine. I've been cheated by better."

He reached into the circle.

The vellum did not take his blood or his name. It took the taste of him — he felt it go, felt the circle read him the way a tongue reads a spoon, cataloguing every poison his body had ever refused, every death declined, every bitter green and mineral white and sweet almond lie. It took the hemlock from the chancellor's banquet. It took the arsenic from the merchant's honey, the nightshade from the princess's perfume, the lead from the rival's paint. It took the twelve years of the cellar, the tallow and the ink and the small cups the guildmasters had held to his lips with hands that never trembled, saying *drink, drink, you were made for this* — and it took the child he had been, the one who had cried the first time and been told that crying was a flaw in the tempering, and it took the armor that child had grown instead of a childhood.

It took all of it. And when it was done, Noor Yarrow stood at the threshold of the Veil as ordinary as bread.

He felt the difference immediately, and it was nothing like he expected. He had expected lightness. What he felt was *heaviness* — a warm, mortal, terrifying weight, like a coat he'd forgotten he'd owned, like gravity finally being allowed to have him. His own heartbeat sounded loud. The air tasted of dust and grass and nothing else, and for the first time in twenty years, tasting nothing felt like falling.

He sat down hard on the stone.

"Noor—" Casper was beside him instantly, her hands on his face, turning it toward her, searching his eyes for the thing people search for when they are afraid they've already lost someone. "Talk to me. What does it feel like?"

"Like dinner," he said. His voice came out rough. "Like I'm about to have dinner, and for the first time in my life, I don't know what's in it."

She made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed her forehead to his.

"Is it done?" she asked the envoy. "Is his part paid?"

"The mortal armor is surrendered," the envoy said. "The covenant's mortal side stands. But the covenant has two sides, keeper, and the stone is cracked, and the Veil is still bleeding." Its gaze moved to Casper, and Noor saw her go still in his arms — saw her understand, in the same slow terrible way he had understood an hour ago, what the second half of the arithmetic was going to ask of her. "The anchor-stone was made of Veil-memory. It is ash. The new anchor must be made of the same. And there is only one fragment of the Veil's memory left in your realm, envoy."

Casper's hand found Noor's and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

"My name," she said quietly. "The fragment. It's him. It's the memory of him. It's the only thing I have."

The envoy said nothing. The gray wound pulsed. Somewhere beyond the threshold, in the fraying world, a bell was ringing the hour, and Noor held her hand and understood that the covenant was not finished with them — that it had saved its cruelest clause for last, and that the woman who had already given everything was about to be asked for the one thing she had left.

"Six days," the envoy said. "Until the dark of the moon. Then we learn what the keeper is willing to forget."
