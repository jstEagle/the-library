## 20. Strangers at the Door

The door of the rebuilt guildhall was new oak, pale as unbleached linen, and Casper had polished it three times that morning for no reason she could name except that her hands wanted something to do.

She had been in the guildhall for nine days now. That was the whole of her life, as far as she could measure it: nine days of waking in a narrow room above the counting floor, of learning the stairs by the way they creaked, of standing in the great hall where the memory-vaults had burned and smelling, faintly, always, of smoke that was no longer there. Nine days of being a woman with no yesterday.

They had told her what she was. A memory-keeper. An envoy. A woman who had given up everything she was to seal a covenant between two worlds. They had told her the name of the man who had loved her before her forgetting, and the name meant nothing, a coin from a country she'd never visited. They had told her she had traded her final fragment — the memory of choosing — to become the anchor-stone's replacement, and that the Veil held because of her, and that both realms breathed easier for her sacrifice.

She believed them. That was the strange part. She believed them the way one believes weather.

What she could not do was feel it. Grief without a subject. Love without a face. She walked through the rebuilt guildhall like a guest in a house that kept almost recognizing her.

The tea, at least, the tea she knew.

That was the one mercy of her condition: her hands remembered what her mind did not. On her second morning she had gone down to the kitchen, opened the cupboard, and made herself a cup of smoked silverleaf steeped four minutes with a curl of dried pear and one clove, crushed but not ground, and she had drunk it and wept without knowing why, and then drunk a second cup. No one had taught her the recipe. It had simply arrived in her hands, like a letter from someone who no longer had an address.

She made it every morning now. It was the closest thing she had to a self.

* * *

The knock came at midday, when the autumn light came through the new windows at a slant and turned the counting floor to honey.

Casper had been reorganizing a shelf of ledgers that Noor — the man with the kind, careful eyes, the one who visited every evening and told her one true story — had left for her to catalog. She had learned that she liked ledgers. That had surprised everyone, including her. Numbers, it turned out, were a kind of memory that didn't hurt. Every column balanced was a small promise the world had kept.

The knock was three raps, unhurried, spaced like a sentence with pauses in it.

She wiped her hands on her apron and crossed the hall. Through the new oak she could see a blur of a figure — tall, broad-shouldered, standing with the particular stillness of someone who had rehearsed this moment and then forgotten his lines.

She opened the door.

The man on the step was ordinary in the way that made her chest ache, though she could not have said why. Brown hair going gray at the temples. A coat with a mended cuff. Hands that looked like hands that had held dangerous things carefully for a very long time. He was holding a paper-wrapped parcel against his chest like a shield, and when the door opened he blinked at her as if the sun had come out in the wrong place.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello," said Casper.

"I'm—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm Noor."

"I know," she said. "You come every evening. You tell me stories."

"Yes." He looked at the parcel. Looked at her. The pause stretched. "I — this isn't an evening visit. I'm sorry. I know the evenings are — we agreed the evenings were for the stories, and I didn't want to—"

"You're rambling," Casper said.

"I am," he agreed, with a small, helpless laugh, and something in the sound of it snagged on something in her, a hook finding old cloth, and she had to grip the doorframe. "I'm sorry. I practiced this. I had a whole speech."

"About what?"

"About how I'm going to stop being a stranger." He said it plainly, no drama in it, and somehow that was worse. "Or — how I'm going to try. I can't give you back what you gave. I know that. I've made my peace with the fact that you don't remember me, and I'm not here to — I'm not going to stand on your step and narrate our history at you like a debt collector. That would be monstrous. But I thought—" He lifted the parcel slightly. "I thought maybe I could stop being a stranger in the ordinary ways. The small ways. If you'd let me."

Casper looked at him for a long moment. The light was doing something unkind to his face, showing her every line of it, and her heart was doing something unkind to her, beating like a bird against a window at the sight of a man she did not know.

"What's in the parcel?" she asked.

His whole face changed — relief, terror, hope, all at once, like weather over water. "Tea," he said. "Smoked silverleaf. And dried pear. And one clove."

The hall tilted, very slightly, the way a ship tilts.

"How do you know that's my tea?" she asked. Her voice came out careful, level, a taster's voice, though she had never tasted anything in her life that she knew of.

"Because I've watched you make it a hundred times," he said. "Because I used to bring you the silverleaf from the harbor market every month for six years, because you said the guild's supplier scorched it. Because you take the clove whole and crush it with the back of the spoon and you always say—" he stopped, and his eyes went bright and wet and he laughed at himself, "—you always say *not ground, it turns bitter*, and I have heard you say it so many times that when I stood in the spice shop this morning I heard it in your voice."

Casper did not say anything. She could not. There was a pressure behind her ribs like a door held shut by weather.

"Anyway," Noor said, and held out the parcel. "I thought you should have your own supply. Since you live here now. Since the kitchen is yours. I—" His hand wavered. "I don't actually know how to do this, Casper. I know how to survive poison and balance impossible ledgers and lie to inquisitors. I don't know how to court a woman who has no memory of me. There's no ledger for it. I've looked."

And Casper — mortal, memoryless, nine days old — did the only thing her hands knew how to do.

She stepped back from the door and said, "You'd better come in and show me how you crush the clove. Because if you do it wrong, it turns bitter."

* * *

The kitchen of the rebuilt guildhall was new, but it had been built on the old foundations, and Casper had already noticed that some things survived fire the way bones survive a body — invisible, structural, load-bearing. The kettle knew the stove. The cups knew the shelf.

Noor stood at her counter with the parcel open, silverleaf and pear and one clove laid out like instruments for surgery, and he was nervous. She could see it in the set of his shoulders, in the way he checked the water three times. It was, she thought, absurd. This was a man, by all accounts, who had faced down a royal inquisitor and an exiled envoy and the price of a covenant. And he was afraid of a teaspoon.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm assessing," she said. "Go on."

He crushed the clove with the back of the spoon — one firm press, not a grinding — and Casper felt the smell of it move through the kitchen like a hand passing over her hair. Something behind the shut door of her pressed its ear to the wood.

"Four minutes," Noor said. "Not three. You always said three was for people who were afraid of their own tea."

"Did I?" Casper said. "That sounds like something I'd say."

"It is." He glanced at her, quick and shy. "Was. Is. I don't — the tenses are a nightmare."

The kettle sang. He poured. The silverleaf uncurled in the water like something waking, and the pear-sweetness rose with the smoke, and Casper stood in her own kitchen watching a stranger make her tea and felt, for the first time in nine days, that the house recognized her.

He set the cup in front of her and sat down across the table with his own, and for a while neither of them spoke. The steam went up between them. Outside, a cart went by; somewhere a child was counting stairs.

"It's good," Casper said at last.

"Thank you."

"You're still nervous."

"I'm sitting in your kitchen drinking tea with the woman I love, who doesn't know me," Noor said. "I'm going to be nervous for approximately the rest of my life. I've made my peace with it."

Casper turned her cup. The clove sat at the bottom like a small dark moon.

"Tell me a small thing," she said. "Not a story. The evening stories are — I like them, I do. But they're history. They're about a woman who isn't me anymore. Tell me a small thing about now."

Noor thought about it. She watched him think; he had a way of going very still when he was deciding something, like a man testing ice.

"Small thing," he said finally. "The first morning after — after everything, after the Veil sealed, I woke up in the guildhall and I went down to make tea and I stood in this kitchen and I couldn't remember how you took it. I stood here with the clove in my hand and I couldn't — I've known you for six years, and grief had eaten the one recipe I cared about." He turned his cup too, matching her. "I had to ask Oren's old apprentice. He knew. Everyone knew. Apparently I'm the only man in the city who can recite the guild's hidden ledgers from memory but can't hold onto how his—" he stopped. Recalibrated. "How the woman he loves takes her tea."

"You just did it perfectly."

"I practiced," he admitted. "Last night. In my rooms. With a clove and a spoon. Twice."

And Casper laughed.

It came out of her before she could catch it, surprising her with its shape — and Noor went absolutely still, staring at her across the rising steam, with an expression she would spend a long time learning to read: as if she had set down something very heavy he had been carrying alone.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," he said, and his voice wasn't steady. "You just — you laugh the same. That's all. Everything else is different and the laugh is the same."

The pressure behind her ribs again. The door with the weather against it. She drank her tea and let it be.

* * *

He stayed an hour, then two. They cataloged ledgers. He was, she discovered, ruthlessly good at it, and she caught herself competing with him — column against column — and catching him letting her win, and telling him she'd noticed, and him saying with great dignity that he would never do such a thing, and her laughing again, twice in one day, which felt like a small riot.

When the light began to go amber, he stood to leave, and Casper found that she did not want him to go, and did not know what to do with not wanting, so she said the first practical thing that came into her head:

"You said you bring the silverleaf from the harbor market."

"Every month."

"Then bring it here," she said. "Instead of to — wherever you brought it before. I live here now. The kitchen is mine. If you're going to stop being a stranger in the small ways, you can start by supplying them."

Noor looked at her for a moment with an expression she was beginning to suspect meant *I love you* in a language she used to speak.

"That's not a small thing," he said quietly. "That's a standing appointment. That's a life."

"It's a delivery," Casper said. "Don't make it heavy. It turns bitter."

He laughed at that — really laughed, head back, and she thought: *oh, I did love that sound. I must have. Some part of me is still in love with it, because it feels like coming home to a house I've never seen.*

"First of the month," he said. "I'll be at the door."

"Three knocks," she said. "Unhurried. Like a sentence with pauses in it."

"I've always knocked like that."

"I know," said Casper, and did not know how she knew, and let it be true.

He went to the door, and paused with his hand on the new oak, and turned back. For a moment he looked like he was going to say something enormous — the speech, maybe, the one he'd practiced, the one about debts and histories and the shape of six years. She watched him decide against it. She watched him choose, instead, the small way.

"The tea's better in your kitchen," he said. "It was always better in your kitchen. I just never told you, because you were very proud of your method and I didn't want to disrupt the ritual."

"Get out," said Casper, and he went, grinning, and she stood in the doorway and watched him cross the yard in the amber light, and she raised one hand, and he raised his, and neither of them waved, exactly. It was more like a salute between people who had been through something together, though she could not have said what.

* * *

That evening she sat at the kitchen table with her cup and the clove at the bottom, and she did the thing she had started doing every night, which was to take inventory.

Nine days of self, and here is what she had: she liked ledgers. She liked mornings. She was proud of her method. She laughed easily, which seemed, from the stories, to be a survival rather than a trait. She made tea like a woman performing a small sacrament, and the recipe was older than her memory, and somewhere in her hands lived six years of a man bringing silverleaf from the harbor market, and her hands had not forgotten him even though she had.

She thought about the door with the weather against it — the shut place where her past was, the place that ached when he laughed. She thought about what Noor had told her of the covenant, of the price she'd paid, of the fragment she'd given: the memory of choosing. *She chose this,* the others said. *She chose him, and she chose the Veil, and she gave up the choosing to save everything.*

Casper looked into her cup, at the small dark moon of clove.

"Whoever you were," she said aloud to the woman who had made that choice, "you were right about the tea."

And then, because she was a keeper of accounts in whatever language her bones still spoke, she opened a fresh page in one of the blank ledgers Noor had left, and at the top, in a careful hand, she wrote:

*Inventory of a new life. Item one: smoked silverleaf, four minutes, pear, one clove — not ground, it turns bitter.*

*Item two: a man who knocks in sentences.*

She sat with the pen hovering. There should have been more. There should have been pages. There had been, once — a whole vault's worth, decades of traded lives, a history with two realms in it, a love with a before. All of it spent. All of it holding up the sky.

But the page was not empty. That was the thing she kept discovering, night after night, and it kept astonishing her: a blank ledger is not an empty one. It is a promise with the columns waiting.

She wrote *item three: he lets me win at cataloging, and I let him think I don't notice* — and heard, faintly, from the counting floor above which her kitchen had been built, the sound of the front door, though she had locked it, though no knock had come.

She went up with a candle.

The great hall was empty. The new oak door was shut. But on the threshold, just inside, where no draft could have carried it, lay a single leaf of smoked silverleaf — harbor-market quality, unscorched, fresh — as if the house itself had decided to begin the inventory early.

Casper picked it up. It was warm.

Somewhere beyond the Veil she had sealed with her own forgotten self, something on the other side had noticed her, and had sent a calling card.

She stood in the empty hall with the warm leaf in her palm, mortal and memoryless and nine days old, and felt the first true fear of her new life — and, underneath it, unmistakable, the first true thrill.

*Item four,* she thought, going back down to her ledger. *The house remembers more than I do. Find out what it knows.*

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a bell rang once — the hour, or a warning, or a welcome. She wrote until the candle guttered.
