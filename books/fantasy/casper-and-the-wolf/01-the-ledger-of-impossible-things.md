## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The ledgers smelled of tallow and salt and the particular melancholy of old paper—paper that had been handled by too many hands that hoped to be forgotten. Oscar Eriksen ran her thumb down the column of figures and let the smell settle over her like a second coat. In the counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things, at the hour when the harbor fog pressed its gray face against the shutters, this was the closest thing to safety the world offered: a page, a rule, an arithmetic that could not lie.

She was good at not being seen. She had been practicing it since she was nine years old, when her mother first caught her drawing the river's course in the dirt and the river *obliged*, bending three feet eastward overnight so that the miller's wheel turned wrong and the whole village spoke of omens for a season. A gift for mapping living realms—the kind of thing kings burned for and courts bought cheap. So she had folded herself into clerical drudgery the way one folds a letter: small enough to hide, creased where it would never be read.

"Oskar." Corvin's voice through the door, gravel over ice. "Numbers."

"Always numbers, guildmaster," she called back, pitching her voice a half-step lower than it wanted to sit. Oskar Eriksen, clerk. Thin-voiced, stooped at his desk, dull as dishwater. She had built him out of ink and habit, and he had held for eleven years.

Corvin did not come in. His boots went on down the corridor toward the salt-room, where the smuggled goods were weighed, and Oscar let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

Above her, in the rafters, something shifted.

She didn't look up. Looking up was unnecessary; looking up was, in its way, a confession. But she felt him there all the same, the way you feel weather coming—a pressure behind the eyes, a coldness in the teeth. Casper had taken to the rafters again. He liked the dark between the beams, where the dust hung like unmelted snow and no light reached except what he brought with him, which was none, because Casper cast no shadow at all.

*You're dripping,* she said, without speaking, without moving her lips. It was a private channel between them, older than either of them fully understood. *On my ledger.*

*I am not dripping,* came the answer, dry as autumn grass, amused. *I am condensing. There is a difference, and your ledger is too important for me to damage it. I know how you feel about your ledger.*

*I know how I feel about you dripping on it.*

A soft sound from above—not quite a laugh. Wolves do not laugh; whatever Casper was now, he had kept the wolf's grammar of joy, which was breath and stillness and the slow blink of pale eyes. He had been haunting these rafters for six years, ever since the night she'd found him half-drowned in a tide-pool beneath Vellmark's seawall, too large for any wolf, too *present* for any animal, watching her with eyes the gray of deep water. She had mapped the shore that day out of pure nervous habit, and the map had shown her a shape in the water that should not exist, and she had gone to look anyway, because curiosity was the flaw at the center of her character and always had been.

He owed her, he said. A debt. He never said for what. She had stopped asking around the third year.

Oscar dipped her pen and returned to the inventory. The Guild of Impossible Things kept, among other things, a catalog of acquisitions that would have made a bishop weep or laugh, depending on his temperament. Entry 447: a lantern that burned only in the presence of lies (currently leaking, handle cracked). Entry 512: a key that opened nothing but was very warm to hold. Entry 619: a compass whose needle pointed, unfailingly, at regret.

Entry 619 had come in off a Vellmark wherry eight months ago, traded by a sailor who said he'd won it at cards from a man who wept the entire game. Oscar remembered writing the line. She remembered the scratch of the nib, the fog against the shutters, Casper's weight settling onto the beam above her like a decision. Some entries stayed with you. That one had stayed because she'd written *regret* and thought, briefly, of the bend in the river outside her mother's village, and of everything else that bent when she touched it with her mind.

She checked the shelf.

The compass was gone.

Not misplaced—gone. The slot between the lying-lantern and the warm key stood empty, its dust unbroken except for a single smear, deliberate, as though someone had lifted the object straight up and away. Oscar set down her pen with exaggerated care, the way one does when the alternative is to throw it.

"Casper," she said aloud this time, because some things deserved air.

He dropped from the rafters the way snow falls upward—no sound at all until the moment of landing, and then only a softness, a displacement of air that smelled faintly of pine and cold rivers. He stood taller than a wolf had any right to stand, his coat the color of wet slate, his eyes catching the lamplight and giving none of it back. When he moved his paws left no prints. She had checked, once, obsessively, early on. Nothing. As if the floor declined to remember him.

"You saw who took it?" she asked.

"No one took it." He circled the shelf once, twice, nose working. "That is what troubles me. Someone entered, someone left, and the room remembers neither. But—" He paused at the desk, at her open ledger, and his ears flattened by degrees. "Your book has been moved."

"It has not."

"It has. It sits two fingers' width further from the lamp than it sat yesterday evening. You are exacting, Oscar. Two fingers is a shout."

She looked. He was right. The faint ring of dust where the ledger's corner usually rested had been disturbed, wiped clean by a careless sleeve or a careful glove. And when she opened the book to Entry 619, she found the page itself subtly wrong—not torn, not marked, but *handled*. The paper had the limp warmth of a thing recently breathed upon. Someone had read her records. Not skimmed them. Read them closely enough to find the compass, and quietly enough to leave no fingerprints, and then had walked out through a locked warehouse in a city full of royal criers, leaving nothing behind but a two-finger shift in the position of a book.

Her skin went cold in the way it went cold before storms, before patrols, before every bad thing that had ever taught her to keep her head down and her hands on the arithmetic.

* * *

The fog thickened toward evening, rolling up from the harbor in slow gray banks until Vellmark became a rumor of itself—the gulls crying somewhere overhead like doubts, the lamplighters' flames swallowed to smudges, the salt-stink of the warehouses sharpened by damp. Oscar closed the counting room, locked it, locked it again out of ritual, and made her rounds of the guildhouse with the lamp held high.

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a decommissioned rope-walk near the Salt Gate, four long buildings strung together like beads on a cord, home at any given hour to perhaps twenty souls: Sable Ives the forger, who slept days and forged nights and called it keeping honest hours; Maren and her boys, who ran contraband upriver under loads of lawful herring; Old Tetch, who claimed to be retired and was merely unemployed; a rotating population of debtors, exiles, and the occasionally gifted, all of whom had found their way to Corvin's door because Corvin had a talent for collecting people the world had finished with. Found family, Sable called it, laughing. Oscar had never dared call it anything at all. Naming things gave them edges, and edges could be traced, and traced things could be taken.

Casper paced beside her through the corridors, silent on boards that groaned under anyone else. The others knew him the way sailors know the sea: with respect, with stories, and without any desire to get closer. Maren crossed herself when she thought no one watched. Old Tetch left fish on the back step sometimes, which Casper ignored with magnificent disdain, and which Tetch continued to leave, which told Oscar more about hope than any sermon.

In the kitchen, Sable Ives sat hunched over a table doing what passed for relaxation in her life: practicing another woman's signature until it flowed from her pen like water finding a channel. She looked up as Oscar passed, and her eyes—quick, brown, miss-nothing eyes—flicked to the ledger under Oscar's arm, then to Casper, then back.

"Bad night?" Sable asked lightly.

"Inventory discrepancy," Oscar said. "One item."

"One item," Sable repeated, and there was something in the way she said it, something almost gentle, that made Oscar's spine prickle. But Sable only dipped her pen again and returned to her forgery, and the lamplight lay along her shoulders like a blade.

Outside, the fog had eaten even the Salt Gate's torches. Somewhere beyond it, muffled to a heartbeat, the city bells rang the ninth hour, and beneath the bells—so faint Oscar might have imagined it, and told herself she had imagined it—came the far-off cadence of a crier's bell, ringing in a district where no crier had business ringing at night.

Casper stopped at the threshold. His hackles rose in a slow wave, from shoulder to skull, like wind moving through grain.

"They're counting," he said softly. His voice was always a surprise, human-shaped and low, a voice that belonged to a throat he no longer had. "Someone in this city has begun counting things that cannot be counted. Can you feel it?"

Oscar could. Her gift stirred under her sternum like a second pulse, the way it always did when the world's shapes grew restless—maps wanting to redraw themselves, borders breathing in their sleep. And beneath the fog, beneath the bells, she felt the shape of Vellmark shifting by a hair's breadth, the way a river shifts before it floods.

She looked down at the ledger in her arms, at Entry 619's empty place, at the two-finger shift that shouldn't have mattered and mattered entirely.

Someone had read her records. Someone had taken a compass that pointed at regret. And tomorrow, she suspected, the whole city would learn why.
