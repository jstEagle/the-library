## 2. Edict of Ash

The criers came at dawn, before the gulls, before the fishwives, before the fog had finished deciding whether to be sea or sky.

Oscar heard them from the warehouse steps, where she stood with the day's first ledger open against her forearm like a shield. Their bells were wrong—too bright for Vellmark, too eager—and their voices carried the particular flatness of men reciting words they had not written and did not understand.

*By decree of the Crown Regent, all persons possessed of unnatural sight, unlicensed craft, or gift of any kind shall present themselves to the registrars within thirty days. Those who comply shall be marked, taxed, and bound to the crown's service. Those who conceal themselves shall be found. And those who are found shall burn.*

*Shall burn.* The phrase repeated down the salt-stained street, crier after crier, each one passing the sentence along like a bucket in a fire line, and by the third repetition it had stopped sounding like a threat and started sounding like weather. Something that would simply happen, to someone, somewhere, eventually—or today, or now, or here, on this exact stretch of cobblestone where Oscar Eriksen stood pretending to be a clerk with cold hands and no opinions.

She closed the ledger. Her fingers were steady. She noted that the way one notes a lie in someone else's account: precisely, and without believing it.

Behind her, through the warehouse door, the guild was already waking into its particular chaos—Sable Ives arguing ink prices with a smuggler named Brann, the twins hauling crates of mislabeled saffron toward the false wall, old Maren sweeping yesterday's forgery shavings into the stove because burning evidence was the closest thing the guild had to a morning prayer. And above it all, in the rafters where the lamplight gave out, Casper lay along a beam like a shadow that had decided to have weight, his yellow eyes fixed on her with the patience of something that had been watching her sleep for years and intended to keep doing it.

He knew before she said anything. He always knew first. The wolf-shaped spirit uncoiled from the rafter without sound, dropped the last twelve feet the way rain drops, and stood beside her on the step, shoulder nearly to her hip, close enough that his warmth bled through her coat like a second skin.

*Yes,* she almost said. *I know.*

She didn't say it. Saying things made them real, and Oscar Eriksen had built an entire life on the principle that unreal things could not be collected, taxed, or burned.

* * *

The guild meeting convened at noon in the back room, behind the shelf of ledgers nobody was supposed to read, which meant everyone read them, which meant they were perfect.

Corvin sat at the head of the table the way he always sat—at ease, expansive, a man who had talked his way out of so many corners that corners had begun to feel like home to him. He poured wine without asking who wanted it. He talked while pouring, which was how he thought: out loud, in circles, arriving.

"The edict is theater," he said. "It's always theater. A new regent needs an enemy the mob can see, and the Gifted are convenient—we're already strange, already whispered about, already blamed for the bad herring year. Thirty days from now there'll be a harvest crisis or a border squabble, and the registrars will be reassigned, and we'll all still be here selling impossible things to people who swear they don't believe in them." He drank. "We do nothing."

"We do nothing," Sable repeated, flat as slate. He was a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and his father's habit of never blinking when he lied, which was why everyone trusted him; a man who blinked when telling the truth was a man you could read. "And when they come door to door with the sight-sniffers? Because they will. There's talk of hounds bred to smell gift-work. Forged papers won't survive a hound."

"Forged papers," Corvin said, "have survived everything else."

"Not everything," Oscar said.

The room turned to her. It always took a moment—the guild saw her as furniture that balanced numbers, quiet Oskar the clerk, the one whose silences were so consistent they'd stopped registering as silences at all. When she spoke, people listened the way you listen to a clock that has suddenly chimed at the wrong hour.

"The edict isn't theater," she said. "I've been reading the registrar schedules that come through our own smuggling channels. They've doubled the patrols on the mountain roads. They've requisitioned pyre timber from three coastal towns—pyre timber, Corvin, that's not a line item you request for theater. And the registration offices aren't taking names." She kept her voice level, clerical, a voice designed to carry information and nothing else. "They're taking blood. A drop, pricked at the desk, tested on the spot. You can't forge your way past your own veins."

Silence, then, the real kind, the kind with weight. Somewhere above them the rafters ticked as the sun warmed the beams.

"So we run," Maren said. "North, over the ice passes, before the snow closes them."

"And go where?" Brann asked. "Every realm this side of the mountains has signed the regent's accord. The free cities are the last places left that haven't. Running means leaving the only ground where we can still stand."

"Then we fight," said one of the twins, who fought everything.

"With what?" Sable asked. "Good intentions?"

Oscar let them argue. Arguing was useful; arguing filled the room with noise that wasn't hers, and while they filled it she did what she always did, which was think in maps. Routes out of Vellmark. The salt marsh paths that drowned twice daily and were therefore unmapped and therefore safe. The disused mine shafts under the eastern hills where the air went bad but the air going bad was itself a kind of lock. She had a dozen ways out of the city drawn on the inside of her skull, precise as engraving, and not one of them could she show anyone, because showing them meant explaining how she knew, and explaining how she knew meant the pyre.

That was the arithmetic of her life, and it had always balanced. Safety purchased with silence. Belonging purchased with a lie. She had paid it gladly for eleven years, and told herself the price was fair.

But sitting at that table, listening to them debate flight and forged papers and fighting, she felt the ledger tilt. Because the truth was that the edict terrified her—not in the way it terrified them, as a practical problem with solutions, but in the older, deeper way, the way a childhood fear resurfaces whole. She had known since she was nine years old that her gift was a death sentence looking for its day. Every realm she'd ever mapped in her head, every border she'd watched breathe and shift, had been one more entry in a record that, if ever read aloud, ended in fire. She had hidden so long and so thoroughly that hiding was no longer something she did. It was something she was.

And hiding, she understood now, listening to the criers' bells fade, had a flaw the ledger finally exposed: it only worked if the world agreed not to look.

The world had stopped agreeing.

* * *

She argued for forging anyway. That was the irony she carried out of the meeting like a stone in her boot.

"Forge," she told them, when the argument wore itself down to embers. "Forge everything. Registration papers for those who'll risk the offices—blank ones, backdated, with registrar seals Sable can copy in his sleep. Travel writs for the northern passes. New names, new trades, dull histories. We make ourselves boring. The edict hunts the Gifted, so we stop being Gifted on paper. We become tanners and toll-keepers and widows with bad knees. Paper doesn't bleed when it's pricked, if the paper is good enough and the clerk is lazy enough, and registrar clerks are always lazy by the third week of a purge. They get tired. Tired men stamp things."

"You'd gamble our lives on clerks getting tired," Maren said.

"I'd gamble our lives on the thing I know best," Oscar said. "Records. Records are how power sees. If we control what the records say, we control what power sees." She paused, and heard herself, and hated the small thrill under the words—because it was true, and because saying it felt like standing closer to the window. "I've spent eleven years keeping this guild's records clean enough to pass any inspection. Let me keep us clean the same way."

Corvin looked at her for a long moment, wine glass turning slowly in his ringed fingers, and she could not read his face, and that frightened her more than the criers had, though she filed the fear away unexamined, in the drawer where she kept everything she couldn't afford to look at yet.

"All right," he said finally. "Forged papers. But quietly. No one works alone, no one leaves the warehouse after dark, and no one—" his eyes moved around the table, resting nowhere, "—does anything gifted where it can be seen. We survive this the way we survive everything. By being nothing at all."

Being nothing at all. Oscar nodded with the rest of them, and felt the words go through her like a needle finding an old scar. Eleven years of being nothing at all. She had gotten so good at it that even she sometimes lost the thread of the woman underneath the clerk, the way a map worn smooth by handling loses its coastlines.

She just hadn't expected the world to start demanding the coastline back.

* * *

Evening came down over Vellmark like ash settling, gray and fine and everywhere. From the warehouse roof, where she went to breathe, the city looked the same and entirely different: same crooked rooftops, same lantern-light bleeding up through the fog, but now every window was a question, every neighbor a possible informant, every bell in the distance a countdown.

Casper came up the roof ladder without using the ladder properly—he simply flowed up it, four legs and intention—and settled beside her at the roof's edge, close enough that his fur pressed warm against her arm. Below them, a patrol moved along the canal road with torches, six men and a dog, checking doors. The dog's breath steamed. Its handler held its lead short.

"They'll come here eventually," Oscar said, to the dark more than to him. "Everyone's door gets checked eventually."

Casper said nothing. He never spoke where others might hear, and rarely spoke at all; his answers were arrangements of his body, and she had learned to read them the way she read borders. Now his answer was simple: he shifted until his shoulder pressed fully against hers, a weight and a warmth, and stayed.

"I mean it," she said, quieter. "When they come—if they come—you should be gone. You slip through walls, Casper. You've done it before. A patrol with a dog can't hold what they can't touch. Go north when it starts. Wait it out. Come find me when—" She stopped. She didn't know how to finish the sentence. *Come find me when it's over* required an *over*, and she had stopped believing in one.

The wolf pressed harder against her side. It was the most stubborn thing he knew how to be.

"Don't," she said. "Don't do that thing where you decide something with your whole body and then make me live with it."

His ears tilted toward her. In the torchlight from below, his eyes caught orange, and he looked at her with an expression she had no better word for than *already decided*. As if the conversation had happened years ago, somewhere she couldn't remember, and he had simply been waiting for her to catch up.

She looked out at the city instead of at him, because looking at him made the fear worse, and the fear was already bad enough. Six torches moving along the canal. A dog sniffing at a threshold. Somewhere across the roofs, faintly, a woman screaming—not hunted, probably, just drunk, probably—but the sound went through Oscar like a blade through wet paper, because she could no longer hear a scream and assume it was only drink.

Thirty days. The registrars would take blood. Her blood would tell the truth her mouth had spent eleven years refusing. And the guild—her ragged, impossible, beloved guild—would forge its way forward on the strength of her lies, never knowing that the clerk balancing their books was the single most dangerous thing in the building, more dangerous than the saffron, more dangerous than the seals, more dangerous than Corvin's mouth.

Below, the patrol passed. The dog sneezed at the canal wind and moved on. The torches dwindled toward the harbor and went out one by one, like the edict losing interest, and Oscar knew it was only resting.

Casper's head came to rest against her knee, heavy as a verdict.

"Stubborn animal," she murmured, and put her hand between his ears, and felt the tremor in her own fingers that she'd been concealing all day, all year, all decade, finally surfacing where exactly one creature in the world could feel it and would never tell.

He leaned into her palm. His answer, again, was his whole body: *where you go, I go; where you burn, I burn; and no edict, no regent, no army of dogs and tired clerks will move me one street farther from you than I choose to stand.*

She sat with him until the fog swallowed the harbor lights, and then she went down and lit the lamp over her ledger desk and opened the book to a fresh page, because work was the only prayer she trusted.

At the top of the page, in her small clerk's hand, she wrote the date, and beneath it: *Inventory, pending inspection.*

And beneath that, without quite knowing why, without letting herself examine the impulse, she wrote one line more—a line that was not inventory, a line that belonged to no ledger the guild had ever kept:

*Item: one soul, female, gifted, concealed. Location known to one wolf. Value: unsold.*

Her pen hovered over the words. She should cross them out. Every instinct she had, every rule of her hidden life, said cross them out, blot them, pretend the hand that wrote them belonged to someone else.

She did not cross them out.

Outside, the fog thickened until the window was only gray, and somewhere far off, at the edge of hearing, a bell began again—one of the criers', working late, working the night streets, repeating the decree to whoever was still awake to hear it.

*Those who conceal themselves shall be found.*

Oscar blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening, with the uncured entry drying on the page behind her and the wolf a warm weight against the door of her room, refusing, with his entire silent body, to be anywhere else.
