## 3. What the Wolf Remembers

The world, to Casper, is made of smells the way the world, to others, is made of walls.

He lies against Oscar's door with his spine pressed to the wood and lets the night come through him in layers. First the fog: wet stone, cold iron, the salt-rot of the harbor breathing through every seam of Vellmark like a slow tide. Then the warehouse above them: tallow and old paper, ink gone sour in its pots, the mouse-warm smell of the rafters where he sometimes sleeps when sleeping is allowed. Then her.

Oscar smells of lamp oil and ledger dust, of cheap soap and cheaper wine, of the particular bitterness of a person who does not sleep enough and has taught herself not to want it. But underneath all that—beneath the clerical drudgery she wears like a second skin, beneath the flat voice and the ink-stained cuffs and the careful nothing of her—there is a scent he has never found anywhere else in the living world.

It is the smell of a page. Not paper. A page of a ledger, written on by something older than ink. When she is frightened it sharpens; when she lies it blooms. Tonight, behind the door, it is blooming so strongly he can taste it at the back of his throat: copper and ash and the cold mineral tang of a contract being read aloud somewhere far away.

*Those who conceal themselves shall be found.*

The bell keeps ringing. He counts its strokes because counting is one of the few human things left in him that still works properly, and because the number changes each time, and a changing number means the criers are moving, which means the city is awake, which means the edict is not a bad dream he can wait out under the eaves.

Casper was human once. He remembers this the way an amputee remembers an itch: not as a memory but as a place on his body where something used to be attached.

He remembers hands. That is what he clings to, on bad nights. Hands—broad, chapped, a scar across the left palm shaped like a river delta. He remembers they were good hands, clever ones, a cooper's hands or a mapmaker's, he has honestly forgotten which, and the forgetting is the worst part, worse than the fur, worse than the teeth. The court beneath the mountain took his name first—that is how it works, that is how all of it works—and once the name went, everything the name had been holding up came down slowly, floor by floor, until only the shape remained and even the shape was theirs to choose.

They chose wolf.

He does not know why. Perhaps because wolves run, and running souls are easier to herd. Perhaps because a wolf's nose can smell debt, and debt is the only currency the court respects.

That last one, he suspects, is true. It is why they kept the nose.

* * *

The mountain court buys souls the way merchants buy grain: in bulk, in futures, in contracts signed by people who do not read the fine print because the fine print is written in a language that rearranges itself when you look away. Casper's father sold him. He remembers that much—the handshake, the smell of ale and panic, the delta-shaped scar trembling as it closed around another man's palm. A bad harvest year. Three children younger than Casper. He does not blame the dead man; blame requires a face, and the face is one of the things the fog took.

What he remembers instead is the vault.

Souls down there do not scream. They are filed. He lay on a shelf of black slate for—he cannot say how long, time below the mountain is a spiral, not a line—and he smelled the other souls stacked around him. Grief smells like wet wool. Hope smells like green apples going soft. And every soul in that place, every single one, bore the same mark burned into whatever a soul uses for skin:

a ledger-line. A thin, silver seam, like the spine of a book, like a stitch.

*Property of the Court Beneath the Mountain. Entered in the Ledger of Impossible Things.*

He did not know, then, that there was a ledger in the living world that mirrored the one below. He learned that later, above ground, in Vellmark, in a warehouse that smelled of tallow and sour ink, when the door opened and a young clerk walked in with a crate of mislabeled compasses and stopped dead in the doorway because the wolf on the loading dock had stood up, hackles lifted, staring.

Not at the clerk. At the clerk's chest. At the place where, if you had eyes for such things—or a nose—you could see a thin silver seam running through the soul like a stitch through cloth.

The same mark as his own.

Casper had spent forty years smelling humans and finding them blank—whole, unentered, unclaimed, their pages unwritten. And here was one whose soul carried the court's handwriting. Here was someone else who had been sold without knowing it, entered without consenting, filed in a ledger she did not know existed.

He should have howled the news to the rooftops. Instead he followed her home.

* * *

Debt, he told himself at first. That was the word he gave it, because words are the last furniture of a human house and he hoards what remains. The court had marked her; therefore the court would come for her; therefore someone who knew the way down ought to stay near her, the way a soldier stays near a border he expects to be crossed. Debt and strategy. Debt and duty. Debt and the cold arithmetic of a creature who no longer trusts his own heart to keep accurate books.

But arithmetic does not explain the rafters.

Arithmetic does not explain why, on nights when she works late over the ledgers, he comes down from the beams and lies across the threshold of her room like a bar dropped into brackets—not guarding the door from outside threats, but holding it shut from within, as though the room itself might otherwise open and take her. Arithmetic does not explain the specific pleasure of her hand in his ruff, brief and absent-minded, while she reads, the way she scratches behind his ear without looking up, without thinking, as though he were a dog, as though he were hers, and how that thought—which should offend him, which would have offended the man with the delta-scarred hands—settles into him instead like warmth into stone.

There is a warmer word for what he feels. He knows the word. He has been circling it for years the way he circles the warehouse before sleep, three turns, four, never landing. Saying it would require a self to say it with, and the court holds most of his self in a slate drawer beneath the mountain. What is left over loves her with what remains, and what remains is apparently enormous, because it keeps trying to get out through his chest at night in sounds he refuses to let become words.

Tonight the door stays shut between them, but he presses his ear to the wood anyway. Her heartbeat is slower now. The fear-smell has banked itself down to embers. She is doing the thing she does when she is terrified, which is to go very still and very quiet and begin planning, and he can hear the small sounds of planning: the scratch of a pencil, the whisper of a page turned back to its beginning.

She is re-reading her own ledger. Looking for herself in it.

He wants to tell her: *Don't. Your name isn't in that book. Your name is in mine.* But telling requires speech, and speech requires the name they took, and so he does the next best thing, the only thing, the thing he has done every night since the edict was proclaimed: he puts his body against the door and stays.

* * *

Later—much later, when the bell has finally stopped and the fog has eaten the windows whole—she opens the door.

He had thought she was asleep. She looks down at him with lamplight-less eyes, a shadow in a dark doorway, and says, very quietly, "You heard it too. The crier."

He huffs. Once. Which means yes, which means many things, which means *I have been listening to the city die for six hours while you pretended to sleep.*

"They'll come for the guild first," she says. "Or last. I can't decide which." Her fingers find his ears, and he hates how completely the touch undoes him, how the wolf-body leans into her palm like a plant toward light, betraying him, testifying. "Casper. If they post the lists—if they start reading names—" She stops. Swallows. He hears the swallow; he hears the fear-bloom surge fresh and silver through her blood, the ledger-mark singing under her skin like a struck wire. "Never mind. Go to sleep."

He will not sleep. They both know he will not sleep.

She closes the door most of the way, leaving it the width of a paw, the way she always does, the concession neither of them ever names. And in the gap he watches her sit down at the desk in the dark, and he sees—because his eyes are the court's eyes, made to see debts in darkness—the faint silver seam light along her sternum as she breathes.

His own mark, worn by someone else. His own contract, walking around above ground with ink-stained cuffs and a false name and no idea what she is owed, what she is worth, what has been lying in a slate drawer beneath the mountain for years with her name on it—her *true* name, the one she doesn't use, the one the court has already entered.

The armies will come. The criers said so, and criers only repeat what the crown has already decided. And when they come, the court's claim on her will surface like a drowned body, because claims always do, and she will stand in some square or some courtroom and hear her real name spoken aloud by something pale and patient, and she will not even know what is being taken until it is gone.

Unless someone who knows the way down gets there first.

Casper settles his chin on his paws and begins, silently, to count. Not the bells this time. Days. Rations of days between tonight and the moment the salt gates fail, between tonight and the descent he swore he would never make again, down through the spiral dark, past the shelves, to the drawer where two souls lie filed side by side—one his, taken long ago, and one hers, taken without her knowledge, bearing his same silver stitch.

Two marks. One ledger.

He had wondered, all these years, why the court let him keep the nose. Now, listening to her pencil scratch in the dark—mapping, always mapping, drawing borders in a realm that moves—understanding arrives with the quiet horror of a trap closing: they kept the nose so he could *find* things. Find debts. Find the marked.

Find her.

And the worst part, the part that makes him press himself flatter against the cold boards, is that he is not sure anymore whether staying was ever his choice at all—or whether devotion was simply the leash they chose for him, and love merely the shape it took when it pulled.

Outside, the first gray hint of dawn touches the harbor, and far away, at the edge of hearing, hoofbeats begin on the north road.
