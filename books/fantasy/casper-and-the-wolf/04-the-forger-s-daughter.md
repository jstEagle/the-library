## 4. The Forger's Daughter

Sable Ives worked in a room without windows, which everyone in the guild understood to be a matter of principle rather than architecture. There had been a window once; she had bricked it over herself with her own hands and a bucket of mortar mixed with ash, because light, she said, told the truth about ink, and she could not afford to have anything in her workshop that told the truth.

Oscar climbed the narrow stair with a lantern held low, its flame shuttered behind green glass, and knocked twice, then once, which was the knock for *I come alone*, which was the knock Sable had invented precisely so that no one would ever use it by accident.

"Enter, Oskar Eriksen," came the voice through the door, dry as old vellum. "Or enter as whoever you actually are. I'll know either way."

The door swung inward before Oscar's hand reached it. Beyond lay the workshop: a low room lined floor to ceiling with drawers, each drawer labeled in Sable's small crabbed hand — *seals, minor*; *seals, municipal*; *hand, bishop's, late period*; *waxes that weep*. A single lamp burned under a tin shade, throwing everything into amber and shadow. The air smelled of gum arabic and iron gall and something older underneath, like the inside of a book that had been rained on and dried and loved anyway.

"You wanted papers," Sable said. She did not look up from the document pinned before her, a travel writ half-finished in a hand that was not hers and not anyone's — a hand that belonged to a dead customs officer named Aldric Voss, resurrected nightly at this desk. "I heard you wanted them badly enough to climb my stairs at an hour decent people are asleep."

"The edict comes to Vellmark within the week." Oscar kept her voice level. "Criers in every square. Registration or the pyre. I need documents for nine guild members that say they've already been inspected and cleared."

"Mm." Sable lifted the page, tilted it against the lamp, frowned at some ghost of texture only she could see. "Nine souls. Cleared by whom? Under whose seal?"

"Inspector-General Halloway's office. Southern circuit. He died last spring, which is convenient, because dead men can't be asked to confirm their own signatures."

Something moved at the corner of Sable's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where one might live. "You've been reading my ledgers of technique again."

"I keep the guild's ledgers. Yours were on the same shelf."

"They were on the same shelf because you put them there, clerk. I watched you do it." She set down the page at last and turned, folding her arms, and looked at Oscar the way she looked at a forgery brought in by a stranger: patiently, thoroughly, waiting for it to confess what it was. "I'll make your nine sets of papers. They'll be beautiful. They'll survive a magistrate's lens and a bored officer's thumb. But I don't work for free anymore, and I don't mean coin."

"Sable—"

"I want the truth." She said it simply, the way one names a price. "One truth. Any truth, but it has to be real, and it has to cost you. That's my rate now, in times like these. Everyone who walks out of this room carrying my work should know what I'm owed, even if they never pay it. It keeps me honest. Or dishonest," she added, with the ghost-smile flickering back, "in a principled way."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere above them, faint as weather, Casper shifted along the rafters — Oscar felt him the way one feels a change in pressure before rain, an absence-of-weight passing overhead. He had followed her up. He always followed her up now, since the criers had begun rehearsing their proclamations in the harbor district, since the word *pyre* had entered the common speech of Vellmark like a coin too often handled.

"A truth that costs me," Oscar said.

"That's the tariff."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave with nothing, and I'll grieve it, because I think you're the most interesting thing this guild has smuggled through its doors in twenty years, and I include the reliquary we shipped to Tarn in a crate marked *pickled eels*." Sable pulled a stool toward herself and sat, unhurried, hands folded on her knees. Ink stained her fingers to the second knuckle, dark as bruise. "Take your time. I'm patient. Forging teaches patience — you spend three weeks becoming another person, you learn how long a person can take to arrive."

Oscar stood in the amber light and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that she had been walking toward this room for years. Every careful clerkish gesture, every lowered voice, every ledger balanced to deliberate tedium — all of it had been leading here, to a woman with ink-stained hands asking the only question that mattered, the question Oscar had spent eleven years and two cities and one stolen childhood outrunning:

*Who are you?*

She thought of the compass that pointed at regret, missing from its case. She thought of Corvin's easy smile and the ledgers she kept and the way the edict's word — *registered*, as though the gifted were parcels, freight, inventory — had lodged under her ribs like a splinter. She thought of Casper's eyes in the dark of the warehouse, gold as lamplight through honey, watching her with a patience that sometimes seemed to say he already knew, that whatever she was hiding, he had smelled it from the beginning and stayed anyway.

"My name isn't Oskar," she said.

"No," Sable agreed mildly. "I'd guessed that much. Go on."

"It's Oscar. Spelled the other way. The way women spell it, when they're allowed to spell it at all." Her voice came out steadier than she expected, or perhaps simply quieter, the way things are quiet when all the force has gone into holding them upright. "And I have the sight. The true gift, not hedge-cunning. I read the land the way you read ink. I see where borders want to go. I've been hiding it since I was thirteen, when the king's men took my mother for less than what I carry, and I have hidden it every day since, in ledgers, in drudgery, in being dull enough that no one ever looks twice." She swallowed. "That's the truth. That's the whole invoice. I'm not the clerk Oskar Eriksen. I'm a woman who maps living realms, and if the Hunt finds out, they won't just burn me. They'll take what I am and wear it like a coat."

The lamp burned. Sable said nothing for a long moment, and Oscar found that the silence did not wound the way she'd feared; it settled around her like snowfall, soft and total, the silence of a weight set down after years of carrying.

"Well," Sable said finally. "That's more than one truth. I'll consider the surplus credit toward future work."

"Sable—"

"I'm joking. Badly. It's a defense mechanism; ask anyone." But she rose, and crossed the little room, and did something Oscar did not expect: she took Oscar's hand — the right hand, the ledger hand — and turned it palm-up, and studied it as though reading lines there, which perhaps she was. "Eleven years," she said. "Dull enough that no one looks twice. Do you know what I see, when I look at you?"

"What?"

"A person holding a door shut with their shoulder." She released the hand. "I know that posture. My father was a forger before me, and my mother before him, and the family trade is not documents. The family trade is doors. We make doors where walls were, and walls where doors were, whichever keeps people alive." She turned back to her desk and began pulling drawers with sudden brisk purpose — *seals, minor*; *hand, inspector's, southern circuit* — her voice businesslike again, which was, Oscar understood, its own kind of kindness. "Your nine papers will be ready in four days. Your secret goes nowhere. Not to Corvin, not to the rafters, not to my own reflection. Co-conspirators bind each other, clerk. That's the only oath worth taking."

"Sable Ives," Oscar said, and meant every syllable of it, "you are the best liar in Vellmark, and I believe you completely."

"There it is," Sable murmured, bending over the fresh page. "Now hold still while I make you nobody. It's easier than it sounds. You've clearly had practice."

* * *

They worked until the third hour past midnight, Sable inking, Oscar grinding pigment and holding pages flat and learning, despite herself, the grammar of forgery — how a living signature breathes at the descenders, how official wax prefers to crack along its heart rather than its edge, how the state's whole authority rests on marks made by tired hands in bad light. It was almost pleasant. It was almost, dangerously, like being known.

Which was why neither of them heard the boots on the lane outside until the lanterns were already there.

Light swept the bricked-over window — a pale blade sliding across the mortar seams — and beneath it came voices, clipped and official, and the particular cadence of a hunt patrol working door to door. Oscar's blood went cold in a way she recognized from childhood, from the night her mother's name was called off a list.

"Back stair," Sable breathed, already snuffing the lamp.

But the boots stopped below. On the landing. A fist tested the outer door of the dye-shop front, and a voice called the hour and demanded entry, and there was no time, there was no time —

Then the screaming started.

It came from the alley side — a sound like a wolf, except wolves did not sound like that, wolves did not sound like grief given lungs, like every regret in the world howled through one throat. It rose along the walls of the lane, impossibly near and impossibly vast, and the lantern-light scattered, and boots thundered away from the stairs instead of toward them, six men chasing a voice that had no body, that was already moving, already elsewhere, drawing the hunt after it like a lure drawn through water.

Oscar stood in the dark with her heart slamming and knew exactly whose voice it was, and loved him with a ferocity that frightened her.

"He'll lead them to the salt flats," Sable whispered, hand on her arm. "There's nothing out there to find. He knows it. Go to him."

Oscar went — down the back stair, through the yard, into streets still ringing with that fading, impossible song. At the mouth of the alley she paused once, looking back at the lit seam under Sable's door, at the friend she'd made in four hours and eleven years.

By morning, the patrol would report a wolf spirit sighted inside the walls, and the city's fear would sharpen into something with teeth. And by evening, Oscar knew, Corvin would call a guild meeting, and someone would ask the question she was not yet ready to answer:

*Why does the wolf keep saving the clerk?*
