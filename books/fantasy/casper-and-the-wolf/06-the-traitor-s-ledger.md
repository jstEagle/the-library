## 6. The Traitor's Ledger

Sable Ives had three hands, if you counted the one he'd been born without and learned to build.

The first was his left, ink-stained to the knuckle. The second was a fine steel burin that could lift a wax seal whole from a letter and lay it down again on fresh paper so perfectly that even the man who'd pressed it would swear to its provenance. The third was patience—the kind you needed when copying a chancery hand for eleven hours straight, when your eyes watered and your wrist screamed and still every loop of every capital had to fall exactly where the original had put it.

He was using all three tonight in the back room above the guildhouse, working by a single tallow candle because lamplight showed through shutters and tallow did not. The job was simple: forty travel passes for forty names on Corvin's list, each one stamped with the seal of a magistrate who had died of a fever in spring and would never know his signature was still walking around Vellmark signing things. Sable had done harder work before breakfast. He could do this one half-asleep.

Except he couldn't sleep at all, and hadn't for three nights, and it wasn't the work keeping him up.

It was the smell of the guildmaster's office.

Corvin kept his ledgers locked in an iron-banded chest beneath the floorboards, and Sable had known about that chest since he was nine years old, since the winter Corvin had pulled him out of a debtor's cell and set him to work with a pen instead of a pickaxe, saying *a boy who can make paper say anything is worth ten boys with knives*. Sable owed the man everything. His name—his real one, the one nobody used. His trade. The steady roof over the foundlings and frauds and exiles that called themselves the Guild of Impossible Things.

And three nights ago, passing the guildmaster's door at an hour when honest men were asleep, he had smelled candlewax burning where no candle should be. And beneath it, faint as a ghost's breath, the smell of mountain iron—that cold mineral tang that came off documents from the crown's survey corps, the ones Sable forged copies of so often he knew their stink better than his own.

Corvin had been reading crown correspondence. Alone. At midnight. With the door barred.

Sable told himself it was nothing. Guildmasters read letters. Guildmasters made bargains. That was the trade: impossible things moved through Vellmark because somebody paid somebody, and the paying required paper, and the paper required a man like Corvin to hold the quill.

But then there was the other thing. The thing that had been eating him alive since the edict came down.

Oskar's name.

Not Oskar Eriksen, clerk, third entry from the top of the guild roster. *Oskar Eriksen* in full, written in Corvin's own cramped hand on a separate sheet—a sheet not of the guild's records but of something else entirely, something Sable had glimpsed only once, folded into Corvin's breast pocket, before the old man had caught him looking and smiled that easy smile and changed the subject to the price of salt.

Sable finished the fortieth pass, blotted it, stacked it square. Then he blew out the candle, waited until his eyes adjusted to the dark, and went downstairs to commit the worst betrayal of his life or the only honest thing he'd ever done.

* * *

Corvin's office smelled of pipe smoke and old leather and, underneath, always, the river damp that seeped into everything in Vellmark. The chest was under the fourth floorboard from the window, the loose one, the one Sable himself had planed smooth when he was fourteen because Corvin said a hiding place should never creak.

The lock was new. That was the first wound. Three months old, maybe four—Sable could date any lock in the city by its wear pattern, and this one had been turned perhaps two hundred times. Two hundred nights, give or take, of the guildmaster consulting whatever lived beneath the floor.

Sable's burin slid into the keyhole like a friend coming home. He felt the wards—cheap ones, crown-issue, the kind he'd copied a hundred times—and unpicked them in the time it took the river fog to shift past the window. The lid came up on a smell of cold iron and dust.

Ledgers. A dozen of them. The guild's true accounts, going back twenty years—these Sable expected, these were Corvin's heart, the record of every debt and favor and impossible delivery that kept them all fed.

And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger bound in grey leather that was not leather at all. It was cool to the touch in a way no skin ever was, and it drank the light from Sable's little shuttered lantern instead of reflecting it.

He unwrapped it anyway. Some part of him already knew what he'd find, had known since the smell of mountain iron, had known and refused to know, the way you refuse to name a sickness until the fever forces you.

The pages inside weren't paper. They were something thinner and paler, like vellum cured from something that had once breathed, and the writing on them shifted as he looked—not moving exactly, but settling, the way frost settles on glass. The headings were in Chancery script. The entries were in Corvin's hand.

*Item: one map, the Weirwater passes, corrected edition. Received: writ of immunity, household of Corvin Ash, guildmaster, and dependents.*

*Item: one map, the Salt Gate approaches, showing the sally-tunnels. Received: extension of same.*

*Item: one name.*

Sable's mouth went dry. He turned the page, and the grey vellum was warm now, faintly, like something sleeping, and there at the head of the final page, in letters that seemed to press deeper into the skin the longer he looked:

*Oskar Eriksen — soul — held in surety against collection. Term of contract: seventeen years. Balance due upon proclamation of the Hunt.*

Seventeen years ago. Sable sat back on his heels and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. Seventeen years ago was the year Corvin had taken in a sharp-eyed, silent child who drew maps on scraps of slate and never spoke of where they'd come from. Seventeen years ago was the year the guild had stopped losing money, stopped running from the crown's riders, started becoming what it was.

He had thought Corvin had saved them all. Corvin had. But the price of the saving had been written down in a ledger bound in something that wasn't leather, and the price had a name, and the name belonged to the quietest person in the building, the person who balanced their accounts and hid behind clerical drudgery and flinched—Sable saw it now, had always seen it and filed it away wrong—flinched every time the word *edict* crossed the room.

The vellum was warm under his fingers. Warm, and getting warmer, and somewhere far below the city, below the river, below the roots of the mountains themselves, Sable had the sudden sick certainty that something had just felt him reading.

*You are seen,* said a pressure against his mind, faint and vast and patient, like deep water. *The account stands. Collection is come.*

Sable slammed the ledger shut and wrapped it in its oilcloth and got out of that room with shaking hands, and it wasn't until he was three streets away, standing in the fog with his back against a warehouse wall, that he understood what he was holding.

Because he hadn't taken the grey ledger.

He'd taken the guild's true accounts too—out of habit, out of some instinct that said evidence travels in company—and tucked under the last page, where Corvin had used it as a marker, was a single sheet of ordinary paper. Crown stationery. Sealed with the privy signet.

An order of march. Dated six days hence. Destination: Vellmark. And in the margin, in a surveyor's precise hand, a note that turned Sable's blood to ice water:

*Routes verified by source within. Gates will be open.*

* * *

He walked the long way back, which was to say he walked nowhere near the guildhouse, because his feet refused to carry him there and his mind refused to explain why not. The fog had thickened off the river. Somewhere a patrol bell rang the second watch, and boots splashed through standing water, and Sable stood in a doorway with a stolen ledger against his ribs and tried to weigh one man against the truth.

Corvin had pulled him out of a cell. Corvin had taught him that a pen was a weapon and a name was armor and family was whoever ate at your table. Corvin had raised him, in the way that mattered more than blood—which was exactly why the betrayal cut both ways. If Corvin had sold Oscar's soul to keep the guild alive, then the crime was also a sacrifice, monstrous and tender at once, a father spending one child to feed the rest.

But Corvin hadn't spent one child. He'd spent one child and then kept spending, year after year, maps and passes and finally open gates, trading the whole free city's safety for his own household's immunity. And he'd let Oscar walk those halls for seventeen years, eat at that table, balance those accounts—let her trust him—without ever saying that her name was already sold, that the edict wasn't a threat arriving but a bill come due.

That was the part Sable couldn't forgive. Not the selling. The silence after.

A shape moved in the fog across the street. Big, low, wrong for a dog. Eyes like banked coals catching the lantern-light of a passing linkboy.

Casper.

The wolf-spirit stood at the edge of the lamplight, utterly still, watching Sable with an expression that was not a wolf's expression at all. And Sable—who had spent his life reading faces, forging signatures, knowing what people meant rather than what they said—read that look and felt his stomach drop.

*You know,* the look said. *You have it with you. I can smell the mountain on it.*

"I'm deciding," Sable said aloud, and his voice cracked on the second word.

The wolf tilted his head. Then he turned, deliberate, and looked down the street toward the guildhouse, and then back at Sable, and then—he did the thing he almost never did for anyone but her—he came closer, close enough that the fog curled away from his fur, and sat down at Sable's feet like a sentry taking post.

Not blocking his way. Guarding his choice.

Sable looked down at the ledger against his ribs, warm as a heartbeat, and at the wolf sitting faithful in the dark, and understood that the decision had already been made somewhere deeper than loyalty. Corvin had taught him that paper could make anything true. Fine. Then let this paper tell the truth for once.

He turned toward the warehouse district, toward the rafters where a certain clerk worked late over accounts that were never wrong, and the wolf fell into step beside him, silent as snowfall.

Behind them, the fog swallowed the guildhouse whole. Ahead, a single window burned yellow over the salt-stained docks, and behind that window someone who believed herself invisible was adding columns by candlelight, unaware that her name was already written in a book that drank light.

Six days, said the order in Sable's pocket. Maybe less.

"Sable," he said to himself, testing his own name like a forged signature, wondering if truth would hold where forgery never failed. "My name is Sable, and I'm about to break the only man who ever loved me."

The wolf made a sound low in his chest. It might have been agreement. It might have been grief.

They walked on together into the dark.
