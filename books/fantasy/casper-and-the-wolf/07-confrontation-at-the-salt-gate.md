## 7. Confrontation at the Salt Gate

The salt gate smelled of old storms and older lies. Oscar stood beneath its arch with Sable's ledger open in her hands, the pages trembling in a wind that came off the flats like a warning. Beyond the wall, the usurper's army was a smudge on the horizon, patient as weather. Within it, the walls of Vellmark held their breath.

Corvin was in the counting room when she found him, doing what he always did at dusk—trimming the lamp wicks, checking the locks, pretending the guild was only a warehouse and he only an old man who loved order.

"Close the door, Oskar," he said without turning.

She closed it. She set Sable's ledger on his desk between them, opened to the page that did not belong there: her own name, written in Corvin's careful hand, dated seven years back. *Sold to the court beneath the mountain. One soul, marked ledger-bound. Payment deferred.*

"Read it aloud," she said. "I want to hear you say it."

He turned then. In the lamplight his face was the face of every kindness he had ever done her—the man who had taken in a frightened clerk with ink-stained fingers and never asked why the ledgers balanced themselves too well. That face made it worse. Betrayal by a stranger is a wound; betrayal by family is an amputation.

"Oskar—"

"*Oscar*," she said, and let him have it whole: "My name is Oscar Eriksen. I am not your clerk's boy. I am the mapmaker whose routes kept this guild alive through three famines and two wars, and my name is in your book as collateral."

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere above them, in the rafters where the shadows pooled thickest, Casper's growl rolled low and continuous, like a stone grinding in the dark.

Corvin sat down slowly, as if his bones had aged twenty years in a sentence. "You read it wrong," he said. "Not sold. *Pledged.*"

"There is no difference to the one pledged."

"There is to me." He pressed his palms flat against the desk, against the ledger, as though he could hold the past shut by weight alone. "Seven years ago the mountain's steward came to Vellmark wearing a customs officer's coat. He had a list of every gifted soul in the free cities, and mine was the guild's name at the bottom of every page. They were going to burn us out, Oskar. All of us. The forgers, the smugglers, the ones with small strange talents nobody could hide. I had nothing they wanted except—" his voice caught, steadied— "except something they wanted more than any of you."

"You gave them me."

"I gave them your name." His eyes, when they lifted, were wet and unashamed of it, which was somehow the cruelest part. "A promise on paper. Deferred payment. I told myself the court beneath the mountain never collects—that it deals in debts the way the sea deals in drowned sailors, endlessly and never specifically. I bought the guild ten years with your signature. I would buy it again."

"That's the confession?" Oscar heard her own voice go very quiet, very cold, the way it went when her gift woke and began measuring distances she didn't want measured. "That you'd do it again?"

"The edict changed things," Corvin said. "The new king doesn't deal in deferred promises. The Hunt has collectors now, real ones, and the first name on their list is the one already promised. You were always going to be collected, Oskar. At least this way the rest of us—"

"The rest of you." She laughed, and hated the sound. "Sable forged papers for me at risk of the pyre. Casper—" the growl overhead deepened, and dust sifted down through the lamplight— "Casper has been guarding a debt he recognized because it carries the same mark as his own. And you sat across from me at supper every night for seven years and passed me the bread."

"Family makes hard arithmetic," Corvin whispered.

"No," she said. "Arithmetic makes hard arithmetic. Family is what refuses the sum." She closed the ledger. Her hands were steady; she noted that distantly, the way her gift noted everything, borders and bearings and the exact width of the gulf opening between herself and the man who had raised her. "I'm leaving. Anyone who wants to come, can."

"Through the salt gate? The army's a day out."

"The army is *twelve hours* out," she said, because she could feel it—she had felt it for days, the way the roads bent toward Vellmark like iron filings toward a lodestone, guided by maps better than hers, drawn by someone who knew her passes. "And they aren't marching blind."

Something moved behind Corvin's eyes—not guilt, worse: confirmation. "Then go," he said softly. "Before I have to decide what I'll trade next."

* * *

They went up through the loft, where Sable was already waiting with three satchels packed and her father's forgery kit strapped across her back like a weapon. Down in the yard, half the guild had gathered—Marta from the rope-walk, the twins who ran the river smuggle, old Fen with his lantern that burned without oil. Faces she had fed and mended and mapped safe passage for.

"We can make the fen road by dark," Sable said. "If the gate holds till then."

"The gate won't hold," Oscar said. "It was never meant to. Look at the mortar lines—they've been redrawn. Someone fed the army our routes, and someone inside opened the way."

"Corvin?"

"Corvin sold my soul years ago. Whoever sold the roads did it this week." She looked at the faces below, at Fen's lantern guttering though there was no draft, and understood that some of them had known. Some of them had always known what she was, and had said nothing, and their silence was its own kind of ledger.

"Casper," she called softly, and the shadow on the rafters detached itself and flowed down the stairwell like spilled night given teeth. The wolf paused at the bottom, looked back up at the gathered guild with eyes like banked coals, and growled once—a sound that was less threat than farewell.

Then the horns began.

They came off the flats in waves, low and brazen, and beneath them came a sound like the sea going out—hundreds of boots finding a rhythm. Through the arrow-slit windows Oscar saw them come on, rank after rank, banners black as burnt paper, and leading them a rider in grey whose horse never stumbled on ground that shifted under everyone else's hooves. The traitor cartographer. Even at this distance, even through stone, Oscar felt her gift recoil from the other's maps like a hand from a hot stove.

"They're at the gate," Marta breathed.

They were. And the gate—the great salt-crusted gate that had held against sieges since before Vellmark had a name—did not break so much as *remember itself wrongly*. The hinges turned the wrong way. The mortar crumbled along lines that had been drawn and redrawn until the stone forgot it was ever whole. It folded inward like a page being turned, and the army poured through into the streets like ink into water.

"Back ways," Oscar said. "Now. Everyone who's coming, follow me and don't ask how I know."

They went down through the guild's bones—cellars that connected to cellars that shouldn't have connected, passages Oscar had charted in secret over seven years and never shown anyone. Behind them the warehouse caught, flames climbing the tarred rafters where Casper had slept a hundred nights, where the Ledger of Impossible Things had lived until tonight. Above the roar she heard shouting, and once—cut short—a scream she knew.

Fen. Fen with his oilless lantern, who had stayed.

She didn't stop. Stopping meant dying, and dying meant every route she'd ever mapped died with her.

* * *

They came out through the tannery sluices onto the salt flats as the city caught behind them, fire blooming district by district like terrible flowers, the smoke standing up straight in the windless air. The twins had scattered east with the river boats. Marta had gone to ground with cousins in the clay quarter. The guild that had been forty souls and one impossible ledger was now three people and a wolf on a white plain under a blackening sky.

Sable sank down onto a salt crust, laughing or sobbing, impossible to tell. "Ten years," she said. "He traded you for ten years, and it lasted seven, and now it's lasted none."

"He believed the debt would sleep." Oscar stared back at the burning city. The salt gate was a broken tooth in the wall, silhouetted by flames. "That's the worst of it, Sable. He wasn't cruel. He was *careful*. He weighed everything and chose, and I was the thing that weighed least."

Casper pressed against her side, solid as grief, warm as a hearth. When she buried her hand in his ruff she felt it—the ledger-mark under his fur, the scar of contract, the same mark she now knew was written into her own soul in handwriting she couldn't see. Two entries in a book kept somewhere beneath the mountain, in a vault that smelled of pale candle-smoke, waiting for collection.

"The steward will send for you now," Sable said quietly. "Debts get called when debtors run."

"I know." Oscar turned south, toward the horizon, toward the massif that even here, even at night, she could feel pulling at the edges of her gift like a wound pulling at thread. The mountain. The court. The vault where her name was filed beside a wolf's. "So we don't wait to be sent for."

Sable followed her gaze and went still. "That's not a rescue plan, Oskar. That's walking into the collector's house."

"My name isn't Oskar." She said it to the mountain, to the fire, to the wolf, to herself—stripping the last disguise off in front of the only two souls left who mattered. "My name is Oscar Eriksen, mapmaker of living realms, and I am going down beneath the mountain to take back what was sold. Both entries."

The wind changed. Off the burning city it carried a smell that didn't belong to smoke—cold stone, candle wax, something old and patient exhaling through a door that had just been opened. Casper's hackles rose along his spine. He looked south and whined once, low.

Far away, beneath the roots of the world, something had begun to count.

Oscar took the first step south, and the salt crust rang beneath her boot like a struck bell.
