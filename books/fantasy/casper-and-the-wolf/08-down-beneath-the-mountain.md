## 8. Down Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not have a mouth so much as it permitted one.

Oscar felt it as they descended—a corridor of stone that had not been carved but *allowed*, the way a throat allows a swallow. The air tasted of iron and old paper, the same smell as the guild's ledgers left too long in a damp cellar, and beneath it something sweeter and worse: the smell of a thing remembering what it had been.

She walked with her gift open for the first time in her life, unhidden, unashamed of the watching. It poured through her like cold water through a net. The passages around them were not fixed. They *breathed*. Corridors lengthened when they were regretted and shortened when they were forgotten, and Oscar could feel the whole architecture shifting like a sleeper turning over, dreaming of the lives that had been traded away in the vaults below.

"Tell me what you see," Casper said. He walked at her left shoulder, always the left, as if he still expected a sword-arm to need guarding. In the grey light his wolf-shape was more shadow than fur, his eyes catching what little luminescence the stone offered and giving it back doubled.

"A map that hates being read," Oscar said. "The walls move where people have grieved. There's a corridor ahead that keeps trying to become a nursery."

Sable, behind them both, made a small sound. "That's cheerful."

"It's honest." Oscar lifted her chalk and marked the wall—a habit from a hundred ledgers, a small white cross that meant *here, and no further, and I was here when I learned it*. The chalk line held for the space of three heartbeats, then the stone drank it. "The mountain is built out of bargains. Every passage is someone's regret, and it rearranges itself to keep you walking through the worst of them. Unless—" she paused, hand flat against the wall, listening with the part of her that had always been able to hear the shape of living things, "—unless you can read it faster than it can grieve."

"Can you?" Sable asked.

Oscar thought of the ledgers, thousands of entries in her careful clerk's hand, every impossible thing the guild had ever bought or sold or smuggled past a border. *A compass that points at regret.* She had written that entry herself, years ago, and had not understood until this moment that she had been describing her own gift all along.

"Yes," she said. "I think that's why they sold for me. The court doesn't collect souls for their strength. It collects them for their *legibility*."

Casper's growl was low and continuous, a sound she felt through the soles of her boots more than heard. "Then it will find you very legible."

"It already has." She kept walking. "It's been reading me since the salt gate."

* * *

They descended for what might have been hours. The mountain kept its own time; Oscar's pocket-watch ran backward for a while, then wept condensation and stopped, and after that she stopped measuring. She mapped instead. Chalk on stone, chalk on stone, a cross here, a numeral there, the grammar of a woman who had spent her whole life making the unmakable hold still.

The labyrinth tested them in the order of their wounds.

For Sable it was a corridor lined with doors, each one the door of his father's workshop, and behind each door the sound of a quill scratching and a voice saying *you'll never be the artist she was, boy, but you can be useful.* Sable walked through it with his jaw set and his eyes fixed on the back of Oscar's head, and when it was over he said nothing, but his forged papers—still folded against his heart—were damp with sweat.

For Oscar it was worse, because Oscar's labyrinth knew her name.

The passage narrowed and the walls grew close and warm, and she was seventeen again, standing in Corvin's office with a bill of sale on the desk between them, and the ink was still wet, and the ink was her own handwriting, because of course it was, because she had copied out the guild's ledgers for six years and her hand was in everything the guild had ever signed, and somewhere in that mountain of paper was the page with her name on it, *Oscar Eriksen, one soul, sound and unmapped,* and she had written the fair copy herself without knowing—

"Oscar." Casper's muzzle pressed against her palm, solid and cold and real. "You're here. The wall is lying."

"The wall is *quoting*," she whispered. But she let him pull her forward, one hand buried in the ruff of his neck, and the corridor of ink closed behind them like water closing over a stone.

It was only later, much later, at the edge of a vast descending stair where the dark below them was not dark but *pale*—a light with no source, the color of bone and milk and moonrise on snow—that she noticed the chalk marks she'd left behind them had all vanished. Every cross. Every numeral. The mountain had eaten her map whole.

Except it hadn't. Because when she looked down at her own hands, the map was there instead—faint white lines across her knuckles and wrists, her gift turning inward, writing her route on the only surface the labyrinth couldn't erase.

*Here, and no further, and I was here when I learned it.*

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was not a court of thrones. It was a court of *counting*.

They came out onto a gallery of black stone above a hall that went down and down, tiered like an amphitheater or a ledger opened to its deepest page, and in every tier sat souls. Not ghosts—souls, still wearing the faint outline of the people they had been, dim as coals under ash. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. And moving among the tiers, tending them the way a gardener tends rows, was the steward.

It was tall and thin and the color of unmarked paper, and it did not walk so much as *proceed*, and when it turned its face toward them Oscar saw that it had no face at all, only a smooth expanse with a single vertical seam, like a book waiting to be opened.

"You are early," it said. Its voice was the sound a quill makes on good vellum. "The contract specifies collection at the turning of the year. But contracts bend for the interesting. Come down. We will discuss the account."

They descended the tiers, and the dim souls turned to watch them, and Oscar made herself look at them—made herself *read* them, because that was her gift and her weapon and she would not arrive at this bargaining table illiterate. Most of them were ordinary. A baker's soul with the shape of bread still warm in it. A soldier's. A child's, and she had to close her throat against that one and keep walking.

Then, on the ninth tier, she saw a soul that made her stop.

It was wolf-shaped.

Not Casper. Older, dimmer, but the same essential form—a soul that had spent so long in a wolf's shape that the shape had become its truth. And around its edges, faint as a watermark, was the same ledger-mark she had seen in her gift's sight a hundred times on Casper: the court's own signature, the seal of ownership, the mark of a soul that had been *sold*.

"Casper," she breathed. "There are others like you here."

"There were nine of us," Casper said quietly. He had gone very still. "Once. I stopped counting the years when I stopped hoping." His eyes, when he turned them to her, were the warmest thing in all that pale cold. "You came anyway. Knowing what this place is. That was foolish, Oscar Eriksen."

"That was Tuesday," Sable said, but his voice shook.

The steward waited at the bottom of the hall, beside a desk that had not been there before and was, somehow, obviously the only real object in the entire mountain: a plain clerk's desk, scarred and ink-stained, with a single ledger open upon it. The ledger was enormous. The ledger was *everything*. Oscar could feel it the way she felt the mountain breathing—every soul in every tier had its line in that book, and the book was the tiers, and the tiers were the book.

"The account of Oscar Eriksen," the steward said, turning pages with fingers like folded paper. "Purchased in trust, thirteen years past, by one Corvin of the Guild of Impossible Things. Payment rendered: passage of the guild's smuggled goods through the crown's salt roads, immunity of record, and the continued silence of the court regarding certain guild members of gift." It looked up, faceless, and somehow managed to convey the sense of a raised eyebrow. "The guild has been very profitable. You are the most legible soul we hold in escrow. Your gift maps living realms. Do you understand what that is worth to a court that must constantly re-survey its own boundaries?"

"You want me to work for you," Oscar said.

"We want what we purchased. Your soul, delivered, to serve as the court's cartographer for the term of eternity." The seam of its face parted slightly—the closest thing it had to a smile. "However. The court honors one ancient courtesy. A soul may be reclaimed before filing, if an equivalent is left in its place. Equivalent value. Equivalent legibility." The paper fingers turned back through the ledger, and stopped. "We note that your companion's contract is also held here. Casper, formerly Anders Vey, sold in the year of the ash winters. His soul is old, well-kept, and carries a debt of devotion that the court finds... flavorful."

"No," Oscar said.

"You haven't heard the offer," the steward said.

"There's nothing to hear. I'm not trading him."

"Oscar." Casper's voice was very quiet. He had moved, she realized, so that he stood between her and the desk, and there was something in the set of his shoulders she recognized from the night the hunt patrol had nearly caught them at the forge—the stillness of a thing preparing to spring, except this time the thing he meant to spring at was a bargain, and the thing he meant to save was her. "Oscar, look at me."

"I'm looking at you, and the answer is no."

"Thirteen years I have walked beside you because I owed you a debt I couldn't name and a warmth I couldn't spend." His voice did not rise. It went *down*, the way grief goes down, the way bedrock goes down. "You gave me a name when I had none. You fed a spirit off your own plate. You drew me a map to the sea when I told you I remembered the sea, and you never once asked what I had been before, because you understood that some things a person gets to keep." He turned back to the steward. "I am equivalent. I am legible. I am *old*, and the court likes old things. Take me, and let her walk out with her soul and her name and her ridiculous chalk."

"Casper—"

"Let her walk out," he said, "and I will go to the ninth tier and lie down beside what's left of the others, and I will do it *gladly*, and the court may flavor that devotion however it likes."

The steward's seam parted wider. The ledger's pages turned themselves, eager, and Oscar felt the whole mountain lean in around them, the labyrinth holding its breath, and she understood with terrible clarity that the court had arranged this—that every corridor of regret, every vanished chalk line, every quoted memory had been the mountain herding them down to exactly this desk, exactly this bargain, exactly this moment where the man she loved would pay her debt with the only coin he had left.

The steward dipped a paper finger in ink that was not ink.

"Then let us amend the contract," it said. "One soul reclaimed. One soul surrendered. Witnessed and—"

"Wait," Oscar said.

And she stepped forward to the desk, and put her hand flat on the open ledger, and felt the whole court beneath the mountain shudder at her touch, because no one in thirteen thousand years had ever laid an ungloved hand upon the book.

"Before you amend anything," she said, "I have a counteroffer. And you're going to want to hear it, steward, because I can do something no soul in your vaults can do." She looked up at the faceless face, and did not let her voice shake. "I can redraw your borders."
