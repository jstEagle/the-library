## 9. The Price of a Name

**Oscar**

The vault door was not a door.

It was a wound in the mountain's living stone, a seam of pale light where the rock had been peeled back like fruit skin, and through it came a cold that did not belong to any season Oscar had ever mapped. It smelled of iron and old paper—the smell, she realized with a lurch, of her own ledgers. Of every record she had ever kept. The court beneath the mountain kept accounts too.

The pale steward stood before the seam, and behind it, shelf upon shelf upon shelf receded into a dark so deep that even her gift could not find its edges. Souls hung there in rows like lanterns in a festival she had never been invited to. Some burned gold. Some guttered blue. And some—most—were the color of old ink, dim and patient, waiting to be read again.

One of them was hers. She could feel it the way you feel a hand pressed flat against your back: the ledger-mark, the small cold signature the mountain had stamped into her when Corvin sold her name all those years ago. Casper carried the same mark. She had never known why he stayed. Now she knew it was because they were both entries in the same terrible book.

"Two souls stand at my threshold," the steward said. Its voice was not sound but pressure, the way a map feels when you press your palm against it. "One may be reclaimed. One must remain. This is the arithmetic of the court, and the court does not do subtraction."

Casper moved first.

She saw it coming—had seen it building since the labyrinth began reshaping itself around their regrets, since the corridors had shown Sable her father's forge, shown Oscar a warehouse burning—and still it broke her heart to watch him step forward, wolf-shaped and wrong, his fur stirring with light that wasn't his own.

"I have stood in this vault before," he said. His voice was rougher than a wolf's should be, half human at the edges, as though something in the deep places had begun remembering him. "I know how the bargain is struck. Take me. My soul is already yours by contract—it was sold once, and what has been sold can be surrendered. Leave her whole."

"No." The word left her before thought, before strategy, before anything but the oldest part of her. "No, Casper."

"It is fair accounting," the steward said, and there was something almost gentle in it, the way an avalanche is gentle to the mountain it comes from. "A debt for a debt. A soul for a soul. The books will balance."

"The books," Oscar said, "will not."

* * *

**Casper**

He smelled her decision before he heard it.

That was the trouble with knowing someone across two lifetimes—you learned their scent-language, the small chemical weather of them, fear and resolve and grief each carrying its own temperature. What rose off her now was none of those. It was something rarer, something he had caught only twice before: once on a rooftop in Vellmark when she'd redrawn a smuggler's route out from under a patrol's nose; once in the dark of the guildhall when she'd thought herself alone, tracing maps on the floorboards with one finger, breathing like a woman setting a broken bone.

It was the scent of a border being drawn.

"Casper." She stepped between him and the vault. Between him and the steward, which no living thing did lightly. "You were a man once. I've known it since the passes—I've felt it in your scent, in the shape of the debt that binds us. You sold yourself for something. Or something was taken from you. Either way—" her voice cracked, and she let it crack, which was new, which was the point— "either way, I am not going to buy my freedom with the last of yours. Not when I finally understand what we are to each other."

"You don't understand what I was," he said, low. "You understand what I am now. There's a difference, and the difference is teeth."

"Then show me the man," she said. "Show me, and I'll show you what I've been hiding since before you ever caught my scent."

The steward shifted. Even the mountain seemed to lean in.

"There is another currency," Oscar said, and her hands were steady now, steady as they had never been over the ledgers, steady as they had only ever been over maps. "One worth more than a soul already pledged. One the court cannot collect by force, because force makes nothing freely given. You want accounts. Then take mine—my true name, spoken aloud and witnessed. And my gift. All of it. Freely offered."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere far above, muffled by miles of stone, she thought she could hear the usurper's armies marching, boots on frozen ground, guided by a traitor's maps toward the free cities.

"A name freely given," the steward said slowly, "is a foundation stone. It cannot be unspoken without the speaker crumbling." Its pale face turned toward her, featureless except for the suggestion of eyes, like marks on a chart where a cartographer has noted *here be depths*. "And a gift surrendered willingly... the court has not received such payment in an age. We are owed much, little mapmaker. But we are owed nothing so fine as that."

"Then witness it." She turned to Casper, and took his great head in her hands, and pressed her forehead to his. His fur was warm. Underneath, faintly, absurdly, she felt the ghost of a jaw, a beard, a human cheekbone. "Whatever happens," she whispered, "remember Vellmark. Remember the rafters. Remember that someone read your name in an old book and chose to stay."

Then she faced the steward, drew breath down to the bottom of her lungs, and gave away the thing she had spent her whole life hiding.

"My name is Oskara Eriksen. Daughter of a mapmaker, keeper of impossible things, woman, gifted, guilty of nothing but concealment. I speak it freely. Witness it."

The vault drank the words like water into cracked earth.

"And my gift—" Her voice rang now, filling the seam-lit dark. "Every line I have ever drawn. Every border I have ever seen breathe. I give it to this court, freely, to use as it will—"

And then, because truth chosen over safety means truth *all the way through*, she knelt and laid her palms flat against the mountain's living stone and did the last thing, the unforgivable thing, the only thing:

She redrew it.

* * *

**Casper**

What happened next, he would spend the rest of whatever he was trying to describe, and fail.

Her gift poured out of her like light from a broken lamp—not lost, *given*, and given things move differently than stolen things. He watched her fingers trace lines in the stone, and the lines were true, truer than any map the court had ever owned, because they were drawn by a hand that loved what it mapped rather than a power that wanted to own it.

She redrew the boundaries of the court itself.

The shelves of souls shuddered. The steward made a sound like a page tearing. And Casper felt the ledger-mark inside him—cold, contractual, the scar of his own long-ago sale—begin to *burn*, and then to blur, and then to read differently, as though a clerk somewhere had dipped a pen and corrected an ancient error.

The borders collapsed inward.

Not violently. That was the terrible beauty of it. The court's power had always depended on its boundaries—on being *beneath*, on being *other*, on holding souls at arm's length behind walls only the dead could cross. And Oscar, with the last stroke of her gift, drew those walls tight around the court's own black heart until the geometry failed, until the vault folded in on itself like a fist closing on nothing, until the shelves of lantern-souls flared gold and blue and ink-dark and drifted upward like sparks from a bonfire, going home.

His soul went home.

It didn't fit right—that was the first thing he understood. Humanity came back to him the way feeling comes back to a frozen limb: agonizing, partial, real. He dropped to the stone on hands that were hands, mostly, fingers too long, knuckles furred, and gasped with a throat that remembered speech. In the polished black floor he caught his reflection: a man's face, wolf's eyes, ears that could not decide. Neither wholly. Both truly.

Above them—far above, beyond miles of rock and winter—the change reached the surface. He felt it leave her, felt the last thread of her gift pass out of the world's ordinary reach, and knew without knowing how that somewhere on a frozen road an army had just gone blind. The traitor's maps, drawn from a stolen echo of her talent, unraveled mid-march. Passes that had opened for the usurper's columns closed like wounds healing. Officers stared at charts that now showed honest mountains and lying roads, and the great machine of the invasion stuttered, stalled, and began, in confusion, to turn.

* * *

**Oscar**

When it was done she was kneeling on plain stone in a plain cavern, empty of shelves, empty of souls, empty of everything except a pale steward who looked—for the first time in its long existence—*uncertain*.

"The account," it said, "is settled."

"Good," she said. Her voice was hoarse. She felt hollowed and scoured, like a house after a fire, like a page after erasure. Empty. But emptiness, she thought dizzily, was also room. Space to write something new.

"You gave away your sight," the steward said. "You will never see a border breathe again."

"I know."

"And yet." The steward tilted its featureless head. "The realm remembers what you drew. Borders hold the shape of the hand that made them. When men ask, in the years to come, who redrew the world—they will say your name. Your *true* name. Names freely given travel farther than names stolen."

Behind her, footsteps—half pads, half boots. Casper came to her side, and when she looked up at him she saw the man-shape clearly now, tall and gaunt and gray-eyed, with a wolf's ears and a wolf's calm, wearing his humanity like a coat that hadn't fit in thirty years and fit better for the wearing.

"Oskara," he said, tasting the name.

"Say it again sometime," she managed. "It's been a while since anyone did."

Sable was shouting somewhere up the passage—something about light, about air, about the mountain *opening*. Because of course it was. A court whose boundaries had collapsed inward no longer needed to be beneath anything. The way out was simply a way, now, lit by the same pale glow that had once guarded the vaults, leading up and up toward a thin line of daylight that smelled of snow and smoke and, very faintly, of home.

Oscar got to her feet. Her legs held. Her hands, empty of gift, were still a mapmaker's hands, and they were shaking, and she let them.

"Casper," she said. "We have an army to mislead, a city to save, and a guild that thinks I'm a man named Oskar. Walk with me while I explain?"

He smiled—a human smile, crooked, with too many memories in it. "I walked beside you hidden," he said. "I'll walk beside you named."

They started toward the light. Far above, the mountain was opening like a book.
