## 10. Casper and the Wolf

The first thing I learned with hands again was how much they shake.

I had forgotten that. In the shape of the wolf, my body never trembled—grief ran through it like water through gravel, absorbed, gone. But this half-thing I am now, this creature of the space between, has fingers, and the fingers remember everything. This morning they shook while I tried to button a shirt Sable had stolen for me from a Vellmark tailor who no longer needed it. The buttons were small as teeth. I snarled at them, low in a throat that still knew how to growl, and gave up, and wore the shirt open like a man who had made a choice rather than lost an argument.

Oscar laughed at me from the doorway. Not Oskar. Never Oskar again.

"You could just be a wolf," she said. "It's allowed now. It's practically fashionable."

"It's not the wolf part I'm bad at," I told her, and she laughed again, and the sound went into me the way sunlight goes into stone—slowly, and all the way down.

We stood together at the window of what had been the guild warehouse and was becoming, plank by salvaged plank, something else. Below us Vellmark was rebuilding itself the way cities do after fire: crookedly, stubbornly, with tarps where roofs should be and cooking smoke rising from a hundred improvised hearths. The salt gates were shattered. The sea smelled stronger than it used to, brine and kelp and char, as if the ocean had leaned in to see what the city would become without its walls.

And beyond the city, on the long table Oscar had dragged up from the counting room, the maps lay spread out like a garden in bloom.

Her maps. Drawn openly now, signed with her own hand, her true name written in the corner of each one in ink she mixed herself: *Oscar Eriksen, Mapmaker of the Free Cities.* She did not hide the gift anymore. She could not—she had spent it, or given it away, or planted it like a seed in the court beneath the mountain, and what grew back was stranger and better. Her borders breathed on the paper. I could see them moving when the light was right, the lines of rivers and roads shifting by a hair's width, alive, listening to the country they described.

I had been human once. I remembered ledgers. I remembered that a map was supposed to be a *record*—here is what is, here is what was, here is the truth pinned flat under glass. But watching Oscar work, I understood that she had never believed that. Even in the years when she hid behind clerical drudgery and a dead man's name, even when she kept the guild's impossible inventory with a clerk's careful hand, she was doing something else entirely. She was not recording the world. She was courting it.

* * *

Sable found us at dusk, coming up the stairs three at a time the way he always does, as if gravity were a rumor he'd chosen not to believe. He is guildmaster now. Corvin's chair sits empty in the counting room below—no one would burn it and no one would sit in it, so it stands there like a gravestone you can trip over—and Sable presides over the smugglers and forgers and exiles from a stool he pulled up beside it, because, as he put it, "I'm not going to pretend I've earned the big chair. I've earned the stool. The chair's earned nothing but ash."

He had papers under his arm. Sable always has papers; the boy was forging documents before he could forge his own signature, which is a sentence he says about himself with enormous pride and no irony whatsoever.

"New edict drafts," he announced, dropping them on the map table. "From the council of the free cities. They want the gifted registered." He paused, savoring it. "*Voluntarily.* Can you believe it? Six months ago it was register or burn. Now it's register and we'll give you a little brass pin."

"They learn," Oscar said, not looking up from the border she was redrawing along the Ashwater. "Slowly. Loudly. But they learn."

"They learn because of you." Sable said it plainly, the way he says everything that matters, saving his cleverness for things that don't. "You walked into the court beneath the mountain with your name in your mouth and came out with the usurper's armies blind. Every gifted soul in five cities sleeps easier because a clerk got brave." He tossed the last packet onto the table. "Also, there's bread. Casper, there's bread with your name on it, which is a phrase I never thought I'd say, because for two years I thought your name was 'the dog.'"

"I answered to worse," I said.

"You answered to 'get off the table,' mostly." He grinned, and then his grin softened into something rarer, the expression he wears perhaps once a season, like a comet. "Glad you're back. Both of you. The whole—arrangement. Whatever you are now."

Neither of us knew what to call it either. I am half-restored. The court beneath the mountain broke apart around Oscar's redrawn boundaries, and in its collapsing the contracts it held began to unravel, and mine unraveled halfway before the whole place folded inward like a lung exhaling. Half my soul came home. The other half is still out there somewhere, in whatever country lies past the edge of a broken court, and some nights I feel it like a missing tooth—the tongue keeps returning to the gap.

But I have hands. I have a voice that can say *Oscar* instead of only thinking it. And when the moon is full I can still take the wolf-shape if I choose, run the ridgelines above Vellmark with the wind in my teeth, and come home to a window left open for me. Neither wholly man nor wholly wolf. A border-walker. It occurs to me sometimes that I have finally become the thing I was always shaped to be, and that the shaping took two lifetimes and cost more than I can count—but that Oscar's gift, freely given, bought back exactly enough of me to make the having worthwhile.

She says she doesn't regret it. She says the gift works better now anyway, that mapping a realm you've named yourself beats mapping one you're hiding inside of. I believe her. I also know the price of believing someone, and I pay it gladly.

* * *

That night I dreamed the old dream, the one that opened everything.

I am in the counting room of the world, the vault beneath the mountain, standing among shelves of souls in their glass and lead jars. I am wearing my human body, the one I sold away at nineteen, and my hands are steady because in dreams they always are. Somewhere in the dark, a steward made of pale light is reading a ledger aloud, and every entry is a debt, and every debt has my scent on it.

Except that in the dream now—this is new, this started after—Oscar is there too. She walks the aisles with a pen behind her ear and an ink-stained finger, touching each jar, and where she touches them the glass warms. "These aren't records," she tells the steward, in the voice she uses for arguments she has already won. "They're promises nobody kept. That's different. That's forgivable."

And then the dream does what it always does, the reason I keep it like a coal in my chest: it gives me the memory of the day we met. Rain on the Vellmark docks. A girl of seventeen with a ledger clutched to her chest like armor, smelling of ink and fear and, underneath it all, of contract-paper and mountain-cold—the unmistakable scent of a soul already marked for sale. I had been a wolf for sixty years by then, hollowed and running, answering to no one. I caught her scent across a whole harbor and every hair on my body stood on end, because I knew that smell. It was the smell of my own name in someone else's book.

An old debt, I told myself, in the beginning. She carried the mark; I owed the court; therefore I owed her keeping. That was the story I lived in for years, sleeping in her rafters, haunting her ledgers, telling myself it was obligation and nothing warmer.

It was never obligation. Obligation doesn't learn the sound of your particular breathing. Obligation doesn't stand between you and a hunt patrol with its hackles up, doesn't prepare to trade its second chance for your first, doesn't lie awake—insofar as wolves lie awake—counting the ways the world might take you. What bound me to Oscar Eriksen was never the ledger-mark. It was the woman writing in the margins.

In the dream, she closes the steward's ledger gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone has died, and says: *Done. We keep our own accounts now.*

I woke with the moon full and high, and I did not choose to be a man or a wolf. I lay between them, in the warm dark of the rebuilt loft, and listened to the city breathe.

* * *

Spring came early to the free cities, or maybe it only seemed to, because everyone was watching for it.

On the morning the council met to ratify the new borders, I walked beside Oscar through streets still smelling faintly of ash, and people looked at us—not at her, at *us*, the woman and the wolf-thing, the mapmaker and her strange companion—and no one crossed to the other side. A child ran up and asked if I could really turn into a wolf, and I crouched down on my shaking, wonderful hands and said, "Halfway. Ask your mother what halfway looks like," and the mother, laughing, pulled the child away, and that was all. That was all it was. To be seen and not hunted. I am still learning how light that weight is.

The council chamber had been a granary before the war, and it smelled of grain ghost and lamp oil. Delegates from the five free cities sat around a table that was, I noted with private satisfaction, built from the same salvaged beams as our warehouse floor. At its head, on a stool (he had refused, loudly and repeatedly, anything grander), sat Guildmaster Sable Ives, twenty-two years old, ink to the elbows, presenting the territorial accords with the air of a man auctioning stolen horses.

"The border at the Ashwater follows the river's *actual* course," he was saying, "not the course it followed thirty years ago, not the course the crown drew while drunk. Which means it moves. Yes. On purpose. The realm shifts; the map shifts; anyone who can't live with that can take it up with the mapmaker herself."

Every head turned to Oscar.

She stood, and I watched her do the thing she had practiced in front of windows and mirrors and, once, memorably, in front of me: lift her chin, plant her feet, speak her name aloud in a room full of strangers. "Oscar Eriksen," she said. "I drew these borders, and I'll defend them. Not because they're permanent. Nothing true is permanent. Because they're honest."

Later—after the votes, after the handshakes, after Sable celebrated by forging nothing whatsoever for a whole hour, a personal record—we climbed the ridge above Vellmark where she likes to work when the weather is kind. She spread the master map on the flat stone, weighted its corners with pebbles, and unstoppered her ink.

"Last line," she said. "Every map gets one. I've been saving this one."

I stood beside her, half-man, half-wolf, wholly hers, and watched her write in the lower corner of the great sheet, in her small fierce hand:

*Some maps are records. Some are promises. This one intends to keep its word.*
