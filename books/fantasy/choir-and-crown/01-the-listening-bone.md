## 1. The Listening Bone

The bone had to be right, and the bone was never right at first.

Vesper Amberdale turned the length of antler in the lamplight of her workshop, tilting it so the flame's glow ran along the grain like water along a streambed. Deer antler, shed in the autumn before last, gathered from the high pasture above Greyhollow where the stags rubbed their crowns raw against the granite. It was good bone — dense, sweet-smelling even now, with a faint mineral tang that reminded her of the inside of old churches — but it was not yet *his* bone, and that was the whole of the difficulty.

A memorial whistle could not be made of just anything. It had to be made of something the dead man had loved, or carried, or bled upon. The widow had brought her three offerings on a gray Tuesday: a knife handle he had gripped for thirty years, a walking staff worn smooth as river stone, and this antler, which he had found as a boy and kept on his mantel through an entire life, moving it from house to house like a relic. Vesper had weighed all three in her hands the way a midwife weighs an infant, and the antler had hummed against her palms — faintly, shyly, the way bone hums when it remembers being part of a living thing.

The knife handle had said nothing. The staff had said only *wood, wood, I was wood once*. But the antler — the antler had whispered a boy's laugh, high and startled, the sound of someone seeing snow for the first time.

She set her chisel to it now, the finest one, the one with the blade no wider than a grass stem, and began.

The workshop smelled of shavings and linseed oil and, beneath that, the particular cold sweetness of bone dust, which is a smell like no other and which Vesper had long ago stopped noticing, the way a bell-ringer stops hearing bells. Shelves climbed the walls to the low beamed ceiling, crowded with work in every stage of becoming: whistles half-bored, combs with teeth like a city's skyline, small carved birds with their wings folded, waiting. And in the corner, on a shelf of its own, apart — the pieces she did not sell. The ones that had voices in them still.

There were forty-one of them. She had stopped counting at thirty because the number frightened her, and then counted again anyway, because not knowing was worse.

Every voice stayed with her. That was the cost of the gift, and no one had warned her of it, and she had learned it too late to do anything but live with it the way one lives with a limp or a scar — by never letting anyone see how much it ached. A carving made for the dead, made with love and intent, opened a door. And doors, once opened, did not close all the way. A thread of the dead man remained in the bone, the way smoke remains in a coat, and that thread spoke to her in the small hours, in the pauses between her own thoughts, in the space just before sleep when the mind's guards all go home.

She had her father's voice in a pipe carved from his shin — he had asked for it, laughing, on his deathbed, *put me on the mantel, girl, and I'll whistle at your suitors* — and he did whistle, sometimes, tuneless and cheerful, and she had not had the heart to silence him. She had her mother's voice in nothing at all, because her mother had vanished into the Thornfells twenty years ago leaving no body and no bone, and that silence was the loudest thing in the workshop.

And she had thirty-nine others. A drowned miller who recited the levels of the river in his sleep. A child who had died of the fever and who sang a skipping rhyme over and over, not because she was trapped in pain but because it had been her favorite song, and the dead keep what they loved. An old soldier who said nothing at all, ever, but whose silence had a shape, like a man standing at attention in a corner of her mind.

The living, Vesper had learned, lied easily and often and sometimes without knowing it. The dead could not lie. They had nothing left to protect. When a voice came up through the grain of carved bone, it came up true — and so Vesper trusted it, the way a sailor trusts the pull of the tide, and she had come to trust it more than she trusted any living face, including her own.

The chisel whispered along the antler's curve. Outside, Greyhollow went about its evening: cart wheels in the muck of the high street, the smith's hammer keeping its patient time, gulls arguing over the fish-market leavings because even here, three days from the sea, the gulls always found them. A market town, unremarkable, wedged in the green lap of the foothills with the Thornfells rising gray and snow-shouldered beyond, close enough to see the weather coming down off them like a slow gray flood.

*Careful along the fork there,* said a voice. *The grain turns. It always turns at the fork.*

Vesper's chisel paused. "I know," she said to the empty workshop. "I can see it."

*I know you can,* said Tam Merrow, dead these six weeks, patient as a schoolmaster. *I only like to watch you work. It's the best thing I've seen from this side.*

Tam was the antler. Tam was the whistle taking shape under her hands, the man who had found it as a boy, and he had been present in the bone all evening in the way of the recently dead — vivid, chatty, still surprised by the strangeness of being a voice without a chest. In a year he would quiet. In five he would be like the others, a murmur, a habit of sound she heard only when she listened. But now, now he was all commentary, all the life of him still sloshing around the bone like water in a jug.

"Tell me about the fork," Vesper said, and bent closer to the work. It was not only kindness. A whistle's voice depended on the airway, and the airway depended on the grain, and Tam had held this antler for sixty years. He knew it better than her eyes did.

*My father cut the fork off himself,* Tam said, as she pared the channel deeper. *Said an antler with a fork was luck, two paths instead of one. Superstitious old goat. I kept it anyway. Kept it through the bad winter when we ate the seed corn, kept it when your mother —*

He stopped. Vesper's chisel stopped.

*When the Amberdale woman came through,* Tam finished, more carefully. *Before you were born, that was. Or just after. She bought tallow from my wife and asked strange questions about the high passes, and my wife said she had eyes like a person counting money that wasn't hers.*

The shavings curled away from the blade, pale as fingernails. Vesper did not answer. She had learned, over a lifetime, that the dead mentioned her mother the way weather mentioned rain — unpredictably, and always from an angle she hadn't braced for. A woman who walked into the Thornfells with a carver's kit and did not walk out. A woman whose last letter had said only: *The mountains are full of doors, little wren. Some of them are singing.*

"Tell me about the mouthpiece," Vesper said. "Was it round or oval, when you imagined it? Boys always imagine it."

*Round,* Tam said, grateful for the steering. *Round, like a pebble from the Wrenwater. I used to hold the antler to my mouth when I was small and pretend it was a horn, and my mother would say, Tam, you look ridiculous, and I would say, one day they'll make it a real horn, one day it will speak.*

"And now it will," Vesper said softly.

*Now it will.* A pause in the voice, the particular quality of a dead man about to say the thing he had died not saying. *Vesper. When it's done. Will she hear me? My wife. Will she hear what I —*

"I don't know," Vesper said, because she did not lie to the dead. "No one who's bought one has ever told me they heard anything. But they come back. They come back and buy more, for their mothers, their sons. So either they hear something, or they need to believe they might. I've stopped asking which."

*That's an honest answer.*

"It's the only kind I give."

*No,* said Tam, and there was a smile in the voice, dry and fond. *You give the kind that keeps people from worrying. I watched you do it for forty years, girl, from across the market. You smile like a locked door.*

Vesper's hands went still on the antler.

She had not known she was known. That was the whole architecture of her life, built so carefully: the competent carver, the bone-witch with the steady eyes who took your grief and made it into a small singing thing and never once let you see what it cost her, never once let you hear the crowd of the dead that stood murmuring in the corners of her skull like petitioners outside a magistrate's door. Loneliness was a thing you wore under the competence, the way a soldier wears mail under a tabard. No one was meant to see it. Certainly no one meant to name it.

"Carving now," she said, gruff, and bent her head, and Tam — who had been a kind man, and dead men are kinder still, having nothing left to prove — let her be.

* * *

The whistle took until well past midnight.

It was slow work, the boring of the airway, a matter of fractions. Too wide and the whistle breathed but did not speak; too narrow and it shrieked like a kettle. She tested it six times against her own lips, and six times it gave her only breath, and Tam gave her only encouragement, and the forty-one voices on the corner shelf murmured among themselves in the low tide-murmur that was their habit, a sound like a village heard from far across a valley.

On the seventh try, at the hour when the fire had burned down to a red eye and the frost was climbing the inside of the windows, the whistle spoke.

It was not a whistle, precisely. It was a voice — thin, air-borne, made of breath and bone and the shape of a channel cut by a patient hand — and it was Tam's voice, or the ghost of it, the way a footprint is the ghost of a foot. It sang the three notes he had asked for: the rising phrase his wife had hummed at the washtub all their married life, a tune with no name that every married couple in the valley seemed to know, passed mouth to mouth like a flame.

And beneath the tune, wrapped in it the way a seed is wrapped in its husk, came the last words.

Vesper had not asked for the last words. She never asked. But the last words were what the bone held closest, pressed into it like a flower in a book, and when the airway opened, they came out whether she willed it or not. She sat in the firelight with the small bone voice singing in her cupped hands and heard Tam Merrow die.

He had died well, which was a mercy. In his own bed, in October, with the rain on the roof and his wife's hand in his and his son's hand on his shoulder, and his last words were not a message, not a confession, not a name spoken into the dark. They were: *There's the tune. There it is. I've been hearing it all day. Don't grieve loud, love. Grieve quiet, like the tune. I'll be in the bone.*

*I'll be in the bone.*

The whistle sang the three notes again, and again, and Vesper sat with it until the fire died and the frost flowers on the window turned gray with dawn, and she did not weep, because she had learned long ago that the dead did not need her weeping. They needed her hands. Weeping was for the living, and the living had their own hands to hold.

* * *

The widow came at mid-morning, in the gap between the fish-cart and the wool-sellers, when the market noise was at its thickest and no one would mark a woman in gray slipping down the alley to the carver's door.

Marta Merrow had been handsome once and was now the kind of weathered that made people call a face honest. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry. Grief, Vesper knew, came in two kinds: the kind that flooded and the kind that froze. Marta's had frozen. It stood in her like ice in a river, and everything moved around it.

"You have it," Marta said. Not a question.

"I have it." Vesper wrapped the whistle in wool — good wool, she always used good wool, the dead deserved warmth — and set it in the widow's palms. "It sings his tune. Three notes, rising. Hold it to your lips and breathe easy, the way you'd breathe on embers. Don't blow. Breathe."

Marta turned it over in her hands. The antler's fork sat against her fingers like something that had always lived there, which, in a manner of speaking, it had. She did not raise it to her mouth. She only held it, and her thumb moved over the carving — Vesper had cut a wren into the side, small and alert, because Tam had called her *wren* in the bone and it seemed right, and because a carver signs her work the only way that matters.

"Will I hear him?"

Vesper had answered this question a hundred times and had never once found the true answer, only the kind one, only the locked door. She looked at the widow's frozen river of a face, and she thought of Tam saying *you smile like a locked door,* and she heard herself say, before her caution could catch it:

"I don't know. But he loved you. That's in the bone. Whatever else it does or doesn't do, it holds that, and bone holds longer than stone. Longer than grief. He said —" and here she stopped, because this was the true answer, and she had never given the true answer before, and it came out of her like a splinter coming out of flesh, painful and relieving at once. "He said not to grieve loud. Grieve quiet, like the tune. He said he'd be in the bone."

Marta Merrow looked at her for a long moment. The ice in her face did not crack, but something behind it shifted, a dark movement under white, the way a river moves under its frozen skin.

"You heard him," the widow said. "You hear them. All of them. That's what they say about you in the market, and I never believed it, because it's too terrible a thing to be true."

"It's true," Vesper said.

"And they stay? After? They stay with you?"

"Every one."

The widow's hand closed around the whistle, knuckles whitening. "Then I'll not ask you to make another. Not for a while. It's too —" She stopped, and her eyes went to the corner shelf, to the forty-one small shapes in the lamplight, and Vesper watched her understand, watched her count, watched the horror and the pity arrive together in her face like two riders.

"Forty-two," Marta said quietly, "if you count him."

"Forty-two."

"And you live with that."

"I live with that."

The widow reached out and, with the suddenness of the old and grieving, took Vesper's hand — took the hand that had held her husband's voice all night — and pressed it. Her palm was rough and warm and alive, and Vesper realized, with a small cold shock, how long it had been since a living person had touched her without wanting something carved.

"Girl," Marta said, "who holds *you*?"

No one, Vesper did not say. The dead, she did not say. Forty-two voices in a cold workshop, and not one of them can warm my hands.

"The work holds me," she said, and smiled the locked-door smile, and the widow let it stand, because the grieving learn early which doors are not theirs to open.

Marta left with the whistle inside her coat, against her heart, and Vesper stood in her doorway a moment, watching the market swallow her gray figure. The morning was bright and cold. Gulls wheeled. Somewhere a cartload of turnips was being argued over with real passion, the living quarreling over small things because small things were what the living had.

*She'll be all right,* said Tam's voice from the shelf where the wool wrapping had lain, fainter now, already settling into the long quiet. *She's stronger than me. Always was. Vesper —*

"Yes?"

*Thank you for not lying to her. It mattered, that you didn't lie.*

Vesper closed the door on the cold. She banked the fire, set her chisels in their roll, and stood a moment in the middle of her workshop, in the middle of her forty-two voices, in the middle of her competent, locked, solitary life.

And that was when the knocking started — not at her door, but somewhere down the alley, urgent, uneven, the knocking of someone who was falling as they knocked. Then the sound of a body against her threshold, and a hand scrabbling at the latch, and through the door's thin boards a voice, wet and failing, gasping words she would spend the rest of this story trying to understand:

"The Choir — the Choir still sings —"
