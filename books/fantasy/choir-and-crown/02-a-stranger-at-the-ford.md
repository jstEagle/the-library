## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The rain came down Greyhollow's single street in ropes, and Vesper Amberdale was glad of it. Rain meant the market stalls would empty, the gossips would scurry under eaves, and her workshop — a lean-to of wattle and slate wedged between the chandler's and the tannery, smelling always of bone dust and linseed oil — would belong to her alone. She had work to finish. She always had work to finish; that was the trouble with being good at the one thing no one else could do.

On her bench lay the whistle: a memorial piece, commissioned by the widow Marta Crane for her husband Tam, drowned three weeks past when the mill weir took him sideways and held him under. Vesper had chosen a goose femur for it, hollow and clean, and she had spent four evenings on the carving — little waves along the barrel, a reed at the mouthpiece, and along the underside, where only a thumb would rest, Tam's name in letters small as stitches.

Names were the whole craft, really. Anyone could drill a hole in a bone and make it sing. What Vesper did was different. She carved the name into the bone the way you'd set a lock into a doorframe, precisely, with the grain, with the memory of how the syllables sat in a living mouth. And then, when someone breathed through the finished piece, they heard what the dead man had carried with him across the water. Not a whole soul. Just the last of him — a sentence, sometimes a song, once, memorably, a complaint about his brother-in-law's pigs.

And every voice stayed with her after. That was the part she never told the grieving. They came to her bench weeping, paid their coppers or their chickens, left holding a whistle that whispered, and went home lighter. Vesper remained in the workshop with all of them layered inside her skull like guests who never learned to leave. Eleven years of the dead of Greyhollow and the villages round about, murmuring in the walls of her mind while she ate her supper and trimmed her lamp and lay awake counting heartbeats that were not her own.

She trusted them more than the living, if she was honest. The dead didn't lie. The dead didn't ask anything of her but to be heard.

The rain thickened. Down the street, the ford over the Hollow Brook ran loud and brown, and above its noise she thought she heard hoofbeats — then dismissed it. Riders wouldn't cross in this water. Only a fool or a dying man would try the ford tonight.

She bent back to the whistle, touched the burin to the last wave-crest —

— and heard the sound every carver dreads: a body hitting wood. A heavy, wet, final thud against the workshop door, followed by a scrabbling, like something that meant to get up and couldn't.

Vesper set down the burin. Her heart did an odd, arrhythmic thing, because she already knew, in the way she knew things, what was on the other side of that door. The dead announced themselves to her before they arrived. It was fainter than hearing — more like pressure, like weather changing behind her eyes — but there it was now: a presence at her threshold, flickering, guttering, a candle in a draft.

She opened the door.

He fell into her arms. That was the truth of it; there was no graceful way to describe a grown man collapsing against a bone-carver half his weight. He was soaked black with rain and redder than rain should make anyone, blood diluted to pink water sheeting off his cloak and pooling around her boots. His lips were gray. One hand clutched his side where a blade — she'd know the wound anywhere, she'd carved names for enough men who'd died of it — had gone in below the ribs and angled up.

"You're the—" He coughed. "Amberdale. The carver."

"Inside," she said, though inside was six feet away and he weighed as much as an anvil. She got him through the doorway somehow, half-dragging, half-carrying, and lowered him onto the floor beside her hearth, where the coals still glowed from morning. She pressed her hands to the wound out of pure reflex, the way any fool does, knowing even as she did it that nothing she owned would close it. Blood welled between her fingers, warm as bathwater.

"Listen." His hand shot up and caught her wrist with shocking strength. His eyes were pale hazel, wide, fixed on hers with the terrible clarity people sometimes found at the very end. "You're the carver. They said — in the Thornfells, they said there was a carver in Greyhollow who listened true."

"I listen true," she said stupidly. "Who did this to you?"

"Doesn't matter. There's no time. There's never—" Another cough, wetter. He fumbled beneath his cloak, at his chest, working stiff fingers at a cord knotted around his neck. "Take it. Take it *off me*. If they find it on my body they'll burn the town looking for whoever I gave it to. Do you understand? You take it, you're nobody. You leave it, everyone's somebody."

"What is it?"

"The map." He got the cord loose and pressed the thing into her palm, folding her fingers shut over it with both of his ruined hands. It was heavier than it looked — a case of yellowed bone, long as her forearm, capped with horn, warm from riding against his skin. "To the Hollow Choir. Under the Thornfells. The old road, the keeper's road — no one living knows it, no one but—"

He stopped. His face changed. Not pain — something worse, some grief arriving late.

"No one but who?" Vesper asked.

But he wasn't answering her anymore. He was looking past her, at the firelight, and his lips moved, shaping words too quiet to catch, the way the dying talk to people only they can see. Then his eyes came back to hers and gripped them.

"The Choir still sings," he whispered.

Three words. She would carry those three words for the rest of her life, and she knew it even then, the way she knew the weight of every voice she'd ever carved. He exhaled — a long breath that seemed to go on longer than lungs should allow — and then stopped breathing altogether, and the pressure behind Vesper's eyes changed quality: from guttering candle to settled ash. Dead. Here, on her floor, in the first hour of rain-dark.

His ghost did not speak to her. Sometimes they did, in the first minutes; most often they simply dissolved into that layer of murmur she carried everywhere. But before he went — she would swear this later, to Aldric, to anyone who asked — before the last of him unraveled, she felt him look at the map in her closed fist. Felt him *hope* at her. It was the strangest sensation of her strange life: being hoped at by a dying stranger.

* * *

She did not sleep.

She lit every lamp she owned, which made her feel braver and changed nothing. She examined the case by firelight: bone, old bone, older than anything she worked with, yellowed like teeth, dense as ivory. No hinges she could find. No seam. Whatever was inside had been sealed in — grown in, almost, the way a tree swallows a nail. Along one edge ran a line of script she didn't recognize, angular and deliberate, and beneath the script, scratched shallowly as an afterthought, two initials: *M.A.*

She stared at those letters a long time.

*M.A.* Amberdale was her father's name, dead twelve years, a cooper who couldn't carve his way out of a bread loaf. But M.A. — *Maren* Amberdale? No. The queen was Maren, but queens didn't scratch initials on smuggler-bone. And there was another M.A., wasn't there? A name she kept in a box under her bed with a broken comb and a river stone. A woman who had walked into the Thornfells twenty years ago with her carving kit and her secrets and had never walked out.

Vesper put the case down as if it had bitten her.

Outside, the rain kept on. Inside, the corpse cooled on her floorboards, and the widow Crane's whistle sat unfinished on the bench, and Vesper discovered that her hands were shaking so badly she couldn't have carved a straight line if the queen herself had ordered it.

What do you do with a dying man's last trust? Every instinct said *bury it*. Bury him, bury the map, tell no one, go back to whistles for widows. She was a bone-carver in a market town. She mended griefs for coppers. Whatever war or crown or conspiracy this courier had run through the dark with, it was sized for people who lived in palaces, and she was not one of those people, and neither was Greyhollow.

But she could still feel him hoping at her. And underneath that, older and less deniable, was the carver's law her mother had taught her before leaving: *when the dead hand you something, you carry it. You don't get to choose what. You only choose how far.*

Hoofbeats. Real ones this time, unmistakable, many, coming up the ford road despite the flood-water — which meant riders who hadn't cared about drowning, or hadn't expected to live long enough for it to matter. Lantern-light swung wild through the rain-slit window.

Vesper's body moved before her mind finished arguing. She swept the courier's cloak over his face — not decency, just speed — snatched the bone case off the bench, and stood in the middle of her workshop holding the thing like a hot coal, with nowhere in the world to put it.

Under the floorboards? First place anyone looked. In the fire? Burned evidence drew worse questions than hidden evidence. Out the back into the tannery yard? They'd have circled the buildings; riders always did.

Then her eye fell on the bench, and on the great half-finished femur lying beside the whistle — a cow's bone, thick as her wrist, commissioned weeks ago by a farmer for his father's memorial staff and abandoned mid-work because the old man's family kept quarreling over the inscription. It was hollowed at one end for the fitting of a handle. It was exactly, insultingly, the right size.

She worked fast and she worked scared, and scared hands are precise hands; eleven years of fine carving had trained her fingers to steady themselves without consulting her. She packed the case into the femur's hollow core with shavings and wax, sealed the mouth with a plug of rawhide and pitch, and scored the exterior with fresh, ordinary, boring carving-marks — a border of running knots, nothing meaningful, the kind of decoration that says *nothing to see here* to any man who has ever searched a house. By the time boots hit her doorstep, the femur looked like what it had always looked like: an unfinished commission, dusty and dull, waiting on a carver's bench.

The knock nearly took the door off its frame.

There were five of them when she opened it, rain streaming off oiled leather, swords at their hips and the flat, tired eyes of men who had ridden hard for days. Their leader wore no livery she recognized — which itself was information, because everyone in the borderlands knew every livery there was — but his cloak-pin was a silver circle, plain and closed, and Vesper had heard enough smuggler-talk over enough pints to know what a closed circle meant. Royalist. Not the queen's men. The *other* royalists. The kind that met in cellars and spoke of restoration in whispers.

"Lady." The leader inclined his head with a courtesy that fit him like a borrowed coat. Behind him his men spread out along her frontage without being told, casual as crows. "Forgive the hour. We're seeking a traveler. A courier, riding south ahead of us. Have you seen him?"

"A courier." Vesper kept her voice level by keeping it slow. "No one rides the ford tonight."

"He may have crossed anyway. Desperate men do." The leader's gaze drifted past her shoulder into the workshop, unhurried, cataloguing: the lamps, the bench, the tools, the shrouded shape on the floor. "May we come in? The rain is filthy."

Every answer had teeth. *Yes*, and they'd turn the place over. *No*, and they'd wonder why a carver with a corpse on her floor refused help. She stepped aside. "Mind the shavings. Bone dust gets into everything."

They came in like they owned it. Two of the men went straight for the body, and Vesper's stomach dropped — until the leader himself said, "Leave him. He's past questions." They turned the corpse's cloak back anyway, looked at the wound, at the face, and exchanged a glance that told her everything: *this is our man, and he is dead, and the thing he carried is missing.*

"Did he say anything?" the leader asked. "Before the end. Dying men talk."

"To whom?" Vesper said. "I found him at my door already dying. I'm a bone-carver, sir, not a surgeon. I put my hands where my hands shouldn't be and watched him stop breathing. That's the whole of my evening." She let a beat pass, then added, with the weary honesty of a woman with nothing to hide, "You're welcome to ask the widow next door. She'll tell you I sat up all night with her husband's whistle and cried into my ale. That's the sort of excitement I keep."

It was good work, she thought distantly, watching him weigh it. Better than her carving. Loneliness, it turned out, made a fine disguise; no one suspected the woman everyone pitied.

The leader wandered her shop instead. He picked up finished pieces, set them down. He paused over the memorial whistles hanging on their rack, and something in his face flickered — recognition, maybe, or memory. "They say you carve the dead's voices into these."

"They say a lot of things."

"Is it true?"

"It's true that widows pay me and don't complain twice."

He smiled thinly, and set down the whistle he'd been holding, and his eyes traveled — she tracked them the way you track a spider on the ceiling — across the bench, across the tools, across the half-finished femur with its fresh border of running knots. Stopped there. Came back to her.

"Rough country between here and the Thornfells," he said. "A lone woman, keeping a shop full of dead men's voices." He said it almost gently. "If your traveler comes back — if he left anything, said anything, marked anything — you'd send word to the sign of the Closed Circle in Millbrook. There'd be coin in it. More coin than carvings ever brought you."

"There usually is," Vesper said. "Coin. In other people's errands."

He laughed at that, a short dry sound with no warmth in it, and it was worse than any threat. At the door he paused, rain roaring behind him, and looked back at her with those flat tired eyes.

"The Choir still sings, they say," he remarked, conversational as a man commenting on weather. "Old smugglers' nonsense. Still — if you hear it singing, lady, you'll send word. Won't you?"

"I don't hear anything but the rain," Vesper said.

Which was, for the first time in eleven years, a lie.

* * *

They rode on toward the mountains, lanterns shrinking down the ford road, and Greyhollow's street filled again with drumming rain and nothing else. Vesper barred her door, dragged the courier's body onto her own bed because the floor was cold and he deserved better, covered him with her good blanket, and sat down at her bench with the lamp turned low.

For a long time she did nothing. The murmur of the dead rose and fell inside her skull, the familiar tide of it — Tam Crane complaining amiably about the cold, a child lost to fever humming a skipping rhyme, forty others, eighty, the whole drowned congregation of her life's work. She let it wash. It was the closest thing she had to company, and tonight company was the only thing standing between her and screaming.

Then she took up the burin, and unstoppered the femur, and drew out the bone case, and set it in the lamplight where the script along its edge caught fire.

*M.A.* Her mother's initials, or her imagination's. The Choir still sings. A sealed road beneath the Thornfells that no one living knew — except, perhaps, one woman who had gone into those mountains twenty years ago and never come home.

Vesper thought about the courier's hands folding her fingers shut. About the leader's voice, gentle as a knife laid flat: *if you hear it singing.* About Corvane — for she'd heard that name too, in tavern whispers, attached to these closed-circle men like a shadow attached to feet — the Queen's Inquisitor, the man who burned songs out of the world.

Her mother had taught her the carver's law. Her mother had also taught her, the same winter, how to read a smuggler's trail-mark, and had never explained why a respectable bone-carver's wife needed such knowledge, and had walked into the hills before Vesper could ask.

"When the dead hand you something," Vesper said aloud, to the corpse on her bed, to the rain, to whatever was left of the man who had hoped at her, "you carry it."

She wrapped the case in oilcloth, tucked it into the false bottom of her tool chest, and began, with steady hands and no appetite for sleep, to pack.

At dawn, if the ford allowed, she would ride east into the Thornfells, following a dead man's map toward a singing vault, hunted by men whose faces she now knew — and toward a mountain where, if the scratched initials told the truth, something of her mother might still be waiting.

Behind her, on the bed, the courier lay silent. But in the tide of murmuring dead that Vesper carried always, a new voice had settled overnight, faint and urgent and repeating, the way the newest ones always did, the last words over and over:

*The Choir still sings. The Choir still sings.*
