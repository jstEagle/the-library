## 4. The Knight Without a Banner

The rain had followed Aldric Vane for three days up the Thornfell passes, as if it had been assigned to him. It came in sideways now off the high col, needling through the gaps of his cloak, and he stood at the edge of the switchback road with his hood down and his sword hand bare, because he had decided that if he was going to do this thing — whatever this thing turned out to be — he would do it with his face open to the weather and his hands where they could be seen.

Below him, half a mile down the serpentine track, a lone figure climbed through the grey.

He had been watching her since Greyhollow. Not following, not exactly. *Shadowing* was the word Ser Osric would have used, back when Aldric had still been a man who took orders from Ser Osric. The bone-carver woman — Amberdale, the widow's neighbor said, Vesper Amberdale — had left her town two days behind the courier's death, carrying nothing visible but her satchel and her knives, walking like someone who knew which stones on a smuggler's road were loose and which were watchers. She had slipped past the Queen's checkpoint at Marrow Ford so cleanly that the garrison there would spend another week believing no one had come through at all. That alone told him things about her that no dossier could: she had grown up on these roads, or under them; she had learned to move before she had learned why moving mattered.

And she was carrying something worth killing for. He knew that too, though he did not yet know what it was. Three men had died in Greyhollow within an hour of the courier's collapse — no, four, counting the courier himself — and every one of them had died around a map that Aldric's own order had spent eleven years trying to find.

His horse shifted behind him, blowing steam. He kept it tethered well back from the trailhead, in the stand of black pines where the smugglers' path forked away from the King's Road — the old king's road, he corrected himself, though the stone markers still bore the antlered crown, because Queen Maren had never ordered them chiseled out. She didn't need to. Erasure by patience was cleaner than erasure by hammer, and Maren was a patient woman. He had to grant her that. Eleven years on the throne and the realm was fed, the roads were safe, the border lords were quiet, and not one honest soul in the capital could say precisely how any of it had been accomplished. That was the trouble with her reign. It worked too well, the way a lock works, the way a gag works.

Aldric had stopped being able to tell, some months ago, whether he hated her for usurping the throne or for making the theft look like governance.

*You're stalling,* he told himself, and went down to meet the road.

---

He chose the narrows above the second waterfall, where the track cut between a shoulder of wet granite and a drop into white water, because there was no room there for either flight or fight to go well, and because he wanted — he admitted this to himself with some distaste — to give her every reason to stop. If she ran, he'd have to chase her, and chasing her meant becoming the thing he'd sworn off becoming. If she fought, she'd lose, and then he'd have to decide, right there, in front of a corpse or a captive, what kind of man he actually was.

Better to let the mountain do the work. Better to leave only one direction available: forward, toward him, into conversation.

She came around the shoulder of rock with her hood back and her eyes already on him.

That surprised him. Most travelers saw the horse first, or heard the water, or noticed the shape of a man standing in the rain and mistook him for a boulder until they were too close. This woman looked straight at him from twenty paces, in the rain, and did not break stride, and Aldric felt something turn over in his chest that he refused to name as respect.

"Good morning," he said, because it was, barely, and because courtesy cost nothing.

"It's raining," she said. Her voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone who had priced the danger of a situation and found it affordable. "And you're blocking the road."

"I am." He spread his empty hands slightly, showing the lack of drawn steel. "I'll ask you not to reach for anything in your satchel. I've no wish to hurt you."

"You've no wish to hurt me." She stopped, finally, six paces off, and pushed her hood fully back, and he got his first real look at her face: sharp-boned, rain-wet, younger than he'd expected and older in the eyes than the rest of her. There was a fine white scar along her jaw, old and deliberate-looking, the kind that comes from a blade held steady rather than swung wild. Her gaze went over him with the frankness of a tradeswoman assessing stock — the cloak, the boots, the sword, the lack of any badge or livery — and he watched her arrive, without hurry, at her conclusion.

"No banner," she said.

"No banner."

"That usually means one of three things. Outlaw, deserter, or someone whose banner would be worse than none." Her eyes flicked to his sword belt, to the plain steel hilt without ornament. "You're not an outlaw. Outlaws dress poorer. And you don't move like a deserter — deserters flinch at their own names. Which leaves the third thing, and I notice you haven't told me your order yet, which means your banner would be worse than none. So." She tilted her head. "Royalist?"

The word landed in the space between them like a dropped coin, ringing.

"Yes," Aldric said.

"And here you are, alone, on a smuggler's road, stopping a bone-carver in the rain." Something moved behind her expression — not fear. Calculation, maybe, or its cousin, contempt. "Let me guess. You want what the courier carried."

He should have denied knowledge of the courier. A competent agent would have. But he was tired of competence in the service of lies, tired of it the way a man gets tired of a food that has begun to taste of ash, and so he said, "I want to know if you have it. And then I want to know what you intend to do with it. In that order."

"In that order," she repeated. "Most of your brothers-in-cause skip the second question entirely."

"My brothers-in-cause," Aldric said, and found that his voice had gone quieter and harder without his permission, "burned a granary in Lowmarsh last spring to draw out a tax garrison. Forty-one people lived in Lowmarsh. Thirty-one still do. They fly the antlered crown from every ruin they make, and they call themselves loyalists, and I have watched them weep genuine tears over the memory of a dead king while stepping over living children to get at the gold in his name." He stopped. The rain filled the pause, drumming on granite, hissing into the falls. "So yes. I'm a royalist. God help me, I still believe the old bloodline matters. And I am standing in the rain asking you questions my order would simply put to the knife, because I no longer trust myself to know which side of that line I'm standing on, and I thought perhaps the answer might be found somewhere other than in a corpse."

She stared at him. For a long moment the only sound was the water and the weather, and then she laughed — one short, disbelieving breath of it, gone as fast as it came.

"You're serious," she said. "You actually mean that."

"I mean every word. Whether any of them are wise is another matter."

"They're not," she said. "Wise. Telling a stranger all that. Telling *me*, of all people, that you can't trust yourself." Her hand had drifted, very casually, to the strap of her satchel, and he noted it and did not comment. "But it's the truest thing anyone in a crown's colors has said to me in years, so I'll return the courtesy. Yes, I have it. No, I won't give it to you. And what I intend to do with it is find out what it is before I decide anything else, because everyone else who's touched it has ended up dead, and I'd prefer to break the pattern."

"The courier—"

"Died holding my doorframe," she said, and for just a moment the flat trade of her voice cracked and he saw underneath: the memory of a dying man's weight, the blood, the words pressed into her ear. Whatever the courier had whispered, it had lodged in her like a splinter. "He gave it to me. Not to your order. Not to the Queen. To me, a carver he'd met once, because apparently I was the safest pair of hands in Greyhollow, which tells you everything about the state of this realm." Her chin lifted. "So. You know what I carry and that I won't yield it. Why am I still talking to you instead of walking past?"

"Because I'm not going to take it from you," Aldric said.

"You couldn't," she said, with such simple certainty that it startled a huff of laughter out of him despite everything.

"Probably not," he admitted. "But that's not why. I'm not going to take it because taking it is the one thing I've already decided against, and I find that these days I hold onto my decisions like a drowning man holds driftwood." He stepped aside, deliberately, clearing the narrow way past him. Water sheeted off the rock where he'd been leaning. "Go on, then. The pass closes by nightfall if the rain keeps up, and there's shelter in the drowned chapel at Hollowmere, if you know to look for it."

Her eyes narrowed. "How do you know about the chapel?"

"Because I've been on these roads longer than you've been alive, and because I've been watching you since Greyhollow, which you may as well know, since pretending otherwise would insult us both. I know you bought dried plums and a whetstone and lied beautifully about your destination to the ferryman. I know you slept cold rather than light a fire on the ridge. I know you talk to yourself when you think no one's listening—" he paused, hearing how that sounded, "—and that sometimes, from a distance, it sounds less like talking to yourself and more like listening to something."

That landed. He saw it land — a small stillness in her, the stillness of a door closing softly somewhere inside. When she spoke again her voice was carefully level.

"You've wasted three days of your life, Sir Whoever-You-Are. Whatever I'm doing, it isn't treason against your queen, and it isn't a gift for your pretenders. It's a debt. A dead man asked me for something, and I intend to see it done, and then I'm going home to carve whistles for widows and be left alone."

"A debt to the dead," Aldric said quietly. "Yes. I understand those."

"Do you." It wasn't quite a question. She looked at him a moment longer — measuring him again, but differently now, he thought; the way one measures weather rather than stock — and then she moved forward through the gap he'd made, close enough that he caught the smell of wood shavings and lamp-oil and rain on wool, and she did not slow, and she did not look back, and she passed him as easily as if he had been the boulder she'd first mistaken him for.

Then, five paces beyond, she stopped.

"You said the chapel at Hollowmere," she said, not turning. "That's a day south of where I'm going."

"I know."

"Why tell me?"

He considered lying. He was, after all, still technically an agent of the Restoration, and the Restoration paid him to lie the way millers pay for grain. But the rain was coming harder, and the truth had been sitting in his chest for three days like a stone he could neither swallow nor spit out, and so he set it down where she could see it.

"Because whoever comes next won't be me," he said. "Corvane has the scent now. You don't know Corvane. I pray you never learn him the way I have — he doesn't burn villages, he doesn't threaten grandmothers, he sits down at your table and asks about your mother and leaves an hour later having taken everything you know without ever once raising his voice, and you thank him at the door. When he finds you, and he will, no amount of smuggler's roads will save you, because he doesn't follow roads. He follows people." He drew a breath. "I can't stop him. I'm one man with no banner and no order that will have me back after today. But I can walk behind you at a distance, and watch the trails, and know when the hunt changes direction. Call it penance. Call it curiosity. Call it whatever lets you sleep."

Now she did turn. Rain ran off her chin, and her eyes were unreadable as river-stone.

"If you follow me," she said, "I'll know."

"I expect so."

"And if I catch you getting close—"

"You'll hear me coming. I'm too old and too honest to skulk well. It's one of my many failings as an agent." He almost smiled. "I'm Aldric Vane. Late of the Order of the Antlered Crown. Currently of nowhere in particular."

"Vesper Amberdale," she said, as if the information cost her something. Then, after a beat, with the ghost of dry humor surfacing through the wariness: "Currently of nowhere in particular either, it seems."

They regarded each other across five paces of rain and ruined certainty, knight and carver, each holding a piece of a question the other needed answered, neither willing yet to say aloud that they might be walking the same road.

Then she pulled her hood up and went on down the track, and this time she did look back, once, briefly — not to check whether he followed, but as if confirming to herself that he existed, that the strange confession in the rain had been real and not a fever of the mountains. He raised one hand, not a wave, just an acknowledgment: *still here, still behind, still unarmed.*

She disappeared around the bend below the falls.

---

He did not follow immediately. That was the discipline of it, and also the cowardice, and he no longer pretended to himself that he could reliably tell the difference.

Instead he stood in the rain and made himself say the rest of it, silently, to the water: *I am going to follow her. I am going to betray my order to do it, or discover that I already have. And I am not doing it for the map.*

That was the part that frightened him. Eleven years Aldric Vane had served the Restoration, and for eleven years the map to the Hollow Choir had been the order's holy grail — the sealed vault beneath the Thornfells where, so the oldest loyalists whispered, the last true king had hidden something that could unmake Maren's peace with a single word. A testament. A legitimacy. A song. Every faction dreamed its own version of what slept down there: Cassel's hardliners wanted a coronation, the moderates wanted leverage, the desperate wanted a miracle. Aldric had wanted, mostly, for it all to mean something — for the blood he'd spilled in the order's name to point at a door instead of a ditch.

And now the map was in the hands of a bone-carver with a scar on her jaw who talked to the air and owed a debt to the dead, and when Aldric imagined taking it from her — the mission entire, the grail delivered, his honor restored by a single act of theft — he felt nothing. No hunger. No triumph. Just the grey rain and the sound of the falls, and beneath them, faint and persistent, a question that would not shut up:

*What is actually down there? What is it, that everyone wants it and no one will say plainly what it is?*

Ser Osric would call it weakness. Osric believed questions were doors left unlocked. But Osric had ordered the burning at Lowmarsh, and had signed the requisition for the pitch with the same steady hand that signed his devotions, and Aldric had learned in that firelight that a man's certainty and a man's conscience can live in the same chest for decades without ever once meeting.

Honor at war with itself. His old tutor at Caer Dunlan had used the phrase about a rival house, dismissively, but Aldric had carried it home like a splinter, because he'd recognized it. Two honors, both genuine: keep faith with the bloodline, and keep faith with the innocent. For most of his life they had pointed the same direction, or nearly enough. Now they pointed at each other across a mountain road, and somewhere between them walked a woman with a dead man's map, and Aldric had chosen — for today, at least, until the choosing got harder — to walk behind her and watch which way she turned.

*Not to take it,* he told himself again, mounting up, turning the horse south along the ridgeline where the pines broke the wind. *To understand it.*

The distinction felt thinner with every mile. He knew that. He rode anyway.

By dusk the rain had thickened into true mountain weather, the kind that erased trails and drowned voices, and from a shelf of rock above the valley he saw, far below and ahead, a small dark figure picking her way along the lake shore toward the sunken ruins at Hollowmere — toward the drowned chapel whose bell tower leaned out of the water like a broken finger. She moved surely even in the failing light, finding footing by instinct or by something stranger.

And behind her, further back than she could possibly have seen, a second set of riders had entered the valley from the north. Six horses, moving without torches, without banners. Moving the way Corvane's men moved — patiently, quietly, like weather.

Aldric sat motionless in the dark and watched the distance between them close, and understood that whatever he intended to be to this woman — shadow, guardian, judge — the choice was about to be taken out of his hands.

He turned his horse down toward the water.
