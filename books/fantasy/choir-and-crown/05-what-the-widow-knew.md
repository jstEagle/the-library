## 5. What the Widow Knew

Vesper came back to Greyhollow the way she had learned to come back to all the places she'd sworn never to see again: at dusk, hooded, with her hands hidden and her heart hammering like a trapped bird.

The smuggler track let her down through the alder brakes behind the tannery, where the smell of oak-bark and rot covered the scent of any traveler fool enough to use it. From there it was a hundred paces of hedgerow, a stile with a broken third rail she'd climbed since childhood, and then the back lane behind the candle-maker's, where the light through shutters fell in warm gold stripes across the mud. She counted the stripes as she passed them. It was a habit from girlhood, counting things when fear made the world too large. Seven stripes. Eight. The ninth fell across the door of Maren Ashwick's widow — no relation to the Queen, though the poor woman had suffered a decade of jokes about it — and Vesper stopped beneath it before she could talk herself into turning around.

The house was small and crooked, leaning east like an old man listening for footsteps that would never come again. Smoke curled thin from the chimney. A pot of something with leeks hung its smell out the window. Ordinary things. Living things. Vesper pressed her knuckles to the door before courage failed her.

Three slow knocks, the old signal between her mother's clients and their carver's daughter. *I come quietly. I mean no harm.*

Shuffling inside. Then Tam Ashwick's voice, cracked and wary: "Who knocks quiet in Greyhollow? Quiet folk want something."

"Tam." Her own voice sounded strange to her, roughened by three days of mountain wind. "It's the bone-carver."

A long silence. Then the bar lifted, and the door opened onto lamplight and the round, anxious face of a woman who had once paid Vesper two silver marks and a jar of honey for a whistle of hazel-wood and riverbone.

"You shouldn't be here," Tam whispered, dragging her inside by the wrist with a strength that grief had not yet eaten. "Half the town knows your face, and the other half is being paid to learn it."

* * *

The interior was exactly as memory kept it: low beams black with smoke, herbs hanging in bundles from the rafters, a loom shoved against the wall with half a blanket still on it, abandoned mid-weave the way everything in this house had been abandoned mid-something since Tam's husband died. A fire tickled in the hearth. On the mantel, in the place of honor where other widows kept portraits or saints' medals, sat the memorial whistle.

Vesper looked at it too long.

She had carved it eleven days ago from a length of elderwood and a sliver of riverbone she'd cured herself, and when Tam had first put her lips to it, the dead man's last words had come pouring through the bone the way they always did — not a haunting, nothing so theatrical, just a voice arriving whole in the listener's skull, intimate as a thought that wasn't yours. *Tell Tam the north field first,* Wynn Ashwick had said, dying somewhere on the Thornfell road with his blood freezing in the ruts. *The barley won't wait for mourning.* And then, softer, the way the dying speak when they've stopped performing: *Don't let them make me a story. I only wanted to come home.*

Every voice stayed with her. That was the cost no one warned you about when they marveled at your gift. Wynn Ashwick lived now in the back room of Vesper's mind, alongside four hundred others, each one a lamp left burning that she could not put out.

"Sit," Tam said, pressing her into a chair. "Eat. You look like something the crows argued over and gave up on."

"I can't stay long." But Vesper took the bowl anyway, because her body betrayed her with hunger, and because refusing kindness was a skill she'd never mastered even when it was dangerous. Leek broth. Barley bread. Salt. She ate like a person remembering she had one.

"They came," Tam said quietly, when the bowl was half empty. "Two days after you left. Riders. Six of them, in grey and black, with a badge I didn't know — a circle with a line drawn through it, like a struck bell."

Vesper's spoon stopped. "The Queen's Inquisition."

"That's what the miller's boy called it, who's got cousins in the capital and loves saying grand words. They went to your workshop first. Found it empty." Tam's eyes flicked to the shuttered window, then back. "They asked questions gentle as a mother. Where does the carver go in winter. Who buys her work. Does she keep letters, papers, maps. The smith told them true, mostly — he's frightened of losing his charter — but he didn't know anything worth telling. Nobody knows where you go, Vesper. That's the one mercy of how you live."

"And then?"

"Then the tall one — the one in charge, the one they called Inquisitor — he came here. Alone. Sent his riders to stand in the square like six crows on a fence." Tam wrapped both hands around her cup and did not drink. "He took off his hat in my doorway. Asked if he might come in. *Might* — like asking a duchess. He looked at Wynn's whistle on the mantel for a long time and said, 'That's fine work. Riverbone, is it? Cured in willow-ash.' He knew. Vesper, he *knew* what it was made of and how, better than some carvers would."

Vesper set down the spoon. Her hands were steady; she made them be steady. "What did he ask you?"

"What everyone asks a widow — was I comfortable, was I eating, did I have family nearby. And then, soft as snowfall: 'Your husband carried things for people, I think. Small parcels. Messages. Did he ever speak of his employers?'"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice at nothing and gave it up.

"And you told him?" Vesper asked.

"I told him the truth, which is that Wynn kept his pockets shut even from me. He'd come home with coin I couldn't account for and a look on his face like a man who'd seen a debt collector in a dream." Tam leaned forward, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "But I'll tell you what I didn't tell him, because I didn't understand it until you walked through my door with that same look on *your* face. Wynn wasn't just carrying messages. Not these last months. There was a lady."

"A lady."

"Came twice. Never used the road — came up the sheep track behind the fell like she knew every stone. Fine boots under a plain cloak. First time, she brought Wynn a parcel wrapped in oilcloth and told him, in front of me, that if anything happened to him I should find the bone-carver in Greyhollow. Not send for her. *Find* her. She said it like she knew the carver would run." Tam's gaze was steady and terrible. "Second time she brought nothing. She and Wynn talked low by the fire for half the night, and when she left he sat up till dawn holding his head in his hands, and in the morning he kissed me like a man kissing his own grave goodbye, and three days later he was dead on the mountain road with his belly opened."

Vesper's mouth had gone dry. "Did you see her face?"

"No. Hooded. But—" Tam rose suddenly, crossed to the mantel, and took down the whistle. She held it out in both palms, an offering. "When she was gone that second night, Wynn gave me this. Said the carver had made it special — no, listen, don't look like that, I know you made it for *me*, after. But he said it was already made. Already carved, weeks before he died. He said, 'If I don't come home, give this to Tam and tell her to take it to the carver, because there's more in it than a song.'" Tam's chin trembled, and she mastered it the way old soldiers master shaking hands. "I put it to my lips that night, Vesper. I heard him say the field, the barley, come home. All the things you heard, or near enough. But I'm not the carver. I only got the top of the well. You'd get what's underneath."

* * *

Vesper held the whistle and did not put it to her lips.

Not because she feared the dead — she trusted the dead more than the living, had built her whole solitary life on that inverted faith. She feared instead the arithmetic of it. Every voice she raised was a voice she kept. Four hundred lamps already burning in the dark rooms of her skull. And beneath Wynn Ashwick's simple dying words, according to a woman who barely understood what she was handing over, lay something meant specifically for her — laid down in bone like a letter sealed in wax, waiting for the one reader alive who could break it.

She thought of the map sleeping inside a half-carved femur in her pack, wrapped in oilcloth, showing tunnels through the Thornfells that no smuggler had ever found because no smuggler had ever been meant to find them. She thought of a courier who had known, weeks in advance, that he would die carrying it. Who had prepared. Who had chosen *her*.

"Tam," she said slowly. "This lady. Did she leave a name?"

"She called herself the Keeper's friend." Tam watched her closely. "And when Wynn laughed at that — bitter, like — she said, 'The Keeper is dead these twenty years, but her work keeps, and so must we.'"

The hearth-fire seemed to lean away from Vesper. Twenty years. Her mother had walked into the Thornfells twenty years ago this autumn, chisel and mallet in her pack, and never walked out, and Vesper had spent two decades telling herself the mountain simply took her, the way mountains do, indifferent as weather. Now a stranger's ghost of a phrase came walking out of a widow's parlor wearing her mother's title like a borrowed coat.

*Her work keeps.*

"The Inquisitor," Vesper said, to stop herself thinking aloud. "Is he still in Greyhollow?"

"Gone up-country yesterday, they said. Toward the fells." Tam paused. "But that was yesterday's gossip, and gossip ages fast. There's a horse at the smithy tonight nobody recognizes — big grey, capital-shod, standing saddled in the yard like it's waiting for someone to finish a conversation."

Vesper stood. Her body decided before her mind caught up, the old animal wisdom that had kept her alive through winters that killed hardier folk. "I need to go out the back."

"You need to eat more first—"

"Tam." She gripped the widow's shoulders. Through the wool and flesh and bone beneath, she felt the ordinary human pulse of her, quick and scared and brave. "You've done more than you know, and I can't explain, and I'm sorry for both. Lock your door. If anyone asks, you fed a tinker's daughter passing through, and she went north toward Millbrook. Say it plainly — you're a terrible liar and plainness will protect you better than cleverness."

Tam's eyes filled, and she laughed wetly despite herself. "Even your kindness sounds like a lesson. Your mother was the same." She thrust a cloth bundle into Vesper's arms — bread, cheese, the honey jar from a year ago, hoarded. "For the road. Go on, then. Go."

At the back door Vesper paused. "One thing. When you listened to the whistle — after his last words — did the silence feel like silence? Or did it feel like something waiting?"

Tam considered honestly, brow furrowed. "Like a held breath," she said at last. "Like a room where someone's stepped out and means to come back."

* * *

The back lane was empty. The hedgerow swallowed her. She was forty paces toward the tannery brakes, moving at a walk that wanted to be a run, when the voice came from the shadow of the candle-maker's wall — mild, unhurried, cultured, and utterly without malice, which was somehow worse than any threat could have been.

"You count the light through shutters when you're nervous. Nine windows on this lane. Your mother did the same."

Vesper turned.

He stood where the lamplight ended, and the darkness seemed arranged around him rather than merely present — a tall man in unremarkable travelling grey, hat in hand, a thin white scar bisecting one eyebrow like a stitch closing a wound. No sword visible, which meant nothing. His face was kind in the way of a physician's, attentive in the way of a confessor's, and patient in the way of a man who had already read the ending of the book and was enjoying watching you reach it.

"Inquisitor Corvane," Vesper said. Her voice came out flat and cold and she was grateful for it.

"Mistress Amberdale." He inclined his head, courteous as a dance partner. "Or should I say — forgive me, names are such fragile things — the daughter of Elara Amberdale, Keeper of the Hollow Choir, who went into the Thornfells twenty autumns past and was mourned by no record I have ever been permitted to read. Which has always struck me as the most interesting kind of death. The kind someone paid to leave blank."

Every instinct screamed at her to run. She had survived this long by running, by knowing the drains and the deer-paths and the exact moment a conversation curdled. But her feet had rooted, because he had said *Keeper* the way Tam had said it, the way the map's margin said it in her mother's cramped hand, and some starved part of her that had waited twenty years for anyone to speak that word aloud refused to move.

"My mother carved spoons," Vesper said.

"Yes," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "And couriers carry barley reports, and queens are chosen by God, and the wards over the capital have hummed without pause for a hundred years because the founders were very thorough engineers. We all believe what lets us sleep." He settled his hat back on his head, unhurried, a man closing a pleasant evening. "I am not going to arrest you, Mistress Amberdale. Arrest makes noise, and noise makes martyrs, and martyrs make royalists, and royalists make corpses faster than I can catalogue them. I only want to ask you one question, and then you may vanish into your mountains and I will report to the Queen that the trail went cold."

The honey jar dug into her ribs through the cloth bundle. Somewhere behind her, far off toward the square, a horse shifted and stamped.

"What question?" she heard herself ask.

Corvane smiled, and it reached his eyes, and that was the most frightening thing she had seen in all her twenty-six careful years — because the smile was real. Whatever he wanted, whatever burned at the center of him, it was not ambition. It was conviction, banked and glowing like a forge left overnight.

"When you find what your mother kept," he said softly, "and you will, because you are her daughter and the finding is in your blood — when you stand in front of it and hear it, as I know you will hear it — ask yourself only this: whether the dead would thank you for letting them out. Ask yourself what a voice does, freed, with nowhere left to go. I have seen the answer, Mistress Amberdale. I carry it with me everywhere. It is why I am so very kind." He stepped backward into the shadow between the window-strips of light, dissolving as neatly as ink in water. "Travel safely. Count the lights."

Then the lane was empty, and the ninth stripe of gold lay across the mud where he had stood, and Vesper Amberdale ran for the alder brakes with the widow's bundle against her chest and the widow's whistle in her pocket, and behind her in the dark of Greyhollow a saddled grey horse turned its head and watched her go without ever being told to.
