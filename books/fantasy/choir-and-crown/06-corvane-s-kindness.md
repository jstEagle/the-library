## 6. Corvane's Kindness

Inquisitor Corvane arrived in Greyhollow at dusk, alone and on foot, which was the first kindness. The second was that he stopped at the well in the market square, set down his travel-worn satchel, and waited to be noticed rather than announcing himself.

The town noticed him anyway. They always did. There was something about a man who wore no weapon openly but carried one the way other men carried their names — close, familiar, unremarkable — that made even the dullest villager straighten when he passed. He was not tall. He was broad through the shoulders in the manner of someone who had once done heavy labor, with grey shot through his dark hair like frost finding the edges of a field first, and a face that would have been called kind if you met him anywhere else: soft around the eyes, lined at the mouth as though from smiling, or from holding back smiles.

He wore the Queen's grey, but without the black sash of the Inquisition's full authority. That too was deliberate. The sash made people lie badly — frightened, florid, over-precise. He preferred them honest.

"Evening," he said to the woman drawing water, and asked her where a man might find a bed and a bowl of stew, and whether the Widow Sarel still took lodgers above her dye-house.

The woman answered before she thought about why he was asking. That was how it always began.

* * *

He had been three days out from the capital, riding hard and then walking the last mile, because the report had troubled him and troubled reports deserved slow approaches. A courier dead at a bone-carver's door in Greyhollow. Royalist riders sweeping the valley behind him. And a name in the courier's ledger — recovered from the wreck of his saddlebag, half-burned by whatever had pursued him — that Corvane had read four times before it settled into meaning.

*V. Amberdale.*

The daughter of Maren Amberdale. Or rather — the daughter of the woman who had once been Maren Amberdale, before the mountains took her name and gave back only rumors. Keeper of the Hollow Choir, if the oldest palace records were to be believed, though "keeper" was a word the records used the way a physician uses *palliative*: technically accurate, deliberately vague.

Corvane had spent twenty years learning what that vagueness concealed. He had been young then, younger than he liked to remember, an archivist in the ward-towers with good handwriting and no imagination, cataloguing the maintenance schedules of stones older than the kingdom itself. He had believed, in the way of young men who have never seen a wound, that wards were wards: geometry and intention, stone and formula. It had not occurred to him to ask what fed them.

Then they had sent him to Harrowmere.

He did not think about Harrowmere often anymore. He had trained himself out of it, the way one trains oneself out of touching a burn. But on the road to Greyhollow, watching the Thornfells build themselves against the western sky — blue-black, snow-collared, patient — the memory had come up in him like water into a cellar, and he had let it, because he needed it. He needed to remember exactly what was at stake, so that his hands would stay steady in the days ahead.

A valley. Four hundred souls. He could still recite the names. Not because he had memorized them — because he had heard them, all of them, screaming, for six days, after the old shrine's anchor-stone cracked and the memories it held came loose like bees from a smoked-out hive. Unanchored memory did not dissipate. It *sought*. It poured into the nearest open vessel, and human grief is the most open vessel there is, and for six days the people of Harrowmere had lived the deaths of soldiers dead eight hundred years — had drowned in rivers they'd never seen, had burned in halls that were ash before their grandmothers' grandmothers were born. Some had survived. The mind can hold almost anything, briefly. But forty-one had died of hearts simply stopping, and more than a hundred had never fully come back. They sat in the charity houses now, murmuring battle-orders to children who had never existed, asking after wives whose bones were dust in another century.

And that had been *one* cracked stone. One small shrine, holding perhaps two dozen memories.

The Hollow Choir, by every account Corvane had assembled across two decades of quiet inquiry, held thousands.

So yes. He walked the last mile. He left the sash off. He asked after beds and stew. Mercy, he had learned, was mostly a matter of approach.

* * *

Widow Sarel's dye-house smelled of mordant and wet wool, and she received him in a low room where skeins of yarn hung drying like flayed rainbows. She was perhaps sixty, quick-eyed, with the particular stillness of someone who had already decided to be afraid and was managing it.

"You'll be the Queen's man," she said.

"I'm a man looking for a bed," Corvane said mildly, "who happens to serve the Queen. The distinction matters to me."

"It won't matter to me by morning."

"It might," he said. "May I sit?"

She gestured. He sat. He complimented the madder-red of the yarn nearest him, and meant it, and told her about a dye-house he'd known in the capital run by a woman who could pull seven shades from a single root, and within a quarter hour the widow was talking — not about anything yet, just talking, the natural loosening of a person who finds herself speaking with someone who listens as though listening were a form of prayer. This was the part of his work Corvane was genuinely good at, and the part that some of his colleagues despised him for. Inquisitor Hale would have nailed a writ to the door and begun seizing papers by nightfall. Hale got resistance. Corvane got truth.

"The courier," he said eventually, gently, the way one turns over a sleeping child. "Tam Rennick. He stayed here, I think. Perhaps more than once."

The widow's hands went still over her wool.

"He was a good boy," she said. "Not a boy — a man, but young to my eyes. Carried letters for folk in the city who didn't want their letters seen." Her chin lifted slightly. "Nothing shameful in that. Half the great houses send messages that way. Love letters, mostly. Money sometimes."

"Sometimes," Corvane agreed. "Did he ever speak of his work? Of where he traveled?"

"He spoke of the mountains." She frowned, remembering. "Never the passes themselves, mind. He said—" and here her voice dropped, taking on the cadence of reported speech, of words repeated so often they'd worn smooth, "—he said the mountains sang to him. Said it jokingly, first few visits. Then less jokingly. Last time, he asked me — he asked me strange. He asked whether I'd ever heard singing that seemed to come from under the ground. From under my own floor." Her eyes came up sharply. "I told him he was tired. I should have asked more questions. Now he's dead in the road and everyone tells me riders came asking after him with drawn bows, and now *you're* here, and I find I don't know whom I ought to be frightened of."

"You needn't be frightened of me," Corvane said, and made it true as best he could, leaning forward, letting his voice go low and unhurried. "I'm not hunting Tam Rennick's killers. The Queen has people for that, and they are very good. I'm hunting a thing he carried. A map, likely. Bone-cased, small enough to fit in a palm. If it passed through your house, I need to know. Not for punishment — the Queen holds no grudge against messengers or those who shelter them. But because of what the map leads to, widow. And what it leads to is suffering. Old suffering, ongoing, that the Crown has a duty to end."

She studied him for a long moment. Outside, the evening bell rang from the chapel of Saint Vered, and somewhere a dog began complaining at nothing.

"There was a girl," the widow said at last. "The carver. Vesper Amberdale. Tam visited her shop the morning he died — I saw him cross the square toward it, and I saw the royalist riders come through not an hour behind. She hid him, I think. Or tried to." Her jaw worked. "I'll not swear to what I haven't seen. But I'll tell you what I do know, since you ask kindly, and since I'd rather it be you than men with bows. The girl carved me a keepsake after my husband died. A whistle, of all things. And I'll tell you something else, Inquisitor, something I've told no living soul, because they'd call me mad." She lowered her voice. "That whistle whispers. At night. My husband's voice, or something wearing it. Saying things he said near the end. And I love it, and it frightens me, and I don't know which of those is worse."

Corvane kept his face still, but inside him something turned over, cold and certain, like a key finding its lock.

*A bone-carver who hears the dead.* The reports had said it obliquely — *local accounts of unusual sensitivity; possible auditory hallucination; recommend assessment* — written by a subordinate who hadn't understood what he was reading. But Corvane understood. Bone remembered. Every practitioner of the old craft had known it, and the Choir's keepers above all, and if the bloodline ran true in this Vesper Amberdale, then the daughter of the last keeper could hear what the vault held.

She could hear ten thousand voices. And she was walking toward them with a map.

"Widow," he said softly, "you've been enormously helpful, and I want you to know that no harm will come to you or anyone in this town through anything you've told me. But I must ask one more thing, and then I'll trouble you no further tonight. The girl. Vesper. Did she say where she was going? When she left?"

"She hasn't left." The widow blinked. "Or — she did, days ago, up-mountain, but folk say she came back. Quietly. Yesterday, maybe the day before. Asking after Tam's visits, of all things. And then gone again this morning, before light, with a pack and her carving tools." A pause, and then, with the reluctance of a woman handing over the last coin she owns: "Folk say she took the smuggler roads. The high ones. Toward the Thornfells proper."

* * *

He sat long that night in the room the widow rented him, a single candle burning, and did not sleep, and was glad of it, because sleep lately brought Harrowmere back in its worst aspect — not the screaming, which at least had been honest, but the aftermath. The charity house. An old farmer sitting by a window with his hands flat on his knees, saying, in a voice gone gentle and wrong: *"I have to get back to the line, they need me at the line,"* while his daughter wept and Corvane stood in the doorway with his warrant and his certainty and understood, for the first time, that he was going to spend his life doing terrible arithmetic.

Because here was the arithmetic. The wards of the capital — the great lattice that had held the peace for a hundred years, that kept plague from the gates and fire from the granaries and, yes, kept certain restless things beneath the palace foundations from waking — were fed by the Choir. Everyone in the deep archives knew it and no one said it aloud. The song of the dead, sustained and sealed, poured its strength upward through the ward-stones, and the realm slept safely on top of it, the way a rich man sleeps on top of his ledgers without reading them.

Destroy the song, and the wards guttered. He had modeled it himself, years ago, late nights and borrowed figures: not instant collapse — the lattice had inertia, weeks of dimming, perhaps months — but collapse all the same, unless the Crown found some new foundation, and no such foundation existed in any text he had ever read.

And yet.

He turned his cup slowly on the table. He was not a fool, and he was not a monster, and he knew precisely what he was proposing, and he had made his peace with it the way one makes peace with a scar: not gladly, but finally. Because the alternative — leaving ten thousand minds imprisoned in the dark, conscious, singing, *forever*, so that the living could keep their comfortable peace — that alternative had a name too, and the name was atrocity. Slow, sanitized, institutionalized atrocity. The kind that wins monuments instead of trials.

His colleagues in the Inquisition spoke of the Choir as an asset. The royalists spoke of it as a throne waiting to be claimed. Even the Queen, he suspected, though she did not know its nature, thought of it as a wall holding up her house. No one — no one but Corvane, in twenty years — had thought of it as a prison full of prisoners, and asked the only question that mattered: *what do we owe them, and how quickly can we pay it?*

Destruction was mercy. He believed that down to the marrow. Loose the memories suddenly, as the shrine's breaking had loosed them at Harrowmere, and you killed valleys. But destroy the anchor entirely — burn the vault, unmake the bones, scatter the pattern past any hope of reassembly — and the memories had nowhere to pour. They would simply end. Gently, comparatively. A held breath released. Whatever consciousness endured in that place would stop enduring, and the dead would be done at last, and the living would grieve their wards and rebuild, because the living always rebuilt.

It would cost the realm its shield. It might cost the realm its peace.

It would cost ten thousand souls their chains.

He blew out the candle and watched the moonlight move on the wall, and permitted himself, as he rarely did, the small honesty of doubt. *Almost right,* whispered something in him that sounded uncomfortably like the archivist he had once been. *You are almost right, and the difference between almost right and right will be measured in graves.*

"Then let them be fewer than the alternative," he said aloud, to the empty room, and slept two hours, and woke clear.

* * *

In the morning he did the rest of it, and did it kindly, and it was worse for the kindness.

He went shop to shop. He bought bread and paid over the odds. He admired a cooper's barrels and a smith's new hinge-work and asked after grandchildren. And everywhere, folded into the warmth, the questions came — soft, specific, unstoppable. When had the carver left. Which road. Was she alone. Had anyone seen the knight — ah, yes, they'd seen the knight, a big man on a roan, shadowing her, some said guarding her, some said hunting her, opinions differed, faces flushed with the pleasure of mattering.

By noon he had the whole shape of it laid out in his mind like a map of his own: Vesper Amberdale, bone-carver, hearer of the dead, carrying her mother's map up the smuggler roads toward a vault she did not yet understand. Aldric Vael, disgraced, honor-haunted, following her for reasons he probably hadn't admitted to himself. Somewhere behind them both, Lord Cassel's hardliners, who wanted a crown cut from the dead. And ahead of them all, the Choir, singing its endless grief into the roots of the kingdom.

Three kinds of people racing toward ten thousand prisoners. A girl who would want to free them. A knight who wanted to crown them. Hardliners who wanted to own them. And none of them — not one — understood what happened when that much unanchored memory touched open air.

Only Corvane understood. Only Corvane had stood in a valley afterward and counted.

He settled his bill with the widow Sarel, overpaying as he'd overpaid everyone, and she pressed a wrapped loaf into his hands at the door and said, unexpectedly, "You're not what I expected, Queen's man. God help me, I liked you. Does that make me a traitor to the girl?"

"No," Corvane said. "It makes you honest. Keep being so, and Greyhollow will see spring."

He meant it as comfort. Watching her face, he realized it had landed as prophecy, and not a kind one, and he did not correct it because he did not know how.

At the edge of the village, where the high road began its climb into the foothills, his escort waited — six of the Queen's quiet riders, unmarked, patient, exactly enough force to be respectable and not enough to look like an army. He mounted, and looked back once at Greyhollow, red-roofed and smoke-threaded in the morning light, ordinary and precious and utterly unaware of how its peace had been purchased and by whom.

"Hold the road behind us," he told the captain. "No one follows. No one warns her. If Lord Cassel's men come through, delay them however you must — politely." A thin smile. "Politeness is underrated."

"And if the girl reaches the vault first, my lord?"

Corvane looked north, to where the Thornfells stood shoulder to shoulder against the sky, white-capped, immovable, hiding in their depths a wound a hundred years old and still open.

"Then I climb faster," he said, and put his heels to the horse.
