## 8. Two Roads Down

*Vesper*

The mountain did not want them on it. That was the first thing Vesper understood about the Thornfells, and she had grown up in their shadow her whole life. The paths were less roads than arguments the rock had lost — switchbacks of scree, ledges worn smooth by smugglers' boots and nothing else, and everywhere the wind, which came off the high snowfields carrying a sound like a choir rehearsing very far away.

She tried not to think about that sound.

Aldric walked a half-step behind her, which she suspected was deliberate. A knight would want to lead; a knight following a bone-carver up a goat track wanted something else, or was pretending to. He carried his saddlebag over one shoulder and no sword at his hip — he'd left it at Sorrow Bridge, wrapped in his own cloak, buried under stones like a man burying a dog he'd had to put down.

"You're limping," he said.

"I'm favoring my left knee because the right ledge is rotten. There's a difference."

"There is," he agreed, and said nothing else for an hour.

She should have hated that about him — the patience, the willingness to let silence sit between them like a third traveler. Most people filled silence the way they filled a room, too loudly, afraid of what grew in the empty spaces. Aldric seemed to have made peace with empty spaces. Perhaps that was what disgrace did. Perhaps it cleared out everything except what you actually were.

They made camp at dusk in the mouth of an old mine adit, its timber supports black with age and smelling of cold iron. Vesper built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand and set their supper — dried mutton, hard cheese, a twist of salt — on a flat stone between them.

"Tell me," she said, before she could decide not to, "what order you refused."

Aldric looked into the fire for so long she thought he hadn't heard. Then: "You know how it ends. You've seen me walking beside you without a banner. Do you need the middle?"

"I carve bones for a living. I spend my days making the inside of things visible." She turned the cheese knife over in her fingers. "Endings are cheap. It's middles people pay for."

Something moved through his face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might have been, in a man who had unlearned them. "Very well."

He fed the fire a stick and began.

* * *

*Aldric*

"I was nineteen when I first stood guard outside the old king's council chamber," Aldric said. "Back when there still was an old king, and a chamber worth guarding. My father bought the commission, as fathers did. I thought I had been chosen for my swordwork." A dry note entered his voice. "I was chosen for my father's purse. It took me another ten years to understand that most honors are purchased and none of them are clean."

Vesper said nothing, but she had stopped eating to listen, and he found he minded that less than he expected. Being listened to by her was different from being listened to by anyone at court. She listened the way she must listen to bone — waiting for the voice inside it.

"The old king died badly. Fever, they said. The court said many things. Queen Maren took the throne within the month, and the realm — here is the part that shames me — the realm did *well*. Granaries full. Borders quiet. Justice administered by someone who read the petitions herself. I told myself loyalty was to the crown, not the head wearing it, and for six years I believed it, because believing it was comfortable and I was good at comfortable."

"And then?" Vesper asked quietly.

"Then came the order." He watched the fire eat down through the stick he'd given it. "There was a village in the Marches. Ferrowdale. Do you know it?"

Her face went carefully blank. "I know the name."

"A petition had come up from Ferrowdale — taxes, grievances, the usual. But one line of it mentioned the old king's bastard, a grandson living hidden among them. Maren's council feared a claimant more than they feared a famine. The order came down sealed: burn the village's stores in winter, scatter the folk, bring back proof the boy was dead or gone. Quietly. No banners." His jaw tightened. "*No witnesses.*"

"And you refused."

"I rode ahead of the troop and warned them. All of them. Even the boy — though the boy was eight years old and thought I was there to arrest his grandfather." Aldric laughed, short and bitter as a snapped wire. "They gave me a choice, afterward. Disavowal, public, kneeling, or the block. I knelt. They stripped my rank, my name from the rolls, my right to bear arms in any garrison in the realm. And they executed the boy anyway, two years later, when they finally found him. So my grand act of conscience bought exactly nothing except—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the firelit dark of the adit, at the whole ruined shape of his life, "—this."

Vesper was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "It didn't buy nothing."

"No?"

"You said the troop was going to burn stores in winter. In Ferrowdale." Her eyes were steady on him, and he saw she had done the arithmetic already, the way she probably did everything — precisely, and without flinching. "People lived. Whatever happened after, that winter, those people ate. You can't carve that on a monument, but it's true, and I deal in true things."

He looked away first. He found, uncomfortably, that his eyes were hot.

"What about you?" he asked, to move the weight off his own chest. "You knew the smuggler roads before we ever met. You read that map like a priest reads scripture. Who taught you the mountains?"

And Vesper Amberdale went still in a way he had learned to recognize — the stillness of a woman standing at the edge of something deep.

* * *

*Vesper*

"My mother," Vesper said.

The word felt strange in her mouth, the way a word does when it has been locked in a box too long and come out changed.

"Maera Amberdale. Bone-carver, like me. Better than me — though I was twelve when I decided that, and daughters always overestimate their mothers, or underestimate them, I've never worked out which." She pulled her knees up toward the fire. "She used to take me into the foothills every autumn. Not to hunt, not to gather. To *look*. She'd stand in front of cliff faces and cave mouths for hours, running her fingers along the stone, and she'd say things like 'this one remembers' and 'not this way, the rock is lying.' I thought it was a game. Carvers' talk. Stone has grain the same way bone does; you learn to feel for it."

"But it wasn't a game," Aldric said.

"When I was fourteen, she went into the mountains and didn't come back. No body. No note. The magistrates wrote it down as lost to weather — half of Greyhollow dies to weather eventually — and my father grieved for a year and then quietly, politely stopped, and I—" She heard her own voice flatten and let it. "I kept carving. Because carving is how I talk to the dead, and I couldn't talk to her, so I talked to everyone else. Every whistle, every memorial bead, every name-coin. Ten years of strangers' dead. And never once hers. Do you know why?"

"Why?"

"Because there was no body. No bone to carve. The dead speak through what they loved, what they wore against their skin. She had nothing. She just — ended, like a sentence cut off mid-word." Vesper stared into the fire until her eyes stung. "I told myself for ten years that she fell in a ravine. That the mountain took her the way mountains do. It was easier than the other thing I told myself, which was that she left on purpose."

"And now?"

"Now I'm following a map drawn by a dying man through tunnels that shouldn't exist, and last night—" She stopped. Last night, crossing the high col, she had unrolled the courier's map by firelight, and Aldric had held the corner, and she had seen it again: the fine marginal annotations in a hand she knew better than her own. The little hooked *h*. The way the mapmaker dotted her crossings instead of striking them.

"Last night what?" he pressed gently.

She reached into her pack and drew out the map, unrolling it across the flat stone between them, weighting the corners with pebbles. The ink showed the vault road descending in long spirals beneath the Thornfells, and along its margins ran annotations in a small, sure script.

"Read me the second annotation on the eastern spur," she said. "The one above the tunnel marked with three dots."

Aldric bent close, lips moving. "'Water sings here. Do not carve past the singing stone.'" He frowned. "Odd instructions for a smuggler's map."

"That's not a smuggler's handwriting." Vesper touched the margin, not quite letting her finger land. "That's my mother's. I'd know it blind. She wrote her *h*s like little hooks, and she dotted crossings because she said a crossed-out path was a path someone died refusing, and the dead deserved the courtesy of a dot." Her voice cracked on the last words and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "This map was annotated by Maera Amberdale, who vanished twenty years ago into these mountains, on the exact road we're taking toward the Hollow Choir. Which means either she helped draw it—"

"—or she went where it goes," Aldric finished softly, "and never came back out."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved along the ridge, and again Vesper heard that far-off choral sound in it, and this time she did not look away from it.

"She was the best carver I ever knew," Vesper said. "If anyone could hear what the mountain was saying — if there was something under the Thornfells that *needed* hearing — she'd have gone. She wouldn't even have hesitated." She rolled the map closed with hands that were not entirely steady. "I spent ten years grieving a woman who abandoned me. Now I have to consider grieving a woman who was maybe doing exactly what I'm doing right now."

Aldric was silent. Then he said, in the careful voice of a man handling something fragile, "Then we may find her answer down there. Whatever it was."

"Yes."

"Are you afraid it won't be the answer you want?"

Vesper looked at him — this disgraced knight with no banner and no sword, who had followed her not to take the map but to understand it, who had confessed his worst night to a stranger by firelight because she'd asked for the middle of the story.

"I'm a bone-carver," she said. "I've never once gotten the answer I wanted. I get the true one." She lay back against her pack. "Sleep, Sir Aldric. Tomorrow we reach the singing stone."

* * *

*Aldric*

They descended all the next day, and the map proved itself again and again in ways that unsettled him more than any lie would have. Where it marked a hidden stair behind a waterfall, there was a hidden stair, its steps worn hollow by feet long absent. Where it marked a rope-anchor, iron rings still jutted from the rock, rusted orange but holding. Whoever had drawn this route had known the mountain the way a surgeon knows a body — intimately, and from the inside.

And twice now, Vesper had stopped at the tunnel mouths and pressed her palm flat against the stone, eyes half-closed, listening.

She thinks she's hiding it, Aldric thought, watching her do it a third time at the entrance of a cut passage the map promised would descend toward the vault road. She thinks the listening is private. But she'd done the same thing at the widow's cottage, at the ford, at every grave-marker they'd passed — a slight tilt of the head, as if catching a voice under loud water.

"The map says three dots means the passage forks below," he offered, when she straightened. "Fork-left descends to the vault road. Fork-right is marked—" he checked the annotation, "—'*closed. Do not open. —M.A.*'"

Vesper had gone pale.

"What is it?" he asked.

"She marked it closed," Vesper whispered. "Twenty years ago she marked it closed, and she dotted the crossing, because someone died refusing it." Her hand rose unconsciously to the leather case at her breast where the map lived. "Aldric — the fork-right passage. The one she sealed. It runs *toward Greyhollow*. Toward home." She swallowed. "My mother didn't just find the Choir. She came back from it once. And whatever she saw down there made her seal a door behind her — and then go back anyway."

Far below them, through a hundred fathoms of living rock, faint and unmistakable in the stillness of the deep passage, something began to sing.
