## 9. The Toll of Names

The gate did not look like a toll-gate. It looked like nothing at all — a shelf of grey stone jutting from the mountainside, half-swallowed by pine, with a cleft behind it no wider than a coffin stood on end. Vesper would have walked past it twice, three times, before believing it was anything but a trick of the light and her own tired eyes.

But the map knew.

She had stopped trusting her own judgment somewhere on the third day of Aldric's company, and had not decided yet whether that was wisdom or surrender. She unrolled the map now against the flat of her forearm, careful to keep it angled away from him, though he had already seen most of what it held and she had already stopped pretending otherwise. The courier's ink showed the cleft in fine brown strokes, and beside it, small as a flea, a mark she had learned to read as *toll*. Beneath the mark, in that same impossible hand, a single line:

*Pay what is asked. Nothing more. Nothing less.*

"It wants a name," Aldric said.

Vesper looked up. He was standing at the edge of the shelf, one hand resting on his sword hilt out of habit rather than intent — she had learned that distinction over the past days, learned to read the difference between the knight who reached for steel and the man who only remembered it was there. His grey eyes were narrowed at the cleft, at the darkness inside it where no darkness should have been so complete under noon sun.

"You can hear it too?" she asked.

"I can see the posts." He nodded toward two weathered stakes flanking the cleft, so old their carving had worn to suggestion. "Boundary stones. Old law, older than Maren, older than the last king. Mountain folk didn't trust coin. Coin could be stolen. A name given freely—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, as if he'd said something he hadn't meant to say aloud.

"Given freely," Vester echoed. "And taken how?"

"That depends on who's asking."

* * *

They made camp above the shelf rather than approach at dusk, because Vesper had spent enough years among smugglers' roads to know that gates like this were approached hungry or desperate or never. She built a small fire in a hollow of stone, screened so its glow wouldn't show, and sat sharpening her knives while Aldric pretended not to watch her do it.

He was better company than she had expected, which annoyed her. He asked questions the way a surgeon asks them — precisely, without flinching, and always about the thing you least wanted probed. Where had she learned the Thornfell roads. Why a bone-carver carried smuggler's maps in her head. What she intended to do when she reached whatever lay at the end of the courier's ink.

She had answers for all of them. None of them were true, or none of them were whole, and the gap between the two had been widening since the drowned chapel, since she'd carved the map's casing and heard the courier's ghost describe a prison of singing bones. Since Aldric had caught up to her there and offered alliance instead of arrest, and she — fool, fool — had said yes.

Now they traveled together, and every mile the map revealed something new: tunnels that predated the kingdom, roads that bent where roads shouldn't, and margins filling, as they went, with annotations in a second hand — smaller, sharper, feminine. Vesper had noticed them first on the second night, studying the parchment by firelight while Aldric slept. Someone had corrected the courier's map. Someone who knew the mountain better than any courier alive.

Someone whose handwriting she recognized.

She hadn't told Aldric that. She wasn't sure why, except that saying it aloud would make it real, and real things demanded decisions, and she was tired of decisions. Her mother had left Greyhollow twenty years ago without explanation and without goodbye, walking into these mountains with a satchel of chisels and never coming back, and Vesper had built an entire life on top of that absence the way a tree builds around a fence post. She did not want the fence post examined. She did not want it turned into a door.

The fire cracked. Aldric slept badly, as he always did, one hand curled near his sword even in rest.

Vesper took out the map again.

In the firelight the margin notes seemed darker than the rest, as if written with a wetter ink, or pressed harder. She traced one with her fingertip — a correction to the tunnel depths near the drowned chapel, precise to the foot — and felt the familiar prickle along her jaw, the one that meant bone nearby remembered something. But parchment was not bone, and the prickling passed, leaving only the words themselves, and beneath the deepest annotation, in letters so small she'd needed firelight and squinting both:

*If you are reading this, you are later than I hoped.*

She had told herself it referred to the courier. It had to refer to the courier. The courier was dead; the courier had carried this map through checkpoints and rain and finally into her workshop to die against her door, whispering about a Choir that still sang. Whoever wrote the note had expected him sooner.

Hadn't they?

Sleep did not come easily after that, and came finally as a grey, uneasy shallow thing full of corridors.

* * *

Morning came cold and clear, the kind of mountain morning where the light has edges. They broke camp without speaking much — a language of shared tasks they had developed without noticing, Aldric kicking dirt over the coals while she rolled the hides, him taking the heavier pack without discussion, her adjusting the strap across his chest because it chafed the shoulder he favored. Small domesticities. Dangerous ones. She caught herself cataloguing the way he moved, the set of his shoulders under the plain travel cloak that hid what he was, and made herself stop.

The gatekeeper was waiting for them inside the cleft.

That was the first wrongness. There had been no one there the evening before — Vesper had checked, with her eyes and then, quietly, with the smallest bone in her kit, a knuckle-bone she kept for listening, cupped in her palm like a secret. The knuckle-bone had heard nothing but wind and stone and the distant complaint of pines. Now a woman sat on a stool of living rock just inside the shadow of the cleft, hands folded in her lap, as if she had been sitting there a hundred years and would sit a hundred more.

She was old in the way of mountains — not frail, but weathered, reduced to essentials. Iron-grey hair braided tight to her skull. Eyes the pale grey-green of lichen. She wore layers of undyed wool and around her neck, on a cord, a collection of small objects Vesper's professional eye catalogued before her fear caught up: teeth, mostly. Some animal. Some not.

"Two travelers," the woman said. Her voice was dry and unhurried, like wind through a cave mouth. "One road."

"One toll," Vesper said.

Something flickered across the old woman's face — approval, maybe, or amusement. "You know the custom."

"Smuggler's arithmetic," Vesper said. "A name spoken aloud, given freely, held by the gate. No coin. No blood."

"No coin. No blood." The gatekeeper's gaze slid past her to Aldric, and stayed there, weighing. "Him I know. Or knew. The bannerless knight who walks like a knight. He'll pay like anyone."

Aldric's jaw tightened. "You've heard my name before?"

"I've heard *of* you." The old woman smiled without warmth. "Names travel. That's rather the point of them." She turned back to Vesper, and her lichen eyes went suddenly sharp, suddenly interested, the way a hawk's go sharp when something moves in grass. "You first, carver. Your friend can wait outside until his turn."

"He's with me."

"He'll still wait." Not unkind. Absolute. "Custom."

Vesper glanced at Aldric. He gave the smallest nod — *go*, the nod said, *I'll be here* — and stepped back out into the light with a soldier's easy obedience that sat strangely on him, and she understood suddenly that he was giving her privacy, or protecting her flank, or both, and hated how much she appreciated it.

She stepped into the cleft alone.

Inside, the air was cool and mineral-smelling, and the walls pressed close enough that she could have touched both with outstretched arms. Somewhere deeper, water dripped in slow metronome time. The gatekeeper watched her from her stool, hands folded, patient as geology.

"The toll," the woman said. "A name. Spoken aloud. Given freely. Choose carefully, carver — a name given here is *held*. Held names can be called. Called names must answer. Most travelers pay with a dead grandmother or a childhood enemy, and the gate takes it gladly, because the gate knows the difference between a name given and a name discarded." Her voice dropped, softened. "What will you give?"

Vesper thought of her stock of safe names. Dead grandmothers — she had two, conveniently deceased. Childhood enemies — Greyhollow had supplied plenty. She opened her mouth to spend one of them cheaply, and then the map shifted against her ribs where she carried it, and the margin note flashed up behind her eyes — *if you are reading this, you are later than I hoped* — and she heard herself say, instead:

"Maren."

It was a good choice, she thought dizzily. The Queen's name. Let the gate hold *that*. Let some dark mountain custom call the usurper to answer—

But the gatekeeper did not reach for it. Did not take it at all. The old woman's lichen eyes had gone very wide and very still, fixed not on Vesper's face but on the satchel at her side, on the corner of rolled parchment protruding from it, on the margin visible there in the dim light — the fine brown strokes of the courier's map, and beside the toll-mark, in that smaller, sharper, feminine hand:

*The toll remembers its keepers.*

The silence stretched so long that the water dripping somewhere in the deep found room to count three drops, four, five.

"Where," the gatekeeper said, and her dry voice had changed entirely, lost its cave-wind boredom and gained something underneath it, something that trembled like the ground before snow gives way, "*where did you get that map?*"

"A dying man gave it to me."

"Dying men don't carry that route. That route hasn't been walked in twenty years." The old woman rose from her stool, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed the narrow space between them, and her lichen eyes dropped to the margin again, and Vesper watched recognition move across her face like frost across a window. "I know that hand," she whispered. "Gods below and above, I know that hand. She paid me this toll herself, once. Twenty-two years ago. She gave me her name freely, and the gate has held it ever since, and I have wondered every winter since whether she'd come back to reclaim it." Her eyes lifted to Vesper's face, searched it with terrible thoroughness — the jaw, the brow, the set of the mouth. "You have her chin. You have exactly her stubborn chin."

The bottom fell out of Vesper's stomach.

"My mother's name," Vesper said, and her voice sounded far away, underwater, "was Sable Amberdale."

"No," the gatekeeper said gently. "It wasn't. Not when she came through here. She gave the gate the name she was born with, the one nobody else living knew, the one she'd kept hidden her whole life because names have weight and hers had more than most." The old woman's hand rose, trembling slightly, to the cord of teeth at her throat, and touched one tooth near the center — smaller than the others, human, worn smooth by decades of handling. "Sable Amberdale was a name she wore like a coat, carver. The name she gave me, freely, to hold — the true one, the buried one — was *Keeper*."

The word landed like a stone dropped down a well. Vesper heard it fall and fall without reaching bottom.

"She told me," the gatekeeper went on, softer now, almost tender, which was somehow worse, "that if anyone ever came through carrying that map — anyone with her chin — I was to tell them the truth. All of it. She made me swear it on the toll itself, and the toll does not release its sworn things lightly, as you now know." She drew a breath. "Your mother was not a smuggler, child. She was not a fugitive, whatever story she let you believe. She was the last Keeper of the Hollow Choir. Warden of the vault. Tender of the song. And she did not leave Greyhollow because she was tired of you, or frightened, or faithless. She left because her own teacher had died and the ward-stones of the capital were faltering, and someone had to go down and keep ten thousand dead voices singing, and she went knowing she would likely never come back up."

Ten thousand.

The number went through Vesper like a blade finding the gap in armor. In the drowned chapel, the courier's ghost had spoken of a prison of voices, and she had imagined dozens, scores, a chapel-full. Ten thousand was not a haunting. Ten thousand was a war with the graves still awake in it.

"She failed," Vesper heard herself say. "Or she—"

"She succeeded," the gatekeeper said, "for eighteen years. Alone. Keeping the song steady with her own two hands and her own two ears, because the gift runs in the blood, carver, and yours is the same blood." The old woman's lichen eyes held hers, merciless with pity. "Why do you think your carvings hear the dead? Why do you think the voices stay with you, all of them, crowding your skull? You were born to tend a choir. Every bone you've ever carved has been practice. Every voice you've ever carried has been rehearsal." She paused, and her voice gentled further, into something almost like grief. "She wrote to me, the last winter. One letter, by courier, the same courier whose map you carry. She said the song was growing heavy. She said she was growing old. She said that if the wards above began to fail, it would mean she had failed, and that her daughter — *her daughter with the stubborn chin and the listening hands* — should be told everything, and given the choice she herself had never been given. Because she was taken into it, you see. Chosen before birth, trained from childhood, never asked. She would not do that to you. She made us all swear it. The choice would be yours, freely given or not at all."

Freely given.

The toll wanted a name, given freely.

Vesper stood in the cold mineral dark of the cleft with her mother's ghost pressing against her from every direction — the margin notes, the map, the gatekeeper's sworn truth, twenty-two years of absence reassembling itself into meaning — and understood, with a clarity that felt less like revelation than like a wound finally being cleaned, that she had been walking toward this gate her entire life. Every memorial whistle for grieving widows. Every voice that stayed. Every lonely competence, every trust placed in the dead because the dead at least told her the shape of things.

Her mother hadn't abandoned her. Her mother had *trained* her, from a distance, from the grave's edge, and never once told her — because telling her would have been choosing for her, and Sable Amberdale had loved her too much for that, and too little, both at once, and Vesper could not decide which betrayal was worse.

"The toll," the gatekeeper said quietly. "You still owe it, carver. A name, given freely."

Vesper closed her eyes.

There was only one name left worth anything. She spoke it into the dark of the cleft, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her.

"Sable Amberdale."

The gate took it. Vesper felt it leave — a faint coolness, like a ring sliding off a finger, like the last of a breath leaving a room. Somewhere in the stone around her, something settled, satisfied, and held.

The gatekeeper bowed her head. "Paid," she said. "Pass, and pass freely. The road beyond runs straight to the Keeper's Door, three days' walk, and it will know you now — the mountain marks its own." She hesitated, then added, almost gently: "Your mother's letter. I was to give it to whoever paid the toll with her name. I have kept it twenty-two years." From within her layered wool she produced a packet, oilskin-wrapped, soft-cornered with age, and pressed it into Vesper's hands. "Read it before the Door, not after. She was very particular about that."

Outside, blinking in the hard-edged light, Vesper found Aldric waiting exactly where she'd left him, and something in her face must have shown, because he straightened off the rock he'd leaned against and said, simply: "What happened?"

Vesper looked down at the oilskin packet in her hands, at her mother's name already fading from her memory like a word spoken in a dream, at the mountains ahead where three days' walk away a door waited that had been waiting for her since before she was born.

"My mother was the Choir's keeper," she said. "And the letter says—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Aldric, the letter says the wards are failing *because she died*. And that when they fail, we won't have days. We'll have hours."

Behind them, deep in the cleft, the gate began to close — stone grinding against stone with the sound of a held name being swallowed — and the mountain, all around them, shuddered once, softly, like a sleeper turning over.
