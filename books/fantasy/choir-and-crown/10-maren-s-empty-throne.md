## 10. Maren's Empty Throne

The lamps of the council chamber guttered at the same hour every night, and Queen Maren had learned to read them the way a sailor reads weather.

Tonight they did something no lamp in Ashfall Keep had done in her lifetime — or in anyone's living memory. They dimmed, all together, as though the room had drawn one long breath and held it. The flame above the map table shrank to a blue bead. The great iron chandeliers, each holding forty candles that had never once wavered since her coronation, bent their light sideways as if bowing to something unseen.

Then, after three heartbeats, everything steadied.

No one else in the chamber seemed to have noticed. Lord Chancellor Vey was still droning about grain tariffs for the eastern provinces; Master of Coin Halloway was still pretending his arithmetic proved what he wanted it to prove. Only Maren had looked up from the ledger before her, only Maren had watched the light flinch.

She said nothing. That, too, she had learned: a queen who admitted to seeing ghosts would soon be governed by men who claimed to interpret them.

"—and so, with the harvest bonds restructured, Your Majesty will find the eastern granaries solvent by spring," Vey concluded, with the satisfaction of a man who believed he had just performed surgery.

"Solvent by spring, provided nothing burns down between now and then," Halloway murmured, and there were the usual dry chuckles around the table.

Maren let them have their moment. Her eyes went instead to the window behind Vey's shoulder, where the evening sky over Ashfall City showed the first stars — and beneath them, faint as breath on glass, the shimmer of the wards.

Every child in the capital knew the wards the way children knew the sun. They had been raised on the fact of them: the pale auroral veil that arched from tower to tower across the city each night, keeping out more than cold. Keeping out, the priests said, the old hungers of the world. Keeping in, the smugglers said, everything worth taxing. The wards had burned steady for a hundred years, through three kings and one regency and the civil war that had put Maren herself on the throne — the war everyone called the Sorrow, as if grief had been its architect rather than ambition.

The wards had never flickered.

Until tonight.

"Your Majesty?" Vey's voice, careful now. He had followed her gaze to the window and seen nothing but sky, but he knew that look on her face. Everyone in this room made a study of her face. It was the price of serving a usurper: you learned to read the sovereign like weather, because you could never be sure the season would hold.

"The lamps dipped," she said. "All of them. Did no one feel it?"

A pause. The particular silence of men deciding whether their queen was testing them or losing her mind.

"The draft from the west corridor can be temperamental—" Halloway began.

"It was not a draft." She rose. Around the table, chairs scraped back in obedient unison, eleven men standing because one woman stood, and Maren felt the familiar vertigo of it — that this whole edifice, this kingdom of ledgers and laws and loyal men, balanced on her say-so alone. On the *fact* of her, which was to say, on the fiction of her. "Dismissed. All of you. Chancellor, stay."

They filed out with the quick, relieved steps of men escaping a room where something had gone wrong. Vey remained, folding his hands. He was sixty, gray as ash, and had served her father-in-law before he'd ever sworn to her — which meant he remembered things she had not been told and would not volunteer unless asked exactly right.

"You felt it too," she said. "Don't lie to me. You're the only man in this keep who doesn't bother."

Vey's mouth twitched. "I felt the candles lean. I've felt it once before, Majesty. Once, thirty-one years ago, during the last winter of King Aldous's reign. For a single night, every flame in the palace bowed east." His hands tightened slightly on each other. "The old king sent for the Wardens. They came up from the undercrofts looking as though they'd seen the end of the world, and they told him the wards were *hungry*. That whatever fed them had grown thin. And the king asked what fed them, and the Wardens looked at one another, and then they lied to him. Beautifully. I was young enough to believe the lie for another decade."

"And what was the lie?"

"That the wards were sustained by the devotion of the realm. By prayer and loyalty." Vey's eyes were very level. "Devotion is free, Majesty. Whatever actually feeds those lights has a cost. Someone has been paying it for a hundred years, and tonight the payment was late."

* * *

The Wardens' undercroft lay beneath the oldest wing of the keep, down stairs worn into shallow curves by feet that had climbed them when the mountain itself was younger. Maren went down alone, as she always did, because a queen who feared her own cellars would soon fear her own shadow, and a queen who feared her own shadow was already dead.

The undercroft was not a cellar. It was a throat.

The stair opened into a vaulted space vast enough to swallow the throne room entire, and at its center rose the First Stone — a pillar of gray rock taller than six men, veined through with lines of soft gold light that pulsed in slow rhythm. Like breathing. Like a heartbeat slowed almost past recognition. The ward-lines ran outward from it along the ceiling in silver channels, fanning toward the city above, and where they passed overhead Maren could hear it: the sound.

She had heard it since childhood, on the rare occasions she was brought below. A singing. Distant, layered, endless — thousands upon thousands of voices braided so finely together that no single note could be pulled free of the whole. Not words. Not quite music. Something older than either, like wind through a forest of hollow bones.

As a girl she had found it beautiful. As a queen, descending now through air that tasted faintly of dust and tallow, she found it terrible — because she finally understood what beauty on that scale *cost*, and who might be paying.

Three Wardens waited at the stone's base. Their order was ancient and nearly extinct; they wore robes the color of wet slate, and their faces, in the gold-veined light, looked like faces carved by someone who had only heard about human beings secondhand. The eldest — Warden Ossel, whom she had met twice before, both times funerals — stepped forward and knelt with the economy of a man conserving strength for worse tasks.

"Majesty. You should not have come down."

"I rule here. There is nowhere in my keep I shouldn't come." She kept her voice even. "Tell me what happened tonight."

Ossel rose slowly. Up close his eyes were the color of river ice, and behind them moved something Maren recognized from her own mirror on bad nights: a person carrying knowledge too heavy to set down anywhere.

"For a century, the Stone has sung without pause. The song feeds the lines; the lines feed the wards; the wards keep the city — safe. Kept." He paused on the word as if tasting rot in it. "Three hours past sundown, the song faltered. Not ended. Faltered. As if a thousand voices stopped singing at once — and then resumed. We do not know why. We know only that the Stone drew deeper afterward. It is feeding harder on its source to make up the loss."

"And its source?"

"Majesty." Ossel's gaze did not waver, but something behind it did. "There are questions whose answers change the asker. I have served four monarchs, and I have watched two of them ask this question and become lesser women and men for hearing the reply. I am asking you — truly asking — whether you wish to be the third."

The singing rolled through the vault above them, vast and patient. Maren thought of the lamps bowing. Of the ledger still open on her desk upstairs, columns of numbers that represented bread in mouths and roofs over heads and eleven years without open war — eleven years of peace she had bought with compromise after compromise, each one a small theft from some principle she'd once held sacred. She had told herself that was the cost of ruling well. That good governance was built on small dishonesties, tolerable ones.

She had never imagined the peace itself might be a debt accruing somewhere beneath her feet.

"Tell me," she said.

Ossel exhaled, and it was strange to watch relief and sorrow cross the same face at the same time.

"The wards are not fed by devotion, Majesty. Nor by ley-fire, nor any force of earth or heaven. They are fed by the dead." His voice dropped, though there was no one left alive to overhear. "Beneath the Thornfells lies a vault the old kings built in the last years of the Sorrow War. The Hollow Choir, the founders named it. Within it are the memories of the war-dead — ten thousand souls taken up at the war's end, bound in bone and set to singing. Their remembering is the fuel. Their grief, harvested forever, is the light over your city."

The vault was silent except for the song, which suddenly sounded like nothing so much as screaming heard through a very thick wall.

"They are aware," Maren said. It was not a question. Some part of her had known it since the lamps bowed; some part of her had perhaps known since coronation day, when she'd first stood beneath the wards and felt watched by the whole shining arch of them.

"Some of them. The binding was imperfect — the founders were masons and soldiers, not saints. Enough awareness remains that the song does not go *flat*. Despair, Majesty, is what powers the lines. Not memory alone. Memory alone is inert. It must be suffering that remembers itself." Ossel folded his hands. "The founders called it mercy, in their records. The dead preserved rather than lost. The realm protected rather than broken. Both true, I suppose, the way most monstrous things are true."

"How long has this been known?"

"By the Wardens? Since the founding. We are the keepers of the account, not its authors. By the crown?" He hesitated. "King Aldous asked. I told you he was lied to. But the king before him — Edric the Second — he read the founders' records himself, alone, one night. In the morning he ordered the records sealed and never spoke of it again. He doubled the Warden stipend and tripled the wine requisitions for our undercroft, and he ruled nine more years, and historians call his reign gentle."

Maren turned away from him, toward the First Stone. Its golden veins pulsed against her face, warm light, honest light, light that had lit every oath she had ever sworn — including the one where she swore to protect the realm, hand on stone, before ten thousand cheering subjects who did not know what the stone ate.

She was a usurper. She had never pretended otherwise, not to herself. She had been a general's daughter with a decent claim and a better army, and when the old bloodline drowned in its own war she had taken the throne because someone competent had to, and because the alternative was another decade of burning. Eleven years she had governed honestly — fairly, even, by the standards of kingdoms — levying fair taxes, sparing the conquered, feeding the poor in famine years. She had built a peace real enough that people had begun, cautiously, terribly, to trust it.

And all of it — every market day, every wedding, every child asleep unafraid beneath the auroral veil — had been purchased with this. Ten thousand prisoners, singing.

*Even good rule rests on theft.* The thought arrived with the flat certainty of a verdict already rendered.

"The faltering," she said, because the alternative was to stand here drowning. "What caused it?"

"We don't know." Ossel's composure cracked, just barely. "That is what frightens us, Majesty. The Choir has sung uninterrupted for a hundred years. Nothing disturbs it. Unless—" He stopped.

"Unless someone has gone looking for it."

"Or someone has gone looking for the way in. The access roads were sealed at the founding. The maps were destroyed." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Mostly destroyed, Majesty. One copy was entrusted to the royal courier service, in case maintenance was ever needed. It traveled by relay, piece by piece, no courier ever knowing the whole route. The final custodian of the complete map was a rider named Brellan Ost. He failed to report at the Thornfell relay station eleven days ago."

The cold that went through Maren then had nothing to do with the undercroft.

"Find him."

"He cannot be found, Majesty. His body was recovered from the Greyhollow ford two days past. Drowned — or near enough. But the map he carried has not surfaced among his effects." Ossel's ice-colored eyes held hers. "Which means it is in someone's hands. And whoever holds it is walking toward the Choir — whether to serve it, or sell it, or break it open."

* * *

She summoned Corvane at midnight, in the small map room off her private chambers, where the walls were papered with surveys of the whole realm and the candlelight was ordinary, mortal, flickering at nothing.

Inquisitor Corvane arrived promptly, as he arrived at everything, dressed in unremarkable gray with the Queen's seal at his breast and no weapon visible anywhere on his person. He was fifty, spare, silvering at the temples, with the kind of face that innkeepers trusted and murderers confessed to. He had served her for seven years, and in seven years he had never once raised his voice, never once demanded, never once lied to her — which made him, in Maren's experience, the most frightening man in her employ. Kindness like his required absolute conviction, and conviction like his required a cause, and Corvane's cause was the protection of the realm by whatever means the realm's protection required.

He listened to everything — the lamps, the Wardens, the Choir, the dead courier — standing perfectly still, hands clasped, nodding occasionally as though she were reporting a leaky roof.

When she finished, he was quiet for a long moment.

"Ten thousand," he said at last. "I confess I had wondered. The wards' appetite never diminished, year over year, no matter how the realm prospered. Devotion should grow with prosperity. Grief doesn't." He looked up, and his eyes were gentle, and that gentleness was worse than any fanatic's fire. "You want the map found before the royalists reach the vault."

"The royalists will use it to crown a pretender. Cassel's faction has been sniffing at the mountains for a year. If they bind the song to some puppet king—"

"Yes. A living figurehead anointed by the dead. Unassailable legitimacy. Civil war within the month, and the Choir's suffering made a sacrament besides." Corvane inclined his head. "And you want me to prevent that."

"Yes. Find the map. Find whoever carries it. Recover it intact." She leaned over the map table, palms flat on the parchment of the Thornfells — those jagged white teeth at the realm's edge, drawn in confident ink by surveyors who had never once descended past the tree line. "Corvane. Hear me clearly. The vault is not to be opened. The Choir is not to be disturbed. Whoever holds the map is to be stopped, but they are *subjects of this realm*, not traitors yet, and I will not have a massacre in the passes. Am I understood?"

Something moved behind Corvane's mild expression — the smallest settling, like snow shifting on a slope before the slide.

"Understood, Majesty." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and did not turn fully around. "May I ask one question? Not in my capacity as your Inquisitor. As a man who has served you faithfully."

"You may."

"When you learned tonight what your peace is built upon — what did you feel? Truly."

Maren thought of lying to him, and understood immediately that it would not work, and wondered briefly why she tolerated a servant she could not lie to, and answered because the answer was already in her mouth.

"I felt that I had been robbing graves for eleven years and calling it governance."

Corvane nodded slowly, as though she'd confirmed something he'd long suspected. "Yes," he said softly. "That is what I hoped. Because there is a third path, Majesty, that neither the royalists nor the Wardens will ever offer you — and I will show it to you when the time comes. The royalists want to wear the chains. The Wardens want to oil them. But there is a way to simply *end* them. To give those ten thousand what no binding gives: rest. True rest. It can be done. I have seen it done."

"Where?"

His profile, half-turned in the candlelight, was unreadable. "In a valley called Merrow's Bend, many years ago. Ask me again someday, and I will tell you what remained afterward." He opened the door. "I'll need riders, authority over every garrison between here and the Thornfells, and a name — the name of whoever the courier trusted. Dead men confide in someone. I'll find who."

"Go," Maren said. "And Corvane — whatever you find in those mountains, bring me truth before you bring me solutions. I've had enough solutions built on things I wasn't told."

He smiled then, briefly, and it transformed his face into something almost warm.

"That, Majesty, is the difference between you and every king who kept this secret," he said. "And it is why I grieve — genuinely grieve — for what you're about to learn."

The door closed behind him.

Maren stood alone in the map room, in the ordinary flickering light, and looked down at the Thornfells — at the tiny inked teeth of the range, blank and innocent, hiding a throat full of song. Somewhere in those white spaces, a dead courier's map was moving south with whoever he'd trusted enough to die for.

Above her, through the window, the wards burned steady and beautiful over the sleeping city, and for the first time in her reign, Queen Maren looked at the light and felt what the light truly was:

A hundred years of screaming, made lovely by distance.

She snuffed the candle herself, sat down in the dark, and began to write the orders that would send Corvane hunting through her mountains — praying, with a usurper's practiced pragmatism, that whatever he found, it would arrive before the royalists did.

It did not occur to her — could not have occurred to her — that the person carrying the map was a bone-carver's daughter who listened to the dead.

Not yet.
