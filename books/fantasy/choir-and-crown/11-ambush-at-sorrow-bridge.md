## 11. Ambush at Sorrow Bridge

The bridge had been named by someone with a gift for grief.

Aldric felt it before he saw it — that was the soldier in him, still alive after all these years of disgrace, still whispering warnings into the back of his skull like an old comrade who refused to die. The gorge ahead narrowed to a span of grey stone no wider than a cart, the river below running white and loud over rocks the color of old teeth. Mist clung to the water like a shroud someone had hung out to dry and forgotten. Sorrow Bridge, the smugglers called it, though Aldric suspected the name predated even them. Places like this collected names the way wounds collected scars.

"We could go around," Vesper said beside him.

She had been saying it for half a mile, and each time she said it, Aldric heard what lived underneath the words: not fear of the bridge, but fear of what going around would cost them — another day on the mountain, another night of cold rations, another stretch of road where Corvane's riders might find them. She was counting in the currency of exhaustion now. So was he.

"Going around means the high pass," he said. "The high pass means open ground for two miles. If someone's watching us, I'd rather they watch us cross a bridge than watch us crawl along a ridgeline like ants."

"If someone's *watching* us—"

"There is." He said it plainly because she deserved plainness. "There has been since yesterday morning. A crow that never lands. A glint off rock that isn't ice. Someone is tracking us, and they're good enough that I can't tell how many."

Vesper stopped walking. Her breath came out in white plumes, and her eyes — those strange, patient eyes that always seemed to be listening to something beneath the world — fixed on his face.

"You've known since yesterday," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I wanted to be sure."

"And instead you let me walk into—" She gestured at the gorge, the mist, the narrow stone spine of the bridge. "*That.*"

"I let you walk toward it while I thought about whether I'd rather meet whoever it is here, on ground where they have to come at us two abreast, or in some meadow where they can ride us down from six directions." He kept his voice level. He had learned long ago that level voices were armor; men who shouted were men who had already lost. "This is the better ground. For us. If we take it fast."

Something moved behind her expression — anger giving way, grudgingly, to arithmetic. She was a carver; she understood material, structure, where a thing would break if struck. He watched her read the bridge the way she'd read a piece of bone.

"Two abreast," she said quietly. "You mean to fight."

"I mean to be ready to fight. There's a difference."

"Is there?" She pulled her cloak tighter. In the grey light, with the mist rising around her boots, she looked less like a bone-carver from a market town and more like something out of the old stories — one of the thin gray women who stood at the edges of battles and decided which dead got remembered. "Aldric. You told me you left the royalists because they ordered you to burn a village. You told me you haven't drawn a sword against a living man in six years."

He hadn't told her that last part. She had carved her way to it somehow, or simply listened until it fell out of him. That was the thing about Vesper Amberdale that unsettled him more than any ghost story: she listened to the living the way other people listened to the dead. Completely. Without flinching.

"No," he said. "I haven't."

"And if it's them? If it's your old brothers-in-arms on that bridge?"

He looked past her at the span. The stone was slick with spray. On the far side, the pines came down almost to the rail, dark and close, full of places for men to wait.

"Then there's no neutral ground left for me anywhere in this kingdom," he said. "And I should have admitted that a long time ago."

* * *

They crossed at midday, when the mist thinned but did not lift, because Aldric knew that waiting for perfect conditions was how soldiers died old and stupid. He went first. He had insisted, and Vesper had not argued, which told him she understood the shape of the argument better than most: a shield went in front. It was the only honest use a disgraced knight had ever found for himself.

The bridge groaned under their weight in small, intimate ways — a shifting of stones, a complaint in the mortar, the river's roar coming up through the gaps like breath through clenched teeth. Halfway across, Aldric stopped.

The pine forest on the far side had gone quiet.

Not silent — the wind still moved, the river still thundered — but quiet in the specific way that meant birds had been frightened off or killed. He had heard that quality of silence outside the walls of Harrowmere. He had heard it the day he refused the order, standing in the smoke of a village that had done nothing but exist near a royalist supply line, hearing the same hush where songbirds should have been.

"*Down*," he said, and shoved Vesper toward the bridge's low rail as the first crossbow bolt cracked past where his head had been and buried itself in the stone with a sound like a tooth breaking.

Then the treeline bloomed with movement.

Six of them — seven — eight, fanning out along the far bank, and two more behind, on the south side, cutting off retreat. They wore no colors, which was itself a color; hardliners had learned after Maren's coronation that the old blue-and-silver drew too many eyes. Their leader stepped onto the bridge's far end and pushed back his hood, and Aldric's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Ser Dannic Hale. Broad as a barn door, beard going iron-grey at the chin, a face Aldric had once trusted with his life on the fields at Colder's Ford — and had, and been trusted back. They had eaten from the same mess kettle for eleven years. They had buried friends together. Aldric had held the man's hair back the night they burned Prince Edran's body and Dannic drank himself sick with grief.

"Aldric." Hale's voice carried easily over the water, warm as a hearth, terrible as a hearth fire escaping its grate. "Seven years, and this is how I find you. Skulking toward the Thornfells with a bone-witch and a stolen map."

"The map isn't stolen."

"It isn't yours, either. Hand it over, old friend. Walk away. I'll tell the Lord Commander you died at Sorrow Bridge defending it — a clean death, an honorable one. Better than you deserve." Hale smiled, and it was almost the old smile, and that was the worst part. "Better than I want to give you."

"Hale." Aldric stepped forward, putting himself fully between the crossbows and Vesper. His sword came out of its scabbard with a whisper he felt in his teeth. "Listen to me. Whatever Cassel has told you about the Choir, whatever pretty story about a king's memory-song — it isn't true. What's down there isn't a treasure. It's ten thousand people. Dead men kept conscious, kept singing, kept screaming, for a century, to power the capital's wards. And you mean to bind all of that to some puppet boy's crown so the realm can go on pretending nothing happened."

For a moment — one heartbeat, maybe two — he saw doubt move through Hale's face like a fish under ice. He saw the man he'd known at Colder's Ford, the man who'd wept for a prince he'd never met but had loved the idea of.

Then the doubt froze over, and Hale spat into the river.

"Cassel says the song can be shaped. Bound to a willing vessel. Given *purpose*." He raised his hand. "Last chance, Aldric."

"No," Aldric said quietly. "But thank you for offering."

Hale dropped his arm, and the bridge became hell.

* * *

The first bolt took him in the left shoulder.

He heard it more than felt it — a wet, heavy *thock*, like an axe biting wood — and then the pain arrived, white and total, and his left arm simply stopped being his arm. He turned the fall into a lunge, because the second bolt was already coming and Vesper was behind him, and he put his body between hers and death the way he'd sworn, silently, somewhere on the mountain road, that he would.

The second bolt missed him and hit the rail.

They came onto the bridge three abreast, exactly as he'd predicted, and that small correctness was the only comfort the next ninety seconds offered him. He fought right-handed, badly, brutally — no form, no finesse, just the ugly close geometry of steel and desperation on a span barely wide enough for two men to pass. He took a cut across the thigh that opened muscle. He broke a man's wrist with his pommel and put the point of his sword through the gap beneath the fellow's jaw before the man finished screaming. He recognized him. Young Torvald, who used to sing harvest songs off-key on night watch. Aldric felt the life leave the body around his blade and thought, distantly, *now there are two ghosts following me, and neither of them will forgive me.*

Behind him, he heard Vesper shout — not a scream of terror but a word, thrown like a weapon — and then the bridge lurched. Later she would tell him what she'd done: pressed her palm flat against the ancient stone and spoken a name the courier's ghost had given her, the name of the mason who built the span four hundred years ago, and asked him, politely, to shake loose a railing-stone. Whether the dead answered or the mortar simply chose that moment to fail, Aldric never learned. But a section of rail burst outward on the north side, and one of Hale's men went over the edge with it, his cry swallowed whole by the river's throat.

It bought them three heartbeats. Three was enough to cost Aldric everything else.

Hale himself came through the gap in his own line like a landslide. The big man had always fought the way he drank — hugely, directly, without artifice — and his first blow rang against Aldric's guard hard enough to numb his bad arm to the elbow. His second opened a gash above Aldric's eye, and the world went red and swimming. His third—

His third never landed.

Because Aldric stopped trying to defend and simply stepped inside the blow, into the space where a friend would never strike and an enemy must, and drove his sword up under Hale's breastbone with both hands, using the weight of the man's own charge to bury the blade to the hilt.

They stood like that, cheek to cheek, breathing each other's air, the battle raging and fading around them both into a kind of private weather. Hale's hands gripped Aldric's shoulders — not pushing away, holding on. His mouth worked. Blood came between his teeth.

"Colder's Ford," he rasped. "You remember. You remember—"

"I remember everything," Aldric said, and his voice broke on it. "God forgive me. I remember everything."

Hale slid off the blade and Aldric caught him, because some things the body does without asking permission, and lowered him to the stones. Around them the remaining attackers were retreating — two dead, one drowned, the rest melting back into the pines at some signal Aldric didn't see. It was over in less time than a funeral takes, and it had been a funeral the entire time.

He knelt there holding a dying man's hand on a bridge named for sorrow, and the blood on the stones steamed faintly in the cold, and he could not make himself stand up.

* * *

Vesper knelt opposite him. He didn't know how long she'd been there. Her hands hovered over Hale's wound, useless — bone-craft could not mend flesh — and her face was pale as the mist.

"He's still here," she whispered. "Aldric. He's still *here*. I can hear him."

"Don't." The word came out raw. "Don't listen to him. Don't — he deserves better than to be a voice in someone's skull."

"He knows that." Her eyes were wet. "He's saying — he's saying he's glad it was you. He says the others would have taken the map and crowned a corpse. He says—" Her voice caught. "He says he's sorry he made you do it. He says he remembers Colder's Ford too."

Aldric bowed his head. Somewhere below them the river went on devouring the mountainside, patient as a debt.

Hale died with his hand in Aldric's grip and, perhaps, with Vesper's voice carrying something of his own back to him — she murmured his name, and rank, and the words *you were seen, you were known,* and if that was a lie it was the kindest lie Aldric had ever witnessed. When it was done, she helped him rise, and gasped, because his shoulder was a ruin and his thigh was pouring and the gash over his eye had painted half his face crimson.

"You need binding," she said, all business, because he could see her choosing business over weeping. "Now. Before the cold sets in."

"Leave me."

"What?"

"Leave me here." He tried to say it firmly and heard it come out slurred, blood-loss softening his tongue. "Cassel will send more. You know the tunnels now. The map shows the paths past the gorge. You don't need a lame knight slowing—"

She hit him.

Not hard — she had the arm strength of a woman who carved bone for a living, which was not nothing, but it was hardly a killing blow — a short, furious cuff against his good shoulder that nevertheless rocked him, mostly because he had never imagined it.

"Listen to me," Vesper Amberdale said, and her voice was the voice of every ghost she'd ever carried, layered and low and absolutely certain. "I have spent my whole life among the dead, and do you know what they all say, at the end? All of them? Every single one? They say *I wish I had told them.* Told whom. Whoever. The person across the table, the person on the road, the person kneeling in the blood. I have heard ten thousand regrets and not one of them was *I wish I had stayed silent longer.*"

Her hands were shaking. Her eyes were blazing.

"So I am not leaving you on this bridge to bleed into the river, Aldric of the old banner, and you are going to sit against that rail and hold still while I bind your shoulder with your shirt, and while I do it you are going to tell me what you were about to say to me on the mountain road three nights ago before you lost your nerve. Because I heard it. Living ears, not dead ones. I *heard* it."

The world swayed gently. The mist curled. Behind them, Ser Dannic Hale lay still on the stones of Sorrow Bridge, and Aldric understood with sudden, piercing clarity that the man had been the last door — the last version of himself that could ever have gone home to the royalists, the last neutral ground, and it was closed now, and locked, and he had locked it with his own hands.

There was only forward. Only her.

"I love you," he said. It came out smaller than the truth of it. He tried again. "I love you. I think I have since the flooded chapel, and I've been strangling it ever since because I'm a coward with a sword and you deserve better than a man who kills his friends on bridges."

Vesper went very still. Then she tore his shirt sleeve open, pressed cloth to the wound hard enough to make him hiss, and said, in a voice that wobbled only slightly:

"Good. Now stay alive, damn you. Stay alive, and I'll decide whether you deserve better."

She bound him by the river while the mist closed over the bodies of the dead, and it was only when they finally limped away from Sorrow Bridge, up into the pines, that either of them noticed the thing that made her stop dead in the snow.

Far below, at the gorge's southern mouth, a line of torches had appeared. Dozens of them. Moving with the unhurried discipline of men who had all the time in the world and knew exactly where their quarry was going.

"Not royalists," Aldric breathed, squinting through the failing light. The torches burned a color he knew from old reports he'd hoped never to verify — a white flame that gave off no heat. "Inquisitor's fire. That's Corvane."

Vesper's hand found his. Her fingers were ice and iron.

"Then we run," she said.
