## 12. Bone-Set and Bone-Song

The cave Vesper found was less a cave than a wound in the mountainside — a hollow where the rock had slumped inward like a man leaning against a door too long. She had dragged Aldric into it by the collar of his ruined cloak, one arm hooked under his shoulders, her boots skidding on scree slicked with his blood. He had tried to walk. She remembered that with a strange, fierce tenderness: even half-conscious, even with the bone of his forearm jutting white beneath torn skin, he had tried to walk beside her rather than be carried.

"Sit," she had said, and for once in his stubborn life, he had sat.

Now she knelt over him in the blue-grey light of dusk leaking through the entrance, sorting through what remained of their supplies with hands that would not quite stop shaking. The ambush at Sorrow Bridge lay three hours behind them, but it lived in every sound: the drip of water became hoofbeats; the groan of settling stone became the wet rattle of Ser Brandt dying with Aldric's sword through him.

Aldric lay propped against the wall, grey-faced, his broken arm cradled across his chest like something stolen. His eyes were open. They had been open the whole time, watching her with a focus that had nothing to do with pain and everything to do with the fact that he had killed four men that day — men who had once slept within arm's reach of him, shared his watchfires, sung his orders back to him across frozen courtyards.

"You should sleep," Vesper said.

"I should hold still." His voice came out scraped raw. "Sleep is a different thing."

She found the last clean cloth, the needle, a stub of waxed thread. Found, too, the willow bark she'd been hoarding since the drowned chapel, bitter as regret, and made him chew it while she worked.

"I'm going to set it," she told him. "On my count. And then I'm going to be very busy not apologizing."

He almost smiled. It cost him something — she could see the muscles of his face arguing about it — but it arrived. "You've set bones before?"

"Birds' wings. A goat's leg once." She flexed her fingers. "And my own wrist, twice, which is why I don't recommend fainting."

"And mine?"

"Yours is bigger," she said. "That's all. Bigger bone, worse break, same arithmetic. Bone wants to knit. Everything alive wants to keep being alive. My job is just to convince it you're worth the effort."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound that turned into a wince. "You always talk like this when you're frightened?"

"Only when I'm frightened *and* right."

She took his hand — the good grip of his fingers around hers, warm and deliberate — counted three, and pulled.

The sound his body made was nothing. The sound *he* made was everything: a roar bitten down to a groan, his whole frame bowing, his free fist striking the stone hard enough to split the skin of his knuckles. Then it was done, the bone seated beneath the flesh where it belonged, and they were both breathing hard in the small dark as though they'd run a mile together instead of moving an arm four inches.

"There," she whispered. "Alive. Told you."

"Told me," he agreed faintly.

* * *

The problem was the splint.

She had wood — two flat pieces of kindling from the chapel bundle, straight enough, strong enough. But wood chafed. Wood swelled with the damp of the mountain and pressed its grain into healing flesh, and she had watched splinter-wounds go bad in Greyhollow often enough to fear them more than the break itself. What she wanted — what her hands wanted, before her mind caught up — was smooth material, light and dense, that could be shaped close to the limb and would not rot.

What she wanted was bone.

Her eyes went, without permission, to the map's casing.

It lay at the bottom of her pack where it had ridden since the ford, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as a seed. A tube of some pale composite — not ivory, not whalebone, nothing she could name, worked by hands long dead or never born. The courier had died pressing it into hers. Inside it, the impossible map coiled: tunnels beneath the Thornfells that no smuggler had cut, roads drawn in a hand that seemed to know the mountain's dreams. She had promised herself she would not touch it again until the Hollow Choir itself forced the question.

But the casing was wide. The casing was smooth. And there was a section near the cap — a plain band two inches tall, unmarked, redundant — that could come away cleanly if she scored it right, and would give her two curved plates exactly the width of a forearm.

"It's only the edge," she heard herself say, to Aldric or to the cave or to whatever part of her had already decided. "Just the trim. Not the map."

Aldric watched her draw her smallest knife. "Is that wise?"

"No," she said honestly. "But neither is letting your arm fester, and I've decided which foolishness I can live with."

She set the casing across her knee. Scored the band. The knife sang a thin note against it, and the note went *through* her.

Not through her ears. Through the place behind her ears, the place where the dead lived now — the widow's husband murmuring his last *tell her the blue dress*, the courier's ghost still whispering fragments of *the Choir still sings* in the corner of her skull like a man pacing a room he couldn't leave. Every voice she had ever carved stayed with her; that was the gift's price and had been since she was nine years old and her mother first put a chisel in her hand. She carried a graveyard behind her eyes and called it competence.

But this was different.

This was the casing answering her blade — and under the thin note of scoring steel, something vast shifted, the way a lake shifts when ice begins to give. The courier's presence, which had been a whisper, a smear, a haunting half-voice, suddenly *sat up*.

Vesper's knife stopped.

"Vesper?" Aldric said.

"Hush," she breathed. "He's — hush."

She carved. One clean line, then another, and between them the memory poured out of the bone like water finding a crack.

* * *

Later, she could not have said how long it lasted. Minutes. An hour. The cave walls dissolved; the cold went out of the air; she knelt inside another man's dying as though it were a country she'd crossed into.

His name was Tam. Tam Ellery, second son of a cooper in Marrowford, who had taken the courier's oath at nineteen because it meant shoes and bread and a reason to see the world beyond his father's staves. She knew this the way she knew her own scars — completely, without effort. She knew the smell of the tannery street where he'd grown up. Knew his sister Liss's laugh and the way his mother hummed off-key when she thought no one listened. Knew the taste of the apple he'd eaten on the road north, sour and perfect.

And she knew what he had carried.

Not the map alone. The map was a key, and keys are dull things. What Tam had carried was the *telling* — sworn into him by the Choir's last keeper with salt and blood and a ritual so old it predated the kingdom, so that the secret could not be tortured or bought or lost, only given freely to one chosen ear. He had been carrying it for eleven years. Eleven years of knowing, sealed behind his teeth, walking through markets full of people whose peace was built on what he knew.

Vesper saw what he had seen.

She stood — as Tam, as herself, as both — in a vault beneath the Thornfells, in the Hollow Choir, and the name was a lie and the truth of it broke something in her that did not afterward mend.

Bones hung in the dark. Thousands of them. Suspended on threads of old ward-silver from a ceiling lost in blackness, arranged in spirals and rings, and each one *lit* — not with light, with voice. With song. Ten thousand overlapping melodies braided into one endless chord, and the chord was grief, and the grief was *awake*.

They were not relics. They were not echoes. They were the war-dead of the Sundering — the slaughtered of both armies, of the villages burned between the lines, of the plague-fields after — and they had never been allowed to finish dying. Some king's necromancer, some desperate founding generation, had bound them here, conscious in the marrow, singing their deaths forever, because their song fed the wards. Because ten thousand voices crying without end made a power that could shield a capital city, murrain-proof the granaries, turn storms aside from the walls.

Every year of peace Maren's realm had known was paid for in this room, by these throats.

She saw a soldier's finger-bone still trying to write his wife's name in the air, over and over, the same six strokes for a hundred and forty years. She saw a child's skull singing a lullaby its mother had never finished teaching it. She saw a woman who had been a weaver in life still moving her phalanges through the pattern of a loom that had burned with her, threadless, endless, patient.

And she saw — this was the worst, this was the thing she would carry into every silence for the rest of her life — that they *knew*. They knew where they were. They knew the sky was somewhere far above. Some of them had gone mad, and their madness was part of the music too, woven in like a wrong-colored thread. Some of them prayed. Most of them had stopped praying and simply *sang*, because singing was the shape of their prison and the prison was the shape of the peace, and no one had ever come.

No one except the keeper. Her mother. A figure in grey at the edge of Tam's memory, standing before the hanging host with a chisel in her hand and her face turned away, always turned away — and even in the borrowed memory Vesper felt the weight of that turned face, the shame of it, the unfinished decision hanging over twenty years like a blade that had forgotten to fall.

Then the vision narrowed, rushed, became the courier again: Tam running through the Thornfell passes with royalist riders behind him and the telling burning in his chest, choosing a stranger at random because the keeper was twenty years dead and someone, *someone*, had to know. Choosing a bone-carver because the keeper had said, in the last instruction Tam ever received: *if I do not return, find one who listens to bone. They will understand.*

Tam bleeding out against a workshop door in Greyhollow. Tam whispering *the Choir still sings* because it wasn't a warning. It was a plea. It was the closest his oath would let him come to saying: *they are still in there. Still awake. Please.*

The memory ended. The cave came back.

* * *

She was on her knees with her forehead nearly touching the stone, and the map's casing lay scored and whole in her lap, and she was crying in great silent convulsions that hurt her ribs, and she could not remember starting.

"Vesper." Aldric's hand — his good hand — found her shoulder. "Vesper, look at me. What did you see?"

She looked at him. His face swam, doubled, tripled — and behind his face, horribly, beautifully, she could still see them. The hanging rings. The writing finger. The child's skull singing a song with no ending.

"They're people," she said. Her voice came out wrecked. "Aldric. The song. The Choir. It isn't a weapon, it isn't a relic, it isn't a *key* to anything." She pressed the heel of her palm against her sternum, as if she could hold her own heart quieter. "It's ten thousand people. The war-dead. Bound there since the Sundering, conscious, *aware*, singing their deaths to feed the wards. All of it. The capital's shields. The mild winters inside the walls. The granaries that never fail. All of it is *them*."

He had gone very still. She watched the knight in him reach for the strategic implications and the man in him strangle the knight mid-reach.

"The wards," he said slowly. "Maren's peace."

"Maren's peace. Cassel's crown. Corvane's mercy. Every side of this hunt has been fighting over a *prison*, and none of us—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, smaller. "None of us knew there were prisoners."

Aldric leaned his head back against the stone and closed his eyes. For a long time the only sounds were water dripping somewhere in the dark and the wind worrying the entrance of the cave, and Vesper understood that he was doing arithmetic of his own — the same grim sums she was doing, the sums that rearranged everything.

"My order," he said finally, without opening his eyes, "wants to bind the song to a pretender. Crown a king with ten thousand screaming souls as his scepter." A muscle moved in his jaw. "Corvane wants to burn them out. And I rode after you thinking this was about legitimacy. About bloodlines. About which ass belongs on which throne." He opened his eyes, and they were wet, and he did not seem ashamed of it. "I was a fool with a sword."

"We were both fools," Vesper said. "Mine just didn't have a sword."

Something that was almost laughter moved through him, and then faded. "Your mother," he said gently. "The keeper. In the memory — the woman with the chisel—"

"Was her." The words came out of Vesper like splinters. "Twenty years she knew. Twenty years she walked away down that mountain and raised a daughter in a market town and never said a word. She was the last keeper and she *left*, and she left instructions instead of answers, and I—" Her voice cracked and she let it. "I spent my whole life wondering why she went. Why she chose the mountain over me. And the answer is she couldn't choose at all. She froze. She stood in front of ten thousand suffering souls with a chisel in her hand and the power to free them, and she *turned her face away* and walked out, and then she died at the door she couldn't walk back through."

"You don't know that she—"

"I know exactly what she did." Vesper wiped her face with the back of her wrist, roughly, the way you wipe a table. "Because I've got her hands, Aldric. I've got her gift and her craft and now her secret, and the same question sitting in front of me that sat in front of her. Free them, and the wards fall, and gods know what comes down on the capital. Leave them, and every day of peace is bought with ten thousand voices that never stop singing." She looked at her palms, at the calluses her mother had also worn, at the tool-marks that were almost a family crest. "She couldn't answer it. She died not answering it. And now it's mine."

Aldric reached over — awkwardly, wincing, with his splinted arm held carefully against his chest — and took her hand. His grip was warm and careful and entirely without strategy. No banner in it anywhere.

"Then we'll answer it together," he said. "Or badly. Probably badly. But together."

Vesper held onto his hand and let the cave be quiet around them, and behind her eyes the ten thousand sang on, patient as stone, waiting for an answer that had already waited a hundred and forty years.

It was Aldric who broke the silence, his voice dropping low, his eyes going past her shoulder to the mouth of the cave and the night beyond it.

"Vesper. How far to the Keeper's Door?"

She followed his gaze. Far below in the valley, small and wrong against the darkness, torches were moving. Not two. Not five. A procession of them, winding up the smuggler roads she knew so well, toward the heart of the Thornfells.

Lord Cassel had found the mountain.
