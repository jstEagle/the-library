## 14. Inside the Choir

**Vesper**

The Keeper's Door did not open so much as *remember* them.

When Vesper pressed her palm against the carved threshold—her mother's last work, she knew now, the lines of it worn shallow by twenty years of waiting—the stone exhaled. A breath of cold air, old and mineral and faintly sweet, like the inside of a church that had never been used. Then the door swung inward on hinges that had not screamed in two decades, because nothing down here had been disturbed enough to rust.

Aldric stood at her shoulder with the torch held high, his splinted arm bound across his chest, his sword drawn for no reason either of them could name. "After you," he said quietly.

"No," Vesper answered. "*Together.*"

The passage beyond was narrow at first—a smuggler's cut, barely shoulder-width, the walls scored with tool-marks that grew finer as they descended. Vesper ran her fingers along them without thinking, the way she read any carved surface, and what she felt there made her stumble.

Not tool-marks. *Names.* Hundreds of names, thousands maybe, cut in a script she almost recognized—her mother's hand, but younger, hurried, the letters leaning like people running from something. And beneath each name, smaller still, a single word repeated over and over down the length of the corridor:

*Remember. Remember. Remember.*

"She knew," Vesper whispered. Her voice came back to her strange and flattened, swallowed by the dark ahead. "All those years I thought she'd abandoned us. She was down here. Carving."

"Carving what?"

"I don't know." But the bone-cased map against her ribs had begun to hum, faint as a struck tuning fork, and some part of her already dreaded the answer.

The corridor opened without warning, and Aldric's torchlight simply gave up.

Not because the dark was thick—but because the space beyond was *full*. The light fell into a cavern so vast that the flame's reach ended like a hand touching water, illuminating only the first few feet of a floor that sloped away into nothing. Vesper stood on the threshold and felt the scale of it enter her body through her bones before her eyes could make sense of it: a pressure, a depth, an *audience*.

Then the torch caught the nearest of the suspended things, and Vesper stopped breathing.

Bones.

They hung from the cavern's unseen ceiling by cords of woven hair—thousands upon thousands of them, vertebrae and femurs and small delicate finger-bones, arranged in slow spirals that descended into the dark below. Not piled. Not scattered. *Hung*, with care, each one oriented, each one placed, the whole vast arrangement turning almost imperceptibly in a draft that had no source. Where the torchlight touched them they glowed faintly—not reflecting light but producing it, a pale luminescence like moonrise through fog, and with every glow came a sound.

Singing.

No—that was the lie of it, Veper realized, standing frozen on the ledge. Singing implied melody, implied consent. This was ten thousand voices layered over one another, each one murmuring its own note, its own word, its own grief, until they merged into a single sustained chord that filled the cavern like water fills a drowned bell. It was beautiful the way a cathedral is beautiful: by making you feel small and complicit at once.

"Oh," Aldric said beside her, and his voice cracked on the single syllable. "Oh, gods. What *is* this place?"

Vesper didn't answer, because she was listening.

She had spent her life listening to the dead—one voice at a time, one carving at a time, each soul arriving in her workshop with its final words and then staying, always staying, a quiet crowd behind her eyes. She had thought herself burdened. She had thought herself cursed.

This was not that.

Here the dead were not echoes trapped in objects. They were *present*. As her gift reached out along the nearest spiral of hanging bone, the murmur resolved into distinct voices, distinct pleas, and Vesper gripped the edge of the stone until her nails went white:

*"Maren of the Fenlands, aged nine. I was carrying water when the riders came. Tell my mother the well was—"*

*"Hollis. Hollis Amberdale-line. I held the gate. I held the gate. I held the—"*

*"It is cold. Why has no one told them we are cold? We have been singing so long. We only wanted someone to ask our names."*

Name after name after name. A farmer who'd died holding a scythe he'd never learned to use in war. A boy of eleven whose entire memory was his mother's kitchen. An old woman who had survived three battles and died of a fever in the aftermath, furious about it even now, even here. Ten thousand souls, and the vault held them the way a jar holds fireflies—not cruelly, perhaps, but completely, and forever.

"They're not resting," Vesper said. Her own voice sounded foreign, scraped thin. "They've never rested. They've been *awake*. All this time. Awake and singing."

"Singing what?" Aldric's torch had gone unsteady in his grip; the spirals of light swung and danced. "Why would anyone build—"

And then the chord shifted, and both of them heard it.

Beneath the voices—beneath, or woven through, or made *of* them—ran a second music. Deeper. Structured. A pattern imposed on the grief the way a frame imposes shape on a screaming mouth, drawing the ten thousand individual sorrows up through the mountain's roots, along channels Vesper suddenly understood were carved into the very rock, and out. Out toward the capital. Out toward the wards that had kept the realm's borders sealed and its fields fertile and its peace unbroken for a hundred years.

Every ward-stone in the city was fed from here. Every protective charm, every blessing laid over Maren's reign, every quiet night in every village—powered by this endless, conscious, suffering song.

"It's a machine," Aldric breathed. He had gone gray around the mouth. "A grief-engine. This is what the wards run on. This is what the peace *costs*."

"Not cost." Vesper's hands had begun to shake, and she let them. "Cost implies payment ends. This is theft. A hundred years of theft. Someone found that dead souls could be made to sing together, and instead of freeing them they built a kingdom on top of the noise."

She stepped forward along the ledge, and the nearest spiral of bones turned toward her—or seemed to—and the voices sharpened, individual again, desperate:

*"You can hear us. You can hear us—you're one of hers, aren't you, you have her hands—please, please, we don't want to power anything, we just want to be done—"*

*"My name is Sera. My name is Sera. If no one says it am I still—"*

*"Don't sing. Don't make me sing. I'm tired. I'm so tired, and the song doesn't let us stop—"*

Vesper pressed both palms against her ears and it did nothing, because the voices weren't arriving through her ears. They arrived the way all the dead arrived: directly, intimately, into the listening place behind her sternum where every soul she'd ever carved still lived. She had carried dozens of ghosts for years, a private choir of the grieving. Now she stood inside a vault containing two hundred times that number, and every single one of them knew she could hear.

*A hundred years,* she thought, staggered. *A hundred years of peace above, and not one soul in the realm ever asked why the wards needed feeding. Not one priest, not one king, not one queen asked what the song was made of.*

"We have to tell someone," Aldric said behind her. "The Queen. The Council. Whoever—"

"Tell them what?" She turned, and saw him properly in the bone-light: hollow-eyed, torch trembling, the honor in his face at war with itself exactly as it had been on the mountain road. "That their peace runs on prisoners? That every harvest festival, every safe road, every year their children didn't die in border wars was paid for by ten thousand people who can't stop screaming? Who do we tell, Aldric? Who *hears* that and doesn't flinch?"

He had no answer. The chord swelled between them, patient and enormous, and somewhere far above—so far above that it was more idea than sound—the capital's wards drank another day of grief.

* * *

**Aldric**

He had knelt before kings. He had stood vigil over a dying queen and sworn oaths in three languages. He believed himself past awe.

The Choir unmade that belief in the space of a single breath.

Aldric had come down the mountain expecting a treasure—a relic, a coronation-song, something a man could hold. Even after the courier's ghost, even after everything Vesper had carved free of that terrible map, some soldier's corner of his heart had imagined a *thing*: an object with edges, weight, a thing that could be defended or surrendered or burned.

Instead there was this: a cathedral of the dead, hung by loving hands, singing against their will.

He watched Vesper move along the ledge, and what he saw frightened him more than the vault itself. She walked among the spirals like a physician walking a plague-ward—not recoiling, not covering her ears anymore, but *listening*, stopping here and there to lay her good hand against a hanging femur, and wherever she touched, the bone-light brightened and a single voice rose clear from the chord:

*"Tomas. My name is Tomas Ellerby. I plowed the south field. Is the south field still—"*

"Yes," Vesper said aloud, to a dead man, in a steady voice. "The south field is still there. Barley, last I passed. They rotate it with clover now."

*"Clover,"* the voice repeated, wondering. *"Good. Clover's good for the soil."* And then, quieter, in a tone Aldric would remember for the rest of his life: *"Thank you for answering. No one answers."*

Aldric turned away, because his eyes were burning, and because looking at the deeper spirals—descending into darkness where the light of ten thousand bones faded to a red-black gleam like coals under ash—he understood that the ones nearest the door were the *lucky* ones. The ones near the surface. The deepest circles had been singing longest.

His oath, sworn on the old bloodline, rose up in him like bile. *Defend the realm. Uphold the crown. Preserve the continuity of the blood royal.* Every knight of his order had recited it knowing, in the way soldiers know things without knowing, that somewhere beneath the words lay machinery none of them were meant to inspect. He had refused one order in his life and been broken for it. Now he understood that his entire order—all its banners, all its restoration plots, Lord Cassel and his pretender kings—existed to protect *this*. To ensure that whoever sat the throne, the song below went on uninterrupted.

"The royalists know," he said hoarsely. "Cassel knows. The old families have known for generations—it's in the founding charters, I see that now, the clauses about 'maintenance of the deep assets.' We called it stewardship." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "I trained my whole life to guard a prison and called it honor."

"You didn't know."

"That's not absolution, Vesper. That's the point. None of us knew because none of us *wanted* to know. There's a difference between a secret and a thing everyone agrees not to look at." He gestured at the vast dark, at the spirals descending beyond sight. "How long? How long has this been running?"

Vesper was quiet a moment. When she answered, she had gone pale in a new way—not horror now but arithmetic.

"The war was a hundred and twelve years ago. The wards date from the same decade. So—a hundred years. Give or take a generation of keepers." She looked at her own hands. "My mother was the last keeper. She came down here to end it. She got as far as the door."

"Twenty years ago."

"Twenty years ago." Vesper's voice flattened. "And in twenty years, no one else ever came. Because the ones who knew how to get here were the ones who benefited from it staying shut."

The chord beneath them shifted again, and this time the shift had texture—a ripple moving outward through the spirals, bone-light flaring spiral by spiral, like wind crossing a field of wheat. The voices rose, not louder but *clearer*, and Aldric realized with a lurch that they were turning toward Vesper. All of them. Ten thousand faces he couldn't see, orienting on the one living soul in the vault who could hear.

*"Keeper's daughter,"* the chord murmured, and the words moved through it like light through water. *"Keeper's daughter. Keeper's daughter. She has the chisel-hand. She has the listening ear."*

"What do they mean?" Aldric asked, though he feared the answer.

"I think," Vesper said slowly, "they mean the vault recognizes me." She lifted her mother's chisel from the satchel at her hip—the tool they'd found still gripped in a skeleton's hand, twenty years silent—and as her fingers closed around it, every spiral in the cavern blazed at once.

For one impossible moment, the chord resolved. Ten thousand voices became one voice, and it spoke with the weight of a century:

*"Will you finish it?"*

The silence afterward was worse than the singing. The bones dimmed, waiting. Somewhere far overhead, Aldric felt rather than heard a tremor pass through the rock—the first tremor, though he didn't yet know what it meant—as if the mountain itself had leaned in to hear her answer.

Vesper stood at the ledge's edge with the chisel in her hand and tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face, and she did not answer. Not yet. Below her, the deepest spirals pulsed like a heartbeat learning hope; and far above them both, faint through a hundred fathoms of stone, came a sound neither of them expected.

Boots. Many boots. Descending.

Aldric's hand found his sword hilt on instinct, then fell away, useless. He knew that cadence. He had marched to it for fifteen years.

"Cassel," he breathed. "They've found the door."

The chord began, very softly, to sing again.
