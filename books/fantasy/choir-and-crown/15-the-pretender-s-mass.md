## 15. The Pretender's Mass

The descent into the vault took three days from the toll-gate, and Aldric spent every hour of it rehearsing the speech he would never give.

He knew this about himself. He had always been a man who composed his defiance in the quiet hours and then swallowed it at the moment of delivery. He had done it at Vell's Ford, when Captain Hesse ordered the surrender of a village that had already flown white banners, and Aldric had ridden to the captain's tent with a speech on his tongue about honor and the laws of war — and had come out again having said nothing, only nodded, only watched the smoke rise afterward. That was the wound his disgrace had been carved from. Not the refusal itself. The refusal he had failed to speak aloud.

Not this time, he told himself, as the tunnel narrowed and the air took on that strange resonant quality, as though the mountain itself were humming through its teeth. Not this time.

Vesper walked ahead of him, her lamp throwing her shadow long against the wet stone. She had not spoken much in two days. Since the courier's memory had poured out of the map's casing — since the word *ten thousand* had entered the world between them — she carried herself like a woman walking toward her own funeral, upright and deliberate, saving her breath for something. He wanted to take her hand. He did not. There would be time for softness later or there would be none, and either way the tunnel demanded all their attention.

Behind them came the sounds he had been dreading: boots. Many boots, disciplined, unhurried. Lord Cassel did not hurry. Cassel was a man who believed the world would arrange itself around his arrival if he simply declined to rush toward it, and so far in his long life the world had obliged.

"They're an hour behind us," Vesper said quietly. "Maybe less."

"I know."

"You could still go back to them. Walk out to meet them, say you found me wandering, say—" 

"Vesper."

She stopped. In the lamplight her face was hollowed, beautiful in the way of things worn thin by weather. "I don't want you to lose everything twice," she said. "Once for a king who's dead. Once for dead people you've never met."

"That's just it," Aldric said. "They were never strangers to me. I've spent my whole life being told what the dead want. Banners with old men's faces on them. Oaths sworn over tombs. Every man who ever sent me to kill someone claimed the authority of the dead." He looked past her, down the tunnel, where the humming had grown until he felt it in his molars. "I'd like, just once, to hear what they actually want. Before anyone decides for them."

Her expression softened, and then hardened again into resolve, which he had learned was how she carried tenderness — wrapped in armor, like everything else she valued.

"Then let's not keep them waiting," she said.

* * *

The door at the bottom of the Keeper's stair opened onto a sight that Aldric knew he would be describing on his deathbed, if he lived to have one, and failing to find words for even then.

The cavern was vast beyond the reach of the lamps — the eye simply gave up and reported *sky*, because there was nothing else in human experience shaped like this. Bones hung suspended in the air. Thousands of them. Ribs curved like the frames of unfinished ships. Femurs and humeri arranged in slow spirals, turning without wind, without touch, drifting in orbits that obeyed some gravity older than stone. And threaded through them, light — pale gold, sourceless, moving like current through water — and with the light, the voices.

He had expected a sound like singing. What he heard instead was more terrible: ten thousand conversations overlapping, ten thousand individual voices each distinct if you leaned toward them, each saying something small and particular and human. A woman counting the names of her children over and over. A boy reciting a prayer he half remembered. An old man telling someone — no one — how the barley had come in that year, the last year, before the fire.

It was not a choir. It was a city. A city of the drowned, still knocking on the underside of the world.

"Saints preserve us," whispered Ser Brandt behind him, one of Cassel's picked men, and Aldric heard the young knight's voice crack on the second word.

"It doesn't need preserving," Aldric said. "It needs leaving alone."

"Ser Aldric." Cassel's voice, mild as milk, from behind. Aldric turned. The old lord stood at the head of thirty men, silver-haired, immaculate even in mountain mud, holding his lantern as though it were a chalice. "You're a difficult man to predict. I was told you were escorting the bone-carver as a prisoner. You appear to be escorting her as a pilgrim."

"I appear," Aldric said, "to be standing between her and you."

Cassel smiled the smile he used in council chambers, the one that made disagreement seem like bad manners rather than treason. "Look at it, Aldric. Truly look." He gestured with his lantern, and the light swept across the hanging bones like a benediction. "Ten thousand loyal subjects of the true crown, preserved. Not rotted in ditches. Not forgotten. *Kept.* Every king since the Sundering has drawn his ward-light from this place and called it divine right, and not one of them ever came down here to see the cost of their throne. We will be the first to do it properly. Reverently. We will bind the song to a living claimant — young Edmun Varro, perhaps; he has the brow, the blood is close enough — and the dead themselves will legitimize him. Not a usurper's decree. Not a paper marriage. The voice of the ancient kings, made audible at last." His eyes shone. "This is not theft, Aldric. This is coronation."

Aldric looked at the bones again. He listened — really listened, letting go of the roar of the whole and finding the single voices beneath, as Vesper had taught him without knowing it — and he heard a girl asking whether her mother had escaped the east gate. He heard a man weeping without stopping, quietly, the way men weep when they have been doing it a very long time.

"My lord," Aldric said, "when did any of them consent to this?"

Cassel's smile flickered. "Consent is a luxury of the living. The dead serve."

"There it is." Aldric's voice came out low and steady, and he marveled at it, because inside he felt the old cowardice rising like floodwater and something new rising faster, something that had been twenty years in the digging. "There it is, my lord, in six words. *The dead serve.* That's the whole catechism, isn't it? It's what the priests said at Vell's Ford when they burned the granary. It's what Hesse said when he took the village. The dead serve, the small serve, the ones who can't answer back serve — and the ones who speak for them grow fat on the service." He unclasped his sword belt. Slowly. Deliberately. He held it out before him in both hands, the way a man surrenders, the way a knight renounces. "I swore an oath to the bloodline when I was sixteen years old, before I'd ever seen a man die, before I understood what oaths cost the people who don't make them. I have been trying for fifteen years to find a version of that oath I could keep with clean hands. There isn't one. There never was."

"Aldric," Cassel said, and now his voice had lost its warmth entirely, "think carefully."

"I am thinking," Aldric said, and dropped the sword. It rang on the stone, and the sound went out into the cavern, and — he would swear this until he died — the nearest spiral of bones shivered, and one voice among ten thousand fell briefly silent, as if listening. "The dead are not a crown. They are not a treasury. They are not ours. They are *them* — ten thousand someones who were murdered to build a peace we've been drinking like wine for a hundred years, and I will not raise one more cup of it. Not for Edmun Varro. Not for the old bloodline. Not for you."

He stepped sideways then, deliberately, until he stood beside Vesper — between her and the thirty lances of the royalist column — and everyone in the cavern understood the geometry of what he had done.

"So," Cassel said softly. "The Knight Without a Banner finally chooses his banner."

"Yes," Aldric said. "Though it isn't mine to choose alone." He looked at Vesper. "Tell them. Tell them what they are."

Vesper lifted her chin. She raised her hands, palms open, toward the hanging dark, and spoke in her carver's voice — steady, precise, unhurried, the voice of a woman doing careful work. "You are not forgotten," she said to the bones. "We came down to ask you what you want. No one has ever asked you that. Ask it now, and I swear on my craft and my mother's craft, whatever you answer, I will carve it true."

And the cavern answered.

It was not words. It was not song. It was a rising, a swelling, ten thousand griefs lifting at once like floodwater finding a breach, and the ward-light guttered, and the bones began to turn faster, and somewhere far above them — Aldric felt it in the floor, in his ribs, in the fillings of his teeth — something in the capital flickered for the first time in a century.

"Seize them both," Cassel said. "Gently, if it can be managed."

Thirty knights drew steel. Aldric had no sword, one working arm, and a bone-carver at his side, and he discovered, with a strange calm, that he was not afraid. He had been afraid his whole life of this exact moment — the moment of open rupture, of choosing visibly, irreversibly, in front of witnesses — and now that it had arrived it felt less like falling and more like setting something down that he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his spine.

He put his body in front of hers.

"Brandt," he said to the young knight nearest him, the one whose voice had cracked. "You sang in the chapel at Coldmere. You know what a choir is for. Is it for this?" He gestured upward at the turning bones, the trembling light, the weeping that filled the world. "Steel won't help you here, lad. Draw it and you'll be drawing it on your own grandmothers."

For one heartbeat, one held breath, the whole royalist column wavered — and Aldric thought, wildly, absurdly, *it might end here, it might end with thirty men lowering their swords in a cave* —

Then the far wall of the cavern bloomed with torchlight.

New voices. New boots. Not royalists. The Queen's warrant was a thing Aldric had never seen up close, but he knew its heraldry at once — the black stag, the balanced scales — and at the head of the inquisitorial column, calm as a man arriving slightly early for a dinner appointment, stood Inquisitor Corvane, carrying a censer of consecrated fire the way other men carried a lantern.

"Lord Cassel," Corvane said pleasantly, into the silence. "How good. Everyone is here. Nobody has to send for anybody."

The censer swung gently in his gloved hand, and its smoke smelled of cedar and burning hair, and above them all ten thousand voices rose together in a sound that was not music and would never again be mistaken for one.

Beside Aldric, Vesper went very still, and then reached into her pack and drew out her chisel.

"Well," she murmured, so only he could hear it. "Three ways to end this. Let's see if we live long enough to try mine."

Aldric took her hand — openly, before both armies, before God and the dead — and held it.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hope it's yours."

Above them, the bones turned, and turned, and the light began, very slowly, to fail.
