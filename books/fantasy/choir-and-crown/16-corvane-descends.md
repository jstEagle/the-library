## 16. Corvane Descends

The mountain told him they were coming before his scouts did.

Corvane had learned, in thirty years of inquiry, to listen to the small treacheries of the world — the way birds went quiet along a certain ridge, the way snow slid from a branch with no wind to move it. The Thornfells were full of such small betrayals now. The whole range was holding its breath.

He stood at the mouth of the lower pass with the warrant in his breast pocket and forty of the Queen's own behind him, and he let himself feel, for one unguarded moment, what he always felt at the end of a long hunt: nothing like triumph. Only the particular tiredness of a man who has been right too many times.

"Inquisitor." Sergeant Ilsa Bray, his shadow these six years, came up the scree with her helm under her arm and frost in her lashes. "The smuggler's toll-gate is abandoned. Gatekeeper gone. But there's a fire still warm in the grate."

"Then we are no more than a day behind them," Corvane said. "Perhaps less. They'll have stopped somewhere dark and safe to rest, because everyone stops somewhere dark and safe to rest, and that is where we will find them."

"You say that as if people were arithmetic."

"People are arithmetic," Corvane said mildly. "It's mercy that requires imagination."

He turned back up the trail. Behind him, the Queen's soldiers shouldered their gear — pitch, oil, the long iron braziers built for exactly this purpose, though none of them knew yet for what. He had not told them. He would tell them when it was time, and they would do it, because the warrant bore Maren's seal and Maren's seal had never once been refused, and that was the whole quiet miracle of her reign: a usurper who ruled so cleanly that obedience had become a kind of faith.

He only wished she understood what she had signed.

* * *

They made camp that night in the drowned chapel — not the same one Vesper Amberdale had sheltered in, though Corvane did not know that; he knew only that the chapel sat where three trails met, and that its flooded nave reflected the stars so perfectly that his soldiers kept looking down instead of up, as if the sky had been moved beneath them without anyone's permission.

Corvane sat apart on the altar steps with his boots off, drying his feet by the small permitted fire, and let the memory come, because it always came in chapels, and because he had long ago stopped fighting it.

There had been another choir. Smaller. A valley called Wrenlow, east of the Ashwater, thirty-one years ago.

He had been young then — young enough to believe that the old orders were superstition dressed in ceremony, that the bone-choirs scattered through the mountains were relics of a crueler age, prisons where dead men's grief was milked like goats to power wards no one needed anymore. He had been an apprentice inquisitor with a scholar's contempt and a reformer's certainty, and he had found Wrenlow: a vault beneath a hillside shrine where perhaps two hundred voices sang, anchored to ward-stones that served nothing but a village well-shrine and a boundary fence.

Two hundred voices. He had counted them. He had stood among the suspended bones with his lamp raised and listened, and what he heard had seemed unbearable — grief without end, sorrow looped like rope around a capstan, turning and turning to warm bathwater and light candles. He had thought: this is slavery. He had thought: no god worth the name asks this.

So he had freed them.

It had taken him four nights of careful work, alone, cutting the anchor-bones free with a ritual he reconstructed from forbidden texts, releasing each voice into the open air of the valley. And for a moment — he would remember this all his life, the way a man remembers the last breath before drowning — for one shining moment it had been beautiful. The singing rose out of the hillside like dawn given sound. The villagers came out of their houses weeping, saying they could hear their grandmothers, saying the valley smelled like bread.

By morning, the first of them had stopped speaking.

By the third day, they had begun to wander. Not mad, exactly — worse than mad. *Inhabited.* The freed memories had nowhere to go, you see. A soul released from its anchor does not ascend; it drifts, hungry and formless, seeking any mind soft enough to hold it. And every human mind is soft enough. The dead of Wrenlow poured into the living of Wrenlow the way water pours into a broken boat, and within a week there was no one left in the valley who could say with certainty whose memories were theirs. A farmer wept for a wife he'd never had. Children spoke in the voices of soldiers drowned two centuries before they were born. One old woman walked into the river on the ninth day, smiling, calling herself by eleven names.

Corvane had burned the vault on the twelfth day. All of it. Every anchor-bone, every scrap of carved memory, with the survivors standing in a silent row watching him work, wearing expressions he could not read because those expressions belonged to other people entirely.

Forty-one survivors out of a village of ninety. He had found homes for them far away, in places where no one would ask about their eyes. He had written his report honestly — the only honest report of his early career — and the old Grand Inquisitor had read it, and looked at him for a long time, and said:

"Now you understand why we leave them singing."

And Corvane had spent thirty years making sure he never forgot it.

* * *

"Can't sleep either?"

Sergeant Bray settled onto the steps beside him, uninvited, which was a liberty only she took. She was a broad, weathered woman from the coast country, and she had followed him since the Harrow Affair, since the day he'd ordered her to arrest a beloved magistrate and then shown her, patiently, the ledgers proving the man had sold grain futures against his own famine-relief fund. She trusted him the way soldiers trust surgeons: completely, and with a certain grim gratitude that it isn't their job.

"I sleep fine," Corvane said. "I just don't waste the night."

"What's down there, sir? Truly. You've not told us. Pitch and braziers and the Queen's seal on a burn-order, and half the men think we're chasing treasure."

"We are chasing treasure," Corvane said. "That's the difficulty. It's real treasure, the kind that works. And it must be burned anyway."

Bray was quiet a moment. Below them, the flooded nave held its false sky. "You've seen something like this before."

"Yes."

"And it went badly."

"It went *perfectly*," Corvane said. "That's the lesson no one wants. If it had gone badly — if my ritual had failed, if the anchors had simply held — I could have lived with being a fool. Instead it worked exactly as designed, and I watched a valley die of it. That is what I am carrying down this mountain, Sergeant. Not doubt. Memory."

She studied him sideways. "The Queen believes this song keeps her peace. That's what the whisperers at court say. Wards fed by something old under the Thornfells."

"The Queen believes correctly." He pulled his boots back on, lacing them with the care of a man arming himself. "Every ward-stone in Cael Maris draws from a single source, and the source is a cavern of the war-dead, singing their grief into stone until it becomes law. That is why no border raid has crossed the marches in a century. That is why Maren's reign holds despite everything she took to win it. The dead are holding the kingdom together, Sergeant. They have been doing it so long that the living have forgotten the dead exist."

"Then wouldn't destroying it break the peace?"

"Yes," Corvane said. "For a time. Perhaps a year of hard winters and hard questions. Perhaps worse." He looked out at the black mass of the Thornfells above them, at the pass they would climb at dawn. "But here is what the royalists don't understand, and what your Queen only half understands, and what the bone-carver — if she is what I think she is — refuses to understand at all. Peace that is powered by stolen suffering is not peace. It is a loan. And the interest comes due eventually, all at once, in a currency no one can pay. Better to default now, deliberately, with our eyes open, than to let the debt mature over our children's heads."

"That's a cold sermon, sir."

"It's the only honest one." He almost smiled. "I am not a cruel man, Bray. People think that, because of the burning. But cruelty is easy. What I am proposing is the hardest thing I have ever done twice. Ten thousand voices, Bray. Perhaps more. And I am going to stand in the middle of them and put the torch to the anchors while they scream, because the alternative — letting them loose — I have already seen the alternative. I have buried the alternative."

Bray said nothing for a while. Then, quietly: "Does it help? Telling yourself it's mercy?"

"No," Corvane said. "And that is how you know it isn't a lie."

* * *

They climbed at first light, and the mountain fought them the whole way, and Corvane could not shake the sense that it was fighting *for* someone.

The smuggler roads were a lattice of switchbacks and cut tunnels, some ancient beyond record, and twice they found signs of passage — boot-prints in melt-slush, a dropped strip of jerky still soft. Once, wedged in a crack of rock beside the trail, he found a curl of bone shaving, pale and fresh, and he held it in his gloved palm for a long moment before pocketing it.

He did not listen to it. That was the discipline. Inquisitors of his rank were trained in the listening arts — how else to interrogate the dead for testimony? — but he had not listened to a carving since Wrenlow. He carried the voices he had already heard; he did not collect others. Even now, in the deep quiet of the high passes, he could sometimes feel them at the edge of his mind like weather: two hundred souls, or what remained of them, drifting in the architecture of his memory. Some men broke under that. He had simply grown a room inside himself where they lived, and he visited it nightly the way other men pray.

"They're close," Bray said on the second day, coming back from the point. "Toll-gate again — abandoned like the last one, but this time the gatekeeper left a mark on the lintel. Three lines and a circle. Old sign, the smugglers use it. Means 'the debt is paid' — travelers passed freely."

"Paid by whom?"

"That's the strange part, sir. There's no payment recorded. Just the sign, and under it, scratched smaller—" she hesitated, "—a word. *Keeper.*"

Something cold moved through Corvane that had nothing to do with the altitude. He thought of the map — the bone-cased map that had cost two couriers their lives and set three factions climbing the same frozen mountain — and of the margins he had seen described in a captured letter: annotations in a woman's hand, decades old, marking tunnels that appeared on no survey. Annotations by the Choir's last keeper.

Who had a daughter in Greyhollow who carved bones.

"Then the bloodline has come home," he said softly. "Of course. It always comes home. That's what a keeper is — not a profession. An inheritance." He started up the trail again, faster now. "Move the men. We're hours behind, not days."

"Sir — what happens when we find them?"

Corvane considered the question honestly, because Bray deserved honesty.

"When we find them," he said, "the carver will be standing in front of ten thousand screaming dead with a chisel in her hand, and the knight will be standing in front of her with his sword drawn, and the royalists will be arriving from the other side with their binding-circles chalked, and every one of them will believe — truly believe — that they are saving the world." He adjusted the warrant in his breast pocket, feeling its weight like a coal. "And I will be standing there with the Queen's fire, believing the same thing. That is what makes it tragedy, Sergeant. Not villains. Arithmetic."

* * *

He heard the Choir before he saw it.

They had descended past the tree line into the roots of the Thornfells, into tunnels lit by lichen-light, and the sound came up through the stone like water through a sponge — a vast, layered humming, grief given grammar, ten thousand voices braided into a single sustained note that was somehow also ten thousand separate notes, each one distinct if you leaned toward it, each one pleading.

His soldiers stopped in the tunnel and crossed themselves, or made the older signs, or simply stood with their mouths open. One of the braziers slipped from a man's grip and rang against the rock, and even that clang seemed swallowed, absorbed, folded into the song.

Corvane closed his eyes.

He had expected it to be worse. It was worse. Two hundred voices at Wrenlow had been a choir; this was a sea. He could feel it pressing against the walls of his inner room, testing the door, finding the seams. Somewhere in that vastness were voices that remembered Wrenlow — the war had touched everywhere — and whether they recognized what he had done or merely grieved it, he could not tell, and did not try. He opened his eyes and gave the order in a steady voice.

"Braziers to the fore. Oil to the anchors. No one touches the hanging bones." He drew the warrant from his breast pocket and held it up so the seal caught the lichen-light. "By the authority of Queen Maren, first of her name by conquest and by competence, this place is condemned. We are here to end the singing."

And that was when the light ahead changed — a warmer light, lamplight, moving — and figures resolved out of the cavern's glow: a woman with a carver's roll slung across her back, a tall knight with one arm bound to his chest, and between them, at the mouth of the great vault itself, a bench. A simple workman's bench, scarred and candle-lit, with bone shavings drifted around it like snow.

The woman stepped forward, and Corvane saw her clearly for the first time — saw her mother's hands, though he could not have known it, saw the particular stillness of someone who had spent her life listening to things other people refused to hear.

"You're the Inquisitor," she said. Not a question. "You're here to burn them."

"I'm here to free them," Corvane said, and meant it, and hated that he meant it, "the only way freedom doesn't kill everyone it touches."

Behind her, deeper in the cavern, the song swelled — and in it, threaded thin but unmistakable, he began to hear names. Individual names. Being spoken aloud, one after another, in a living voice, in a rhythm he recognized from forbidden texts: the naming-rite, the carving of anchors, the third way that existed in no doctrine he had ever sworn to enforce.

The woman lifted a chisel.

"Then you'd best come and see what freedom costs," she said, "before you light your fires — because I've found another price, Inquisitor, and I'm paying it. Name by name. And I need every hand you brought."

The mountain held its breath.

Corvane looked at the warrant in his hand, and then at the bench, and for the first time in thirty years, the arithmetic would not resolve.
