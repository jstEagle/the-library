## 17. Three Hands on One Chisel

The vault had three doors, and death was coming through all of them.

Vesper felt them before she saw them — the way the ten thousand voices in the hanging bones changed pitch, the way a room changes when a stranger enters it. Above, through the throat of the mountain where they'd descended, came the scrape and torchlight of Lord Cassel's royalists, their chanting already beginning, a binding-ritual in the old tongue that made the nearest ward-stones shudder like struck bells. Behind, from the flooded gallery where Corvane's party had entered with the Queen's warrant, the smell of lamp-oil — barrels of it, rolled on timber skids, enough to burn bone to ash and the grief inside it to nothing.

And beneath her feet, in the deep places where no one had walked in a hundred years, something else. A tremor. The mountain itself shifting its weight, as if the wards above the capital were threads and somewhere a weaver had let go of one end.

"They're going to light it," Aldric said. He stood between Vesper and the oil-men, sword drawn but lowered, his broken arm bound in a sling of smuggler's linen, his face gray with pain he refused to name. "Corvane won't wait for Cassel's ritual. They'll kill each other over who gets to end this."

"Let them," said one of the smugglers at the rear — Brannic, the toll-gate keeper's cousin, who had followed them down out of what he claimed was debt and what Vesper suspected was awe. "Burnt bones don't sing. Burnt bones don't crown anyone either."

"No." The word came out of Vesper before she had decided anything, before she had even finished hearing the voices. She pressed her palm flat against the nearest suspended femur, and the dead woman within it sang against her skin — not words anymore, not after so long in the dark, just the shape of a name worn smooth by repetition. *Maren. No—* she caught herself, listening harder. Not the queen's name. *Marren. Marren of the Fenmarch. I had a daughter. Did I have a daughter? I can't remember her face, only the name, why can't I only remember the face—*

Ten thousand souls, conscious still, still singing, because singing was the only thing left to them. And the two armies of the living below were arguing about whether to enslave them or erase them.

"There's a third way," Vesper said.

Every head turned. Even the voices seemed to lean in, a hush running through the hanging bones like wind through a winter forest.

* * *

She had known it since the Keeper's Door, if she was honest. Since her mother's skeleton had pressed the chisel into her hands and her mother's voice had come up through twenty years of stillness: *The song holds because it has nowhere else to be. Give them somewhere else to be.*

A voice needs an anchor. That was the whole of bone-crafting, the whole of her gift, the whole secret she had spent her life half-understanding. A carving gave the dead a place to live — a whistle, a keepsake, a name cut into living bone — and in that place they could speak, could rest, could finally finish whatever they had been saying when they died. The vault did the same thing, but cruelly: it anchored ten thousand souls to stone and fed their grief upward to power the wards of a kingdom that had forgotten they existed.

But an anchor didn't have to be a prison. An anchor could be a grave. Could be a memorial. Could be a hall of names where the dead were honored instead of harvested.

"Bone," Vesper said now, walking the circle of the cavern floor, her voice pitched to carry. "Fresh bone. Carved. Every soul in this vault gets a name cut into it — their true name, spoken into the carving the way I spoke the courier's memory free. Then the anchor is theirs, not the Choir's. They can leave the stone. They can go quiet. They can rest."

Cassel's binding-chant faltered somewhere above. Good. Let him listen.

"And who carves ten thousand names?" It was Cassel himself, descending the last stair with his lords behind him, his voice rich with the patience of a man addressing something simple. He was older than she'd imagined, silver-bearded, handsome in the way old portraits are handsome. In his hands he carried a crown — an actual crown, iron-banded, ugly with age, and Vesper understood with a lurch that he had brought it down here, into the dark, ready to set it on whatever king the song would make. "One carver, one knife? You'd die of old age before you freed a tenth of them. And while you carve, girl, the wards fail, and everything your queen—" he spat the word "—has built comes down on ten thousand heads."

"The wards are failing anyway." She nodded upward, toward the tremor none of them could feel and all of them could hear now, a low groan in the roots of the mountain. "You've heard it. So has Corvane. The song is ending whether we act or not. The question is what happens to these people when it does." She turned to face the oil-barrels, and to Corvane, who stood beside them with a lit taper held loosely, almost gently, in his gloved hand. "If you burn them, they don't rest. Unanchored memory doesn't vanish — you know that better than anyone. It spreads. It finds the living."

Corvane's face didn't change, but his hand tightened on the taper. There it is, she thought. The valley he told us about. The plague of unanchored memories. He's seen what fire leaves behind, and he lights it anyway because the alternative frightens him more.

"You propose," Corvane said quietly, "to do manually, over weeks, what the vault does automatically. While standing in a collapsing cavern between two armed factions."

"I propose," Vesper said, "that we start."

Silence. Ten thousand voices humming. The mountain groaning. Somewhere above, faint as memory, the first ward-stone in the capital cracked — she couldn't have known that, but she knew it anyway, the way you know a bell has broken by the sudden wrongness of the quiet.

Then Aldric did the most astonishing thing Vesper had ever seen him do. He crossed the cavern floor, knelt at her improvised workbench — a fallen ward-stone, flat-topped, her tools laid out on it in their worn leather roll — and unslung his broken arm from its linen. "Set it again if you must," he said. "But take the splint. It's good bone. Hollow, seasoned. Better than starting raw."

"It's your arm."

"My arm will heal around whatever you put in it." His eyes held hers, and there was no apology in them, only the fierce practicality of a man who had decided something down to the marrow. "I told you once I follow you to understand the map. I understand it now. Take the splint, Vesper."

She took it. Her hands were steady. Later she would shake; later, when there was time, when the mountain stopped screaming — but not now. Now there was work.

* * *

The first name took four minutes. Marren of the Fenmarch, carved into the hollow of Aldric's splint-bone, her true name spoken aloud as the blade-bit bit: *Marren, daughter of Hessa, fen-witch, who drowned at Cael Ford and whose daughter's face she could not remember.* When the final stroke closed the name, the suspended femur above them went silent — not empty, Veper felt, but *released*, the way a held breath releases. Somewhere beyond the stone, beyond the dark, a woman who had forgotten her daughter's face for a hundred years finally got to remember it in peace.

Brannic made a sound like a man being shown the sea for the first time.

"The smugglers' roads run through this whole range," Vesper said, already selecting the next blank bone, not looking up. "Your toll-gates keep ledgers. Your people know every hollow and dry cave between here and Greyhollow. I need bone — cattle bone, deer bone, anything fresh enough to hold a cutting. And I need hands."

"Hands that can carve?"

"Hands that can hold. I'll teach the cutting. Anyone who can stitch a wound can learn a letter-form in an hour." She looked up at last, and found she was speaking to perhaps forty people — Cassel's lords and men-at-arms, Corvane's inquisitors with their oil-barrels abandoned, smugglers, Aldric kneeling at her bench with his ruined arm cradled like something precious. Forty living souls, and above them ten thousand dead ones, and between them a chisel.

Lord Cassel had not moved. He stood beneath the hanging choir with the iron crown in his hands, and Vesper watched him do arithmetic — the same smuggler's arithmetic she'd done on the mountain roads, weighing costs — and watched him arrive at the answer she feared.

"No," he said.

"My lord—" began one of his captains.

"I said *no*." The crown rose, and for one terrible moment Vesper thought he would put it on, right there, in front of the dead, and claim the song by presence alone. But the voices had gone strange around him — the released silence spreading outward from Marren's femur like a hole in fabric, and the remaining nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine singing *louder* to fill it, and the sound went through Cassel like cold water. He flinched. Everyone flinched. Only Vesper, who had lived her whole life inside other people's deaths, stood steady in it.

"A hundred years," Cassel said, quieter now, and there was something almost human under the fury — grief, maybe, old and calcified. "My grandfather fought at Cael Ford. My family built this peace. You would tear it down with a *chisel*."

"Your grandfather fought at Cael Ford?" Vesper asked. "What was his name?"

"What?"

"His name. Do you know it? Have you ever spoken it?" She held his gaze. "Because there's a man in those bones who fought at Cael Ford, my lord, and no one has spoken his name in a century, and he is so tired. He is so tired, and your peace is made of his tiredness, and I am asking you to let him sleep." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it crack. Craft, her mother had taught her, was honesty applied with a sharp edge. "Help me carve. Or stand aside. Or draw your sword on me, and explain to these ten thousand witnesses why the dead deserve your crown more than their names."

The mountain groaned again, longer this time. Dust sifted from the vaulted dark. And Corvane — courteous, terrifying Corvane, who had burned nothing and threatened no one and gotten everything he ever wanted — set down his taper, picked up an oil-barrel, upended it over the stones, and then, deliberately, in front of everyone, walked away from it.

"I'll need clean bone," he said, rolling up his sleeves. "I'm told I have steady hands."

* * *

They worked.

Gods, how they worked. Vesper had never imagined labor like it — not craft, not art, but something closer to harvest, or to triage. She sat at the ward-stone bench and carved and spoke, carved and spoke, her voice going hoarse by the second hour, her fingers bleeding by the third, and around her the cavern filled with the strange music of many blades: Cassel's lords learning letter-forms on scrap bone, cursing, breaking tips, learning again. Corvane working with the terrible precision of a man doing penance, each stroke exact, each name spoken in a clear inquisitor's voice as if filing it with heaven itself. Smugglers feeding fresh bone down the tunnels in baskets — deer and elk and mountain goat, anything with marrow and memory-room. Brannic keeping the ledger, reading out names in rotation so the carvers never touched the same soul twice.

And through it all, the singing thinned. Voice by voice, femur by femur, rib by rib, the great chord of the Hollow Choir resolved itself into single notes, then into rests, then into a silence that grew not emptier but *fuller* — the silence of a room where everyone has finally gone home.

Aldric worked one-handed at her side, holding bones steady while she cut, and when her blade slipped on the ninth hour and opened her thumb he bound it without a word, and when his own knees buckled from blood-loss and exhaustion she leaned him against the ward-stone and kept carving with his shoulder warm against hers, and neither of them said anything about love because there wasn't time, because love could wait, because the dead could not.

It was near midnight — she judged by her body, not by any sun — when the counting went wrong.

"Forty-one," Brannic called from the ledger. "We're forty-one short. Names released don't match names carved."

Vesper looked up at the hanging bones. Thousands still sang — they had barely begun, truly; days remained, weeks maybe, and the mountain had begun to shake in earnest, cracks crawling across the vault ceiling like frost on glass, and dust fell in curtains now, and somewhere far above she knew the capital's wards were guttering street by street, quarter by quarter—

Forty-one short.

She counted herself. Recounted. And understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the mountain's depths.

Someone wasn't releasing. Someone was *taking*. Somewhere among her forty carvers, a name spoken into bone was not freeing a soul but binding it tighter — siphoning the song, note by stolen note, into something small enough to hide, something portable, something shaped like—

Her eyes found the iron crown. It lay on Cassel's bench, where he'd set it down to carve.

It lay there. But the man carving beside it was not Cassel.

"Cassel's been gone an hour," Aldric breathed, following her gaze. "Who—"

The false lord raised his head, and smiled with Cassel's face, and around him, forty-one stolen voices screamed silently inside the iron bands of a crown that was drinking the Choir one soul at a time.
