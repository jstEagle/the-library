## 18. The Long Carving

The mountain had begun to sing back.

Vesper felt it first through her bench—a shiver in the old smuggler's table she'd dragged to the vault's mouth, a tremor like a plucked string that never quite went still. Above them, far above, the wards of the capital were failing one by one, and the failure came down through the stone the way water comes down through a roof: slowly at first, then all at once, then everywhere.

She did not look up. There was no time to look up. Her chisel moved, and her voice moved with it, and before her on the bench lay a fresh length of bone, pale and waiting, and into it she carved the two hundred and eleventh name since they had begun.

*Maera Thistlewood, who died holding a door.*

The bone took the name the way dry earth takes rain. Somewhere in the cavern behind her, among the ten thousand suspended dead, a voice that had been screaming for three hundred years went suddenly, blessedly quiet—and Vesper felt its silence settle over her like a hand laid gently on the back of her neck. She breathed. She reached for the next bone.

* * *

It should not have worked. Every one of them knew it should not have worked, and they worked anyway.

The truce, if it deserved the word, had been born in desperation and sealed in exhaustion. Lord Cassel's royalists had come down into the vault with drawn steel; Corvane's inquisitors had come down with fire-oil and warrants; and between them, in the narrow throat of the Keeper's Door, Vesper had stood with blood on her sleeves and a chisel in her fist and made her impossible proposal—that every name be given an anchor outside the prison, that no soul be bound and none be burned, that the work would take days they did not have.

Corvane had looked at the failing wards—at the hairline cracks of light crawling across the great ward-stones like frost across a window—and he had done the arithmetic himself, aloud, in his soft terrible voice. *Burn it now,* he said, *and the memories release unanchored. I have seen what that does. Bind it, and we build another prison. Carve it—* and here he had paused, and Vesper had watched him hate the words even as he spoke them—*carve it, and perhaps we are all wrong together.*

Cassel had agreed for his own reasons. Let the girl exhaust herself, his silence said. When the carving fails, when the wards collapse and the realm burns, the crown will fall to whoever stands ready. He would wait. He was very good at waiting.

And so the bench was built, and the work began, and by the second day there were eleven people at it who had every reason in the world to kill each other.

* * *

Aldric worked the bellows.

It was, he thought, the only job left to him that required more stubbornness than skill—one hand working the treadle, the ruined arm bound tight against his chest where Vesper had set the bone in the flooded chapel what felt like a lifetime ago. The forge they'd built at the vault's mouth softened the bones so they could take a carving without splitting, and Aldric fed it, and watched, and counted.

He counted everything now. It was a soldier's habit gone strange. He counted the bones in the rack: forty-one. He counted the carvers: Vesper at the bench, always; a smuggler woman named Brack who'd followed the toll-gate keeper down and turned out to carve names with the brutal efficiency of someone marking crates; two of Cassel's men, sullen and careful, who had been ordered to assist and assisted because the alternative was admitting they were afraid. He counted Corvane's inquisitors, who stood at the edge of the firelight with their fire-oil untouched and their faces unreadable, and he counted the hours since the last tremor, which had been fewer than the hours since the tremor before that.

And he counted, though he would not say it aloud, the number of times Vesper had looked at him across the bench in the last day: four. Each time, something in her face had asked a question, and each time he had answered it with a nod, and neither of them had needed words for it. There would be time for words later, or there would not, and either way the work came first. She had taught him that without meaning to. The dead came first. Everything else was weather.

"Knight." One of the smugglers—Brack, her hands black to the wrist with bone-dust—jerked her chin at his bellows. "Slower. You're cooking them."

"I'm feeding a forge."

"You're feeding a funeral," Brack said, not unkindly. "Slow down. They waited three hundred years. They can wait for a proper softening."

Aldric slowed down. It occurred to him, distantly, that a year ago he would have struck a man—or a woman—for speaking to him so, would have heard it as insolence from someone beneath his station. Now it sounded like sense. Disgrace, he was beginning to understand, was mostly a change of hearing.

* * *

Vesper lost track of time entirely somewhere around the fourth hundredth name.

This was the danger, and she knew it, and she walked the knife's edge of it deliberately. To carve a name was to hear the whole of a life compressed into a single utterance—not just the death but the living, the whole person passing through her on their way out of the prison. Maera Thistlewood had held a door for six hours while children fled behind her, and Vesper lived those six hours in the space of one breath. Then the next bone. Then the next life. A farmer who had drowned in his own fields when the war turned the rivers to weapons. A boy of twelve who had carried messages and been killed for carrying the wrong one. A woman whose last act had been to bite the hand that killed her, and who was, three centuries later, still fiercely proud of it.

They passed through her and out, freed, and each departure took something small from her and left something else behind. She was becoming a ledger. She could feel herself becoming a ledger. And still she carved, because the alternative was on the other side of the knife's edge, in the dark: stop, and the wards fail, and ten thousand unanchored memories flood a kingdom like a burst dam.

"You're bleeding."

She surfaced. Corvane stood beside the bench—he had a way of arriving without arriving—and was looking at her hands with an expression she might almost have mistaken for concern if she hadn't known better. His inquisitors' gloves were off. He was holding a bone.

"You're carving," she said.

"I am correcting my error." He turned the bone in the light. "I burned a choir once. A small one. I told you." His voice was level, courteous, awful. "I have spent nineteen years telling myself it was mercy. Sit down before you fall down, Amberdale. Your hands are needed steady, and mine are unused to anything else."

She sat. She let him take the chisel. And when the Inquisitor of the Crown bent over the bench and carved *Dennor Hale, who died singing to frighten the enemy and succeeded* into softened bone, his hand did not shake, and the name took cleanly, and Vesper felt the man Dennor Hale go free like a bird released from a closed room.

Enemies become witnesses, she thought. Or perhaps it was simpler than that. Perhaps grief was the one country everyone was already a citizen of.

* * *

The tremors came closer together as the second day wore into the third.

Each one shook grit from the vault's high ceiling, and the suspended bones—the thousands still waiting, hanging in their slow terrible constellation—swayed and clacked against one another with a sound like distant applause. The remaining voices grew louder. Not angrier. Louder, the way a crowd grows louder as it realizes the doors are opening. Hope, Vesper thought, has a sound, and it is indistinguishable from panic.

Brack stopped carving and pressed both palms flat to the floor. "That one came up through the rock," she said. "Not along the tunnels. Through the rock. That's not settling. That's something structural."

"The wards are failing faster than the carving can replace them," Corvane said quietly. He did not look up from his work. "We knew this. Continue."

"We'll never finish," one of Cassel's soldiers said. His name was Pell. His hands had been shaking since the first hour, but he hadn't stopped carving either. "There's thousands left. Thousands. At this rate—"

"At this rate," Vesper said, taking the next bone, "we finish, or the mountain finishes us mid-sentence, and either way the sentence gets finished. Next."

But privately, in the cold ledger-room of her mind where she kept the things she did not say aloud, she had done the same arithmetic. They were losing. The carving was winning battles and losing the war, each freed name bought at the cost of a tremor that undid a little more of the ceiling, a little more of the stones, a little more time. Unless something changed, they would get perhaps half of the dead free before the vault came down and took the rest into unanchored darkness.

Unless something changed.

* * *

Lord Cassel had waited three days, and his patience was a masterpiece.

He had supervised. He had offered supplies—candles, oil, the good steel chisels from his own saddlebags, which had surprised everyone including his own men. He had spoken rarely and reasonably and had positioned himself, always, near the rack of finished bones, the ones carved and named and freed, stacked in neat rows like sheaves awaiting a harvest.

No one noticed that he counted them too. No one but Aldric, who had learned counting in the same school.

On the third night, when the tremors came so close together that the work never fully stilled between them, and Brack and the smugglers had gone up-tunnel to shore the entrance with timber, and Corvane's inquisitors slept in shifts against the far wall, and Vesper carved on in a trance of exhaustion with her eyes half-closed and her hands moving by memory—on that night, Lord Cassel finally acted.

He did not draw a blade. Blades made noise, and noise brought knights. Instead he took up the finished bones, quietly, a handful at a time, and carried them to the deep fissure at the vault's eastern edge—the one that had opened with the latest tremor, breathing cold air from depths below—and he dropped them in.

Freed bones. Named bones. Bones whose anchors, newly cut, were shallow things, young things, needing days to set firm. Without their bone, the anchor dissolved. The memory returned to the dark, unanchored after all—but worse than unanchored, because now it knew hope. Now it had tasted the door.

Aldric found him on his fourth trip.

* * *

Later, no one could agree on how it began. Later, when the tale was told in Greyhollow taverns and eventually embroidered into the kind of story that gets told with gestures, the details blurred. But the shape of it stayed true.

Aldric saw the movement at the fissure's edge and understood in one cold instant what he was seeing—not theft, not sabotage of the work, but murder. Ten murders, twenty, the same souls murdered twice, and worse: every dropped bone weakened the pattern, because the anchors were meant to stand together, a choir, each name holding up the next. Cassel wasn't stealing bones. He was pulling threads.

"Cassel."

The lord turned. In the red light of the forge-fire, his face arranged itself into regret with the ease of long practice. "Ser Aldric. Keep your voice down. The girl is nearly asleep on her feet—I'm merely tidying."

"You're dropping them into the dark."

"I'm preparing for the inevitable." Cassel's voice stayed gentle, reasonable, the voice of a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "The carving fails, Aldric. Look at the ceiling. We are rearranging deck-chairs on a sinking ship, and when it sinks, the strong will need leverage. A few hundred anchored names in reserve—bound to a claimant, sung into legitimacy—that is not villainy. That is statesmanship. You of all people should understand. You swore to the bloodline once."

"I swore to the *realm*," Aldric said, "and then I watched what men like you do in its name, and I unswore myself. Those aren't leverage. They're people. They're *finished*, Cassel. They're free."

"They're *bones*."

"They were soldiers," Aldric said. "Like us."

And Cassel smiled—truly smiled, for the first time, and it was worse than all his gentleness—and stepped back to the fissure's edge with the armful of finished bones held out over the dark, over the breathing cold, like a man offering a ransom.

"Then let us negotiate," he said. "One step, knight, and they all go down. You have one good arm and I have—"

Aldric did not negotiate. He crossed the distance in four strides, and when Cassel swung the bones away he lunged past them, past the blade clearing Cassel's sheath, past reason, past self-preservation, and got his good hand on the lord's wrist—the wrist over the fissure, the wrist holding thirty freed souls—and held.

And Cassel, stronger, fresher, two-handed, twisted.

The sound was small. That was the horror of it, Aldric thought afterward, in the white rooms of pain: how *small* the sound was. A wet crack, barely louder than a knuckle popping. His hand—his sword-hand, his shield-hand, the hand that had held a banner at Redford Field and a bride's hand for one summer and a sword for twenty years—came away in his grip, and the bones in that grip stayed gripped, held fast by fingers that no longer answered to him, and Cassel stumbled back from the fissure empty-handed and screaming, and Aldric fell to his knees holding what remained of his own hand wrapped around thirty rescued names.

The vault went silent except for the ringing.

Every carver had stopped. Corvane stood with a knife already out that no one had seen him draw. Vesper was on her feet, swaying, staring. And Cassel backed against the far wall clutching his ruined wrist, his face gray, his voice climbing toward the register of a man about to order his remaining soldiers to kill everyone in the room—

"—hold," said Corvane.

It was not loud. It did not need to be. The inquisitors had risen, and their fire-oil flasks were in their hands, and Corvane's soft eyes moved from Cassel to the fissure to Aldric kneeling in the forge-light with his own severed hand clenched around a bundle of bones like a man praying.

"Lord Cassel," Corvane said gently, "you have just attempted to cast thirty anchored souls into the unanchored dark. By my warrant, that is a crime against the Crown's peace greater than any treason I have ever prosecuted." He tilted his head. "I find I need to sit down. Ser Pell—bind his wrists. Gag him. If he speaks again before this work is done, I will burn him where he stands, and I will feel nothing, and I want you all to remember that I told you so."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night.

Vesper set what could be set of Aldric's wrist—there was little enough, the stump clean and cauterized against the forge—and he refused the poppy draught twice and accepted it the third time, and even drugged, he would not leave the bellows. He worked the treadle with his feet and cradled the bundle of rescued bones in the crook of his bound arm, and he would not give them up, not to the rack, not to anyone, until Vesper had re-carved each name fresh, deep, doubly anchored, whispering them back into safety one by one while he watched with unfocused eyes.

*Holden Marsh, who died protecting strangers.*

*Yeva Cort, who died laughing at them.*

*Tomlin Ash, who died afraid, and it was allowed.*

Thirty names restored. Thirty souls that had been one breath from falling back into the dark, pulled back over the edge by a hand that no longer existed. When the last one settled, Vesper came to him and knelt, and took his bandaged stump in both her hands, and pressed her forehead to it, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying that the gesture didn't say better.

"You fool," she whispered, finally, into the linen.

"Mm," said Aldric, drifting.

"I had two hands and you had one, and now—" Her voice caught. She mastered it, the way she mastered everything, by force. "Now you've got none worth the name, and I swear to you, Aldric, if you die of this—"

"You'll carve my name?" He smiled, half-asleep, terrible and tender. "Promise me it's a good one. Something with... with a title in it. Sir Aldric. No. Just Aldric. Who died..."

"Don't you dare finish that sentence."

"...who died *holding on*," he murmured, and slept.

* * *

The mountain shook them awake an hour before dawn.

This one was different. This one did not pass. It built—slowly, terribly, a groan rising through the rock like a note held on the world's largest instrument, and in the vault the ten thousand remaining voices rose with it, no longer a murmur but a roar, joy and terror indistinguishable, and the great ward-stones overhead cracked visibly, light pouring from the wounds like blood from a struck man.

Vesper ran to the rack and counted, hands flying, and the number she arrived at made her knees go weak.

Nine thousand, one hundred. Nine hundred names carved in three days of miracle-work. Nine thousand, one hundred still hanging in the dark above the fissure, and by the sound of the mountain, by the spreading web of cracks, by the way the air itself had begun to taste of dust and lightning—

They had hours. Perhaps less.

She turned to the others—to Corvane, already standing, already rolling up his sleeves with the grim calm of a man accepting a sentence; to Brack and the smugglers stumbling back down the tunnel with timber dust in their hair; to the gagged lord straining against his bonds; to Aldric, awake now, one-handed, rising to his feet beside the forge because sitting down had stopped being something his body remembered how to do.

"It wants to end," Vesper said. The roaring of the dead filled the vault, filled her, filled her voice until she hardly recognized it. "The vault. The song. All of it—it knows, and it wants to end, and if it ends wrong, nine thousand souls go into the dark unanchored, and I don't—" Her voice broke, and she let it. "I can't carve nine thousand names in hours. No one can."

"No one can," Corvane agreed softly. He picked up a chisel. "But then, no one could carve nine hundred, either, and yet."

Above them, the first ward-stone shattered, and the mountain screamed, and the dead sang louder—and in the red light of the forge, at the mouth of the Hollow Choir, eleven enemies picked up eleven chisels and began, impossibly, to carve.
