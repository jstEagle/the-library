## 19. When the Song Stops

The mountain had been shaking for three days, and by the last morning it no longer shook in pulses but in a long continuous groan, like an old man turning over in bed who could not decide whether to rise or die where he lay.

Vesper did not look up from the bench.

Her world had narrowed, over those days, to the width of a chisel blade and the length of a name. Around her the vault of the Hollow Choir roared and wept; dust fell in gray veils through the shafts of failing light; men who had come as enemies knelt at her side with bone dust on their hands and blood on their sleeves — royalists, inquisitors, smugglers, all of them reduced to the same humble labor: hold this, turn that, fetch water for the whetstone. But she heard none of it clearly. She was deep inside the work now, deeper than sleep, deeper than grief, in the place where her mother's voice lived in the marrow and the chisel was not a tool but a doorway.

*Marwen of the Ashfield levy*, said the bone under her hands.

She carved.

The letters came out of her without thought, each stroke a syllable, each curve a breath given back. The new bone — sheep femur, river-washed, bought from a smuggler boy at triple price and blessed by nobody — drank the ink of the old names and held them the way a cup holds water: openly, ready to be emptied into a living mouth.

*Marwen of the Ashfield levy,* sang the voice inside the vault's last standing cluster of suspended dead, *who died at the ford at seventeen, who never told his brother he forgave him, whose boots were too large and filled with river water—*

"I hear you," Vesper whispered, because they needed to hear it. All of them needed it. Ten thousand souls hanging in the dark of the Thornfells, strung up like lanterns by the first kings of the realm so that their endless grief could be bled off, drop by drop, to feed the wards of the capital — ten thousand people who were not dead at all, not really, only kept, only sung, only used. "I hear you. One more. You're almost home."

Somewhere behind her, Aldric shifted on his stool. He had been there all night, one-handed since Cassel's sabotage, holding the finished carvings steady while she worked because it was the only labor left to him and he refused to be sent away. She felt his eyes on her shoulders the way she felt sunlight — warm, distant, constant. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing left to say that the work itself did not say better.

* * *

The count stood at nine thousand eight hundred and forty-one when Corvane stopped pacing.

He had paced for two days, the Queen's warrant crumpled and re-smoothed in his fist, watching the mountain die above them and doing nothing, because there was nothing left for him to do. His fire was stacked at the vault mouth — oil, pitch, the dry bones of the smugglers' stores — enough to end it all in an hour, the mercy he had carried across half a kingdom, the mercy he had once believed was the only kindness left in the world. And he had not lit it. Vesper had watched him stand beside it a dozen times, watch him lift the flint, watch him set it down again.

Now he came to her bench and stood at the edge of the lamplight like a man at the edge of deep water.

"How many remain," he asked. It was not a question about numbers. It was a question about time.

"A hundred and fifty-nine." Her chisel did not stop. "Less if you stop talking."

He almost laughed. She saw it flicker across his ruined, gentle face and vanish. "When the last one is carved," he said, "what happens?"

"The song stops."

"And then?" His voice cracked down the middle, and out of the crack came something young. "I have seen what unanchored memory does, Amberdale. I freed four hundred voices from a war-choir in the Vale of Serris when I was younger than you are now, and within a season every soul in that valley walked in their sleep toward the mountains, weeping, calling names that weren't theirs. I buried sixty-two of them myself. So I am asking you, carver, with the roof coming down on our heads: what happens when ten thousand voices have nowhere to go?"

Vesper set down her chisel.

It was the first time in three days she had let herself stop, and the silence of her own hands rang louder than the mountain. She looked up at him — this courteous, terrible man who burned nothing and threatened no one and got everything, this man who was almost right, who had been almost right his whole life, which was the most dangerous thing a person could be.

"They go somewhere," she said. "That's what the carving is. That's what my mother understood and couldn't finish, and what you never understood at all." She lifted one of the finished bones — Marwen of the Ashfield levy, his name in her own hand, his death held in living sheep-bone instead of dead king's stone. "A memory needs a place to live. The Choir gave them a prison. Fire would give them nothing. This—" she turned the bone in the light "—this gives them a door. A door is not a cage, Inquisitor. A door opens both ways."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'll be wrong with them," she said, "and that will still be more honest than what your Queen has done for a hundred years."

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, deliberately, he took the flint from beside his pyre and put it in his pocket, and walked to the nearest unfinished bone, and picked up a graver, and began to cut letters in a hand far neater than hers.

Neither of them mentioned it afterward. Neither of them ever forgot it either.

* * *

Nine thousand nine hundred and two. Nine thousand nine hundred and sixty.

The vault was dying around them. Stalactites the size of cathedral columns sheared loose and fell into the flooded chapel below with sounds like the world's largest bells cracking. The suspended clusters swayed on their ancient cords of woven sinew and silver wire, ten thousand skeletons rocking gently in the dark, and their singing changed — Vesper could feel it changing even as she carved — from the endless droning grief that had powered the capital's wards for a century into something ragged, individual, alive. They were no longer singing to be used. They were singing because they knew.

*We know,* said a woman's voice, very close, very clear. *We know what you're doing. We can feel the doors.*

"Then walk through them," Vesper gasped between strokes. Sweat ran into her eyes and stung like salt in a wound. Her fingers were split and bandaged, the bandages soaked red at the fingertips, and Aldric kept trying to bind them and she kept pulling away because there was no time, there had never been any time, there was only the count and the falling stone.

*Nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight.*

Cassel's binding circle still scarred the floor at the far end of the vault — scorched sigils, broken chalk, the iron frame of a crown-shaped lattice that the hardliner lord had meant to lower over the Choir like a net. He was gone now. Aldric had seen to that, at the cost of his hand, and Vesper tried not to look at the stump bound in linen where a sword-hand used to be, tried not to think about how he had caught the sabotage knife meant for her throat, tried not to think about anything except the next name, the next name, the next name.

*Nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine.*

The last cluster hung before her: a single skeleton, small, its bones fused with calcite so that it glittered like frost. The oldest of them. The keeper's records called it the First Voice, though it had been a soldier once, a girl of the lowland spears, the first to be strung up when the first king decided that grief could be mined like ore.

*Ysolde,* said the map in her head, and her mother's voice beneath the map, and twenty years of silence beneath her mother's voice. *Ysolde of the Fen Marches.*

Vesper took up the fresh bone. Her hands were shaking now, truly shaking, and Aldric rose and steadied the blank femur against the bench with his remaining hand, and leaned close, and said quietly, in the voice of a knight swearing a vow:

"I'm here."

She carved.

Each letter went slow as a heartbeat. Y. S. O. L. D. E. The mountain screamed. Dust poured down like snowfall. Somewhere above them, leagues of rock and root and the whole sleeping weight of the Thornfells groaned toward collapse, and below them the flooded chapel rose in black waves against the walls, and none of it mattered, none of it existed, there was only the letter E, the final stroke, the smallest line she had ever drawn—

—and the bone warmed in her hands like a bird taken up from a nest.

* * *

They came through her all at once.

Not gradually. Not one by one, though she had imagined it that way a hundred times during the carving, rehearsed it, feared it. No — the last name sealed the last door open, and ten thousand souls poured through her as if she were a riverbank breaching, as if she were the gap in the dam, as if every ward-stone in the capital and every sinew-cord in the vault released at a single instant and chose her body for their road home.

She heard everything.

She heard Marwen of the Ashfield levy laugh for the first time since the ford, and call his brother's name, and the brother — alive, old, somewhere in a village south of Greyhollow — would wake tomorrow with tears on his face and forgiveness in his chest and never know why. She heard Ysolde of the Fen Marches put down a spear she had carried for a century. She heard a drummer boy hum a marching song and then, wonderingly, forget it. She heard mothers, and the children they had been searching for in the dark for a hundred years, finding each other midair like sparks meeting. She heard the whole war at once — not the history-book war, not the war of kings and borders, but the true one, the one made of mud and fear and someone's hand going cold in someone else's hand — and she heard it end. Not fade. *End.* The way a fever breaks. The way a bell goes silent after the final strike, holding all its sound inside the metal forever.

She heard her mother.

Not words. Her mother had passed through the Keeper's Door years ago, had gone ahead into whatever waited past the doors of the world, but Vesper felt her in the flood the way you feel a particular face in a crowd — a warmth with a shape she would know anywhere — and it touched her, briefly, on the way through, the way a hand touches a shoulder in passing, and it said nothing at all, and it said everything.

Then it was over.

The silence that followed was not empty. Vesper had spent her whole life believing silence was emptiness — the silence of Greyhollow nights, the silence after a carving, the silence where her mother should have been. This was different. This was the silence of a room after everyone has safely left it. Ten thousand voices, gone free, and the vault rang with the space they had occupied, vast and clean, like a church after the congregation departs, like lungs after a held breath finally released.

She was on the floor. She did not remember falling. Aldric was holding her, one arm around her back, his forehead pressed to hers, and his eyes were streaming and he was saying her name over and over, *Vesper, Vesper, stay with me, come back,* and above them the great cords snapped one after another like harp strings breaking, and the bones of the ten thousand came down gently through the dust, settling at last onto the stone floor of the vault that had been their prison, lying the way bones are meant to lie: together, in heaps, in company, at rest.

"The wards," she managed. Her voice came out scraped hollow. "The capital—"

"It's already done." That was Corvane, standing at the vault mouth with the warrant fallen from his fingers, looking out at the night sky through the splitting rock. His face wore an expression she had never seen on it and never expected to: peace. "Can't you feel it? It's not fire. Look."

She looked.

Through the widening cracks in the mountain's heart, far to the north, the lights of the capital were visible — a scatter of glow on the horizon like embers. And as they watched, the pale dome of light that had hovered over the city for a century — the ward-light, the grief-light, the light Maren's throne drank like wine — flickered. Flickered again. And went out.

And the city did not burn.

The city did not scream. The horizon simply darkened to honest night, ordinary night, the kind of night kingdoms had slept under before the first king learned to farm sorrow — and somewhere up there, Vesper knew, a hundred thousand people were waking in the dark and remembering things they had spent lifetimes forgetting. The war. The cost. The names. Every debt the wards had numbed, coming due all at once in the currency of truth.

"No war comes tonight," Corvane said softly, as if reading her thought. "War needs forgetting to take root. Tonight no one will be able to forget anything." He laughed — a broken, wondering sound. "I spent my whole life trying to protect people from memory. You've just shown me it was the forgetting that was killing us."

* * *

They climbed out of the Thornfells as dawn came, the last of them, moving through corridors that shuddered and shed stone. Behind them the Hollow Choir folded inward with a long exhalation, ten thousand resting bones entombed at last in the mountain's closing hand, and the smuggler tunnels that had matched her mother's carvings closed one by one behind them like a book shutting.

On the high shoulder of the pass, Vesper stopped and turned to look back.

Aldric stood beside her, gray-faced, one-handed, leaning on a smuggler's staff, and he did not ask her why she had stopped, because he already knew. Below them the valley filled with morning light. Far off, the capital sat quiet under an open sky, small and mortal and newly honest. And in Vesper's chest, where the voices had passed through her, there was a stillness she knew she would carry for the rest of her life — not silence, exactly, and not absence. A scar of silence. The shape of ten thousand names pressed into her like letters pressed into wax, permanent, tender to the touch, proof that something enormous had once rested there and then, mercifully, moved on.

Every voice she had ever carved was still with her. That had always been the cost of the gift, and she had always counted it a loneliness. But walking down out of the mountains with the dawn on her face and Aldric's hand — his whole hand, once; his stubborn, honorable, maimed hand now — wrapped around hers, she understood that it had never been loneliness at all.

It had been rehearsal. For this. For carrying them all, gladly, for the rest of her days.

"We'll need a hall," she said at last. Her voice was rough, but it held. "In Greyhollow. Walls of bone, every name carved fresh, so they're never locked away again. A choir that asks nothing of anyone." She looked at him. "It'll take years."

Aldric smiled — the first true smile she had seen on him since the bridge, since the blood, since he had chosen her over every banner he'd ever served. "Good," he said. "I've got time."

Far to the north, in the silent capital, a queen was waking to an empty sky and a kingdom that remembered — and Vesper felt, rather than heard, the first faint whisper of what was coming: councils summoned, confessions demanded, a throne learning, at last and too late, that it had never owned the song it ruled by.

But that was a story for other people, in other rooms.

Vesper turned her back on the mountain, and took her love's hand, and walked down into the honest morning, carrying her silence like a scar, and her scar like a crown she would never wear.
