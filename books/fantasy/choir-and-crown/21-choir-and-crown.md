## 21. Choir and Crown

The hall went up on the old market green, where the well had been and where the gallows had stood before that, in the days when Greyhollow solved its griefs with rope instead of chisels. Vesper had chosen the site herself, though she never said so aloud. The dead who passed through her workshop knew. They always knew. She could feel them leaning against the walls of the place at dusk, patient as weather, watching the timber frame rise bone by bone.

Not bone, of course. Greyhollow was not so rich as to build with bone, and the living had suffered enough of bones made to serve purposes they never chose. The frame was mountain pine, silvered already by the first autumn rains, and the roof was slate from the Thornfell quarries — quarries that had reopened under a council charter rather than a crown's writ, and whose workers now set their own hours and sang whatever songs they pleased while they worked. But the heart of it, the reason anyone came, the reason pilgrims walked three weeks through mud to stand inside it — that part was bone.

Ten thousand small carvings hung from the rafters on cords of braided linen. Each one no larger than a thumb joint. Each one bearing a name.

Vesper had carved four hundred and eleven of them herself, before her hands gave out the first time. The rest had come from everywhere: Aldric's careful, left-handed strokes; the widow Marta's surprisingly deft work learned over one hard winter; smugglers who drifted through town and stayed a season; children too young to hold a chisel properly, whose crooked letters Vesper refused to straighten because crookedness was honest and honesty was the whole point. Corvane, in his last week in Greyhollow before he walked into the mountains alone, had carved seven names in a hand so precise it looked typeset. He had asked her afterward, quietly, whether they would be heard. She had told him the truth: that the hall did not make the dead speak. It only finally let them rest.

That was the difference between this choir and the other one, the one beneath the mountains whose silence still woke her some nights like a hand pressed over her mouth. The Hollow Choir had sung because it was forced to sing — ten thousand memories bound into ward-stones, grief harvested for a century to keep a peace that no one above had ever consented to. This hall asked nothing. It held names the way a cupped hand holds water: lightly, without gripping, ready to open.

* * *

She was at her bench in the workshop behind the hall when the delegation arrived, because she was almost always at her bench these days. Her hearing had changed since the vault collapsed. The ten thousand voices that had poured through her in that final moment — every death, every name, every unfinished sentence of an entire slaughtered generation — had gone quiet, freed, but they had left something behind in her ears the way a flood leaves silt. She heard the dead more faintly now, but more widely. Not just the voices sealed into carved things. All of them. The whole soft murmur of the departed, everywhere, like snow falling on the far side of a wall.

It should have been unbearable. Somehow it wasn't. It sounded, most days, like company.

"Carver Amberdale." The voice belonged to a woman in travel-stained grey, one of six riders standing in the doorway with the awkward posture of people who have rehearsed formality and lost their nerve. Vesper recognized the crest before she recognized the face: a bridge over running water, stitched in silver thread. The Council of the Vale. "We've come from Harrowbridge. The council wishes to—"

"I know what they wish." Vesper set down her chisel. She had known for two months, ever since the first letter came, and then the second, and then the offer delivered in person by a man whose boots cost more than her roof. "They wish to thank me."

"They wish to honor you." The woman flushed slightly. "There is talk of a title. Warden of Remembrance. An estate in the vale lands, and a seat at council when matters concerning the dead arise, which—" she consulted a paper she did not seem to fully believe in, "—they do, increasingly."

Through the wall, Vesper felt Aldric stop moving in the yard. He had taken up splitting kindling after breakfast, one-handed, having taught himself over the summer a method that involved pinning each log against his chest and striking downward — slow, ugly, effective. He had gotten very good at pretending not to listen through walls. He was not very good at it yet.

"And if I refuse?" Vesper said.

The rider hesitated. "Then we are instructed to ask again. Politely."

"You can tell them I refused politely." Vesper wiped her hands on her apron, which only transferred the bone dust to the apron, and stepped out into the grey morning light. Six faces turned toward her with the particular wariness of people confronting a legend who turned out to be a tired woman in her forties with sawdust in her hair. "I'll say it plainly, so you don't have to ride back twice. I didn't free those ten thousand souls so that someone could put my name on a deed. A title is just a smaller version of the thing I broke. Someone gets named, someone gets elevated, and everyone else agrees to be quiet about why it was needed in the first place."

"The wards failed," one of the men said, younger, almost accusing. "The capital has been dark for half a year. Bandits in the north marches. Plague scare at Whiteford. People are frightened, and frightened people want—"

"Want what? A new song? New stones, new prisoners, a new choir to sing their fear quiet?" She kept her voice level. She had practiced this. "Let them be frightened a while longer. Frightened people who remember why they're frightened build better walls than comfortable people who've forgotten. That's the whole lesson of the last hundred years, and your council is trying very hard not to learn it."

The young man opened his mouth. Closed it. In the silence, the kindling-splitting resumed outside, deliberate as a heartbeat.

"There is one more matter," the lead rider said carefully. "Queen Maren sends a personal message."

Vesper went still.

Maren had abdicated her claim to the old bloodline in the spring — stood before the assembled councils in the ruined ward-hall of the capital, where the great stones sat dark and cold as river rocks, and told the truth. All of it. The stolen song, the borrowed peace, the century of suffering humming under every treaty and festival and quiet night's sleep. Half the hall had expected her execution. What they got instead was harder: a queen who unmade herself in public, clause by clause, and offered in its place something nobody had governed with before — elected councils, open ledgers, a realm that would have to hold together on agreement instead of magic. Some said it would collapse within a year. It was autumn now, and it hadn't, though Vesper suspected the verdict would take a generation to render honestly.

"She doesn't ask for forgiveness," the rider said. "She asks permission. To visit the hall. To read the names. She says she owes ten thousand debts and would like to begin learning them by name."

Vesper looked past the riders, across the green, to the half-finished hall with its thousand small bones turning gently on their cords in the wind. Somewhere among them hung a carving of a courier whose name she'd learned too late to save him, and a soldier named Bren who had died holding a bridge, and a girl called Ilsette who had been nine years old when the war reached her village, and — near the western rafter, where the light fell best — a name in a precise, typeset hand, carved by a man who had spent his life destroying evidence of the dead and had ended it trying to give them back.

"Tell her she may come," Vesper said. "Tell her she may sweep the floors while she's here. That's the only office I'll grant anyone."

Something loosened in the six faces, all at once — relief, maybe, or the recognition of a joke that was also not a joke. The lead rider bowed. They mounted and rode out along the river road, and Vesper stood in the yard until the hoofbeats faded, feeling the faint pressure of the day's dead settle around her shoulders like a shawl.

"You could have taken it, you know." Aldric came around the corner of the workshop, carrying the maul awkwardly against his body, his empty right sleeve pinned and tucked the way it always was now. His hair had gone more grey at the temples since spring. His eyes hadn't changed at all. "Warden of Remembrance. You'd have been terrible at it, but you'd have been terrible at it with a warm house."

"I have a warm house."

"This house leaks."

"It leaks honestly." She took the maul from him and leaned it against the doorpost, and then, because he was close and the riders were gone and there was no one left in the world whose opinion mattered enough to perform for, she leaned her forehead against his shoulder. He smelled of split pine and woodsmoke and, underneath it, faintly and forever, of mountain stone. His good hand came up to rest between her shoulder blades, careful as it always was, as if she were something carved.

"Cassel's trial finished yesterday," he said into her hair. "Word came up with the post. Ten years' labor in the quarry. He asked for the Thornfell quarry specifically." A pause. "He asked to carve names."

Vesper lifted her head. "Did the council allow it?"

"They're allowing it. Under watch." Aldric's mouth twisted — not quite a smile, but related to one the way a scar is related to the wound. "He'll spend ten years giving back the names he tried to bind. Either it will remake him or it won't. But it seemed fitting that he should learn the weight of it, one name at a time, the way we all did."

"One name at a time," Vester murmured. "That's the only way anything true gets done. Everything else is just the Choir again — wanting the whole song at once, and making someone else sing it."

* * *

In the afternoon she worked, and he worked beside her, and this was the shape her life had become, and she found she did not miss any of the shapes it might otherwise have taken.

The apprenticeship had begun as penance, or he had claimed it had. *You saved my life and cost me a hand; the arithmetic demands service,* he'd said in the flooded chapel's aftermath, when the mountain had stopped shaking and the world had turned out to continue existing, which neither of them had entirely bet on. But penance, Vesper had learned long ago from the widow Marta, curdles if you leave it sitting. What Aldric actually did, month over month, was learn. How to choose bone — not all bone listened equally, and the dead deserved good instruments. How to sharpen a chisel so it whispered rather than chewed. How to sit with a grieving person for an hour before touching the material, because the living were part of the carving whether they knew it or not. His left-handed letters were getting better. His patience had always been better than his swordwork, though no one had ever thought to reward him for it.

Today they were carving a commission that was not really a commission: a mother from the next valley whose son had drowned in the spring floods, and who could not afford payment and had offered a sack of apples instead. Vesper had accepted the apples. Aldric was doing the rough shaping while she listened for the boy — drowned souls came through strangely, all water-sound and slow drifting, like voices heard from underwater — and the afternoon light came slantwise through the workshop window and caught the bone dust motes and turned them briefly golden.

"You know," Aldric said, not looking up from his work, "when I was a knight, I used to imagine how it would end. For me. Every man does, even the ones who pretend otherwise. Usually it's a battle. Sometimes it's a trial. Once or twice, in the worst years, it was exile in the mountains, eating roots, hating everyone." He blew a curl of shaving off the femur he was shaping. "I never once imagined I'd be happy in a leaky house in Greyhollow, carving memorial spoons for apples."

"Those aren't spoons."

"They're spoon-adjacent. I'm improving."

She laughed — actually laughed, the sound surprising her, coming up from somewhere below the ribs where the ten thousand voices had once lived and where now there was mostly just space, and light, and the ordinary ache of being alive. Aldric smiled at the sound of it the way he always did, as if it were a rare coin he'd found on the road and didn't want to spend too quickly.

"Marry me," he said.

It was not, technically, out of nowhere. He had said it once before, in the dark weeks after the vault, when she'd been sleeping three hours a night and waking to phantom singing, and she had answered with something about how she couldn't promise to be a person yet, and he had said *then I'll wait for the person, whenever she arrives.* Neither of them had mentioned it since. But the afternoon was golden and the apples were paid and the hall on the green was nearly full of names and the world, improbably, was continuing.

"Yes," Vesper said.

Aldric looked up. For a moment the disgraced knight, the man who had killed former comrades on Sorrow Bridge and broken with his order in a cavern full of ghosts and given his hand to stop a saboteur, simply stared at her with the naked astonishment of a boy.

"That easily?"

"That easily. I decided months ago. I was waiting for you to catch up." She picked up her chisel again, because her hands wanted something to do or they would shake, and she was not going to let them shake. "But I want it small. No banners. No councils attending. The widow Marta can officiate, she's been threatening to learn how. We'll do it in the hall, after dark, when the names have the place to themselves. Let the dead be the witnesses. They're the only ones who've earned it."

Aldric crossed the small room and kissed her, bone dust and all, and his remaining hand cradled the back of her head with a tenderness that had survived a war, a disgrace, and a dismemberment intact, and somewhere beyond the wall the thousand small carvings turned on their cords in the evening wind, saying nothing, asking nothing, keeping their own quiet counsel — and Vesper understood, in the marrow-deep way she understood everything now, that this was what the dead had been trying to tell her all along. Not that memory should rule. Not that memory should burn. Just that it should be carried — by hands, in halls, in houses that leaked — lightly, and together, and without fear.

Outside, the first star came out over the Thornfells.

And beneath it, far beneath, in the collapsed dark where the Hollow Choir had sung its century of sorrow, something stirred that was not a voice, not a ghost, not a memory — but was, unmistakably, listening back.
