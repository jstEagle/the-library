## 22. The Next Voice

The frost came early to Greyhollow that year, silvering the market square before the last of the autumn apples had been pressed, and Vesper Amberdale woke on the morning of the first hard freeze with the particular ache in her hands that meant snow before dusk. She lay a while in the warm dark, listening to the house breathe — Aldric's slow, even rhythm from the room beside hers, the tick and settle of the stove iron, the wind worrying the eaves like an old man counting his coins.

The silence was the thing she noticed most, still, after all these months. Not emptiness — she had learned the difference long ago, though it had taken her half a lifetime and one collapsing mountain to understand it. The silence of the workshop was full now: the weight of ten thousand names resting easy in their carved bones, each one anchored, each one *finished*. It was the silence of a room after the last guest has gone home safe, not the silence of a room where no one ever came.

She rose, dressed against the cold, and went down to light the forge lamp before dawn could make the workbench feel like a confession booth.

The hall of names stood finished at the far end of the market square — Aldric had overseen its raising with the same stubborn precision he'd once given to drill yards and battle lines, one-handed, refusing every offer of help with the scaffolding. Ten thousand slivers of bone set into cedar panels, arranged not by rank or by side — there had been sides, in the ancient war, but the dead had stopped caring about that somewhere in the middle of their century of singing — but alphabetically, by name alone. Children came to touch them. Old soldiers came and wept without shame. A delegation had come from the capital in high summer, councilors in their new plain wool, wanting to commission copies for the cities, and Vesper had told them what she told everyone now: the carvings were not for sale, but she would teach any hand that wanted to learn.

Some of them had actually come back to learn. That surprised her more than anything else the year had brought.

She was grinding bone-dust for the day's first order — a memorial comb for a ferryman drowned in the Sorrel, his widow insistent that he'd always combed his beard before seeing anyone and would want to look decent when he spoke — when the knock came.

Three small knocks. The kind a child makes when they have rehearsed the knocking all the way from home and are now terrified someone will answer.

Vesper wiped her hands and opened the door onto the blue-grey light of early morning and a girl of perhaps eight winters, standing very straight in a coat too big for her, her boots crusted with road-mud that had frozen and thawed and frozen again — she had walked a long way, and started yesterday. In her mittened hands, held out before her like something caught between an offering and a weapon, was a bone.

It was a knuckle-bone. An ox knuckle, by the heft and the honey color, polished smooth by years in a pocket. And even before the girl spoke, even before the door was fully open, Vesper felt it — that faint wrongness in the air, the pressure behind the ears, the way candlelight leans toward certain objects. The way a struck bell keeps humming after the sound is gone.

"Are you the bone-carver?" the girl said. Her voice wobbled on *carver*, as if she'd practiced that word too and lost her nerve halfway through saying it out loud. "The one in the songs? They say you freed the dead under the mountain."

"I'm the one," Vesper said. "What's your name?"

"Bryn." The girl swallowed. "Bryn Marsh. My gran died in the summer. This was hers." She turned the knuckle over in her mitten, and Vesper saw that the girl's thumb kept moving on it — rubbing, circling, the way a tongue worries a broken tooth. "It started whispering after she was buried. At night. Everyone says I'm making it up. Mam says I should throw it in the river." Her chin came up, defiant and frightened in equal measure, and Vester recognized the expression the way you recognize your own handwriting. "But it knows things. It knew where Gran hid the good spoons. It knew what she said to me the day before she died, and I never told anybody, because nobody would believe —"

"Because they'd say you were making it up," Vesper finished gently.

The girl nodded, eyes bright and wet with the particular fury of a child who has been right and unheard.

Vesper crouched, slow, so as not to startle her, and looked at the bone without touching it yet. Honey-colored, worn, loved. Carrying a voice the way a riverbed carries water long after the flood. "May I listen?"

Bryn hesitated. Vesper understood the hesitation completely. She remembered being eight years old herself, standing in her mother's workshop with a bone that whispered, torn between the terror of it and the terrible loneliness of putting it down. Her mother had taken it from her hands and said, *You don't have to hold it forever, little bird. You only have to carry it until it's heard.* Twenty years later — twenty years and a mountain — those words still fit inside her like a key in its lock.

"You can keep holding it," Vesper said. "I'll just put my hand near."

She laid two fingers on the bench an inch from the knuckle, closed her eyes, and let her gift lean toward it the way you lean toward a door to hear whether anyone stands behind it.

*Cold,* thought the grandmother's voice, faint as breath on glass. *My knees are cold, and where did I put — no. Never mind the spoons. Bryn. My Bryn-bird. Don't let them make you quiet. You hear fine. You hear true. Find the carver in Greyhollow; your great-gran traded with her mother, and the gift runs down our side like water runs downhill. Don't be afraid of it. Be afraid only of wasting it.*

Vesper opened her eyes. Her throat was tight. Some voices you carried for years without knowing why, and then a stranger's grandmother explained everything in eleven words.

"She says you're not making it up," Vesper told the girl. "And she says the spoons are in the flour crock, behind the false bottom."

Bryn's mouth fell open. Then she laughed — a startled, hiccupping laugh, the laugh of a child setting down a weight she had forgotten she was carrying — and clutched the bone to her chest. "That's exactly where! Nobody knew! She *hid* them!"

"Grandmothers hide things well. They've had practice." Vesper stood, holding the door wider. "Come in out of the cold, Bryn Marsh. Your gran walked you a long way to find me, and I imagine she'd like to rest."

* * *

Aldric appeared from the back room while Vesper was warming milk on the stove, moving with the careful economy he'd taught himself since Sorrow Bridge took his left hand. He wore his apron over his shirt — he ground bone-dust and sanded panels now, badly at first, better every week, and had declared with grave finality that a knight who could no longer hold a sword might as well learn to hold a chisel. He paused in the doorway, taking in the mud-caked child sitting bolt upright on the customer's stool like a duchess at court, and raised one eyebrow at Vesper.

"Ambassador from the capital?" he asked dryly.

"A student."

His other eyebrow joined the first. In a year and a half of apprenticeship he had watched her turn away three grown men and a desperate widow's son who wanted the craft fast and cheap; she had told them all, with varying degrees of kindness, that bone-carving was not a trade you learned but a listening you survived. "A student," he repeated.

"She has the gift. The real one, not the hedge-witch tingle." Vesper poured the milk, watching steam curl. "Her gran sent her. The voice named me — named my mother, actually. Said the family used to trade with her." She glanced up and found him already looking at her, and the look between them held the whole geography of the past year: the mountain, the vault, the ten thousand names passing through her body like a river through a reed bed, the weeks afterward when she hadn't spoken at all and he hadn't asked her to, only sat nearby whittling useless, beautiful things until the words came back. "Her name is Bryn. She's eight."

"Eight," Aldric said. He crouched by the girl, and Vesper watched him do the thing he was best at now — not fighting, not drilling, but the patient, unhurried attention that had made even hardened smugglers confess their histories around his campfire. "Eight years old, and you walked to Greyhollow alone?"

"There was a carter for part of it," Bryn said, with the dignity of someone who has already been interrogated on this point and won. "He had a dog."

"A credible escort," Aldric agreed solemnly, and the girl decided, visibly and permanently, that she liked him.

They fed her bread and honey and let her examine the workshop while they talked in low voices by the stove. There were others, Vesper knew — there had always been others. Hedge-listeners in the villages who heard a word or two through a keepsake and called it second sight. Children who talked to wells. Since the Choir fell and the truth came out, letters had been arriving at Greyhollow weekly, clumsy and hopeful: *my son says the loom sings; my daughter dreams our dead grandfather's memories in order.* For years those families had hidden their strange children, afraid of inquisitors and superstition both. Now they wrote asking if it was safe to let the children listen.

Safe, Vesper thought, stirring the milk. No. Nothing worth doing was safe. But *right* — that she could teach.

"The wards are stable," Aldric was saying, in the tone he used for reports. "Maren's councils took the census of ward-stones last month. Whatever replaced the song, it holds — thin, honest magic, paid for openly. Corvane hasn't been seen since autumn."

"He's in the mountains," Vesper said. She said it without fear now. "Tamsin's smugglers pass him on the high trails. He keeps to himself, walks shrine to shrine. She says he leaves carved markers at each one." She paused. "Names, Aldric. He's carving names."

Aldric was quiet a moment. The Inquisitor who had come to burn the vault and stayed, instead, to labor at her bench through the shaking dark of those last days — who had lost his certainty somewhere between the fourth hundredth name and the collapse, and walked out of the wreckage of his own conviction into the wilderness — that man leaving names on mountainsides was either penance or prayer, and perhaps there had never been much difference between the two. "Let him carve," Aldric said at last. "The dead aren't particular about whose hands hold the chisel."

From across the room, a small gasp. Bryn stood at the display shelf where the finished pieces waited for collection, her mitten hovering over a memorial whistle — and the whistle, touched by nothing, had begun, very softly, to sing.

Vesper crossed the room and lifted it down. The ferryman's piece, waiting for his widow. Inside its hollow, the drowned man's voice hummed contentedly, the way voices did now — settled, unafraid, waiting to be delivered rather than trapped. Bryn stared at it, then at Vesper, then at her own pocketed knuckle-bone, and Vesper watched the understanding land on the child's face like sunrise: *it isn't only me.*

"It talks," Bryn whispered.

"It waits," Vesper said. "There's a difference, and you'll spend years learning it, if you choose to learn at all." She crouched to the girl's level, the way her mother had crouched for her, the way the dead had crouched for her in the dark under the world, and took both of Bryn's mittened hands in hers. "So here is the first lesson, and you may take it or leave it, and no one will think less of you either way. The dead do not need us to speak for them. They had voices of their own. What they need is for someone to *listen* — truly listen, without deciding in advance what they ought to say. Can you do that?"

Bryn considered this with the ferocious seriousness of eight years old confronting a question that mattered. "Even if what they say is bad?"

"Especially then." Vesper smiled. "The truth doesn't need our permission. It only needs our company."

"And the second lesson?" the girl asked. "If there is one."

Vesper glanced at Aldric, who had gone still by the stove, watching them with an expression he probably believed was neutral. "The second lesson takes longer," she said. "It's how to carve a name into living bone so gently the dead feel held instead of caught. It's how to carry a hundred voices and stay yourself. It's how to know when to stop listening and live your own life — which, I warn you, is the hardest lesson of all, and some days I am still failing it." She squeezed the small mittened hands. "Come back tomorrow. Bring your gran's bone. We'll begin with the spoons."

* * *

Bryn's mother arrived at midday, frantic, having traced her daughter's route through three villages, and left at dusk reassured, tearful, and the proud owner of a promise that her daughter would lodge with the widow Tamsin's sister two doors down and walk to the workshop each morning like any apprentice. Word, as it always did in Greyhollow, traveled faster than the frost. By week's end two more children had been brought to the door — a miller's son who heard his drowned brother in the grindstone, and a silent little boy who didn't speak at all but drew, endlessly, in charcoal, faces Vesper recognized from the hall of names, faces he had never seen and could not possibly know.

Vesper began teaching all three on the same morning: how to hold a chisel so it sang rather than screamed, how to close the ear as well as open it, how to grieve well and finish well and let go. Aldric built them a low bench by the south window where the light was kind. On the third day Bryn carved her grandmother's name into a fresh-cut sliver of ox bone, tongue between her teeth, and when the last letter was done the old woman's voice passed through the room like warmth through glass — *that's my girl, that's my Bryn-bird* — and faded into freedom, and Bryn cried, and Vesper held her, and did not tell her that it got easier, because it did not, quite, and lies were poor mortar for a school of truth.

On the evening of that first week, after the children had gone home and the lamps were lit and the snow finally falling thick and soft past the windows, Aldric set down his sanding block and looked at her across the bench.

"Do you ever regret it?" he asked. Not the vault — he never asked about the vault anymore; they had spent their nights of regret together and buried them properly, with names. "Any of it. The throne you could have shaped. The honors you refused. The queen's council writes to you twice a season, you know. They'd give you anything."

Vesper looked down at her hands — scarred, steady, stained faintly gold with decades of bone-dust. Hands that had held ten thousand deaths and been allowed, at the end, to simply hold his.

"They offered me a crown of memory," she said, "and I chose a bench full of it instead. Every name I carve gets to go home. No crown ever gave anything back." She reached across the shavings and took his whole hand and the scarred wrist where the other used to be. "Regret is for choices you can't defend. I can defend mine to the dead themselves, and I have. That's more than any queen can say."

Outside, the snow deepened over Greyhollow, over the hall of names with its ten thousand small stars of bone, over the quiet kingdom learning, slowly and painfully and honestly, to remember. Somewhere in the high passes a grey-haired man carved names into stone and left them for the wind. Somewhere in the capital a former queen chaired a council of farmers and weavers and slept, by all accounts, better than she had on her stolen throne.

And in the warm workshop, at a low bench by the south window, a child's bone lay waiting for morning — whispering, patient, unafraid — and above it hung the tools of the trade, clean and sharp, ready for whatever voice came next.
