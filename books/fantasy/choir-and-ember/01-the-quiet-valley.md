## 1. The Quiet Valley

The morning fog came off the mere like breath from a sleeping mouth, and Zephyrine Dunmore was already at his bench when it lifted.

He worked in the lean-to attached to his cottage, the one room with a south-facing window, because bone wanted good light the way bread wanted warmth. Before him lay a femur—ox, not human; he did his finest memorial work on ox bone, though sheep and deer served well enough—and across its pale curve he was cutting the name *Sela Wren* in letters small enough to fit under a thumbnail but deep enough to last a hundred years. Around the name he carved ivy, because Sela had loved ivy, had let it eat half her garden fence before anyone thought to stop it, and her daughter had wept and said, *she'd want the ivy, carver, she'd want it climbing right over the words.*

So there was ivy, each leaf cut with a single stroke, and beneath the name a line of dates, and beneath that, where the bone widened toward the knee-joint, a small carved wren with its head turned as if listening for something no one else could hear.

Zephyrine understood that bird better than most.

He blew the dust from the groove and held the bone up to the window. The light came through it amber and honeyed, the way light comes through a hand, and for a moment he allowed himself the small vanity of thinking it was good work. It was. He knew it was. Twenty-two years of carving had taught him the one honest pride available to a man: that his hands were better than his hands had been.

"Sela Wren," he said aloud, testing the name's weight on his tongue. "Ivy and a wren. That'll do."

The words fell flat in the cold air, as all words did in Hollowmere. Not dead-flat—the valley was never dead—but muffled, the way sound is muffled under snow. That was the ward. Everyone in Hollowmere knew there was a ward, though nobody agreed what it was made of or who had raised it. Elder Maren said it was old beyond old, older than the kingdom, older than the road that wound up over the mountain pass and down into the next valley and out of their lives forever. The miller said it was the mere itself doing the work, the black water drinking everything strange. The children said it was a giant's grave. Children always said it was a giant's grave.

What Zephyrine knew—what he alone in Hollowmere knew for certain, unless you counted the dead, and they weren't talking—was what the ward kept out.

Magic, mostly. All magic. A traveler's charm would gutter and die at the valley's edge like a candle carried into wind. A hedge-witch's poultice worked here only so far as herbs could carry it. And song-magestry, the great and terrible art of the crown itself, the binding of souls into service—that simply stopped, as if the whole valley sat behind a wall of glass too thick for even a king's choir to sing through.

And with the magic went his gift.

He set the bone down and flexed his fingers. They ached this morning. They often ached, and he had learned not to read anything into it, just as he had learned not to listen for the silence anymore, because listening for it was its own kind of hearing.

He had been nine years old when he first heard the dead.

It had been his grandmother, in the smoke-scented dark of her sickroom, three days after the burial. She had spoken to him plainly, the way she spoke about weather: *the beans need picking, love, and your mother is harder on herself than she lets on, and don't marry that Corwin boy, he counts things.* Zephyrine had told his mother. His mother had gone white as bone-meal and taken him to the priest, and the priest had taken him aside and said, very gently, very kindly, the way men say terrible things when they are being kind: *some souls hear the door after it closes, lad. You mustn't answer it.*

But he answered it. For years he answered it. In the town below the mountains where he'd been born, where magic ran through the world the way water runs downhill, he had heard them everywhere—the recently dead still tangled in their griefs, the long-dead murmuring their worn liturgies of regret, the ones who died badly crying out in voices that scraped him raw inside. He had learned to sleep sitting up. He had learned to keep his face still while the dead begged him, raged at him, thanked him, lied to him. He had learned that pity was a door left open, and every open door let more of them through.

Then, at twenty-three, half-starved and hollow-eyed, following a rumor of a valley where charms died and witches' spells curdled, he had walked down into Hollowmere.

The silence had struck him like water.

Not absence—*silence*, deliberate and deep, the silence of a cathedral or a snowfall, and underneath it nothing at all. No dead. No murmur. No scratching at the walls of him. He had stood in the middle of the ford with his boots in the freezing water and wept, and the miller, passing with his cart, had assumed he was grieving and offered him bread, and that was how Zephyrine Dunmore came to stay.

Twenty years now. He had a cottage with a slate roof that needed patching every third spring. He had a bench, and chisels worn to fit his grip, and a reputation that had grown the way reputations grow in small places—slowly, then all at once, then simply *always*. When someone in the valley was dying, they sent for the carver. Not for the death; for the after. Because in Hollowmere they had an old custom, older than anyone could account for: a bone carved with the dying one's name, buried at the head of their grave, so that whoever they had been would stay put, stay named, stay *finished*. Other valleys had forgotten the custom or called it superstition. Hollowmere kept it, and paid its carver in eggs and firewood and mended coats, and Zephyrine had never once, in twenty years, been tempted to leave.

That was what he told himself, anyway. Telling himself things was a craft too, and he had gotten good at it.

* * *

Midmorning, Tam Voss came clattering into the yard with a basket of apples and the particular energy of a man who has already decided today will be interesting whether or not anyone consents.

"Carver! Put down your knife, I've news that will ruin your whole quiet life."

"It usually does." Zephyrine stayed at the bench, but he turned so Tam could see he was smiling. Some people you could turn your back on while smiling. Tam wasn't one of them; he took silence as a personal affront.

Tam was broad as a door and cheerful as a dog, thirty-one years old, which was two years younger than Zephyrine and looked ten, and he had been Zephyrine's friend since the second week Zephyrine lived in the valley, when Tam—who had been fourteen and enormous—had decided the new stranger needed protecting from the other boys and had protected him so vigorously that both of them ended up in the mere up to their necks. Twenty years of friendship had settled into something comfortable as an old coat: Tam talked, Zephyrine listened, and between them they managed most of what needed managing in the eastern half of the valley.

"News," Zephyrine prompted, when the pause stretched.

"Oh. Yes." Tam set down the apples and became briefly serious, which on Tam's face looked like a man trying on a hat that didn't suit him. "There's a courier come up the pass. King's courier, by the livery—or what's left of the livery. Old Hesk saw him at the top of the switchbacks yesterday evening, riding hard, and this morning there's no sign of him at the inn at Ford's Edge, and his horse is standing lame in the reeds by the mere."

Zephyrine's hands went still on the bench.

"A king's courier," he repeated. "Here."

"That's what I said!" Tam beamed, pleased with the drama of it. "Hesk thinks the man's dead in a ditch somewhere. Nothing rides that hard without reason, and nothing stops that sudden without worse. But here's my thought—" he leaned in, dropping his voice to a stage whisper, "—you should come look at the horse with me. You're good with animals. And if the rider turns up dead, well." He shrugged enormously. "You're good with the dead, too."

The words landed in Zephyrine's chest like stones dropped down a well.

"I carve bones, Tam. I don't talk to them."

"No, no. Of course not." Tam waved this away. "But you know things. You always know things. Remember when the Wren girl went missing and you walked straight to the culvert? Or when you told Maren not to drink the well-water at the shepherd's hut, three days before the rest of us got sick?"

"I guessed."

"You *guessed*," Tam agreed warmly, "with your spooky gift of guessing, which is why everyone likes you and slightly fears you, in the way that keeps a man supplied in firewood. Come on. The horse won't wait, and neither will the ditch, if there's a ditch."

Zephyrine looked at his bench. The Sela Wren bone sat in its pool of window-light, ivy and wren and dates, finished except for polishing. The rest of his day stretched out orderly as furrows: two more memorials to start, a hinge to fix for Widow Cress, wood to split before the frost came down off the peaks for true.

A king's courier, riding hard enough to kill his horse, vanishing at the edge of the quietest valley in the kingdom.

"The ward will have stopped whatever he was carrying," Zephyrine said. "Whatever message, whatever spell-work. Things go flat here. Even kings' errands."

"That's the spirit." Tam scooped up the basket again. "Come look anyway. If we find nothing, I'll owe you a pie. If we find something, I'll owe you a pie and you can tell me you knew it all along."

They went together down the lane, past the smithy where Brannic was hammering out something rhythmless and loud, past the tithe-barn with its roof of silvered shingles, down toward the mere, whose black water lay flat as poured ink under the thinning fog. The path along the shore was soft with leaf-rot. Their breath hung white.

It occurred to Zephyrine—not for the first time, but with unusual force—that he was happy. Genuinely, structurally happy, built on foundations: work, friendship, custom, silence. Twenty years ago he had crawled into this valley like a wounded thing into a burrow, and the valley had closed over him, and he had let it. Whatever he was—whatever the priest's word had been, *door-hearer*, *soul-listener*, whatever the crown's Ember Choir would have called him if they'd ever found him, *resonant*, *unbound*, *material*—it didn't reach him here. Here he was only the carver. Here his name meant bones and kindness, and nothing sang, and nothing whispered, and the dead stayed decently wherever the dead went when nobody could hear them.

He had spent twenty years telling himself this peace was permanent. He had gotten very good at it. But there was a part of him—a part he kept, like everything else, quietly, in the dark—that had always known the truth about hiding places:

They hold only until something wants in.

The reeds began, tall and gold-grey, whispering against each other with a dry sea-sound. Tam crashed ahead, calling out observations about mud and horses. And Zephyrine, walking slower, stopped.

Because the reeds ahead had gone silent.

Not still—silent. As if the sound of them had been drawn out of the air, the way the ward drew sound out of everything. A circle of hush in a whispering world, maybe thirty feet across, and at the center of it, half-collapsed against a stone, was a shape in a rider's cloak.

The livery was blue and silver, or had been. Now it was mostly blood.

"Told you," Tam breathed beside him, all the cheer gone out of his voice. "Told you there'd be a ditch."

The courier was alive. Zephyrine could see it in the shallow, tearing movement of his chest, the lips working around words that hadn't found strength yet. His skin was grey as ash. There was blood on his mouth and blood frozen stiff along his side where something had opened him—something narrow, something deliberate. Not a wolf. Wolves are never deliberate.

"Fetch Maren," Zephyrine said quietly. "Fetch anyone. Go."

Tam went. Zephyrine heard him crashing back up the path, and then it was very quiet, except for the reeds resuming their whispering all around the circle of hush, and the wet laboring of the courier's breath, and—faintly, terribly, for the first time in twenty years—

a voice.

*You're the one,* the dying man whispered, without moving his lips, without any sound reaching Zephyrine's ears at all. Only inside. Warm as a hand pressed over his heart, cold as the mere at its bottom. *I was told. South, past the mountains, a valley where the songs die. Find the carver.*

Zephyrine did not move. Could not. Twenty years of practiced stillness held him upright while everything under his skin woke up screaming, the old channels opening like frozen rivers cracking, the dead man's voice pouring through them thin and desperate and unmistakably, horribly *alive*.

*Please. I haven't got long, and it doesn't stop when I do. Please, carver. The map. Take the map.*

The courier's hand moved—slowly, trembling, leaving a smear of red on the stone beside him—toward the satchel strapped against his ribs.

Behind Zephyrine, far up the valley, the fog over the mere began to churn.
