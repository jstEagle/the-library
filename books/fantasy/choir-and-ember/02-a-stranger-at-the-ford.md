## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The ford had been singing to itself all morning.

Not truly singing—Zephyrine knew better than anyone that the Hollowmere waters held no voice at all. But the ice along the shallows creaked and ticked as the sun climbed over the eastern ridge, and the sound was so like whispering that his hands sometimes forgot themselves and reached for a chisel. He let them reach. Let them hover. Then he tucked them back into his sleeves, where they could do no harm, and went on mending the fence post he had been mending for an hour without mending it much.

Winter was coming early this year. The elders said so at every gathering, in the tone of men and women who had survived forty such winters and expected to survive forty more. The geese had gone south before the harvest festival. The rowan berries hung heavy and red as drops of blood on the trees above the mill. Zephyrine had carved three memorial bones already this month—a child taken by fever, a grandfather who simply lay down one night and did not rise, and old Widow Sennet, who had asked him, with her last lucid breath, to make something *cheerful*, damn it, because everyone else's bones were always so grim.

He had given her a lark. She would have hated it and told him so, and he found he missed being scolded.

That was the thing about Hollowmere that no one who lived elsewhere ever understood: the quiet here was not emptiness. It was a fullness of a different kind. In the lowlands, in the cities, in whatever passed for life beyond the valley's ring of grey hills, the dead spoke constantly—or so Zephyrine had been told, or so he remembered, though the memories had gone soft as old wax after eleven years behind the ward. Voices in market squares. Voices clinging to battlefields like mist. Voices in every churchyard and cradle and roadside ditch, murmuring their unfinished business into any ear that happened to be shaped for listening.

His ears had been shaped for it. Shaped very well indeed, which was the problem.

Here, inside the ward—the great silent working laid down centuries ago by founders whose names even the elders had forgotten—there was nothing. No murmur. No whisper. No dead man pressing his last words against the thin membrane between worlds, begging to be carried one step further. Just wind, water, woodsmoke, and the living voices of two hundred people who thought Zephyrine Dunmore was a bone-carver and nothing more.

He was a bone-carver. That part was true. He liked that it was true.

He picked up his tools from the frost-stiffened grass beside the fence—he had brought them out intending to carve a new marker for the Sennet plot and had instead spent the morning doing nothing—and started up the path toward the village. The light was going amber and long. Somewhere downhill, Tam Vessel was arguing with his own son about firewood, his voice carrying the way Tam's voice always carried, big and warm and utterly uninterested in being ignored.

Zephyrine had almost reached the turn where the path bent around the alder thicket when the dog began barking.

Not the village dogs—they were off by the mill. This was Bramble, the shepherd Hesk's half-mad border collie, and she was barking from the direction of the ford, and she was barking the way dogs bark when they have found something that frightens them badly enough to overcome their sense of self-preservation. A continuous, rising, desperate sound.

Zephyrine stood still. The amber light lay across the path. His breath came out white.

He should have called for help first. He understood that later, turning the moment over in the dark hours afterward like a stone with a flaw in it. He should have shouted for Tam, for Hesk, for anyone, and walked down to the water with other living bodies around him. Instead he turned and went down alone, fast, slipping once on the frozen mud and catching himself on a fence rail that bit his palm through his glove.

The courier was in the shallows.

He had come out of the water—that much was clear—or rather, he had tried to cross the ford and failed halfway, because his horse was nowhere in sight and there was a long torn furrow in the near bank where something, man or animal, had slid down into the current. He lay with his lower half in the freezing shallows and his shoulders wedged against a shelf of ice-slick rock, and he was still moving, feebly, the way insects move when the cold has nearly finished them.

He was dressed for the road, or he had been once: a riding coat of good wool gone grey with filth, boots that had seen hundreds of miles more than their maker intended, and beneath the coat, glimpsed when the man shifted, a leather dispatch case strapped tight across his chest. His face—

Zephyrine stopped three paces away and looked at the man's face, and felt something cold move through him that had nothing to do with the season.

The courier was young. Twenty-five, perhaps thirty. But his cheeks were hollowed and grey, his lips cracked and blackening at the edges, and his eyes, when they found Zephyrine, were burning with a fever that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than blood. Frost had gotten into his hair, whitening it at the temples, and there was a stain on his side—dark, stiff, old-looking, though it must have been fresh—where something had gone into him and not come out.

"Hollowmere," the man said. His teeth chattered so hard the word broke apart. "Is this—is this Hollowmere. The ward. I was told—" A cough took him, wet and deep. "I was *told*."

"You're in Hollowmere," Zephyrine said. He knelt at the edge of the shallows, careful of the ice. "You've crossed into the valley. You need to get out of the water. Can you stand if I pull?"

The man laughed. It was a terrible sound, a laugh with blood in it somewhere. "Stand," he said. "Stand, he says." Then the laughter collapsed and he began to shake, great racking tremors, and Zephyrine gave up asking permission.

He got his arms under the courier's shoulders and hauled. The man was lighter than he should have been—days without food, perhaps a week—and together they got him up the bank and onto the grass, out of the water, into the last of the sunlight. Bramble circled them both at a distance, hackles up, making a low continuous sound in her throat that was not quite growling.

"Fire," the courier gasped. "No. No fire. Listen to me." His hand shot out and closed on Zephyrine's wrist with a strength that astonished them both, fingers like iron wire. "Listen. There isn't time for fires."

"There's time," Zephyrine said. "You've made it. Whatever you're running from, you've made it to the quietest place in Cael Adair. Nothing reaches you here."

"That is what I was told." The courier's eyes fixed on him, and there was something in them now besides fever—something urgent and appalled, a man looking at the last door in the world and finding it already open. "Carver. You're the carver. The one who works the bones."

"I'm a carver."

"They said you'd know what to do." His grip loosened, then tightened again. "They said—if I could only reach the ward, the silence—I could rest. They said no one could follow me *into* the silence."

"No one will follow you," Zephyrine said, and heard how gentle his own voice had become, the voice he used for the dying when he sat with them and their families in the last hours. It was habit, nothing more. It was kindness, nothing more. "What's your name?"

"Ferrant." The word came out slurred. "Ferrant Ossory. Courier of the—of the third road. Sworn under seal." His eyes wandered upward, past Zephyrine's shoulder, to the hills. "It's so quiet here. Do you hear how quiet? Ten days. Ten days of hearing them scream behind me, and here it's just—"

"Just wind," Zephyrine said. "Just water."

But even as he said it, the hair rose on his arms.

Because the courier was right. It *was* quiet. Too quiet, suddenly, in a way Zephyrine had never felt in eleven years inside the ward. The silence of Hollowmere had a texture to it, a weight, like a warm blanket drawn over the whole valley, and Zephyrine had slept beneath that blanket so long he had forgotten it was there. Now he felt it shift. Felt it thin. As though somewhere beneath them a vast lamp had flickered—not gone out, nothing so final, but guttered, the flame bending sideways in a draft that should not exist.

Bramble stopped growling. She sat down abruptly, all four legs folding, and stared at the water.

"What's wrong?" Ferrant whispered. "Your face. What did I say?"

"Nothing. You said nothing." But Zephyrine's heart was beating strangely, quick and shallow, and the air tasted different—sharper, cleaner, like the taste of the world before weather. Like the taste of magic, though he had not smelled it in eleven years and might have invented the memory entirely. "Tell me what happened to you. Slowly. Start wherever you like."

"Cael Adair," Ferrant said. "The capital. I carry dispatches for the Crown. Have done for six years. Six years, and I never—" Another cough, longer than the last, and when it ended there was pink froth at the corner of his mouth. "Six weeks ago I was given a private commission. Sealed orders. Carry this north, tell no one, trust no one. And the man who gave it to me—" His hand fumbled at the dispatch case, at the straps. "The man who gave it to me was the King."

"The King's men give couriers sealed work all the time."

"Not the King's *men*." Ferrant's eyes came back to Zephyrine's face and held there, burning. "*The King.* His own hand on the seal. And I knew his hand, carver, I'd carried his correspondence for years, I'd have known it blind—and it was his hand, but it was *wrong*. The pressure too even. No tremor. The King has a palsy in his writing hand; half the court jokes about it. This seal had no tremor. And when I looked up to ask my question, he smiled at me, and the smile was perfect, and I have never in my life been so afraid of another human being."

The light was failing. Zephyrine noticed distantly that the shadows of the alders had stretched all the way across the ford, and that neither of them had moved, and that the courier's skin had gone from grey to a strange translucent blue-white, like candle wax.

"What did you do?" Zephyrine asked.

"I ran." A ghost of a smile. "Courier of the third road, sworn under seal, six years of service, and I ran like a rabbit. Because I'd heard things. Little things. The Choir sings at dawn now, did you know that? Every city, every garrison town. Dawn and dusk, the Ember Choir, singing the liturgy from the walls. Beautiful, they say. Uplifting. And everyone who hears it walks away a little softer. A little more agreeable." His voice was fraying, unraveling thread by thread. "And the prisons. Carver, the prisons are emptying. Not released—*taken*. Wagons at night, going up to the palace, and the Choir singing as they go in, and nothing ever comes out. And I thought: sealed orders from a king who doesn't tremble, while the Choir sings the whole city to sleep? So I ran. And they sent singers after me."

"Singers."

"You can't imagine." Ferrant shuddered, a full-body spasm. "They don't chase you. They don't need to. They sing, and the song finds you—finds the shape of you, the *sound* of you, everything you are—and where the song touches, you start to forget why you're running. By the fourth day I couldn't remember my mother's face. I cut my arm"—he nodded vaguely at the stained bandage—"just to have pain to hold on to. Pain remembers when the mind won't. And still they gained. Still they sang. And there was a voice in the harmony, near the end, a young voice, a girl's voice, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever heard, and I wanted to stop running so badly. I wanted to lie down in the snow and let them find me and be *filled*."

He was crying now, silently, the tears freezing at his temples.

"But I didn't," he said. "Some stubborn piece of me didn't. And I got here. To the ward. To the quiet." His hand crawled along his chest to the dispatch case again, and this time Zephyrine helped him, working the stiff buckles with numb fingers. "Take it. Take it out. Look at it."

Inside the case, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord, was a long cylinder of leather. Zephyrine drew it out and unwrapped it, and found himself holding a map—not paper, but thin sheets of vellum stitched together, covered in fine inked lines and dense annotation, and the case that held it was fitted with handles of carved bone. Old bone, yellowed, worked with a skill that made his carver's eye ache with recognition. Whoever had made these handles had known exactly what bone was and what it remembered.

"What is it?" he asked.

"A map." Ferrant laughed again, that bloody broken laugh. "A map to a grave. The Ashen Crypt—you'll see, it's marked, follow the northern road past Greyford, up into the mountains before the passes close. Midwinter. It must reach the crypt before midwinter, or it's all—it's all for nothing." His breathing had changed, gone ragged and uneven, each breath a separate decision. "The king is dead, carver. The real king. Dead ten years, and buried secret, and the thing sitting on his throne wearing his crown—" His hand seized Zephyrine's sleeve. "It eats them. It eats souls. The Choir gathers them and feeds them to it and it grows and one day there won't be enough left of anyone to stop it. The map goes to the crypt. The crypt holds what's needed. Promise me. Swear it."

"You're dying," Zephyrine said stupidly. "We need to get you to the village, there's a healer, Maren knows herbs—"

"*Swear it!*"

And Ferrant Ossory pushed himself up on one elbow with the last of everything he had, and thrust the map against Zephyrine's chest, and looked at him with eyes that were already going glassy, and said:

"It flickered. When I crossed the ford. Didn't it. Your ward flickered. I felt it—the silence cracked—and I thought, thank the gods, someone will hear me." His mouth twisted. "Someone who *works the bones*."

Zephyrine could not speak.

"Then hear me," the courier whispered. "Hear the last of me. The king is dead. The crown is a hunger. The map must reach the Ashen Crypt before midwinter. And when you understand what's written on it—" His grip failed. His body sagged back against the grass. "When you understand, you'll wish you'd burned it. But you won't burn it. I saw your face. You're the kind who carries things."

He died between one breath and the next, quietly, almost politely, the way travelers die who have finally been allowed to put down their burden. One moment his eyes were fixed on Zephyrine's; the next they were fixed on nothing at all, reflecting the first stars and the darkening sky.

And the ward broke.

Not wholly—never wholly—but Zephyrine felt it happen the way a man feels a tooth crack: a small, catastrophic wrongness in something he had ceased to notice because it had always been there. The blanket of silence tore along a seam, and through the tear poured ten days of terror, exhaustion, frostbite, desperation, and a love for a mother whose face he had fought to keep—all of it, all of Ferrant Ossory, pressing outward against the world with the dumb unstoppable insistence of the newly dead.

*Cold,* said the voice of Ferrant Ossory, in Zephyrine's mind, in a register no living throat had ever used. *Oh, it's cold. Is this the quiet? Is this where I was promised—*

Zephyrine made a sound he had not made since he was a boy. He staggered backward, dropped the map in the frost-wet grass, and clapped both hands over his ears, which was useless, useless, because the voice was not coming through his ears.

*I carried it. I carried it the whole way. Tell them I carried it.*

"Stop," Zephyrine whispered. "Please. Please stop."

*It's so quiet here. Why is it so quiet? Where did everyone go?*

And beneath Ferrant's bewildered, fading voice—fainter, older, worn smooth as river stones by years of murmuring—Zephyrine began to hear others. Seeping up through the crack in the world like water through a floorboard. Voices in the soil of the valley, patient and countless, the accumulated dead of Hollowmere's centuries, all of them stirring now like sleepers when a door opens.

One of them, very softly, said his name.

* * *

He did not remember walking to the village. He arrived at Tam Vessel's door with the map crushed against his chest and his hands bleeding where he had gripped it too hard, and Tam took one look at his face and set down the axe he was sharpening.

"Zeph. What in the names happened to you?"

"He came up the ford," Zephyrine said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "There was a courier. He was dying. Tam, the ward—" He swallowed. "The ward is cracking. And I heard him. After. I heard him *after*, and then the others, and one of them knew my name—"

Tam crossed the room in two strides and caught him by the shoulders, steadying him the way he had steadied him since they were boys falling out of apple trees. Up close his friend's face was weathered and kind and utterly uncomprehending, and Zephyrine realized, with a grief that arrived whole and unexpected, that Tam had never once heard what Zephyrine heard. Never would. That the silence Zephyrine had treasured for eleven years had been, for everyone else in Hollowmere, simply the way the world had always sounded.

"Sit down," Tam said. "Drink this. Then you'll tell me about the courier, all of it, start to finish." He glanced at the bundle in Zephyrine's arms—at the bone handles, yellowed and ancient, catching the firelight—and something moved behind his eyes, some instinct older than understanding. "And then," Tam said quietly, "you'll tell me why a dying man crossed half of Cael Adair to put that in *your* hands."

Outside, the first snow of the year had begun to fall, early and thick, and down by the ford the dead waited in the dark for Zephyrine to come back and listen.
