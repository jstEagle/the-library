## 3. What the Dead Courier Said

Zephyrine did not sleep.

He had laid the courier out on the trestle bench by his hearth, as was the custom in Hollowmere—dead men were neighbors first and strangers second—and he had done all the small, decent things the custom required. He had washed the frost from the man's face with water warmed over the fire. He had straightened his limbs, which had frozen at angles that spoke of a long, stumbling flight. He had covered him to the chin with a wool blanket because even a corpse deserved not to be cold, though Zephyrine knew better than most that the cold no longer mattered to him.

The bloodstained map lay on his worktable where the dying hand had thrust it. Bone-handled. The handle alone had cost the courier everything, Zephyrine suspected; you did not carry something wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with grey wax across half a kingdom unless it was worth more than your life, and the man's life had been spent paying for its passage.

He should burn it. That was the sensible thought, the valley thought, the thought Hollowmere would want him to have. Whatever this was—a king's quarrel, a crown's business—it belonged to the loud world beyond the passes, the world of bells and banners and men who sang. Hollowmere had bought its silence honestly. The ward-stones stood at every entrance to the valley, older than the village, older than any name anyone remembered giving them, and they drank magic the way riverbanks drank rain. Nothing resonant survived here. No spell, no song-craft, no whisper of the uncanny. And nothing of Zephyrine's gift either—the hearing, the terrible listening that had followed him since boyhood like a second shadow, the voices of the dead that had once made sleep impossible and childhood a kind of drowning.

Ten years ago he had walked into this valley with blistered feet and ringing ears, and the silence had closed over him like water over a stone. He had learned to carve bones instead of hearing them. Memorial bones: little works of patience and love for the families of the dying, names and prayers and small scenes cut into sheep femur and ox rib, so that when grief came it would have somewhere to put its hands. It was good work. It was quiet work.

*Burn it,* he told himself again, and did not move toward the fire.

Because there was the other thing. The thing he had been circling since dusk, the way his tongue circles a broken tooth.

The ward had flickered.

When the courier died—at the ford, in Zephyrine's arms, mid-word—heard it. For the first time in ten years, Zephyrine had *heard* a dead man's voice. Not through his ears. Through whatever it was inside him that had never stopped listening, the sense that slept in the valley like a hibernating animal and had woken, briefly, horribly, at the moment the stranger's soul let go of its body.

And if Zephyrine could hear the dead again, then the ward was not holding. The ward was failing. And Zephyrine did not know which frightened him more—that the quiet he had built his whole life around was ending, or that some traitorous part of him, deep beneath the dread, had missed it.

He fed the fire and sat down across from the corpse, and made himself look at the dead man's face properly for the first time. Young. Younger than Zephyrine had guessed in the chaos at the ford—younger than Tam, maybe, or no older. Frost-burned cheeks, chapped lips split from wind. A courier's calluses on his fingers, reins-worn. Someone had loved this boy enough to mend his coat with careful stitches, two different colors of thread, and someone out there was going to wait for him until waiting turned to grieving.

"Who are you?" Zephyrine asked softly, because asking was habit, and because the question cost nothing when the dead couldn't answer.

Except they could. Except, now, they might.

* * *

It came near midnight, or what he judged was midnight by the fire's settling.

Not through the air. Not through his ears. It rose up through the floorboards, through the bones of his own feet, through the marrow-deep listening place he had spent a decade starving into silence—and it was worse than he remembered. Worse than the drowning chorus of his childhood. Because this voice was alone, and patient, and knew exactly who it was speaking to.

*Carver.*

The word went through him like cold iron. He gripped the arms of his chair. "No," he said aloud, to the empty room, to himself. "You're dead. Dead is quiet. Here, dead is quiet."

*Not here,* the voice said, and it sounded tired now, and young, and terribly human. *Not anymore. Can't you feel it?*

He could. He had been refusing to feel it for hours. The air in the cottage had a texture to it tonight, faint and wrong, like the first tremor before a rockslide—a hum below hearing, a pressure behind the eyes. The ward-stones at the valley's mouths had held back the world's magic for longer than memory. They were old, and old things wear thin, and something about this death—this death specifically, this messenger, this map—had found the thin place and pressed against it.

Or the courier's death itself had torn it. He didn't know. Understanding could come later. Right now the dead boy was talking, and Zephyrine was listening, and both of those facts felt like betrayals of the valley that had sheltered him.

"I don't want this," Zephyrine said. His voice came out rough. "Whatever message you carried, whoever sent you—give it to someone else. There are roads out of this valley that don't pass my door."

*There aren't,* said the dead courier simply. *I looked. I ran three days with them behind me singing, carver, do you know what it sounds like, being hunted by singers? They harmonize while they run. They harmonized while Halric fell.* A pause, and when the voice came again it was smaller. *Halric was my partner. We rode together six years. They took his voice out of him between one verse and the next, and then he kept riding beside me and I couldn't tell if he was still in there, and I couldn't stop, because stopping meant the map stops too.*

Zephyrine's hands had gone still on the chair arms. "The map," he said. "What's on it?"

*You know what's on it. You've felt it since I put it in your hands. You've been pretending you haven't.* The dead boy's voice gained a strange steadiness, the way the dying sometimes do, as if the worst has already happened and everything after is just weather. *Open it. Open it and I'll tell you what I paid for. What Halric paid for. What the woman in Corrowdale paid for, and the ferryman at Lisk, and all the rest of us down the line like beads on a wire.*

Zephyrine didn't move. Then he did. He crossed the room because his legs decided before he did, and he lifted the oilcloth wrapping because his hands decided before he did, and he broke the grey wax seal because some part of him, buried under ten years of carved silence, had always known he would.

Inside was a map—not drawn but *etched*, scratched into a membrane of vellum so thin he could see firelight through it. Roads he half-knew and rivers he didn't, running north, always north, past Bellharrow and Greyford Bridge and the high passes, to a mark at the top of the page rendered in ink gone brown with age: a small abbey shape, ruined, with a spiral beneath it descending into darkness.

Beneath the abbey, in a hand different from the cartographer's—older, harder, pressed deep enough to score the vellum—two words:

**ASHEN CRYPT.**

"The king," Zephyrine said quietly. "This leads to the king."

*The king is dead,* the courier said, and there was no drama in it, only the flat exhaustion of a fact carried too far. *Dead ten years. Everyone thinks he sits his throne in Cael Adair and grows strange in his old age, keeps his own company, lets the Choir speak for him. But I saw the throne room, carver. My last delivery before this one. I stood close enough to touch him.* The voice shivered. *There's nothing inside the robes. There's a thing wearing him like a coat. It smiles with his face and it eats souls, and the Ember Choir feeds it—they bind the captured ones, the gifted ones, and feed them up the resonance-glass to the throne, and the thing on the throne gets stronger, and the winter gets longer, and nobody sings anything that isn't theirs anymore.*

The fire popped. Outside, snow ticked against the shutters, and Zephyrine became aware—gradually, then all at once—of another sound underneath the night. Faint. Distant. Layered.

Singing.

Far off beyond the northern ridge, so soft it might have been wind, harmonies moved in the dark. Voices braided together in intervals no human throat would choose naturally, sweet and wrong, like honey poured over a blade. The Ember Choir. Sweeping the mountain passes, hunting the courier who was no longer running because he was lying on a bench in a carver's cottage with a blanket to his chin.

"They'll come here," Zephyrine said. "They'll follow you here. Into the valley."

*I killed your quiet,* the dead boy said, and for the first time he sounded sorry. *I'm sorry. I chose the ford because the ward was weakest there—I could feel it fraying for miles, like ice in springtime. Your valley was already leaking, carver. I just fell through the crack.* A pause. *But listen. Listen, because I'm fading and I don't have much left to fade with. The map reaches the Ashen Crypt before midwinter. Under the ruined abbey at the top of the world. There's someone there—something there—waiting. If it arrives after midwinter, the thing on the throne will be strong enough that nothing unseats it. Ever. The long winter of legend, the one the old women sing about. Generations under that thing's tongue. So it has to be midwinter. It has to be—*

The voice thinned like smoke in wind.

"Wait." Zephyrine was on his feet without remembering standing. "Wait—who waits there? Who sent you? Whose seal was on this?"

Silence. Real silence, the valley's old silence, except it wasn't real anymore, was it, and never would be again.

Then, at the very edge of hearing, almost fond:

*You'll go anyway. Carvers always do. You make things hold together. It's what you're for.*

And he was gone. Not departed, not passed on—just gone, like a candle pinched out, and the cottage was only a cottage, and the corpse was only a corpse, and Zephyrine stood alone in firelight holding a piece of scored vellum with a dead king's road written on it.

* * *

He didn't burn it.

He wanted to be honest with himself about that, at least. He stood at the hearth with the map in one hand and every reasonable argument in the other, and the fire was warm and willing, and he did not burn it. Because the courier had stitched-coat kindness in his face and a dead partner named Halric, and because somewhere north a thing wearing a king was eating people the way rot eats fruit, and because—

Because Zephyrine Dunmore had spent ten years carving names into bone so the dead wouldn't be forgotten, and it turned out that was not a trade. It was a nature. You couldn't carve your way out of what you were.

He dressed for the cold. He packed bread, the good knife, flint, the roll of finished memorial bones that belonged to Widow Serris's family because he would not leave even now with a promise unkept. He wrote a note for Elder Maren and hated every word of it, because he knew what the elders would say, what they'd have to say: that he was bringing the loud world down on them, that the ward was failing and it was his fault, that the marked man should have stayed marked and silent. He understood them. He'd have said it too, a month ago.

At the door he stopped and looked back once at the courier on the bench. "I'll see you get a stone," Zephyrine told him. "A proper one. With your name on it, if anyone ever tells me what it was."

The corpse said nothing, being dead and finally, mercifully, quiet—but the air of the room felt less empty than it should have, and Zephyrine chose, deliberately, to take that for thanks.

Outside, the night was bitter and brilliant, stars hard as nailheads. And the valley—his valley, his quiet valley—was humming. He felt it through his boots, through the ground itself: the ward-stones' great slow note fraying at the edges, unraveling thread by thread, letting the world's magic seep back in like meltwater through a roof. By spring, he guessed, Hollowmere would be no quieter than anywhere else. The sanctuary was already gone. All that remained was to decide what a man did with the silence while he still had some of it.

He walked to the ford. The ice had refrozen over the hole where the courier had fallen, and beneath the ice the black water moved, patient, carrying snow-melt toward the lowlands and away. North was the ridge. North was the distant sweetness-and-steel of the Choir's harmony, still sweeping, still searching, closer than it had been at midnight.

Zephyrine pulled his collar up, settled the map against his chest, and stepped onto the north road.

Behind him the valley slept, not knowing yet that its age of quiet had ended. Ahead of him the mountains waited, full of song.

By morning, he knew, the elders would find the note. By noon, they would find the courier. And by the time the sun cleared the eastern crags, hoofbeats and lantern-light would already be moving along the valley's southern rim—because Elder Maren, waking early as she always did, would open her door to a sight she had not seen in thirty years: strangers in white-and-grey livery, standing in the snow of Hollowmere's lane, smiling, and asking, very politely, in perfect unison,

whether anyone in the valley had heard a voice.
