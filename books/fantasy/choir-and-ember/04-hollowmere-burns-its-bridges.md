## 4. Hollowmere Burns Its Bridges

The elders summoned him at dusk, and Zephyrine knew before he crossed the square what they would say.

He had spent the day doing what he always did on the third of Hearthmonth: carving the memorial bones for those who would not see spring. Old Widow Corren's hip-bone, to be strung at her door with the rest of her family's. A child's finger-bone, small as a reed, for the miller's daughter lost to fever. His knife moved in the old patterns—spirals for long life cut short, interlaced knots for love that outlasted death—and his hands were steady, because the ward made them steady, because Hollowmere was quiet, quiet, always quiet, and he had told himself for eleven years that quiet was the same thing as peace.

But the courier lay in Elder Maren's smokehouse, wrapped in sailcloth, and the whole valley had smelled the woodsmoke of urgent councils since dawn.

They met in the moot-hall, a low stone building that smelled of tallow and wet wool and generations of nervous sweat. Nine elders sat along the curved bench, and their faces in the firelight were the faces of people who had already decided something terrible and were waiting only for the courage to say it aloud. Zephyrine stood in the center of the floor with his carving knife still tucked in his belt, because no one had thought to tell him to leave it and no one was brave enough to ask.

"Zeph." Elder Bren spoke first, because Bren always spoke first; he had been a merchant before he was an elder and believed all problems could be talked around if you circled them enough times. "You know why we've gathered."

"The stranger," Zephyrine said. "The courier."

"You touched him." Bren's hands spread, conciliatory. "Everyone saw it. You held his hand when he died."

"He was dying in the snow. What would you have me do, fold my arms and watch?"

"No one is saying—" Bren rubbed his jaw. "Zeph, there are riders on the high road. Tam saw them this morning, three days out, flying grey and gold. Grey and gold, boy. That's the crown's colors. That's *them*."

The Ember Choir. The word went through the hall like a cold draft, and nine pairs of eyes dropped to the floor as though looking at Zephyrine might summon them faster.

"They're not coming for the valley," Zephyrine said quietly. "They're coming for the map."

"And where is the map?"

He did not answer. The silence stretched, and Elder Maren closed her eyes briefly, as if in prayer, or resignation.

* * *

Elder Maren had buried two husbands and one king, and she had voted on the council of Hollowmere for thirty years, which meant she had learned that leadership was mostly the art of choosing which grief to carry personally and which to set down for others to share. Tonight she carried all of it alone.

She watched the boy stand in the center of the hall—not a boy anymore, thirty years old if he was a day, but she had known him at seven, when he first came down from the north past with his mother's hand gripping his shoulder and his eyes too old for his face. They had come to Hollowmere fleeing something. Nobody asked what. That was the covenant of the valley: the ward kept magic out, kept the whispers of the dead out, kept everything soft and muffled and survivable, and in exchange nobody asked anybody anything about the world beyond the passes.

Eleven years of good behavior. Eleven years of memorial bones carved clean and true, of sickbeds attended, of never once complaining when the ward's silence pressed on him like deep water. She had watched him build himself a small life the way a man builds a wall, stone by careful stone, and she had thought, until three days ago, that the wall would hold.

"The vote," she said now, and her voice came out harder than she intended. "Let's have it done."

Bren stated it plainly, because plainness was cruelty's favorite disguise: that Hollowmere could not shelter a marked man; that the Ember Choir burned villages for less than harboring one who heard the dead; that Zephyrine Dunmore must be gone from the valley by sunrise, with provisions and a fair horse, and that the elders' official word to any who came asking would be that he had stolen away in the night like a thief.

Maren looked around the curve of the bench. She could stop it. Her vote carried the weight of seniority; a split council would have to deliberate another day, and another day might matter—or might not. She thought about the riders on the high road. She thought about the miller's daughter under the fresh-turned earth, about all the daughters and sons of Hollowmere who were alive because the valley had kept its head down through ten years of gathering dark.

She thought: *I am sixty-eight years old and I am going to choose the living over the good, and I will have to live with the difference.*

Her hand, when the vote came, stayed in her lap.

Eight hands rose. Hers did not, but it did not rise against, either, and Zephyrine saw that distinction land on his face like a blow. She wished, absurdly, that he had been stupid enough not to notice—that he would hate her cleanly instead of understanding her, which hurt far worse.

"So it's decided," Bren said, with relief making his voice too bright. "At first light, then. Zeph—boy—we're not heartless. You'll have the bay mare, and stores, and—"

"I'll want to see him buried," Zephyrine said. "The courier. Properly, before I go."

"There's no time—"

"There's time," Maren said, and her voice ended the argument. It was the least she could give him, and she gave it the way she gave everything tonight: too late, and grudgingly, and meaning it completely.

* * *

Zephyrine dug the grave himself, by lantern light, in the strip of consecrated ground behind the moot-hall where Hollowmere had buried its dead for two hundred years. The frost came out of the ground in black sheets and his breath hung around him like a shroud, and he worked without stopping because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the thing coiled under his breastbone—the map, the message, the dying man's fingers closing over his—would uncoil and swallow him.

Tam found him there. Of course Tam found him there. Tam had a talent for arriving exactly when Zephyrine least wanted company and most needed it, a talent he had exercised since they were boys stealing apples from the cloister orchard.

"I heard," Tam said, which covered everything. He sat down on the grave's edge, careless of the dirt, the way he had been careless of dirt his whole life—a big, warm-handed man, a farrier, with soot permanently worked into the creases of his knuckles. "It's horseshit, Zeph. Eight old cowards voting out the only person in this valley who ever did an honest day's kindness."

"It's not cowardice." Zephyrine leaned on the spade, breathing hard. "Or it is, but it's the kind I'd do. They're protecting four hundred people. I'm one man."

"You're *my*—"

"Don't." He said it fast, before Tam could finish, because he knew how the sentence ended and he could not afford to hear it. Not tonight. "Don't say things we'll both remember."

Tam was quiet for a moment. Then he said, in the reasonable tone he used on horses he was about to shoe, "Then I'm coming with you."

"No."

"Zeph—"

"*No.*" The word cracked out of him louder than he intended, and somewhere in the dark a dog started barking. He lowered his voice, knelt, took up the spade again. "Tam. Listen to me. Whatever's written on this map—and I haven't looked, I swear it, I haven't let myself look—it has the Ember Choir moving behind it like a shadow. Grey and gold on the high road. These are the people who bind souls into service, Tam. They sing you out of yourself and wear what's left. I will not hand them my oldest friend alongside their quarry."

"So your plan is to walk alone into winter with everyone in the kingdom hunting you."

"My plan is to walk alone into winter with everyone in the kingdom hunting me," Zephyrine agreed, "and you're going to stay here and shoe horses and marry that girl from the dairy and live so long and so boringly that you die at ninety with grandchildren arguing over your boots. That's your job. That's the whole of it."

"That's horseshit," Tam said again, but softly now, and Zephyrine heard the grief underneath it, the specific grief of a man watching his best friend choose a door he cannot follow him through.

They finished the grave in silence. Zephyrine laid the courier down with the sailcloth folded back so his face could meet the sky—he had been someone's son, somewhere, before the roads ate him—and Zephyrine set a carved bone at the head of the grave, a quick thing, half-finished, spirals and knots, a name he didn't know rendered in the only language he had to give.

When he stood, Tam was holding out a bundle. Oilcloth, tied with twine.

"What's this."

"Open it."

Inside: a whetstone, a roll of bandages, dried venison, a heavy iron knife with a horn handle that Zephyrine recognized—Tam had made it two winters ago and sworn it was for his own belt—and, wrapped in flannel at the very bottom, a pair of fur-lined gloves, big enough for Zephyrine's hands, stitched with the crooked patience of a man who hated needlework and had done it anyway.

"You're impossible," Zephyrine said. His voice did something humiliating on the last syllable.

"Sunrise," Tam said. "I'll bring the mare around. And Zeph—" he paused at the edge of the lantern light, big and dark and beloved, "—you can refuse my company. You can't refuse my prayers. Those ride free."

* * *

Elder Maren did not sleep.

She sat in her kitchen past midnight with a single candle and the ledger of Hollowmere open before her, though she wasn't reading it. In the morning she would sign the statement Bren had drafted—*the carver Dunmore fled in the night, taking what was not his; we had no part of him*—and her signature would be a lie with ink on it, and the ink would probably save lives, and none of that arithmetic helped her stomach.

She had voted out troublemakers before. Sheep thieves, a wife-beater, once a hedge-witch whose charms had gone bad and rotted a well. She had slept after each one. This one she would not sleep after, and the difference, she understood, was that Zephyrine had never done anything except exist near something dangerous. That was the new age they were living in, the age the riders on the high road were bringing: existence itself was the crime, and every valley and village was going to have to decide, one by one, what its survival was worth, and every elder in the land was going to learn what Maren was learning tonight—that the price was paid not in coin but in the specific weight of a young man's face when eight neighbors refuse to look at him.

Near dawn she rose, went to the chest at the foot of her bed, and took out the thing wrapped in her wedding linens. Her second husband's horseman's cloak, heavy oiled wool, warm enough for the high passes. And beneath it, a purse she had been adding to since the war taxes started, against a day when running might become necessary—for herself, she had always told herself. For herself.

She left the bundle on the mounting-block by the stable gate where he would pass at first light, with no note, because a note could be evidence and Maren had not survived thirty years of councils by writing things down.

But she stood at her window while the sky went grey, and she watched him come, leading the bay mare, and she watched him stop at the mounting-block and understand, and she watched him look up at the village—at the smoke rising from the hearths of the people who had just cast him out—with an expression she hoped she would never see on anyone's face again as long as she lived. Not hatred. Worse. *Comprehension.* The face of a man filing the betrayal away with all his other facts about the world, to be carried quietly forever.

Then he turned east, toward the pass, toward the grey and gold waiting somewhere on the high road, and rode out of the valley that had given him eleven years of silence and was now giving him back to the noise.

Maren blew out her candle and finally, in the full light of morning, slept.

* * *

The ward ended at the standing stones on the eastern rim, and Zephyrine felt it let go of him the way a hand lets go—suddenly, completely, all at once.

One stride, and the world roared back in.

Not sound. Something older than sound. The dead of eleven years, everywhere at once: the bones sleeping in the barrow-field below the pass murmuring in their crowded beds, the old battlefield two valleys north still whispering its endless arithmetic of grievance, the courier's grave behind him already faint but insistent, repeating his last message over and over like a bell rung in another room. *The king is dead. The map. Before midwinter. The king is dead.* And underneath it all, vast and patient, the hum of the mountain itself, which had died once, long ago, in some way Zephyrine did not want to examine, and had never stopped talking about it.

He reined in the mare and sat shaking at the boundary stone, hands over his ears, doing nothing, being nothing, just enduring the flood after eleven years of drought.

When he could breathe again, he fumbled in Tam's oilcloth bundle for the wax he knew would be there—there was always wax in anything Tam packed, farriers' habit—and stopped himself. Not yet. Wax was for the Choir's songs. This, the dead's murmur, was his own weather, and he would have to learn to walk in it again.

He looked back once. Hollowmere lay small and white in the valley floor, smoke rising straight from a hundred hearths, beautiful and safe and finished with him. Somewhere down there, Tam was saying goodbye to a girl from the dairy, keeping his promise, staying alive and boring. Good. That was the point. That was the entire point of everything.

Zephyrine turned the mare's head toward the pass, and the wind coming down off the heights carried something that was not wind—a distant harmonic, thin as a hair drawn across skin, voices in intervals no human throat made naturally, sweeping the high country the way a lamp sweeps a cellar.

His bones ached with it. Every carved bone in his saddlebag ached with it, singing faintly in sympathy, and the dead all across the moor fell silent at once, listening, the way rabbits go still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

The Ember Choir was already hunting.

Zephyrine put his heels to the mare and rode north into the mountains, carrying a dead man's map and a living friend's gloves, and began—to the rhythm of hooves on frozen stone, to the far-off sweetness of the hunters' song—to teach himself the first and hardest lesson of the road:

how to be silent in a world that had suddenly, terribly, learned his name.
