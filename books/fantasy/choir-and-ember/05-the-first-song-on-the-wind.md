## 5. The First Song on the Wind

The mountain road out of Hollowmere climbed through pine and shadow for two days before Zephyrine allowed himself to believe he had escaped.

He told himself it was caution, not cowardice, that kept him off the lowland tracks. Caution that made him walk the shepherd paths instead, the ones worn thin by generations of sheep and forgotten by everyone else. The map rode against his chest in an oilskin wrap, strapped beneath his coat where no eye would find it, and its bone handle—cold even through wool and linen—pressed against his sternum like a second heartbeat he could not put down.

The ward had failed. He felt its absence the way a man feels a missing tooth: constantly, involuntarily, his tongue returning again and again to the gap. For thirty-one years Hollowmere's valley had been silent to him—not quiet, but *silent*, a blessed nullity where the dead had no more voice than stones. Now, on the open mountain, the world hummed with what he'd spent half a life not hearing.

Not much. Not yet. The high passes were sparsely peopled and their dead were old and settled, mostly. A woman's murmur near a cairn of stacked stones, patient as snowfall: *the third stone is my daughter's, she laid it herself, she laid it herself.* The faint grievance of a drover under a landslide scar: *my mules, someone has my mules still.* Faint things. Distant things. Voices like smoke through a keyhole.

He could bear those. What he could not bear—and did not hear until the third morning, when the road crested a shoulder of grey rock and dropped toward a long treeless saddle—was the singing.

It came on the wind from the south and west, so faint at first that he mistook it for weather. A harmonic shimmer in the air, a pressure behind the ears, a sweetness with no source. He stopped. The mules—no, there were no mules; the mules belonged to a dead man under a landslide—he stopped and stood with his pack straps biting his shoulders, and listened, and understood within four heartbeats that he was listening to nothing natural.

Voices. Many voices, braided. They rose in intervals that no village choir would dare, suspensions that hung unresolved like a held breath, and underneath the beauty of them ran something else—a pulse, a rhythm like a slow heart, deliberate as a searchlight sweeping a dark field. The sound passed *through* him. Not past him. Through him. It touched the bones of his skull and rang them lightly, the way a carver taps a blank of bone to hear its density, and Zephyrine knew—with a certainty that arrived whole and cold, without argument—that the song was doing to him exactly that. Tapping. Listening for what rang true.

*Resonant souls.* The courier had said the words with his last wet breath. *They sing and sing and if you answer, even inside, they find you.*

Zephyrine did not answer. He clamped his jaw, locked his throat, thought of nothing—of fog, of flat grey water, of the interior of a closed box—and walked, quickly, down into the saddle and behind the first screen of boulders. The harmony swelled and faded, swelled and faded, moving across the passes like weather, and by the time it had thinned to nothing he was shaking so hard he had to sit on a stone and put his head between his knees.

His bones ached. That was the worst of it. Not his ears—his bones, all of them, a deep marrow-soreness as though he had been played too long, an instrument handled roughly. He sat in the cold wind and waited for the ache to drain out of him, and while he sat he made himself think, because thinking was the one discipline the valley had left him. Carving taught patience; patience taught thought. Think, then.

They were sweeping the passes. Which meant they were searching systematically, which meant they had something to search *for*. A missing courier, perhaps. A stolen map. An unbound voice moving north—they would know by now that something had come out of Hollowmere, because the ward had failed, and wards did not fail politely. Wards failed like beacons.

And the song found resonant souls. His gift made him loud, then. Thirty-one years of silence and he had imagined himself hidden, and instead he was a struck bell in a country of mutes, ringing with every step.

"Right," he said aloud, and his voice cracked on the word, and the emptiness after it seemed very wide and very attentive.

He got up and walked north, and for the rest of that day he listened the way a hunted thing listens, which is to say: constantly, exhaustively, and to everything.

* * *

That night he did not make a fire. He sheltered in a hollow beneath an overhang of granite, wrapped in his cloak with his back to the stone, and worked by the last grey light with his knife and a stub of tallow candle he did not dare light. Below him the pass descended in switchbacks toward a valley where two or three farm lights glimmered, small and warm and fatally unaware. From somewhere beyond the southern ridges—too far to see, not too far to hear—the Choir sang again.

Closer tonight. Or louder. He could not decide which frightened him more.

He listened properly this time, forcing himself past the fear into analysis, because the fear was useless and the analysis might keep him alive. There were perhaps twenty voices. No—thirty. The harmonies shifted as he listened, sections folding over one another, and he realized with reluctant admiration that they were not singing one piece but several interlocked pieces, phased against each other so that the combined pattern swept and circled. It was beautiful. That was the trap of it; he could feel the beauty working on him, feel his own breath wanting to deepen into the phrase-shapes, wanting to follow. If he had been a singer—and he was not, his voice was a carver's voice, low and rough—if he had been a singer he might have hummed along without ever deciding to. And that, he understood, was how it caught people. You didn't answer the Choir. You simply *harmonized*, the way anyone harmonizes with music in the blood, and the moment your note slid in beside theirs they had you. A resonance located. A name entered into some ledger.

*I will never sing again,* he promised himself, absurdly, solemnly, there in the dark under the granite. *I will go mute before I hum.*

Then he took out his knife and began making himself deaf.

The wax came from the candle. He warmed it between his palms, worked it soft, pressed plugs of it deep into both ears. It muffled the wind and the small night-noises of the mountain, but when the Choir next swelled he found—with a sinking that surprised him by its depth—that the song came through anyway. Muffled, yes, but present, and worse: the *bones* carried it. The vibration entered through his jaw, his cheekbones, the small bones behind the wax, and the harmonies rang in his skull as clearly as ever. Wax was no good alone. Wax was a door mat set before an open gate.

So he carved.

He chose a flat piece of fallow deer antler from his kit—he always traveled with material; habit older than sense—and cut from it two small discs, thin as coins, and on the inner face of each he carved a spiral. Not decoration. He didn't understand yet precisely why it worked, only that his hands knew what his mind was learning: that a voice given a shape must follow the shape, that sound poured into a carved groove ran the groove's course like water in a channel and arrived at the center *changed*. He had carved memorial bones for eleven years in Hollowmere—names, dates, litanies of grief—and the families said the bones felt quiet to hold. Peaceful. He had called it craft. Perhaps it had been craft. Perhaps it had also been this: grooves that swallowed sound, spirals that led a voice inward and gave it nowhere to return from.

He fitted disc and wax together and pressed the whole device into each ear, and lay back against the granite, and waited.

The Choir came up over the southern ridge near midnight, or so he guessed by the moon's position, since he could not hear the hours anymore. He felt it before anything—a pressure, a marrow-tremor, the ache returning to his long bones like the memory of a fever—and then the harmonies rolled across the pass above his shelter, vast and intricate and utterly, perfectly silent. He watched the stars shiver—no, the stars did not shiver; his eyes did. The song moved through him and around him and it was as if he stood behind thick glass at his own funeral: he could see the music happen and none of it reached him. The spiral discs drank it. The carved channels took each rising phrase and led it down and down and down into the small blind center of the antler, and there it ended.

For the first time since the ford, Zephyrine slept.

* * *

He learned the discipline of it over the following days, and the learning changed him in ways he noticed and ways he did not.

Walking deaf was harder than it sounded. The world without sound was a world without warning: he startled at shadows on the path, at the flick of a ptarmigan bursting from scree inches from his boot. He learned to turn his head constantly, checking behind like a man followed—which he was, in every way that mattered—and he learned to read the ground for the vibrations his feet sent up through his boots, a dull semaphore of hooves or footsteps or falling water. He learned to speak less, because speaking with sealed ears made his own voice a boom in a barrel, ugly and animal, and he hated it. By the fourth day he had stopped talking entirely except once, to swear, when he nearly walked off a cornice.

But the deeper change was stranger, and it took him until the fifth night to name it.

Silence, he discovered, was not empty. With the Choir shut out and the world shut out, the space inside his own skull grew loud with everything else. Memories he had not visited in years came prowling: his mother's hands dusted with bone-meal, teaching him the grain of a knuckle joint. Tam at fourteen, laughing so hard at the frozen ford he fell through it. The first corpse whose whisper he had ever heard, when he was nine—the old miller, dead three days, murmuring *tell her I fixed the sluice, tell her I fixed it*—and the sick weeks after, when his parents had looked at him differently, gently, the way you look at a wound that hasn't decided whether to close.

He heard all of it now, in the enforced silence, and he began to wonder whether that was the true purpose of the carving. Not just to stop the Choir's song. To stop *everything*, including the past, including himself. He was building his own little Hollowmere and wearing it in his ears, and some cold honest corner of him observed that he had been doing this his entire life.

*You hide well,* said no voice at all. *You have always hidden well.*

He checked the map that night, unwrapping it inside his bedroll with numb fingers, needing to see that it still existed, that the mission was real and not another thing he had invented to justify running. The bloodstain on its corner had gone the brown of dried bracken. The route north wound up the map's spine of mountains, through a market town marked Bellharrow, then down toward the plains and—far off, past rivers he did not know—the black mark of the Ashen Crypt. He traced the line with a fingertip and put it away quickly, before the temptation could form of simply stopping. Of finding some high valley of his own and carving bones until the war above him resolved itself without his help.

*No one knows you're carrying it,* the temptation whispered, in what was nearly Tam's voice. *No one would blame—*

He sealed his ears and slept, and dreamed of singing, and woke with his jaw clenched so hard it ached.

* * *

On the seventh day the passes narrowed and the Choir stopped being weather and started being a hunt.

He came over a rise above a tarn of grey ice and saw them—not the singers themselves but the evidence: a shepherd's hut with its door hanging open, wool still on the carding combs, porridge going to skin in its pot. Fled in haste or taken in haste; he could not tell, and did not stay to learn. Two miles farther, a shrine at a trail junction, its carved saint freshly defaced—not broken, but altered, the serene mouth chiseled open wider, the throat hollowed into a flute-like tube. Someone had made the saint into an instrument. Zephyrine looked at it for a long moment and decided there were things it was healthier not to understand.

And the harmonies came more often now, twice a day, sometimes three times, sweeping the high ground in great methodical arcs. Once, from a ridge, he actually saw them: a double file of grey-cloaked figures strung along the opposite slope like beads, faces lifted, mouths open, and the air between them visibly—visibly!—trembling, a heat-haze where no heat was. Even through the carved silence he felt them pass, a pressure like deep water, and he crouched among the boulders with his arms over his head and his heart slamming and understood that he was seeing the kingdom's new priesthood at its devotions, and that devotion and hunting had become, in Cael Adair, the same act.

One of the figures paused. Turned its hooded face across the valley, directly toward his boulder field. Held there.

Ten heartbeats. Twenty. Zephyrine did not breathe. The spiral discs drank and drank at the searching song, and somewhere inside the antler's channels a hundred borrowed phrases died their small deaths, and the hooded figure tilted its head—listening for a resonance that was not arriving—and finally moved on, and the file of grey cloaks flowed away northward along the slope, and Zephyrine lay against the freezing stone and laughed once, a single bark of sound with no humor in it whatsoever, and was appalled to find his eyes wet.

Not from fear, he told himself. Wind. Altitude. But his hands, when he looked at them, were trembling in the precise rhythm of a held note finally released.

* * *

He came down out of the high passes on the ninth day, because a man cannot eat scenery, and below him, cupped in a fold of foothills where three roads met, lay Bellharrow.

Even at that distance it looked wrong. Market towns made a particular noise, and he knew it well from his rare childhood trips to Lowmarsh—cart-wheels, hawkers, dogs, the rough democratic din of trade. Bellharrow made no such noise. Smoke rose from its chimneys in orderly columns. The roads fed into it and the town sat among its fields like a congregation, and on the wind, faint and intermittent, came a sound he had hoped never to hear outside the high passes: voices, many voices, untrained and ordinary, rising together in a harmony he recognized.

The townsfolk were singing the Choir's liturgy. All of them. At once. As naturally as breathing.

Zephyrine stood on the last shoulder of the mountain with his pack straps cutting his shoulders and the map cold against his chest, and looked down at the warm-lit windows of Bellharrow, and understood that his choices had narrowed to a shape he did not like. His food was gone. The higher passes were being swept in arcs that would close behind him like a snare. Ahead lay the only town for thirty miles, humming with the enemy's song—and somewhere in his kit, three spare discs of carved antler, and enough tallow for a few more pairs of ears besides.

He began, deliberately, unhurriedly, to carve.
