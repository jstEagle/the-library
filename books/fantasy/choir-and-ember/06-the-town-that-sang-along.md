## 6. The Town That Sang Along

Bellharrow smelled of tallow and wet wool and woodsmoke, the way market towns always did in the last gray weeks before midwinter, and beneath all of it—so faint that Zephyrine might have missed it if he had not spent three days learning to listen for it—it hummed.

He noticed it first at the gate.

The toll-keeper, a broad man with a beard crusted with frost, counted out his coppers with one hand while his lips moved without sound. Then, as Zephyrine passed under the arch, the man's humming rose into audibility: four low notes, rising, falling, rising again, patient as water dripping into a well. The tune had no words. It needed none. It was the kind of melody that lived in the body rather than the mind, the kind you caught from a neighbor the way you caught a yawn, and it went on and on because it was built to go on and on, a circle of notes with no beginning worth naming and no end at all.

Zephyrine knew it. That was the terrible thing. He had never heard it before in his life, and he knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat—and by the third note he felt his own lips wanting to shape around it, his own chest wanting to carry it, and only the wax plugs he'd carved from candle stubs and pressed deep into his ears kept the circle from closing around him too.

*Walk deaf*, he reminded himself, and walked deaf.

Inside the walls, Bellharrow was beautiful in the way that rotting fruit is beautiful—glossy, warm-colored, wrong. Market day. Stalls crowded the square with winter stores: turnips gleaming like wet stones, wheels of pale cheese, salted fish stacked in silver rows, bolts of undyed wool. Children ran shrieking between the stalls. Dogs stole sausages and were chased and were not truly angry about it. A fiddler played on the steps of the moot-hall, and half of Bellharrow danced.

And every single soul in the square hummed.

Not together—that was the horror of it, Zephyrine realized as he drifted along the edge of the crowd with his hood up and his eyes down. Not in unison, not like a choir. Each person hummed at their own tempo, their own pitch, weaving in and out of phase with their neighbors so that the whole town sounded like one enormous instrument played by a hundred careless hands. The baker hummed while he weighed loaves. The fishwife hummed through her sales patter. Two old men arguing over the price of a pig hummed *through* their argument, the tune riding underneath the anger like a river under ice. A mother swayed her infant to the four-note circle, and the infant, too young for anything else, hummed back.

They didn't know they were doing it. He could see that. When the fiddler struck up something quick and bright, faces turned toward him and laughed and clapped—but the moment the song ended, the liturgy seeped back up out of them like groundwater, filling the space the music left behind. Nobody looked embarrassed. Nobody looked anything at all. Their faces were comfortable. That was the word that kept coming back to him as he made his way down the row of stalls: *comfortable*. Bellharrow was the most contented town he had ever seen, and he wanted to run from it the way you run from a room where the floor is warm.

In Hollowmere, magic had been a locked door. Here it was an open window on a mild evening, and everything in the house had already crawled out through it.

He bought what he needed with coins counted carefully into gloved palms: hard bread, dried apples, a twist of salt pork, a new flask for water. He kept his answers short when vendors asked where he was from—a traveler, north country, no family—and found that shortness cost him nothing, because nobody in Bellharrow was much interested in conversation. They were interested in the tune. Conversation happened around it, over it, through it, the way reeds grow through a fence. Once, buying bread, he caught himself nodding along to the baker's humming, his foot keeping the circle's time, and he snatched his foot still as if he'd stepped on a snake.

*It doesn't take your mind*, the dead courier had said, eleven days ago now, in the flickering dark beside the ford. *That's what I told myself too. It takes your want. You keep thinking. You just stop wanting anything the song doesn't want.*

Zephyrine looked at the baker's round, peaceful face and believed every word.

* * *

The map rode against his ribs in its oilskin wrapping, sewn into the lining of his coat where a seam should be. He touched it perhaps forty times a day—an ache disguised as a habit, a habit disguised as a prayer. Eleven days since the ford. Eleven days since Elder Maren's council had voted him out of the only home he'd ever known, since Tam had stood in the falling snow offering to come and Zephyrine had said *no* so hard it had cracked something in them both. Eleven days of mountain passes and frozen fords and the distant, bone-aching harmonies of the Choir sweeping the high country, listening for voices that rang true.

He told himself he would stop touching the map. He told himself a lot of things.

The old woman found him at dusk, at the edge of the square where the lamplighter was working his way along the brackets, and she found him because of the map—which meant she found him because of himself, because some part of him had been broadcasting like a struck bell ever since the ward over Hollowmere began to fail, and he could no more hide what he was than a bell can hide its ringing.

"You're leaking," she said.

He turned. She was small and bent as a question mark, wrapped in a shawl the color of ash, with a face like a walnut shell and eyes that were not comfortable at all. In a square full of humming, contented faces, hers was the first face he'd seen in Bellharrow that seemed to be doing any work. She was looking at him the way a farrier looks at a horse with a stone in its shoe.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Leaking." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd tell a man his bootlace was undone. "You're standing there trying to look like a wool merchant, and you're shining like a lighthouse. Anyone with the tune in them can feel you. Half this square has been drifting toward you all afternoon without knowing why." She nodded toward the lamplighter, who had paused on his ladder, head tilted toward Zephyrine like a hound scenting wind. "See? There goes one now. Come with me, boy, before the whole town comes to see what's shining."

Every instinct he had honed in eleven days of flight said: *walk away*. But she was not humming. In a town of two thousand throats carrying the circle, hers was silent, and silence had become the most precious thing in the world to him. He followed her.

Her house leaned against the town wall like a drunk against a friend, one story of blackened timber and turf, and inside it smelled of herbs and lamp oil and, faintly, of cold iron. She shut the door, dropped a bar across it, and hung an iron horseshoe over the latch with the tenderness other people reserved for icons.

"Sit," she said. "Don't touch anything green."

He sat. She put a pot on the hearth and spooned something into it that steamed and smelled of pine, and only when the smell had filled the room did she turn and look at him properly, and he saw that her hands were shaking.

"Do you know what this town is?" she asked.

"A market town," he said carefully.

"It's a throat." She said it without raising her voice, which somehow made it worse. "Two thousand throats, and every one of them singing whether they mean to or not. Twenty-two years since the Choir came through and taught us the Evening Litany—said it was for the king's health, said every loyal household would sing it at sundown. My husband sang it. Our neighbors sang it. It's a pretty thing, four notes, easy to learn, children learn it before they learn their letters." She stirred the pot. Her shadow shook on the wall. "Nobody sings it at sundown anymore, boy. They sing it always. It got into them like water into chalk. And the ones it gets into deepest—the ones whose voices ring truest—they get collected. Wagons come. Quiet men in gray. Families are told their son or daughter has been honored with a place in the royal choir, and there's bread and coin for the grieving, and everyone hums a little louder after, because humming makes it easier not to think. My granddaughter was collected eight years ago. She was eleven. She had a voice like—" The ladle stopped moving. "She had a voice," the old woman finished, "and now the Choir has it, and I have this pot."

The steam rose between them. Outside, muffled by walls and distance, Bellharrow hummed its four-note circle, patient as water dripping into a well.

"What do they do with the voices?" Zephyrine asked quietly. He already knew. The dead courier had shown him, in the last moments, images pressed into him like flowers pressed into a book: vaults of glass, souls folded small as paper, a throne that ate.

"That's above my knowing," the woman said. "What I know is what my grandmother knew, and her mother before her, and what every cunning-woman in this valley knew until the Choir made cunning a hanging offense." She crossed the room suddenly, faster than he expected, and her hand shot out—not to strike him but to press, flat and fierce, against his chest, right over the place where the oilskin bundle hid against his ribs. Her eyes went wide. "There," she breathed. "Oh, you great fool. You great shining fool. You're carrying the seal."

The world narrowed to the pressure of her palm. "You know it?"

"I know it." She snatched her hand back as if burned. "Ashen wax, pressed with the broken crown—the private seal of Aldric's household, the one they banned from use the year the king stopped appearing at court. Ten years ago. My grandmother served at Cael Adair; she taught me the seals the way she taught me stitches. That seal hasn't been seen outside the capital since the year the songs started getting *hungry*." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Where did you get it?"

"A dying man," Zephyrine said. "A courier. He gave it to me at the ford above Hollowmere and died with his hand closed around mine."

The old woman sat down slowly on her stool, all the fierceness gone out of her, replaced by something older and sadder. For a long moment she only looked at him, and the firelight moved on her walnut-shell face.

"Then you're taking it to the crypt," she said. Not a question.

"The Ashen Crypt. Before midwinter."

"And you've seen what walks the streets of this kingdom wearing the king's crown. And you've heard the Choir on the passes. And you came into *Bellharrow* anyway, because you needed bread." She laughed—one dry, cracking sound with no humor in it at all. "They'll find you here. Maybe not tonight, maybe not tomorrow, but the town will sing you out. Every humming mouth in this square is a ear for them, boy, don't you understand? The Litany isn't a song. It's a net. And you are the largest fish that's swum into this valley in twenty years."

"Then I leave tonight. At first light."

"At first light may be too late." She leaned forward, seized his wrist, and her grip was astonishingly strong. "Burn it."

The word landed in the little room like a dropped stone.

"Burn it," she repeated. "Take the map out of your coat, put it in my stove, and watch it curl. Whatever name is written on it, whatever door it opens—you burn it, and you go home, and you live. Do you know why I'm telling you this? Because I buried a husband who thought one brave thing would fix the world. Because I have a granddaughter in a glass box somewhere under the capital, and I am *old*, and I have watched brave people walk toward Cael Adair for ten years, and I have never once seen one walk back. The dead king is dead, child. Dead is dead. What wears his crown now cannot be unmade by a bone-carver and a piece of wax. Burn it. Live. Let the world stay sung."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, close now, the lamplighter had begun humming again—four notes, rising, falling—and another voice had joined it, and another, the circle widening down the street like ripples on a pond.

Zephyrine thought of Hollowmere: the quiet, the ward, the long years of blessed silence in which the dead had let him sleep. He thought of how the peace had never once felt earned—only borrowed, only *postponed*. He thought of the courier's blood freezing on the ford-stones, and the last thing those dying lips had shaped: *the map must reach the crypt before midwinter, or the singing never stops.*

"No," he said.

The old woman's grip tightened, then released. She looked at him for a long time, and whatever she saw in his face made her shoulders drop an inch.

"No," she agreed softly. "I didn't think you would. Cunning-folk never can. It's our curse—we see the trap plain as daylight and we walk into it anyway, because walking away would be worse." She rose, went to a chest in the corner, and came back with a bundle: dried meat, a twist of herbs, a strip of iron on a leather cord. "Iron for your pocket. It dulls their listening a little. Pine-mix for the wax in your ears—renew it nightly, the tune eats through plain tallow by morning. And take the back lane past the tannery when you go; the dogs hate the tune, they'll bark you a clear road."

"Why help me?" he asked. "If you think I'll fail—if you think the world stays sung—why spend anything on me at all?"

She stood in her doorway with the firelight behind her, small and bent and ancient, and for a moment she was not humming, and he understood that her silence had cost her twenty-two years of effort, of biting her tongue every sundown, of iron horseshoes and herb-smoke and never once letting the circle find a home in her throat.

"Because somebody in this town should say it out loud," she said. "Somebody should stand in Bellharrow and *not* sing, so that the walls remember it can be done. Go on, then. And boy—" her voice followed him to the door, thin and fierce, "—if you reach that crypt, and if there's anything left of my Wren in those glass boxes—you tell her her gran kept the silence. You tell her I never sang."

* * *

The back lane past the tannery ran narrow and stinking between high fences, and the dogs indeed barked him along it, hurling themselves at the gaps in the boards with a fury that had nothing to do with him and everything to do with the four-note circle drifting over the rooftops. Zephyrine walked fast, hood up, the iron cold in his pocket, the map a familiar weight against his ribs—forty touches a day, he'd promised himself he'd stop, he touched it now.

He was nearly at the north postern when he heard the wagon.

Gray canvas, gray curtains, four quiet horses, rolling slow through the square he'd just left, and around it the townspeople of Bellharrow gathered without being called, humming a little louder, faces comfortable and smooth as eggs. On the wagon's bench sat a figure in Choir gray with a tuning fork of white glass held up to the lamplight, and as Zephyrine watched from the shadow of the tannery fence, the figure struck the fork against the rail.

The note that came off it was not a note. It was a *question*—a pure, thin, searching tone that slid through the town like a hand sliding along a shelf of bottles, testing each one for cracks. Zephyrine felt it pass over him, through the wax, through the iron, and snag—snag—on something in his chest, and the fork's tone bent, wavered, and sharpened, pointing at him the way a compass needle points north.

The cantor on the bench turned her head.

Down the length of the tannery lane, across sixty yards of frozen mud, their eyes met. Hers widened. Her lips parted around a word he couldn't hear—

—and then a hand closed over his mouth from behind, and an arm he knew, an arm he had known since childhood, hauled him backward off the lane and into the dark between the tanning sheds, as behind them the glass fork sang its sharp, finding note, and boots began to hit the frozen ground at a run.

Tam's breath was hot against his ear, ragged with running and cold and eleven days of following a trail he'd sworn not to follow.

"Don't," Tam whispered, "say a word."
