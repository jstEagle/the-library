## 7. Tam Returns

The road out of Bellharrow climbed through birch country where the frost had turned every branch to white iron, and Zephyrine walked with his hood up and his ears full of wax and carved silence, because the Choir was singing again.

He felt it before he heard it—that deep, subcutaneous ache, like a tooth remembering an old cavity. The harmonies came down off the northern passes in long braided threads, too far away to make out words, close enough that his bones recognized the grammar of them. Resonance-work. They were sweeping the valleys the way shepherds sweep hillsides for strays, listening for souls that rang back when the liturgy touched them.

He had learned to walk deaf. It was not a perfect solution—the wax dulled everything, so he heard the world as if underwater, his own footsteps gone vague, wind a rumor—but it kept the songs out, or most of them. What got through arrived as pressure rather than sound, a faint tugging behind his sternum, as though something on the wind were trying to remember his name.

*You are nobody,* he told himself, over and over, a private liturgy of his own. *You are a bone-carver from a valley that wants you dead. You have no resonance. You are silence wearing a coat.*

The lie worked better some hours than others.

He made camp that night in the lee of a granite outcrop, building the fire small, feeding it with dead birchbark that burned quick and blue-edged. He did not light it at all until full dark; smoke by day would draw eyes, and eyes were worse than songs. Eyes belonged to scouts, and scouts belonged to Vespera's order, and though he had never seen her—had only heard her name once, spat by a dying courier whose voice still visited him at the edges of sleep—he had come to think of the Ember Choir as a single creature with many mouths, and every mouth was hunting.

He was sharpening his carving knife against a whetstone, more for the comfort of the rhythm than any real need, when the hair on his arms lifted.

Not the songs this time. Something closer. A footfall, muffled by snow, wrong-footed and hurried, coming up the slope behind him.

Zephyrine did not turn. He let his hand drift to the knife and kept the whetstone moving, *shk, shk, shk*, and said, without raising his voice, "I hear you."

Silence. Then, wretchedly familiar: "I know. You always did. Drove me mad, that."

He turned so fast the whetstone skittered from his fingers.

Tam stood at the edge of the firelight, half-frozen, filthy to the knees, his pack hanging off one shoulder by a strap that had been repaired twice with twine. He looked thinner than Zephyrine remembered, which was absurd, because it had been barely three weeks since Hollowmere had voted him out like a rotten beam. Three weeks. Tam looked like he'd been walking for years.

"You're supposed to be home," Zephyrine said.

"I am home." Tam came forward into the light, hands out as if approaching a spooked animal, and maybe he was. "Home is wherever you aren't getting killed, apparently, since you seem determined to manage it alone." He dropped the pack. Sat down across the fire without asking, the way he'd sat down across ten thousand fires in their childhood, and held his palms toward the flames. His fingertips were cracked with cold. "Elder Maren cried when I left, if it helps. She said I was a fool. I told her yes."

"They'll mark you for this. Leaving after me."

"They already marked *you*, Zeph. There's no deeper circle." He smiled, and the smile cracked the ice in his beard, little flakes falling into his lap. "Besides. Someone has to carry the tent. You'd have frozen by Bellharrow."

Zephyrine wanted to laugh. Wanted, badly, to cross the fire and grip the man's shoulders and feel something solid and uncomplicated in a world that had become nothing but maps and whispers and dead men's errands. Instead he heard himself say, flat: "Turn around, Tam."

"What?"

"Go home. Tomorrow. Tonight, if your legs will take it."

"No."

"The Choir is hunting me. Not following—hunting. They sing the passes and listen for what sings back, and if you're beside me when they find me—"

"Then they find two of us, and one of us can swing a maul." Tam's jaw set in the old way, the millstone way, the way it had set when they were boys and the older kids from the terraces tried to take Zephyrine's carvings off him. "You refused my help once already. You don't get to refuse it twice. That's not how debts work between friends."

Zephyrine looked at him across the small blue fire—at the frost in his beard, the twine-mended strap, the stubbornness worn like armor—and felt the terrible warmth of being known. And beneath the warmth, faint as a hairline crack in glaze, something else.

*Turn around,* he thought again, and hated himself for thinking it.

They slept in watches. When Tam's watch came, Zephyrine lay listening—not to the wind, but to the absence of it—and told himself the tightness in his chest was only grief for the valley, only the road, only cold.

It was almost true.

* * *

Three days north of Bellharrow the land narrowed into a pass called the Shepherd's Throat, where the rock rose sheer on both sides and the road ran single-file along a frozen streambed. Bad country for a man with dulled ears. Worse country for two men pretending the second one wasn't there.

Zephyrine had taken the lead all morning without discussing it, and Tam had noticed, because Tam noticed everything about people and nothing about his own boots, which were splitting at the toe. Zephyrine led because leading meant he could watch Tam's face in profile whenever the track bent, and watching had become a sickness in him.

Because of the humming.

He'd caught it first on the second night, waking to find Tam on watch and a low sound drifting across the embers—three notes, rising, then a fourth that bent strangely, a quarter-tone off anything human throats should want to sing. Zephyrine knew those four notes. Everyone in Cael Adair knew them now; they were the opening phrase of the Choir's traveling liturgy, the one the market-women of Bellharrow hummed while weighing flour, the one the children skipped rope to. *Obedience dressed as melody.* He had watched a whole town sing along without knowing why, and now the tune lived in Tam's mouth too.

Except Tam hadn't been in Bellharrow. Tam had come up the south road. Which meant either the song had spread faster than the plague, or—

Or nothing. Or Tam was tired, and tired minds wander toward whatever music they've been steeped in since birth. The Choir's liturgy had been leaking into tavern songs for a decade. Every harvest festival for the last five years had ended with the same round, and everyone clapped, and no one asked why the round always left them feeling settled, agreeable, *managed*.

Zephyrine told himself all of this. And then, on the third morning, in the throat of the pass, he heard it again—Tam humming under his breath as he walked, soft, automatic, the four notes rising and bending—and Zephyrine's hand went to his knife before his mind had agreed to anything.

He took it out. Held it loose at his side. Kept walking.

The pass tightened. The stream under the ice made a sound like someone whispering through clenched teeth. And Zephyrine, deaf in his wax-packed ears, hyperaware in every other sense, catalogued his oldest friend's gait, his breathing, the angle of his shoulders, searching for the thing that wore Tam's walk.

That was when the scout stepped out of the rocks ahead.

She was young, robed in ember-orange, a slender silver instrument at her lips—a curved horn-thing, half flute, half sickle. Her eyes were closed. Listening. The song that came off the instrument was thin and needle-fine and slid straight past the wax in Zephyrine's ears as though the wax were a door left open, and it found the place behind his sternum and *pulled*.

He staggered. Felt himself resonate, horribly, helplessly—an unbound voice ringing like a struck bell, broadcasting his location to anyone attuned within a mile. The scout's eyes snapped open. She saw him, and smiled, and raised the horn to call the rest of her squad down out of the rocks.

And Tam hit her from the flank like a landslide.

He'd been carrying the maul since Hollowmere—he'd carried it for twenty years at the quarry before that—and he swung it the way he split stone, no flourish, pure weight and intent. The horn flew from her hands and shattered on the rock. She went down hard, and Tam's knee landed on her chest, and his fist came up—

"Don't," Zephyrine gasped. The song's pull was still dragging at him; he spoke through gritted teeth. "Don't kill her. If you kill a Choir cantor, they will burn three villages looking for us. Bind her. Gag her. Take the pieces of that—" he nodded at the shattered instrument, "-and we go, *now*, before the rest of the squad comes to see why their listener went quiet."

Tam blinked. Then he moved—tearing strips from the scout's own robe, binding her wrists and mouth, dumping her instruments' fragments into a drift. Efficient. Unhesitating. A soldier's economy that Zephyrine had never seen in him before and filed away without wanting to.

They ran. Up the pass, off the road, into the scree where footprints filled with wind-blown snow behind them, until the Shepherd's Throat fell away below and the trees closed overhead and their lungs burned and neither of them could run anymore.

Zephyrine leaned against a pine, wheezing, and laughed—one raw, disbelieving bark of it. "You hit a cantor of the Ember Choir with a quarry-maul."

"She was going to sing you," Tam panted. "I could see it. The way she looked at you—I've seen men look at ore like that." He sat down hard in the snow, grinning, blood-freckled spit on his lips, alive, gloriously, stupidly alive. "Still think you should've sent me home?"

And Zephyrine, who owed this man everything, who had loved him since they were boys stealing apples from Elder Maren's orchard, opened his mouth to say *yes, obviously, thank you, thank you*—

—and Tam, catching his breath, absently, under his breath, hummed the four notes.

Rising. Bending. The fourth note a quarter-tone wrong, sliding home like a key into a lock.

The grin died on Zephyrine's face. He watched Tam realize what he'd done—watched the color leave him, watched his hand come up and press flat against his own lips as if he could shove the song back in.

"Zeph," Tam said. His voice came out strange. "Zeph, I didn't—I wasn't thinking, it's just a tune, everyone knows it, it doesn't mean—"

"When did you learn it?" Quiet. Too quiet. Zephyrine heard his own voice as if it belonged to a stranger, cold and precise, a man interrogating his brother. "Bellharrow? Or before?"

"I never— I haven't *been* anywhere. I followed you up the south road, I swear it, I slept in ditches, ask me anything, ask me what you said at the ford when the courier died, ask me—" Tam's hands were shaking. "Zeph. Look at me. Look at *me*."

Zephyrine looked at him. That was the whole horror of it. He looked and he saw Tam—his Tam, the boy who'd held his hair back when the dead-voices first came, who'd stood in the flood-water of the mill race for six hours to keep the sluice-gate open, who had just broken a cantor's instrument with his bare strength to save a marked man the whole kingdom wanted silent. And underneath Tam, or through Tam, or threaded into him like dye through wool, the liturgy, humming patiently, waiting.

"That's the problem," Zephyrine said softly. "I am looking at you. And I can't tell anymore where you end and the song begins."

"It's just a tune." But Tam's voice cracked on *tune*, and they both heard it. "Everyone hums it. The whole kingdom hums it. That doesn't make them traitors, Zeph, it makes them *people*—"

"People who are managed." Zephyrine pushed off the pine. Picked up his pack. His hands weren't shaking, and he wished they were; shaking would have meant he was still mostly afraid, and fear was honest. This was worse than fear. This was arithmetic. "We walk until dark. We don't talk about the map. From here to wherever this ends, you don't touch my pack, you don't read over my shoulder, and you don't—" he stopped. Swallowed. "You don't hum."

Tam stared at him. Something went out of his face—not anger. Wound. The specific wound of being doubted by the one person who had no right to, and knowing, somewhere underneath, that the doubt might be justified.

"All right," Tam said finally. Flat. Obedient.

And Zephyrine flinched, because *obedient* was the last word he'd ever wanted in Tam's mouth, and because somewhere behind his sternum, faint and patient, he thought he felt the liturgy settle another inch deeper into the world—into Tam, into the towns, into the very air—like frost creeping through a wall.

They walked. The pass widened ahead into high grey moorland, dark with old peat and older graves, and the wind coming off it carried voices that Zephyrine's gift, even dulled by wax, began to stir toward—hundreds of them, thousands, restless, unquiet, calling.

But that was an hour later, and by then the light had failed, and the first thing Zephyrine heard rising out of the Weeping Moors was not the dead at all.

It was singing. Distant, braided, many-throated, sweeping up from the south.

The Ember Choir had entered the moors behind them.
