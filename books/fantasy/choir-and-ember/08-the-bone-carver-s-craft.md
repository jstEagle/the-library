## 8. The Bone-Carver's Craft

The pass above Bellharrow had a name on no map Zephyrine had ever seen, but the locals called it the Shepherd's Throat—a narrow cleft where the road pinched between two shoulders of grey stone and the wind funneled through with a sound like a man whistling through broken teeth. He had meant to be through it before dark. He had meant to be through it before the Choir's sweep reached this far north at all.

Both intentions died somewhere in the last hour of light.

He heard them first as he had learned to hear everything now: through wax and wool packed tight in his ears, through the soles of his boots, through the fine bones of his jaw. Three riders coming up the throat behind him, unhurried, their horses' hooves striking a rhythm that carried up through the rock and into his teeth like a knuckle tapped on a door. Ahead—when he stopped and pressed his palm flat against the cliff face—he felt a fourth. Dismounted. Waiting where the cleft narrowed enough that a single man could block it, or end whoever tried to pass.

A closing hand. He knew the shape of it from the courier's dying rattle, from Tam's warnings shouted half-heard through plugged ears: *they don't chase you, Zeph, they herd you.*

He could go back. The thought came easily, the way surrender always does, dressed in reasonable clothes. Back down the throat, back toward Bellharrow and its humming market and its old woman who had begged him to burn the map, back south into country where the Choir's liturgy soaked into everything like grease into bread. Or forward, into the waiting scout. Or up—

Up was scree and frost-slick stone and a ridgeline that would break an ankle in the dark, if it broke nothing worse.

Zephyrine stood in the middle of the road while the light went copper behind the western peaks and did the arithmetic every hunted man eventually does, and found that all his sums ended the same way.

They were close enough now that even through the wax he caught fragments—not words, never words, the Choir did not waste words—but harmony. A low drone under the wind, three voices braided into one, and beneath his sternum something answered whether he willed it or not, some string in him that had been tuned long ago by a gift he'd spent thirty years pretending he didn't have. His bones ached the way they ached before a storm. The way they had ached in the ford when the courier died and the ward over Hollowmere flickered and Zephyrine heard, for the first time in years, a voice that had no throat.

*Run,* said the memory of that voice, sometimes. *You're carrying what they want. Run.*

He didn't run. He climbed.

* * *

The ridge was crueler than it looked, because ridges always are. Scree shifted under him like a living thing deciding, moment to moment, whether to bear his weight or spill him. Twice he went down to the elbow and felt skin open against stone. The cold up here was honest, at least—no soft corruption in it, no song, just the old indifferent bite of winter coming early off the high snowfields. He breathed it and told himself: *this is clean. This is the world without them in it.*

Below and behind, the harmonies swelled. They had stopped pretending to be weather.

He gained the ridgeline with his lungs full of knives and looked down the far side, and there—of course—was the fourth scout. Not below at all. On the near shoulder of the ridge itself, having climbed while Zephyrine climbed, patient as lichen, cutting off the high road too. A young man in the Choir's dove-grey, no older than Tam, a slender silver pipe at his lips, and out of the pipe came a single sustained note that slid along the ridge like oil along water.

Zephyrine felt it find him.

There was no other word for it. The note touched the place inside him where his gift lived—the place he had bricked over, plastered, papered, furnished with a quiet life and a quiet craft—and knocked. And the bricks were thinner than he had believed. Thin as memorial bone.

*There you are,* said the note, without words. *There you are, unbound thing. Hold still.*

His knees wanted to fold. His jaw wanted to open. He understood, with the detached clarity of terror, that this was how they took people—not with chains but with resonance, finding the frequency of a soul and gripping it, the way a skilled hand grips a bird. The courier had not been chased to death. He had been *sung* to death, run ragged across a kingdom by a note he couldn't stop hearing.

And Zephyrine Dunmore, who had spent his whole life carving names into bone so the dead wouldn't be forgotten, stood on a frozen ridge with his mouth opening against his will and thought, with a fury that surprised him by its size:

*No.*

Not me. Not my voice. I am the one thing in this kingdom that does not belong to you.

He turned from the scout and ran along the ridgeline, eastward, toward a tumble of ancient stones the shepherds used as a wind-shelter—an old cairn-ruin, older than Hollowmere's ward, older maybe than the kingdom. Behind him the pipe-note bent, pursuing, and the two riders below began to sing in earnest, and the sound of it made the ice on the stones shiver and shake loose in glittering sheets.

He fell into the shelter of the ruin with blood on his palms and the map digging its bone handle into his ribs, and for a long moment he simply lay there among the fallen slabs, listening to his own heart try to batter its way out through the wax in his ears.

Then he noticed what he was lying on.

Bones.

Not sheep. Not shepherd. Human bones, old ones, yellowed and rain-worn, tucked into the crevices of the ruin the way offerings are tucked into shrine-cracks. Whoever raised this cairn had buried their dead here deliberately, high and dry above the valley mists, and the mountain had kept them. Long femurs laid in rows. Skulls nested in a hollow of stone like eggs in a nest, facing outward, facing the road, facing the direction from which anyone might come.

Zephyrine knew bones the way a smith knows iron. He had carved ten thousand of them—calf bone and deer bone ordered from the tannery, never human, never once human, because Hollowmere's law was plain and because some part of him had always feared what human bone would say to a man who could listen. He had spent his life making the dead legible to the living, and had refused, always, to make himself legible to the dead.

Now he lay among skulls that had watched the road for centuries, with a Choir scout's note crawling up the ridge after him like a searching finger, and he understood something so simple it was obscene.

Bone holds a voice.

That was the whole secret of his craft, wasn't it? That was why he carved memorials at all—because the bereaved said it helped, said touching the carved name was like touching the person, said the bone *kept* something. He had called it comfort. Superstition. The soft lie carvers tell to sell their work.

But the courier had died clutching a bone-handled map, and the first dead voice Zephyrine had heard in seven years had come pouring out of that handle like water from a split jug. And the map's second script, the hidden script, the script about binding-songs and crowns—it hadn't appeared in ink at all. It had appeared when he held the bone grip to firelight and let his thumb rest, unknowing, in the grooves.

Bone remembers. Bone carries. Every memorial he had ever carved was a vessel, and he had spent thirty years filling vessels with the names of strangers' beloveds and never once wondering what else might pour in, or out.

The pipe-note rounded the ruin. The young scout stepped into view against the bruised sky, cheeks red with cold and effort, eyes bright with the particular zeal of someone doing holy work. He lowered the pipe just enough to speak, and his breath smoked.

"There's no shame in it," the scout said. "The Choir takes everyone eventually. You can come singing or come silent. Everyone comes."

Zephyrine's hand closed around the nearest skull.

It fit his palm like it had been made for him—or like he had been made for it, thirty years of carving having shaped his hand into exactly the instrument such a thing required. The bone was cold and light and terrible. And through it, faint as a pulse under thin skin, he felt them: the cairn-dead. Dozens. Centuries deep. Not screaming, not raging—*waiting*, with the bottomless patience of the interred, the way he himself waited each night for sleep.

They felt him feel them. He felt their attention turn like a tide.

*Carver,* said something vast and layered, a voice made of many voices the way a chord is made of notes. *Name-carver. Little listener. What do you want?*

What did he want?

Behind the ruin, the harmonies of the two riders crested the ridge. In front of him, the scout raised the pipe again. Somewhere south of here, Tam was walking a road alone because Zephyrine had refused his company; somewhere south, a town hummed itself into obedience; somewhere in a vault in Cael Adair, souls hung in glass like flies in amber. And the king was dead. The true king, the one whose name was on the map's hidden lines, the one who had to reach the Ashen Crypt before midwinter or not at all.

"I want," Zephyrine said aloud, and his voice cracked in the cold, "to borrow something."

*We are owed,* said the cairn-dead. *Long owed. Say what we are owed for.*

"Everything," he whispered. "You were sung over and silenced and forgotten. I'm going to give them back the sound."

* * *

He worked fast, the way he worked when a client was dying and the memorial had to be finished before the breath ran out—hands moving ahead of thought. There were flat scapulae in the cairn, wide as small shields. He chose six, and with his belt-knife he cut into them, quick and shallow, not names this time but channels: grooves that spiraled inward, the way water grooves spiral in a basin, the way he had once idly spiraled a pattern into a keepsake for a widow in Hollowmere and been puzzled, afterward, by how her grief had seemed to *drain* into it, leaving her lighter.

He had thought he was comforting her. He had been building instruments all his life and calling them consolations.

The first groove finished, he pressed the bone flat against a skull and spoke into it—not loudly, barely above breath, the way you speak to someone sleeping: "They came with songs to take the living. You know that song. It was sung over you, once, before they put you in the ground."

The skull warmed under his hands. That should have been impossible. Everything after that should have been impossible, and none of it cared.

*Yes,* said the dead. *We know it.*

"Keep it," Zephyrine said. "Hold the shape of it in the bone. All of it—the harmony, the grip of it, the taking-hold. Keep it, and wait for my word."

One by one he charged the scapulae, and the cairn-dead poured their remembered wrongs into the grooves—the binding-liturgies sung over them in whatever century they died, the voices stolen, the names unwritten—and the bones grew heavy in his hands, heavy as loaded crossbows, humming at frequencies that made his fillings ache and the frost on the stones dance in tiny jumping grains.

The scout came around the ruin's edge with his pipe raised, and behind him, descending the scree with impossible grace, came the two riders, their grey cloaks stiff with frost, their mouths open on a chord that gripped the air like a fist.

Zephyrine stood up among the skulls with a scapula in each hand, six of them slung across his chest on a loop of rope like a butcher's apron, and he had never in his life felt less like a craftsman and more like what he was, which was a man standing at the hinge between the living and the dead, holding the door open on purpose for the first time.

The chord hit him. His knees buckled. His throat opened against his will, and something began pulling at his voice from the inside, reeling it out of him like gut from a fisherman's line—

—and Zephyrine raised both scapulae, crossed them before his face like a shield, and gave the word.

"*Sing.*"

Six bones sang at once.

It was not music. Music would have been kinder. It was every binding-liturgy ever forced onto those bones, played back inverted, inside-out, a chorus of the betrayed performing the Choir's own sacred harmony with the reverence stripped out and only the theft left in, magnified, aimed. The sound came out of the scapulae and it did not spread—it *struck*, three narrow beams of it, one per singer, finding them the way his gift found the dead: by resonance, by the private frequency of each soul.

The scout's pipe note shattered into a squeal. The young man clutched his ears and went down on his knees in the scree, mouth working, and the sound coming out of him was not a scream—it was his own voice being pulled back up the channel it had so often used to pull others, dragged out note by note, until he collapsed face-forward among the old bones with his lips still moving and nothing left behind them but breath.

The riders lasted longer. They were trained; they counter-sang; for three horrible seconds the ridge became a battleground of pure sound, the cairn's chorus grinding against the Choir's living harmony, frost exploding off the rocks in white halos. Then one of the scapulae cracked down its spiral groove—the dead spending themselves, spending everything—and the chorus lurched into a higher, rawer register, grief instead of technique, and the riders' chord broke apart like a spun wheel shedding spokes.

One rider fell off her horse. The other turned and fled, sliding and scrambling down the far side of the ridge, and Zephyrine, kneeling in the ruin with blood running freely from his nose and both ears roaring, felt the temptation rise in him like heat:

Let them go. Let them carry the sound of it back. Let them *tell*.

He was smiling. He realized it with a lurch, felt the shape of the smile on his own face as if it belonged to a stranger, and did not take it off.

* * *

Afterward he sat among the cairn-dead while true darkness came down, and the aurora unreeled faint green ribbons over the northern peaks, and he tried to take account of himself the way he'd take account of tools after a job.

Three scouts. One fled, one woman unconscious but breathing, one boy emptied of his voice and lying among the ancestors like an offering returned. Two scapulae spent, cracked through their spirals, mute forever now. Four still humming faintly against his chest, restless, hungry for use.

And underneath all of it, steady as a hearth-fire he had no intention of putting out: the liking. The awful, unmistakable liking. When the chorus struck, something in him had risen up and spread wings it had been folding for thirty years, and it had *fit*, the way his carving knife fits his hand, the way the valley's silence had never quite fit though he'd pretended otherwise every day of his exile.

Hollowmere hadn't been peace. Hollowmere had been a splint on a limb that was never broken, only bound.

"You knew," he said quietly to the nearest skull. Its hollow gaze faced the road, faced the world of the living, patient as stone. "All of you knew what bone could do. You've known longer than my family's been alive. Why didn't anyone—"

But of course. Who would teach a weapon to the kind of man who runs from his own gift? The dead had simply waited, generation after generation, for a carver who might need them. For a carver worth arming.

*I'm sorry,* he started to say, for the boy, for the woman, for what he'd done with their centuries of patience.

*Don't,* said the cairn-dead, and the word was almost gentle. *We are not sorry. We have waited a very long time to be useful. But listen, name-carver, and mark us: power that tastes sweet the first time is never free the second. Ask yourself, on the road, every day, whose voice the bones are really learning to speak in. Ours today. Whose tomorrow?*

Zephyrine gathered the four remaining scapulae, wrapped them in cloth, and tucked them against the map at his back—vessels, weapons, witnesses. Before leaving the ruin he did the one thing his craft demanded and his new appetite resisted: he sat with his knife and carved, carefully, into a fresh slab of stone, the names of the cairn-dead as far as the worn markings on their bones could tell him, so that they would not be forgotten for what they had done tonight.

At the edge of the ridgeline he paused and looked back once. The empty scout lay where he had fallen, breathing, blank-eyed, alive. Zephyrine told himself that counted for something. He was no longer entirely sure what.

Then he turned north, toward the high passes and the frozen road to Cael Adair—and saw, far below on the moonlit valley floor, a single rider keeping pace with his route. Not grey. Not singing.

Riding like someone who had chosen this hunt personally, and intended to see it through.
