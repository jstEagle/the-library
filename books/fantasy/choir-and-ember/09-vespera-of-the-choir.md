## 9. Vespera of the Choir

The Vault of Voices had no windows, and Vespera had learned long ago that this was deliberate. A cantor needed no dawn to tell her when the day began; the Choir woke her with song, fed her with song, tucked her into sleep with lullabies woven from a hundred stolen throats. She was twenty-three years old, the youngest cantor ever raised to the white-and-ember robes, and she could not remember a morning that had not begun with someone else's voice in her ears.

She stood before the great resonance-glass now, its surface dark as frozen tar, watching her own reflection waver. The glass was the Choir's holiest relic—cut, it was said, from the window through which the first binding-song had been sung to a dying king—and every soul the Choir harvested left a ripple in it, a faint silver shimmer that sank and settled like snowfall into deep water.

This morning there were three new ripples. Three souls, taken somewhere on the western roads three nights past. Vespera had not been there for the taking. She told herself she preferred it that way, and then she told herself that lying to oneself was a cantor's most necessary discipline, and then she stopped telling herself anything at all and simply watched the ripples fade.

"Third harmonic," said Cantor Ossian behind her. He was old, dry as kindling, with a voice like a hinge that had been oiled too often—the smoothness itself a kind of rust. "Do you hear it? The glass is singing flat this week."

Vespera listened. The Vault always hummed; the accumulated voices never fully stilled, and on quiet days you could stand among them like standing in a meadow full of insects. But Ossian was right. There was a drag in the harmonics, a heaviness, as though the choir of the dead had grown tired of singing.

"The harvests are coming faster," she said. "The glass needs time to settle between them."

"The glass will do what the crown requires of it." Ossian smiled with all the warmth of a liturgy. "As will we."

She bowed her head at the correct angle. She had learned the correct angles very young. That was what they had found in her, all those years ago, when the Choir's listeners came through her village in the hill country and put their hands gently over children's ears and asked them to sing: not power, exactly, but *precision*. Other children had felt the songs and wept or laughed or gone blank-eyed. Vespera had felt the songs and heard where they were stitched—heard the seam, the join, the place where obedience had been sewn into melody. She had said so, aloud, at seven years old, to a white-robed stranger who had knelt down and looked at her with something like hunger and something like relief.

"You can hear the seams," he'd whispered. "Child, do you know how rare that is? Do you know how useful?"

Useful. She had thought, at seven, that it meant beloved.

* * *

After matins—sung in the Round Hall beneath a dome painted with the Choir's founding myth, nine singers ascending a mountain of flame while a crowned figure reached down to lift them up; the paint was older than anyone living and flaked a little more each year, so that the ascending singers were gradually dissolving into sky—after matins, Vespera went looking for the report.

She should not have. Reports about active hunts were sealed to cantors of the second circle and above, and she was only newly first-circle, and curiosity in the Choir was tolerated the way a small fire is tolerated in a granary: with watchfulness, until it isn't. But she had felt it during matins, the way one feels weather changing behind stone walls. A wrongness in the collective ear of the order. Somewhere north, something was moving that the Choir did not have a name for yet, and the absence of a name made the whole order restless, like an itch under a bandage.

She found the report in the scriptorium, misfiled—or placed where she would find it, though she would spend weeks before admitting to herself that this might be the truth. The paper smelled of ember-seal wax. She read it twice by the grey light of the high slit-window, and the second time her hands were not steady.

*Unbound voice detected. Western passes, moving north-northeast. Not Choir-trained. Not bound. Resonance signature consistent with necromantic receptivity — the subject hears the dead without instrument or training. Subject believed to carry an artifact of interest to His Majesty. All cantors of first circle are instructed to treat this signature as priority.*

An unbound voice. She read those two words again and again, because they were almost obscene in their rarity. Every singer in Cael Adair was bound—not enslaved, nothing so crude, the Choir's language was always gentle—but *attuned*. Tuned, like instruments, to the Choir's key. Their gifts had been opened by Choir hands, shaped by Choir methods, capped with Choir limits. An unbound voice was a wild thing: raw, unshaped, dangerous precisely because it answered to no one.

And it *heard the dead*. Without instrument, without training, without the Choir's permission. She tried to imagine it—tried to imagine hearing the harvested souls of the Vault without the mediation of glass and liturgy—and could not. The idea was like trying to imagine a color.

"Interesting reading?" said a voice at her shoulder.

Vespera did not startle. Startling was trained out of cantors early, along with most other honest reactions. She turned and inclined her head to First Cantor Ilvane, who stood in the scriptorium doorway wrapped in winter-grey vestments, her face a study in mild inquiry. Ilvane had eyes the color of pewter and the habit of looking at people the way a jeweler looks at stones: assessing cut, clarity, flaw.

"Reports were misfiled, First Cantor."

"Mm. Clumsy of us." Ilvane drifted closer, her gaze falling to the page without apology. "You've seen, then. Good. It saves me a speech." She plucked the report from Vespera's fingers with the ease of ownership. "A wild voice, walking north with something the crown wants. The Master of the Round wants it found before midwinter. He wants it found quietly."

"And if it resists?"

Ilvane's smile deepened by a degree. "Then it stops being quiet."

* * *

That afternoon Vespera did a thing she had done perhaps a dozen times in six years, each time telling herself it would be the last: she went down to the vaults beneath the Vault of Voices, to the tier where the newest harvests were kept before their settling, and she listened to them.

It was permitted. That was the horror of it, she thought—it was *encouraged*. Cantors were meant to know the material they worked with. A soul in storage was called a *voice at rest*, and the euphemism was so complete that some young cantors genuinely believed the voices slept, dreamed, waited peacefully for whatever came next. Vespera had believed it once. Then, at fifteen, she had heard the seams in a resting voice, and discovered that the rest was not rest at all but a held note—a scream sustained so long and so skillfully that it had become indistinguishable from silence.

She sat in the cold tier among the glass vessels, each one humming faintly, each one holding a person reduced to their purest continuance, and she pressed her palms against her thighs and listened the way only she could listen: underneath.

Three new vessels from the western roads. A carter. A peddler woman. A boy who could not have been more than sixteen. Their voices circled their vessels like moths around lanterns they could not land upon. And beneath the circling, beneath the held note—

*Please,* said the boy's voice, in the register below hearing, the register Vespera alone seemed able to reach. *Please, I want to go home, please, I don't understand why—*

He had been saying it, she understood, since the night they took him. Three days. Four. He would say it until the settling finished and the words wore smooth and he became a usable tone, a beautiful instrument, a thread in the Choir's great tapestry of control. The Choir did not destroy souls. That was the doctrine, the mercy, the thing that let cantors sleep. The Choir *preserved* them. Preserved them the way amber preserves the fly.

Vespera had once asked Ilvane, years ago, carefully, whether the voices suffered. Ilvane had looked at her with genuine fondness and said, "Suffering is a property of bodies, child. What we keep is beyond suffering." And Vespera had wanted desperately to believe her, and had almost managed it, and had built her whole career on the almost.

She rose. She smoothed her robes. She walked back up the stairs with her spine straight and her face arranged into the correct angles, and inside her chest something turned over like a stone in a streambed, showing a new wet face to the light.

The unbound voice was moving north. Toward the Ashen Crypt—everyone in the first circle knew what lay near the Ashen Crypt, even if the lower circles were kept innocent of it. Toward whatever the crown-king feared enough to mobilize half the order's listening web.

She could request the hunt. She was first-circle now; the right was hers to claim, and claiming it would look like ambition, which was safe. Ambition was legible. Ambition was what Ilvane expected of her, what the Choir had cultivated in her alongside her gift, trellised and pruned.

What she actually wanted—she admitted it only here, in the privacy of her own skull, where even the Choir's songs could not quite reach—was to get out from behind these walls. To stand in open air where the wind did not carry liturgy. And to find this wild, unbound, lawless person who could hear the dead without permission, and see his face, and learn what a voice looked like when no one had ever tuned it.

Perhaps he was a monster. The reports implied monsters. Necromancy was the Choir's oldest enemy, the sin of sins, the reason the Choir existed—to guard the border between the quick and the kept. If he was a monster, she would bind him herself and feel righteous doing it.

But the boy in the vessel had said *please*, and Vespera had started to wonder, in the wordless way of things wondered late at night, which side of the border she was actually guarding, and for whom.

* * *

She requested the hunt that evening, in the Round Hall, before the assembled first circle, using language she knew would be approved.

"I have the youngest ears in the order," she said, "and this signature is unlike anything in our archives. Fresh perception finds fresh seams. Give me the hunt and I will bring you the voice intact—for study, for tuning, for the crown's use." Intact. The word landed well. The Master of the Round nodded along, his ringed fingers drumming the arm of the ash-wood throne that was not a throne, merely a chair with pretensions.

Ilvane studied her from across the hall, pewter eyes narrowing by a fraction, and Vespera understood that the old woman saw something move beneath the surface of the request, the way a fisherman sees the water dimple before the rise. But Ilvane said nothing, because ambition explained everything, and Vespera let it.

"Granted," said the Master. "Take your squad. Take the fast road through Greyford. Midwinter is the wall—if the voice reaches the crypt country before the snows close the passes, we may lose it for a season, and the crown does not wait seasons." He paused, and his voice dropped into the register the Choir used for doctrine. "Remember what you carry, cantor. Your voice is the crown's voice. Your ear is the crown's ear. There is no part of you that is yours alone."

"No part of me that is mine alone," Vespera repeated, with the congregation's precision, and heard, beneath her own words, in the register below hearing, the faintest seam—the stitch-mark where that sentence had been sewn into her at fourteen, after her first solo, when she had cried with joy and someone had decided joy needed capping.

She led her squad out of the Round Hall before her face could betray her.

* * *

They rode north through the first true cold of the season, six riders in Choir grey with hoods the color of banked coals, and Vespera spent the long hours listening.

Listening was not passive work; it was the closest thing cantors had to combat. She extended her attention across the landscape the way other scouts extended their eyes—through the resonance-thread that linked every attuned soul in the kingdom, the vast patient web the Choir had spent two centuries spinning. Through it she felt the ordinary music of Cael Adair: farmers whose fear hummed at the pitch the tax-collectors' liturgy had set; garrison soldiers marching to a cadence-song; market towns where the obedience-liturgy had sunk so deep into the water-table of daily life that people sang it while buying bread, while scolding children, while making love, unaware. A kingdom humming. A kingdom kept.

And threading through it, fainter than any of these, a single line of sound that matched nothing in her archives: a voice moving north-northeast, ragged, untrained, *unbound*, carrying with it—she strained, she narrowed herself to a needle's point of attention—a whispering wake of the dead, like dust rising behind a cart.

He traveled with ghosts. Dozens of them. Perhaps hundreds. They clustered around him the way cold-clinging sparrows cluster around a chimney, and though she could not make out their words, she could feel their texture, and their texture was *hope*, which frightened her more than malice would have. Dead things did not hope. Dead things in the Choir's keeping especially did not hope. Whatever this bone-carver—she had pulled his file by now, Zephyrine Dunmore of Hollowmere, memorial-carver, ward-sheltered, presumed harmless, presumed *quiet*—whatever he was, the dead followed him believing in him.

Her squad made camp the second night in the lee of Greyford's ridge, and the men sang the evening liturgy around the fire out of habit, low and warm, and Vespera sat slightly apart and let it wash over her and searched it, as she always searched everything now, for the seams. There they were, faithful as scars: the obedience-stitch, the loyalty-stitch, the little cap on grief so that sorrow could be converted to devotion. Beautiful work. Her own order's finest craft. She had sung these stitches into a hundred villages herself and slept soundly after.

Tam of Hollowmere, she thought suddenly, for no reason she could name—the file had mentioned a childhood friend, refused at the valley ford—and dismissed the thought.

On the third day, near the Greyford road, the unbound voice passed within ten miles of her, and for one moment—one unguarded, breathless moment—her needle-point attention brushed against the edge of his wake, and the dead nearest him noticed her noticing.

They did not flee. They did not warn him, or if they did, he did not change course. One of them—an old man's voice, frayed and patient—turned toward her across the impossible distance and looked at her, the way only Vespera knew she could be looked at: seeing the seams, seeing the stitching, seeing the girl inside the robe.

And the old dead man said, in the register below hearing, in a tone of terrible gentleness:

*"You can hear us. Why do you serve the ones who keep us in jars?"*

Vespera reined in her horse so sharply that the rider behind nearly collided with her. Her squad glanced over; she waved them on with the correct gesture. She sat in the middle of the frozen road with her breath steaming and her heart going like a siege-drum, and she could not stop hearing it, would never stop hearing it, the question sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal:

Why *did* she?

Ahead, far to the north, the unbound voice moved on toward the Ashen Crypt, trailing hope like dust, and Vespera turned her horse to follow—telling herself, in the last hour she would believe it, that she rode to capture a monster, and not toward the first honest question anyone had ever asked her.
