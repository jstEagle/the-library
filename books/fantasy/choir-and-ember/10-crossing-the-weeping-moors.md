## 10. Crossing the Weeping Moors

The moors began where the road gave up.

Zephyrine felt the change before he saw it—the moment they stepped off the last hard-packed cart-track and onto ground that breathed. The peat was black and soft as old bread, drinking each footfall with a sound like a slow kiss, and above it hung a mist that did not move with the wind. It moved with something else. It leaned toward him.

Beside him, Tam pulled his cloak tighter and said nothing. He had said little since Bellharrow, and Zephyrine had not asked why. Some silences were wounds you didn't probe.

"Three days," Zephyrine said aloud, because saying it made it a plan instead of a dread. "Three days across, and we come out below the Ashen Vale. After that, high ground all the way."

Tam nodded. His eyes kept moving—left, right, left—the way a man watches treeline for wolves. But there were no trees out here. There was nothing but tussock grass gone the color of old teeth, standing pools that held no reflection worth trusting, and the mist, always the mist, folding close around them like something patient.

It took less than an hour for the first voice to find him.

*You've come.*

Not spoken. Not even quite heard. It arrived in the marrow of his bones the way cold does, from the inside out, and Zephyrine stopped so abruptly that Tam walked two paces on before noticing.

"What is it?" Tam asked. His voice came out rough, rusty with disuse.

"The dead," Zephyrine said. "They're—" He swallowed. "There are more of them than I've ever felt. Not one or two lingering. All of them. Hundreds. Thousands." He turned a slow circle. The mist pressed in at every edge, and now that he listened—really listened—he could feel them beneath his feet like heat beneath a floor. Layer upon layer upon layer, going down further than peat had any right to go. "This whole moor is a grave."

*You've come,* the voice said again, closer now, or perhaps just more insistent. *We knew someone would come. We waited. We waited so long.*

"They're talking to me," Zephyrine said quietly. "Tam—they think I'm here for them."

And God help him, some small traitorous part of him wanted to be.

* * *

Tam watched the carver stand in the fog like a man listening to music only he could hear, and hated how much he understood it.

He had been humming again. He'd caught himself twice that morning—some sweet ascending phrase with no words to it, warm as bread, familiar as his mother's kitchen—and both times he'd bitten it off so fast he'd nearly bitten his tongue. He didn't know where the tune came from. That was the lie he told himself as they walked: *I don't know where it comes from.* Because the other answer was that he did know, that it had crawled into him somewhere between Bellharrow and the moor's edge, in the dark hours when the Choir's distant harmonies had swept over the hills like weather, and that it was still in there, curled up comfortable, waiting for him to stop being careful.

He spat, quietly, into the peat.

Ahead, Zephyrine had begun to walk again, but wrong—stopping, tilting his head, his lips moving around words he wasn't giving voice to. Tam had seen him do this before, back in the valley years ago, before any of it, though never this often and never with this look on his face. The look wasn't fear. That was what frightened Tam about it. It looked almost like tenderness.

"You're going to wear yourself hollow," Tam said, catching up. "Listening to all of them."

"They won't stop asking." Zephyrine's eyes were wet-bright in the grey light. "Tam, there's a woman down there. Under the third pool past that ridge—she drowned, I think, or she was put under, and she's been calling for her son for forty years and the son died on this same moor two hundred yards away and *neither of them knows.* They've been calling through the mud at each other for forty years."

"Then that's very sad," Tam said, "and it isn't ours."

Zephyrine looked at him like he'd struck him.

"I'm sorry," Tam said, and meant it, and couldn't unsay it either. "I'm sorry. But we can't dig up a moor. And if you spend yourself on every voice between here and the crypt, there'll be nothing left of you to carry the thing we're actually carrying."

He watched his friend absorb that—watched it land and hurt and be swallowed, because Zephyrine had always been good at swallowing things, which Tam had once admired and was beginning to understand might be the most dangerous thing about him.

"They're going to guide us," Zephyrine said at last.

"Guide us?"

"Through the cordon. The Choir has pickets along the north rim—I can hear their songs from here, thin threads of it, marking the safe lanes by absence. The dead know every inch of this ground. Every sink, every false path, every place the Choir's listeners can't reach because the ground itself is too loud with them." He met Tam's eyes. "They've offered. In exchange—"

"In exchange for what?" Tam asked, though he already knew, and his stomach had already dropped.

"Rest," Zephyrine said. "When this is done, I come back. With everything I learn in that crypt—the binding-song, whatever power Aldric's people buried here—I come back and I give it to them. Every soul on this moor, laid to rest properly. No more waiting. No more calling through the mud."

The mist stirred around them. Tam could have sworn it leaned in to listen.

"That's a big promise," Tam said slowly.

"It's a true one."

"It's a big promise, Zeph." He used the old name deliberately, the valley name, the name that meant *before all this.* "Promises like that get people killed. Promises like that got *me*—" He stopped. Swallowed the rest of the sentence down where it lived now, next to the tune.

Zephyrine's face did something complicated. "What happened at Greyford wasn't your fault."

"I know whose fault it was." Tam's hand went, without his permission, to his own throat. Nothing there but unbroken skin and an intact voice, thank all the quiet gods—but he remembered the scout's song finding him anyway, remember the way the melody had *tasted* him, quick and curious, like fingers checking a lock. Remembered Zephyrine's carved bone singing the betrayed-dead's chorus and dropping three Choir soldiers where they stood, and the look on Zephyrine's face after, which had not been horror. Not only horror.

"We should move," Zephyrine said. "Light's short."

They moved. Behind them, faint as breath, the mist followed.

* * *

The dead led them well.

That was the terrible part, Zephyrine thought that first night as they sheltered in the lee of a fallen waystone older than any kingdom he could name. They led them *well.* A drowned girl who had been dead sixty years showed them the fordable channel through a pool that would have swallowed a horse. A shepherd with a slit throat—sheep-rustlers' work, generations old—walked ahead of them all afternoon as a pressure in the air, steering them wide of a peat bog that wore a skin of healthy grass. They were gentle. They were grateful. They pressed against the edges of his senses like cats against a kind hand, and every hour another of them asked, hopefully, the same question:

*Will you really come back?*

*I promise,* he answered, and meant it, and heard the promise land among them like a coal dropped into dry tinder—a flare of something too much like joy.

He slept badly. When he did sleep, he dreamed of Hollowmere, of the ward humming its one flat note over the valley, and woke to find himself humming too—except his hum had harmonics in it that no living throat should make, and the nearest pools had gone perfectly, attentively still.

On the second day they reached the cordon.

They smelled it before anything else: woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, resin and hot metal, the smell of the Choir's pitch-fire braziers that let their cantors sing at full strength without tearing their own voices bloody. Through the mist ahead, maybe half a mile, orange light bloomed and faded, bloomed and faded, patient as a heartbeat. And threaded through the air came the liturgy itself—that sweet ascending phrase Tam had been fighting since Bellharrow—sung low, sung steady, a net of sound laid across the north rim of the moors.

*Don't sing it,* Zephyrine wanted to say to him. *I can see it in your mouth. Don't you dare hum it here.*

He didn't say it. That was its own kind of cowardice, and he knew it.

"This way," he murmured instead. "Down. The dead say the cordon skips the Hollow of Keening—their own listeners can't bear it. Too many voices. It drives the cantors' harmony apart."

"The Hollow of Keening," Tam repeated flatly. "That sounds like a place where nothing bad has ever happened."

"It's where the moor buries its worst." Zephyrine was already turning south. "Which is exactly why the Choir won't stand in it. Come on."

The Hollow announced itself an hour later as a change in the dead. All along their journey they had been accompanied—a soft crowd at the shoulder of his mind, murmurous, hopeful. As they descended toward the hollow's lip, that crowd fell silent. Drew back. What remained ahead was different: heavier, angrier, a pressure like deep water.

At the bottom lay the bones of a battle no song remembered. Banners rotted to lace. Helmets filled with rainwater and frogspawn. And rising from it, when Zephyrine set his boot on the first slick step of the descent, a sound—not one voice but a chord of them, a thousand throats holding a single note of grief so pure and so vast that Tam staggered and clapped his hands over his ears, and even Zephyrine, who had spent his life learning to carry such things, had to lean against a leaning stone and breathe.

"They died badly," he managed. "All of them. Both sides. And nobody ever—" He stopped. Nobody had ever told them it was over. Nobody had ever come.

*Carver.* The chord resolved, briefly, into something with attention in it. *You bring a living heart into our house. Why?*

"To pass through," Zephyrine said aloud, his voice absurdly small. "To reach the king who's sleeping under the ash. And then—to come back. For you. For all of you. Rest. Real rest. I swear it by my craft and my two hands."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Tam to whisper, "What did they say?"

"They haven't." Zephyrine wiped his mouth. "They're deciding."

And then the chord shifted—lowered, softened, spread itself out along the hollow's floor like a carpet, like a road, like an act of faith—and Zephyrine understood that he had just been believed by ten thousand dead men, and that believing him had cost them something, and that if he ever broke this promise there was not enough distance in the world to run.

"Let's keep moving," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word.

* * *

They camped that night inside the Hollow itself, because no place on the moor was safer than the place even the Choir feared, and because the dead there kept a watch no sentry could match.

Tam sat closest to the fire, feeding it peat cuttings, and Zephyrine watched him from under his lashes while pretending to work on a new carving—a small thing, a bird, started from habit. Tam's lips were moving. Just barely. Just the ghost of a shape, the way a man mouths words when he reads. And the tune—

The tune was the liturgy.

Zephyrine's knife stopped.

*He doesn't know,* said a voice near his ear—one of the moor dead, the drowned girl, drifting close out of what Zephyrine suspected was something like concern. *The seed-song. It's planted in him. He fights it in the day. At night, when the mind goes soft, it sings itself.*

*Can you take it out?* Zephyrine asked.

*No more than you can take a root out of fruit. You'd bruise what's left.*

Across the fire, Tam caught him staring and went still. "What?"

"Nothing." Zephyrine bent back over his carving. "Get some sleep. Long day tomorrow."

Tam lay down. Within minutes his breathing slowed—but his lips kept moving, faintly, faithfully, keeping time with a song the Choir had put in him like a hook in a fish's jaw, and Zephyrine sat up alone with the dead pressing gently at his awareness and the guilt arriving on schedule, punctual as pain.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: the dead were guiding him on a promise. Tam had followed him on loyalty. And Zephyrine had known—had *suspected*, at least, since Bellharrow, since the old woman's terrified face—what the Choir's songs did, what seeds they planted, what they waited for. He had let Tam walk beside him anyway, because he needed the company, because the moor was frightening alone, because some selfish starving part of him had wanted his friend back after refusing him at the exile vote and had taken him without asking whether taking him was safe.

Compassion weaponized. He'd promised ten thousand strangers rest to buy passage, and couldn't carve a single honest warning into his oldest friend.

*You could tell him,* the drowned girl offered.

*And if he panics? If he sings it louder?*

*Then you'd have traded his peace for your convenience,* she said, with the mild brutality of the dead, *which is, I think, the thing you're already afraid you've done.*

Zephyrine didn't answer. He worked on the little carved bird until the fire burned low, and the shape that emerged under his knife wasn't a bird at all—it was a mouth, open mid-song, and he stared at it a long time before burying it in the peat.

Near dawn, the dead woke him.

Not gently. The whole Hollow flared at once, ten thousand voices crying out in unison—not grief this time but alarm, a shriek of attention aimed north, and Zephyrine was on his feet with his knife drawn before his eyes finished opening.

"What is it?" Tam was up too, hand on his sword-hilt. "What's happening?"

"The cordon." Zephyrine's blood had gone cold. "It's moving. Coming south. Straight toward us—no, not toward us." He listened harder, sickeningly certain of what he'd find. "Toward *you*, Tam. One singer. Alone. She's—" He faltered, because what he heard was impossible: one voice moving against the Choir's own net, and the voice was beautiful, and it was searching, and every dead thing on the moor was flattening itself away from it like grass under wind.

"She's not sweeping," he said. "She's hunting. One specific resonance." He looked at Tam. Then, with a lurch, he understood, and looked at the map-case strapped against his own ribs. "An unbound voice. She can feel the map. Or me. Or—"

The mist at the hollow's northern rim parted, briefly, like a curtain drawn by a careful hand.

Far off across the black moor, a point of lantern-light swayed, coming on. And beneath the wind, clear and young and terribly sure of itself, a woman's voice began to sing.
