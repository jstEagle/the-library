## 11. The Map's Second Secret

The fire was small on purpose.

Zephyrine had learned that much in the weeks since Hollowmere: a small fire warmed a man just as well as a large one, and it could be smothered in a breath when the wind carried the wrong kind of silence—the listening kind. He fed it a single knot of bog-pine and watched the resin catch with a hiss and a smell like incense gone sour, and he told himself, as he had told himself every night since the moors, that tomorrow they would make better distance.

Tam slept ten feet away, wrapped in both their blankets because Zephyrine had given up his in an argument Tam had lost without knowing he'd lost it. His friend's breathing came slow and even, the breathing of a man who had not spent the day walking through a country of the dead who called him *carver* and *brother* and *the one who promised*. Tam had heard none of it. Tam had seen only mist and old stones and Zephyrine stopping every hundred yards to stand very still, jaw tight, hands opening and closing at his sides like a man learning to hold water in his fists.

Let him sleep. One of them should.

The map lay across Zephyrine's knees, weighted at the corners with four smooth stones he'd picked for the task with more care than he'd ever chosen gravestones. It was courier's vellum, waxed against rain, and the blood on it had gone the color of old rust where the dead man's fingers had gripped it. The bone handle of its roller was warm now from his hands. It was always warm. He had stopped remarking on this to himself somewhere north of the Weeping Moors, the way a man stops remarking on a tooth that aches only when he bites down.

He had unrolled it a hundred times. He knew the ink lines the way he knew the grain of the bones he carved: the valley road out of Hollowmere drawn in a sure hand; Bellharrow's market square marked with a tiny bell; the moor crossed by a dotted line that might have been a path or might have been the cartographer's despair; Greyford Bridge ahead, and beyond it the long climb into the mountains where the Ashen Crypt waited under its ruined abbey like a stone held underwater.

But tonight the firelight did something to the vellum it had never done before—or rather, something it had done every night, and tonight was the first night Zephyrine had been still enough, quiet enough, empty enough to see.

Heat, he thought later. It was always heat. The Choir sang through resonance; the dead spoke through warmth and cold and the pressure behind the ears. The courier had died holding this map against his chest, and blood carries heat longer than breath does.

Whatever the reason, the firelight caught the blank margin below the crypt's marking and showed him what lived inside the blankness: letters. Faint as frost-ferns on a window, written in something that wasn't ink at all—a shimmer in the fibers of the vellum itself, as though the words had been sung into the skin of the thing rather than written upon it.

Zephyrine went very still. Even his breathing slowed to nothing, because he had learned on the moors that the dead noticed breath the way wolves noticed movement.

*Sing,* said the faint script, in the way that all writing says sing to anyone who knows how to listen. Not aloud. Not with the throat. But Zephyrine had spent thirty years carving names into bone for families who wanted their dead remembered, and if there was one language he understood better than any other, it was the language of things made to hold a voice.

He hummed. One note, low, barely more than the fire itself making sound.

The script bloomed.

It rose off the vellum like breath rising off a pond at dawn—not moving so much as *arriving*, line after line of slender characters arranging themselves in the margin and then flowing outward, filling spaces between the drawn roads, curling around Bellharrow's bell and the moor's dotted path, until half the map wore a second skin of writing that no courier had ever carried in his pack, because it had not existed until a voice asked it to exist.

Zephyrine read. And reading, he stopped being cold, and stopped being tired, and stopped being in the shepherd's hollow at all.

*The binding-song is not lost,* the script said. Or words to that effect—the old tongue of Cael Adair, formal and liturgical, the cadence of it unmistakably the cadence of the Choir's liturgy, which meant the hand that wrote it had belonged to someone who had stood in those ranks. *What sits upon the throne of Cael Adair was made by song. The first song is kept with the king it unmade. In the Ashen Crypt, beneath the third altar, in the hollow where the body lies, the original binding is preserved: scored, sealed, and sleeping.*

*Whoever holds the first song holds the making of kings.*

*Whoever holds the first song can crown another.*

Zephyrine read it three times. Then a fourth, because the fourth time he was not reading it but simply looking at it, letting the meaning wash over him without comprehension, the way you let cold water close over your head.

The map had never been a delivery. That was the thought that arrived last and stayed longest. All these weeks he had thought of himself as a carrier—a bone-carver turned messenger, hauling a dead man's parcel north because a dying stranger had put it in his hands and the valley had put him out its gates and there had been nowhere else for either of them to go. A duty. A grim errand. Deliver the map to the crypt, call the true king back if the stories were true, and go home to a valley that didn't want him.

But the map was not a message about the crypt. The map *was* the crypt's second door. The binding-song—the original one, the first chord ever struck that taught a soul to wear a crown like a coat—was written here, hidden in heat and voice, riding quietly in his pack through every mile of a winter kingdom while he thought of himself as humble.

*Whoever holds the first song can crown another.*

He looked at the fire. He looked at the map. And he felt, rising in his chest with the slow terrible warmth of brandy taken on an empty stomach, the shape of a question he had spent his whole life avoiding.

Because here was the thing about Hollowmere, the thing he had never said aloud even to himself: the ward had not only silenced his gift. It had silenced his *temptation*. In the valley, where no dead voice could reach him, he had believed himself a modest man. Patient. Content with small work—memorial bones, name-beads, the little carvings grieving mothers pressed into their palms. He had thought that was who he was.

Then the ward had flickered, and the world had come back loud, and he had discovered within a month that his craft could kill—that carved bone could hold a chorus of betrayed voices and turn them loose like dogs—and he had tasted it, standing over two Choir scouts in the snow with his heart hammering and his mouth dry, and he had liked it. Not the killing. The *sounding*. The moment when the dead moved through him and the world rearranged itself at his word. He had walked away from that field telling himself he would never do it again, and he had known, in the honest cellar of himself where men keep the truths they don't serve guests, that he would do it again the first time he needed to.

And now the firelight was showing him a song that could make a king.

"Carver."

He nearly drove the knife—he hadn't known he'd picked up his knife—through his own thigh. Tam was sitting up, hair flattened on one side, eyes bleary in the firelight. "You're muttering."

"I'm reading."

"At midnight." Tam rubbed his face. "Bad dreams?"

"No," Zephyrine said, and then, because lying to Tam had begun to cost something new since he'd caught the liturgy humming in his friend's throat outside Bellharrow: "Yes. Not dreams, exactly. The map has more in it than roads."

Tam came closer, cross-legged, careful of the fire. He was a big man, Tam, a stonemason's son with mason's shoulders, and he folded himself down with the ease of someone who had shared a hundred campfires with Zephyrine going back to the year they'd stolen apples off Elder Maren's tree and blamed a badger. Looking at him now, Zephyrine tried to find the seam—the place where the Choir's song might have stitched itself into his friend—and hated himself for trying, and kept trying anyway.

"What kind of more?" Tam asked.

"The dangerous kind." Zephyrine weighed it. Every instinct said show him nothing; the scouts were out there, Vespera's hunters, and the fewer people who knew what the map truly held, the safer the knowledge slept. But Tam had followed him out of a valley that voted him gone. Tam had taken a singing-wound—no. No, that hadn't happened yet. That was still ahead of them, though Zephyrine had dreamed it twice now, the stolen voice, the blood singing out of his friend's throat, and he woke from those dreams with his own hands pressed over his own mouth. He shook the dream off. It was only a dream. The dead sent him warnings dressed as dreams sometimes, but not always, and he could no longer tell the difference, and that frightened him more than the Choir did.

"The crypt," he said finally. "It doesn't just hold the king. It holds the song that made the thing on the throne possible. The first binding. Written here." He tapped the margin, and the script, still faintly awake, shimmered under his fingertip like fish under ice.

Tam stared at it for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was carefully level. "So whoever brings the map north isn't delivering a message. They're carrying a weapon."

"Yes."

"And the courier died for it. And the elders threw you out over it. And we've walked through a country of ghosts for it." Tam looked up. "Zeph. Who else knows what's in it?"

"I don't know. Whoever wrote it. Whoever sent the courier. Possibly the Choir." He paused. "Possibly no one living. The script wakes for a voice—it may have slept through every hand that ever carried it."

"Convenient," Tam said, and there was no accusation in it, only the flat tired honesty of a man too cold to dress his thoughts. "A treasure map that only opens for you."

Zephyrine felt that land in his chest like a dropped stone. "You think I made it appear."

"I think you hear the dead and carve songs into bone and felled two armed men with a chorus of ghosts, and I love you like a brother, and I am asking an honest question because I'm afraid, and because some nights on this road you look at me like you're checking whether I'm still me." Tam held his gaze. "So. Honest answer."

Zephyrine looked at the fire for a long moment. The knot of pine had burned through; the flames sank and the shadows leaned in.

"Honest answer," he said. "I didn't write it. But I'm glad it opened. And I've been sitting here for the better part of an hour trying not to wonder what I could do with it."

Tam blinked. Whatever he'd expected, it wasn't that. "You'd have to be a madman to—"

"To use it? To crown myself?" Zephyrine laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "No. Worse than that, Tam. I've been wondering whether I could *fix* it. Whether I could take the binding apart and rebuild it better. A song that binds a soul to service—but gently. Willingly. A choir where no one gets eaten." He spread his hands. "Listen to me. Three weeks ago I was a bone-carver. Tonight I'm redesigning the machinery of tyranny in my head because I happened to be cold enough and quiet enough to see the words. That's how it starts. That's exactly how it started for whatever sits on that throne—I'd bet my hands it began as someone trying to make the world better with a song."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the bare birches with a sound almost like voices, and both of them flinched, and both pretended they hadn't.

"So we burn it," Tam said.

"We can't burn it. It's the proof. It's the key to the crypt—we think. And if the crypt falls to the wrong hands—" He stopped. Rubbed his eyes. "I don't know. Maybe we burn it. Maybe that's the whole right answer and everything else is vanity."

"But you don't want to."

"No," Zephyrine admitted. "I don't want to."

They sat with that. It was, Zephyrine thought, the most honest conversation they'd had since Bellharrow, and it had taken a haunted map and midnight to get there. Tam reached out and squeezed his shoulder once, hard, the way he'd done since they were boys, and went back to his blankets without another word, because that was Tam's whole philosophy of comfort: one grip, no speeches.

Zephyrine stayed up.

He rolled the map closed and sat holding it across his knees, feeling the bone handle warm against his palms, and he did the accounting he had been avoiding all night. If he delivered the map and the song to the crypt, and Aldric's spirit truly could be called, then the binding-song would rest in a dead man's tomb again—until the next courier, the next war, the next ambitious cantor dug it out. If he burned it, he might doom the true king and leave the crown-thing eating souls forever. If he kept it—

If he kept it, he would be the most dangerous man in Cael Adair, and he would deserve to be hunted, and part of him—the part that had liked the chorus of the betrayed, the part that had redesigned the machinery of tyranny by firelight—would enjoy being worth hunting.

There was a third option, whispering at the edge of the firelight, and he would not look at it yet. He would carry the map north. He would deliver it. What happened after delivery, what a man who could hear the dead might learn from a song that had been *scored, sealed, and sleeping*—that question could wait until it couldn't.

He banked the fire with ash and lay down, and sleep took its time coming.

When it finally came, it came with company. He dreamed of the Ashen Crypt without ever having seen it: a stair of black stone going down and down, walls scored with the same slender script that now rode in his pack, and at the bottom, in a hollow beneath a broken altar, a king lying with his eyes open in death and his lips moving, patiently, endlessly, saying a single word that Zephyrine strained toward the whole dream long and never quite heard.

He woke at grey dawn with frost on his eyelashes and the map's bone handle warm under his hand, and far to the south—too far to be real, surely too far, but real the way the dead were real—he heard the first thin thread of a choir beginning to move, and it was moving north.

Toward Greyford Bridge. Toward them.

He got Tam up without explaining why, and they struck camp in silence, and the map rode against Zephyrine's spine like a second heartbeat all the long cold morning.
