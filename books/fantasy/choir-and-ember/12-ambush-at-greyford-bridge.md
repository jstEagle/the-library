## 12. Ambush at Greyford Bridge

The bridge appeared out of the moor fog like a promise neither of them had asked for.

Greyford Bridge was older than any kingdom Zephyrine could name—three arches of blackened stone spanning a river the color of old pewter, its water moving without sound beneath a skin of ice that had not quite finished forming. The far bank rose into pine-dark hills, and beyond those hills, if the map spoke true, lay the high passes and the road to Cael Adair.

"Real stone," Tam said, stamping his boots on the approach. His breath came out in white plumes. "I'd begun to think everything in this country was peat and ghosts."

"The ghosts are on this side," Zephyrine said quietly.

It wasn't a figure of speech. Since the Weeping Moors had opened around them two days past, he had walked inside a crowd. They pressed close—not touching, never touching, but near as breath on his neck: the drowned, the starved, the fever-taken, generations of the moor's unburied dead who had felt his gift pass over them like warmth through a shuttered window and had followed it the way moths follow a lamp. He had promised them rest. He had promised them he would find a way to quiet them, to lay them down in whatever earth would take them. In return they guided him—whispering where the Choir's cordon thinned, which paths were watched, which watchfires belonged to friend or trap.

They were whispering now. That was the first wrongness.

*Cold ones on the bridge,* said a woman who had died of childbed fever a century ago. *Cold ones breathing steam. Cold ones whose hearts beat too slowly.*

Zephyrine stopped so abruptly that Tam took two more steps before turning back, eyebrows raised against the wind.

"What is it?"

"Get off the road." Zephyrine's mouth had gone dry. "Now."

* * *

Tam saw them when Zephyrine did: four figures rising from behind the bridge's near parapet where they had been crouched below the sightline, patient as stones. Not red-robed cantors but scouts—grey-cloaked, hooded, faces wrapped against cold, each carrying something that looked at first like a shepherd's crook and was not. The crooks' upper curves held coils of thin silver wire, and the wire sang. Even from thirty yards, even to a man with no gift at all, Tam could hear it: a thin, sweet thread of sound threading under the wind and into the space behind his eyes.

"Run," Zephyrine breathed.

He ran. Tam ran.

The fog took them both immediately, swallowing shape and direction, and Tam crashed through frozen tussocks of grass, lungs burning, ears full of wire-song. Behind him he heard Zephyrine shout something—the dead, maybe, calling directions—and then heard the scouts answer, not with words but with harmony. Four voices slid together into a chord that dropped through the fog like a fishhook through still water.

The chord caught him between the shoulder blades.

There was no pain, exactly. There was a *pulling*. Tam staggered, spun, and found one of the grey cloaks standing ten feet away, crook raised, silver wire humming, and the man's eyes above his face-wrap were calm and terribly kind, the way a butcher's eyes are kind to the animal.

*Tam,* Zephyrine screamed, somewhere. *Tam, they're not for you—they're for me, get down—*

Tam didn't get down. Tam did the stupidest thing he had ever done in a life not short on stupid things: he threw himself sideways, not away from the scout but *across* him, between the humming wire and the bone-carver stumbling through the fog with a dead king's map sewn into his coat.

The wire found him instead.

* * *

From Zephyrine's side of the fog it happened in pieces.

He heard the chord change. He heard Tam cry out—a short, bitten-off sound—and then he heard nothing from Tam at all, and that nothing was worse than any scream. He burst through a curtain of mist into a small cleared pocket where the world had arranged itself into catastrophe: three scouts closing in a slow arc, crooks raised, wires singing; Tam on his knees in the frost with his mouth open wide and wide and *nothing coming out of it*, his throat working like a man drowning in air; and the fourth scout standing over him with the crook lowered, drawing the wire gently back along its coil, and coiling off the end of the wire, note by luminous note, a ribbon of pale gold light that might have been beautiful if you had no idea what it was.

A voice. Tam's voice, being reeled out of him like silk off a spindle.

Zephyrine had seen the Choir work only once before, from a distance, on the mountain road—a captured resonant soul dragged singing into a glass vial. He had told himself the stories were exaggeration. They were not. The gold ribbon curled and thickened as it left Tam, and with every inch that passed out of him, Tam dimmed. His eyes lost focus. His hands, clawed into the frozen grass, went slack.

Something in Zephyrine's chest tore loose from its moorings.

"No—" He was running before he knew it. "No no no—*give it back—*"

The scouts turned as one. Three crooks swung toward him, three chords braided together, and the song hit him like a wall of hands. It was the liturgy—he knew it now, Bellharrow had taught him its shape even through wax-stopped ears—the obedience-song, the *kneel-and-be-quiet*, pitched this time not to soothe but to seize. It poured into his ears, his bones, the hollow places the ward in Hollowmere had kept empty for thirty years. His knees buckled. His jaw wanted to open and sing along. Somewhere very far away, a voice that sounded like his own thought: *this is what they do, this is what they do to everyone, this is what the whole kingdom hums in the market squares without knowing why.*

And underneath the liturgy, closer than his own heartbeat, the dead of the moors pressed in against his back like a tide against a sea wall.

*Brother-carver.* The childbed-fever woman. Her voice was ragged with fury. *Brother-carver, they are taking him. You promised us rest. Will you let them take the living too?*

"I promised you rest," Zephyrine ground out, on his knees, blood running warm from his nose across his lips. The liturgy squeezed. He sang a half-line of it before he could stop himself and hated himself for the sweetness of it. "I promised—I can't—"

*You can,* said an old man dead of the moor-fever, sixty years gone. *You have been walking among us asking our help and giving nothing. We do not blame you. But ask now, carver. Ask, and we will give you the only thing we have.*

"Which is?"

*Ourselves.*

The meaning of it landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He understood, in one vertiginous instant, what they were offering. Not their voices. Not their guidance. Their *bodies*—their cold, formless, furious presences, loosed all at once through his gift, through his carved bones if he had any, through the raw open channel of a man on his knees with his gift burning wider than it had ever burned. They would come through him like water through a broken dam. And the scouts—living men, hearing ears, singing throats—would be standing in the flood.

*I promised you rest,* Zephyrine said again, but the words had changed shape in his mouth. They had become a question: *is this rest?*

*Rest is quiet,* said the fever-woman. *Those men will be very quiet indeed.*

On the frost ten feet away, Tam swayed. The fourth scout drew another arm's length of gold ribbon from his lips. Tam's eyes found Zephyrine's, and there was no fear in them—there was something worse, an apology, *I'm sorry I couldn't get up, I'm sorry I'm slow*—and Zephyrine broke.

"Yes," he said aloud. "Yes. Come through."

* * *

Later, Tam would try to describe what he saw, and fail, because he had no voice to describe it with and no true memory of it either—only flashes, seen through eyes that barely worked. A light in Zephyrine like a struck bell made visible. The fog *reversing*, pouring inward toward one kneeling man instead of drifting past him. Shapes in the reversed fog. Many shapes.

What the four scouts experienced was simpler and shorter.

The liturgy collapsed mid-chord. All three singing crooks went silent at once, silver wires shivering like plucked harp strings, because the men holding them had stopped being able to hold anything. One turned to run. The moor-dead were not fast and they were not slow; they simply *were everywhere*, suddenly, the way cold is everywhere, and where they passed through living flesh the flesh forgot how to be warm, how to beat, how to want. The runner made six steps. Another scout dropped his crook and stood with his mouth opening and closing, his own song pulled out of him backward by a hundred jealous dead who had waited lifetimes to hear what a stolen voice looked like from the outside. The last one—the one holding Tam's gold ribbon—had time to look at Zephyrine, really look, and Tam, fading, saw the man's eyes change from kindness to a very precise and human terror before the fog closed over his face.

Then it was over. The fog drifted normally again. Four grey shapes lay in the frost, and the frost did not melt around them.

And the gold ribbon—Tam's voice, coiled bright in the dead scout's slack hand—was gone. Not fallen. Not lying in the grass. *Taken*, snatched into the moor-dark by some quick dead thing, or dissolved, or carried north like a spark on wind. Zephyrine searched the frost on his knees until his fingers were bloody, whispering *no, no, it has to be here, it has to—* and it was not there, and the dead stood silent around him in their hundreds, waiting, patient, terrible.

"Tam." He crawled to his friend. "Tam, I'm here, it's done, they're—"

Tam's mouth moved. His throat worked. His face did all the things a speaking face does, and produced nothing—not a whisper, not a breath-tone, not even the wet clicking of a paralyzed larynx. Just silence shaped like words. He pressed his palm flat against his own sternum as though checking whether the rest of him was still there. Then he looked at Zephyrine, and his eyes asked the question so loudly that Zephyrine flinched.

"I don't know," Zephyrine said. "I don't know if it can be given back. The Choir keeps voices. They keep them somewhere. If there's a vault, if there's a place—"

Tam closed his eyes. When he opened them again, he had arranged his face into something almost steady, and he tapped twice on the frozen ground, then pointed north: *the map. Keep going.* And then, after a moment, he touched his own chest with two fingers and pointed at Zephyrine: *it should have been you. Thank you anyway.*

"Don't," Zephyrine said hoarsely. "*Don't forgive me. Don't you dare—*"

But Tam was already trying to stand, already gesturing at the bridge, at the distance they needed to put between themselves and the bodies before the scouts were missed, and Zephyrine understood with sick clarity that this—practicality, forward motion, the stubborn refusal to lie down—was simply who Tam was, and that he had known this man since they were boys stealing apples in Hollowmere, and that Tam had thrown himself across a singing-wire for him without a heartbeat of hesitation, and that Zephyrine had answered by feeding him to it.

Around them, in the fog, the dead waited for what they had been promised.

* * *

They did not cross Greyford Bridge. They crossed the river a mile south, at the ford the fever-woman showed them, wading shin-deep through water that tried to freeze to their skin, because the bridge was a landmark and landmarks got watched. Zephyrine walked with the map's weight against his ribs and Tam's silence walking beside him, and the silence was the loudest thing in the world.

He kept seeing the gold ribbon. He kept feeling the moment he had said *yes*—the way something in him had leaned into it, eager, before his conscience caught up. He had tasted it on the moor, in chapter and verse: what his craft could do when he stopped flinching from it. He had felled Choir scouts once before with a chorus of the betrayed, and he had told himself it was necessity, and perhaps it had been. But today he had not hesitated at all. Today some part of him had *wanted* it—the flood, the power, the righteous terrible arithmetic of four lives weighed against one friend. And the arithmetic had come out right, hadn't it? Tam lived. The cost was only his voice. Only his name-sound, his laugh, his curses, his bad singing on long roads. Only everything Tam was, poured out into a coiled ribbon and carried off to sit in some vault in Cael Adair next to a hundred other stolen souls, while the thing wearing the king's crown fed.

*This is what they do,* Zephyrine thought. *And I am beginning to do it too. Smaller. Slower. But the same.*

At dusk they sheltered in the lee of a drystone wall, sharing hard bread and melted snow, and Tam wrote in the frost with a stick: *YOU DID WHAT YOU HAD TO.*

"I broke my word," Zephyrine said. "To the dead. To you. To everyone."

*THEY WERE GOING TO TAKE US BOTH.*

"And I'll break it again." He stared at his hands, the carver's hands, scarred and steady and far too capable. "That's what frightens me, Tam. Not that I did it. That next time it'll be easier."

Tam looked at him for a long moment. Then he wiped the frost clear and wrote, slowly, pressing hard:

*THEN WE MAKE SURE THERE ISN'T A NEXT TIME. WE FINISH THIS. WE GET THE VOICES BACK—ALL OF THEM. YOURS TOO, CARVER. THE DEAD ONES YOU KEEP CARRYING.*

Zephyrine laughed—a cracked, awful sound—and did not answer, because across the darkening moor, faint and far and getting less far, he heard something that stopped his heart.

Singing. Not the scouts' working-chords. Something larger. Many voices in perfect discipline, sweeping the northern horizon line by line, a search-pattern made of harmony, and threaded through it—one voice above the others, young and clear and achingly gifted, leading the search with a precision that could only mean she had found a trail.

Vespera. Coming for what her squad had failed to bring back.

Tam saw his face change and grabbed his arm. Zephyrine pointed north, toward the sound, toward the passes, toward the only road left.

"We walk tonight," he whispered. "All night. And Tam—we need to talk about what's actually buried in the Ashen Crypt. Because I read the map's second secret two nights ago, and I haven't told you, and you're going to want to sit down."

Above them, the first snow of the legend-winter began to fall.
