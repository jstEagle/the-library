## 13. Winter Falls Early

The snow began at dusk on the third day out of the moors, and it did not fall so much as arrive — a vast grey weight lowering itself over the shoulders of the world, as though the sky had grown tired of holding itself up and meant to lie down upon the earth for good.

Zephyrine had been climbing for two days when he understood what he was walking into. The dead had told him, back in the Weeping Moors, in their hundreds of small voices: *winter comes early, bone-carver. Winter comes the way grief comes, all at once and without mercy.* He had thought them speaking in riddles, the way the dead often did, their meanings worn smooth by decades of decay. Now, watching the first flakes thicken into a white roar that erased the path three paces behind him, he understood they had simply been precise.

He was still above the tree line when it hit properly. The wind came off the eastern peaks like something released from a long captivity, carrying ice in its teeth, and Zephyrine learned very quickly that the mountain did not care that he was carrying a map that might save a kingdom. It did not care about his mission or his guilt or the ghost of Tam's stolen voice that he still heard sometimes in the wind, singing harmonies he could not follow. The mountain cared only that he was warm-blooded and slow, and it went to work on him with the patient industry of all indifferent things.

He walked with his head down and his hands jammed into his armpits, counting steps because counting steps was the only prayer left to him. At four hundred he lost the trail entirely. At six hundred he lost the sense of which way was north. At eight hundred he stumbled over something soft that groaned, and his heart lurched up into his throat before he realized it was a sheep — a ewe, half-buried already, blinking at him with the dull astonishment of an animal that has accepted its circumstances.

Where there were sheep, there were shepherds. Where there were shepherds, there was shelter.

He followed the ewe. She seemed to know where she was going better than he did, ploughing through the drifts toward a dark shape that resolved slowly out of the white chaos: a bothy, low and stone-walled, its roof weighted down with boulders against exactly this kind of weather. A peat stack leaned against the lee wall under a slab of slate. The door was not latched, because doors this far from anything had no reason to be.

Zephyrine got the ewe inside first — some stubborn animal decency he couldn't have explained — and then himself, and then stood in the dark of the single room shaking snow off his cloak while his eyes adjusted to the dimness. There was a hearth, blackened by years of fires. A straw pallet in the corner. A shelf bearing a clay jug, a knife, and a candle stub no bigger than his thumb. On the wall, someone had nailed a horseshoe and, beside it, a small carved charm — crude work, a shepherd's attempt at a warding figure, all wrong proportions and sincere intention.

Zephyrine looked at it for a long time. Then he hung his wet cloak by the hearth and built a fire, because sentiment did not keep a man warm but peat did.

* * *

By full dark the storm had become a sound more than a weather — a continuous grinding roar against the walls, as if the mountain were trying to chew its way in. The ewe stood dozing by the door. Zephyrine sat close to the fire with the map across his knees, and for the hundredth time since Greyford Bridge, he read it without seeing it.

It was hard, now, to look at the thing without seeing Tam's face. The courier's bloodstained parchment, the bone-handled case, the seal of Cael Adair pressed in wax gone brown with age — all of it had once been just a burden, heavy and strange. Now every line of ink was laid over the memory of Tam stepping between him and Vespera's scouts, arms spread, mouth open around words that never finished, because the singing-wound took the words *and everything else*. One moment his friend was shouting at him to run; the next his mouth moved and produced nothing at all, and his eyes went wide not with pain but with horror — the horror of a man reaching for the one thing every person owns and finding the shelf empty.

Zephyrine had run. That was the part that sat in him like swallowed stone. Vespera's squad had closed in and he had done what he promised the moor-dead he would never do — he had called them up out of the peat and the black water, the betrayed and the drowned and the forgotten, and he had aimed them at living men like a weapon, and in doing so he had broken his word to the dead. *Rest*, he had promised them. *I will give you rest if you show me the way past the cordon.* Instead he had given them one more night of violence, one more chance to be the thing everyone feared they were. And Tam —

Tam was gone. Taken. His voice locked in some vault beneath the Choir's citadel, a jarred specimen among thousands, and Zephyrine had last seen him kneeling in the snow with his silent mouth moving, moving, moving.

The fire popped. The storm ground on. Zephyrine looked down at the map and felt the thought arrive — the way such thoughts always arrive, quietly, reasonably, dressed in sensible clothes — that none of this would have happened if not for the map.

That was not true, of course. He knew it wasn't. But knowing a thought is false has never once stopped it from being comfortable, and comfort was the rarest commodity on the mountain that night.

*If I burn it,* the thought said, *the hunt ends. They can't want what no longer exists.*

*They can,* said a colder voice, the voice that had carried him out of Hollowmere. *They'll want you. You heard the courier. You've been marked since the ford.*

*Then let me die marked,* the first voice answered, gentle as snowfall. *Better than arriving. Better than standing in that crypt and hearing what the price is. You already know what the price is. Aldric will ask for a voice, and whose voice is there to give?*

And there it was. Not the storm, not the cold, not even the guilt over Tam — but this, the arithmetic underneath everything, which he had been refusing to do since the moors. The map led to a dead king who needed a living voice willingly lent and willingly lost. Vespera had argued her trained cantor's voice would serve best; she'd been ready to argue it again, before the bridge, before everything. Tam had written on slate — *I owe a debt* — as if his silence hadn't cost enough already. And Zephyrine had known, in the place where he kept his worst knowledge, that both of them were wrong and he was right, because only his gift could hold two souls in one throat, and because he had been borrowing voices from the dead his whole life and it was time the debt came due.

He had lied to them about when. He had planned to lie to them again.

And now Tam's voice sat in a vault, and Vespera was either dead or turned fully back to the Choir, and the passes were sealed, and midwinter was coming whether he reached the Ashen Crypt or rotted in a shepherd's bothy waiting for spring — and if he waited, he would miss the window the dead courier had died whispering about. *Before midwinter.* The stars would not align twice.

So the choice, stripped of everything, was this: burn the map and live as a hunted man until the Choir found him, or carry it on and erase himself. Those were the doors. Everything else was decoration.

Zephyrine crouched and pulled the map from its bone-handled case.

* * *

The parchment was stiff with cold. He held it near the fire, not quite touching flame, watching the firelight shine through the vellum so that the inked mountains of the kingdom glowed like veins in a leaf. The route to the Ashen Crypt traced a thin dark line northward, through the ruined abbey, down beneath the earth. Somewhere along that line, ten years dead and still lucid, King Aldric waited with his terrible offer.

"You're a fool," Zephyrine told the map. His voice sounded strange in the bothy, flattened by the storm, belonging to no one. "You're a fool, and you killed a courier, and you got Tam's voice taken, and you broke your word to the dead, and for what? For a king you never met. For a kingdom that exiled you."

Hollowmere. He hadn't let himself think the name in days, and now it arrived whole, with weather of its own. Elder Maren's face at the vote, sorrowful and immovable. *We are sorry, Zeph. But we are old, and the valley is small, and you bring a crown's wrath with you like a smell of smoke.* The bridges burned behind him — literally burned, the elders firing the timber crossings so no Choir rider could ride in easily after him, destroying in one evening the work of four generations. His home, made less, because of him. Tam offering to come, and Zephyrine refusing him — refusing him *kindly*, which was worse — and Tam following anyway, and paying with the only thing he had left to pay with.

"If I stop here," Zephyrine said aloud, "it was all for nothing. If I go on, I stop being me." He laughed, and the laugh came out cracked and ugly. "Either way I'm not the man who carved memorial bones in a quiet valley. So what am I preserving, exactly, by keeping my name?"

The ewe stirred by the door and settled again.

He tilted the map closer to the flames. The corner began to curl. Heat breathed up against his fingers, and he watched the wax seal — the king's own seal, the mark of a man ten years dead who still refused to lie down — begin to soften, and something in his chest rose up and howled *no*, not loudly, not dramatically, just a small animal refusal, the way the body refuses to step off a ledge even when the mind has reasoned its way to the edge.

He pulled the map back.

For a long moment he simply knelt there, shaking, furious — at himself, at the storm, at the dead courier for choosing him, at Tam for following, at Aldric for dying in a way that required assistance. Then he folded the map along its creases, returned it to the bone case, and set the case on the far side of the hearth where he could not reach it without getting up.

"That's not a decision," he informed the room. "That's cowardice wearing a decision's coat."

But he slept, eventually, sitting upright with his back against the stones, and the storm sang him down into a dreamless dark.

* * *

He woke to silence.

Storm-silence has a particular quality, different from other silences — a pressure, an absence of the roar the ears have braced themselves against, so that waking feels like surfacing from underwater. Zephyrine opened his eyes to a bothy lit blue-white through the smoke-hole, and for one heartbeat he could not remember where he was, or why his ribs ached, or why the taste of ash was in his mouth.

Then he remembered everything at once, which was worse.

He crawled to the door and shouldered it open against the drift behind it. The world outside was blinding. The storm had passed in the night and left behind a landscape unmade — the trail gone, the cairns gone, the very shapes of the hills rewritten under six feet of new snow. The sky overhead was a hard, clean, merciless blue, the kind of sky that looks like mercy and means frostbite. In every direction the high passes stood sealed, white ramparts rising into white peaks, roads that would not open until thaw, if they opened at all. This was the winter of legend the old people muttered about — the Long White, the year the passes shut and the valleys ate their seed-corn and the roads filled with the frozen honest and the frozen desperate alike. Exactly as the dead foretold. The dead were always right. That was the curse of them; they had nothing left to be wrong about.

Zephyrine stood in the doorway breathing air like broken glass and felt despair settle over him — not the dramatic kind, not the kind that shouts, but the quiet kind, the kind that simply sits down inside a man and makes itself at home. The window was closing. Midwinter approached, and the crypt lay beyond passes no living thing could cross, and Tam was silent somewhere south of here, and Vespera was —

He stopped. Squinted. South, down the long white shoulder of the mountain, a mile off perhaps, maybe less — movement. A speck against the snow, dark against all that brightness, and then another. Two figures, laboring upward through drifts waist-deep, staggering, falling, rising. No sledges. No banners. No column of Choir red.

Two figures, coming toward him. And even at that distance, even through the glittering haze of reflected sun, Zephyrine knew the taller one's lurching gait — knew it the way you know a song you grew up with — and the shorter one walked like someone accustomed to being obeyed, head high, cutting the wind with her shoulders.

His heart did something complicated and painful.

"No," he said to the empty blue sky, to the sealed passes, to whatever stubborn gods arranged such things. "No, don't — you can't have — "

But they were real, and they were climbing, and they were coming to him, and the map sat in its bone case on the far side of the hearth, and midwinter was coming, and Zephyrine Dunmore — exile, liar, breaker of promises to the dead — turned back into the bothy to rake the coals alive, because whether he had decided or not, the decision had just walked a mile through the snow to find him.
