## 14. The Cantor's Doubt

The vaults beneath the Choir's bastion in Cael Adair were called the Quiet Rooms, and Vespera had believed for eleven years that this was a kindness—a place where voices could rest after a lifetime of song.

She descended now through seven levels of spiral stair, each landing colder than the last, her cantor's sash heavy on her shoulder with its silver bells that made no sound. That was the first thing she had never questioned: why a Choir's bells should be silenced. She had been told it was discipline. A voice must be offered, not announced. She had been fourteen, and she had believed it the way one believes the sun will rise—because believing otherwise required an effort of imagination she had not yet learned to make.

She was twenty-five now, and the effort came easily, uninvited, at the worst hours.

The summons had come at dawn, written in Master Cantor Ilyan's own hand, which was unusual enough to frighten her before she'd broken the seal. *Cantor Vespera. The voice taken at Greyford Bridge has proven anomalous. It resists indexing. You will go down and quiet it yourself.* No explanation of what *anomalous* meant. No mention of the scout's report—that the bone-carver had escaped with nothing but his life and whatever the map held, while she had returned with two captured scouts' worth of failure and one stolen voice singing in a lead-lined jar like a trapped wasp.

She carried the jar now against her ribs. It was warm. Voices were always warm; that was the first lesson they taught you, before they taught you anything else. *A voice is a living thing,* said the primer they put in the hands of every choir-child. *Treat it gently, and it will serve.*

They never said *whose* living thing.

* * *

The door at the bottom of the stairs was iron, and older than the bastion above it—older than the Choir itself, if the archivists were honest, which they only were when drunk or dying. The lock answered to a sung phrase, three notes descending, and Vespera sang them softly, the way one speaks near a sickbed.

The door opened onto darkness that breathed.

Not wind. Breath. The Quiet Rooms exhaled a slow, warm tide of air that smelled of candlewax, old wool, and something faintly sweet that she had never let herself name—the smell of a room where many people have slept for a very long time without waking.

She lifted her hand-lamp. Its light fell across row upon row of glass vessels, each no larger than a wine jug, each holding a curl of pale gold light that turned slowly, endlessly, like smoke deciding to be a shape. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They lined shelves from floor to ceiling down aisles so long the lamp could not find their ends. And each one, as her light passed over it, leaned toward her.

She felt it the way she felt weather in her teeth: a pressure, a leaning, a thousand small attentions turning her way at once.

"Indexing," she whispered, because saying the word aloud had always made it manageable. "Cataloguing. Preservation." The words of her training, worn smooth as river stones from handling.

None of the lights in their jars looked preserved. They looked *waiting.*

Her boots sounded wrong on the stone—too loud, too alone—and then she realized they did not sound wrong at all. The silence around them was wrong. In a vault full of voices there should have been resonance, hum, some vast chord pressed flat by the glass. There was nothing. The jars did not muffle the voices. Something drank them before sound could form, and the warmth she'd felt through the lead was not the voice's heat escaping.

It was the jar trying to sing and being eaten for it.

Vespera stood very still in the aisle, lamp raised, and let herself think the thought whole for the first time: *These are not recordings. These are people.*

She knew the doctrine. Every cantor knew it. Voices were harvested from the condemned, from oath-breakers, from those who gave them willingly in exchange for debt-forgiveness or a family fed through winter. The harvested voice was *empty*, the priests said—shorn of the soul, which passed on to whatever waited beyond. Only the sound remained, the music of a life, kept like pressed flowers. Harmless. Beautiful. Useful.

But she had heard Tam's voice in the jar for six days.

It had wept. It had begged—not in words, voices in jars could not make words, only tones—but in the language beneath words, the rising fall of pleading that needed no translation. On the third night it had sung a fragment of a harvest-home song, a country tune, badly, the way a man sings who learned it from a friend rather than a master. Voices shorn of souls did not remember harvest songs. Pressed flowers did not *ache.*

And now, under her lamp, the light in the newest jar—Tam's voice, anomalous, resistant—pressed itself against the glass toward her, and Vespera understood what the crown-thing on its throne ate at midwinter feast, understood what the Choir's great Resonance fed upward through the spires of Cael Adair every dusk when the liturgy rolled off ten thousand obedient tongues.

It ate the waiting. It ate ten thousand voices wanting, wanting, wanting, forever, and distilled that hunger into power. The dead king had not merely been replaced. He had been *farmed*—and the Choir were not priests. They were harvesters, and the fields were people, and she had spent eleven years sharpening herself into a scythe.

* * *

She found the indexing ledgers where she knew they would be, in the alcove behind the third pillar, chained to the desk like everything precious in this kingdom was chained. She had helped maintain them as a novice. Her own neat hand was in these pages—cataloguing, cataloguing, dates and sources and tonal signatures, columns of numbers where names should have been. Voices did not get names. Names belonged to souls, and souls were gone, and so the ledgers reduced lives to pitch and timbre and date of taking.

She turned pages back. Past her own entries. Past Ilyan's. Past the founding hand, cramped and old. Back to the first page, the first entry ever made, and there she stopped, because the first entry was dated thirty-one years ago—ten years before the king's death, ten years before the crown-thing wore Aldric's face—and the source column did not say *condemned* or *willing.*

It said: *Royal commission. Donor: the Court Composer. Purpose: the Making.*

The Making. Capital letters in a ledger that used none anywhere else. She read the entry four times. The court composer of Cael Adair, a man named Ferren Odo whose madrigals she had sung since childhood, had given his voice—had been *commissioned* to give it—a decade before anyone spoke of usurpation. And beside the entry, in the same founding hand, a note:

*First binding successful. Subject retains form. Feeding schedule established: one voice per moonrise during the dark months, drawn through the Resonance. Subject reports the hunger eases but does not end. Recommend expansion of harvest. Recommend a Choir.*

Recommend a Choir. The Ember Choir had not existed to serve the crown-thing. The crown-thing had existed to consume what the Choir gathered. The order she had given her childhood to, the disciplines, the silencing of her own small silver bells, the years of learning to hold a note until it became a leash—all of it built backward from that single line, *recommend a Choir*, the way a cathedral is built backward from the relic it must house.

Vespera closed the ledger and stood in the sweet-smelling dark and felt her loyalty die not with a cry but with a quiet click, like a lock finally answering the right key.

* * *

She did not decide to defect. Decisions, she had always thought, were things you sang—one clean phrase, chosen and released. What happened instead was slower and less musical: she walked back up the seven staircases, and at each landing she asked herself *turn back, turn back now*, and at each landing her feet continued upward, carrying her body away from obedience one step at a time, the way a river carries a leaf out of a pond it has no business escaping.

Master Cantor Ilyan received her report in his tower room, all white stone and vertical light, where he kept his own voice in a jar of black glass on the mantel—a vow of the highest cantors, to surrender even their speaking voice and sing only with the trained instrument of their craft. She had always found the vow beautiful. Now she looked at the black jar and saw a man who had cut out his own tongue and called it devotion.

"The anomalous voice," she said. "I quieted it."

"You did not." Ilyan did not look up from his scores. He was a thin man, dry as a struck match, and his eyes when they lifted were the eyes of someone who counted everything and valued nothing. "The jar still resists shelving. Six days, Cantor. Do you know how long it has been since a voice resisted?" He rose, crossed to the window, and looked north, toward the mountains, toward Greyford, toward the bone-carver who had slipped her net. "Resonance means the soul is still attached. Which is impossible. Unless—" he smiled without warmth, "—the donor gave more than his voice. Some peasants love too loudly. It complicates the harvest."

"And what happens to a voice with its soul still attached?"

"It goes to the throne, eventually. As all the best ones do." He said it the way he discussed tuning. "Why do you ask?"

Because I have carried your prisoner against my ribs for six nights and it knows my footsteps, she thought. Because it sings harvest songs at me in the dark. Because I have begun to hear the jars below this tower even through the stone, even through my sleep, a city of the wanting-dead beneath the holy city, and I cannot unknow it.

"I ask," she said carefully, "to serve better."

Ilyan studied her for a long moment, and Vespera did the hardest singing of her life: she sang nothing at all, kept her face a held rest-note, gave him no overtone to read. At last he nodded and dismissed her, and only at the door did his voice follow her, mild as milk:

"Cantor Vespera. Your doubts are noted."

She froze, hand on the latch.

"They are always noted. We harvest doubt too, in the end. It makes excellent bass."

* * *

Tam's cell was in the infirmary wing, which was the Choir's word for the place where they kept damaged instruments. He sat on the cot with his back straight—he had a soldier's spine, even hollowed out—and when she entered he looked at her with eyes that tracked her movement too carefully, the wariness of a man who has been mute among enemies long enough to become fluent in watching.

His throat bore the singing-wound: a pale spiral scar, precise as script, coiling from jaw to collarbone. She had seen such wounds on failed donors. The extraction took the voice and left a mark shaped like the binding-song itself, a signature of ownership written in flesh.

She set down the tray she'd brought—bread, broth, the ordinary kindnesses that were also surveillance, because she'd laced the bread with a mild truth-tonic the first week and hated herself efficiently, quietly, every time. This tray was clean. She had decided that much already, though she told herself it meant nothing yet.

"Eat," she signed, in the hand-cant the Choir used with the muted. Simple signs: eat, rest, safe.

He ate. Then he signed back, and the question was the one he had signed every day for six days, patient as water wearing stone: *My friend. The carver. Did he escape?*

She should lie. Doctrine said lie; the muted were assets, and assets were managed. Instead she signed: *Yes. North. With the map.*

Something moved through Tam's face that she had no sign for—relief so large it frightened the face containing it. And then he signed again, faster: *You are the one who took my voice.*

Not a question. She signed the truth because lying to him suddenly seemed obscene, here, in front of that spiral scar: *Yes.*

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he touched his scarred throat, and signed, slowly, deliberately: *I don't hate you. That's the worst part. My grandmother was Choir-trained, in Bellharrow. Before the liturgy came. She taught me that the songs were prayers once. So I keep waiting to hate you and it won't come, and I think—I think you're waiting too.*

Vespera stood very still. Outside the narrow window, snow was beginning again, the early winter the dead courier had foretold, sealing passes and plans alike.

*I heard your voice,* she signed. *In the jar. For six days.*

His hands stopped.

*It sang a harvest song,* she signed. *Voices without souls don't remember songs. Everything they told us about harvesting is a lie, isn't it. All of it.*

Tam's eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake. He signed one word: *Yes.*

Then, after a moment: *What will you do?*

She thought of the ledger. *First binding successful. Recommend a Choir.* She thought of ten thousand jars leaning toward her lamplight like flowers toward a sun that only ever took. She thought of Ilyan's mild voice: *we harvest doubt too.*

What will you do. As if she were someone who did things. As if she were not an instrument that had just discovered hands.

*There is a copy of the extraction key,* she signed. *In the vault. The jar with your voice can be opened. But opened is not healed—your voice will need a throat that can carry it, or a vessel that can hold it until one exists. There is a man who carves bones that hold voices.* She hesitated, then finished the heresy: *We find him together, or I open that jar alone tonight and we both take our chances.*

Tam stared at her. Then he laughed—soundlessly, shoulders shaking, the laugh of a man whose voice has been stolen and whose sense of the ridiculous survived the theft. He wiped his eyes and signed: *The Choir teaches its cantors to counter any argument. What do they teach you to do when the argument is your own conscience?*

Nothing, she wanted to say. Nothing. That is the flaw in all their harmony—they tuned us to every note in the world except the one inside ourselves.

*Come on,* she signed. *Midnight bell. Wear your cloak. And Tam—"*

She paused, hands hovering, aware that she was about to commit the first sentence of the rest of her life, and that it would be spoken not in her glorious trained voice but in silence, in signs, like everything true she had left.

*Thank you,* she signed. *For not hating me. It made this possible.*

* * *

The midnight bell rang at midnight, as bells do, and no one in the bastion marked two figures crossing the frozen garden where the Choir's roses grew black and scentless in winter. Vespera went first, through the postern whose warding-song she had sung since she was fifteen—she knew every note of the Choir's defenses the way a daughter knows her mother's breathing, and she hated how much that knowledge felt like betrayal, and how little.

At the wall, Tam stopped and looked back once, at the spires where the Resonance glowed its soft perpetual gold against the snow-clouds, feeding, feeding. He touched his scarred throat. Then he looked at her and signed a single word:

*North?*

North, where the passes were closing. North, after a bone-carver who had every reason to run from a cantor of the Choir, escorted by a man who could not shout a warning if she turned on him. North, into the early winter, carrying nothing but her doubts and a stolen voice in a lead-lined jar that she had taken from the vault an hour ago, warm against her ribs, singing its silent harvest song.

"Yes," Vespera said aloud, and the sound of her own voice speaking freely—unbound, unblessed, hers—was the most frightening and beautiful music she had ever made. "North. And may whatever listens forgive us, because I don't think the Choir will."

Behind them the Resonance flared, once, gold as a scream. Somewhere far to the south, in a tower room full of white stone and vertical light, she imagined Ilyan setting down his pen and noting, in his flawless hand, that the hunt had changed direction.

Let him note it. Let him come. She had spent twenty-five years learning to listen through song—and now, walking out of the only home she had ever known with a thief's heart and a traitor's feet, Vespera of the Ember Choir began at last to hear what the songs had been hiding all along.

It sounded like a name, spoken back into the dark. And it was not hers—but she followed it north anyway.
