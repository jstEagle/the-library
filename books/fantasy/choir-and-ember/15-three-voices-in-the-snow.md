## 15. Three Voices in the Snow

The knife found Zephyrine's throat before the voice did.

He had heard her coming, of course—he always heard them now, the dead clustered along the treeline like starlings, muttering *someone comes, someone comes*—but hearing and stopping were different creatures. The snow was deep, the bothy's single window frosted blind, and by the time he turned from the fire she was already inside, boots silent on packed earth, blade cold against the soft hollow beneath his jaw.

"Where is it," Vespera said.

Her voice. He knew that voice. He had spent weeks learning to flinch from the Choir's harmonies as they swept down the passes like weather, and hers had threaded through all of them—youngest cantor, the scouts called her, silver-throated, merciless. But up close, out of harmony with a hundred others, it sounded smaller than he'd imagined. Younger. Tired in a way songs couldn't fix.

"The map," she said. The knife pressed a fraction deeper. A bead of warmth ran down his neck and froze. "Don't make me ask twice. I've watched men die for less than what you carry."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I've made some of them die."

Something flickered across her face—the briefest hesitation, the kind he'd learned to read in dying men's eyes. She had heard about Greyford Bridge. About the moor-dead rising out of the black water in their hundreds, wearing his promise like a banner. About the two Choir scouts found frozen mid-scream, their throats full of other people's voices.

"You're the bone-carver," she said quietly. Not a question.

"Was." He didn't reach for the knife. He kept his hands open on his knees, palms up, the way you sat with the dead. "Now I'm just a man who owes several hundred ghosts a debt he can't pay."

"They say the dead follow you."

"They say the dead *mistake me for someone useful.*" He met her eyes. Hers were grey-green, wind-burned at the edges, and behind the cantor's iron there was something he recognized: the look of a person carrying a weight they hadn't agreed to lift. "You could kill me. You might even manage it before they answer. But I don't think you came all this way to kill me. I think you came because you don't know who else to ask."

The knife trembled. Only slightly. Only enough.

"The vault," Vespera said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated it—she hated it so visibly that Zephyrine almost reached up and pulled the blade away himself. "Under the Conservatory. Do you know what they keep there? I thought I knew. I thought—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, flat and careful, the way people speak when feeling itself has become a hazard. "I went in to retrieve a confiscated voice. Routine requisition. And there are *shelves*, carver. Rows and rows of glass vessels, each one singing to itself, each one a person. A farmer. A child. A woman who I think had been dead sixty years and whose voice was still *begging*. And I understood that every liturgy I have ever sung, every hymn of obedience that rolls off those hills and makes whole towns hum along without knowing why—that music is built out of stolen throats. We are an instrument, and the instrument is *people*."

The knife lowered. Not lowered away—lowered, as if her arm no longer believed in the mission her mind was still reciting.

"And the voice I was sent to fetch," she said. "It belongs to your friend."

Zephyrine stood very slowly. Her blade tracked him without conviction.

"Tam," he said.

"He doesn't sing anymore. They took it clean out of him at the bridge—a resonant extraction, one note, surgical. He's been in the holding cells since. They were going to shelve him." Her jaw tightened. "I signed the requisition form myself. That's how routine it was. That's how *nothing* it was. And then I saw the shelves, and I saw his name in my own handwriting, and I walked out of the Conservatory with a mute farm-boy under one arm and a stolen key-ring in my pocket, and I have not stopped shaking since."

She finally stepped back. The knife went into its sheath with a small, final sound, like a door closing on a house that had burned down.

"So," Vespera said. "Knives first. Truth after. That was the plan." She looked around the tiny bothy—at the shavings and half-finished carvings littering every surface, the small white shapes of bones waiting to become something. Her eyes caught on one piece in particular, a hollow flute-like length of sheep femur, and stayed there too long. "Truth after. If you'll have me."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded toward the fire.

"It's cold," he said. "Sit down, cantor. You're among the dead here anyway—they never leave. You may as well be warm."

* * *

They woke Tam gently, or tried to; he woke the way he always had, since childhood, all at once and already halfway to laughing at whatever joke the world had played on him. The laugh died when he saw Vespera. He came off his bedroll with his fists up, positioning himself between her and Zephyrine with a ferocity that would have meant more if his hands weren't shaking.

"TAM." Zephyrine caught his shoulder. "Tam. *Stop.*"

Tam pointed at Vespera. The gesture was eloquent, furious, complete. *Her. You brought HER.*

"She brought you back," Zephyrine said. "Say thank you."

Tam's hands moved again—faster now, the angry signing of a man who had spent a lifetime talking and been suddenly robbed of it, all his wit compressed into fingers that couldn't keep up. *She SANG at the bridge. I remember the note. I still hear it sometimes, in my teeth—*

"I know," Vespera said.

Both men turned. She had gone pale in the firelight.

"I know you hear it in your teeth. I sang the extraction at Greyford. It was my squad, my cordon, my—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, hard, as though pressing something back down into her chest where it belonged. "Your name is Tam. You're a cooper's son. You carve little boats into barrel-staves when you think no one's looking. You followed your friend into exile because you loved him more than you feared the road." Her voice went very quiet. "I read your file. All of it. Twice. Once to find you, and once because I couldn't stop."

Tam stared at her. Slowly, his hands lowered.

Then he held one out—not in friendship, not yet, but in the grudging grammar of men who have decided to postpone a fight. Vespera took it. Her grip was steady. His wasn't, but he held on anyway.

"Right," Zephyrine said, into the strange new silence. "Now that everyone's terrified of everyone, let's look at the map."

* * *

It unrolled across the floor between them, bloodstained corners stiff with frost, and the firelight made the inked mountains seem to breathe.

"Ashen Crypt," Vespera murmured, tracing the route with one finger. "Beneath the ruined abbey at Cael Adair's feet. Midwinter's in nine days. The coronation-feast is in ten—the crown-thing will want the whole court gathered to watch it renew its vows, which means the whole court will be in one building, which means—" she glanced up at him, "—you intend to walk into the most heavily warded room in the kingdom."

"I intend to walk into the throne room," Zephyrine said. "The crypt comes first. There's something beneath the abbey the map only shows when it wants to—I found the second script three nights ago. Have you ever seen binding-verse?"

"I've sung it. Never read it. Cantors learn by ear, by rote—we're not trusted with notation." Her mouth twisted. "Another thing I didn't understand until recently."

"Then look." He tilted the map toward the light, angling it so the grease of the tallow candle caught the faint dry lines beneath the painted routes—lines that resolved, slowly, like faces surfacing in water, into columns of tight angular script. "This is the original song. The first binding. Whatever sits on the throne wearing Aldric's crown, this is the recipe it was baked from. The courier died getting this to the crypt because the dead king can't be called back while the original verse exists anywhere but buried with him. And whoever holds it—" he met her eyes, "—could make another one. Could make a dozen."

Vespera was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—the cantor coming forward, reading the script not as words but as intervals, and going whiter with each bar.

"That third phrase," she whispered. "If you sang that over a crowd—" 

"You understand why the elders wanted me gone now," Zephyrine said. "And why the Choir wants me worse."

"Why do *you* want it?" she asked suddenly, sharply. "Be honest, carver. I've spent my whole life being lied to beautifully. I'd rather have ugly truth."

He considered lying to her. Considered how easy it would be, how she half-wanted to be lied to, wanted to confirm that everyone was corruptible, that her defection had merely traded one master for another.

"The honest answer," Zephyrine said slowly, "is that some nights I hold it and I think: with this, no one could ever take a voice again. No one could ever do to anyone what was done to Tam. I could burn the Choir's harmonies out of the air. I could make the valleys quiet forever." He laughed, short and without humor. "And then I remember that's exactly what the thing on the throne thought, once. Whoever wrote this verse started with a wound too. Started with *never again*. The road to the crown-thing is paved with never-agains."

Tam touched his arm. Zephyrine looked down. Tam's hands were moving, slow and deliberate:

*So we bury it. With him. After.*

"After," Zephyrine agreed.

Vespera studied them both—carver and cooper's son, exile and mute—and something in her shoulders finally came down from around her ears, where it had lived for years.

"All right," she said. "The crypt approach. The abbey ruins sit above the old processional way; the Choir holds the gates, but the mummers' guild still has tunnels under the feast-hall from the days when kings liked surprises at their coronations. My defection will have been noticed by now—they'll expect me to run south, over the border. So we go north, in plain sight, among the feast-crowds. Mummers' cloaks. I know the recognition signs." She paused. "There's a problem."

"There's always a problem."

"Him." She nodded at Tam, gently. "A mute man traveling with a known defector and the bone-carver. Mutes get reported. Mutes get *noticed*." She bit her lip. "Unless he isn't mute anymore."

The silence stretched. Tam looked between them, eyebrows climbing.

"Not the Choir's kind of voice," Zephyrine said, already reaching for the carved sheep femur on the shelf. "Mine. Or—the dead's. I told you my carvings can hold them. Hold voices. This one's been waiting for a singer since Bellharrow." He turned it in his fingers; the firelight slid along the fine grooves, the latticework of names cut so small they were more texture than writing. "It won't be his voice. Not really. It'll sound like wind through a reed-bed, like a hundred people agreeing at once. But it'll be *words*. His words, carried by theirs."

Tam's hands were very still.

*Will it hurt them?* he signed. *The ones inside.*

"No," Zephyrine said. "That's the point of the craft. They rest in it. Being carried is the closest thing to sleep some of them have had in years." He hesitated. "It might feel strange. Like speaking and being spoken through at the same time. Tam—I won't do it if you don't want it."

Tam looked at the bone for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled—that same crooked, undimmed smile he'd worn since childhood, since before the valley, since before any of it. He tapped his own throat twice, then pointed at the bone, then spread his hands wide: *What have I got to lose?*

"Everything," Zephyrine said quietly. "That's why it has to be given. Nothing taken."

*Given,* Tam repeated, and held out his hand for the bone.

* * *

Teaching him took until dawn.

The trick, Zephyrine explained, was breath—Tam had to give the air, the shaping, the intent, and let the dead supply the tone. Too much push and the chorus drowned him; too little and nothing came but a hollow hiss. Tam failed a dozen times, twenty, his face reddening with frustration he couldn't curse aloud, until Vespera—of all people—knelt beside him and said, "Stop trying to sing. You're not a singer. Just talk. Talk like you're complaining about the porridge. Let them handle the pretty part."

Tam glared at her. Then he took a breath, thought *complain about the porridge* with his whole body, and spoke.

"—*oats again,*" said a hundred soft voices in rough unison, sardonic, warm, unmistakably shaped by Tam's exasperated grin. "*By all the saints, oats AGAIN—*"

Vespera burst out laughing—a real laugh, startled out of her, high and rusty, as if the mechanism hadn't been oiled in years. Tam grinned like sunrise. And Zephyrine sat back on his heels and felt something loosen in his chest that had been clenched since the ford, since the courier died in his arms, since the valley burned its bridges behind him.

Three of them. One thief, one traitor, one ghost-talker. None of whom could go home. All of whom had been used by the same master, in one way or another—the Choir had sung at all of them, one way or another, had reached into all three lives and rearranged them without asking.

"Well," Vespera said, wiping her eyes, sobering. "It'll do. It'll pass, at ten paces, in the dark, with luck." She unrolled a scrap of parchment and began sketching the abbey's undercrofts from memory, quick sure strokes. "Here's the approach. We enter with the mummers two nights before the feast. The tunnels come up behind the reliquary. From there, down."

"Down," Zephyrine echoed. His eyes drifted to the map, to the hidden verse gleaming faintly beneath the paint, and he thought of the dead king waiting below the world, lucid and alone for ten years, held together by refusal. He thought of what Aldric would ask. He thought of what the answering would cost, and of the lie he was already, quietly, beginning to build.

Because he had known, since the firelight revelation of the second secret, since the long climb through the passes—had known in his marrow the way he knew the dead were near—that only his gift could hold two souls in one throat. That Vespera's trained voice would serve the unbinding better and she would offer it, and he would refuse. That Tam would write *I owe a debt* and mean it with his whole stubborn heart.

That someone had to lend their voice and forget everything, and he had already decided it would be him, and neither of them would know until it was too late to stop him.

"Carver?" Vespera was looking at him. "You went somewhere."

"Just counting days," Zephyrine said. "Nine left. Enough."

Outside, the snow fell without pause, filling the tracks of the living, erasing the past, laying down the deep white silence over which they would walk to the capital. In the firelight, the carved bone lay on the floorboards between the three of them, humming very softly to itself—a hundred small voices resting, patient, keeping Tam's borrowed words safe until morning.

And far to the north, beneath the frozen abbey, something that had refused death for ten years stirred in the ash, and listened, and hoped.
