## 16. The Ashen Crypt

The abbey had been dead longer than the king.

Zephyrine felt that truth settle over him as they picked their way through the ruins above the crypt's mouth—broken arches shouldering snow, a bell tower collapsed into its own throat, stained glass long since shattered so that the wind came through the empty frames singing in thin, tuneless whistles. Whatever god had been worshipped here had left no forwarding address. The stones remembered hymns the way bones remembered flesh: as an ache, a shape, a hollow where something warm had been.

He stopped at the edge of the collapsed nave and looked back at his companions. Vespera stood with her hood back, letting the snow gather in her dark hair, her face tilted toward the ruin as if listening for something beneath it. Tam knelt by a fallen pillar, tracing the old devotional carvings with one gloved finger—reading them, Zephyrine realized, the way Tam now read everything, with his whole body, since the Choir had taken his voice. His friend looked up and met his eyes, and lifted one shoulder in a shrug that said *well?*

*Well,* Zephyrine agreed silently, and turned toward the dark.

The crypt entrance was not hidden so much as *unwanted*. A stair of black stone descended beneath the altar platform, half-choked with ice, and every step down made the hair on Zephyrine's arms rise—not from cold, though the cold was a living thing down there, breathing against his skin—but because the silence changed. He had spent years inside Hollowmere's ward, wrapped in a quiet so total that even the dead forgot to whisper. This was nothing like that. This was the silence of a held breath. Of an audience waiting.

Of a door about to open.

"You feel it too," Vespera murmured behind him. Not a question. Her hand rested on the knife at her belt out of habit, not intent; she'd stopped reaching for her song-magic days ago, ever since she'd seen what the Choir did with what they harvested. "It's like standing backstage before a performance."

"You would say that," Zephyrine said.

"It's apt."

"It's unsettling is what it is." He gripped the bone-handled map tighter—the courier's map, bloodstained, patient, treacherous with secrets—and started down.

* * *

The stairs went further than any crypt had a right to go.

They counted as they descended, at first. By two hundred steps they had stopped counting, because the numbers had begun to feel obscene, and by three hundred the ice on the walls had given way to dry stone carved with words in Old Caelic that Zephyrine's fingers itched to translate but his courage refused to touch. Vespera read them aloud anyway, softly, her cantor's diction making even warnings beautiful.

"*We buried him deep so the sky could not hear him.*"

"*We buried him singing, so he would not be lonely.*"

"*Forgive us. Forgive us. Forgive us.*"

"The last one repeats," she said quietly. "For nine lines."

"Stonemasons don't repeat themselves for decoration," Zephyrine said. "They repeat themselves when they're afraid."

The passage opened at last into a chamber that stole the breath from all three of them.

It was round, vast, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its floor a mosaic of white and grey stone arranged in spirals—concentric rings radiating outward from a central bier of black marble. Hundreds of candles lined the walls, and here was the first impossibility: many of them were burned down to puddles of wax, drowned wicks floating in their own tallow, and yet others still burned with steady, unwavering flames. Ten years of unattended fire, and some of these flames had never gone out. They burned without consuming, little tongues of gold standing sentinel, and Zephyrine understood with a lurch that they were not fed by wax at all.

They were fed by attention. Someone—or something—down here had been watching itself burn for a decade, and would not permit the light to die.

And on the bier, in the center of the rings, lay the King of Cael Adair.

Aldric had not mummified and had not rotted. That was the second impossibility, and the worse one. He lay as a man lies who has merely closed his eyes—a big man, broad through the chest, with a beard carved close and hands folded over a sword that had rusted to red lace across his breastplate. His skin was grey as the mosaic, but intact. Composed. There was even, Zephyrine thought wildly, a faint crease between the dead brows, the kind a man gets from frowning at bad news, or from thinking very hard for a very long time.

Ten years dead. Ten years of thinking very hard.

"He knows we're here," Zephyrine whispered. It wasn't intuition. It was his gift, waking in this place like a man rising from a sickbed—the valley's ward had muted him for years, but here, deep under the ruined abbey, in the oldest silence in the kingdom, he could hear everything. And what he heard was not the murmur of restless dead, not the moaning chorus of the Weeping Moors or the betrayed howling through his carved bones. What he heard was *focus*. A single consciousness, vast and slow as a glacier, turning its attention upon him the way winter turns upon the last green thing in a field.

"Your Majesty," Vespera began, and dropped to one knee, and then caught herself, confused, because what does one kneel to—a corpse?

The corpse answered.

Not with sound. With pressure. The candle flames all leaned inward at once, toward the bier, and the temperature dropped until Zephyrine's next breath crystallized in his nose, and in his skull a voice assembled itself from nothing—from the creak of stone, from the hush of flame, from the memory of a thousand court proclamations soaked into the walls:

***WHO COMES TO THE ASHEN CRYPT?***

Zephyrine's knees nearly went. He heard Vespera gasp; heard Tam stumble forward to catch her elbow. But Zephyrine had spent his life hearing dead voices, and this one, though it dwarfed them all—though it was less a voice than a weather system of grief and will—he could answer.

"A bone-carver," he said, and his voice cracked in the cold air. "From Hollowmere. I carried your map."

Silence. The candles righted themselves slowly, like soldiers recovering formation.

Then, almost gently—so gently it was somehow more terrible than the thunder had been:

***THE COURIER. LITTLE MAETH. DID HE SUFFER?***

Zephyrine swallowed. "He froze at the ford. Before the Choir found him. He died free."

***FREE.*** The word resonated through the stone, through Zephyrine's teeth. ***TEN YEARS I HAVE HEARD THE CHOIR'S NET CLOSE OVER THIS KINGDOM, AND EVERY VOICE IT TAKES IS A DOOR I HEAR SHUT. YOU BRING WORD THAT ONE DOOR STAYED OPEN.***

"He brought more than word," Zephyrine managed. He knelt—not for protocol now, but because his legs would not hold him—and laid the map on the mosaic floor before the bier, unrolling it with numb fingers. "He told me the throne is occupied. By something wearing your crown. He told me you could be called back to unseat it. He told me—" and here his voice failed, because the next part was the part he had climbed mountains not to say, "—he told me someone has to lend you their voice."

* * *

Aldric told them the rest.

He did not speak quickly. The dead do not; speech for him seemed to cost effort, each word quarried out of ten years of silence, and so he spoke in long, weighted intervals while the three living people huddled near the bier and the impossible candles burned. Zephyrine listened with his gift wide open, and what he learned made his craft—the gentle craft of carving names into memorial bone, of giving the dying somewhere to put their last words—seem like a child's game of pebbles at the edge of an ocean.

The thing on the throne had been born of a song. The original binding-song, composed by Aldric's own court mage in the last desperate year of the Border Wars—a song meant to bind a dying king's spirit to his crown so that his wisdom might guide his heir. It had never been sung for Aldric. It had been sung *at* the crown itself, and the crown, being older and hungrier than anyone had guessed, had learned the song, worn the song, become the song. It had waited through Aldric's reign like a spider in a rafter. When he died—when he was killed, Aldric corrected himself, in a voice like grinding millstones, poisoned at his own victory feast—it had taken the throne and begun doing what crowns do when no hand holds them: it reached for power. And the only power it knew was binding. So it built the Ember Choir, and taught men and women to sing souls into service, and it ate, and ate, and ate.

"I know," Vespera whispered, when Aldric described the harvest-vaults. Her face was white. "I sang for it. Gods help me, I thought—I was told—we were *binding traitors*. Giving order to chaos." She pressed her fist to her mouth. "I gave it voices."

***YOU GAVE IT VOICES,*** Aldric agreed, without cruelty. ***AND NOW YOU BRING ME THE MAN WHO CAN UNMAKE THE SONG. THE BONE-CARVER WHOSE CARVINGS HOLD THE DEAD. YES. I HAVE BEEN LISTENING TO YOU SINCE THE MOORS, ZEPHYRINE OF HOLLOWMERE. YOU PROMISED THREE HUNDRED DEAD REST AND COULD NOT GIVE IT. I FELT THAT PROMISE BREAK FROM FORTY LEAGUES AWAY. IT WOKE ME FULLY.***

"I'm sorry," Zephyrine said, and meant it, and hated that he meant it, because what kind of apology was that to a dead king—that he had broken a promise to ghosts in order to survive?

***DO NOT BE SORRY FOR SURVIVING. BE PRECISE INSTEAD.*** The pressure gathered, concentrated, became something almost like a man leaning close. ***YOU WANT TO KNOW THE PRICE. THE COURIER TOLD YOU A VOICE MUST BE LENT. LET ME TELL YOU WHAT LENDING MEANS, SO THAT NO ONE STANDS IN MY THRONE ROOM AND LEARNS IT TOO LATE.***

The candles guttered. In the sudden deeper dark, Zephyrine saw—or dreamed he saw—the dead king's eyelids tighten, the frown-line deepen.

***A SPIRIT CANNOT SPEAK. I HAVE HAD TEN YEARS TO CURSE THIS FACT. I NEED A THROAT. A LIVING THROAT, OFFERED WILLINGLY—THAT PART CANNOT BE FORCED, THE SONG WILL NOT ACCEPT A COERCED GIFT, WHICH IS THE ONE MERCY ITS MAKER BUILT INTO IT. BUT A VOICE IS NOT AIR AND SPIT. A VOICE IS EVERYTHING THE SPEAKER IS. YOUR NAME. YOUR MOTHER'S KITCHEN. THE SMELL OF THE VALLEY IN SPRING. EVERY MEMORY THAT EVER SHAPED THE WORDS YOU SAY. WHEN I SING THROUGH YOU, THE SONG MUST PASS THROUGH ALL OF IT—AND THE SONG DOES NOT PASS. IT CONSUMES.***

"How much?" Vespera asked. Her cantor's training made her ask it clinically, and her shaking hands betrayed what the asking cost. "How much is consumed?"

***ALL.***

The word fell into the chamber like a stone into a well.

***THE LENDER GIVES THEIR VOICE AND THEIR SELF TOGETHER. THEY CANNOT BE SEPARATED; I HAVE SEARCHED THE SONG'S STRUCTURE FOR TEN YEARS LOOKING FOR A SEAM, AND THERE IS NONE. THE MAN OR WOMAN WHO STANDS BEFORE THE THRONE AND OPENS THEIR THROAT TO ME WILL SING THE UNBINDING AND THEN—they will forget. Everything. Their name first, because the name is the door. Then their mother, their home, everyone they have loved. They will stand in the wreckage of the throne room with a stranger's triumph in their lungs and no idea whose lungs they are.***

Tam moved. Zephyrine felt it before he saw it—his friend stepping forward, jaw set, reaching for the slate he carried, and Zephyrine grabbed his arm hard enough to bruise. *Don't*, he mouthed. *Don't you dare.*

Tam shook him off and wrote, fast, angry: **You saved my life. I owe a voice for a voice.**

**You owe me nothing,** Zephyrine wrote back, pressing so hard the slate squeaked. **You owe yourself your own name.**

***IT IS WELL THAT YOU ARGUE,*** Aldric rumbled, and there was something in it that might, in a living man, have been grim humor. ***ARGUE LONG. WEIGH IT AS I HAVE WEIGHED IT FOR TEN YEARS, ALONE, WITH NOTHING BUT MY OWN REFUSAL TO HOLD ME TOGETHER. UNDERSTAND WHAT I AM ASKING. I AM ASKING SOMEONE TO BE UNMADE SO THAT A KINGDOM CAN BE REMADE. I HAVE NO RIGHT. I ONLY HAVE THE NEED, AND THE TRUTH, AND THE MAP THAT LED YOU HERE.***

"Why you?" Vespera asked Zephyrine quietly, and it was not accusation; it was worse. It was the question of someone who already suspected the answer. "Why does it have to be him?"

But Zephyrine barely heard her. He was looking at the dead king—at the folded hands, at the rusted sword, at the frown-line carved by a decade of solitary vigilance—and he was hearing, beneath Aldric's words, the thing the king had not said. That Aldric himself had searched for another way for ten years. That the refusal holding the corpse together was not just refusal to fade. It was refusal to let the crown-thing win *cheaply*—refusal to accept a kingdom bought with a soul unless the soul's owner chose it freely, eyes open, knowing the whole price.

Awe rose in Zephyrine like floodwater. And beneath the awe, cold and rising faster, terror—not of death, he realized, surprising himself; he had walked among the dead so long that death felt like a neighboring room—but of *erasure*. Of becoming the hollow thing he had spent his whole life carving memorials against. Every bone he had ever engraved bore a name, because a name was the last thing the bereaved could hold. And he was being asked to consider surrendering his.

His mother's kitchen. The smell of the valley in spring. Tam's laugh. The weight of the carving knife in his hand. All of it—consumed, passed through, spent like fuel to burn away a usurper.

***TAKE WHAT TIME YOU NEED,*** Aldric said, and his voice gentled to something almost human. ***THE CROWN-THING FEASTS AT MIDSUMMER'S CORONATION-RENEWAL, BUT IT GROWS STRONGER EVERY DAY. CHOOSE, AND CHOOSE WHOLE. I WOULD RATHER DIE A SECOND, FINAL TIME THAN TAKE AN UNWILLING VOICE.***

They climbed back toward the grey daylight in silence. At the top of the stairs, Tam caught Zephyrine's sleeve and held up his slate:

**Promise me you won't decide alone.**

And Zephyrine—who had already, in some locked room behind his ribs, begun to decide—looked at the friend whose name he was already mourning, and lied.

"I promise," he said.
