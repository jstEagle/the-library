## 17. Who Gives Their Voice

The bothy had become a kind of church.

Not deliberately. It had simply gathered to itself the gravity of their waiting—the storm outside sealing them in like a reliquary, the fire burned down to patient orange coals, the three of them arranged around it the way mourners arrange themselves around a coffin that has not yet been filled. Tam sat closest to the warmth, his blanket drawn over his shoulders, a slate balanced on his knees and a stub of chalk in his fingers. Vespera stood at the narrow window, watching snow erase the world in long grey sheets. And Zephyrine sat apart, as he always did, his knife moving over a scrap of sheep bone with the slow devotion of a man praying in a language only he could read.

They had been arguing about the voice for two days.

It began, as most of their arguments did, with Vespera refusing to let silence stand where a decision should be. She turned from the window, and the firelight caught the pale scar at her throat—the mark every Choir cantor wore, cut into the skin at initiation so the binding-song would seat itself properly. She touched it without seeming to know she was doing it. A habit, Zephyrine thought, like his own habit of reaching for bones in his pocket.

"Say it plainly," she said. "One of us lends Aldric our voice, and whoever does is unmade. Not killed—worse than killed. Everything they were is spent paying for the song." Her voice was steady, cantor-steady, trained against wind and distance and the acoustics of vast stone halls. "So we choose. Deliberately. Like adults. Not by who falls asleep last or who loses at cards."

Tam's chalk scratched. He held up the slate:

*Me.*

"No," Zephyrine said, before the word was even fully formed in his own mouth.

Tam wiped the slate and wrote again, pressing harder, the chalk squeaking: *I have no voice to lose. They took it at Greyford. What's left for me to give? I owe you my life twice over. Let me pay something back while I'm still able to pay anything.*

"You'd be giving everything else," Vespera said quietly. "Your name. Your mother's face. The reason you followed him up the mountain in the first place." She nodded toward Zephyrine without looking at him. "The dead king said it himself. The lender forgets being loved. That's what you'd be trading, Tam. Not your throat. Your *why*."

Tam looked down at the slate for a long moment. When he wrote again, his letters were smaller.

*Then maybe I don't get to keep my why. Maybe that's what owing means.*

Zephyrine set down his knife. The half-carved bone lay in his palm—a wren, unfinished, wings folded as if it had decided against flight. He had carved a hundred birds like it in Hollowmere, one for each dying soul that asked him for something small and lasting. He found now that he could not remember which grave this one had been meant for. The thought slid through him like cold water.

"Nobody's choosing tonight," he said. "Aldric waited ten years in the dark. He can wait until we're past the walls."

"That's cowardice dressed as caution," Vespera said, but gently, because she knew it too—that any delay was a luxury they might not be able to buy once they were inside Cael Adair.

"It's arithmetic," Zephyrine answered. "And the numbers aren't finished."

* * *

Vespera took the middle watch, and used it, as she used all her solitude, to argue with herself.

She knelt by the fire and ran her scales under her breath—not the Choir's liturgy, never that, but the old exercises from her first years of training, when singing had still felt like a gift instead of a leash. *Do. Re. Mi.* Her voice barely stirred the air. Even so, she felt the power in it coiling, obedient, precise. Ten years of discipline lived in her throat. She could hold a note through a burning building. She could sing a grown man to his knees or sing a wound closed. If the unbinding-song required strength, hers was the strongest instrument among them. That was simply true, the way it was true that steel cuts better than wood.

*My voice would serve best,* she had told them, and meant it, and had watched Zephyrine flinch as if she'd offered to walk into the fire herself.

Because he would do it, she realized. He was already doing it, somewhere behind those grey eyes, arranging his surrender the way he arranged his carvings—with patience, with craft, with that terrible quiet competence that had gotten them across half a kingdom. He would decide alone. Men like Zephyrine always decided alone; it was the same instinct that made them carry other people's burdens and call the carrying humility.

Well. She had been made into an instrument once before. She knew exactly how much of yourself the Choir left when it finished using you, and it was more than nothing—she was living proof. But Aldric's bargain was not the Choir's binding. The Choir took your obedience and left your shape. This would take the shape itself.

She looked at Tam sleeping by the fire, chalk dust still on his fingers, and at Zephyrine in the corner with his knife and his bones, guarding everyone including himself, and she made a vow of her own, silent, in the key of the old exercises: *If he tries to spend himself in secret, I will hear it. I was trained to hear. I will hear the lie in his voice before he finishes telling it.*

* * *

In the morning the storm broke, briefly, into a hard blue glare that made the snowfields blaze. They used the light to plan.

Vespera spread the map on the slate table and weighted its corners with stones. The hidden script still glimmered beneath the ink when firelight struck it at the right angle—the original binding-song, the seed from which the crown-thing had grown. They had agreed it must be buried in Hollowmere, in the deep ward-ground where magic went to sleep. But first it had to survive the journey to the throne room, because the binding-song was also the key: the usurper had woven itself out of that melody, and only the melody could unravel it.

"The coronation-feast is in nine days," Vespera said, tracing the road east. "Every noble house in Cael Adair will be inside the walls, drunk on the thing's borrowed majesty. The gates will open for mummers, jugglers, anyone with a drum and a painted face. We go in as entertainers. We go up through the servants' stair to the old chapel gallery, and from there—" her finger stopped above the crude ink drawing of the palace, "—the throne room has one door and a thousand ears. Whatever happens, happens fast."

*I'll break the resonance-glass,* Tam wrote, holding the slate up. In the days since Vespera had told them about the vaults—the great panes of singing-glass through which the Choir harvested and stored souls—he had become grimly expert on the subject. *I've seen how it's mounted. One good strike and the whole choir-loft comes down. That will blind them.*

"And I countersing the liturgy from the gallery," Vespera finished. "Which leaves the throne, and the king, and the price." She looked at Zephyrine. Both of them did. The silence stretched, taut as a wire.

"Tell us again," Vespera said, "exactly what Aldric told you. About whose voice will serve."

Zephyrine kept his eyes on the map. He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it in the dark hours of the night watch while the others slept, turning the lie over and over until it sat smooth in his mouth, worn round as river stone.

"He said the lender's voice becomes the vessel," he said. "Two souls, one throat. His spirit and the singer's breath, braided together long enough to sing the unbinding through." He paused, letting the truth do its work before he laid the falsehood gently on top of it. "He said the vessel must be able to hold the braid. Most voices tear. His words." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "A trained voice holds notes beautifully. But training teaches control, and control fights the braid. The vessel has to *surrender*, he said. Completely. That's rarer than skill."

Vespera frowned. "You're saying my ten years of discipline are a liability."

"I'm saying Aldric chose his words carefully, and I'm repeating them faithfully." Which was true. Every word of it was true. Only the conclusion he wanted them to draw was his own.

Tam wrote slowly: *What about me? My voice is gone. Could the braid grow in empty ground?*

"Maybe," Zephyrine said, and hated how much hope he let into the word. "But there's no voice to lend, Tam. That's the whole bargain—a living voice, willingly given. Yours was taken, not given. Stolen ground isn't the same as fallow ground." He met Tam's eyes. "If we win, we'll buy your voice back from their vaults. All three of us walk away. That's the plan."

*All four,* Tam corrected, tapping the slate. *Count the dead king.*

Zephyrine smiled, and the smile cost him more than anything he had ever carved. "All four," he agreed.

* * *

That night he went out into the blue snow-light to speak with the dead, because the living had heard enough of his lies for one day.

The bothy's door groaned with cold. Beyond it, the world lay white and absolute, the stars sharp as nailheads, and in that vast silence Zephyrine could feel them gathering—the moor-dead who had followed him since Greyford, keeping their distance, keeping their hope. He had broken his promise to them at the bridge. He had torn them loose from their rest and thrown them at the Choir like stones, and still they came after him, still they pressed close whenever he listened, because a man who could hear the dead was the nearest thing to mercy they would ever touch.

"I need to ask you something," he said aloud, his breath smoking. "And I need you to tell me the truth, because no one alive will."

The cold thickened around him. Not menacing—attentive. He had learned to read their attention the way farmers read weather.

"Aldric says the vessel must hold two souls in one throat. That only someone who can listen and speak at once can manage it." He wrapped his arms around himself. "Is that true? Or did I make it up so I'd have a reason?"

The answer came not as words but as weight, as pressure, as the unmistakable sensation of many hands settling on his shoulders—not holding him back, but steadying him. Yes, they said, in the way of the dead, who had no talent for kindness and therefore none for lies. Yes, and yes, and yes.

Only you.

Zephyrine bowed his head. Somewhere in the drifts a fox screamed, thin and mortal, and he envied it with his whole heart.

He thought about his mother then, deliberately, the way you press on a bruise to prove you can still feel it. Her hands dusted with bone-meal. Her voice calling him in from the ford at dusk, *Zeph, Zeph, the water's rising*. He held the memory up against the dark and examined it like a carving, learning its grain, its flaws, the places where love had worn it smooth. Nine days. Perhaps fewer. Then it would be gone, and worse—it would be as if it had never been, because forgetting is not loss, it is erasure, the page returned to blank.

But the crown-thing would be unraveled. Tam would sing again. Vespera would be free of the scar at her throat. Children in Bellharrow would hum songs that belonged to no choir. And some bone-carver, someday, in some quiet valley, would make small birds for dying people, whether or not he remembered making them.

"There's one more thing," he told the dead. "When it's done—when I'm hollow—if someone speaks my name to me, will it mean anything? Can a name rebuild a man?"

The dead were quiet a long time. Then, faintly, from very far off—from the direction of Hollowmere, he could have sworn—he heard his mother's voice, or the memory of it, or the dead's merciful imitation of it:

*Zeph. The water's rising. Come home.*

He walked back inside and slept, for the first time in weeks, deeply and without dreams.

* * *

On the third morning, Vespera caught him packing.

Not fleeing—packing was too orderly for flight. He was wrapping his carving knives in oiled cloth, sorting his bones into pouches, checking the map's oilskin twice. Preparation. The methodical ritual of a man getting ready to leave, and she understood in one cold flash that he intended to go ahead of them. To reach the capital early. To make his arrangements alone.

"When?" she said.

Zephyrine didn't turn around. "When what?"

"When you're going to do it." She crossed the room and put herself between him and the door, arms folded, cantor-still. "Don't insult me. I've spent my life listening to men lie with their whole bodies. You lied to us yesterday—you lied *beautifully*, I almost believed you—but you've been saying goodbye to your own name for days. I've watched you do it. So. When?"

For a heartbeat something raw moved behind his eyes—grief, fury, gratitude, all tangled—and she saw him weigh the option of telling her the truth. She saw him decide against it.

"At the crypt," he said mildly. "As planned. We descend together, Aldric explains the terms, we draw lots or argue it out like adults. Isn't that what you wanted? Deliberate choice?" He shouldered his pack and smiled at her, and it was the worst thing she had ever seen him do, because it was warm, and because it was false all the way through. "Nine days, Vespera. Keep counting them for me."

He stepped past her and out into the snow.

Vespera stood in the doorway a long moment, watching his figure dwindle along the white road toward the pass, growing smaller and more determined with every step, a man walking briskly toward his own unmaking with a bag of knives and bones on his back. Behind her, Tam's chalk squeaked against the slate—*Where is he going?*—and she realized her hands were shaking.

Then she turned, snatched up her own pack, and shouted down the road, in a voice trained to carry across cathedrals, trained to crack stone and stop hearts:

"Zephyrine Dunmore! You don't get to spend yourself alone!"

He didn't stop walking. But his shoulders flinched, just once, at the sound of his own name—and Vespera, running, knew that she had heard it too: beneath the lie, beneath the smile, a single thread of the true answer, sung low and unwilling from the deepest part of him.

Three days. He meant to leave in three days.
