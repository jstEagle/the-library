## 18. The Long Climb to Cael Adair

The capital announced itself first by smell.

They had been three days in the frozen outskirts—abandoned farmsteads with their doors left open, as if the owners had stepped out mid-chore and simply never returned—and on the third evening the wind shifted, carrying up from the valley below a scent that had no business existing in a kingdom gripped by the long winter. Roasting meat. Spiced wine. Woodsmoke from ten thousand hearths burned generous rather than desperate.

"Coronation-feast," Vespera said, crouched beside the dead fire in an abandoned shepherd's hut, her breath ghosting silver. "The crown-thing will want the city fed and drunk and singing. Nothing binds a people like a festival."

Tam sat against the wall with his carved bone at his throat—the one Zephyrine had shaped in the snowbound weeks after Greyford Bridge, the one that carried a sliver of Tam's stolen voice back to him, thin and reedy, enough to whisper but not enough to sing. He tapped it twice with his fingernail: *yes*. Then he held up three fingers, folded one down, and pointed at himself.

"He says three of us walking in won't be noticed," Zephyrine translated, "if we're wearing the right coats." He almost smiled. Three weeks of learning Tam's language of hands and taps, and he could hear the dry humor underneath it now, unmistakable as a heartbeat. "You've gotten cheeky since you lost your voice. I think you enjoy making me do the talking."

Tam's grin was quick and crooked in the firelight, and for a moment Zephyrine let himself simply look at his friend—the frost-burned cheeks, the scarred throat where the singing-wound had closed like a seam, the eyes that had followed him out of Hollowmere and across half a kingdom without ever once wavering—and let himself grieve him in advance.

That was what the climb had become, these last days. Not a journey toward the throne room but a long procession of small farewells, each one taken quietly so the others wouldn't see. He said goodbye to things in odd moments, between steps, while chewing, while lacing his boots. *This is the last time I'll remember how Tam takes his tea. This is the last time I'll remember the smell of bone-dust. This is the last time I'll remember my mother's voice saying my name.* He had learned to do it the way a man learns to hold his breath underwater—one count at a time, no panic, just the slow deliberate emptying of his lungs.

He did not know if practicing made any difference. Aldric had not been able to tell him. The dead king's instructions had been precise about everything except this: *the forgetting comes when the song begins, and you will not feel it happen. You will simply be less, and you will keep singing anyway.*

So Zephyrine practiced being less. It seemed only courteous, somehow—to introduce himself to the emptiness before it arrived.

* * *

They went down toward the city walls at dusk, moving through fields where the snow lay blue as old bruises. Cael Adair rose ahead of them out of the white plain like something grown rather than built—a mass of towers and spires furred with ice, every window burning gold, the whole vast shape of it lit from within like a lantern made of a city. Above the highest tower, above the palace where the feast was already roaring, hung a light that was not light. Zephyrine felt it before he saw it: a pressure behind his eyes, a wrongness in the air, the way the dead felt to him when they were angry.

"The resonance-glass," Vespera murmured, following his gaze. "You can see it from here."

"I can *feel* it from here." He flexed his fingers inside his mittens. Since the moors, since the ambush, since he had learned what his carvings could hold, his gift had stopped feeling like hearing and started feeling like weather. The whole capital was full of voices—he could sense them even through the wax plugs in his ears, even through the carved silence-beads Vespera had taught him to wear at his temples. Thousands of souls packed together, most of them humming the Choir's liturgy without knowing why, all of them resonating faintly toward that glass spire like iron filings toward a magnet.

"And the mummers?" Tam wrote on his slate, then turned it.

Vespera had arranged it through the last loyal thread of her old life—a costumer in the Tanners' Row who owed her more than she liked to admit. The bundle waiting in the hollow tree at the crossroads contained everything: three sets of motley, patched and sequined and reeking of greasepaint, and painted masks on sticks, and a letter of passage stamped with a mummers' guild seal that would get them through the feast-gates with the other troupes summoned to entertain the court.

"They always invite performers to a coronation," Vespera had explained. "A hundred troupes. Nobody counts faces under the paint. And nobody looks twice at mummers—they're furniture that moves."

Now, standing at the crossroads with the bundle open at their feet, Zephyrine lifted one of the masks and looked at its grinning painted face for a long moment. A fool's face. Jester-red mouth, diamond tears under the eyes.

"Fitting," he said quietly.

"What is?" Vespera asked.

"Nothing. Help me with this coat."

* * *

The gates of Cael Adair were open, because a city celebrating does not guard itself well. They joined the river of revelers on the processional road—farmers come in from the outlying holds, merchants with carts of feast-wine, whole families wrapped in every bright scrap of cloth they owned—and slipped among them wearing their motley, and no one gave them a second glance. That was the terrible thing, Zephyrine thought. How easy it was. A kingdom could be stolen right out from under its people, and the people would still line the streets and cheer the thief's festival, because the wine was free and the music was everywhere and obedience, when it came soaked in sugar, tasted like joy.

Guards stood along the route in their black-and-crimson, Ember Choir colors, and interspersed among them—worse—cantors. Zephyrine counted seven before they reached the gate arch. Each one wore the Choir's grey robes beneath a soldier's cloak, and each one stood with lips slightly parted, eyes unfocused, listening to the crowd the way a fisherman watches water.

Zephyrine kept his head down, kept his silence-beads pressed to his temples, kept Tam's arm looped through his like they were brothers in a traveling troupe, which was even true in a way that hurt. At the arch itself a cantor stood close enough that Zephyrine could see the fine tremor in her jaw—the constant motion of someone singing under their breath, scanning.

The cantor's gaze slid over them. Paused. Came back.

Zephyrine's heart went very still and very loud.

Then Tam, bless him, bless him forever, chose that moment to break character entirely and trip spectacularly over his own enormous clown shoes, crashing into the cantor with a squeak of greasepaint and a shower of sequins, flailing, apologizing in frantic silent gestures, holding up his slate with *SO SORRY SO SORRY* scrawled across it, and the cantor recoiled from the contact with the reflexive disgust of the trained for the untrained, waved them through with two sharp fingers, and was already turning to scan the next hundred faces.

"Mummers," Vespera breathed once they were through, steering them into a shadowed lane off the processional road. "Gods. Never again."

Tam was shaking—not with fear, Zephyrine realized, but laughter. Silent laughter, shoulders heaving, tears freezing at the corners of his eyes, and Zephyrine caught it too, the wild giddy relief of the nearly-caught, and for one whole minute the three of them stood in a stinking alley in fool's coats and laughed until it turned into something else that none of them named.

Because around the corner, up the hill, the palace blazed. And tomorrow night—tonight, actually; the feast ran through midnight and the coronation-oath came at dawn—was all the time they had.

* * *

They found a place to wait in the mummers' encampment in the outer yard, a sprawl of painted wagons and canvas shelters where a dozen troupes warmed up, argued, drank, and rehearsed bits for the court. No one questioned three more fools. One troupe-master with a beard dyed green looked at their letter, grunted, and told them they were slotted after the acrobats and before the fire-breathers, "so don't be clever, the King's in a mood tonight."

*The King*, Zephyrine thought. *Which king? The one on the chair, or the one in the crypt whose bones I'm carrying in my pack?*

Because he was carrying them. Had been since the Ashen Crypt. A small oilcloth bundle against his spine, holding a skull and two fingerbones and a scrap of shroud, and the whole time they'd climbed and walked and hidden, Aldric had been there—not speaking, the dead rarely spoke unless spoken to, but *present*, a cool weight at the edge of Zephyrine's gift like moonlight at the edge of a window. Tonight, though, as the feast-noise swelled over the walls and the resonance-glass above the palace began to pulse visibly, like a heartbeat made of aurora, the weight stirred.

*Carver.*

Zephyrine excused himself from the wagon where Tam was mending a costume seam and Vespera was going over the palace servant-stairs map for the tenth time, and walked out beyond the firelight, into the shadow of the encampment wall, and answered aloud in a whisper only the dead needed to hear.

"I'm here."

*You have been saying goodbye to yourself,* Aldric said. It was not a question. Dead kings notice things. *All week. I have watched you do it, and I would ask you to stop.*

Zephyrine leaned against the cold stone. Above him the glass pulsed. "Why?"

*Because it is wasted effort, and worse than wasted—it teaches you to let go. You must not let go, carver. Not of anything. The song requires a voice given freely, yes—but a voice is not just sound. It is everything a person has ever been. If you arrive at that throne room already hollowed, already halfway surrendered, there will be nothing left to anchor Aldric-the-king against the thing we are trying to unmake. Your memories are not the price of the song. They are the fuel of it.*

"That's not what you told us in the crypt."

*I told you what you needed to hear to make the choice,* the dead king said, and there was something in his voice that Zephyrine had never heard from the dead before and recognized, sickeningly, as shame. *I am sorry. But hear me now, truly: the forgetting is not a door you walk through. It is a storm you stand inside. And men who have spent weeks practicing surrender drown in storms. Men who spend those weeks holding on—remembering harder, loving harder, gripping every name and face like rope—those are the ones who come back.*

Zephyrine's throat was tight. "Tam spoke your name back to you," he said. "At the crypt. When you were starting to fray. That's how you stayed lucid."

*Tam,* Aldric said, with unmistakable warmth. *Your friend has a king's courage and a fool's timing. Keep him close. And carver—if you remember nothing else when the song has taken the rest, try to remember that you were loved. It is the last memory to leave and the first to return. I know this from the inside.*

Zephyrine stood in the dark a long time after the presence faded, looking up at the pulsing glass, and slowly, deliberately, he took back every goodbye.

*My name is Zephyrine Dunmore.* He said it in his head like a man driving a stake into frozen ground. *I carve bones. My mother sang off-key and didn't care. Tam fell in the ford when we were nine and blamed a fish. Vespera hums when she's frightened and denies it. The valley smelled of cut bone and pine smoke and I hated leaving and I would leave again, a thousand times, if it ended here.*

He was not letting go. Whatever came at dawn, he would walk into it full.

* * *

Near midnight, they went over the wall.

Not the great gate—Vespera had been explicit about that; the feast-gate would be sealed once the oath-hour approached, and the Choir would sweep every guest. Instead they climbed the servants' stair on the palace's north face, the one marked on the map in faded ink, up ice-slick steps with the wind clawing at their motley, Tam hauling himself along on the rope with his mute strength, Vespera moving like she'd been born on walls, Zephyrine with Aldric's bones strapped tight against his spine and his heart hammering so hard he was sure the whole city must hear it.

From the height, the throne room's windows glowed below them like the open door of a furnace. And through the stone, through his wax-plugged ears, through everything, Zephyrine could feel the crown-thing—not a voice, not yet, but a *hunger*, vast and patient, feeding on the joy of ten thousand feasting subjects the way a spider drinks from a wrapped fly.

It knew they were coming. He was almost certain it knew. Things that eat souls develop an appetite for the particular.

They gathered on a landing in the wind's roar, and Vespera checked the resonance-glass countermeasure one last time—the shard of true glass, uncut, that Tam would carry to shatter at her signal. Tam patted his slate, patted the bone at his throat, and then, unexpectedly, reached out and gripped Zephyrine's forearm hard, and held his eyes, and tapped the bone twice.

*Yes,* the tap meant. But his face meant something larger. His face meant: *whatever happens in there, whatever you forget, I will be the one who remembers it for you. That is my job now. I accept it.*

Zephyrine couldn't speak past the knot in his throat. He nodded instead, and hoped it was enough, and knew it wasn't, and knew it also didn't matter, because some things don't need words, which was fortunate, since Tam had none.

"One hour to the oath," Vespera said. "When the bells ring midnight, the whole court kneels. That's our moment—everyone's eyes down, everyone's guard changing. We go in during the kneeling." She looked at them both, and for once the ex-cantor's composure cracked, just slightly, showing the young woman underneath who had defected rather than harvest one more soul. "Whatever's in there—we end it tonight. For everyone the Choir ever sang silent."

"For everyone," Zephyrine agreed.

And beneath his breath, so quietly that even the wind barely caught it, he said his own name one more time—*Zephyrine Dunmore, Zephyrine Dunmore, Zephyrine Dunmore*—not as a farewell this time, but as a flag planted. A claim staked. *Come and take it, then. Come and try.*

The bells began to ring midnight.

Below them, through the glowing windows, they saw the glow dim as ten thousand candles were shielded for the kneeling, and the throne room opened its doors to receive its court, and somewhere in the dark beneath the gold, the thing that wore a king's face turned its attention upward, toward the north wall, toward the three small figures in the wind—

—and smiled.

Zephyrine felt it like a knife laid flat against his soul.

"Now," whispered Vespera, and they went down.
