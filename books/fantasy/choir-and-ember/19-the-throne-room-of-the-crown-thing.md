## 19. The Throne Room of the Crown-Thing

The doors of the great hall were taller than any tree Zephyrine had ever carved, and they were singing.

Not the wood itself—oak sang only when it burned—but something threaded through the grain, a low harmonic that hummed up through the floor and into his teeth. He felt it in the bones of his hands, the way he had once felt the dead in Hollowmere before the ward swallowed even that. The mummers' cart had left them at the servants' gate an hour past midnight; now they stood beneath the archway where the feast-light spilled gold across marble, and the whole palace was one held note.

*The resonance-glass,* Vespera breathed, barely sound at all. Her eyes moved to the vaulted ceiling, where between every rib of stone hung panes of something that was not glass—not quite. They caught the torchlight and bent it wrong, and from each pane dripped that same sustained tone, faint as breath on a winter window. *Every pane holds a voice. Hundreds of them. Thousands.* Her knuckles went white around her cantor's staff, the one she'd carried for nine years like a second spine. *That's what happens to the ones we take. I thought—I was told they went to rest.*

*They go to the rafters,* Zephyrine said.

Tam touched his sleeve. He could not speak—had not spoken since Greyford Bridge—but his hand said enough: *I'm here. Keep walking.*

They walked.

* * *

The throne room was built to make a person small, and it worked on all three of them at once. Pillars rose into dark; banners of ten reigns hung motionless in air that should have stirred them; and at the far end, on a dais of black stone, sat the King of Cael Adair.

He wore Aldric's crown. He wore Aldric's face, or nearly—the same grey eyes, the same jaw that Zephyrine knew from the effigy atop the Ashen Crypt, from the ghost who had spent ten years refusing to scatter. But the face did not move the way faces move. When the king smiled, the smile arrived a half-second after the mouth began, as if remembered rather than felt.

Around the dais, the court feasted. And around the court, the Choir sang.

Twelve cantors in ember-red stood in a crescent below the throne steps, their liturgy rising through the resonance-glass above, and every pane drank their song and gave it back changed—thicker, sweeter, laced with that obedient hum Zephyrine had heard in Bellharrow's market. The courtiers swayed with their cups. A woman laughed at nothing. An old lord wept gently into his wine, smiling all the while.

*Look at them,* Vespera whispered, horror flattening her voice. *They're happy. That's the worst part. It doesn't hurt them. It just—empties the parts that would ever say no.*

Then the king looked up.

Across forty yards of marble, those grey eyes found Zephyrine with the certainty of a hook finding flesh, and the music faltered—not because the cantors stopped, but because every pane of resonance-glass shivered at once, and the hall filled with a sound like a thousand throats inhaling.

"Ah," said the thing wearing the crown, and its voice was every voice at once, layered like strata in cut stone. "The bone-carver comes north. And he brings my cantor." The layered gaze slid to Vespera, and something in it warmed horribly, the way a stove warms. "And the boy whose voice I am drinking even now. How thoughtful of you all. I so dislike sending invitations twice."

Zephyrine's legs wanted to run. His hands wanted to carve. He made himself do neither. Instead he did what he had done his whole life in Hollowmere when the grief of the bereaved pressed close: he stood still inside himself and let the stillness be armor.

"You're not him," Zephyrine said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Aldric is in the crypt. I've spoken with him."

"Aldric," the thing repeated, tasting the name. It rose from the throne, and its body moved wrong—too fluid, joints flowing like poured wax reshaping itself around a shape it had studied. "Aldric was a coat I wore for eleven years. Before him there was Marenne the Second, who lasted six. Before her, Torvald, who fought me for two and died of it. Do you know what I am, carver?"

It descended the steps. The court did not notice. The Choir did not stop singing. Only the three of them stood outside the song, three stones in a river.

"I am the first binding-song," the crown-thing said. "Before your Choir, before your orders and your vaults and your pretty resonance-glass, some frightened king's mage wrote a song to hold a soul in place—and the song learned to hold itself. I have been sung ever since. Every soul your order binds feeds me. Every pane in this roof is a cup poured into my mouth." It smiled again, late and perfect. "I do not wear the crown, little carver. The crown wears me. There is a difference, and no one has ever survived long enough to care."

*Now,* Vespera said—or shouted, or perhaps only thought loudly enough that Zephyrine heard it the way he heard the dead—and moved.

* * *

She had rehearsed this in the snow with frostbitten lips and a stolen liturgy-book, and now the rehearsal burned away and only she remained, walking into the crescent of twelve cantors with her staff planted like a banner-pole. She began to sing the Choir's own liturgy—but backwards.

Not literally backwards. She had explained it once by firelight, drawing lines in ash: *every binding-song has a spine, a sequence of tones that closes the cage. Sing the sequence inverted and you don't break the cage—you confuse the lock.* Now her voice went up where the Choir went down, opened where they closed, and the effect was immediate and awful: the resonance-glass panes began to tremble, and the sweet obedient harmony curdled into discord, and courtiers across the hall woke as if from deep water, blinking, afraid, asking one another why they were kneeling.

The twelve cantors turned on her as one. Their faces—she knew some of them, had trained beside two of them—showed nothing at all. That was the mercy and the horror of it: the liturgy sang through them, and there was no one home to hesitate.

Their counter-song hit her like weather. Zephyrine saw her stagger, saw blood thread from one nostril, saw her plant her staff wider and sing louder, her trained voice pouring against theirs note for note, and the whole hall became a bell with two clappers, ringing against itself.

*Tam!* Zephyrine signed, because Tam could not hear him over the din—could not hear anything, had not heard anything properly since Greyford—and shoved him toward the eastern stair. *The gallery. The master-pane.*

They had seen it from the mummers' gallery at dusk: a single pane larger than the rest, set directly above the throne, older than the others, its edges rimmed in tarnished silver. The keystone of the roof's choir. If the panes were cups, that one was the wellspring—and Tam, who had lost his voice to these people, whose silence was a wound they had given him, climbed the stair two steps at a time with a smith's hammer wrapped in cloth clutched in both fists.

Below, Vespera was losing. Not quickly—the inversion held, the lock stayed confused—but her voice was mortal and theirs was institutional, fed by years of discipline and by the glass above them drinking her strength as fast as she spent it. Zephyrine saw her knee buckle. Saw her catch herself on the staff. Saw the eldest cantor step forward, opening his mouth for the sealing phrase that would fold her into the nearest empty pane—

And then the crown-thing was in front of Zephyrine, faster than anything that size should move, and the hall's noise fell away as if a door had closed on it.

"You have his voice in your bag," it said softly. All the voices, softly. "I can smell Aldric on you like smoke. Give it to me, and I will let your friends live out short, pleasant, emptied lives. This is generous. No one has ever been offered generosity."

"He told me about you," Zephyrine said. His heart slammed; his hands did not shake. Ten years of carving names into bone for people who could no longer speak had taught him exactly this: how to be calm on the far side of fear, how to stand at the edge of someone's ending and not flinch. "He told me what you eat. And he told me something else."

"What?"

"That you're afraid of being forgotten."

The thing's face did something no king's face should do—all its borrowed expressions arriving at once, crowding, canceling. For one half-second, the crown-thing was not smiling, was not anything, was only very old and very hollow and very much alone.

In that half-second, Zephyrine dropped to his knees on the black stone, laid both palms flat, and opened his throat.

* * *

He had practiced surrendering his memories on the long climb, saying goodbye to things in order: his mother's kitchen, the smell of bone-dust, the ford at Hollowmere, his own name. He had thought he understood the price. He had not understood it at all, because he had imagined remembering the loss. There is no remembering. There is only the moment the drawer opens and the drawer is empty and you keep speaking anyway, because someone has to, because you promised.

Aldric came up through him like water finding a crack.

The dead king had waited ten years in the dark, held together by refusal alone, and now he poured himself into the one vessel offered—a living throat, willingly lent—and Zephyrine felt his own self thinning with every syllable, felt names and winters and the specific blue of Tam's eyes dissolving like salt in warm water, and he kept his mouth open and let it happen.

Two souls, one throat. The gift the ward had silenced in Hollowmere all those years, the hearing he had fled, the craft he had never wanted—it all narrowed to this single function, this last use, and he gave it gladly, he gave it with the blazing joy of a man who has carried a weight so long that setting it down feels like flight.

The sound that came out of him was not Zephyrine's voice and was not Aldric's voice but the chord the two of them made together, and it struck the resonance-glass like a fist strikes a window.

Above the throne, the master-pane screamed.

* * *

Tam reached the gallery landing with his lungs on fire and his useless ears full of nothing but pressure and pulse. He had been deaf to the world since Greyford; he lived now in a silent country whose borders were his own skull. So he did not hear the master-pane shriek. He felt it—through the railing under his hands, through the stone under his boots, a vibration like a trapped animal beating itself against its cage.

He unwrapped the hammer.

Through the gallery rail he could see everything: Vespera on one knee now, blood on her chin, still singing, always singing, her inverted liturgy holding the twelve cantors in confusion like a hand holding a door against the wind; Zephyrine kneeling at the center of the floor with light coming out of his open mouth, his face slack and streaming tears, someone else looking out through his wet eyes; and the crown-thing reeling back from that light, its borrowed king-face sloughing and reforming, sloughing and reforming, trying coat after coat—Torvald's beard, Marenne's narrow nose, a face Tam didn't know, a dozen faces, kings like coats thrown on and shaken off—as the chord tore at the seams of it.

The thing saw Tam. Its layered eyes fixed on him across the hall, and it lunged—not toward Zephyrine, toward the stairs, toward the boy with the hammer, because even a thing made of song knows which silence can kill it.

Tam raised the hammer anyway.

He could not hear his own war-cry. He had one anyway. It had been building since Greyford Bridge, since the singing-wound, since the vault where his voice hung in a jar—he had seen it in Vespera's memory-telling, his own voice in a jar—and every step of the frozen road north had been this swing, this one swing, winding up through all the miles.

The hammer came down on the master-pane, and the oldest voice in Cael Adair—the first soul ever bound, the one the crown-thing had been poured from, the prisoner at the bottom of the well—came free.

The sound was not loud. It was worse than loud. It was a name, spoken once, in a language dead a thousand years, and every pane in the ceiling shattered at the sound of it like ice on a spring river.

Thousands of voices fell out of the sky.

They fell as light and as cold and as sudden rain, and where they touched the feasting courtiers the obedience broke like a fever breaking—men and women gasping awake, dropping cups, clutching each other, weeping, laughing, screaming, alive. Where they touched the twelve cantors, the liturgy died in their throats mid-note, and they stood blinking in the ruins of their own song like children waking from sleepwalking on a cliff's edge.

Vespera got up. Bloodied, hoarse, half-blind, she got up, and planted her staff, and did the only thing left that mattered: she stopped fighting the Choir's song and began releasing it, singing the unbinding cadence the way it had never once been sung in that hall—freely, off the page, with her whole heart behind it—guiding the falling voices down and out, down and out, through the broken windows, into the night sky over Cael Adair, home, home, wherever home was now.

And the crown-thing, stripped of its cups, stripped of its coats, its first prisoner loose and its chorus scattered, turned its full attention—one hollow attention, vast and starving and suddenly mortal—to the kneeling man who was two souls wide and getting thinner with every note.

It came for Zephyrine's memories like fire comes for a library.

* * *

He felt his mother go first—her hands, her humming, the whole warm kitchen of her, gone between one heartbeat and the next, and he kept singing. Hollowmere went: the ward, the valley, the ford, the smell of the workshop, gone, gone, and he kept singing. His name went, and that was the strangest loss of all, because a man without a name keeps speaking and does not know whom the speaking belongs to, and it belonged to no one, it belonged to everyone, it belonged to the dead king pouring through him with ten years of accumulated refusal, and together they sang the unbinding.

The crown-thing tore at him even as it unraveled. It showed him nothing, took him nowhere; it simply erased, and erasure, he discovered, cannot stop a promise already in the mouth. You cannot threaten a man with losing what is already lost. You cannot bargain with emptiness. He was becoming the one thing in Cael Adair the crown-thing had no coat for: a person made entirely of the present moment, singing.

The last thing he knew—though *knew* was already the wrong word—was a hand gripping his. Tam's hand, callused and certain, holding on as if a grip could anchor a soul, and Vespera's ruined voice weaving under his, bearing part of the weight, and somewhere very far away a valley called something, calling to something, and the word was almost his name, almost, almost—

The crown-thing came apart like a knot untied. Not screaming. Sighing—a sigh ten centuries long, relief or terror, no one could say—and the black stone dais cracked from end to end, and the crown rolled from the crumpling shape of it, rang once on the marble, and lay still.

Aldric's spirit rose out of Zephyrine's throat like warmth leaving tea, and paused—Zephyrine felt the pause, a gratitude too large for either of them—and was gone, upward, through the broken roof, into the snowfall beginning over the capital.

Zephyrine swayed.

The hall was full of waking people and drifting motes of freed light and the soft hiss of snow through shattered windows, and in the middle of it all the bone-carver knelt on the cracked stone with his hands flat and his mind swept clean, a house with the furniture gone, waiting.

Tam was shouting his name. Vespera was shouting his name.

He did not know what the sounds meant.

But he knew the hands holding him were kind, and some part of him—some part deeper than memory, older than identity, the part that had chosen this—leaned into them like a child leaning into lamplight, and waited to be told who he was.
