## 20. One Voice, Two Souls

The first note came out of Zephyrine's throat like a bird startled from a burning house.

It was not his note. He felt it pass through him—felt it the way a doorway feels weather—rising from somewhere deeper than his lungs, older than his voice. King Aldric had waited ten years in the dark of the Ashen Crypt, held together by nothing but refusal, and now that refusal had found its instrument.

The throne room answered with light.

Not the gold-warm light of the coronation feast still guttering beyond the great doors, where Vespera's countersong was holding three hundred courtiers in a trance of forgetting. This light came from the crown-thing itself, from the thing that wore Aldric's face like a well-fitted glove: a cold radiance, silver-white, the color of frost forming on glass. It poured off the usurper in waves as the unbinding-song struck it, and where the light touched, the air screamed.

Zephyrine heard the screaming and understood, distantly, that some of it was coming from his own mouth.

*Hold*, Aldric said—not in words anymore, not since they had merged at the threshold of the dais, two souls crowded into one throat—but in pressure, in intention, the way a hand presses against the small of your back. *It will try to take you apart. Let it take what it takes. Do not stop.*

*What will it take?*

*Everything you are. That is what I promised you it would cost. That is why I asked.*

And then there was no more time for questions, because the crown-thing stopped pretending to be a king.

---

It happened all at once, the way ice lets go of a branch. The shape of Aldric—the broad shoulders, the grey-shot beard, the tired and terrible dignity—sloughed off the creature beneath like a costume cut away. What remained did not have a face. It had *surfaces*. It stood twice the height of any man and yet seemed to occupy no space at all, a figure made of woven sound, threads of the first binding-song given flesh ten years ago by sorcerers who had wanted a king that would never die and had gotten exactly what they asked for. Its crown was real—that alone was real, iron-black, fused to nothing, floating above a head that was more suggestion than substance—and from the crown hung chains of light, hundreds of them, thousands, each chain anchored in a soul the Choir had harvested over a decade. Zephyrine could see them now, could hear them: the faint chorus of the harvested, singing their obedience from inside the thing's architecture, each one a stone in its wall.

*There you are*, Aldric breathed, and there was such grief in it that Zephyrine felt it shake his ribs. *Hello, old wound.*

The crown-thing looked at them—or oriented itself toward them, for looking implied eyes—and spoke without speaking, its voice arriving fully formed inside Zephyrine's skull like a knife laid flat on the tongue.

***YOU BROUGHT ME A VOICE,*** it said. ***HOW THOUGHTFUL. I HAVE EATEN SO MANY.***

Then it reached into him and began to tear.

---

It did not go for his body. Bodies were nothing to it; bodies were coats, and it had worn kings like coats. It went for the softer country underneath—the landscape of memory where a self is built—and it tore with the indiscriminate hunger of a flood.

Zephyrine felt his mother go first.

She was simply *gone*, one heartbeat present and the next an absence shaped exactly like her, a hole in the world wearing her warmth. He had been carving a bone for her when she died—he remembered that he had been carving, though not for whom, though not what—and then even that context dissolved, leaving only the knowledge that something had been taken and the taste of its taking, which was bitter, which was like chewing winter.

He kept singing.

The song required no memory. That was its mercy and its horror both: the unbinding was older than language, a melody that lived below thought, and so long as one thread of him remained to carry it, the thread would carry it true. He sang through the loss of his mother. He sang through the loss of Hollowmere—the valley going out of him like water from a cracked cup, the ward, the ford, the smell of wet stone and pine smoke, Elder Maren's disapproving frown, the specific gray of the sky in late autumn—all of it draining, all of it *taken*, and the crown-thing growing brighter with every stolen piece, feeding his life into its own fabric even as the song unraveled its edges.

*Clever*, Aldric murmured, grim. *It eats what you love and grows fat on grief. Do not grieve. There is no time and no room. Sing.*

*I don't know how not to grieve.*

*No,* Aldric said, and something almost like laughter moved through their shared throat. *I suppose you wouldn't be here if you did.*

---

Through the haze of dissolution, Zephyrine saw the others fighting at the edge of everything.

Vespera stood on the steps below the dais, arms raised, pouring the Choir's own liturgy backward up at its maker—a countersong she had spent her whole life learning without knowing what she learned it for. Her voice was extraordinary. Even now, even here, some part of Zephyrine that was still himself marveled at it: pure and precise and furious, a blade of trained sound cutting the crown-thing's chains one by one. Each severed chain released a soul, and the souls rose like sparks off a bonfire, flickering once in the frozen air, and were gone—free, finally free, going wherever souls go when the debt is paid.

Tam had climbed the eastern colonnade and was swinging a mallet—where had he gotten a mallet?—against the resonance-glass, the vast curved panes the Choir had installed above the throne to catch and amplify the binding-songs. The glass fought back. Every blow rang wrong, a wounded-bell sound, and the crown-thing flung tendrils of light at him, and Tam dodged, mute and stubborn and alive, and struck again, and again.

*They are doing their part,* Aldric said. *Do yours.*

But Zephyrine was losing the map of himself faster than he could read it. The valley was gone. His mother was gone. The courier at the ford was going now—the bloodstained hands, the last breath, the words pressed into his palms along with the map—and Zephyrine grasped after that memory the way a drowning man grasps at driftwood, because it mattered, because it was the beginning of everything—

The crown-thing took it.

And took the moors, and the dead who had trusted him, and the scouts he had felled with a chorus of the betrayed, and the taste of the power he had liked too much. Took Bellharrow's humming streets. Took the shepherd's bothy and the nearly-burned map. Took, piece by piece, the entire road he had walked to arrive at this moment, until he was a man standing in a throne room with no idea how he had come there, singing a song he did not remember learning, beside friends whose names he was about to lose.

*Tam*, he tried to say. The name wobbled. *Tam. Vespera.* He held onto those two the way a man holds two sparks in cupped hands. *Let me keep those. Take anything else. Let me keep—*

***NO,*** said the crown-thing.

And it took them.

---

The silence where their names had been was worse than any pain.

Pain, he discovered, was survivable; pain was even useful, a landmark in the fog. But the absence of Tam—of the friend who had followed him into exile, who had lost his voice at Greyford Bridge, who had learned to speak again through carved bone—was not absence like an empty chair. It was absence like a word torn out of a sentence, leaving the grammar bleeding. He knew someone had been there. He knew he loved someone who had been there. The love remained, orphaned, pointing at nothing.

He forgot Tam. He forgot Vespera. He forgot the crypt, the bargain, the reason.

And he kept singing.

Because by then it was not a choice. Because the song had become the only thing left in him, the final room of the house with the last lamp lit, and the lamp would not go out while he drew breath. Later—if there was a later—he would understand that this was what Aldric had meant by *willingly*: not the willingness of the moment of giving, but the willingness that survives the loss of every reason for willing. A bargain kept past the point where the bargainer can remember making it. There was a purity in that. There was a horror in that. They were, perhaps, the same thing.

*Almost done*, Aldric whispered. His presence was thinner now too; the unbinding was costing the dead king what remained of his cohesion, spending him like coin. Zephyrine could feel him fraying, could feel them fraying together, two candles burning down in the same draft. *The last verse. The one they never wrote down. Listen—I'll give it to you once.*

The crown-thing understood what was coming. It threw everything it had left at them—not at Zephyrine's memories, which were nearly bare, but at the song itself, flooding the throne room with counter-harmonies, dragging the harvested souls back into formation, rebuilding its wall of obedience note by note. The temperature plunged. Frost raced across the flagstones. Somewhere far away, muffled behind the great doors, Vespera's countersong faltered—

—and Tam's mallet came down one final time, and the resonance-glass above the throne burst inward in a rain of singing shards, and every harmony in the room collapsed into chaos.

Into the chaos, Zephyrine opened his mouth wider than a human mouth should open, and gave the dead king the last of his voice.

---

The final verse of the unbinding was not loud.

That was the strangest part. After all the thunder and tearing, the song that ended the crown-thing was quiet as snowfall, a descending phrase of five notes, repeated, each repetition softer than the last, and it worked not by force but by *permission*. It told the binding-song it was allowed to stop. It told the harvested souls they were allowed to rest. It told the crown-thing, gently, terribly, the one sentence no tyrant has ever survived hearing:

*You may put it down now. You have carried it long enough.*

The crown-thing shrieked—a sound with no voice in it, pure refusal, ten years of stolen sovereignty howling—and began to unravel.

Zephyrine watched it come apart from somewhere outside himself. He had forgotten his mother. He had forgotten his valley. He had forgotten his friends, his craft, his crimes, his name. He was, by any measure he could still apply, no one at all—a hollow instrument through which a dead king sang a lullaby to a monster—and yet he watched the unraveling with something that in a person might have been called joy. The chains of light broke and broke and broke, and the souls went up and out, hundreds of them, thousands, a slow bright migration through the shattered glass, and each departing soul took a thread of the crown-thing with it, until there was less and less of it, until the iron crown dropped from the empty air and rang on the flagstones like a bell announcing midnight, until the last of it was a shimmer, a shiver, a sigh—

—and Aldric, in the last moment before the song ended, gathered what was left of himself and stepped forward out of Zephyrine's ruined interior, and stood for one instant in the throne room as a man again: tall, worn, crowned by nothing, smiling with an expression of such profound relief that Zephyrine's borrowed heart ached to look at it.

*You gave everything*, the dead king said. *I want you to know that I saw it. All of it. Even the parts you've already lost. Someone should remember, if you cannot.*

*I can't remember anything,* Zephyrine tried to say. It came out as breath. *Who am I?*

*A man who kept singing,* said Aldric. *When there was nothing else left of you, there was that. Hold onto it. Someone will come to tell you the rest.*

And the spirit of King Aldric of Cael Adair bowed to him—bowed, king to carver, as to an equal—and let go.

The peace that came off him as he departed filled the throne room like sunrise. It touched the frost and melted it. It touched the broken glass and made the shards glitter. It passed through Zephyrine on its way out of the world, and where it passed, it left one small gift: not a memory, not a name, but a single warm certainty, planted deep below all the absence—

*You were loved. Whoever you were: you were loved.*

Then the doors burst open, and torchlight and shouting spilled in, and the courtiers woke from Vespera's trance to find their false king gone and a stranger kneeling alone on the dais among shards of glass, staring at his own hands as if he had never seen them before.

A young woman in Choir robes pushed through the crowd, her face streaked with ash and tears. Behind her came a big man moving fast, calling out a name—

Calling out *his* name.

The sound reached Zephyrine across the ruined throne room like a rope thrown to a man in dark water. He did not know the name. He did not know the voice. But his heart, which had forgotten everything else, remembered how to answer it.

He turned toward the sound, and the chapter of his annihilation ended, and the slower, harder work of being rebuilt had begun.
