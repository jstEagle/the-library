## 22. Carving Bones in a Louder World

Spring came to Cael Adair the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and all at once.

Zephyrine stood at a window of the rebuilt palace and watched the snow retreat up the mountainsides like a tide going out, leaving the city's rooftops black and glistening, steaming in the first honest sunlight in half a year. Below, in the square where the crown-thing had once paraded its stolen king, someone had planted a rowan tree in a barrel. It was already budding. The people of the capital had a talent for that, he was learning: for planting things in barrels, in ruins, in the cracks of a broken year. For insisting on green.

Behind him, the council chamber hummed with voices. Real voices, living ones, arguing about grain tariffs and garrison rotations and what to do with the Choir's great vault beneath the Conservatory—the one where stolen voices hung in glass like lanterns, waiting. Vespera was in there now, her trained soprano cutting clean through the men who kept suggesting that perhaps the vault's contents could be *useful*, that perhaps a kingdom needed its instruments. Zephyrine could not hear the words through the door, but he could hear her tone, and it was the tone of a woman who had spent a decade being useful to monsters and would not preside over it a day longer.

He heard other things too. That was the part no one had warned him about—though perhaps no one could have, since no one had ever done what he had done, lent his throat to a dead king and forgotten himself into the bargain and been spoken back into being. The gift that the Hollowmere ward had smothered all those years had come back with the spring, and it did not whisper anymore. It was everywhere.

The dead of Cael Adair filled the city like water fills a cellar. They stood in doorways and on balconies and in the long colonnades, translucent as breath on glass, and they watched the living rebuild. Ten years of the crown-thing's feeding had left the capital crowded with them—souls unbound when Vespera countersang the liturgy, souls freed from the resonance-glass, souls that had simply died afraid and stayed. On his first morning in the palace Zephyrine had walked through a corridor solid with them and had to press his back to the wall and breathe until his hands stopped shaking.

But they did not clamor. That was the thing. He had expected the moor-dead's hunger, the courier's desperate clutching, the chorus of the betrayed he had once turned into a weapon. Instead they waited. And when he passed, they inclined their heads—not in worship, not in pleading, but in the manner of neighbors acknowledging a neighbor—and they asked, in a hundred quiet voices, only one thing.

*Remember me. I was Aldwin, a cooper, and I mended barrels for forty years. Remember me. I was Sefa, and I sang to my daughter when the fever came. Remember me. Remember me. I was here. Remember that I was here.*

He had filled three notebooks already.

"Zeph." Tam's voice, from the doorway—Tam's voice, which was still new enough to both of them that they sometimes just stopped and listened to it, the way you listen to rain after a drought. Tam had learned to speak again through the carved bone Zephyrine had made him, a rib of ash wood and moor-bone that held a sliver of Tam's own stolen voice until it could be restored whole. Even now that his voice was his own again, he kept the bone on a cord around his neck. "They're ready for you in there. And before you ask—no, I couldn't talk them out of it."

"Out of what?"

"You know what." Tam's mouth did the thing it did when he was trying not to smile at Zephyrine's expense. It had been doing it a lot lately, and every time it landed in Zephyrine's chest like a coal, warm and almost painful. "The heir wants to give you a title."

The heir. Aldric's heir—a niece, as it turned out, a sharp-eyed woman named Isolde who had spent ten years as a minor functionary in a minor province, keeping her head down and her ledgers honest, and who had walked into the ruined throne room three days after the unbinding and looked at the scorch marks on the dais and said, *Well. Someone has to sweep up.* The court had been so starved for a king that they had accepted her almost gratefully, and she, to her credit, seemed to intend to earn it.

Zephyrine followed Tam into the chamber. Isolde sat at the head of a table that had seen better centuries, and Vespera stood at her shoulder with her arms crossed, wearing her Choir grays still, because she refused to wear anything else until the order was formally dissolved and she said the grays reminded her what she was dismantling. Elder Maren would have approved of her. The thought arrived unbidden and ached, as thoughts of Hollowmere always did now.

"Master Dunmore." Isolde gestured him to a chair. "Thank you. I'll be brief, because I know you dislike ceremony and I'm told you've been avoiding me for six days."

"I've been busy."

"Writing down the names of the dead. Yes. Vespera told me." Isolde folded her hands. "Here is what I want to offer, and then you may refuse me and we can both get on with our mornings. The Choir's bindings are still active in forty-one locations across the kingdom. Ward-stones, resonance-arrays, the liturgy-lattices woven into village bells. Someone has to unmake them, and the only person alive who has ever unmade one from the outside is you. I want to give you a commission. A house. A staff. Whatever you need. Dismantle the Choir's work, Zephyrine Dunmore, and Cael Adair will owe you a debt no ledger can hold."

The chamber went quiet. Vespera was watching him with an expression he had learned to read: not hope, exactly. Readiness. She had told him once, on the long climb, that she had spent her whole life being told what her voice was for, and that the only gift she trusted now was the one she gave away on purpose.

Zephyrine looked at the table. At the map of the kingdom spread across it, with its forty-one small marks like a rash, like a pox, like the places where someone had once decided that souls were a resource.

"I can't," he said.

"Zeph—" Tam started.

"No. Let me say it properly, because I've been rehearsing it for six days and if I stop I'll lose my nerve." He laid his hand flat on the map. "If I stay, I become an institution. The King's Unmaker. People will bring me their dead and their enemies and their grievances, and I will spend the rest of my life deciding whose voices get silenced. I've already done that once, at Greyford Bridge, and I still dream about it. I liked it, Isolde. That's the thing nobody says. When the scouts fell, some part of me sang along. I know what I am when I'm given power over the dead, and it isn't a dismantler. It's a carver who's found a sharper knife."

He took a breath. The dead in the walls of the old palace stirred faintly, listening. They always listened now.

"But I'll tell you what I will do. I'll teach. Before I go—however long that takes—I'll write down everything I know about unbinding, and Vespera knows the liturgy better than its makers did, and between us we can train people who aren't me. People who'll do it as work, not as war. And—" he tapped the map, at a point far to the south, in the hills where a valley lay folded between two ridges, too small to appear on most maps, "—there's one thing I have to carry myself."

The silence changed quality. Vespera straightened.

"You're going to Hollowmere," Tam said.

"I'm going to Hollowmere." He met Isolde's eyes. "The binding-song. The original one, from the crypt. I told you I'd buried it. I lied, or I half-lied—I hid it, because I couldn't decide. A song that can make another crown-thing shouldn't exist, but it also shouldn't exist in the head of one tired bone-carver who can be killed on any road between here and anywhere. It should be buried where the ward still holds. Where magic goes to be quiet. Where I spent ten years not hearing the dead, because the valley knows how to keep a secret."

Isolde studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she probably nodded at ledgers. "The valley that expelled you."

"The valley that was afraid." He said it without bitterness, mostly because he had spent the whole winter failing to be bitter about it and had finally, somewhere in the snow, succeeded. "Fear isn't a betrayal. It's just a door that opens the other way. I'm going home to find out if it opens both directions."

* * *

They left on the fourth morning of the thaw, three riders and a mule, and the city turned out to watch them go, which Zephyrine hated, and which Tam enjoyed so openly that Zephyrine eventually had to laugh.

The road south was soft with mud and loud with water—snowmelt roaring down every gully, the world rinsed and new. And the dead were with them the whole way, though that was the wrong word for it. They were everywhere, the way birds are everywhere, the way wind is. In the villages they passed, Zephyrine heard them in the churchyards and under the floorboards of old houses, and he stopped, now, when they asked. He had a notebook for each settlement. *Remember me* was not a hard prayer to answer. It cost him an hour here, an afternoon there, a hand cramping around a stub of pencil. It cost him nothing at all, and it was the only use of his gift that had ever felt clean.

In the town of Bellharrow—the town that had sung along, the town whose humming had once made his skin crawl—they found the bells already silent. The villagers had taken them down themselves, the week after news of the unbinding arrived, and melted two of them into a plowshare. An old woman with the same face as the woman who had once begged him to burn the map stopped him in the market square and pressed a heel of bread into his hands.

"You came back," she said. "I told you to burn it. I'm glad you didn't listen to me. I'm usually glad when nobody listens to me, but this once—" She patted his arm with a hand like a bird's foot. "The singing's stopped. Did you know? We didn't even know we were singing until it stopped. My husband's ghost sat on the end of my bed last night, first time in ten years, and he didn't say anything. He just looked relieved. Is that strange?"

"No," Zephyrine said. "That's about the least strange thing I've heard this year."

They camped that night under a rowan tree that someone, long ago, had planted on a barrow-mound—old magic and older kindness sharing the same small hill. Vespera took first watch, and Zephyrine lay awake listening to the dead of the barrow, who were soldiers from some war whose name had worn away, and who were, of all things, *content*. They had their mound and their tree and the sound of three living people breathing nearby, and when he asked them—silently, the way he asked now—what they wanted, the answer came back like a lullaby: *to be counted. To have been.*

"Zephyrine." Vespera's voice, low, from the edge of the firelight. "You're doing the thing where you talk to the dirt instead of sleeping."

"I'm taking attendance."

She came and sat beside him, her back against the rowan's trunk. For a while neither of them spoke. That was the other thing the winter had given him: two people with whom silence was not a wound.

"Isolde's offer was good," she said at last. "I'm going to take it. Someone has to train the unbinders, and it can't be you, because you'll be hiding in a valley carving memorial bones for farmers, which, incidentally, is the most *you* plan I've ever heard."

"It's a good plan."

"It's a terrible plan. It's the best terrible plan I've ever heard." She poked the fire. "I'll come see you. When the work allows. I want to see this valley that kept you quiet for ten years. I want to know what it sounds like, a place with no voices in it at all."

"It doesn't sound like anything," Zephyrine said. "That's the whole point. It sounds like rest."

* * *

The ridgeline above Hollowmere looked exactly as it always had, which was the cruelest and kindest thing about it.

Zephyrine reined in at the crest of the pass and looked down into the valley, and his chest did something complicated that he decided not to examine. The terraces were greening. Smoke rose from the chimneys in the same lazy threads. The ford glittered where a courier had once crawled half-dead out of the water and died with a map in his fist, and Zephyrine's whole ruinous winter had begun. Someone had put up a new fence there. Life went on in the places that expelled you. That was, he supposed, what made them worth going back to.

"You don't have to come in," he said to Tam and Vespera. "If they won't have me, they certainly won't—"

"Zephyrine Dunmore." Tam said it in the voice he had used in the throne room, in the aftermath, when Zephyrine had been sitting hollow as a shed skin among the broken glass, and Tam had knelt in the wreckage and said his name like a man striking flint. "I walked three hundred miles to be insulted by your elders once already. I'm not waiting at the pass for a second round."

They rode down.

The valley felt them coming, of course. By the time they reached the ford, half of Hollowmere was standing in the road—Elder Maren at the front of them, older than Zephyrine remembered, her spine still straight as a chisel. Behind her: faces he knew. The cooper's widow. The children he had carved bones for, taller now. Tam's mother, who pushed through the crowd before anyone could speak and hugged her son so hard he made a sound like a hinge, and then, over his shoulder, fixed Zephyrine with a look that said *you brought him back and I will be grateful until I die, and also you are still a fool.*

Maren came forward alone. She stopped an arm's length away and looked at him for a long time—at the scar the crown-thing's cold had left along his jaw, at the notebooks in his saddlebag, at whatever she saw in his face that had not been there when he left.

"We voted you out," she said.

"You did."

"We were wrong." She said it flatly, without ornament, the way she said everything. "The ward's been failing since midwinter—we know why, now, we know what was pressing on it from the north. It'll keep failing. The valley's going to have to learn to live in a louder world, and there's exactly one person born here who knows how to do that." She held out her hand, an old woman's hand, work-hardened. "The vote was to expel you. I'm calling a new one. But I wanted to say it to your face first, off the record: welcome home, Zeph."

He took her hand. Around them the crowd made a sound—not a cheer, Hollowmere didn't cheer, but a sort of collective exhale, a valley's worth of held breath released. And through it, faint as light through water, Zephyrine heard the valley's dead: generations of them, the carvers and shepherds and midwives under the churchyard turf, murmuring their names to him like an offering. *Remember me. Remember me.* And beneath them, one voice he knew—his mother's, or the memory-shaped echo of her that lived in his own rebuilt mind, since her body lay far away and her soul, he believed, had gone wherever souls go when they are done. But the echo said it anyway, the way echoes do: *Remember me, love. You're doing it already.*

* * *

They buried the binding-song on the first of May, in the old way.

Zephyrine had carved a vessel for it over three weeks of evenings—a long bone case, white ash wood and the rib of a moor-ox, worked with every silence he knew: the carved quiet of the Hollowmere ward, the deaf-wax stillness of the mountain passes, the hush he had learned in the snow. Into it, wrapped in wax and wool, went the script of the first binding-song, the one that had made the crown-thing possible, the one that had cost a dead king ten years of refusal and a bone-carver everything he had and more than he could name. They carried it up the valley at dawn—Zephyrine, Tam, Vespera, and a dozen Hollowmere volunteers who asked no questions—and they buried it beneath the rowan on the barrow-mound where the old soldiers slept, and Maren spoke the ward-words over it, and the soldiers of the barrow stood watch around the grave like a honor guard that would outlast every living person present.

"No one can sing it," Vespera said, brushing dirt from her knees. "Not even you."

"Especially not me." Zephyrine looked at the small mound of fresh earth. It looked like every grave he had ever carved a bone for, and it felt, for the first time in his life, like enough. "That's the whole point."

They walked back down as the sun cleared the ridge. Below, the valley was waking—smoke, birdsong, the ford running loud with meltwater, Tam's mother already shouting at them to come eat before the bread went cold. Zephyrine paused at the top of the terrace path and listened, the way he did now, to the whole crowded quiet world: the living chattering, the dead murmuring their names, the ward humming its old slow note under everything like a held breath.

It was louder than it used to be. He was louder in it than he used to be. And he was, against all odds and all arithmetic, still Zephyrine Dunmore—rebuilt from testimony, held up by loyalty, a man who carved bones and answered prayers that cost nothing and had refused a crown's gratitude in favor of a valley's bread.

"Coming?" Tam called.

"Yeah," Zephyrine said, and went down toward the smell of fresh bread and the sound of his name, spoken back to him by everyone he loved, in a world that was never going to be quiet again.
