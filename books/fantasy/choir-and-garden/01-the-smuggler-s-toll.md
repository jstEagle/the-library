## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The goats told Mara about the Wardens an hour before the lanterns showed.

*Two-legs with iron boots,* said the lead doe, whose name was Bristle because of the coarse ridge of hair along her spine. *Four of them. They smell like oiled leather and bad intentions, and they are coming up the switchbacks from the river road.*

"How far?" Mara asked, keeping their voice low even though no human ear could have caught the words. That was the first rule: never let them see your lips move.

*Far enough that you could finish eating,* Bristle said. She was chewing a mouthful of something she'd found under the snowmelt, some bitter root the other goats wouldn't touch. *But not far enough for napping. I want you to know I considered not telling you. You only ever bring oats.*

"You're getting oats."

*I had better be getting oats.*

Mara crouched at the edge of the shelf trail and looked down into the dark. The caravan was strung out below like a rosary of covered lamps — nine mules, fourteen people, and one very nervous man named Corvin Hale who called himself a spice merchant and smelled, to anyone with a working nose, like poppy resin wrapped in cinnamon. Mara had been guiding his outfit through Thornfell twice a season for three years. He paid well. He never asked questions. In Mara's experience those two qualities came bundled together more often than not.

Three years. Long enough to learn that Corvin's youngest porter hummed when he was frightened, that the twins Fen and Rook cheated at cards but always let the newest hand win their first night, that the old cook Tansy salted everything twice because she'd gone half-deaf to high sounds and couldn't taste what she couldn't hear. Three years, and Mara still slept apart from the fires, in the lee of the mules, because sleeping near people meant talking to them in the morning, and talking in the morning meant questions, and questions were how invisible lives ended.

Below, a lamp swung. Corvin's signal — *hold position* — though there was nowhere to hold on a shelf trail barely wide enough for a determined goat. Which was, in fairness, why the goats mattered.

Mara scrambled up the scree to where Bristle's herd browsed along the ridgeline, forty-odd pairs of horizontal pupils catching the moonlight like struck flints.

"I need the narrow way," Mara said. "The one above the Hanging Stair."

A murmur went through the herd, that low grumbling rumble that meant negotiation. An old billy named Cobble — missing one horn, opinionated as a tax collector — pushed forward.

*The narrow way is ours,* Cobble said. *You humans broke it last spring with your loud feet and your falling rocks, and my granddaughter nearly went over the edge. Where is our compensation?*

"Oats."

*Oats are nothing. Oats are the crumbs of your civilization. We want the south meadow.*

"The south meadow has Warden snares in it by midsummer."

*Then we want the snares removed.*

"That's not mine to promise."

*Then it's not yours to ask.* Cobble turned away, which among goats is a devastating rhetorical maneuver.

Mara closed their eyes. Below them, four Wardens were climbing toward a smuggler caravan carrying enough contraband to hang every soul in it, and the only thing between those two facts was a conversation with a stubborn billy goat on a ridge in the dark.

"Cobble," Mara said. "If I clear the snares myself — every one, before midsummer — will you take the herd across the upper shelf tonight and make noise on the east face? Loud noise. Enough noise that anyone listening thinks the whole mountain is moving."

Cobble paused. Goats love two things beyond all others: high places and drama.

*Define loud noise.*

"Rockfalls. Screaming. Whatever you like. Make it sound like the end of the world, just on the wrong side of it."

The old billy's whiskered mouth curved in what Mara had long ago learned to read as a smile.

*For snare-clearing and oats,* he said, *I will give you a performance worthy of legend.*

* *

Corvin Hale didn't believe in Mara's gift. This was a fiction they maintained together, the way polite households maintain the fiction that nobody knows about the drinking. Corvin had seen Mara stand motionless on a hillside at dusk, head tilted, listening to nothing, and then say, "Take the lower ford, the bridge ice won't hold," and the bridge ice hadn't held. He'd watched Mara refuse to enter three separate canyons for reasons they declined to explain, and later learned all three had collapsed or been garrisoned within the month. He did not ask. Asking would mean knowing, and knowing would mean he'd have to decide what kind of man he was.

So when Mara dropped down to the caravan and said, "Wardens on the switchbacks, four of them, maybe ninety minutes out," Corvin simply nodded and began moving bodies.

"Ninety minutes." He rubbed his jaw. "The Shelf Road's a day around."

"We're not taking the Shelf Road. We're going up." Mara pointed to a black seam in the cliff face that most eyes would have filed under *not a path*. "There's a route. Narrow, ugly, and the mules will hate it, but it puts us above the patrol instead of in front of it."

"Above." Corvin squinted at the seam. "That's not a route. That's a rumor of a route."

"It's a route," said Fen, materializing at Mara's elbow the way she always did, silent as spilled flour. Her twin Rook appeared on the other side a heartbeat later, carrying two mule packs he had absolutely not been authorized to carry. The twins were nineteen, sharp-faced, and had decided sometime last year that Mara was a puzzle worth solving, which made them more dangerous than any Warden.

"You've done this before," Rook said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've done everything before. That's why you pay me."

"And how do you know these routes?" Fen's eyes were quick and green and missed nothing. "No mapmaker's been up here in thirty years. Half these trails don't exist on paper."

"They exist on hooves," Mara said. "Move."

It was a deflection and everyone knew it was a deflection. But the lamplight downstream was real, and fear is an excellent substitute for curiosity, so the caravan moved.

The climb took two hours and cost one mule its dignity, sliding to its hocks in scree until six porters hauled it free by tail and halter while it screamed curses in whatever language mules scream in. Mara understood every word and kept their face carefully blank. The mule, whose name was Duchess and who considered herself grossly underappreciated, spent the rest of the climb delivering a detailed account of her grievances to anyone who would listen. Mara listened. It was easier than listening to the humans, who kept glancing at them sideways with that particular expression — gratitude with a blade of unease in it. Useful. Uncanny. *How does she do it?* (They always assumed Mara was a woman until corrected, and Mara had stopped correcting.) *What is he?*

Nobody asked. Nobody ever asked. And Mara told themselves, for the thousandth time, that this was the arrangement working exactly as designed.

* *

They gained the high shoulder of the mountain an hour past midnight, and that was when the east face woke up.

It started with a clatter of stones — small, almost apologetic — then built, stone calling to stone, a cascade of sound rolling down five hundred feet of black rock. Then the screaming began. Anyone who has never heard a goat decide to scream has not truly heard the mountain speak; it is a sound like a child being murdered by a landslide, and forty goats doing it in chorus, echoing off the cliffs, is indistinguishable from apocalypse.

The caravan froze. One of the younger porters dropped to his knees. Corvin's hand found his knife.

"East face," Mara said calmly. "Rockslide. Or close enough."

"That's—" Rook tilted his head. "That doesn't sound like rockslide."

"It sounds like forty goats having opinions," Fen said slowly, watching Mara with an expression that had sharpened from curiosity into something dangerously close to understanding. "Doesn't it, Mara?"

"Goats scream," Mara said. "It's known."

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, the iron-boot rhythm of the Warden patrol stumbled, halted, turned. Voices carried up through the thin air — orders snapped, boots double-timing back toward the noise. Toward the east face. Away from the seam of shadow where fourteen criminals and nine mules stood pressed against cold stone, holding their breath.

Bristle's voice drifted down from the heights, breathless and triumphant: *Did you hear? Did you hear us? We were magnificent. The little one fell off a ledge on purpose. On purpose! She says to tell you she wants extra oats.*

"She'll get them," Mara murmured.

"Mara," Fen said quietly, right beside their ear, making them flinch. "Who are you talking to?"

"The mountain," Mara said. "Go sleep."

Fen held their gaze for three full seconds — long enough to make clear she didn't believe a word of it, and longer than anyone else had ever dared — then shrugged and walked off to bully her brother into setting up camp. Mara stood alone at the edge of the ledge a moment longer, listening to the goats' celebration fading into ordinary bleating, and felt the familiar shape of the night settle back over them: the job done, the danger passed, the payment owed, and not one single person on this mountain whose life Mara would still be part of next season if the work dried up.

That was the bargain. That had always been the bargain. Be useful, be strange, be brief. A guide is a tool, and tools don't get asked to stay.

They went down and took their usual place apart from the fire, in the lee of the mules, and told themselves the ache in their chest was just the altitude.

* *

Dawn found them above the cloud line, and above the law.

Corvin paid in silver at first light, counting coins into Mara's palm with the ceremony of a man settling a debt he hoped never to owe again. "Same terms in the autumn?" he asked.

"If the mountain allows it."

"The mountain seems to allow you anything." He smiled — genuine, tired, almost warm — and for one stupid second Mara let themselves lean toward it. Then Corvin added, "One of these days you'll tell me how you do it," and the warmth closed like a door.

"One of these days," Mara agreed, in a tone that promised never.

The twins saw them off. This was new. Usually Mara slipped away unremarked, a fact of the camp dissolving like frost. But Fen and Rook walked them to the edge of the high pasture, and Fen thrust something at them — a cloth bundle, still warm. Bread and hard cheese, wrapped with a sprig of something green.

"You didn't eat," she said.

"I ate."

"You watched us eat and stood there looking like a heron. Take it." When Mara hesitated, Rook chimed in: "We'll just leave it here and it'll get rained on, and then a marmot gets fat, and honestly the marmot's already smug enough."

Mara took the bundle. It was such a small thing. Their hands didn't seem to know what to do with it.

"Why do you two keep doing this?" they asked. "Being decent. You're smugglers."

"We prefer *logistics specialists*," Rook said.

"You talk to animals," Fen said, ignoring him, watching Mara with those quick green eyes. "Don't argue. I've got eyes. Last winter you told Corvin the pass wolf was denning with pups and we should detour, and you said it in a way like you'd been *introduced*. And last night —" she jerked her chin toward the east face — "that wasn't a rockslide. That was a choir."

The word landed somewhere under Mara's ribs and sat there, heavy.

"Everyone talks to animals," Mara said. "'Get up, you great lump.' 'Don't eat that.' Everyone talks."

"Yeah," Fen said softly. "But nobody else gets answers."

Silence stretched across the pasture. Somewhere below, a hawk rode the thermals, screaming its own name over and over, the way hawks do. Mara could have told the twins what the hawk was actually saying — *mine, mine, the valley is mine* — and watched their faces change forever. Instead they tucked the bread bundle inside their coat and stepped back.

"Autumn," Mara said. "If the mountain allows."

And they turned and walked down into the green folds of Thornfell alone, the way they had come, the way they always would — invisible, indispensable, and belonging to no one.

They were halfway to the river trail when the hawk stopped screaming mid-cry.

Mara stopped too. Birds didn't stop like that. Birds screamed because the sky was safe, and this silence was the silence of a sky that had suddenly, violently, stopped being safe. Above the tree line, the hawk wheeled once and fled north.

From somewhere downslope, deep in the old growth where no honest road ran, came a smell on the wind that didn't belong to any animal Mara knew: crushed herbs and candle smoke and paper. Old paper. The smell of a library, blooming wild in the middle of the wilderness.

And beneath it, faint as a memory, a voice that was not a goat's and not a bird's and not any voice the mountain had ever used on Mara before:

*Mara Sterling. Come and be found.*

Mara stood very still on the trail, heart hammering, and realized with cold clarity that for the first time in their careful, invisible life —

something knew their name.
