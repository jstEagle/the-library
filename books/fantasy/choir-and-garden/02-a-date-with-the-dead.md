## 2. A Date with the Dead

The goats had warned Mara about the weather three days early, so when the rain came down in gray ropes over Thornfell, they were already dry under the waystation's eave, watching it fall and feeling smug about it.

"You're brooding," said Fen, dropping onto the step beside them. She cracked a walnut against the stone, picked out the meat, and offered half without looking. That was as close as the twins came to affection.

"I'm not."

"You've got your listening face on. The one you make when something's talking that isn't a goat." Rook appeared at the other end of the step, mirror-image of his sister except for the scar splitting his left eyebrow. "Which one is it? Badger? Weasel?"

"Nobody," Mara said, and ate the walnut.

It wasn't nobody. It was Ilex Vane, who had turned up at the low market two weeks ago with mud to his knees and a satchel full of seed packets, who had asked Mara—casually, terribly casually—whether they knew anything about the old prophet's garden up past the switchbacks, because he'd heard there were orchid species growing wild there that existed nowhere else in the six provinces. Who had smiled at Mara like Mara was a person rather than a tool with legs, and who had said, last market day: *Come with me. Night after tomorrow. I'll show you something worth the climb.*

Mara had spent eleven years making themselves invisible. They knew every Warden patrol route through Thornfell by heart, could read a campfire's smoke like a letter, could walk a caravan of stolen goods through a mountain pass so quiet that the mules seemed ashamed of their own hooves. Invisibility was a craft. And here was this botanist with ink-stained fingers and a laugh that carried, looking directly at them, seeing them, *inviting* them somewhere—as if being seen were a gift instead of a risk.

They should have said no.

They'd said yes before he finished asking.

* * *

Sable's garden sat in a fold of the mountain where the switchbacks gave up and the forest took over. Even the smugglers avoided it—not from fear exactly, but from an old unspoken agreement that some places belonged to no trade. Prophet Sable had died a hundred years ago, and the stories disagreed about everything except that she'd been strange: talked to animals, they said, wrote books nobody finished, planted a garden that outlived her on purpose.

The gate had rotted off its hinges decades back. Ilex was waiting under the archway of it anyway, lantern lit, looking pleased with himself in a way that made Mara want to both kiss him and push him into the nettle patch.

"You came," he said.

"You doubted it?"

"I hoped. Hoping's different." He handed them the lantern. "Rules first. Don't touch anything with white flowers. Don't eat anything, obviously. And stay on the paths until I say otherwise—the garden has opinions about trespassers."

"The garden has opinions."

"Mm. Everything up here does. Haven't you noticed?" He set off down what might once have been a gravel walk, now a riverbed of moss, and Mara followed, telling themselves the quickness of their pulse was just the climb.

And then they stepped into the garden proper, and forgot to be wary at all.

It was a ruin, but a ruin the way a song is a ruin when only half the singers remain. Roses had gone feral over a collapsed trellis, blooming black-red in the lantern light. A stone fountain held a century of rainwater where newts hung suspended like small green thoughts. Somewhere overhead an owl called—a real owl, saying nothing but its own name—and the whole hollow smelled of wet earth and night-blooming stock, sweet enough to ache.

"It was a working garden," Ilex said quietly. He'd stopped walking; he did that when he talked about plants, went still as if listening for them. "Not ornamental. Look—" He pointed his stick at rows gone to grass, still faintly legible as rows. "Medicinal beds. A seed nursery. She was cataloguing, not decorating. Every plant up here is either useful or unknown, and most are both."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I've been coming here for two years." He glanced back, and his smile had a sheepish edge. "I told you I found the orchids by accident. That part was true. What I didn't tell you—" He hesitated, and Mara watched him decide, actually watch the decision move across his face like weather. "There's something I wanted to show you. Something I couldn't show anyone else."

"That's a terrible thing to say to a stranger."

"We're not strangers anymore, though. Are we?" He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "Two markets' worth of conversation, one very long letter about soil acidity, and the way you looked at me when I said the word *orchid* like I'd handed you a key." He held out his hand. "Trust me for ten minutes. Then hate me if you need to."

Mara should have run then. Eleven years of craft said *run*. But the owl was calling again, and the roses were leaning, and there was a thread of something in Ilex's voice that sounded less like seduction than supplication, and Mara took his hand.

He led them past the fountain, past a fallen statue whose face the moss had kindly erased, to the far wall where the mountain itself rose up in a shoulder of granite. Vines covered it thick as fur. At its base, half-swallowed by a rosebush with thorns like fishhooks, was a door.

Not a grand door. A cellar door, low and iron-banded, the kind that led to root cellars in farmhouses all across the low country. Except this one had no handle, only a keyhole shaped wrong—not round, not square, but a shape like a leaf seen sideways.

"I found it fourteen months ago," Ilex said. "Took me a year to work out that it wasn't locked. It was *closed*. There's a difference. Things that are locked want keeping out. Things that are closed want—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I think it wants someone. I just don't know who."

"And you thought it might be me."

"I thought it might be you," he agreed. "Or I hoped. Or I'm a fool who's spent too long alone with dead women's gardens. One of those."

Mara knelt. Up close, the keyhole-leaf had veins in it, fine as the veins in a moth's wing. The stone around the door was warm. Not sun-warm—it was night, and raining, and this wall faced north. Warm like a sleeping animal.

Every sense Mara owned, the trained ones and the forbidden ones, went silent at once, the way a forest goes silent when something large passes through.

"Ilex," they said carefully, "what is behind this door?"

"Honestly? I don't know. I got it open once, a hand's width, and the smell alone made me sit down in the nettles for an hour." His voice dropped. "But I know what the garden is built around. Sable didn't plant a garden here because it was pretty. She planted it the way you'd post a guard. Everything in this hollow is arranged around whatever's behind that door, and it's been tended by roots and birds and rain for a hundred years, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For whoever comes next." He crouched beside them, close enough that Mara could smell crushed mint on his hands. "I can't open it alone. I've tried. The door doesn't care about my hands." He paused, and now there was no charm in him at all, just a bare, frightened honesty that was somehow more attractive than everything that had come before. "I think it cares about yours."

The sensible thing—the thing Bram would say, the thing eleven years of staying alive said—was to stand up, laugh it off, walk back down the mountain and never come back.

Mara put their palm flat against the door instead.

Warmth moved under the stone like a pulse. And the door—there was no other word for it—*leaned* toward them, the way a horse leans toward a familiar hand.

"Oh," said Mara, very quietly.

"Yeah," said Ilex. "That."

The door swung inward without a sound. Cold air breathed out, smelling of paper and dust and, beneath it, green things growing in the dark. Stairs led down into a room lit by nothing, and yet visible—Mara could see shelves, a desk, a chair, all of it preserved like a held breath.

They went down together. Later, Mara would try to remember which of them touched the ledger first, and would honestly not know.

It lay on the desk. A book the size of a paving stone, bound in vellum gone the color of old honey, its cover worked with a design Mara's eyes kept sliding off—vines, maybe, or veins, or writing. Around it the desk held jars of seeds, each labeled in a spidery hand. A hundred years of patience, shelved.

"She called it her ledger," Ilex whispered. "In her letters. Her *ledger of what's owed*."

"Letters? Whose letters—"

But Mara had opened the book.

The pages were dense with handwriting, margins crowded with little drawings—a fox mid-leap, a crow's eye, a goat's crooked grin—and the handwriting itself was strange, slanting, urgent, the script of someone writing faster than thought. Mara couldn't read the old provincial glyphs, but it didn't matter, because halfway down the first page, the text shifted into Trade Tongue, plain and clear, as if the book had noticed them and adjusted:

*And the mountain will choose its speaker, as it chose me.*

Mara's mouth went dry.

They turned the page. The fox drawing reappeared, watching them. More Trade Tongue, more of that terrible clarity:

*The Beast-Tongued will walk between, and the pact shall hang upon their word.*

"This is prophecy," Mara said. Their voice sounded far away. "This is actual—Ilex, this is a century old, how is it in Trade Tongue, how is it—"

"Keep reading." Ilex's hand hovered near theirs, not touching. "There's more. There's a part I couldn't—when I read it, I couldn't make sense of it, but you—"

Mare turned another page, and the world narrowed to a point.

There was a name inked in the margin, older than any of the Trade Tongue around it, written in the same slanting hand, in ink gone brown as dried blood:

*Mara.*

Their own name. Their exact name, spelled exactly right, in a book buried before their grandmother's grandmother learned to walk.

The room tilted. Mara gripped the desk. Somewhere above them, faint through a hundred feet of earth and root, the owl called again, and this time it sounded less like a name and more like a warning.

"That's not possible," Mara heard themselves say. It came out calm, which frightened them more than screaming would have. "People don't—prophecies don't get names right. Names aren't even stable. My mother named me after a river, I could have been anything—"

"Read the line next to it," Ilex said softly.

*The Beast-Tongued Heir, Mara, born under the Thornfell sign, shall hold the pact between root and roof in the palm of one hand,* the ledger said. *Whether it is renewed or severed depends upon the word they choose to speak. The Wardens will hunt them. The exiled court will claim them. Both will be wrong. And the heir will stand at last where I stood, before the blank page, and learn what I could not teach: that inheritance is a question, not an answer.*

Mara read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. The fox in the margin kept its painted eye fixed on them with an expression that looked, horribly, like pity.

"Heir," Mara repeated. The word tasted like a coin bitten between teeth. "Heir to what? Renewed or severed—the pact, what pact, nobody knows about any pact, the Wardens burned the last person who even mentioned—"

They stopped. Because a piece slid home, cold and heavy, into a place inside them that had been waiting for it without knowing: the Wardens' patrols, doubled these past two years. The questions at the low market about strangers with unusual talents. The way Captain Vess's soldiers had started searching carts not for contraband but for *books*, for *papers*, for anything old. Bram's warnings, lately, to keep the gift quieter than ever, quieter than ever before, *they're looking for something, Mara, and it isn't smuggling*.

They weren't hunting a codex.

They were hunting the person the codex described.

And here Mara stood, in the dead prophet's cellar, holding the evidence, standing next to the man who had led them to it—who had sought them out at the market, who had asked about orchids, who had known—

Mara looked up slowly. Ilex met their eyes, and she saw it land in him too, the understanding arriving a heartbeat late, the way thunder follows lightning.

"Mara," he said carefully, "how long have you been able to talk to animals?"

The rain began again above them, soft on a century of leaves. On the desk, in its jar, a seed that had waited a hundred years split its coat with a sound like a whisper, putting out a root toward the dark.

And in the margin of the page, beneath Mara's name, the ink shifted—subtly, like something breathing—though neither of them saw it happen.

* * *

They climbed back up into the rain without speaking. The door closed behind them with the softness of a held breath released—or held again. At the ruined gate, Ilex finally stopped, water running off his chin, and turned.

"I need you to know," he said, "that two weeks ago, before I ever saw you, I already knew your name."

Mara's blood went cold. "What?"

"Not yours specifically. The description. Born under the Thornfell sign, speaks with beasts, walks between—" He dragged a hand through his soaked hair. "There are collections. Private ones. Fragments of Sable's letters that survived the burnings. I've spent four years chasing them. Four years, Mara. And every fragment says the same thing: the heir exists, the heir is real, and the heir doesn't know what they are yet."

"So you found me at the market."

"So I found you at the market," he admitted. "And then you laughed at my joke about alkaline soil, and taught me which goat was lying to me about being pregnant, and stayed up half the night arguing about whether moss is a failure or an achievement—" His voice cracked, just slightly. "And somewhere in there I forgot I was supposed to be investigating a prophecy and started just... wanting to know you. Which may have been the plan all along, or may be the truest thing I've ever done. I genuinely don't know anymore. That's the truth. It's the only thing I have to give you."

The rain fell between them. Somewhere down-slope, a night bird went silent all at once, the way birds do when something passes that they don't like.

"You used me," Mara said. Flat. Not a question.

"I introduced myself to a prophecy," he said. "You're the part I didn't plan for."

Mara looked at him—at the ink stains, the honest terror, the ridiculous man standing in a dead woman's garden having led them to a buried book with their own name in it—and felt wonder and dread sitting in their chest like two birds in the same fist.

"I have to go," they said.

"Mara—"

"I have to go *now*." Because down the mountain, in the camp, were Bram and the twins and forty people who trusted them, and Mara had just understood, all at once, that invisibility was over—that the moment the Wardens confirmed the heir existed, everyone near them would stop being people and start being *leverage*. "Don't follow me. Don't write. Don't—"

*Ilex.* They almost said it softer. They swallowed it.

They ran.

Behind them, in the dark of the garden, the roses turned their black heads to watch them go, and the door under the mountain stayed warm, and patient, and closed.
