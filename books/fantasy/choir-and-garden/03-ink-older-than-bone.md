## 3. Ink Older Than Bone

Ilex Vane had lied to exactly three people in his life with any real commitment: his mother, about the university; Warden Captain Odile Vess, about everything; and now Mara Sterling, about the most important thing he'd ever done.

The lie was this: that he had stumbled into them.

The truth sat in his satchel in the ruin of Prophet Sable's garden, wrapped in oilcloth, still warm somehow—or Ilex imagined it warm, the way a confession is warm in the throat before you choke it out. The Vellum Codex lay six feet from where Mara had stood an hour ago, face gone white as paper-bark, staring at their own name written in iron-gall ink by a hand dead a hundred years.

*I will not run,* Mara had said. Not to him. To the book. Then they'd walked out of the garden without looking back, and Ilex had let them go, because following would have meant explaining, and explaining meant the truth, and the truth was a door he wasn't ready to open.

He'd been ready for two years, actually. That was the problem.

* * *

The garden at night was a different country than the garden at dusk. Ilex knew it the way he knew all growing things—by texture and patience. Prophet Sable's estate had been abandoned for four generations, but the plants hadn't received the news. They'd gone feral with purpose. Foxglove stood in ranks along the collapsed conservatory like sentries who'd forgotten which side they were on. The roses had swallowed a sundial whole.

Ilex knelt beside the spot where he'd first dug, three springs ago, following a footnote in a footnote in a banned botanical survey: *Sable's ledger reportedly interred beneath the western yew, planted—note well—on top of it, as if the tree were grown deliberately to hold the thing down.*

The yew's roots had cooperated beautifully. He'd only needed to ask.

That was the part that should have told him, early on, what he actually was. Ordinary botanists did not ask trees for favors and receive them.

He didn't dig again tonight. There was no point. Mara knew the book existed now; the codex would not stay buried, and neither, frankly, would Ilex's conscience, which had been clawing at the back of his ribs for eleven days—since the market at Coldbrook, since Mara had crouched in the mud to murmur something to a lame mule and the mule had stopped trembling, since Ilex had watched them do it and felt his heart perform a small, catastrophic maneuver that reconnaissance does not account for.

"You came back," said a voice from the foxglove. "Stupid."

Ilex did not startle, because startling was a luxury for people whose hearts weren't already hammering. "You came back too."

"I came back because I couldn't sleep." Mara stepped out of the green dark with their arms crossed, hair tied back wrong, the way people tie it when they've done it one-handed in the dark without a mirror. "You came back because you're the kind of person who returns to crime scenes."

"Botanist," Ilex said. "We return to specimens."

"Mm." Mara sat down on the sundial's pedestal, which the roses had left exposed like a pulled tooth, and looked at him across six feet of dead prophet's ground. Moonlight did Mara Sterling no favors and every favor at once: it sharpened the wariness in their face and made no progress whatsoever against the tiredness underneath. "So talk, Ilex. You have until my legs stop being brave."

* * *

Here is what Ilex had rehearsed, in the eleven days, in the three years, in the walk up from Coldbrook:

Here is what Ilex had planned to say: *I found a fragment of the codex in a Warden archive five years ago. A single page, describing a beast-speaker born under the Thornfell sign, in the month of deep snow, who would come to the garden when the foxglove bloomed twice in one year. I spent two years confirming the astronomical details. When the foxglove bloomed twice last spring—the second bloom in October, wrong season, wrong world—I began searching the mountain passes. Your reputation preceded you by a year. Smugglers' talk. A guide who could quiet wolves with a word. I arranged our meeting through the herbalist at Hollowmere because I knew your caravan bought willow bark there monthly. Every conversation we have ever had, I initiated on purpose. I am sorry. Also: you are named in a century-old prophecy, and I needed to know if it was true.*

Clean. Complete. Damning in a tidy way, the way a pressed specimen is damning.

What Ilex actually said, sitting in the dirt with his hands open on his knees like a man surrendering, was: "I chose you before I ever met you, and I need you to know that, and I need you to know I don't know what that means anymore."

Mara went very still. People who grow up invisible learn stillness the way soldiers learn drill. "Explain."

So he explained. It came out worse than the rehearsal and better than the truth usually does—haltingly, in the wrong order, with detours into botany that even he could hear were cowardice. The archive page. The Thornfell sign, that crooked constellation the mountain folk used to mark midwinter births. The description in Sable's cramped hand: *the heir will speak the older tongues; the beasts will gather to them unbidden; they will be known by walking unharmed among creatures that fear all other flesh.*

"The month of deep snow," Mara said, when he ran down. Their voice was flat in a way Ilex recognized from the mountains—the sound the air makes just before a slope gives. "I was born in deep snow. Under Thornfell's rise. My mother told me the sign was over the house the night I came."

"Yes."

"And the animals gathering unbidden." Mara laughed, one syllable, no humor anywhere in it. "Goats follow me. Goats, Ilex. Wolves have walked beside me since I was nine. I thought—" They stopped. Started again. "Everyone has always assumed I fed them. Left scraps. Trained them. I let them assume it, because the alternative was being the thing mothers pull children away from."

"I know."

"How do you know? How could you possibly—"

"Because I asked a tree to hold a book for me and it did." Ilex heard his own voice crack on the word *tree*, which was mortifying, and kept going anyway, because apparently tonight was for mortification. "Sable wasn't only a prophet. Sable was like us. Like you. The codex isn't prophecy the way the chapels sell it. It's testimony. Written by someone who spoke to growing things and heard them answer, and wrote down what they said would happen. And one of the things they said would happen is you."

The silence after that lasted long enough for an owl to cross the garden and consider them both and leave.

"Say the rest," Mara said finally. "There's a rest. I can see it behind your teeth."

"The rest is that I don't know when it stopped being reconnaissance." His hands had closed into fists without permission; he opened them deliberately, palms up, an old gesture from a greenhouse childhood—*see, nothing held.* "I can show you the letters. I wrote to myself, at first, keeping notes—*subject acquired trust, subject suspects nothing, day forty-one*—and somewhere around day sixty the entries change. Day sixty-one says: *they gave half their bread to a jay that didn't need it.* Day seventy says: *I think about them when they're not there.* I have read those pages a hundred times trying to find the exact line where I stopped hunting you and started—" He gestured helplessly at the garden, the moonlight, himself, the entire humiliating situation. "There isn't one. It's gradual. Like rot. Like rooting. Pick your metaphor; I've tried them all."

"Day sixty-one," Mara repeated.

"Give or take."

"You counted days."

"Botanists count everything," Ilex said. "It's how we pretend we're not feeling anything."

And then—this was the part he had not prepared for, the part no amount of rehearsal survives—Mara laughed. Really laughed, short and cracked and real, the first true laugh he'd gotten out of them in seventy-some days of deliberate cultivation and maybe ten of whatever the other thing was.

"You're terrible," they said. "You're the worst spy I have ever been hunted by."

"Reconnaissance," Ilex corrected weakly. "Spies get paid."

* * *

They talked until the moon moved. It went like this, in alternating currents: accusation and honesty, the way a river goes over stones.

Mara wanted to know who else knew. Ilex told the truth: nobody, entirely. Odile Vess knew the codex existed—he'd stolen his fragment from her own archive, which she either didn't know or was saving like a card, and with Vess those were the same thing. The exiled court knew a heir was coming; the spirits' rumors traveled faster than any post. But the name, the face, the specific person—until eleven days ago, that had lived only in Ilex's notebooks and Sable's buried pages.

"Now it lives in your head too," Mara said. "Congratulations. You've made me legible."

"That was never—" He stopped, because it was exactly what he'd done. "Yes. That's fair."

"What does the rest say?" Mara's chin tipped toward the satchel, toward the codex's memory. "The page you stole. After the birth-signs. What am I supposed to *do*, according to a dead gardener?"

Ilex weighed lying and found, with some surprise, that he didn't want to anymore—that the habit had come loose somewhere during the laughing, or maybe during the confessing, or maybe during day sixty-one, whenever that had actually taken root. "Renew or destroy," he said. "The pact between the mountain and what lives under it. Sable's words are careful—they always are—but the page reads like a scale. Two pans. You tip it one way or the other. There's no third option written."

"There's always a third option," Mara said. "It's just never the one anyone wrote down."

Something turned over in Ilex's chest. He thought, absurdly, of the foxglove blooming in October, out of season, out of agreement with every almanac—life refusing the schedule it was given. He thought: *that's what that sounds like.* He thought: *oh no. This is what it feels like from the inside.*

"Mara." He said their name and they flinched, very slightly, hearing it fresh—as if until tonight they hadn't known it was possible for him to say it and mean two things at once, or mean only one thing, honestly, whichever was worse. "I'm not going to ask you to believe the tender parts. I wouldn't. If someone told me they'd sought me out on purpose and then claimed the affection was real, I'd check my pockets. So check my pockets. Take the notebook—day sixty-one onward—and read it, and decide for yourself whether I'm a liar who got lucky or a fool who got found. Either way, I'm yours to judge."

"Yours to—" Mara shook their head slowly. "You realize what you've done. Don't you. Whoever wrote that page—Sable, whoever—they put you in it too, whether they named you or not. The person who finds the heir. There's always a finder. And finders don't get neutral endings."

"I know."

"Do you?" Mara stood, brushing soil from their knees with sharp, economical gestures. "Because I've spent my whole life making sure no one's story had room for me in it. Invisible. Useful. Gone by morning. And you've gone and inked me into a book, Ilex. Into *history*. Do you understand what happens to people in books? They burn. Bram taught me that—" They caught themselves on the name, shut their mouth, opened it again. "People die on pages. That's where the Wardens look first."

"Then we take you off the page."

"With what pen?"

Ilex looked at the yew tree. At the ground beneath it, where a dead prophet had planted a living lock over a book of futures. At Mara, standing in foxglove light with their whole careful life collapsing under them, still—God, still—asking good questions.

"I don't know yet," he admitted. "But I know a hundred ways living things rewrite themselves when the script doesn't suit. Grafts. Sports. Mutations. Nothing grows according to its label, Mara. Not even prophets."

For a long moment Mara studied him, and Ilex felt the full weight of being read—which, he was beginning to understand, was what it must feel like to be spoken to by a beast-speaker: seen through, catalogued, judged fit or unfit.

"Day sixty-one," Mara said at last. "I'll want that notebook. Tonight. Before I decide anything about you."

"It's at my lodgings in Hollowmere. I'll—"

"No." Mara's voice hardened, and beneath the hardness Ilex heard the other thing, the thing they both kept pretending wasn't running alongside the fear like a river under ice. "Together. You walk with me, in front of me where I can see your hands, and if you're lying—if any of this is still reconnaissance—"

"It isn't," Ilex said. "Or it is, and I'm reconnoitering my own heart, and I keep finding you in it, and frankly the survey results are devastating."

Mara stared at him. "That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's not." But their mouth was doing something at the corner that betrayed them, treacherous as any root system, and Ilex clung to that small mutiny like a botanist clinging to one green shoot in a burned field.

They gathered their things. Ilex shouldered the empty satchel—he'd send for the codex tomorrow, or better, tell Mara where it slept and let them choose—and fell into step behind them, then beside them, at their flat gesture, where they could see his hands.

At the garden gate Mara paused. Downhill, in the valley, a light was moving that shouldn't be: orange, steady, wrong. Not a lamp. Lamps wandered. This one stood in one place and grew.

"That's the waystation," Mara said. Their voice had gone very quiet, the way voices do when they arrive somewhere ahead of the mind. "That's Bram's light."

Then they were running, and Ilex ran after them—no longer, he understood, out of assignment, and never again out of assignment—down toward the fire that would make liars of them both, and orphans, and family, though none of them knew the last word yet.
