## 4. What the Wardens Burn

Mara smelled the smoke before the goats did.

That was the first wrong thing. The goats always smelled it first — they'd been screaming about the Warden patrol on the north switchback since noon, a chorus of flat, disgusted *bleats* that Mara had translated for Fen as *leather-men, many, coming slow*. The herd had opinions about Wardens. Most of them unprintable even in goat.

But this smoke was different. This smoke was coming from the waystation.

Mara crested the ridge above Hollowmere with a crate of contraband tea on their back and their heart already climbing into their throat. The waystation sat in its little bowl of valley like it had sat for a hundred years — stone walls, turf roof, the long stable, Bram's vegetable garden gone riotous with late summer — except now the turf roof was burning. Orange light licked out of the stable door. Figures moved through the yard in the grey evening: grey cloaks, grey helms, the cold discipline of the Crown's Wardens, moving through the smugglers' camp the way a comb moves through hair, slow and thorough and indifferent to what gets torn out.

"No," Mara said, to nobody. To the crate on their back. To the whole mountain. "No, no, no."

The goats, strung out behind them on the trail, went silent.

Mara had spent nine years learning how to be invisible. Not the spirit kind of invisible, not glamour or mist — the human kind, the patient kind. Keep your head down. Keep your gift quiet, because beast-speech was a hanging offense in three provinces and a pyre offense if the Wardens decided you were pact-touched. Take the routes nobody watched. Smile at the right people. Never be the most interesting thing in any room.

And for nine years it had worked. Mara Sterling, the quiet one, the one who carried crates and knew the goat trails and never asked questions. The camp's best guide and its most forgettable resident.

Now the most forgettable resident stood on a ridge watching Captain Odile Vess's raiders put torches to the only home they'd ever had, and understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, that invisibility had been a story they told themselves. The Wardens had come anyway. Because of them. Because of a name inked in a dead man's garden a century before Mara was born.

*The Beast-Tongued Heir.* Mara could still see the letters. Could still feel Ilex's hand shaking around the lantern.

Down in the yard, a Warden dragged Bram's cookpot into the fire and smashed it under his boot for no reason except that he could.

Mara put down the tea.

* * *

They went down the goat trail, which was the only way to go down fast without being seen, and the goats came with them, which Mara hadn't asked for and couldn't spare the breath to refuse. The herd flowed down the rocks around them like grey water, sure-footed, silent, and Mara realized distantly that the animals had made their own decision — that when Mara had turned toward the fire, every beast on the ridge had turned with them, because that was what the herd did with its strange, quiet, two-legged member. It followed.

*Not now,* Mara told them. *Please. Not now.*

The lead nanny — Bram called her Duchess, because she had the manners of one — butted Mara's hip, hard, and kept walking. Fine. Follow. But stay back from the fire.

The camp was half-empty, which was the only mercy in it. Most of the crew was two valleys over, running a silk shipment through the low passes. What remained was the skeleton crew: Bram, the twins off gathering, old Marta, three hired hands whose names Mara was ashamed to be struggling with even now, even running toward the flames. And the Wardens had picked the skeleton apart already. Marta and the hands knelt in the yard in a row, wrists bound, faces down, guarded by two raiders with crossbows. The rest were searching. Tossing bunks. Splitting mattresses. Looking for one thing.

A book.

"Captain." A young Warden came out of the stable, and his voice carried across the yard the way voices do at night, flat and clear. "Nothing in the loft. If there was a codex here, it's gone."

Odile Vess stood in the center of the yard with her hands clasped behind her back, watching her own bonfire with the expression of a woman checking a ledger for errors. She was smaller than Mara had imagined, from the stories. Neat. Grey-eyed. The kind of face that would be kind in a portrait and was terrible in person, because it was the face of someone who had never once doubted herself.

"Gone," Vess said, "or never here."

"Can't say, Captain."

"Then we'll ask." Vess turned to the kneeling row. She didn't raise her voice. She never would; Mara would learn that about her much later, and wish they hadn't. "Which of you is Mara Sterling?"

The yard went very quiet. Fire crackled. Somewhere up-valley a night bird called and got no answer.

Marta lifted her head. She was sixty and had hands like tree roots, and she looked up at Captain Vess the way you look at weather. "Don't know the name."

"No." Vess crouched, unhurried, and took Marta's chin in her gloved hand, tilting the old woman's face to the firelight. "But you know the face. Everyone here knows the face. The quiet one. The one who talks to animals." She said it mildly, almost kindly, and that was the worst part — the kindness in it. "I'm not here for you, grandmother. I'm here for a heretic. Help me find the heretic and you'll be home by morning. Refuse, and I burn this valley to the stone and start on the next one. I have warrants for both."

*She knows my name.* The thought went through Mara like ice water. Not a description. Not a rumor. The name. Which meant the codex wasn't the only thing that had leaked. Which meant someone had talked — Ilex, some part of Mara whispered, and another part said, *or the Wardens have their own readers, their own gardens, their own dead prophets* — and it didn't matter, because Marta was shaking her head and Vess was standing up and signaling to a soldier with a brand already glowing in the brazier by the stable door.

"Last chance," Vess said.

Mara was moving before they decided to move. That was the thing about nine years of invisibility: it turned out to be a kind of training too. They knew every shadow in that yard, every sightline, the exact spot where the turf wall's shadow swallowed a crouching person whole. They came down the last of the trail, through the fence gap behind the woodpile, and the goats — bless them, curse them, *love* them — chose that moment to spook, forty animals stampeding out of the dark and through the yard in a clatter of horns and screaming, because Mara had thought it at them, hard, the way you think at family: *now, run, now.*

The yard dissolved. Wardens shouted. A crossbow bolt went into the night at nothing. And in the chaos Mara was at Marta's side with a belt knife, sawing through her bonds.

"Mara." Marta's eyes were wet and furious. "You stupid, brave child. *Go.*"

"Come with me."

"I'm sixty-two and my knees are shot. I'll slow you to a walk and they'll take us both." Marta grabbed their wrist with those root-hard hands. "Bram's in the stable. Get him. He'll not leave otherwise, and he's too stubborn to burn."

*He'll not leave otherwise.* Because Bram had built that waystation with his own hands at nineteen, because he'd raised Mara in it from the age of eleven when they'd come down the mountain half-starved and mute, because Bram did not leave things. Mara kissed the top of Marta's head — she smelled like woodsmoke already, they all did — and ran for the stable.

* * *

The stable was burning from the inside. Of course it was. Vess's people had started there, Mara realized — the stable, the heart of the place, where the animals lived, where Bram lived above the tack room in a loft that smelled like hay and leather and the particular pipe-tobacco he'd smoked for forty years.

"Bram!" Mara got the side door open and the heat came out at them like a fist. "Bram, it's me, we're going —"

He was already at the loft ladder, coming down, which should have been a relief and was, for one whole second. Then Mara saw what he carried: not his go-bag, not the strongbox. A bundle wrapped in oilcloth, long and flat, and a short sword — the old one, the one he'd sworn he'd retired.

"Bram, what —"

"Found this under my floor this morning." He shoved the bundle into Mara's arms. It was heavy. It smelled of dust and old vellum. "Been there since before you were born. Sable's book, girl — boy — *Mara*. The one they're burning my home for. I should have burned it myself thirty years ago and I was a coward, and now look." His voice cracked, and not from the smoke.

"You knew? You knew about — the codex, the name, you knew and you never —"

"Didn't want it to be true." Bram's face was black with soot and shining with sweat, and behind him the loft collapsed inward in a bloom of sparks. "Wanted you to just be my kid. The one who talked to goats." He smiled, and it was the smile from a hundred mornings, porridge and trail-bread and *mind the Duchess, she bites*. "Still are. That's the whole prophecy as far as I'm concerned. Now *move*."

They got three steps toward the door.

The front of the stable lit up — not fire this time, but lantern-light, and the silhouette of a Warden in the doorway with a crossbow raised, and behind him two more, and beyond them the yard, and beyond the yard Vess's grey cloaks closing like a hand.

"Captain!" the Warden shouted. "The stable! I've got —"

Bram didn't hesitate. Mara would replay this for the rest of their life, and in every version, he didn't hesitate. He stepped past Mara, put his body in the doorway, and swung the old short sword in the flat, ugly, effective arc of a man who'd learned to fight before he'd learned to be gentle, and took the crossbowman's legs out from under him. The bolt went into the roof. The second Warden lunged. Bram caught the man's spear-shaft and *pulled*, and they went down together in the doorway, in the light, in full view of the whole yard.

"Bram —" Mara's voice came out of them in a scream they didn't recognize. "*Bram!*"

He was on his back under the Warden, and he looked up at Mara, at the bundle in their arms, and his soot-black face did the thing it did when he was telling Mara to do something and knew Mara would argue.

"Run, little one." Calm. Absolutely calm. The voice he'd used on their first night, eleven years old and mute with fear, when the whole camp had stared at the strange child who'd come down the mountain. "Run *now*. Don't look back. It's just a building. It's just an old man. You're the thing that matters."

"I can't — Bram, I can't just —"

"You can. You must. That's what family *is*, Mara — it's the people who run so the others don't have to." He grinned through the blood at the corner of his mouth. "Besides. I've been meaning to punch a Warden since I was your age. Consider this my retirement."

The third Warden reached the door. Bram turned to meet him, and Mara ran.

* * *

They ran. That was the whole of it, the whole truth Mara would carry up the mountain: they ran, and the goats ran with them, and behind them the waystation — the turf roof, the long stable, the garden with the beans Bram planted every year no matter what the altitude did to them — went up like a second sunset.

They ran until the trail forked at the old boundary cairn, where the smuggler paths went three ways and Mara knew all three, and then they stopped, because running wasn't a plan, and the bundle in their arms was heavier than any crate of tea had ever been.

Mara sat down on the cold stone of the cairn and unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that would not stop shaking.

The Vellum Codex. Sable's own book, hidden under Bram's floor for thirty years — or longer, maybe, maybe since Sable died, maybe Bram had inherited it the way he'd inherited everything, from someone who'd inherited it from someone, a chain of cowards and keepers going back a century. The cover was soft grey leather gone bald at the corners. The pages inside were vellum, cream and dense, and the handwriting was small and sure.

Mara didn't open it. Couldn't. They knew what was in it. They'd seen their own name in Ilex's lantern light, in ink older than their grandmother's grandmother, and they'd spent three days telling themselves it was a trick, a coincidence, a botanist's forgery — anything.

Now the Wardens had burned their home for it. Now Bram was dead for it. Now Marta knelt bound in a yard, and the twins would come back to ashes, and every soul in that camp was a hostage to a name Mara had never asked to carry.

"I didn't want this," Mara said aloud, to the book, to the mountain, to nobody. Their voice came out cracked and small. "I never *asked* for this."

The mountain said nothing. The mountain had never said anything; that was the cruelty of the gift. Beasts spoke. Stones didn't. And Mara had spent their whole life talking to everything that breathed because everything that breathed was better company than the silence of the people.

Except.

Except that on the ridge above them, in the dark, the goats had not left. All forty of them stood in a line along the skyline, Duchess in front, looking down at Mara with their horizontal pupils and their terrible patience, and Mara realized they could feel them — not words, not exactly, but the pressure of them, the attention, the way a room feels when people are waiting for you to speak.

*Well?* said the pressure of forty goats, which is not a sentence but is unmistakably a question. *What now?*

Mara looked back down the valley, at the glow of their burning childhood. Somewhere in that glow, Bram was dead. Somewhere in it, Marta was in chains, and Vess was reading her ledger and planning the next valley.

Mara looked up the mountain, into the dark above the tree line, where the paths stopped being paths and the stories stopped being stories. Where the old things lived. The ones the codex called the spirit-court. The ones who, if even half the legends were true, had been waiting a hundred years for the Beast-Tongued Heir to come home.

Every instinct Mara had — nine years of invisibility, nine years of *keep your head down* — said: go low, go sideways, go to the low passes and the sea and never stop moving.

But invisibility had failed. It had failed Bram, who was dead. It had failed Marta, who was chained. It had failed everyone who'd ever shared a fire with Mara Sterling, and it would keep failing them, one burned waystation at a time, until Mara stopped being invisible and became something else instead.

"Okay," Mara said. To the goats. To the book. To Bram, wherever he was. "Okay. Up, then."

They stood, and wrapped the codex back in its oilcloth, and slung it on their back where the tea had been, and started climbing.

Behind them, the goats came down off the skyline and followed.

Ahead, above the tree line, something in the dark was already watching — Mara could feel it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck — something old, and patient, and *delighted*.
