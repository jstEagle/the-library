## 5. The Court of Roots and Teeth

Mara climbed for two days.

They didn't decide to. That was the strange part—after the waystation burned and Bram went down under Warden steel with his hand still shoved against Mara's chest, *go*, the body simply chose. Feet found the old goat-trail above the treeline while the mind stayed behind in the smoke, replaying the sound Bram made when the crossbow bolt took him. Not a cry. A grunt, like a man lifting something heavy. Like it was work. He'd spent forty years making himself into a wall between other people and trouble, and he died doing it without ceremony, and Mara had run because he told them to, and they hated themselves for obeying so easily.

So: climbing. Two days. The Thornfell peaks wore their snow like old men wear hats, crooked and permanent, and above the treeline the world narrowed to stone and wind and lichen the color of bruises. Mara knew these trails the way most people knew their own kitchens—not from maps but from conversations. The ibex had shown them which ledges held. The ravens had mapped the thermals. Once, years ago, an old chamois doe had walked them three miles out of her way to show them a cave where the wind couldn't reach, and had refused every offer of payment except a scratch behind the ears.

Mara thought about that doe now and realized, with a lurch, that they hadn't heard anything since the fire. No birdsong commentary. No marmots whistling warnings from the scree. The mountain had gone quiet around them the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in.

Or when something is listening.

The trail bent through a saddle between two crags, and Mara stopped, because ahead the trail simply wasn't there anymore. Not washed out, not blocked. *Absent.* Where yesterday there had been forty feet of switchback down into a hanging valley, there was now a wall of pine—black pines, wrong pines, growing at an altitude no pine could survive, packed so tight their trunks touched. The wind dropped all at once, like a door closing.

"Ah," Mara said aloud, because their voice was the only company left, and even it sounded small. "That's new."

The trees parted.

Not like a gate opening. Like a throat. The trunks leaned inward and the dark between them deepened until it wasn't shadow but absence, and cold rolled out of it carrying a smell Mara had no name for—green sap and iron blood and something older underneath, the smell of the inside of the mountain itself. Every hair on their arms stood up. Their gift, useless silent gift, suddenly screamed: not words, just pressure, like standing too close to a waterfall.

*Run,* said every instinct Mara owned.

But Bram was dead, and the waystation was ash, and Vess's Wardens were combing the lower valleys with fire-cant lanterns, and downhill was death by law while uphill was only death by mystery. Besides—and this was the thought that finally moved their feet—the quiet hadn't felt hostile. It had felt like waiting.

"I'm coming," Mara told the darkness. "You don't have to swallow me whole. I walk fast."

Something in the dark laughed. It was not an unkind laugh. It was worse than that—it was a *delighted* laugh, the laugh of someone who has waited a long time for exactly this.

Mara walked into the trees.

* * *

Inside, it was summer.

That was the second impossible thing. Outside the saddle, it was late autumn and the frost came at dusk; inside the pines, the air hung warm and gold, thick with pollen and the drone of bees, and the ground was carpeted in moss soft as bedsheets. Light fell in shafts though Mara could see no gaps in the canopy. And the trees—up close, the trees had *faces*. Not carved faces, not faces like humans imagine them. Faces the way bark naturally knots and folds, except here the knots were eyes and the folds were mouths, and every one of them was turned toward Mara as they passed.

They tried the gift on instinct, reaching out the way they'd reached toward goats and goshawks their whole life, expecting the usual half-speech, the sense-impressions and emotional weather.

What came back was a sentence. Whole. Articulate. In their own skull's voice, which was somehow the most frightening version possible:

*The heir walks among us.*

"I'm not—" Mara started, then stopped, because arguing with a forest seemed unwise, and also because they'd been saying *I'm not* for three days now—to Ilex, to themselves, to Bram's memory—and it had gotten them nothing but ash. They kept walking instead.

The path descended gently, though they'd entered going up, which said everything about how much geography mattered here. The moss gave way to grass, the grass to a meadow, and the meadow opened into a hollow ringed by the black pines, and in the center of the hollow stood the court.

Mara had grown up on smuggler tales of the spirit-court, and every tale disagreed. Hesper—no, not Hesper, that came later—Bram had described them as lights. Old Tamsin swore they were animals grown huge and wrong. One drunk carter insisted they were beautiful beyond bearing and that he'd wept for a week. They were all of them correct and none of them prepared Mara for what they saw: figures that were rooted and moving at once, tall as the pines, their bodies shifting between forms the way water shifts between shapes—a woman whose hair was a swarm of moths, a knight whose armor was interlocking beetle-shell, children made of foxfire who chased each other through the air and left streaks of green light. Antlered. Flowering. Moss-shouldered. Every one of them wearing the mountain like clothing.

And on a throne of woven roots at the far side of the hollow, the queen.

She looked, first, like a briar rose grown to the size of a cathedral pillar, thorns running down her arms like a ladder, blossoms opening along her collarbone in slow succession, red as warning. Then she looked like a woman—tall, gray-skinned as weathered wood, with eyes the exact amber of tree resin with something ancient caught inside. Both things at once. Neither thing. She did not blink, and Mara understood after a moment that she had no need: the whole grove was her attention. Every face in every trunk. She was looking at Mara from everywhere.

"Beast-tongued," the queen said. Her voice came from the throne, from the pines, from the moss underfoot, from somewhere behind Mara's teeth. "A century we have waited in exile, and the mountain sends us a smuggler."

"Mara," they said. Their voice cracked on the second syllable. "Just Mara. Not—whatever word you're using. And nobody sent me. I'm running from people who burned my home."

"Yes." Something moved across the resin-dark eyes that might, in a human face, have been pity. "We watched. We are sorry for the old man. He fed the crows, you know, and the crows remember him fondly, and the crows tell us everything." She rose from the root-throne, and the motion took too long, joints unfolding like branches straightening over decades. "Come forward, child of the trails. You have read your name in Sable's book. Let us hear what you think it means."

Mara's feet carried them forward without consultation. Around the hollow, the court had gone still—all those shifting forms frozen mid-transformation, dozens of eyes (some of them actual eyes, some of them knots in wood, some of them flowers turning on their stems) fixed on the space Mara occupied.

"You know about the codex," Mara said.

"We know about everything written in that garden. Sable wrote it with our leave and our ink. The ink was ours, beast-speaker. What do you think pressed the pages? What do you think whispered the verses?" The queen smiled, and her teeth were thorns. "The prophecy is ours as much as yours. More. It was our last act before the pact-bound kings drove us into this hollow and salted the doors behind us."

"So it's real." Mara's mouth had gone dry despite the summer air. "The Beast-Tongued Heir. Renew or sever. That's—it's actually about me."

"It is about the blood," the queen corrected, gliding closer, and Mara noticed that where she stepped, the grass flowered and then frosted, both in the same instant, as if she couldn't decide which season to bring. "Sable saw the line of speakers running down the mountain's western slope like a vein of ore. Thin. Scattered. Nearly pinched out. But one thread runs true, born under the Thornfell sign with the old tongue still wet in its throat." She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Mara could smell her—roses and cold iron. "One thread, smuggler. You."

"I guide cargo," Mara said. "Goats, mostly. Sometimes brandy."

The court laughed. It was a terrible sound, beautiful and layered, moth-wing and birdsong and grinding stone, and it stopped the instant the queen lifted one thorned finger.

"Humility is a human virtue. We do not require it." She circled Mara slowly, and the temperature swung with her—warm, cold, warm. "Hear the terms of the old pact, since no one living has spoken them truly. When the first men came to Thornfell, they starved. The mountain's elder powers—my predecessors, my kin—fed them, sheltered them, taught them which berries kill and which streams run clean. In return, mankind bound itself: a share of every harvest left on the field, a word spoken at every threshold, and one speaker in each generation to carry the voices between. Not a master of beasts. A *translator*. A bridge."

"And the bridge broke," Mara said quietly.

"The bridges broke. Generation by generation, your people forgot. The offerings became superstition, then habit, then nothing. The speakers dwindled to goat-charmers and cattle-whisperers, hiding their gift like a disease, because your Wardens burn what they cannot tax." Her voice hardened, and every blossom on her body snapped shut at once. "A century ago we protested. Your kings called it rebellion. They drove us here, into Sable's warded garden-hollows, exiles in the spine of our own mountain. So yes, smuggler. The codex speaks true. The heir may renew the pact—or sever it. Speak the Severance, and the spirits walk free of every binding, free to take back what was always ours. Free to make Thornfell *ours* again."

"And everyone in the valleys?" Mara asked. They already knew. They'd known since childhood, in the buried place where they kept the knowledge they couldn't afford to look at. "The villages. The farms. Everyone downstream of 'free.'"

The queen's silence lasted exactly long enough to be an answer.

"There is a price to every freedom," she said at last. "Yours paid it for a century of comfort."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer kings ever give, and you will notice they sleep well." She returned to the throne and seated herself, and the court relaxed fractionally around the hollow, like a held breath half-released. "But. Hear me. We are not your kings. We would rather not drown the valleys in roots and teeth—we are old, and tired, and we remember being loved here. Which is why the codex offers a second road, and why I am offering you more than prophecy offers."

She extended one hand. On her palm lay a circlet—no, not a circlet. A thin band of braided briar, thornless, faintly glowing, small enough to circle a wrist or a brow.

"Wear this, and the Severance can never pass your lips unwilling. Wear this, and the court stands beside you against Vess and her fire-cant and her burning waystations. Wear this, and no Warden blade touches you; no spirit hand turns against you. Renewal, protection, vengeance for your dead—" the amber eyes held Mara's, patient as geology, "—all of it, freely given. All we ask is that you carry our voice. As the old speakers did. As the bridge."

Mara looked at the band. It was lovely. It was the most beautiful object they had ever seen, and they had seen the codex with their own name inked in century-old brown.

Their whole life they had been useful and invisible. Guides who belonged to no caravan. Voices that belonged to no conversation. And here, at the bottom of their grief, something enormous and radiant was saying *you are the one we've waited for*, and every lonely year of Mara's life leaned toward that sentence like a plant toward light.

Which was, they understood dimly, exactly the problem.

"The old speakers," Mara said. "Did they wear bands like that?"

"Some did. Willingly."

"And the others?"

The queen smiled again, thorns and roses. "The others were persuaded. It is very persuasive."

Mara thought of Bram's hand shoving them uphill. Thought of Ilex's face in the garden lamplight, saying *I sought you out* —the tenderness and the reconnaissance tangled past untangling. Thought of the way every family they'd ever guided had thanked them warmly and never once asked them to stay.

Everyone wanted to hold the leash. The Wardens wanted it. The prophecy wanted it. And now the beautiful exile-queen with her summer hollow and her watching trees.

Being chosen, Mara thought, felt a great deal like being owned. The only difference was the quality of the cage.

"No," they said.

The hollow went silent in a way that had weight, like the moment before an avalanche.

"Not yet," Mara amended, because Bram had taught them never to insult a client, and queens were clients of a kind. "I'll carry messages. I'll speak for the court when speaking serves us both. But I don't wear leashes, and I don't say your Severance, and I don't take sides in a war I haven't finished reading. Sable wrote that book as a warning—Ilex thinks it's a command, Vess thinks it's a license, and you think it's a deed of ownership. Maybe it's none of those. Maybe I get to find out before anyone puts anything on my wrists."

For a long moment the queen regarded them, and the amber eyes churned like sap catching fire.

Then she laughed—one short note, genuine, startlingly young.

"A century," she murmured, "and the mountain sends us a smuggler with a spine of hawthorn." She closed her hand over the briar band, and it sank into her palm like water into soil. "Very well, beast-speaker. No leash. Yet. But hear this: Vess has taken your healer's inn, or will within the week—her fire moves faster than grief. The ones you love are gathering in a hollow tree on the eastern slope, and they are gathering *because of you*, and they will die there unless someone warns them that the Wardens' route runs through the Goat-Knows trails."

Mara's stomach dropped. "How do you know about—"

"We are the mountain," the queen said simply. "Everything that walks on us, we feel." She gestured, and the black pines at the far edge of the hollow leaned apart, opening onto grey ordinary twilight, real altitude, real cold. "Go, then. Run your errands of loyalty. But know that you run them as our heir whether you accept the name or not—the prophecy does not require your consent, only your tongue. And when you have saved your strays, come back to us. We have waited a hundred years. We can wait a little longer."

Mara walked to the edge of the hollow, then stopped.

"For what it's worth," they said, not turning around, "the crows liked Bram. If you really want renewal someday—start by being worth liking. He'd tell you the same."

Behind them, the whole grove rustled. It might have been wind.

It might have been applause.

Mara stepped out into the cold and ran east, toward the hollow tree, toward the strays, downhill into the smoke that was already rising on the horizon.
