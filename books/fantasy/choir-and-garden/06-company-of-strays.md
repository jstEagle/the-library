## 6. Company of Strays

### Mara

The hollow tree announced itself three ways: first by the goats, who stopped dead and stared; then by a smell of yarrow and woodsmoke that had no business in the middle of a burned clearcut; and finally by a door, set into bark so thick it had forgotten it was ever alive.

"You're sure about this place?" Fen said behind me. She'd been asking variations of that question since Bram died. I think she needed to hear someone say *yes* out loud, even if she didn't believe them.

"No," I said. "But Hesper's name was on Bram's list of last resorts, and we've run through all the earlier ones."

I knocked twice, paused, then once—the rhythm Bram had drummed into every runner: *friend coming slow, friend carrying hurt.*

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared in the gap, gray as slate weather, taking inventory of us: me, still wearing Bram's blood on my sleeve; Fen and Rook flanking like bookends made of knives; and Ilex at the back, hands raised slightly, the way you stand when everyone's decided what you probably are.

"We're closed," the eye said.

"Bram Weller sent us."

A long silence. Somewhere inside, water dripped into a basin, patient as a clock.

"Bram," the voice said, "is a fool who's been dead ten years as far as the Wardens know."

"He's dead now for real," I said. "Captain Vess burned the waystation tonight. She took his ledger. His actual last words were *go to Hesper*, which I'll admit he shouted while shoving me out a window, but I don't think he was being poetic."

The chain rattled. The door opened the rest of the way, and there stood a woman built like a root cellar—short, broad, silver-streaked hair cut brutally short, and hands that hung at her sides with the particular stillness of people who have done surgery on other people and not enjoyed all of it.

She looked at my sleeve. At Rook, who'd taken a crossbow bolt graze across the ribs and was holding himself upright mostly through spite.

"Inside," she said. "Touch nothing green. And you—" this to Ilex, "—botanist. You'll sleep near the door where I can hear you breathe."

"That seems fair," Ilex said.

Inside, the tree swallowed us. The hollow went up and up, shelves cut into living wood, bundles of herbs hanging from rafters of heartwood, and everywhere—everywhere—plants. Moss on the walls like velvet upholstery. A fern growing out of a knothole at eye level. Something flowering pale and sweet-smelling in a pot by the fire, turning its face toward us as we passed.

"It listens," Hesper said, catching me looking. "Don't say anything you'd regret. That's house rule one."

Rook made it exactly four steps before his legs quit on him. Hesper caught him under the arms with an ease that would have insulted him if he'd been conscious enough to notice, and laid him on a cot near the fire, and had his shirt open and the wound cleaned before Fen finished telling her not to touch him.

"Bolt went through meat, not gut," she announced. "He'll live to be rude again by morning." She threaded a needle without looking at it. "Who stitched this first attempt? A drunk cobbler?"

"I did," Fen said, chin up.

Hesper grunted. "Keep the tension even next time and stop apologizing to him while you work. It distracts your hands."

Fen opened her mouth, found nothing to say, and closed it again. In fifteen years of knowing the twins, I had never seen anyone win an exchange with either of them that fast.

I sat against the wall with my knees drawn up and watched the fire, and the fire watched me back—I could feel it, the faint animal attention of the flame, wanting to be fed, wanting to spread, saying nothing useful. Everything in this room wanted something. The moss wanted damp. The fern wanted light. The pale flower wanted—I turned my head toward it and got a wash of feeling so simple it stopped my breath.

It wanted company.

"Hey," Hesper said, crouching in front of me with a cup. "Drink. You look like a woman deciding whether to exist."

I drank. It was willow and something bitter and honey, and it hit the grief sitting in my chest like rain on a hot road.

"They'll come looking," I said. "Vess doesn't burn a waystation and stop."

"I know." Hesper sat down beside me, not too close, the way healers learn to sit. "Bram always did collect strays and hand them my direction. Thirty years ago it was injured hawks. Now it's apparently the end of the world." She nodded toward Ilex, asleep already or pretending, curled near the door with their satchel for a pillow. "You trust the botanist?"

"No."

"Good. Trust is for after." She pulled a blanket over my shoulders like I was twelve. "Sleep. The tree watches the treeline better than any of you would."

### Ilex

I did not sleep. I inventoried.

This had been my method since the academy: when fear arrived, make lists. *Satchel: intact. Codex: hidden in false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth. Cover story: thinning. Value as ally: declining by the hour.*

Mara Sterling slept against the wall looking like a person who had been carrying the whole mountain on their shoulders and had only just now been informed of it. The healer—Hesper, and the name had gone through me like cold water when Mara said it, because I *knew* that name, from Vess's own files, from a case closed and buried eleven years ago—sat beside them doing nothing, which was its own kind of skill.

I had come to Thornfell with a commission and a plan. Find the beast-speaker described in Sable's codex—the description fit perhaps six people in a generation, and I had narrowed it to one within a season. Flirt. Observe. Confirm. Report to no one, technically; my commission had lapsed when I left the capital, but habits of mind are harder to leave than institutions.

Somewhere between the third stolen evening in Sable's garden and the moment Mara's face went white over that page of ink, my lists had stopped adding up. *Flirtation: reconnaissance, originally. Current status: unclear.* There was no column for the way it felt when Mara laughed at my terrible jokes about moss taxonomy—genuinely laughed, once, the sound surprising both of us—and no ledger line for how much I'd hated myself for having engineered the conditions of that laugh.

Now their smugglers bled on cots, their mentor burned, and every hour I stayed, my presence made them easier to find. Vess knew my methods. Vess knew my handwriting. Vess knew I kept letters, because she'd taught me to keep letters.

"You're thinking loudly," Hesper said, without turning around. She was grinding something in a mortar, slow and even. "House rule two. Think quietly."

"My apologies."

"Mm. You're the one they wrote about in the files. Pemberton's student. The one who walked out of the Wardens' botanical division and vanished into the hills cataloguing weeds." She glanced over her shoulder. "Vanished cataloguing *us*, more likely."

"The distinction escaped me at the time," I said.

"And now?"

"Now I'm sleeping by the door where you can hear me breathe," I said. "Which I understood to be your judgment on the matter."

She considered me with those slate eyes. Healers' eyes—they take you apart to see what's wrong, and they're honest about what they find, including when the answer is *nothing fixable from the outside.*

"Bram vouched for worse," she said finally. "Get some sleep, spy. If you're going to betray these children, at least be rested enough to feel bad about it properly."

That night I dreamed of the garden, and in the dream the prophet Sable stood among the ruined roses saying nothing, only watching me with Mara's face, and I woke before dawn with my heart going like a rabbit's and the certain knowledge that whatever I told myself about reconnaissance, the word had stopped being true some weeks ago, and everything that came next would depend on whether I admitted it in time.

### Fen

Fen counted exits. Always had, since she was small enough that Rook had to carry her over the high passes. This tree had four: the front door, the root-cellar hatch, the chimney—suicidal, but present—and a knothole two body-widths up the inside that a determined person and a motivated person could squeeze through, meaning her and not Rook.

Four exits. Two strangers she didn't trust, one stranger she trusted less, and Sterling, who had gotten their mentor killed and hadn't said more than ten words since the waystation burned.

She sat across the cold hearth from Mara in the gray before dawn, whittling a stick into nothing, waiting until their eyes met.

"Talk to me about the plan, cargo."

"Don't call me that."

"You carried our goods up the mountain for years and never let us carry anything for you. That makes you cargo." She shaved another curl of wood. "So. Plan. Vess has your name in a prophecy book and Bram's ledger besides, which means she knows every cache, every contact, every farmer who leaves a lamp burning for runners. We can't go low. We can't stay here forever. So?"

Mara looked at their hands. "I don't have one yet."

"Great. Terrific." Fen pointed the knife at Ilex, who had gone very still by the door. "And this one? Because I've seen the way you two look at each other, and I've also seen the way this one lies, and I want to know which one is the act."

"All of it was real," Ilex said quietly. "That's what I keep trying to explain. That's what makes it unforgivable."

"Huh," Fen said. It was, she had to admit, the most honest answer she'd heard in days. "See, that you admit that buys you maybe a week of me not smothering you in your sleep."

"Fen." Rook's voice from the cot, rough with sleep and painkillers. "Let the nice spy finish breakfast first."

"You're supposed to be unconscious."

"I'm multitalented."

She went back to her stick. The truth was—and she'd have chewed off her own arm before saying it—that watching Mara sit there hollowed out and admitting they had no plan had scared her worse than the fire had. For nine years, whenever the passes closed, whenever the patrol boats came up the river, whenever things went wrong in ways money couldn't fix, it had been Sterling who fixed them. Sterling who talked the mule train calm. Sterling who knew where the goats would go before the goats did. Sterling who belonged to everybody and nobody, and Fen had never once thought about what that cost until last night, seeing them shoved out a window by a man dying so they wouldn't die too.

"Fine," she said. "New arrangement. You don't get to be cargo anymore. You're crew. And crew means when you're drowning, you tell somebody." She jabbed the knife into the hearthstone and left it standing there. "That's the toll. Pay it or walk."

Silence. Even the plants seemed to lean in.

"Okay," Mara said. Barely a sound.

"Okay," Fen repeated, disgusted with how relieved she felt. "Somebody wake me when breakfast happens." She lay down next to her brother's cot, back to the room, and pretended very hard that her eyes weren't stinging.

### Rook

Rook woke properly at noon, ribs screaming, to the smell of actual soup and the sight of the strangest table of people he'd ever shared bread with.

At the head, the healer-woman, Hesper, eating like a soldier: fast, thorough, watching everyone. To her right, Sterling, who looked like they'd slept for the first time in years and hated it. Then the botanist, Ilex, elegant even while eating stew, hands never far from that satchel. Across from Rook, Fen, radiating the specific satisfaction of a person who has recently yelled at somebody and considers it handled.

"So," Rook said. "The gang's all here. What's the job?"

"There is no job," Fen said.

"There's always a job. Question's just whether we picked it or it picked us." He accepted soup from Hesper with a nod that cost him something around rib number four. "My money says it picked us. Go on, Sterling. What's the shape of it?"

Mara set down their spoon. Rook had known them almost a decade, and he'd catalogued every flavor of their silences the way other men collected coins. This one was new. This one was a person deciding how much truth a room could hold.

"There's a book," Mara said. "A hundred years old. It says things about me. About—" a nod at Hesper, "—you, apparently. It says I can either renew an old pact with the mountain's spirits or destroy it, and the Crown wants to burn the book and everyone in it, and the spirits want me to speak their side of it, and both of them are willing to kill to make sure I choose correctly."

Rook chewed on that along with his bread. "And what do *you* want?"

The table went quiet. Fen stared at him. Ilex's spoon paused halfway to their mouth. Even Hesper looked interested.

"What?" Rook said. "Nine years I've watched everyone ask Sterling what they can *do*. Nobody's asked what they want. Seems like relevant information, given the armies."

Mara's throat worked. When they spoke, their voice came out cracked and then steadied. "I want Bram's death to mean something. I want Vess to lose. And I want—" they stopped, started again. "I want to choose something for the first time in my life instead of being chosen. That's what I want."

"Well," Rook said, and lifted his bowl. "Then we'd best keep you alive long enough to do it. To the cargo."

"To crew," Fen corrected, clashing her bowl against his hard enough to slop soup.

Ilex hesitated, then raised theirs. Hesper rolled her eyes at all of them—but she touched her cup to the center of the table anyway, and Rook figured that counted double, coming from her.

Later, when the others argued maps, Hesper caught Rook's eye across the room and tilted her head toward the herb shelf, where a bundle of dried feverfew hung wrapped in paper. Paper that had writing on it. Writing that, even upside down, Rook could see was old and careful and not herbal notes at all.

"Found that sewn into the wall when I bought the place," Hesper murmured, passing close. "Page from a book. Names a healer who opens doors for the hunted." Her jaw tightened. "Been sitting on it eleven years, telling myself it was coincidence. It isn't, is it."

"No," Rook said softly. "I don't suppose it is."

Outside, far downslope, a hawk screamed—a specific scream, a warning-shape, the kind Sterling had taught the twins to recognize years ago. Mara's head came up sharply, and the color drained out of their face.

"That's not a hawk," they said. "That's a goat lying about being a hawk. Someone taught it that signal." They were already on their feet. "Someone who knows my codes. We have maybe twenty minutes."

Hesper was blowing out lamps. Fen had her knife. And Rook, reaching for his boots and swearing at his ribs, thought: *there's the job.*
