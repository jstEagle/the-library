## 8. Goats Know Best

Fen didn't trust the beast-speaker. That was the honest truth of it, the truth she kept behind her teeth like a coin she wasn't ready to spend.

She trusted Mara's feet, sure—anyone who'd walked Thornfell's smuggler paths as long as Mara had earned that much. She even trusted Mara's silences, which were rarer and worth more than most people's words. But trusting a person's feet and trusting their heart were two different climbs, and Fen had been burned on both before.

So when Rook proposed it—grinning that gap-toothed grin he thought made him look harmless, which had never once worked—she said yes before Mara could.

"Goat-trails," Rook said, crouched by the cold fire ring in the hollow tree inn's yard, drawing lines in the ash with a twig. "The old ways. The ones only goats know."

Hesper, who was inside pretending not to listen while she sorted herbs with more clatter than necessary, snorted loud enough to hear through the wall.

"The Wardens watch the passes," Rook went on. "They don't watch the goat-trails because nobody but goats uses them. If we're going to move the codex—and we are going to move the codex, because I'm not sleeping another night under this roof with that thing breathing at us from the shelf—we need paths no Warden has ever bothered to map."

"And you want to test her," Fen said. Not a question. Rook's schemes always had a second layer, and the second layer was usually the real one.

"I want to *see*," Rook said, which was worse. "Everyone keeps saying what she is. Beast-speaker, heir, cargo we have to haul uphill. Nobody's asked what she can actually do. So let's ask. Let's put her somewhere she can't fake it."

Mara stood at the edge of the firelight, arms crossed, face doing that thing where it became a locked door. Fen watched them take it in—the scheme, the test, the insult folded inside the invitation like a stone inside bread—and waited for the refusal.

"All right," Mara said.

Fen blinked. "Just like that?"

"You were going to do it whether I agreed or not." Mara's mouth twitched, almost a smile, gone again before it landed anywhere. "Better I know the trail than wake up halfway down one someday."

*There,* Fen thought. *That's the thing about her.* Even cornered, she made it sound like her idea. Fen had met a hundred smugglers who talked big and died small, and exactly none of them had that particular stillness. It wasn't arrogance. It was something else. Something Fen hadn't named yet and resented having to think about.

* * *

They left at grey dawn, three of them, leaving Hesper minding the inn and the codex wrapped in oilcloth and buried under turnips in the cellar. Fen carried rope and the good knife. Rook carried his grin and an unearned confidence in his own ankles. Mara carried nothing but a water skin and that same closed-door expression.

The first trail started behind a fallen fir where the hillside looked like nothing at all—a seam in the rock, barely wider than Fen's shoulders, climbing at an angle that made her thighs burn inside twenty minutes. Goats could dance up it. Humans had to apologize their way.

"Who found this?" Mara asked from above her, voice steady despite the scramble.

"Our father," Rook called back. "Traded a keg of plum brandy to a shepherd for it. Shepherd swore it came from the goats themselves—watched them go up one morning when the pass below was crawling with Wardens."

"He never used it," Fen added, pulling herself over a lip of stone. "Said a path that belongs to animals isn't safe for men. Superstitious old—"

She stopped, because Mara had stopped, three switchbacks above, head tilted in a way Fen was starting to recognize.

"What?"

"Shh." Mara held up a hand. Then, softer than breath: "*You first, then. Show us.*"

Fen heard nothing. Wind in the scree, water somewhere under the rock, Rook's boots scraping. But she saw the change come over the slope above them—a ripple in the loose grey grass, and then the goats.

Six of them, wild and shaggy, horn-curved like question marks, standing along the ridgeline looking down at the three humans with the flat contempt of their kind. Fen's hand went to her knife out of pure habit. Mountain goats near a trail meant either trouble or a shortcut, and usually both.

But they weren't fleeing. They stood there, chewing, watching Mara the way merchants watch a customer counting coins.

"They say the trail forks ahead," Mara translated, voice low and strange with concentration. "Left side washed out two winters ago—there's a hollow under the scree. Right side holds, if we keep to the wall and don't trust the third ledge. The third ledge lies."

"The ledge *lies*?" Rook said.

"That's what they said." Mara shrugged. "I don't argue with goats. It's undignified for everyone."

They took the right fork. At the third ledge—which did, in fact, dip treacherously outward over a drop that would kill a person as thoroughly as anything in Thornfell could—Mara pressed a palm flat against the rock for a moment, murmuring something too quiet to catch, and stepped across without hesitation. Fen followed, hating how much easier it was to follow someone who knew things.

By noon they'd crossed a shoulder of the mountain that appeared on no map Fen had ever stolen or seen, dropping into a green hanging valley where the air smelled like snowmelt and sheep had clearly never set foot. Wild goats grazed the far slopes in scattered dozens.

Rook flopped onto the grass, winded and delighted. "Right. Good start. Now the hard part."

Fen felt her stomach tighten. "Rook."

"We need to know if she talks to everything," he said, "or just the friendly stuff. Goats are easy. Goats are basically people with better balance. What happens when it's something that doesn't want to talk? Something with opinions about us?" He sat up, and for once there was no grin under it. "Because Vess is coming. We all feel it. And when she comes, we won't get to pick our company. We need to know if our beast-speaker can handle a mountain that hates being disturbed."

Mara had gone very still, sitting on a stone at the valley's edge, and Fen realized the test had already worked—had maybe been working since dawn. Every word Mara had spoken to the goats today had cost something visible. A tightening around the eyes. A slight lean, like a person carrying water.

"It takes it out of you," Fen said. Not asking permission anymore.

"Yes."

"How much?"

Mara considered the question honestly, which Fen appreciated and hated in equal measure. "Enough that I've spent my whole life rationing it. Enough that I learned to be quiet so I'd never owe anyone an explanation for why I was tired."

*Rationing,* Fen thought. Like a drunk hiding bottles. Like a girl hiding a bruise. Like every smuggler who ever hid the thing that got them paid.

"Then here's the last part of the test," Rook said gently, and pointed east, where the valley climbed into black crags streaked with old slide-scars. "The Staircase. Goat-trail the whole way, straight through the worst ground on this flank. If we can cross it with you talking to the mountain instead of around it—" He shrugged. "Then you're not cargo. You're crew."

Mara looked at the crags a long moment. Then nodded once, and stood.

* * *

The Staircase earned its name. Switchbacks of broken stone stacked like a giant's discarded masonry, climbing between walls of crag that shed grit with every gust. The goats had led them to its foot—an escort now, a dozen strong moving along the slopes above like grey shadows—and Fen noticed Mara speaking to them less and less as they climbed. Conserving. Rationing.

Halfway up, the mountain spoke first.

It wasn't sound, exactly. It was a shiver through the boot-soles, a fine rain of dust off the crag walls, a wrongness in the way the loose stone settled under their weight. Fen froze with her hand flat on the rock and felt the mountain deciding something.

"Slide," she breathed.

"*Move!*" Rook was already lunging for the wall, and Fen grabbed his pack strap and threw him toward an overhang as the world above them came apart.

Not the whole mountainside. Just enough. A throat-clearing of the crags, a few tons of it, stone and scree pouring down the switchback in a brown roaring wave that swallowed the trail behind them. Fen had Rook shoved into the shadow of the overhang, her body covering his, dust choking everything, the roar filling the world—

And then, through it, a voice.

Not Fen's voice. Not a human voice at all, though it wore a human shape. Mara was standing in the open, ten paces away, in the falling dust, facing the mountain with her arms spread and her chin lifted, shouting—not words, or not words Fen's ears were built for. It sounded like language the way a river sounds like a road: familiar shape, impossible content.

The roar faltered.

Fen felt it falter, the way you feel a fight pause mid-blow. The tumbling stone slowed, caught, hesitated—and settled, sliding to a stop in a long grey tongue that ended six feet short of the overhang where Fen crouched with her brother. Dust drifted. Silence rang.

Mara stood in it, chest heaving, arms lowering slowly. At her feet, a single rock the size of a skull rolled to a gentle stop, nudged once, and lay still.

"Third ledge," Mara rasped. "Told us it lied."

For a moment nobody moved. Above them on the slopes, the wild goats had lined up along the ridge, silent, watching. All of them. Dozens of them, motionless, heads turned toward the woman who had shouted down a landslide.

Rook pulled himself out from under Fen, coughing, staring at the wall of stone that should have killed them. Then he did the last thing Fen expected.

He laughed. Big, wheezing, disbelieving laughter, bent double with his hands on his knees. "The ledge *lies*. She *told us the ledge lies*, and I said 'the ledge *lies*?' like an idiot, and then—" He gestured helplessly at the fresh scar of rock. "I nearly *died* on a lying ledge!"

Fen didn't laugh. Fen was looking at Mara, who had sunk down onto a boulder, gray-faced and shaking with exhaustion, water skin already half-drained. Who had stepped into the open while the mountain fell, because apparently that was the kind of person she was. The kind who rationed her gift her whole life and then spent it all at once on two smugglers she'd known a month.

Something shifted in Fen's chest, some load-bearing wall rearranging itself.

"Cargo," she said.

Mara looked up, wary.

"We don't call you cargo anymore." Fen dug in her pack, pulled out the strip of dried venison she'd been saving, and tossed it over. "Crew eats first. That's the rule. Crew eats first, sleeps dry, gets told the plan. All of it. No more of this—" she waved vaguely at Mara's whole guarded existence, "—door-in-the-face business. If you're crew, you're crew."

Mara caught the venison. Stared at it. Something moved behind those locked-door eyes—surprise, maybe, or the beginning of something harder to survive than any landslide.

"Crew," Mara repeated, testing the word.

"Don't make it weird," Rook said, wiping dust from his face and immediately making it weird. "Also I'm naming the next trail after me. Rook's Run. It'll be famous."

"You nearly died on a lying ledge."

"A *famous* lying ledge."

Fen rolled her eyes, but she was smiling, and she didn't bother to hide it. Below them, the blocked trail was a tomb; above them, the goats began picking their way upward along a route no human had ever mapped, unhurried, certain. Waiting.

"There's another way up," Mara said quietly, watching them. "They're showing me. It's steep, but it comes out above the Warden stations—high line, clean sightlines. We could see Vess coming a full day before she arrives."

Fen felt the smile sharpen into something else. Something with edges. "Then what are we waiting for?"

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the high line, a scoop of sheltered stone the goats had shown them, with a view south across the whole spine of Thornfell. Fen took first watch, mostly because she wanted to be alone with the view and the strange new arithmetic in her head.

From up here, she could see the low country: the smoke of the waystation ruins still rising, thin and grey, three days' walk away. The dark line of the Warden roads. And farther out, at the edge of seeing, a smudge of torchlight moving where no torchlight should be at night.

Organized. Column-shaped. Coming on.

Fen counted fires until her eyes watered. Too many. Far more than a patrol.

She slipped back down to the camp, where Rook slept like a dropped sack and Mara lay awake, staring at the stars, still gray with spent magic.

"Change of watch," Fen said softly, settling beside her. Then, because crew told crew the plan—all of it: "There's an army coming up the valley. Torches in column. That's not a patrol, Mara. That's not even a raid."

Mara was silent a long moment, following her gaze south.

"That's Vess," Mara said at last. "That's her closing the net."

Below them in the dark, faint and distant, one of the goats bleated—a short, sharp note, like an alarm.

"Yeah," Fen muttered. "They know it too."
