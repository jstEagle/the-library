## 10. The Garden Speaks

The garden remembered me.

That was the first wrongness, before I even reached the gate. I had come up the goat-path in the grey before dawn, alone—Fen and Rook had wanted to come, and Hesper had wanted to come, and Ilex had *insisted*, and I had refused them all with a firmness that surprised even me. Some doors you have to walk through by yourself. But the brambles that had torn my sleeves the night Ilex first showed me the codex now parted like courtiers. The nettles leaned away from my boots. A wren landed on a fencepost an arm's length off and looked at me with one bright eye and did not fly.

*You came back,* said every green thing at once, so quietly it was almost not speech at all. More like a temperature. Like walking into a room where people had been talking about you.

"I didn't have anywhere else to go," I told the garden, because lying to plants is pointless; they can't hear you anyway, but they can feel the shape of a lie, the way you can feel someone smiling wrong.

The wren chirped. *Liar,* it said, in the way of wrens, which is to say honestly and without malice. *You came because the book is here.*

I let myself in through the sagging gate. Prophet Sable's garden had been dead a hundred years—or so the village stories went—and like most village stories, it was half wishful thinking. Dead things don't smell like this. The roses had gone feral, climbing over their own trellises and strangling a plum tree in a slow green embrace, but they were blooming out of season, fat and reckless, in colors I had no names for. Herbs I couldn't identify crowded the beds in rows too straight for volunteers. And in the center, under a stone arch swallowed in ivy, stood the little dry-stone well-house where Ilex had pried up the flagstone and shown me my own name written in ink older than both of us.

*Beast-Tongued Heir.*

My name underneath it. Mara Sterling. In a hand that had been dust before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

I crouched at the edge of the well-house and made myself look at it again. Last time, Ilex had been holding the lamp, and Ilex's voice had been doing its warm honeyed thing, and I'd had somewhere to put my eyes besides the page. Now it was just me and morning light and the codex lying where we had left it, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, and there was no one to perform steadiness for.

It didn't help. My name still sat on the page like a nail driven into wood.

"Who were you?" I asked Sable, out loud, because talking to the dead is only strange if nobody answers, and lately everything answered.

Something answered.

Not the plants this time. Not the goats on the hillside or the jackdaws in the ruined tower. It was the silence itself—the particular hush of a garden that has been listening for a century—and it moved through the beds like wind though no leaf stirred, and when it spoke, it spoke in the voice I imagine a person has after they stop needing a body. Dry. Patient. Amused in the way of old teachers.

*You speak Beast. Then it is true, and I am sorry for it.*

My skin prickled. "Sorry? Everyone who's said my name lately has wanted something. The Wardens want me burned. The spirit-court wants me leashed. What do you want?"

*Nothing,* said the voice of Prophet Sable, or whatever remained of her in the roots and rain and rotted cedar of her garden. *That is rather the point. Come inside. Well. Come closer. I am mostly compost, these days, but compost hears everything.*

I should have run. Every instinct that had kept me alive beneath the Wardens' notice for years said: unknown voice, unknown power, walk away uphill and don't look back. Instead I picked up the codex, hugged it to my chest like Bram used to hold his ledger, and stepped under the ivy-choked arch.

* * *

She showed herself as a pattern, not a shape. If I squinted—there was no squinting away something like this, but the mind tries—a figure of green shadow standing among the roses, tall, stooped, hands stained to the elbow as if she had just finished planting something and would always have just finished planting something. Her face was the part my mind refused to build. I got the impression of laugh lines and left it at that.

*A warning, not a command,* she said, and I realized she was answering the question I hadn't asked yet, the way the best teachers do. *You've read it as a sentence. Everyone does. The Crown reads prophecy as inventory: what will happen, so we may seize it. The spirits read it as a promise: what will happen, so we may hasten it. Both of them are reading a weather report and calling it fate.*

"The codex says I'll renew the pact or destroy it."

*The codex says the heir will stand between two hungers and be eaten by one or feed the other. That is what I wrote. It is what I saw.* She drifted closer, and the roses turned their faces after her like sunflowers after the sun. *Seeing is not sentencing, child. I was a beast-speaker too.*

The words went through me colder than Vess's name ever had. "No. Beast-speakers are born once a generation, maybe. You lived a hundred years ago."

*And I lived a hundred years ago precisely because I was born once a generation. Who do you think taught the mountain to expect another of us? Who do you think the pact was written with? Not kings. Kings came later, with their fire-cant and their ledgers, taking credit for a bargain struck between herders and hedge-witches.* The green shadow laughed, and the sound was leaves. *I spoke to goats, girl. Goats and crows and one extremely opinionated salmon. I know exactly how your gift works and exactly what it costs, because I paid the same bill.*

"What does it cost?" I asked, and hated how fast the question came out, how hungry.

*Later,* she said, gentle and merciless. *First you must understand what you are carrying.* She gestured—not a hand, more a suggestion of a hand—at the codex against my chest. *Open it. Not to your page. To mine.*

I unwrapped the oilcloth with fingers that wouldn't quite behave. The codex fell open where it always seemed to fall open, to the page with my name, and I made myself turn past it, back toward the beginning, to the first prophecy in the book, the one about the founding of the pact itself. Sable's handwriting filled the margins there—dense, crabbed, annotated like a smuggler annotating passes through Thornfell. I had never looked closely. Margins are where cowards write.

*Read the small words,* Sable said.

I read them aloud, because some things need air:

"*This is not what will happen. This is what will happen if no one speaks otherwise.*"

The garden was very quiet. Even the wren had stopped fidgeting.

"Every prophecy," I said slowly. "In the whole book?"

*Every one.* The shadow settled onto the lip of the empty well as if sitting down after a long day's work. *A beast-speaker does not hear the future. There is no future to hear—only weight. Choices pile up, and the pile leans certain ways, and those of us with the ear can hear which way it groans. I heard the lean. I wrote down the lean. And then I watched kings and queens and spirit-queens spend a century treating my weather report as holy writ, marching their armies confidently into storms I merely predicted.* Her voice frayed, and for a moment the green shadow flickered like a candle. *I meant it as a warning flare. They built a religion on it. I meant to give them a map of the flood. They drowned each other arguing over whose feet the water would reach first.*

"And me." My throat was tight. "The Beast-Tongued Heir. Renew or destroy."

*The lean, as things stand. As things stand.* She leaned forward—I felt it more than saw it, a pressure like weather changing. *But here is what neither Briarthorn nor your Warden captain understands, because neither of them has ever been a small creature speaking with a large world. The pact is not a document. It is not carved on any stone or sealed in any blood. The pact is a conversation. It began as a conversation—herders asking the mountain's leave, the mountain's wild things answering—and conversations can be rejoined by anyone willing to speak and listen. Prophecy cannot command you, Mara Sterling. It can only describe the conversation as it stands. If you don't like the terms—*

"—you change the conversation," I finished.

Something in my chest cracked open, pain and relief in the same breath. Because I had spent weeks being a nail in wood. Driven. Fixed. Renew or destroy, chosen or hunted, everyone's instrument—the Crown's excuse, Briarthorn's lever, Vess's target, even Ilex's, though Ilex's wanting was gentler. And here was a dead woman in a rose garden telling me the book was a draft.

*There is one more thing,* Sable said, and her amusement drained away, leaving something rawer. *Turn to the end.*

I turned to the end. Past the prophecies of wars and heirs and traitors who open doors, past the last line of Sable's cramped script, and found—

Blank paper.

Not damaged. Not faded. Blank the way fresh vellum is blank: smooth, waiting, faintly luminous in the dawn light, as if the page itself were holding its breath. I touched it and the surface was warm. Warm as a living thing.

"It's empty."

*It is waiting.* Sable's shadow had drawn close now, close enough that the roses around us bowed. *A prophet writes what she hears. But I heard, at the very end of my hearing, something I could not transcribe—because it had no words yet. A voice not yet spoken. A conversation not yet begun. All I could do was prepare the page.* Her voice dropped to almost nothing, and the whole garden leaned in with her. *The pact was written once, by speakers, in living words. It can be written again. But not by me—I am compost and echo. Not by Briarthorn, who wants the old terms with herself on top. Not by your Captain Vess, who wants no terms at all, only silence. The blank page waits for a beast-tongue, Mara. It has waited a hundred years. It waits for someone who can hear the mountain and choose to answer it with words of their own.*

"And if I don't?"

*Then the lean holds. Renewal under Briarthorn's leash, or severance and war, and either way the villages burn and the gift devours its bearer, as it devoured nearly all of me.* She paused. *I won't pretend the choice is free. The weight is real. The armies are real. Vess is marching even now, and Briarthorn is digging into the old powers below. But a lean is not a law. Hear me, girl, and hear me clearly: no one inherits a sentence. We inherit a room full of unfinished arguments, and the terrible freedom of saying our piece.*

I looked down at the blank page. Warm under my fingertips. Patient as soil.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "What's in it for a hundred-year-old ghost?"

The green shadow smiled, and this time I saw the face—or the ghost of one, laugh lines and tired eyes and the specific kindness of someone who has failed and kept gardening anyway.

*I wanted someone to tell me,* she said. *When I was young and the weight first settled on me, I wanted one voice—one—to say: this is a draft, not a verdict. No one did. So I became the voice, for whoever came next.* The shadow began to thin, unraveling into the green dimness under the arch, into root and rain and roses. *They'll come for the garden soon. Vess knows about it; Briarthorn watches it. Whatever you mean to do, do it quickly, and do not carry the codex out of here in daylight—*

The voice frayed like cloth tearing. The wren burst from the fencepost in a clatter of wings. Down the valley, thin with distance, a horn sounded—Warden brass, three short notes and a long one. *Patrol sighted. Sweep formation.* Somewhere below, dogs barked in the flat disciplined rhythm of animals trained by people who had never once asked them what they wanted.

I wrapped the codex with shaking hands and stood in the middle of a dead woman's roses, holding a draft of the future.

Behind me, at the treeline, a goat I knew—Bram's old lead nanny, gray-muzzled, too clever for her own good—stepped out of the shadows and looked at me. Not grazing. Not passing. Waiting.

*Well?* she said, in the wordless way of goats, which is to say plainly and without flattery. *Are you going to stand there reading, or are you going to say something back?*

I closed the codex. I thought of the blank page, warm as a heartbeat. I thought of Hesper's ledger, Fen and Rook's scarred hands, Ilex's honeyed voice gone honest, Bram's blood on the waystation boards.

"No," I said to the goat, to the garden, to the whole leaning weight of the world. "I'm going to say something new."

The nanny turned uphill. And far below, the horns sounded again—closer now, and multiplying.
