## 11. Amnesty

They came for me at the seed archive, which was either a compliment or an insult—I hadn't decided which when the door opened and Captain Odile Vess stepped through it with two soldiers and the particular stillness of someone who has already won.

The archive sat in the cellar of the Greymoss Collegium's botany wing, three floors of drawers and clay pots and labeled envelopes, everything catalogued in my own handwriting. My handwriting. That was how I knew this had been coming for weeks: no one else in the Collegium could have found the false drawer, the one where I kept the seeds that weren't seeds at all but coded letters pressed into wax pods. Vess held one of those pods now, turning it in her gloved fingers like a jeweler assessing a stone she intended to crack.

"Botanist Pemberton," she said. She'd never once used my first name, not even in the years when I'd have given anything to hear it.

"Captain." I set down my tweezers with exaggerated care, as if interrupted mid-thought rather than mid-collapse. "If this is about the requisition forms, the nightshade variance is legitimate. Wounds don't dress themselves."

"This is about Thornfell." She crossed the room without hurry. Her boots made no sound on the stone; I'd forgotten that about her, the way she moved like a debt arriving quietly. "This is about a smuggler named Mara Sterling, and a book that burned in a fire you reported with suspicious precision, and eleven letters written in a cipher I taught you myself."

Eleven. I'd sent nine. Which meant either she'd invented two, or there were two I didn't know about—two I'd written drunk on ipecac tea and grief after Bram died, or two someone had forged, or two that existed only because Odile Vess wanted them to exist. With Vess, all three possibilities were equally likely.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said, and it came out steady, which surprised me. Fear, it turns out, is an excellent preservative. It keeps the voice fresh.

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't. You never did know what you were talking about. That was always your genius, Ilex—you could make nonsense sound like scholarship." She held up the wax pod between thumb and forefinger. "Shall we open it together? I've been told there's a route map inside. The Hollow Tree waystation, the goat-trails above the scree, something called the Choir Stair." Her mouth did not smile so much as remember smiling. "You've been busy."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being caught: it clarifies. For months I had been living inside a lie so large I'd stopped feeling its walls—the lie that I could serve two masters by lying to both equally well, that love and loyalty were different rooms in the same house. Now Vess stood in front of me holding the walls in her hand, and I discovered something almost restful about the moment. There was nothing left to protect except the people I'd been protecting, and they were miles away, uphill, trusting me.

Trusting me. Fen would laugh if she ever read these thoughts. Rook wouldn't laugh; Rook would just look at me with those flat gray eyes and start counting exits.

"You want the codex," I said. "Or you want Mara. Or both."

"I want order," Vess said, and here was the terrible thing, the thing that made her more dangerous than any fanatic: she meant it. "Do you know how many villages burn every year because the mountain spirits sulk, Ilex? How many caravans vanish, how many children are taken as tithe-tolls by things the Crown pretends don't exist? The Wardens hold the line. Not perfectly. But we hold it. And then some prophet's century-old riddle surfaces, and suddenly everyone with a grievance thinks the mountain will speak to *them*—" she tossed the pod onto my workbench, "—and people die. Bram Halloway died. He didn't have to. Your smugglers lit the fuse."

"Bram died because you burned his waystation around him."

"Bram died because he chose to stand in a building I had already ordered emptied." Something flickered behind her eyes—not quite regret, but its tax collector. "I gave the evacuation order twice. Ask anyone. Ask the survivors you've been hiding."

I couldn't ask anyone. That was the trap within the trap: everything she said was true enough to hurt, arranged into a shape that lied. Odile Vess had never needed falsehood. She just rearranged facts until they pointed where she wanted, the way I rearranged Latin binomials to make a weed sound like medicine.

"What do you want from me?" I asked.

"Amnesty." She said the word gently, like setting down something fragile. "Full pardon. Restoration of your standing. Your old rooms in the Collegium, your stipend, your name struck from every watchlist. In exchange: the routes. Where they'll run next, where the codex will be, where the heir sleeps." She paused. "And Ilex—I'm offering this because I believe you'll take it. Not because I'm certain. Because I looked at your letters, all eleven, and do you know what I saw?"

"Tell me."

"Fear for one person. Not ideology. Not ambition. One name, over and over, wrapped in botanical excuses. *The specimen requires shade. The specimen must not be exposed to frost.*" She shook her head slowly. "You wrote about Mara Sterling the way you used to write about orchids. I taught you cipher, boy. I know what devotion looks like when it's trying to be discreet."

My face, I am proud to report, did absolutely nothing. Ten years of hiding what I felt behind taxonomy had finally paid off in the only currency it could.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you're a traitor to the Crown who consorted with smugglers and spirit-courts, and I arrest you here, and I extract the same information anyway, slower, and you watch it happen through whatever they let you see." She straightened her cuffs. "But you won't refuse. Not because you're weak. Because you're honest, underneath all the camouflage, and honesty means you already know what I'm offering is the best available version of survival."

* * *

After she left—or rather, after I watched her leave and confirmed via the archive's one grimy window that her soldiers withdrew past the yew hedge—I stood among my seed drawers for a long time doing arithmetic.

Because here was the problem, the beautiful awful problem: she was right. Amnesty *was* the best available version of survival, if survival were the goal. And I did intend to accept. I intended to accept so hard it took me several minutes to notice I'd already decided, before she finished speaking, before she even offered—that I would say yes, and yes would be a knife I turned in her hand.

That's the part I need to set down honestly, because later—when it matters, when someone asks why—I don't want to remember myself as noble. I wasn't noble. I was terrified and in love and furious about Bram, and I had spent my whole life learning that the best lies are grown, not built: plant them early, water them daily, let the other person believe they cultivated the truth themselves.

Vess thought she was buying me. Fine. Let her buy me. Let her march on the coordinates I gave her, and let those coordinates be wrong in exactly the ways that mattered.

The false route was already in my head, half-composed. It was good work, if I say so myself—it used real trails, real water sources, plausible timing. A route Vess could follow for two days before realizing the trail bent somewhere impossible. Two days bought uphill was two days for Mara to reach Sable's garden, for Hesper to move the codex, for the twins to scatter the caravan's tracks across forty miles of scree. I'd feed her the Hollow Tree first—a place already abandoned, already ash—and watch her waste a day burning ruins.

I sat down at my bench and began pressing the lie into wax.

* * *

She came back at dusk. Of course she did. I should have known—Odile Vess never lets a hook sit overnight; she checks the line while the fish still remembers the taste of the bait.

She didn't knock. The door opened and there she was, alone this time, carrying two cups of the Collegium's terrible chicory. She set one on my bench like we were colleagues again, like we were back in the training yard at Fort Meridian where she'd found a hungry scholarship boy with a gift for languages and made him hers.

"You've composed something," she said.

"You haven't even asked yet."

"The answer's in your hands. Ink under the nails, though you washed. Wax residue on your left cuff. You've been writing since I left, which means you've accepted, which means we can skip the part where you pretend to struggle with your conscience." She drank. Grimaced at the chicory, as always. "Give me the pod."

I gave her the pod. My hand did not shake. I had rehearsed that too.

She cracked the seal with her thumbnail and read in silence, and I watched her face the way I'd once watched examiners' faces, cataloguing micro-movements: the slight lift at the Hollow Tree entry (good), the pause at the Choir Stair (careful), the long stillness at the final notation (what final notation—no, I remembered, I'd added a decoy timestamp, a note about a rendezvous at the drowned mill, deliberately wrong).

"Well?" I asked.

"It's thorough," she said. "It's very you." And then she folded the wax shell closed, tucked it into her coat, and did the last thing in the world I expected.

She thanked me, and she left.

No escort. No arrest. No soldiers waiting in the hedgerow. Just gone, into the blue dusk, and I stood in my cellar full of sleeping seeds understanding—with the slow, cold certainty of frost moving up a stem—that I had made a mistake I couldn't yet name.

* * *

It took me until midnight to name it, and then it arrived all at once, the way landslides arrive: quietly, then all at once, and then you're buried.

*I believed you.*

Not "I accept your betrayal." Not "welcome home." She had said *it's very you*, pocketed my false routes, and walked out without insurance, without leverage, without a single soldier to make sure I didn't simply warn the others the moment her boots cleared the gate.

Nobody does that. Nobody *can* do that—unless the information doesn't matter unless it's true, unless—

Unless she'd already known. Unless she'd known the routes before I wrote them, known the Hollow Tree was empty, known the whole shape of our flight, and what she'd actually purchased tonight was not my knowledge but my *movement*. Because here is the mechanism, elegant as anything in nature: a double agent feeds false intelligence, and the enemy, knowing it's false, simply follows the agent instead. I would run to warn Mara of the trap I'd described—and in running, I would lead Vess up the mountain along the true path, my own feet drawing the map she couldn't otherwise read. Love used as bait. I was the bait. I had volunteered.

I sat in the dark of the archive and laughed, once, a sound like something breaking.

She'd learned that trick from watching me, I think. Or maybe she'd always known it. Maybe that's what she'd seen in the scholarship boy all those years ago—not talent, not loyalty, but a creature who could be moved by loving things, and therefore steerable. Point the heart; the body follows.

Fine.

Then let her steer me up the mountain. Let her follow me through every pass, every goat-trail, every frozen ford—because I knew something she didn't, something her maps had no margin for: the mountain itself was waking. The spirits were stirring. Briarthorn's court was moving below the snowline, and the Wardens' fire-cant weapons were matches in a house full of lamp oil, and when Vess marched her neat column into that valley following my treacherous little footsteps, she would find not a fugitive and a codex but a war already halfway begun—one she'd started believing a prophecy licensed her, against a queen who believed the same book justified her, and the only person in either army who had read the ending and understood it was a dead prophet whose garden I had helped desecrate for a salary.

Guilt is supposed to paralyze. Everyone says so. But mine had done the opposite: it had refined itself, condensed, the way sap becomes syrup becomes sugar-glass—thin shame boiled down into something hard and sharp and load-bearing. I was guilty. Guilty of the letters, guilty of the garden, guilty of every hour I'd spent charming Mara while cataloguing them for another master. I would carry it. But guilt carried properly is fuel. It burns hot and it burns toward something.

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, the seed drawers ticked and settled around me, ten thousand small dormant things waiting for their season.

One season, I promised them. One more lie, planted deep, watered daily. And when it bloomed—if it bloomed—let it flower into whatever truth I had left.

I packed a bag with what a fleeing botanist would carry, and what a fleeing traitor would forget, and what a lover would bring: feverfew for wounds, a wax-sealed letter addressed to no one, and the pressed violet Mara had once, drunkenly, tucked into my buttonhole on the Prophet's Road, which I had preserved between glass because I was apparently the kind of fool who preserved things.

At dawn I went over the wall, and behind me—precisely far enough back to be deniable, precisely close enough to keep me in sight—a Warden patrol took up the trail.

Good, I thought, climbing. Follow me, Captain. Every step is a lie.

Just pray you figure out which direction the truth runs before we all reach the top.
