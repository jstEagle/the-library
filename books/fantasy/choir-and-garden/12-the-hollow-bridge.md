## 12. The Hollow Bridge

The bridge found us before we found it.

That was my first thought, anyway—Rook's thought, and I'll own it, because I've spent twenty-two years trusting my gut about the Thornfell and it hasn't lied to me yet, not even when it told me Mara Sterling was trouble. The trail just ended at a lip of granite, all of us standing there in the fog like idiots, and then the fog peeled back the way a curtain peels when someone behind it decides you've waited long enough.

The bridge hung across a gorge so deep the bottom was a rumor. No stone in it. No wood. Just woven roots and silvered bone and lengths of what looked like spider-silk but moved wrong, moving against the wind instead of with it, like the bridge was breathing.

"Nobody asked me," Fen said, "but nobody ever does."

"That's because you volunteer opinions free of charge," I said. "Charge them and people will stop."

Hesper came up beside me, and I felt her go still the way a healer goes still—reading something. "Spirit-toll," she said quietly. "I've read about these. Pre-Warden construction. They don't carry weight. They carry *honesty*."

"Wonderful," Fen said. "I'm an extremely honest person."

"You once told a customs officer you were your own twin sister to dodge a tariff," I said.

"And it *worked*."

"It worked because the officer couldn't prove otherwise. This bridge can." I looked at Hesper. "What happens if we lie?"

Her mouth did the thing it does when she's deciding how much truth she can afford. She'd been doing that a lot since the inn burned. Since Vess took her. We'd gotten her back out of that cell three days ago—Ilex's stolen seal, Ilex's white face, Ilex not talking about it—and she'd been rationing herself ever since, like a woman who'd learned her honesty had a body count.

"I don't know," she said finally. "And I hate not knowing worse than I hate most things."

Mara stood apart from us, at the very edge, looking down into the gray. They'd been quiet since the burning. Quiet since Hesper. Quiet in a way that wasn't their usual quiet—their usual quiet was a tool, a smuggler's silence, useful and chosen. This was different. This was a silence with something inside it, rattling around like a coin in a dead man's boot.

"We could go around," Ilex offered. "Add four days, maybe five, drop south through the—"

"There is no around," Mara said.

Everyone looked at them.

"The spirits pulled this bridge up for us," Mara said. "Or for me. Bridges don't grow where roads don't want to go. It wants us on it." A pause. "It wants something, anyway. Tolls always do."

Fen spat over the edge, which was either bravado or a death wish, hard to tell with her. "So we pay it. What's the toll? Coin? Blood? Firstborn?"

"Truths," Hesper said. "Old stories say each traveler surrenders one truth they've never spoken aloud. The bridge holds it. That's what carries you—not planks. Secrets."

Silence, except the wind doing its wrong thing through the silk.

"That's the stupidest magic I've ever heard of," Fen announced.

"It's the oldest," Hesper said. "Wardens burn books about it. Which usually means it works."

* * *

We argued about it for an hour. Real arguing, the ugly kind, the kind that only happens between people who've started to think of themselves as stuck with each other. Fen wanted to split—her and me going wide while the rest crossed. Hesper pointed out that splitting was how Vess picked us off at the inn, and Fen went quiet, because Fen doesn't apologize but she does recalibrate, fast, when the math turns against her.

Ilex wanted to cross first, alone, as a test. Hesper shot that down too: "Toll bridges don't take volunteers. They take the whole crossing or nothing. You pay together or the toll takes its pick."

"The toll *takes its pick*," Fen repeated flatly. "Love that. Love everything about this."

Mara hadn't spoken again. I watched them watch the bridge, and I did what twins do—I watched my sister watching them, and put the two readings together. Fen had been carrying a grudge since the fire, the shape of which was simple: if Mara had moved faster, decided faster, been less careful about being invisible for one single night, Hesper would be unburned and whole and the inn would still smell of cedar and stew. Fen had said as much, once, in a voice I'd never heard her use. Mara had taken it without flinching, which somehow made it worse.

Now Mara stepped onto the first span of silk.

Nothing happened. No lurch, no snap. The bridge accepted the footfall the way soil accepts a seed, and Mara turned back to us, face pale as milk, and said, "Come on, then. Before it changes its mind."

* * *

Crossing a truth-bridge is a strange thing to describe, so I'll describe it badly and honestly, which feels appropriate.

The surface held us fine—that wasn't the problem. The problem was what rose up out of the weave as we walked. Not words exactly. More like the bridge asking a question with every step, and the question being: *what are you hiding?*

You could feel it sort through you. I felt it go through my pockets, metaphorically speaking, turning things over. My fear of heights, no, too shallow, it passed that by. My fear that Fen would die someday doing something brave and stupid and that I'd be the one who had to tell our mother's ghost. Deeper. Deeper. Looking for the thing under the floorboards.

Halfway across, the wind stopped.

Everything stopped. The mist hung like held breath. And the bridge spoke—or didn't speak, but made itself understood, the way Mara says animals make themselves understood, meaning directly into the meat of you:

**A TRUTH. UNPAID.**

Fen went first, because of course she did. She planted her feet, glared down into the gorge like it owed her money, and said, "Fine. FINE." Then, quieter, in a voice I'd only heard maybe twice in my life:

"I knew the Wardens were coming to the inn. Two days before. A bird told me—a real one, a jackdaw on the south ridge, and I've understood birds since I was small, same as Mara, same gift, and I never said anything because I thought if I named it, it would be true." Her jaw worked. "I let it happen because I was afraid. So the fire is half mine. There. Paid."

Nobody moved. I don't think any of us breathed. Mara stared at her—not angry, worse than angry, some complicated grief—and Fen stared back, chin up, daring anyone to make it a thing.

The bridge hummed, satisfied, and let her pass to the far span.

Hesper next. She crossed her arms like a woman facing a tribunal, which, given recent history, fair enough.

"I didn't leave the Wardens because of conscience," she said. "Not only. I left because I was a coward with good timing. When they ordered me to treat the spirit-child before the burn, I refused—but I refused quietly. I filed objections. I wrote letters. If Captain Vess hadn't come herself, I'd have stood in that yard and watched it happen, and hated myself forever, and done nothing. My conscience is a committee that meets too late." She exhaled, long and shaking. "The codex calls me the traitor who opens doors. Fine. But know what kind of traitor you're traveling with. A slow one."

She walked on. The bridge carried her like it was pleased.

Ilex hesitated longer than anyone. When they spoke, their voice was stripped of all that botanist charm, down to bare wood.

"I chose Mara before I met them," Ilex said. "Read the description in the codex, matched it to a beast-speaker working the Thornfell runs, and engineered the meeting in Sable's garden. Every laugh, every touch—reconnaissance first. I've told myself the tenderness came after. I don't know anymore whether that's true, and I've stopped pretending I do. What I know is this: I would burn every page of the codex now to give them back the life they had. That's the truth. Take it."

They paid it and walked, and didn't look at any of us, and none of us made them.

My turn came faster than I wanted. The pressure built up behind my ribs like water finding a crack, and I figured out fast that the bridge wasn't going to take a convenient secret. It wanted the load-bearing one. The one everything else rested on.

"Fen and I aren't staying for the cause," I heard myself say. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "We're staying for Mara. The smuggling, the prophecy, the war brewing—we were out. We had a buyer lined up in Lowmarsh, a clean retirement, out of the mountains by midwinter. Then the codex happened, and I looked at Mara standing in that garden looking like the world had ended, and I—" My throat closed. I pushed through. "Our mother died alone because everyone she loved was busy being useful somewhere else. I swore I'd never let that happen to anyone again. So yes, I'm here for the pact and the spirits and all of it. But mostly I'm here so that when Mara dies—and people like us die, it's practically a trade requirement—they won't be alone. That's the truth. It's smaller than the others. It's the biggest thing I have."

The bridge took it gently, almost kindly, and I walked, and Fen caught my sleeve for two steps and then let go, because that's how we say things.

Then there was Mara.

* * *

They stood at the center of the span, where the silk braided thickest, and the bridge pressed on them and pressed and pressed, and I watched their shoulders climb toward their ears like a creature trying to make itself small enough to escape its own skin.

"No," Mara said. To the bridge, to us, to nobody. "No, that's not—you can't have that one."

The pressure in the air doubled. The silk beneath our feet tightened like a drawn bow.

"Mara," Hesper said carefully. "Bridges like this don't ask twice."

"I KNOW what bridges like this do." Their voice cracked down the middle. They gripped the root-cables, knuckles white, and looked out over the gorge, and I saw them decide. I've seen a lot of decisions get made on that mountain. Smugglers deciding to run. Wardens deciding to burn. This was neither. This was a person deciding to stop lying to themselves in front of witnesses.

"When I was nine," Mara said, "I found the old pact-stone above the summer pastures. The first one, the original, the one the histories say is lost. It isn't lost. It's cracked, and the crack goes all the way through, and I could hear through it—into the deep places where the old powers sleep, the ones Briarthorn keeps chained below her court. And the stone spoke to me. Or I spoke to it. Either way, I learned its name, and its name is also its function, and if I ever said that name aloud—at the stone, at the right hour—it wouldn't renew the pact and it wouldn't sever it. It would end the whole arrangement. Humans and spirits both. Clean cut. Everything wild goes free and everything human goes... unfed. The mountain stops holding its side. The valleys starve within a generation. Maybe less."

The gorge was absolutely silent.

"I've known since I was nine years old," Mara said, "that I could end it. And I never told a soul. Not Bram. Not the twins. Not Ilex. Because as long as nobody knew, I was just a smuggler with a strange ear, and the moment somebody knew, I became a weapon—and weapons get used, or they get locked away, or they get killed. So I stayed invisible. For twenty years I stayed invisible, and I told myself it was wisdom, and it was cowardice, and Bram died in a fire I could have—"

Their voice went.

"—Bram died," they finished, barely sound at all, "because I taught everyone I loved how to need me without letting any of them know me. That's the truth. That's all of it. Take it. Take all of it. I'm so tired."

The bridge drank it down. I felt it go—felt the whole span shiver and settle, deeper and steadier than before, like a keel finding water. The mist below us stirred, and something vast turned over down there, listening, and did not rise.

Mara stood swaying at the center of the bridge, empty-eyed, lighter and more broken than I'd ever seen a person look.

Fen crossed to them. My sister, who'd thrown the fire in their teeth two days ago, took Mara's face in both hands and said, "Nine years old. You were NINE. You kept a secret like that alone for twenty years and you wonder why we're all half-mad? You idiot. You absolute idiot. You should have told us. We'd have carried it."

"We'd have carried it," I said, and meant it with everything in me, and saw Mara understand, finally, that the confession hadn't been a toll extracted. It had been a hand extended.

They walked the last span together, all five of us, and the far side received us onto ordinary rock, and the bridge sighed loose behind us, threads unwinding into mist, its toll paid in full.

We stood there a moment in the thin cold light, five people who knew each other properly now, for better and considerably for worse.

Then the ground trembled—not rockfall, not weather. Deliberate. A summons. Out of the dark of the upper trail came roots, moving like slow serpents, weaving themselves into an archway of living wood, and through it drifted a scent of crushed brambles and cold iron.

Down the archway walked a herald of the spirit-court, bark-skinned, crowned in thorn, and it did not bow.

It beckoned.

"The Queen of Root and Tooth," it said, in a voice like sap dripping in a hollow tree, "requires the Heir. Tonight. Come alone, Beast-Tongued One—or bring your found family, and let them learn what their loyalty will cost."

Behind me, I heard Mara exhale, long and steady, the way you breathe before a dive into deep water.

"Well," said Fen. "At least we got the crying part over with first."
