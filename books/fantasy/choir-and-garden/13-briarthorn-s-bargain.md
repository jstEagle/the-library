## 13. Briarthorn's Bargain

They took Mara down into the mountain the way a river takes a leaf—gently, inevitably, without asking.

Roots parted for them. Stone doors that had no hinges swung inward at Briarthorn's gesture, and the air changed from pine and snowmelt to something older: loam, iron, and a sweetness like flowers rotting in honey. The court of roots and teeth received its heir with bowed heads and eyes that glowed like coals banked under ash. Somewhere below, water dripped in a rhythm almost like speech.

Mara had been given a chamber, of sorts—a hollow lined with moss soft as any featherbed, lit by fungus that glowed pale gold. They had been given clothes too, woven of bark-silk that fit as if grown to measure. Given food, given honor guards of thorn-woven figures that never blinked, given every courtesy except the one thing Mara actually wanted.

A door that locked from the inside.

"You are restless."

Briarthorn stood in the entrance, which was to say she simply *was* there now, where a heartbeat before there had been nothing but shadow. The queen wore her antlers like a crown she'd forgotten she was wearing, and her face was beautiful the way winter is beautiful—from a distance, and only if you don't have to survive it.

"I'm a prisoner," Mara said. "Prisoners are usually restless."

"A guest." The queen drifted closer, her hem trailing moss. "There is a difference, though I understand how it might escape you. Humans keep their guests in rooms with locks and call it hospitality. We keep ours in comfort and call it nothing at all. No one has ever named it. That is our failing, perhaps."

"Your failing," Mara said, "is that everyone in this mountain can hear your thoughts except me, and I'm starting to think that's deliberate."

Briarthorn laughed—a sound like wind through hollow reeds—and settled onto a root-bench across from Mara with the unhurried grace of someone who had outlived patience itself.

"You are sharper than Sable's book promised. Good. I dislike negotiating with dull things."

"Negotiating implies I have something you want."

"You have *everything* I want." The coal-eyes fixed on Mara, and for a moment the warmth in them was genuine, and that was worse than the coldness had been. "You have the voice. You have the words. One hundred years ago, Prophet Sable sat in his garden and heard what you will soon be asked to hear, and he wrote it down, and then he spent the rest of his life apologizing for it."

"Apologizing to whom?"

"To us. To them. To everyone." She rose again, pacing, and the shadows moved with her like loyal dogs. "Sit with me, Beast-Tongued Heir. Walk with me instead. There is something you must see before we speak of bargains. It would be dishonorable to ask anything of you while you remain ignorant of the stakes."

*Dishonorable.* Mara filed the word away. In their experience, anyone who kept saying *honor* was about to do something dishonorable and wanted company for it.

* * *

The mountain's heart was a cavern so vast that Mara's eyes gave up trying to find its ceiling. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some terrible organ, and between them flowed rivers—not of water, but of light. Pale, silvered light, moving slow and thick as honey, pooling in basins carved by no human hand.

And around those pools, the spirits gathered. Hundreds of them. Some were tall and crowned like their queen; others were small as children, or shaped like foxes made of smoke, or like nothing Mara had a name for. They pressed against the edges of the light-pools the way starving things press against glass.

"What is it?" Mara asked, though they already knew. You couldn't spend your life listening to beasts and not recognize hunger when you saw it.

"The pact," Briarthorn said. "What remains of it. When your people first climbed Thornfell, they were afraid, and fear makes men generous, so they bound themselves to us in oath and offering. We tended their herds through hard winters. We turned storms aside from their villages. We taught their goats where grass grew in the deep snow. And in exchange—" her voice frayed, just slightly, on the next words "—in exchange, they left us the wild places, and they *remembered* us. That was the true payment, Heir. Not milk. Not grain. Memory. Every story told by a fire, every child warned away from the deep woods, every farmer who set out a bowl of cream 'for the mountain's folk'—that was the tithe, and it fed us as surely as bread feeds you."

"And now?"

"Now your Wardens burn the stories. Now the maps say *uninhabited* where our courts once held festival. Now a whole generation of humans grows up thinking the mountain is only stone." She gestured at the spirits at the pool's edge. A small one—a child-shaped thing with ferns growing from its scalp—dipped both hands into the silver light and drank, and even from across the cavern Mara could see how thin its wrists were. "We are starving, Heir. Slowly, politely, over a hundred years—but starving. And my court grows impatient with politeness."

Mara thought of Bram. Of the waystation burning. Of Odile Vess's face, calm as a ledger, ordering flame into everything Mara had ever loved.

"They're wrong," Mara said quietly. "The Wardens. What they're doing—it's murder dressed up as tidiness. I know that better than most."

"Yes," Briarthorn said softly. "You do. Which is why I offer you this instead of taking it."

She led Mara to the far side of the cavern, where the light-rivers converged on a single basin, and above the basin, carved into the living stone in letters old as the mountain's bones, ran lines of script that hurt to look at directly—the First Tongue, the language beasts spoke and stones remembered.

"This is the Severance," the queen said. "Not a spell, exactly. An oath with teeth. It was written as a last resort, in the days when the pact was young and either side might have needed to end it cleanly. Spoken aloud, by one who carries both blood and beast-speech, it dissolves the binding entirely. All of it. The obligations, the protections, the memory-tithe. Everything."

Mara frowned. "That sounds like what you want. Freedom."

"It is what half my court wants." Her jaw tightened. "But listen to the rest, Heir, because I will not trick you—I have watched what tricks did to Sable's reputation, and I refuse the practice. The pact does not only bind us. It binds the *mountain*. The protections run both ways. The storm-turning, the herd-tending, the holding of slopes against avalanche—all of it flows from the same root. Sever the root, and everything dies."

The cold arrived slowly, the way cold does when you finally understand something.

"The villages," Mara said.

"All of them," Briarthorn agreed. "Thornfell's valleys would see a winter such as they have not seen since before the pact. Rockfall. Flood. Starvation. Perhaps one soul in three survives the year. My seers say fewer." She said it plainly, without flinching, and Mara realized with a lurch that this was the queen's idea of honesty—as if plainness could launder the number.

"And you'd ask me to speak it anyway."

"I would ask you to *choose* it anyway. There is a difference. I cannot compel the heir; the words will not come from a coerced mouth. The magic knows the taste of freedom, and it will only leave a free tongue." She turned to face Mara fully, and the light of the pools rose in her eyes until they burned. "I am asking you to weigh one in three of ten thousand souls against an entire people—my people—who are dying by inches and whom no one will mourn because no one remembers they exist. I am asking you to do arithmetic that has no clean answer."

"There's a third answer," Mara said. "Renewal. The codex says renewal. Teach them the stories again. Remind them you exist—"

"With what army of storytellers?" For the first time, something cracked in the queen's composure, and beneath it Mara glimpsed exhaustion centuries deep. "Heir. I sent envoys. Forty years ago, sixty years ago. Beautiful ones, gentle ones, who walked into the valley towns wearing borrowed shapes and asked—*asked*—to be remembered. Do you know what happened? Your Wardens called them infestations. I have jars, Heir. Would you like to see my jars?"

Mara said nothing. There was nothing to say. They thought of Vess's torches, of Bram falling, of the waystation ash, and understood—with a sickness—that both sides had been keeping score in bodies for a century, and neither side had ever stopped long enough to count.

"So yes," Briarthorn went on, quieter now, which was somehow more frightening than the shouting would have been. "If you refuse, I will go below. To the ones who were old when the pact was young. The ones even I do not trust. They have offered me a different road to freedom—one that does not require an heir's consent. It requires only blood, and time, and it will not distinguish between the guilty and the innocent. Between Vess and a shepherd's child. It will simply *end* the remembering, and everything after will be silence."

"You'd unleash that rather than wait?"

"I have waited two hundred years." The words came out flat and final as a headstone. "My patience is not infinite. It is not even, anymore, very large."

The fern-child at the light-pool looked up at Mara across the cavern. Held their gaze. And in the way of beasts and near-beasts, it showed them something without words: a memory of green valleys, of being welcomed, of bowls of cream on doorsteps. Then it showed them the present. The thin wrists. The empty dark.

Mara's throat ached.

"If I refuse," they said carefully, "you'll go below. If I accept, I kill thousands. Those are my options."

"No," Briarthorn said. "Those are your options *tonight*. But you are clever, and stubborn, and Sable chose your line for a reason. So here is what I give you that no one else in this war will give you: time. Three days. Go back to your strays, your botanist, your disgraced healer. Tell them everything. Let them argue, plead, curse me. And then come back and choose—not because I forced you, but because you weighed it yourself, and found the scales tipped."

"And if I don't come back?"

The queen smiled, and it was the saddest thing Mara had ever seen.

"Heir. You will come back. Not because I hold anything over you. Because you've already done the arithmetic, and you know that walking away doesn't erase the sum." She extended a hand of woven shadow and briar. "Three days. Honor demands I tell you the truth entire before the asking. Consider it told."

Mara didn't take the hand. They walked out of the mountain's heart alone, past the thin-wristed children of the dark, past the pools of dying light, and the roots parted before them all the way up, all the way to the grey morning light of the treeline—

where Fen stood waiting with a drawn knife and an expression that said the world had ended twice since dawn, and Rook wasn't beside her.

"Mara," Fen said, and her voice cracked on the name. "It's Hesper. Vess found the inn."
