## 14. Ash on the Wind

Fen counted exits the way other people counted sheep. It was how she'd slept since she was nine years old, since the night the Wardens burned the ferry-house at Lowmarsh and her mother shoved her and Rook into a culvert and told them to count to a thousand before they came out. She'd never reached a thousand. She'd gotten to six hundred and eleven when the screaming stopped.

So it was habit, not prophecy, that had her awake in the loft of the Hollow Tree with one eye on the window, counting.

Four ways out of Hesper's inn. The front door, which was a lie — anyone watching it would see anyone leaving. The root-cellar hatch that came up behind the woodpile. The chimney, if you didn't mind dying of soot or dignity. And the goat-trail off the north balcony, which wasn't an exit for people but was an exit for goats, and Rook had spent three weeks teaching himself to move like one.

She'd mentioned this to Mara twice. Mara had said, "Hesper's wards are good," in the tone of someone who believed wards were a kind of promise.

Wards weren't promises. Wards were a countdown.

Fen smelled the smoke before she heard anything else. Not hearth-smoke — hearth-smoke was fat and bread and pine resin, smoke that meant morning. This was pitch and oil and something chemical underneath, Warden fire-cant, the stuff they fed to torches so that wet wood would burn like dry straw. It smelled like a lie told by a chemist.

She was moving before her brain finished naming it. "Rook." One word, low, and across the loft he rolled out of his bedroll already reaching for his boots, because they'd done this drill in their heads a hundred times without ever saying it aloud. You don't say the drill aloud. Saying it aloud is how you jinx it.

"Mara," Fen hissed down through the floorboards. "Mara, wake up, we're lit."

Below her, footsteps. Then Hesper's voice, calm as a woman checking on stew: "I know."

That was the thing about Hesper that had taken Fen a month to understand: she never sounded scared, and you could never tell if it was courage or if some part of her had died quietly years ago and nobody had held a funeral.

* * *

The inn was built inside a hollowed oak older than the kingdom, three stories wound around the living trunk, stairs grown rather than carved. From the balcony you could see the whole of the dell: the stream, the goat-pasture, the treeline where the spirit-court's territory began its gray-green blur.

From the balcony, Fen could see the ring of torches.

Not charging. Not shouting. Wardens didn't charge anymore, not under Vess. They walked. They formed a perimeter with the unhurried precision of people doing a job they were very good at, and they waited for their captain to give the word, and the word would come, and everything inside the ring would become arithmetic.

"How many?" Rook at her shoulder, whispering even though whispering was pointless now.

"Forty. Fifty." Fen's mouth was dry. "They've got the cant-torches. They're going to burn it either way, Rook. Tree's alive. You can't fight fire-cant with a tree."

"We can fight them with a tree," Rook said. He meant the trails. He meant running. He always meant running; it was the family trade.

Then Mara came up onto the balcony and Fen saw her face and felt the first cold drop of the night go down her spine, because Mara wasn't looking at the Wardens. Mara was looking at the pasture.

"The goats are out," Mara said. "They should have been screaming. Beasts scream when fire-cant comes near — I've heard them do it from a mile off. They're silent. Someone's dampened them." A pause. "Or they're being kept quiet. On purpose. By something with more authority than me."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning Vess brought a silencer. Meaning she's known about my gift for longer than we thought, and she planned for it." Mara's hands were shaking. Fen noticed, and hated noticing, because Mara's hands never shook. That was the whole point of Mara. Mara was the one who didn't shake while everyone else did.

"Hesper's still below," Rook said.

"I know where Hesper is."

"She's packing the codex—"

"I said I *know*, Rook." Too sharp. Fen heard it land wrong and didn't take it back. There wasn't time to be kind. Kindness was a thing you did after, in the ashes, and there would be plenty of ash.

Below them, Captain Odile Vess stepped forward into the torchlight, alone, hands empty and visible, the way you approach a wild animal. Her voice carried up the trunk of the great oak like sap rising.

"Mara Sterling! You have something that belongs to the Crown. Bring it out, bring yourself out, and these people walk away. That is the entire offer. I will make it once."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on your eardrums.

And then Hesper opened the front door herself, and stood in it with a lantern in one hand and nothing else visible, and said, in her infuriating healer's calm, "You'll forgive me if I check the offer against your record, Captain. Your record is short on 'these people walk away.'"

"Step aside, Healer Kestrel-Hall. Or whatever name you're wearing now."

"No."

"Hesper—" Mara started down the stairs.

"*Stay.*" Hesper didn't raise her voice. She never needed to. "You go out that door, she takes you and burns the rest anyway. You know it. I know it. Everyone standing in this tree knows it except apparently the two of you, who keep thinking this is a negotiation." She turned back to the door. To Vess. "Here is my counter-offer, Captain. You turn around, and I don't publish what's in this book to every village between here and the coast."

Fen saw it then — saw Hesper's left hand, hidden behind the doorframe, pressed flat against the codex's cover. Saw the way the lantern light caught old paper. The madwoman had it right there. At the door. As a hostage.

"That book is a century of dead prophet's ravings," Vess said.

"It's a century of dead prophet's *records,* Captain. Including records of what the Wardens burned, and who ordered it, and what the spirits did in reply. You want to find out how many villages remember?"

For one moment — one long, stupid, hopeful moment — Fen thought it might work. Thought the arithmetic might balance.

Then Vess said, softly, almost sadly: "Burn it."

* * *

Fire-cant doesn't behave like fire. Fen learned that in the first ten seconds. Ordinary flame climbs and dances; cant-fire *walks*. It went up the inn's outer bark in sheets, deliberate as soldiers scaling a wall, and the great oak — alive, ancient, full of water and green wood — began to scream.

Trees don't scream. Except when they do, when the fire is cant-fed and hungry for living things, and the sound comes up through the floors and into your teeth, and every animal within a mile goes silent because even beasts know there's no negotiating with that.

"Root cellar!" Rook shouted. "Now, now, NOW—"

Fen went. No hesitation, no heroics; she'd learned at nine years old that hesitation was how families got smaller. Down the grown stairs two at a time, through the common room where the heat was already pressing in like a hand, past the woodpile—

—and stopped, because Mara wasn't behind her.

"Mara!"

Mara stood in the middle of the burning common room, looking up, toward Hesper's room above. Toward the sound — Fen heard it too now, under the roar — of Hesper singing. The old Warden field-hymn, the one they sang over the dying. Steady. Unhurried. Getting quieter.

"She's coming," Mara said. It wasn't a question. It was worse than a question; it was a statement made by someone who needed it to be true.

"She's *staying,*" Fen screamed. "Look at her, look at what she's doing, she bought us the door, USE THE DOOR—"

"I'm not leaving her."

"Yes you ARE—"

It was Rook who did it. Rook, who never fought anyone, put his shoulder into Mara's back and drove her through the hatch, and Fen slammed it behind them and threw the woodpile over it and stood on top of the pile with her boots planted while the world above turned orange.

Through the cracks between the logs, light strobing, she saw Mara fighting — actually fighting, clawing at Rook's arms, and Rook holding on, tears cutting tracks through the soot on his face, saying over and over "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry" in the small voice he used for exactly this, for the moments when love required betrayal.

The last thing Fen saw of the Hollow Tree, through a gap in the woodpile, was the balcony railing catching fire, and above it the night sky filling with sparks like upside-down snow.

Then they were in the root tunnel, crawling, and there was only dirt and dark and the three of them breathing.

* * *

They came up behind the stream, half a mile south, where the ground dipped and the alders grew thick. Behind them the glow ate the sky. The heat of it reached them even here, a pressure on their backs like a hand pushing them onward, and Fen realized she was counting again. Six hundred and twelve. Six hundred and thirteen. Thirty-eight years late finishing the count from Lowmarsh, and it didn't help, it never helped, but she couldn't stop.

Mara sat in the alder-shadow with her knees drawn up, staring at the fire, and hadn't spoken since the hatch.

Rook had his head in his hands.

Fen discovered she was holding a stick. She didn't remember picking it up. She was gripping it so hard her knuckles had gone white, and when she finally spoke, what came out was:

"You hesitated."

Mara didn't move.

"At the hatch. You hesitated, and Rook had to *shove* you, and every second we lost was a second closer to them finding the tunnel, finding US—" Her voice cracked upward. She hated the sound of it. She kept going anyway. "Hesper stayed behind a door because she knew — she *knew* — somebody had to be smart enough to leave, and you couldn't do it. You couldn't do the one thing. You guide smugglers past patrols, you talk to goats, you're the great Beast-Tongued Heir of the bloody Codex, and when it mattered you froze like a—"

"Fen." Rook's hand on her arm.

"No." She shook him off. "No, let me — because she needs to hear it. We all keep tiptoeing around it. We all keep waiting for Mara to decide things, because the book says Mara decides things, well the BOOK got Hesper killed tonight, or captured, or whatever Vess does with traitor-healers, and I am DONE letting a hundred-year-old dead man's handwriting make our choices for—"

"I heard her stop singing." Mara's voice came out sanded flat. "While we crawled. I heard it stop. I was listening for it the whole way and it just... stopped, mid-verse, and I thought—" Her breath hitched, caught, failed. "I thought if I went back fast enough."

"Fast enough for what?" Fen heard her own cruelty and watched it land and could not make herself stop. "Fast enough to burn? Then there'd be four of us dead instead of one, and you call that loyalty? Hesper called it arithmetic, and for ONCE in your life you should have listened to her instead of your—"

"Don't." It wasn't loud. It was the opposite of loud. Mara stood, and the firelight found their face, and Fen saw something she'd never seen there before: not the steady guide, not the chosen heir. Just a person, cracked straight down the middle. "Don't you dare tell me what she'd want. Don't you dare. You didn't hear her sing to dying men for three months. You didn't learn the hymn. I know exactly what she'd want, Fen, and it's exactly what she did, and I hate it, I hate it, I hate that she got to choose it without asking any of us—"

"THAT'S MY POINT!" Fen was crying now, furiously, hating that too. "Nobody asked! Nobody ever asks! The prophecy doesn't ask, Vess doesn't ask, Briarthorn doesn't ask, and now HESPER didn't ask, she just — she just decided we were worth more than her and went and DID it, and we're all supposed to carry that like it's a gift—"

The alders shivered. Not wind. The stream stuttered in its course for half a heartbeat, water pulling backward, and both twins went still, because they'd been around Mara long enough to know what the mountain sounding agitated meant.

Mara closed their eyes. When they spoke again, the flatness was back, but it was a different flatness — not shock now. Something harder underneath.

"Ilex," Mara said. "Where's Ilex?"

The cold came back into Fen's stomach all over again.

Ilex had gone out at dusk. Alone. Said they were checking the eastern trail for signs of pursuit. Said they'd be back by moonrise.

Moonrise had been an hour ago.

"Oh," Rook said softly. "Oh no."

"They wouldn't." But Fen heard the doubt in her own voice, and Mara heard it too, and that was the worst part — that none of the three of them, standing in the light of their burning home, could swear anymore that the charming botanist with the soft hands and the convenient knowledge hadn't walked out that gate straight into Vess's arms.

Or walked out that gate having already been in Vess's arms for weeks.

"The letters," Mara said. "Vess knew about the gift. She brought a silencer for the animals. She knew this place, Fen — she didn't circle the valley looking for it, she came straight to the dell like she'd read it off a map." Mara turned to face them fully, and behind them the Hollow Tree groaned, a vast wooden death-rattle that none of them flinched from, because flinching required having feeling left to spend. "Somebody drew her that map."

* * *

They ran east along the stream because running was the trade, and because grief with nowhere to go will settle for motion.

Two miles on, at the ford, they found the sign. Fresh hoofprints — Warden cavalry, a dozen at least, headed northeast. And beside the prints, dropped deliberately in the mud where it couldn't be missed, a single sprig of ilex, the holly-leaf plant Ilex wore tucked behind one ear when they wanted to be disarming.

On the underside of the leaf, scratched tiny with a pin, in a hand Fen recognized from the margins of every map Ilex had ever annotated:

**Trust the garden. Go to Sable's. Whatever you hear about me — go to the garden.**

Rook read it twice. "Is it true or is it bait?"

"Yes," said Fen.

And Mara, already turning toward the high country, toward the dead prophet's ruined garden where a blank page waited in a buried book, said nothing at all — and that silence frightened Fen more than the fire behind them, more than the hoofprints ahead, more than anything, because it was the silence of someone who had just finished deciding something, and Fen Pemberton had learned at nine years old what it meant when the people who were supposed to protect you started making decisions alone.
