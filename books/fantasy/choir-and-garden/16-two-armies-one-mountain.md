## 16. Two Armies, One Mountain

Odile Vess had burned forty-one structures in her career as a Warden, and she could still name every one of them.

Not the number—numbers were easy, numbers were ledgers—but the structures themselves. The mill at Colder's Ford with its wheel still turning when the flames took it. The waystation above Greywater, where a smuggler's child had watched from the treeline with eyes too old for his face. The hollow tree inn, three nights past, that had gone up like a struck match and taken with it whatever fragile lie these people had told themselves about being safe.

She kept the list because forgetting was how men like her predecessor had become butchers. She would not be a butcher. She would be an instrument, and instruments did not flinch, and instruments did not forget—they recorded.

"Captain." Lieutenant Sarn fell into step beside her, boots crunching on frost-hardened scree. "Scouts report movement below the treeline. Animals. Hundreds of them."

"Animals don't worry me, Sarn."

"These might. They're moving in formation."

Vess stopped at the ridgeline and looked down into the valley where the Thornfell's heart lay exposed like a wound: the sunken garden of the dead prophet, and beyond it, the roots of the mountain itself, where the old powers slept—or had slept, until a smuggler with a beast's tongue woke them by existing.

Her column stretched behind her along the ridge, two hundred Wardens in slate-grey, carrying the fire-cant weapons crated up from the capital in secret. Each weapon was a length of black iron banded with cant-script, and each one, when charged, threw a lance of flame that no water and no prayer could quench. The Chantry called them purifiers. The soldiers called them what they were: torches for burning things that fought back.

The Crown had authorized this action under the Sedition Act, clause nine, which permitted the destruction of "unlicensed spiritual entities constituting a threat to crown sovereignty." Vess had read the clause aloud to her officers the night before departure, her voice flat, her hands steady, because if she let even one tremor through, the whole column would smell fear and fear was a contagion worse than any spirit-plague.

Two hundred souls marching on the mountain because a book said so.

Because she said so.

"The prophecy is unambiguous," she had told the assembled officers, holding the confiscated pages of the codex—the copies; the original had eluded them again, always again, slipping through their fingers like water finding a crack. "'When two hosts meet upon the mountain, the mountain shall choose.' That word, *shall*. Not *may*. The mountain will choose, gentlemen, and I intend to ensure that when it chooses, there is only one side left standing to be chosen."

Sergeant Okafor, grey at the temples, had raised a hand. "And if the mountain chooses wrong, Captain?"

"There is no wrong," Vess had said. "There is order, and there is the absence of it. Everything else is poetry."

She believed it. She wanted that recorded too, somewhere in the ledger of herself: *she believed it.* Belief was not the same as certainty—certainty was for fools and prophets—but belief could be marched on. Belief could carry a pack and a weapon and the memory of forty-one fires.

* * *

They descended into the valley at dawn, or what passed for dawn beneath the Thornfell's permanent cloud-shroud: a bruised grey lightening toward something almost merciful. The fire-cant crews walked at the center of the column, weapons shouldered like pikes, the cant-script along each barrel glowing a sullen ember-red as they neared the mountain's heart. The script fed on proximity. The closer they came to the old powers, the hungrier the weapons grew.

Vess had felt it before, in other campaigns against other remnants—the lowland river-spirits put down twelve years ago, the marsh-court scattered when she was still a lieutenant learning which orders to obey without question. The weapons woke before the soldiers did. They knew what they were for.

"Captain." Sarn again, his voice carefully level. "You should see this."

He led her off the trail to a stand of birches, white as bone in the half-light. Carved into every trunk, at exactly the height of a human hand, were marks. Rows of them. Claw-marks, or knife-marks, or something between—spirals and slashes and shapes that hurt to look at directly, the way certain words hurt to say.

"It's writing," Sarn said. "It's been here for years. Decades, maybe."

"I can see that it's writing, Lieutenant."

"It's a muster roll." He said it quietly, the way men announced deaths. "Look at the repetition. Names, or the equivalent. It goes tree after tree after tree. Captain, they've been counting themselves. Preparing. This isn't a court we're surprising. This is a garrison."

Vess studied the marks. Under her gloved fingers, the nearest birch was warm. Warm, in the frost, warm like a living thing pretending to be wood, and she snatched her hand back before the thought could finish forming that it *was* a living thing, that the whole forest might be one creature with ten thousand mouths, and that she was about to lead two hundred children of the lowlands into its throat.

"Note the position," she said instead. "Map it. Move out."

But she carried the warmth in her palm the rest of the morning, like a coin she couldn't spend and couldn't discard.

* * *

At midday the scouts found the first of the dead.

Not people. Things. A stag the size of a plow-horse, antlers grown through with moss and small white flowers, lying in a clearing as though it had lain down mid-stride and simply stopped. Its body was whole. There was no wound. But when Vess knelt beside it—because someone had to, because a captain who asked her soldiers to face a thing must face it first—she saw that the ground beneath it was scorched in a perfect circle, and the grass within the circle was not burnt but *blessed*, some old cant woven through it that made her teeth ache.

"A boundary," murmured Wren, the company's cant-reader, a thin woman who spoke to weapons the way other people spoke to horses. "It crossed a line it wasn't permitted to cross. Or it died defending the line. I can't tell which, Captain. The script is older than ours. Older than anything in the archives."

"Then burn the archive when we get home," Vess said, and heard the cruelty in her own voice arrive a half-second after the words, too late to catch. Wren looked at her with something that was not quite reproach. Vess stood. "I didn't mean—" She stopped. She had never once in twenty years begun a sentence she couldn't finish, and she wasn't going to start now, not here, not with the mountain listening. "Move out."

That afternoon she allowed herself the letter.

She wrote them at night usually, in camp, by lamplight: letters to a sister in the capital who never answered, letters to a dead mentor whose grave she paid a gardener to keep, letters to a protégé named Ilex Vale who had vanished four years ago into a fog of botanical obsession and quiet treason. The letters were never sent. They lived in a locked case in her quarters, three hundred strong, and she understood perfectly well what they were—a ledger, again, a record of every thought too dangerous to speak—and she understood equally well that understanding a weakness is not the same as curing it.

Today's letter was short. She composed it walking, dictating silently, the discipline of it keeping her spine straight:

*Ilex. Today I found trees full of names and a god wearing a deer's body, and my soldiers are afraid, and I am afraid, and I have discovered that fear in a commander is not a flaw to be corrected but a fuel to be rationed. You would find that funny. You found everything funny except the things that mattered, which is why you left, or why I drove you out—I have never decided which, and I suspect the answer would cost me more than I am willing to pay.*

*We are going to win tomorrow. I want that written plainly, so that whoever reads this understands: I chose this. The prophecy gave us a shape and I stepped into it with both eyes open. If there is a reckoning, let it find me standing.*

She sealed the letter and put it in the case and did not think about the fact that the last page of the codex, the page every scholar agreed existed and none had seen, was blank.

Blank pages were for cowards. Blank pages meant someone had refused to finish a sentence. In Odile Vess's experience, everything true had already been written; the work of a life was reading it correctly.

* * *

They reached the threshold at dusk.

The prophet's garden opened before them like a held breath: terraces gone to riot, hedges grown into arches and corridors, roses the color of arterial blood climbing walls of ancient stone. At its center, visible from a mile off, stood the ruin of Prophet Sable's house, its roof fallen in, its windows empty—and beyond the garden, the land dropped away into a vast bowl of rock where the air itself moved wrong, thickening, deepening, the way air moves before lightning.

The spirits were already gathering.

Vess had expected shadows, whispers, the guerrilla tactics of creatures that feared iron and daylight. Instead the bowl filled with light. Roots surfaced from the stone like whales breaching, vast and pale and deliberate. Shapes formed among the trees at the bowl's rim—tall ones, crowned ones, faces like weather seen through leaves. And beneath everything, rising through the soles of her boots, a vibration like a drum played by something with a heartbeat the size of the mountain's.

"They're summoning the old powers," Wren whispered. "Captain, the deep ones. The ones from before the pact. The ones the pact was built to keep asleep."

"Then the pact is already broken," Vess said, "by them. Note it. Record it. When the histories are written, let it be remembered that they broke faith first."

Even as she said it, some cold auditor in the back of her mind raised its pen and wrote: *You do not know that. You are choosing to believe the version that licenses the fire.* She let the auditor write. She let it fill its little pages. Then she gave the order.

"Fire-cant companies forward. Form the crescent at the garden wall. Nothing crosses that wall until my signal." She turned to Sarn. "Run up the standard."

The Crown's banner rose over Prophet Sable's ruined house, grey and gold, snapping in a wind that smelled of sap and smoke and something older than either. Two hundred soldiers looked at it and straightened. Two hundred soldiers needed it to mean something. Vess had spent her whole life making banners mean things; it was the only magic she had ever been offered, and she had mastered it.

Across the bowl, the answering light gathered and gathered, and Vess understood—with the clarity that comes only at the point of no return—that the thing assembling itself down there was not an army. Armies were made of soldiers who could break, flee, surrender. What was forming in the dark below was more like a choir. A choir of everything she had spent twenty years insisting was not real.

And it was singing.

Not sound, precisely. Pressure. Meaning arriving through the ground, through the teeth, through the soft parts behind the eyes. Her soldiers were beginning to feel it—she saw men glance at one another, saw a boy near the front drop his pack and clutch at his ears—and Vess drew her sidearm and fired a single shot into the air, the crack of it cutting across the gathering hum like a blade.

"Hear me!" she shouted, and her voice carried the way it always carried, trained on parade grounds and siege lines, a voice built to be obeyed. "Down there is a power that has slept a hundred years while your villages burned and your roads went unsafe and your children went hungry! Down there is the reason for every levy and every lost harvest and every name on every memorial stone in Thornfell province! Tonight it wakes, and it offers you a choice: kneel or be ruled. And I am telling you"—she leveled her arm at the bowl, at the light, at the choir—"we did not climb this mountain to kneel!"

The roar that came back from her soldiers was real. That was the terrible part. It was not fear dressed as courage; it was courage, genuine, earned, fed on every grievance the lowlands had ever nursed against the high dark places. They believed her. She had made sure they believed her, and now their belief was a weapon pointed at a thing that had done nothing yet, that was only waking, that might have woken into peace—

*Stop*, said the auditor in the back of her mind, gently, fatally. *Read the sentence you just wrote. Read it all the way to the end.*

She read it. She let it stand.

Because the alternative was to lower her arm in front of two hundred people, and captains who lower their arms get their columns killed, and she had promised—she had *promised*—that no one else would die for her hesitation. Bram's people. The inn. The waystation child. Every fire on her list had a lesson attached and the lesson was always the same: *decide, Odile. Decide before the deciding is done to you.*

"Signal watch," she said quietly to Sarn. "If anything comes up the slope, the fire-cants answer. My authority alone. No one fires before my word."

"And if the word doesn't come, Captain?" Sarn asked. "If they come up the slope and the word doesn't come?"

Vess looked out at the valley filling with light, at the standard snapping over a dead man's garden, at the ember-glow of two hundred cant-scripts brightening along their barrels like veins filling with blood.

"The word will come," she said.

* * *

It came at moonrise, but not the way she intended.

The mountain spoke first. Not Briarthorn, not the choir, not any of the exiled court she had catalogued and dismissed—the mountain itself, a groan through thirty miles of stone that set the scree sliding on three slopes at once and knocked every soldier in the crescent to one knee. Vess stayed upright by gripping the garden wall, and in that moment of grinding dark she felt the cant-weapons surge, all two hundred scripts flaring from ember to blaze, drinking the mountain's fury like wine.

And in the flare, she saw him.

A figure on the far rim of the bowl, small against the massed light of the spirit-host, standing on a spur of rock where no soldier of hers could reach. Even at that distance she knew the silhouette—the slight forward lean of someone perpetually about to ask a question, a habit she had once found charming and then insufferable and then, in the four silent years, had privately renamed *courage*.

Ilex Vale stood between the armies.

Vess's hand closed around the garden wall hard enough to bite stone. Of course. Of course he was here. The false routes he'd fed her people, the amnesty she'd dangled and revoked, the careful arithmetic of letting him run free because a running traitor leads you truer than a caged one—all of it had converged here, tonight, on this ridge, and now the variable she had counted on had planted himself in the one place her equations couldn't reach.

"Captain," Sarn breathed. "Orders?"

Below, the choir swelled. Above, the standard cracked. Between them, on his impossible rock, Ilex raised both arms—not in surrender, not in defiance, in the gesture of a lecturer calling a room to order—and though no voice could carry that distance, the mountain carried it for him. The words arrived through the ground, through her boots, through the bones of her jaw, in a voice that was his and was not his, braided through with something vast:

"*Both of you are reading the same book!*"

Vess went very still.

"*She thinks the last page is hers,*" the voice said, and it was speaking to the host below, but it was speaking to her, it had always been speaking to her, and God help her, she listened. "*And the queen thinks the blank page belongs to her heir. You're both wrong. Come and see what the prophet actually wrote—or burn the library before anyone reads it. Those are your choices. Choose fast.*"

Silence. Real silence, the mountain holding its breath.

Two hundred soldiers looked at their captain. A mile away, a queen of roots and teeth looked at the same figure on the rock. And Odile Vess, instrument of the Crown, keeper of forty-one fires, author of three hundred unsent letters, felt the cold auditor in her mind finally set down its pen and fold its hands, waiting to see whether she would read—or burn.

Her hand moved toward the signal horn.

Paused.

Moved again.
