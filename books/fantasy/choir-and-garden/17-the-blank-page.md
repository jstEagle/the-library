## 17. The Blank Page

The garden had changed since the night Mara first stumbled into it.

That was the first thing they noticed, standing at the broken gate with dawn still gray behind the peaks. The overgrowth that had once hidden Prophet Sable's ruin like a secret now arranged itself into paths—briars pulled back from the walkway, moonflowers turned on their stems to face the door of the collapsed observatory, as if the whole place had been tidied for company. Not for a guest. For an heir.

"You feel it too," Ilex said behind them. Their voice was rough; they'd barely slept since Hesper was taken, since Fen's accusation had split the group like a dropped plate. "It knows you're here."

"It knew me before I knew myself," Mara said. "That's the problem."

They went in anyway.

* * *

The heart of the garden was a ring of white stones half-swallowed by moss, and in its center stood the thing Mara had been circling for weeks without understanding: an old plum tree, dead these hundred years, its trunk split down the middle by lightning or by something older than weather. The Vellum Codex had been found beneath its roots. The book itself rode in Mara's satchel now, heavy as a sleeping animal, warm through the leather in a way books should not be.

Mara knelt and pressed their palm to the split trunk.

And the mountain spoke—not in words, not in the goat-speech or the crow-speech or the low grammar of stone that only came to them in dreams, but in something underneath all of those, something like the silence between notes. *Here,* it said. *Here is where she stood when she understood.*

"Sable," Mara whispered.

The tree answered with memory.

It came up through their hand like cold water: a woman with silver-streaked hair kneeling where Mara knelt, a century ago, her hands shaking around a quill made of a jay's feather. She was weeping. Mara felt her weeping as if it were their own throat closing—the specific grief of someone who has just written a prophecy and watched it begin to come true. Around her, spirits crowded the garden's edge: root-things and smoke-things, the small bright presences of brook and beetle, all of them waiting for her verdict. All of them afraid.

She wrote the codex as a cage, Mara realized. No—as a lock. A prophecy is a lock, if you write it well enough. Everyone who read it would be bound by it: Wardens hunting the heir, the court demanding the heir, the heir themselves bent under the weight of words inked before their grandmother's grandmother drew breath. Everyone reading the same book toward war.

But a lock needs a key. And a good locksmith always—

"Leaves herself a way out," Mara said aloud. The words fell into the split trunk and vanished, swallowed.

"What?" Ilex crouched beside them, soil darkening their knees. "What did it show you?"

"She left the last page blank." Mara pulled the satchel around and drew out the codex. Its cover was warm under their fingers, warmer than the morning justified. "Not blank because she ran out of time. Blank because—" Their voice caught. They tried again. "Because the whole thing is a question. Renew the pact or sever it, the court says. Obey the prophecy or burn it, Vess says. And every answer everyone's given so far has been one of her two bad options, because nobody looked at the last page and asked why it was empty."

Ilex stared at them. Then, slowly, they smiled—that crooked botanist's smile that meant *you've just described a plant I told you was extinct.* "A trapdoor."

"A door," Mara corrected. "She built the house. She left the door unlocked for whoever came after." They opened the codex.

* * *

The pages turned themselves, or seemed to—the wind moving them, or the mountain's breath. Prophecies slid past that Mara now knew by heart: the beast-tongued heir, the traitor who opens the door, the fire that eats what order builds. At the end, the final page. White. Patient.

Mara had touched this page before, in the hollow tree, with Hesper watching over their shoulder, and felt nothing but paper. Now they laid their palm flat against it and the mountain rose up through the page like a tide.

*Speak,* said everything at once—goats and gulls and the deep slow voice of the roots, the choir that had followed them across three chapters of running. *Speak, and it will be law.*

"I don't know the words," Mara said. "Sable knew the old cant. She trained her whole life. I know goat-trails and smuggler's tolls and how to keep people invisible. That's not—I can't write a pact. That takes power I don't have."

The mountain was quiet in the way that a teacher goes quiet when the student has almost got it.

And then Mara did have it, all at once, the way you understand a fall only after it starts. The gift. The beast-speech. The thing they'd been born with, the one true thing about themselves, the reason smugglers paid in silver and Wardens burned waystations and a spirit-queen had put a leash in their hands and called it honor.

The page didn't want Sable's words. It wanted the heir's. And the heir's words could only be spoken by someone the mountain could actually hear—which meant someone still joined to it. Which meant:

"No," Mara said.

*Mara.* The tide of voices gentled, the way Bram used to gentle his mules. *Child. Speak.*

"If I speak my own terms—if the pact gets remade in my words—then the words have to be *mine*, all the way through. Not borrowed. Not translated." Their hands were shaking. They kept talking anyway, because if they stopped they'd run, and they'd spent their whole life running invisibly and look what that had bought. "The magic doesn't translate. It *is* the translation. The speech is the bridge. If I make the bridge out of myself—"

The page warmed under their palm. Confirmation. Agreement. Yes.

"—then after, there's no bridge. There's no speech. There's no—" Mara heard their own voice crack and hated it. "There's no me-as-I-am. I'd be speaking my personhood into the pact. My gift *is* the price, and the price is the whole of it, and afterward I'd be—" 

Human. Just human. Just Mara Sterling, who couldn't ask a goat which pass was clear, who couldn't hear the mountain coming, who couldn't call the crows as scouts or calm a spooked pack-mule with a word. Who would stand in the middle of a battlefield between Odile Vess's fire-cant and Briarthorn's roots and be, for the first time in their life, completely ordinary. Completely deaf to everything they loved.

"Ilex," Mara said. "If I do this. Tell me what I am afterward."

Ilex didn't answer right away. They sat back on their heels, and Mara watched them choose honesty over comfort—it moved across their face like weather—and then say, quietly: "Someone who finally belongs to us instead of to the mountain."

"That's a terrible answer."

"It's the true one." Ilex reached out and closed their hand over Mara's, over the page, so that four hands rested on the blankness. "You've been the cargo and the guide and the heir and the weapon. You've never once gotten to just be Mara. This is the only door out of that, and she built it a hundred years ago for exactly this moment, and you're allowed to be terrified of it. But don't pretend it isn't a door."

"The armies are already marching." Mara could feel it distantly, the way you feel a storm through a floor: Vess's columns on the south road, fire-cant humming in its iron casings; below, Briarthorn waking the old powers, the ones even the spirit-court feared, the teeth under the roots. Two readings of the same book, converging on the mountain's heart. On this garden. Probably on tonight. "I don't have time to learn new words."

"You're not going to learn them." Ilex's grip tightened. "You're going to mean them. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that's ever mattered to you."

Mara laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "When did you get wise?"

"When I started losing things." A shadow crossed their face—Vess, amnesty, the letters, the betrayal that wasn't one and nearly was. "Ask me again when we survive."

* * *

Mara stayed alone in the garden while the light climbed.

They walked the ring of stones. They spoke—to the air, out of habit, and got nothing, because the garden's small spirits had gone quiet, listening, giving them room. That silence was the worst part. All their life the world had been full of voices: the complaint of goats, the gossip of gulls, the long slow patience of pines. Mara had complained about the noise often enough, privately, lying awake in waystations. Now, imagining it gone forever, the noise sounded like the only home they'd ever had.

Bram's voice surfaced, unbidden: *Gifts aren't wages, little wren. They're tools. And a tool you can't put down owns you.*

He'd said it about drink, once, drunk himself, the night the twins' mother died. Mara hadn't understood then. They understood now, kneeling in a dead prophet's garden with a blank page burning gently against their chest.

Because here was the clarity, terrible and clean as ice water: the choice wasn't power versus safety. It wasn't even the pact versus war. Those were the false options, Sable's two locked doors, the ones everyone else was still battering their shoulders against. The real choice was smaller and vaster. It was this: remain the mountain's voice and let everyone else decide what the voice should say—or become only themselves, once, briefly, at the exact center of everything, and say it plain.

Power, or personhood.

The gift had never been theirs. That was the truth under the terror. It had arrived without consent, shaped every friendship (the smugglers valued the tool), defined every enemy (the Wardens feared the tool), and even now the spirit-court called them *heir*—a word for property passing between owners. Briarthorn's leash. Vess's warrant. The prophecy's title. Everyone had held a handle, and Mara had been the blade.

The blank page asked only: *who holds the pen?*

"I do," Mara said aloud, testing it. Their voice sounded strange in the empty garden. Small. Human. Exactly right.

They took out the codex one more time and looked at the final page for a long moment—not reading it, since there was nothing to read, but greeting it. An invitation a century old, addressed to no one, meaning them.

Then they closed the book and stood, and their legs held.

Far to the south, faint as a struck match, the sky over Thornfell's lower passes glowed the wrong color. Fire-cant, waking. And beneath their feet—no, not beneath their feet anymore, they realized, they couldn't feel it properly, that was the point—in the roots and the stone, something vast began to move uphill, answering.

Dawn broke gold over the ridge, and down the garden path, limping and arguing and alive, came the strays: Fen with Rook leaning on her shoulder, bandaged and furious about it; Hesper, free, ash still in her hair, carrying the stolen Warden seal like a trophy she intended to melt down; Ilex walking backward ahead of them all, talking fast, pointing at the plum tree.

Fen saw Mara standing in the ring of stones and stopped dead. "Well?" she called. Her voice carried the whole ruined night in it—the inn, the flames, the blame. "Did the dead woman tell you what you're supposed to do?"

Mara looked at the family that had chosen to stay, at the armies climbing toward them from opposite ends of the world, at the book in their hands.

"No," Mara said. "She told me what I'm supposed to *say*. And I'm going to need everyone to hear it."

Below the ridge, the first horns of Vess's column sounded through the trees.

"*Everyone*," Fen repeated flatly. "Mara, the entire mountain is about to—"

"I know," said Mara, and opened the codex to the blank page, and began, at last, to write.
