## 18. Choir of Beasts

**Vess**

Captain Odile Vess had marched on many things in her twenty years of service—a smuggler's den, a heretic's chapel, a village that sheltered a spirit-touched child—and every time, the arithmetic had been simple. Fire against flesh. Order against rot.

She had never marched on a mountain that was looking back at her.

The Thornfell pass opened before her column like a throat. Two hundred soldiers, fire-cant lances burning blue-white in their racks, boots striking stone in rhythm. Ahead, the spirit-court's host poured down the slopes: figures of root and antler and moss-heavy bone, the old powers rising out of the ground itself like water finding its level. And below them both, wedged into the narrow vale where the pact-stone stood, a handful of humans—smugglers, a disgraced healer, a botanist with dirt under his nails—arrayed like a fence post between two avalanches.

"Captain," said Lieutenant Corren, his voice carefully flat. "The beasts."

She had already seen them. She had been trying not to see them for a quarter mile.

Goats first—hundreds of them, spilling down the scree in their ragged, sure-footed way. Then deer, then wolves moving without hunger in their bodies, then bears ambling with the unhurried confidence of creatures who had never once been afraid. Birds in rivers overhead. Things she had no name for, shapes from the deep caves, pale and blinking at daylight. Every animal on this mountain was coming down it, and they were not fleeing.

They were assembling.

"Fire-cant ready," Vess said. "On my mark only."

Corren glanced at her. "Ma'am, if they charge—"

"They aren't charging." She lifted her glass and swept the field. "Look at their ears. Look at their spacing. That isn't a stampede formation, Lieutenant. That's a *procession*."

It was worse. A charge she could answer. A procession meant intention, and intention meant a mind behind it, and the only mind on this mountain that animals had ever obeyed—

"Sterling," she said quietly.

The codex sat in her saddlebag, recovered from the burned inn, Hesper's blood still crusted on its cover. She had read the final pages by lamplight while her soldiers slept. *The Beast-Tongued Heir shall renew or sever.* Renew or sever. Those were the options. Those had always been the options. The book was a century old and perfectly clear, and Odile Vess had built her whole life on the principle that clear things could be managed.

So why did the blank leaf at the codex's end keep surfacing in her thoughts like a body in a pond?

"Mark," she said. "Lances lit. We advance on my word. And Corren—" she lowered the glass, "—if any creature comes at us with its teeth bared, you may defend yourself. If they come at us with anything else, hold."

"Hold, Captain?"

"You heard me." She spurred her horse forward. "We did not come here to make war on goats. We came here to end a prophecy. Let's see which one we actually get."

**Fen**

Fen had fought in three scrapes in her life, and all of them had been the kind where you picked your ground, counted your knives, and knew roughly how badly it would go. This was not that kind. This was two armies and a menagerie and her brother standing beside her with a broken wrist wrapped in a sling made from Ilex's shirt, and the whole vale smelled like crushed pine and ozone and fear-sweat that was mostly hers.

"They're coming," Rook said. "Both lines. Same moment. Of course they are."

"Course they are," Fen said. "Everyone wants to be first through the door when nobody knows what's behind it."

Below them, past the smugglers' ragged line—what was left of it, thirty-odd souls who'd chosen a lost cause over a safe one—the Warden column advanced in perfect squares, fire-cant glowing along the ranks like a lit fuse. From above came the spirit-host, and the sound of them was the worst part. Not war drums. *Growing.* The creak of roots forcing stone. It sounded like the mountain clearing its throat.

And threaded through all of it, the animals. Flowing between the armies like a river between banks. Wolves pacing the Wardens' flank. Deer standing in the spirit-court's path and not moving. Goats everywhere, because there were always goats, and Fen caught herself scanning their faces like she was looking for someone.

She was. They both were.

"There," Rook said.

Mara stood at the center of everything, alone, at the pact-stone, and around them the animals had arranged themselves—not as a wall, not as a weapon, but as something else. Rows. Curved lines, tiered back up the slopes. Fen had grown up on smuggler songs and chapel hymns both, and she knew what rows of bodies like that were called.

"That's a choir," she said stupidly. "Rook. That's a *choir*."

"Choirs need a song."

"Then let's hope our beast-speaker remembered the words."

A hawk screamed somewhere above, high and long, and every animal in the vale answered it—not the same cry, but the same *moment*, wolves and wrens and cave-things all lifting their voices together, and the sound went through Fen's chest like a struck bell. Beside her, hard-eyed Rook, who hadn't cried when his wrist snapped, made a small wet sound in his throat.

"Don't," Fen said, because her own eyes were doing the same thing. "Don't you dare make me feel things before a battle."

"Too late," Rook said. "Cargo."

That was the old joke. *The cargo.* Except somewhere between a rockslide and a burned inn, neither of them had noticed the cargo becoming crew, and now the whole sky was full of voices, and Fen drew her knives because it was the only prayer she knew.

The armies collided at the edges of the vale—fire meeting root with a crack like the world splitting—and the choir sang louder.

* * *

**Ilex**

Ilex had spent his life among plants, and plants taught patience: everything worth having grew slow. But watching Vess's fire-cant burn a line through the spirit-host's outer ranks—watching briar-champions come apart into smoke and embers—he understood that some growths happen in an instant, catastrophically, like a frost.

"Mara can't hold both of them," Hesper said at his shoulder. The healer's hands were steady, but her voice wasn't. "The armies or the choir. They're trying to be the hinge of the whole door and they're one person."

"Then we're the frame." He looked down the slope at the Warden line, at the tall figure in captain's grey directing it like a conductor. Odile Vess. His teacher. His almost-friend. The woman whose letters he'd fed lies for three weeks while his stomach ate itself. "Vess believes the codex gives her permission. So we take the codex out of the argument."

"With what? You've read it. The last page is blank."

Ilex reached into his coat and felt the shape of it—the codex, stolen back from the ash of the inn, wrapped in oilcloth against his heart. Hesper stared at him.

"You—when did you—"

"While you were being dragged out of a burning tree by your hair. You were busy. I wasn't." He allowed himself half a smile. "Traitor's privileges."

"If Vess sees that book on you, she'll cut it out of you."

"She'll have to catch me first." He turned to the smugglers nearest them—Fen, Rook, a dozen others with borrowed weapons and no doctrine. "Here's the shape of it. The armies meet where the ground's widest. Nobody's watching the flanks except goats. We go through the goat-lines, left of the pact-stone, and we put this book in Mara's hands before either army reaches them. Because right now both sides think the prophecy is a command. And it isn't. It's a page waiting for ink."

"And if we don't get there?" Rook asked.

Ilex looked out at the fire, and the roots, and the small lone figure between them.

"Then we find out what the mountain sounds like when it loses its voice," he said, "and I never want to know. Move."

**Briarthorn**

The Queen of Roots and Teeth had waited a hundred years beneath the frost-line, and she had imagined this morning ten thousand ways. In most of them, the Beast-Tongued Heir spoke the Severance with a steady voice and grateful eyes, and the chains of the old pact dissolved like sugar in rain, and the human villages that had fattened on spirit-bound luck burned or starved or fled—she had not pretended, even privately, that justice would be gentle. Justice outside law has teeth. She was named for hers.

In none of her ten thousand imaginings had the heir stood silent at the pact-stone while *animals* gathered to sing at her.

"What is it doing?" snarled Cragvetch, her shield-lord, bark-face cracked with fury as a fresh volley of fire-cant tore through the eastern rank. "The armies bleed and the heir stands there like a stone!"

"It is listening," Briarthorn said.

"To what?"

"To something older than my claim." She hated saying it. Saying it made it true. She descended the slope through the smoke, root-legs drinking deep, and the wild chorus parted around her—wolf and wren and the pale cave-things, all of them singing, none of them kneeling. None of them *hers*. She had assumed, as she had always assumed, that the beasts belonged to whoever held the pact. But they moved like free people moving toward a thing they wanted.

Like her people had moved, once, before the binding.

A fire-cant lance screamed past her shoulder and took an oak-brother apart into embers, and her grief came up green and furious—and still the animals sang, and still the heir stood unmoving at the stone, lips slightly parted, hands open, and Briarthorn understood with cold, descending horror that she was about to lose. Not the battle. The *argument*. Whatever was happening at that stone was bigger than Severance, bigger than renewal, and she had brought ten thousand years of grievance to a fight that had already changed shape.

"Cease the advance," she said.

Cragvetch gaped. " Majesty—"

"*Hold the line and cease advancing.*" Her voice shook loam loose from the hillside. "If we trample that heir before they finish speaking, we will never know what we killed."

And beneath her, far beneath, the old powers stirred in their long sleep, listening too.

* * *

**Hesper**

Hesper had healed soldiers through six border campaigns, and she knew the exact moment a battle stops being a battle and becomes a disaster: it's when both commanders start making decisions based on what they're afraid of instead of what they can see.

Vess was afraid. She could read it from three hundred yards—the too-straight back, the orders snapped twice, the way the fire-cant volleys came faster and less aimed. And Briarthorn—Briarthorn had gone still in the wrong way, the stillness of a chess player realizing the board is larger than she thought.

Between them, the choir.

Hesper had refused one order in her life, and it had cost her everything—her rank, her name, her place in the world—and she had never once regretted it, though she had often regretted being alone in it. Now she crouched behind the pact-stone's shadow with a stolen seal in her pocket and a burned lung that ached with every breath, and she watched a hundred animals sing, and she thought: *this is what I refused the order for.* Not this exact hour. But this kind of hour. The kind where mercy needs witnesses.

"The codex," Ilex gasped, arriving at a run with the twins behind him, smoke-streaked and alive. "Where's—"

"There." She pointed.

Mara stood at the stone with their head bowed, and as Hesper watched, they raised their hands—not commanding, she realized, *inviting*—and the choir's song shifted key, rose, broke open into harmonies that had no business existing in animal throats, and every hair on Hesper's arms stood up, and her ruined breath came easier, and the fire-cant nearest them guttered like a candle in a doorway.

"They're not fighting," she whispered. "They're *translating*."

"For who?" Fen demanded.

"For everyone," Hesper said. "For anyone willing to hear it. Which means—" and the old Warden discipline surfaced through the shame like a bone through skin, "—which means our job is to make sure nobody interrupts. Wardens, form a screen. Smugglers, flank. Twins, keep Ilex upright, he's about to do something noble and stupid."

"I'm right here," Ilex said.

"I know, dear. That's how I know."

* * *

**Mara**

Mara had expected to die here.

They had walked into the vale knowing the odds—two armies, one gift, one chance—and they had put their hands on the pact-stone expecting the end. What came instead was the beginning of something so large they could barely hold the edge of it.

Because the animals came. Not summoned—Mara had summoned nothing, dared nothing—but *coming*, streaming down from every hollow and height of Thornfell, every creature they had ever spoken with across nine years of smuggling routes and midnight camps. The goat matriarch with the crooked horn from the first pass they'd ever crossed. The wolf-pack whose pups they'd pulled from a flood channel. The blind cave-fox. The old bear who traded riddles for honey. Birds whose names Mara had only ever known in birdsong.

And none of them knelt. None of them waited for orders. They arrived the way family arrives at a sickbed—not because they were called, but because there was nowhere else they would rather be—and they arranged themselves in curved, patient lines, and when the hawk gave the first high note, Mara understood what the mountain had decided to do.

It had decided to speak *with* them.

The song rose through Mara's gift like water through a root. Not words. Older than words. The sound of the pact itself—the first bargain, struck a thousand years ago between Sable's people and the spirits' elders, back when it had been a covenant and not a chain. Mara heard it whole for the first time: what was promised, what was taken, what had rotted in the centuries of quiet. They heard where it had bent out of true. And underneath it, faint as a heartbeat under wool, they heard the blank page calling—not demanding words, *waiting* for them, the way good soil waits.

*Renew or sever,* the codex had said.

But the song said otherwise. The song said: *there was always a third verse. No one stayed to sing it.*

On the slopes, the armies crashed and burned and bled. Briarthorn's grief, Vess's certainty, ten thousand years of injury and twenty years of order grinding against each other—and Mara stood between them with the whole mountain singing through their ribs, and felt the moment arrive the way you feel a storm arrive, pressure first, then hush.

Their voice, when they finally used it, was very small against all that music. It didn't matter. Everything was listening.

"Stop," Mara said—in beast-speech, in plain speech, in the old cant of the pact, all three at once. "Both of you. Before another name goes into the ground."

And whether it was the choir or the pact-stone or simply the strange authority of one exhausted person refusing to move, the front lines wavered. Fire-cant flickered. Roots paused mid-stride. Two hundred soldiers and ten thousand years of exile looked, for one suspended breath, at the small figure at the stone with their hands open and their face wet.

Mara turned to Ilex, running through the goat-lines with an oilcloth bundle hugged to his chest, and held out their hand.

"Ilex," they said. "Bring me the book. It's time to write."

Behind him, Captain Odile Vess spurred her horse forward through the smoke, sword drawn, shouting orders no one could hear over the singing—and the mountain, gently, began to close its ranks around her.
