## 19. Words of My Own

The garden had never been quiet. Mara understood that now.

Even as the mountain shook above it—even as the sky to the south glowed orange with Warden fire-cant and the roots below groaned like a ship in a storm—the garden of Prophet Sable hummed with a patience older than either army. The roses Sable had planted still climbed their trellises, black-thorned and blooming out of season. The well still breathed cold air. And at the center of it all, on a stone table worn smooth by a century of rain, the Vellum Codex lay open to its final page.

Blank.

Waiting.

Mara stood over it with ink-blackened fingers and a quill cut from a goose feather Ilex had pressed into their hand an hour ago, back when the world still had edges. *You're the only one who can write it,* he'd said. *So write it.*

Behind them, the pass screamed.

Mara turned once, because they were human and couldn't help it. From the garden's high terrace they could see it all: Odile Vess's column winding up the pilgrim road, torches strung along it like a lit fuse, the fire-cant engines gleaming wet and hungry in the dark. Below and to the east, the forest itself was moving—Briarthorn's roots surfacing in waves, thorn-walls rising and collapsing, the exiled court pouring uphill in their antlered hundreds. And between the two armies, holding the line no one had ordered them to hold: the animals.

Wolves shoulder to shoulder with sheepdogs. Goats standing in the fire-line, chewing, immovable, the same goats Mara had bargained with for years on the smuggler trails. A bear sat down in front of a Warden engine and simply did not move, and the engine did not fire, because the crew could not make themselves do it. Every creature Mara had ever spoken with—not commanded, never commanded—had come, and formed a living choir between the lines of two armies who both believed the same book licensed them to burn the mountain.

*Ilex,* Mara thought. *Hesper. Fen and Rook.* Somewhere down there in all that smoke. They'd scattered them at the inn, at the cell door, at the bridge, but the family kept reassembling around Mara like water finding its level. That was the thing about found family nobody warned you about: you didn't get to protect it by pushing it away. It just kept choosing you back.

*Enough.*

Mara turned to the page.

The blankness looked different now than it had three days ago, when they'd first understood what it was. Then it had looked like a test. Like a trap dressed as mercy. Now, with Sable's whole story finally legible in their mind—the prophet who'd been a beast-speaker too, who'd written the codex not as command but warning, who'd left the last page empty on purpose—it looked like something else entirely.

It looked like a door held open by someone long dead, saying: *I couldn't finish it. It wasn't mine to finish.*

The ground lurched. A root the width of a house broke through the terrace wall, showering petals, and from its crown rose Briarthorn—or her voice did, carried through wood and sap until every leaf in the garden trembled with it.

"Heir." The queen's voice was honey poured over gravel. "The moment is now. Speak the Severance. Free my people, and be remembered forever as the hand that broke our chains."

"And bury Thornfell's villages under everything my people ever built," Mara said aloud. Their voice sounded small and steady, which surprised them. "I know the terms, Your Majesty. You've been very clear."

"The terms were written before your grandmother's grandmother. You do not renegotiate prophecy."

"No," Mara said. "You rewrite it."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere below, a fire-cant engine discharged—a whump of heat and light—and the bear roared, and men screamed, and Briarthorn's attention did not waver from the small figure at the stone table.

"You would dare," the queen said slowly, "to put your own words where Sable's should be."

"Sable left it empty." Mara lifted the quill. "That's the whole point of her, and none of you ever read her right. Not the Wardens, not you. She wasn't giving orders down a century. She was asking a question. And questions don't get answered by the people she named. They get answered by whoever's actually standing here when it matters."

"Your words have no power," Briarthorn said. "Only the pact's words have power. Only the old forms bind."

"Then we'll find out," said Mara Sterling, smuggler's guide, beast-speaker, orphan of the waystation, heir to nothing but a choice—and bent to the page.

* * *

The quill moved strangely, as if the paper were drinking rather than receiving. Mara wrote fast, in the plain hand Bram had drilled into them for ledger-keeping, because fancy words felt like lying.

They wrote:

*We are not heirs. We are neighbors.*

*Let this stand instead of the old pact, and instead of the Severance:*

*One. The spirits hold the high places—the peaks, the deep woods, the rivers' sources—and walk where they will, unhunted, unchained, unbought. No leash. No queen's claim on any speaker's throat. Freedom is the first term or there are no terms.*

*Two. The lowlands belong to the people who farm and herd and bury their dead there. The spirits cross those bounds only in peace, as guests, as weather passes—as anything passing may.*

*Three. Where the two meet, we meet in person. Once each year, at Sable's garden, a speaker chosen by the beasts and a voice chosen by the court sit at this table and speak grievances aloud. Nothing festers in silence. Silence is how the last pact died.*

*Four. When harm comes—from either side—the one who caused it answers for it. Not their kind. Not their blood. Them. Accountability has a face, or it has nothing.*

*Five. This pact binds no one forever. Any generation may gather at this table and write new terms, in their own hand, for the world they actually live in. The mountain changes. So must the words.*

*Signed not by destiny, but by consent:*

And there Mara stopped, quill hovering, because the last line needed more than ink.

*It needs your voice,* whispered the garden—all of it at once, roses and well and stone. *Sable's design ends in speech. Ink prepares; the tongue seals. That was always the price. To speak the pact yourself is to give up the gift that lets you hear the other side at all.*

Mara closed their eyes.

They had known. Since childhood—since the day they'd realized they knew how the pact ended, how the Severance ran, how easily a voice could unravel a century of binding—they had known, and stayed silent out of fear. Fear on the bridge. Fear in the hollow tree. Fear in every bargain with every goat, hiding inside usefulness so no one would ever look closely enough to ask what else they were.

But fear wasn't the whole story anymore. There was also Fen calling them *the cargo* and then not calling them that. Rook's hand on their shoulder after the landslide. Hesper saying *you eat at my table, that makes you mine to worry about.* Ilex's terrible, beautiful honesty. Bram dying so they could run, and running being over now.

If speaking cost the gift—if the price of making peace was never again hearing the mountain talk—then it was a price paid once, in full, instead of collected forever from everyone else.

Below, the war reached its crescendo. Fire-cant met root-wall and the night went white. Mara heard, impossibly, over all of it, a wolf howling a note that meant *now*, and knew whose side the mountain had taken, and wept without stopping writing.

They finished the last line:

*Mara Sterling, who chooses.*

Then they lifted their face to the burning sky, filled their lungs with smoke and rose-scent and rain, and spoke every word aloud.

* * *

The magic did not explode. It *unraveled*.

Mara felt it go the way you feel a knot slip loose—every strand of the old pact, a century of binding laid into the mountain's bones, releasing thread by thread. The Wardens felt it too; the fire-cant engines guttered as the cant that fueled them lost its purchase on the land. Briarthorn cried out—not in pain, in *shock*, as the leash she'd guarded so jealously dissolved from her own neck, and Mara heard, for the last time, the true sound of the spirit-court: not a chorus of the bound, but a vast, ragged, astonished intake of breath, ten thousand voices realizing at once that nothing was holding them.

The roots stopped advancing. The fires burned down to honest flame. And the codex drank the spoken words off the page—Mara watched the ink sink, letter by letter, until the vellum glowed soft gold and then went quiet, holding the new pact the way soil holds a seed.

Then the gift left.

It didn't hurt. That was the cruelest part. It simply withdrew, like a tide going out, like a friend stepping backward out of a room. One moment Mara could feel every creature on the mountainside—the bear's stubborn heart, the goats' dry amusement, the wolves' fierce joy—and the next—

Goats were just goats.

The night was full of animals behaving like animals, and Mara Sterling stood alone inside their own skull for the first time since they were six years old, and the silence where the song had been was so enormous they nearly fell.

Hands caught them. Ilex—smoke-streaked, singed, alive—lowered them onto the stone bench. Behind him came Hesper leaning on Fen, Rook half-carrying a limping twin, all four of them filthy and breathing hard, having fought their way up a burning mountain to a garden because of course they had.

"Mara?" Ilex's voice cracked on it. "Did it—"

"It worked." Their own voice sounded strange in the new silence. Flat. Human. Just a voice. "It's done. The pact is—" They laughed, wet and disbelieving. "It's written. My handwriting's terrible. It's in there anyway."

Rook stared at the codex, then at Mara. "Can you still—" They stopped. Everyone stopped. They all knew what Mara had traded; Hesper had guessed it days ago and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

"No," Mara said. "It's gone. All of it. I'm just—"

They couldn't finish. Didn't need to. Fen sat down heavily on the bench beside them and bumped their shoulder, hard, the way the twins did instead of hugging.

"Just ours," Fen said. "Good. We were getting tired of sharing you with the wildlife."

And Mara—beast-speaker no longer, heir to nothing now but a garden and a book and these specific, impossible people—laughed until it turned into crying, which was fine, because everyone on that bench was doing some version of the same thing.

* * *

They were still there when the footsteps came up the terrace path.

Odile Vess climbed into the garden alone, sword sheathed, helmet under her arm, her column halted far below where the bear and the wolves and the unkillable goats still held the line. Fire-cant wouldn't light anymore. Mara could see her working that out even at a distance, see the exact moment a woman built of certainty arrived at the edge of a cliff in herself.

She stopped at the table. Looked at the codex. Looked at the ragged, bleeding, laughing group of smugglers and traitors and strays gathered around it like a hearth.

"What did you do?" Vess asked. Not a commander's question. Something rawer.

Mara stood. Without the gift, they read her the ordinary way—by her face, her stance, the exhaustion underneath the iron—and found, to their surprise, that ordinary reading was enough. It had always been enough. That was the whole secret Sable had died trying to leave behind.

"I answered the question," Mara said. "Come to the table next year. Ask it yourself."

Vess stood in the rose-light for a long moment, a woman stripped of certainty rather than life, her war dissolved out from under her by five sentences in bad handwriting. Her jaw worked. Her eyes went, briefly, to Hesper—to the healer she'd once ordered to burn a child, who had refused, who was sitting here among traitors looking more like a Warden than anyone Vess had commanded in years.

Then Captain Odile Vess of the Crown Wardens did the one thing no prophecy had predicted.

She set her helmet down on the edge of Sable's stone table, and sat.
