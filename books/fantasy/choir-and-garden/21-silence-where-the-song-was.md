## 21. Silence Where the Song Was

The morning after the mountain stopped singing, I woke up and the world was ordinary.

That's how it felt, anyway. Ordinary like a room with all the furniture moved two inches to the left. The light through the waystation window — Hesper's rebuilt waystation, or half-rebuilt; she'd insisted we start with the roof because "a person can survive a crooked wall but not a wet bedroll" — came in the same gold it always had over Thornfell. The kettle on the stove ticked as it heated. Somewhere outside, Fen was swearing at a cart axle in the inventive dialect of someone raised by smugglers and goats.

And the goats were just goats.

I'd known it would be this way since the moment the words left my mouth and the old magic dissolved like frost off slate. I'd had a night to prepare. It hadn't helped.

I went out to the pen anyway. Habit, or hope, or some third thing that didn't have a name yet.

The nannies looked up when I came through the gate. Brown eyes, horizontal pupils, the flat incurious stare of an animal deciding whether you're worth standing up for. Before, they would have *spoken* to me — not in words, nothing so neat, but in weather and hunger and the low grumble of opinion, a language I'd been fluent in since before I could write my own name. Before, Bram's old lead nanny, Sorrel, would have pressed her skull against my knee and told me, wordlessly and completely, everything about her morning: the wet grass, the itch behind her ear, the distant wrongness of crows.

Sorrel looked at me. Chewed. Looked away.

"Morning," I said aloud.

She didn't answer. She never would again. My voice fell into the pen and lay there like a dropped coin no one bothered to pick up.

I stood there longer than I meant to. Long enough for my hands to go cold on the rail, long enough for the kettle inside to start its thin whistle and for Rook to yell that tea was ready and did anyone care if the milk went over. I catalogued what I felt, because cataloguing is what I do instead of crying: there was grief, yes, vast and specific, like losing a limb I'd never seen. And underneath it something worse — shame, almost, at how much of me had been *that*. The gift. The useful thing. Twenty-nine years of being the one who could talk the mountain down, talk a patrol past, talk a frightened mule across a scree field, and now —

Now I was a woman standing in a goat pen saying good morning to an animal who owed me nothing and gave me nothing and wanted breakfast.

"Mara!" Rook's head poked out the door, hair sticking up like he'd fought his own pillow and lost. "Tea. Also Fen says the axle is cursed and I said axles aren't cursed and Fen said *this one is*, so bring your unbiased opinion."

"I don't have opinions anymore," I said. "I'm just a person."

"You've always been just a person," Rook said, baffled. "Come eat."

*

The thing nobody tells you about losing something enormous is how small the first day of life without it is.

We ate eggs. Fen fixed the axle — not cursed, merely bent, though Fen maintained these were the same thing — and I held things and handed things and was, for the first time in living memory, simply another pair of hands. When a mule spooked at the woodpile, it wasn't me who calmed it; it was Rook, talking to the animal in the slow low voice smugglers learn from each other, the human kind, the kind that works because patience works. He was better at it than I'd ever been, honestly. He'd learned it the honest way, by paying attention.

I watched him do it and felt something twist that wasn't quite envy and wasn't quite relief.

"You're staring," Ilex said, appearing beside me with two cups of tea, handing me one without asking. Ilex always handed without asking. "You've been staring at Rook for ten minutes. Either you're in love or you're grieving, and I know which one I'm betting on."

"Grieving," I said.

"I know." Ilex leaned against the fence post next to me, close enough that our shoulders touched, which was as much as Ilex ever admitted to wanting before noon. "How is it?"

"How is what?"

"The silence." Ilex looked out at the valley, where the mist was burning off the terraces. "In your head. You told me once — before any of this, that first night in the garden, actually, though I pretended I didn't notice — that your own thoughts had never been alone in there. That there was always the hum of everything else underneath."

I hadn't remembered telling Ilex that. I hadn't remembered it as confession, either. Just talk, on a night when I'd thought Ilex was a charming botanist and not a spy sent to find me. Funny, what turned out to matter later.

"It's quiet," I said. "It's so quiet I keep checking whether I still exist."

"And?"

"And I think I do. It's just that existing used to come with a chorus."

Ilex was quiet for a while. Then: "When Vess's fire took the inn, and Hesper was taken, and we scattered — I kept thinking about the silence too. What mine would sound like if you all died. I decided it would sound exactly like my life before I met you, which was technically silent and actually deafening." A pause. "So. For the record. The quiet after isn't the worst thing. The worst thing is the quiet *before*."

"That's very morbid for breakfast."

"I'm a botanist. We're morbid about growth cycles."

I drank the tea. It was bad tea — Hesper bought it from a man in the lowlands who watered his leaves, and everyone complained, and nobody stopped buying it, because complaining about Hesper's terrible tea had become one of the load-bearing rituals of our lives. Grief braided itself right through the middle of it, and I let it. There's a version of mourning where you refuse to feel anything until the feeling is convenient, and I'd done that version for years, invisibly, competently, alone. I was done doing versions alone.

*

Hesper found me at midday, sitting on the steps of the half-built waystation, whittling a stick into nothing in particular.

"Bram's marker came back from the stonecutter," she said, sitting down beside me without ceremony. She set a flat slab of river slate between us. On it, cut clean and deep:

*BRAM TULLER*
*KEPT THE DOOR. FED WHOEVER CAME.*
*THAT WAS THE WHOLE RELIGION.*

"He'd hate it," I said. "Too many words."

"He'd love it. He'd complain about it for a decade, which is the same as loving it." Hesper took the stick out of my hands, examined my progress, and handed it back. "You haven't cried yet."

"No."

"You cried when the twins' mule died. Two years ago. I heard you through the wall."

"The mule talked to me."

"There it is." She nodded slowly, like I'd confirmed a diagnosis. "You think if you cry now, it means you only loved them — the beasts — because they answered. That without the answering you're not allowed to grieve properly."

I stared at her. "Are you reading my mind? Because I thought I'd finally gotten rid of the mind-reading adjacent nonsense."

"Warden healer. Twenty years of listening to people say the second-truest thing because the truest thing scares them." She nudged the slate toward me. "Cry whenever you want. Cry at the marker. Cry at the goats. Cry because the tea is bad. Nobody here will grade you on it. That's the entire point of what you built on that bridge, if I understand it right — a pact where nobody has to earn their place by being useful."

"That's not exactly the legal text—"

"Mara." Hesper put her hand over mine, rough palm, steady grip. "You gave up the only thing that made the whole world make sense to you, and you did it to save people who weren't even in the room yet. Let yourself be wrecked about it in front of witnesses. That's not weakness. That's the *new* religion."

I looked at Bram's marker until my eyes blurred, and then I did cry, ugly and undignified, on the steps, in daylight, with Hesper's arm around my shoulders and the stick whittled to nothing falling out of my lap. I cried for Sorrel and the other goats, for every mule and crow and fox and bear whose voices had been the floor under my feet, for Bram most of all, whom I couldn't hear anymore in any language, anywhere.

Nobody graded me. Somewhere in the middle of it, Fen walked past with an armload of shingles, saw me sobbing, and said, "Finally," and kept walking, and somehow that helped more than anything.

*

Three days after the pact, I tried the goats one more time.

Not the words — those were gone, scoured out of me like a name off a ledger. But the old physical fluency, the shoulder-angle reading, the ear-language, the way you move around a frightened animal. That part had never been magic. That part had been twenty years of work.

I sat in the straw of the pen and did nothing dramatic. Just sat. Let Sorrel decide about me.

It took forty minutes. Then she ambled over, sniffed my hair, and leaned her weight against my side — not speaking, not answering, just choosing proximity, the way animals choose warm spots and safe people. My throat closed up.

"Okay," I whispered into her coarse neck. "Okay. That counts. That counts."

From the gate, Ilex watched me with an expression I couldn't read, and for once I didn't need to. Some things you could just let be unread.

*

On the fifth evening, I found them all in the kitchen arguing about me.

I heard it before I reached the door — the particular hush-and-hiss of a conversation that stops when footsteps approach, except nobody stopped, because apparently they'd all decided I was allowed to hear it now.

"—all I'm saying," Fen was saying, "is that she keeps volunteering for the heavy hauling like she's got something to prove, and somebody should tell her the proving finished already. She won the war. She can take a nap."

"She doesn't nap," Rook said. "Napping requires believing tomorrow is optional."

"Hesper, you're the healer, medically, does she need a nap?"

"She needs to stop carrying water for the whole valley and let the valley carry something for once," Hesper said. "But I've told her that, and she smiled at me, which is her way of not hearing it."

"And you," Fen said, and I could hear the finger-point even through the door. "Botanist. You've been circling her for five days like a moth with a plan. Say whatever it is you're not saying."

A pause long enough that I almost stepped inside. Then Ilex, quieter than I'd ever heard them: "I met her for a reason. Bad ones. Reconnaissance, prophecy, the codex, all of it. And somewhere along the way the reason stopped being the point and I couldn't tell anymore which parts of me were the mission and which parts were real, and then the pact happened and the mission evaporated, and now—" Another pause. "Now there's no prophecy. No heir. No beast-tongue. Just a woman who cries at goat pens and holds the lantern steady when I'm out late collecting specimens. And I keep waiting for the part of me that was pretending to pack up and leave, and it doesn't. It just stays. So what I'm not saying is that I don't know how to be loved when I'm not useful either, and apparently neither does she, and maybe we could figure it out badly together, and I've rehearsed this sentence eleven times and it sounded better every time before this one."

Silence in the kitchen.

I pushed the door open.

Five faces — four, plus Sorrel, who had followed me in because goats have no respect for dramatic timing. Fen looked stricken. Rook looked delighted. Hesper looked like a healer watching a wound finally drain. Ilex looked like a person caught mid-confession, which was accurate.

"How much did you hear?" Ilex asked.

"Enough," I said. "Eleven rehearsals and you buried the good part."

"The good part?"

"'Badly together,'" I said. "That's the good part. Everything else was preamble." I crossed the kitchen, took Ilex's teacup out of their hand, set it down, and kissed them — badly, a little clumsily, teeth clicking, both of us smiling into it, which is the only honest way anything gets done in this family.

Fen made a gagging noise. Rook threw a bread roll at us. Hesper poured six cups of the terrible tea and said, "Well. Now that the two least functional members of this household have sorted themselves, we can discuss the garden."

"What garden?" I asked.

"Sable's," she said. "It's been sitting up there growing wild around a dead prophet's bones while we've been down here having emotional breakthroughs. Spring's coming. That soil spent a century composting prophecy — I want to see what it grows when we plant something we chose ourselves."

I looked around the kitchen. The smuggler twins who'd once called me "the cargo" and now argued about whether I napped enough. The disgraced Warden who'd traded her career for a spirit-child's life and her safety for ours. The spy who'd stayed after the mission died. The goat chewing on the curtain.

A month ago, every one of them had needed me to be the Beast-Tongued Heir. I'd braced myself, these five days, for the slow drift — for the family dissolving politely now that the function was gone, the way every group I'd ever belonged to had dissolved once I stopped being useful.

They were still here. They were arguing about my sleep schedule. They were planning spring planting.

"Tomorrow," I said, and my voice cracked on the simplest word in the language, and nobody mentioned it, and everybody noticed. "We'll start on the garden tomorrow."

But that night, unable to sleep, I lit a candle and pulled out a blank notebook I'd bought in the lowlands — plain paper, no vellum, no waiting pages, nothing prophetic about it at all. I wrote at the top of the first page, in my own uneven hand:

*Things that are true now.*

And beneath it, the first entry:

*The goats don't talk. They stay anyway.*

Outside, far off on the dark shoulder of Thornfell, something sang — wind in the passes, or water, or the new pact settling into the stone like a seed. I couldn't tell which. I couldn't ask.

For the first time in my life, that felt less like loss and more like the beginning of a story I was going to write myself.

I dipped the pen again, and started page two.
