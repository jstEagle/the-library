## 22. Planting Season

The first frost of the year came early to Thornfell, and the garden took it like a held breath finally let go.

Mara stood at the gate of Prophet Sable's garden with dirt already under their nails and a wheelbarrow handle worn smooth against their palms, watching the light come up over the terraces. A year ago this place had been a ruin they'd stumbled into by accident—a dead man's overgrown secret, walls collapsed, beds gone to bramble, the whole hillside holding its silence like a grudge. Now the walls were mended where mending mattered. The paths were cleared. The cold frames Ilex had built from salvaged glass caught the sunrise and threw it back in long amber stripes across the turned earth.

It smelled like work. Real work—the honest kind, the kind that made your back ache for reasons you could point to.

"You're standing there thinking again," Fen called from the third terrace. "I can tell. You get that goat look."

"I do not have a goat look."

"You absolutely have a goat look. It's the one goats give you when they're deciding whether to headbutt you." She straightened up from the bed she was weeding, a trowel stuck through her belt like a knife, her braid full of straw. "Rook says the compost needs turning before it freezes solid, and Rook is right, which should worry you."

"Rook being right *should* worry me," Mara said, and got the mud-splattered laugh they were aiming for.

They went down to turn the compost together, because that was the thing about this new life that still surprised them most: nobody did anything alone anymore. The twins argued over the pitchforks the way they argued over everything, which was to say competitively and with great affection underneath. Hesper came out of the potting shed with her sleeves rolled and her healer's hands black to the wrist, muttering about nitrogen ratios like they were battlefield triage. And Ilex was up on the far wall with a notebook of their own, cataloguing what had come up volunteer from last spring's planting—feverfew, valerian, a squash nobody remembered putting in the ground.

The garden kept its own secrets now. That felt right.

* * *

The codex pages had gone into the earth in the first week of thaw, almost a year ago now.

Mara remembered every part of that day. How Hesper had carried the Vellum Codex out of the house wrapped in linen like something sick. How the four of them—Ilex, the twins, Hesper, Mara—had stood at the edge of the old prophet's deepest bed and argued, briefly, about whether burning would be cleaner. How Rook had said no, quietly, in the voice he almost never used: *"It's not ours to burn. It's ours to finish."*

So they'd cut the binding loose and torn the pages into strips, all those centuries of prophecy, all that ink older than bone, and worked them into the soil like mulch. The words that had named Mara the Beast-Tongued Heir. The words that had named Hesper the traitor who opens the door. The words that had sent two armies up a mountain and cost Bram his life and the hollow tree inn its branches and nearly everything else besides.

Bram's name wasn't written anywhere in the codex. He'd been too ordinary for prophecy—just a smuggler with bad knees and a good heart who'd taught a lonely child how to read goat-sign and stay invisible. But his grave was here now, under the plum tree on the south wall, because Mara had wanted him somewhere things grew. They planted crocuses around him every autumn. He'd have hated the ceremony and loved the crocuses.

The pages rotted the way paper does, into soft gray nothing, into food. By midsummer the bed where they'd buried the final chapters had come up thick with comfrey and borage, plants that heal the ground they grow in. Ilex swore that was coincidence. Mara had stopped needing it to be more than that.

That was the whole inheritance, as far as Mara cared to keep it: a book that became a garden. Words that fed roots instead of wars.

* * *

The children arrived on market days, mostly.

Word had gotten around, the way word does in mountain villages—that the people restoring Sable's garden would take apprentices, that they taught animal husbandry the plain way, no cant, no secrets. Parents brought them up the switchback trail with bread and cheese for payment, because coin was thin after the war and everyone knew it, and Mara never once asked for more.

There were six of them this morning, arriving red-cheeked out of the cold: the miller's two girls, a shepherd boy missing two fingers to a fox trap, twin brothers from Lowthorpe who fought constantly, and a small fierce girl named Wren whose family had lost their farm in Vess's march and who looked at every animal like she was deciding whether to trust it.

"First lesson," Mara told them, gathering them into the goat pen while the does crowded close, curious and greedy for the dried apple slices in Mara's pocket. "What do you notice?"

"They stink," said one of the Lowthorpe boys.

"True. What else."

"The brown one's limping," said Wren, immediately. "Left front. She's hiding it."

Mara crouched down beside the doe—Juniper, sweet-tempered, three years old—and ran a hand along the leg without any magic at all, just patience, just hands. A stone bruise, shallow. "Good eyes," they said, and Wren stood a little taller for the rest of the day.

That was the curriculum, such as it was. Notice first. Touch second. Speak last, and mean it. Mara taught them to read ears and tails and the set of a shoulder, to hear the difference between a complaint and a warning, to move slow around frightened things. Everything they'd once done with the gift, they now did with attention—which turned out to be most of it. The gift had always been less than everyone believed. The listening had always been the real work.

The children didn't know what Mara had been. Some of them had heard stories—the Beast-Tongued Heir, the one who stopped the war—but the stories were already going strange in the telling, growing feathers and horns, and Mara never confirmed anything. When the miller's youngest asked, shyly, if it was true Mara could talk to animals, Mara handed her a brush and said, "You can too. Watch the ears. Go on."

She watched the ears. Juniper leaned into the brush. The girl's whole face opened like a gate.

That was enough. That had always been supposed to be enough.

* * *

In the evenings, when the children had gone home down the mountain and the light went blue and cold, Mara sat in the garden house with a blank notebook and tried to write.

Not prophecy. Nothing so grand. Just—a record. What the weather did. What came up where. Which goats were bred to which buck, which hens went broody, when the first crocus showed by Bram's tree. Hesper had given them the notebook in the spring, plain leather, unmarked, and said only, "Sable wrote a century of warnings. Write yourself a decade of notes instead. See which one does more good."

Hesper was usually right about things. It was insufferable.

Tonight the entry was short:

*Frost. Turned compost, all four beds. Juniper lame—stone bruise, resting her. Wren spotted it before I did. Remember that.*

Then Mara sat with the pen hovering, the way they did some nights, over the line where the practical record ended and everything else began. There were things that belonged nowhere else. The way the mountain had sounded during the choir—all those creatures, every beast they'd ever spoken with, arriving unbidden between the armies, not commanded but *choosing*, and the sound of it living still behind Mara's ribs even though the hearing itself was gone. The way Briarthorn had bowed at the end—not to an heir, but to an author—and withdrawn her court to negotiate terms that were still, a year later, slowly, stubbornly holding. The way Odile Vess had laid down her fire-cant weapons on the frozen field, stripped not of her life but of her certainty, and ridden away with soldiers who had heard the land itself singing the new pact and would not raise flame against it.

Those things weren't notes. Those things were grief and gratitude in equal measure, and Mara had learned—was still learning—to let them sit on the page without shaping them into anything useful.

Some nights they wrote them anyway. Tonight they added one line:

*Missed the song today. Goats were just goats. Good day anyway.*

* * *

Ilex found them there after dark, bringing tea and that particular expression that meant a discovery.

"You'll want to see the north terrace," they said. "But bring a lamp, and don't wake the twins, or they'll trample it arguing about whose fault it is."

"What is it?"

"Crocus. Off-season. Blooming *now*, in the frost, in the bed where—" Ilex paused, and Mara understood: the bed where the codex pages had gone. "And there's something else. Come see."

They walked out together through the cold, breath fogging, the lamp throwing their shadows long across the terraces. At the north bed, Ilex knelt and lifted the lamp low, and Mara saw it: among the pale crocuses, pushing up through the frost-hardened ground, a single shoot of something neither of them had planted. Broad-leafed, unfamiliar, silver-veined, glowing faintly at the edges the way spirit-things glowed—the way Queen Briarthorn's court had glowed, the way the bridge had glowed, the way everything from the old pact had glowed before the old magic dissolved.

Except the pact hadn't fully dissolved. That was the thing they were all still learning. Mara had spoken new words—coexistence, accountability, both peoples bound by choice rather than blood—and the old magic had loosened its grip on the mountain, but the spirits remained, diminished and free, learning to live in the world rather than beneath it. Briarthorn sent an envoy twice a season now, stiff and formal, to argue water rights and boundary stones like any other neighbor. Coexistence, it turned out, was not a single act but a practice. Like gardening.

"This isn't in any of my books," Ilex said quietly. "I've checked everything. It's not in Sable's notes either."

Mara crouched beside it. Up close, the silver veins pulsed faintly, like something breathing in its sleep. Without the gift, they couldn't ask it what it was. They could only look, and wonder, and decide.

"It came up from the pages," Mara said. "Or the pact left a seed. Or the mountain's trying a new thing and didn't consult us." They smiled, tired and unafraid. "Either way—it's growing in our garden. So we treat it like everything else here. We watch. We tend. We find out what it needs."

Ilex looked at them for a long moment, lamplight and starlight mixing on their face. "You know what Sable would say."

"Sable would say write it down." Mara pulled the notebook from their coat, opened to tomorrow's blank page, and began, in careful ordinary handwriting, at the top:

*Something new came up in the north bed. Don't know its name yet. Tending it anyway.*

Below the frost-bright shoot, the garden waited—mended walls, sleeping beds, a plum tree keeping a smuggler's grave, six children coming back at week's end with questions, a found family scattered through the houses below already arguing about whose turn it was to mend the cold-frame glass. A year ago Mara had been a name in a dead man's book, hunted by everyone who thought the story belonged to them. Now the story was compost, and the future was a seedling with silver veins, and the notebook in their hands was blank exactly as far as they could see.

Mara finished the entry, closed the cover, and sat a while longer in the cold with Ilex's shoulder warm against theirs, watching the small bright thing grow.
