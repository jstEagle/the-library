## 1. The Weight of Unpaid Vows

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width again, and Edmund Vasquez did not relight it.

He sat at the small table in his rooms above the counting house, coat still on, boots still crusted with the day's slush, and watched the flame gutter in its dish of tallow. It was a cheap candle. That was the joke of it, the private joke he'd carried for eleven years now: that he tended a god with the cheapest tallow in Vhalen's Row, when once — once, before the silence, before the guild, before all of it — the god had been fed on frankincense and myrrh and the rendered fat of consecrated oxen, poured out in rivers down marble channels while ten thousand voices sang.

Now it was a stub of candle bought three for a copper from the widow Ossa, who watered her lamp-oil and shorted her wicks and thought nobody noticed.

Edmund noticed. Noticing was his trade.

The flame leaned sideways, righted itself, leaned again. He had never heard the god speak. No one alive had, not since the Sundering, not since whatever night it was that the voice went out of the world like a snuffed lamp. The priests had starved or scattered or gone mad trying to mime sermons to a thing that no longer answered. Edmund had been twenty-two then, an altar-boy with ink-stained fingers and a talent for arithmetic, and when the temple fell he had done the only sensible thing a man with those two qualifications could do.

He had gone to work for people who counted things.

The Hollow Ledger Guild kept accounts for half the underworld of Carreth Vale — debts, favors, contraband, blood-prices, all of it entered in ledgers whose columns were as binding as any crown charter. Edmund was their Keeper of Accounts, which meant he knew where every coin in the city slept and what it dreamed about. It also meant, though only four living people knew it, that he had sworn an oath over the guild's oldest book, and that the oath had been sworn in the old way, in the old form, with his hand pressed to a page and a single word spoken —

Except the word had never come.

That was how oaths to the silent god worked, or had worked, or were supposed to work. You spoke your vow aloud, and the god took hold of it, and the vow became a weight in you, a gravity, something you could feel behind the sternum on bad days. Every other god still listening took its oaths that way. But Edmund's god had been silent for a century before he was born, and so when he spoke his vow into the dark of the undercroft, holding the ledger open, nothing had taken hold of it. Nothing had answered. He had said the words anyway, because Maren — no, not Maren yet, that came later — because the rite demanded it, and because some stubborn altar-boy part of him had believed that a god could be silent without being absent.

Eleven years later he still didn't know if that was faith or foolishness. He suspected the two had stopped being distinguishable somewhere around year six.

The candle gutters. He watched it, and did not light it, and then of course he lit it, because he always lit it, because eleven years of nightly habit is its own kind of prayer even when you've stopped believing in prayers. The new flame caught, steadied, threw his shadow long across the wall where his spare coat hung from a nail.

"Evening," he said to the god. He said it every evening. "Stores are short. Winter will be long."

The god, as ever, said nothing back.

* * *

Morning found him in the guild's cellar-vaults with a wax stylus and a tally-slate, doing what he did best: finding the places where the numbers lied.

The winter stores occupied three vaults beneath the counting house — salt pork in brine-barrels, hardtack in oilcloth sacks, casks of ale that Corvane insisted be recorded as "provisions, general" rather than by name, because Guildmaster Corvane did not like the word *ale* appearing too often in documents that might someday be read aloud in a court. Edmund moved down the rows with his slate, prying lids, weighing sacks against the counterweights chained to the wall, running his thumb along barrel-staves to feel for the hollow sound of a cask refilled with river water.

Barrel fourteen, third vault. Hollow.

He stood there a moment with his knuckles still resting on the stave, listening to the echo die, and felt the familiar weariness settle over him like the damp that got into everything below street level this time of year. Barrel fourteen was supposed to hold smoked eel, tribute from the Fishmongers' Compact against their quarter-year interest. It held instead a clever forgery: real staves, real hoops, real smell of smoke laid into the wood, and inside, packed around a false bottom, nothing but wet straw.

Someone in the Compact was skimming. Someone in the Compact was very good at skimming. And someone in the Compact did not know that Edmund Vasquez had spent eleven years learning to hear the difference between a full barrel and a lying one, the way a priest learns to hear the difference between true repentance and performed remorse — except that Edmund had never been a priest, not really, only almost, and the comparison was one more thing he declined to think about.

He chalked the barrel. He chalked three more in the next row. By the time he climbed back up into the grey daylight of the counting floor, the tally read: *Compact short by 340 pounds weight, distributed across four vessels, fraud deliberate, execution skilled.*

Corvane would want it collected today. Corvane always wanted things collected today; patience, in the Guildmaster's cosmology, was simply interest accruing against you.

"Vasquez." Corvane stood at the high desk beneath the window, a lean man built like a closed door, silver at his temples and gold rings on the fingers he used for signing things. He never looked up from his papers when he spoke to Edmund. It was a habit, Edmund had decided years ago, not contempt — or possibly it was contempt, and he had decided otherwise because deciding otherwise made the days easier. "The Compact?"

"Short," Edmund said. "Three hundred forty pounds. Fraudulent barrels. Good workmanship."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvane's mouth. Approval, maybe, or appetite. "Then go and collect it. Take Brakk. And Vasquez—" now he looked up, briefly, eyes pale and flat as river ice, "—collect it *all*. I'm tired of the Compact testing our margins every quarter like we're a charity."

"Their margin isn't fraud," Edmund said. "It's hunger. Two of their boats went down in the autumn storms. If we strip them bare they'll default entirely by spring, and then we own a debt no one can pay, which is worth less than the three hundred forty pounds we'd have taken."

For a moment the ice-eyes studied him. Then Corvane returned to his papers. "Collect the shortfall. Leave them their hulls. Write it however you like afterward — you always do." A pause, precise as a knife laid down. "You're useful, Vasquez. Try to remain so."

Edmund inclined his head, gathered his coat and his collection-book, and went out into the cold with Brakk falling into step beside him, all shoulders and bad teeth and the cheerful menace of a man who enjoyed his work far too much.

* * *

The Fishmongers' Compact kept its offices in a rotting customs-house down by the weir, where the river ran slow and brown and smelled of scales and old rope. The walk took them through streets going grey with the first spit of snow — not real snow yet, just the sky rehearsing — past brazier-sellers and a funeral procession for somebody's grandmother, six hired mourners walking behind a cart because nobody else had been left to do it.

Edmund walked with his collar up and the collection-book under his arm and grief folded away in the same inner pocket where he kept everything he refused to carry openly.

Brakk talked the whole way. Brakk always talked the whole way. Something about a woman in Tanner's Lane, something about a dog he meant to win money on, something about how the Compact's factor was a worm and worms should be stepped on early before they bred. Edmund answered in the monosyllables of a man conserving warmth and words alike, and let the city wash past: shutters banging, gulls screaming over the fish-stalls, a child crying somewhere in the upper windows of a dye-house with a sound that snagged briefly on something behind Edmund's ribs before he folded it away too.

The factor of the Compact was named Ollen Prue, and he received them in a room with a leaking ceiling and a map of the river pinned up behind him with rusted nails. He was sweating in the cold, which told Edmund most of what he needed to know before anyone said anything at all.

"Master Vasquez." Prue spread his hands. "You've come about the barrels."

"I've come about the barrels."

"There's been a mistake in the weights—"

"There hasn't," Edmund said, and set the collection-book open on the table between them, and turned it so Prue could read the entry. He watched the man's face do the small terrible journey from denial to calculation to surrender, and felt nothing in particular about it. This was the work. This was what competence looked like from the outside: a man turning a book around.

"Three hundred forty pounds," Edmund said. "In goods, by Friday, or the Compact renegotiates its whole position with us at rates you won't survive. Master Corvane sends his regards."

Prue's hands were flat on the table now, trembling faintly. "I can't. I *can't*, do you understand? The storm took the *Maren's Hope* and the *Little Ann* both, and the insurance — there is no insurance, the insurance was a fiction I sold to my own factor-board, and if I pay you three hundred forty pounds of goods by Friday then there is nothing left, nothing, and my people starve with the ledgers balanced perfectly, and what does the Ledger Guild care—"

"We care," Edmund said quietly, "that the account settles."

"And what do you care?" Prue's voice cracked upward, desperate, and for one unguarded instant his eyes were not a factor's eyes but a man drowning's eyes. "You write it all so neatly, Master Vasquez. Debts and payments and carried balances. Does the book say anywhere what happens to the people in the margins?"

Behind Edmund, Brakk shifted his weight with a soft leather creak, the sound of a man preparing to remind everyone present what enforcement meant.

And that was when Edmund saw the child.

She was half-hidden in the doorway that led back into the customs-house warrens — a girl, perhaps eight years old, perhaps younger, thin in the way that winter makes children thin, with hair the color of river-clay and eyes that were watching Edmund with an attention no frightened child should possess. Frightened children watch the door. Frightened children watch the men with the books and the muscle. This child watched *him*, specifically, the way one watches a lock one intends to learn.

She was barefoot. In the slush. Barefoot, and standing utterly still, and utterly silent, in a house full of shouting.

"Prue," Edmund said, without taking his eyes off her. "Who is that?"

Prue followed his gaze, and something in the factor's face collapsed inward, relief and shame arriving together like twins. "Ah," he said. "Ah. Yes. Well." He straightened his cuffs with trembling fingers, summoning the ghost of a merchant's manner. "As it happens, Master Vasquez, the Compact may be able to offer payment after all. Payment in kind."

"That's not kind, that's a child."

"She's not mine. She's not *anyone's* — she turned up on the weir steps in the autumn, wild as a gull, doesn't speak, bites if you grab her, eats like a cormorant and costs me more in stolen fish than she's worth, which is nothing, which is *less* than nothing." Prue was talking faster now, selling, the way men sell when they are selling the last thing they have. "But she's young and she'll work, and the kitchens up-hill always need scullions, and honestly, Master Vasquez — honestly — I'd be doing her a kindness. Three hundred forty pounds of value, standing right there. Take her, enter her, and we're square, and everyone walks away whole."

The room had gone very quiet. Even Brakk had stopped shifting.

The child had not moved. She stood in the doorway with the grey light behind her, looking at Edmund Vasquez with those learning-the-lock eyes, and then she did the one thing no one in the room expected, the one thing that turned a routine collection into the hinge of a life:

She spoke.

Not Vhaleni. Not Low Trade-Cant, not the fisher-dialect of the weir districts, not any of the dozen tongues Edmund's ears had catalogued over eleven years of listening to debtors lie. The sounds that came out of her were older than any of them — low, resonant, structured, each syllable placed with the precision of masonry, a language that sounded less like speech than like architecture. It was three words, maybe four. Her voice did not waver.

Edmund did not understand a single syllable.

He understood, with a certainty that arrived in his chest like a dropped anchor, that he had heard the language before. Not conversation — never conversation. Once. In a rite-book, in a dead man's marginalia, in the phonetic fragments a blind archivist had once traced with her fingertips and called *the old command-forms*, laughing sadly, saying *nobody knows what these sounded like, Edmund, the tongue died with the temples.*

It hadn't died.

It was standing barefoot in a doorway, eight years old and hungry, having just addressed him in the language in which the gods themselves had once been commanded.

"What did she say?" Brakk asked, uneasy, hand drifting toward his belt-knife the way dull men do when the world misbehaves.

"I don't know," Edmund said truthfully. And then, because eleven years of keeping accounts had taught him exactly which entries must never be written down: "And it goes in no book."

He closed the collection-book. He looked at the child a long moment, and the child looked back, and outside the sky finally committed to snow — fat flakes coming down slow through the brown air over the river, settling on the rotting customs-house roof, on the weir, on the whole patient machinery of debts.

"Tell Corvane the Compact pays in goods," Edmund said, rising, tucking the book under his arm. "I'll arrange terms."

"And the girl?" Prue called after him, greedy hope cracking his voice. "The girl as part-payment—"

"No," Edmund said, at the door, not turning around.

Behind him, soft as snowfall, unhurried as judgment, the child spoke again — those same strange syllables, that impossible architecture of sound — and this time, Edmund could have sworn he felt them land somewhere behind his sternum, in the exact place where an oath that had never been claimed had waited, empty, for eleven years.

He did not look back. He walked out into the snow, and heard small bare feet follow him through the slush, and did not tell them to stop.
