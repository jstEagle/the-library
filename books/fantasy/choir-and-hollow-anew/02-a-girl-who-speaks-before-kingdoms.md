## 2. A Girl Who Speaks Before Kingdoms

The rain had turned to something worse by the time Edmund finished with the debtor — a wet grey slush that fell in fat, indifferent clots and turned the streets of Lowmarsh into a soup of ash and horse dung. He stood on the threshold of Wren's tenement with his ledger wrapped in oilcloth under his arm and the taste of the morning still in his mouth: tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of a man who has just tried to sell him a child.

"No," Edmund said again, because the man seemed to be waiting for him to say it a third time as though repetition might change arithmetic.

"She's worth more than the debt," Wren said. He was a dyer by trade, or had been, before the guild's interest rates ate his vats one by one. He was not an evil man. That was the trouble with Lowmarsh; evil was expensive, and most people here could only afford desperation. "Healthy teeth. Good hips when she's grown. The fighting pits take them young if they've got the look, and she's got the look."

Edmund looked at her.

She stood in the corner of the room where the damp came through the plaster like a bruise, a thin child of perhaps eight years with hair the color of river mud cut ragged at her jaw. She wasn't cowering. That was the first thing he noticed, and it unsettled him more than cowering would have. She was watching him the way a gull watches a fisherman — not afraid, exactly. Calculating. Her eyes were a pale grey-green, the color of shallow water over slate, and they did not blink enough.

"The debt is eleven crowns," Edmund said. "The guild does not deal in persons. It's in the charter." It was also, though he did not say it, in him — some old splinter of the god he'd sworn to that still ached when men talked about children the way merchants talked about bolts of cloth. Eleven crowns. He had carried debts of four hundred. This one sat in his chest like a stone swallowed wrong.

"Then I'll sell her to someone who does," Wren said, and there it was — the flinch in his own voice, the part of him that hated what he was saying and would say it anyway, because winter was coming and the vats were cold and his wife was dead and there were two other mouths in the house that weren't strange silent girls.

"She's not mute," Edmund said. "She just doesn't speak your tongue?"

"Doesn't speak any tongue. Chatters sometimes. Sounds like—" Wren waved a hand, disgusted or embarrassed, hard to tell. "Like water over rocks. Like a fool babbling. The priest from the canal mission said she was possessed. I said fine, exorcise her, but he wanted paying."

The girl's eyes came to Edmund's face and stayed there. Not his purse, not the ledger, not the sword hilt visible under his coat the way most people's eyes went. His face. As though she were reading something written there in small characters.

He should have walked out. He told himself later, many times, that he should have walked out, that the ledger-law was clear, that mercy entered in no book was a debt that compounded forever. Instead he set eleven crowns on the table, one at a time, so Wren could hear each coin land.

"That clears you," Edmund said. "It buys nothing else. If I ever find her sold, or given, or left at a foundling wheel because you decided she was coin after all, I will come back, and I will not be carrying money."

Wren looked at the coins and then at Edmund's face and made the wise choice, which was the only kind of choice he'd ever made.

* * *

The girl followed him.

He realized it two streets later, in the shadow of the aqueduct where the guild's collectors liked to conduct their less official conversations. She was perhaps twenty paces back, walking in the churned slush without seeming to notice the cold of it, barefoot — barefoot, in this — and when he stopped, she stopped. When he started, she started. She kept his pace precisely, which was somehow worse than if she'd scrambled after him like a stray.

"Go home," he said.

She tilted her head. And then she spoke.

It was not words, not as he understood words. It was sound shaped with intention, low and layered, syllables that seemed to move against each other like currents in a deep channel. It raised the hair on his arms. There was a quality to it he had no name for — a weight, a pressure, as though the air between them had been briefly made dense. A cart horse tied outside the Wheelwright's Rest shuddered and rolled its eyes and strained against its rope for no reason anyone saw.

"I don't understand you," Edmund said, and hated how rough his voice came out.

She said it again, softer, and this time he caught the shape of it — the same phrase, repeated, patient. She was not babbling. Babbling does not repeat itself with such care. She was *saying* something, and saying it twice because the stupid mercenary hadn't answered the first time.

"I have work," he told her, which was true, and walked on.

She followed him across the Marsh Bridge, past the tanneries where the stink was thick enough to chew, up the Cutwater Stair with its iced-over steps that broke an ankle every winter. Twice he stopped and pointed her back the way they'd come. Twice she waited until he moved and then resumed her twenty paces, exact as a debt accruing interest.

By the time he reached the guildhouse door on Candlemakers' Row, dusk was coming down purple and oily, and the lamps of the counting room glowed behind frost-furred glass, and he admitted to himself that he had run out of streets.

"You can't come in here," he said. "This is a place of accounts. Everything that enters gets written down."

Something flickered across her face at that — he would think about it later, that flicker, and wonder what she'd heard in his tone, whether the First Tongue had taught her to hear the word *written* in any language. But that was later. In the moment she simply looked at him with those slate-water eyes, and then held up her hands, palms out, fingers spread, and spoke again — that dense, layered sound — and this time she touched her own sternum with two fingers at the end of it, as though finishing a signature.

Asking. He was almost sure she was asking.

Edmund Vasquez had been bound to a silent god for nineteen years. He knew every species of asking there was, and he knew the difference between the ones that took and the ones that gave. He stood in the falling slush on the guildhouse step and felt the old ache in his chest, the shrine-shaped hollow, and he cursed himself with great fluency and precision.

"One night," he said. "There's straw in the cellar and bread that's going stale anyway. One night, and tomorrow I find you somewhere better than this."

She didn't smile. But something in her posture changed — a settling, like a ship taking harbor — and she stepped past him through the door as though she had lived there all her life.

* * *

The guildhouse at night was a body with two hearts. Above stairs, the counting room ticked and murmured with clerks closing the day's books; below, the cellars ran back into the older dark where the first guildmasters had kept their vaults, and where Edmund kept the thing that mattered most to him, which was a candle.

He lit it now, in the little alcove off the cellar stair where the walls still bore scratches from some devotion older than the guild. The candle was cheap tallow, guttering, and the shrine it served was nothing — a niche, a shelf, a stone worn smooth by a forehead pressed to it over centuries. His god had no name anymore. Had perhaps never had one that human mouths could hold. The god had gone silent before Edmund was born — before his grandmother's grandmother, maybe — and the order that had served it had thinned to nothing, and Edmund was what remained: a keeper of accounts who lit a candle every night for a listener who had never once, in nineteen years, given him so much as a flicker of answer.

He lit it anyway. That was the whole of his theology.

The girl watched him do it. He'd meant to settle her in the straw by the coal store and get upstairs before Corvane's evening audit found an unregistered person in the building, but she had drifted after him down the stair again, silent as silt, and now she stood at the edge of the alcove's light with her head tipped back, staring at the flame.

And the flame — he would swear this later, and swear it sober — leaned toward her. Just slightly. Just the way a flower leans at the sun.

"Don't," Edmund said, and didn't know what he was forbidding.

She looked at him. Then she reached out and touched the smooth-worn stone of the shrine, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and said one word.

One word, in that deep layered voice, and the candle did not flare — it *stood*. Went perfectly still, flame rigid as carved amber, and the whole cellar went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sound. The drip of the seep-water stopped. The settling groan of the old timbers stopped. Even the noise of the city above seemed to pause, leaning in, and Edmund felt the silence press against his eardrums like deep water, and beneath the silence, faint as a pulse through a wall, something *noticed*.

Nineteen years of lighting candles. Nineteen years of nothing.

His hands were shaking when he snuffed the flame. "Upstairs," he said hoarsely. "Now. Don't touch anything else."

* * *

Maren kept a room at the top of the archive tower, which was against half a dozen guild regulations and tolerated because Maren was the only soul alive who knew where everything in the archive was, including several things the guild had officially lost. She was past seventy, blind as a mole, and read by running her fingers over pages the way other people read by lamplight. She received visitors with tea already poured, because she always heard them coming three floors early.

Edmund brought the girl up at midmorning, after a sleepless night spent listening to the cellar and telling himself he'd imagined the candle. He'd tried the scholars first — young Pellam with his six languages, old Cotter who'd translated tariffs from four kingdoms. Neither had recognized a syllable of what the girl said. Pellam had guessed "highland dialect" and then "some river-gypsy cant" and then run out of guesses and gotten offended, as scholars do.

Maren poured tea by touch, three cups, unasked.

"You've brought me a riddle," she said, "and a child, and you haven't slept. Sit down before you fall down, Edmund."

"She speaks something. Nobody downstairs knows it."

"Mm. Pellam came sniffing up here last night asking whether we had grammars from the Salt Reaches. I sent him away hungry." Maren's face was a landscape of soft wrinkles, her eyes pale and fixed on some middle distance that contained no light at all. "Sit the child where I can hear her. And Edmund — ask her properly. Children know when they're being handled like specimens."

So Edmund knelt, feeling foolish, and said, "Will you speak for her? The way you spoke last night? Anything at all."

The girl studied Maren's face — studying a blind woman's face, which was its own strangeness — and then she spoke. A short phrase, unhurried, those overlapping currents of sound filling the little round room. She finished and folded her hands, waiting.

Maren went very still.

Edmund had seen Maren calm through a fire in the west stacks, through Corvane's purges, through the news of her brother's death delivered by a clerk who read the letter aloud badly. Stillness was her native country. But this was different. This was the stillness of a woman listening to a sound she had heard once before, long ago, in circumstances she had spent a lifetime persuading herself she had dreamed.

"What did she say?" Edmund asked quietly.

Maren didn't answer. Her hand was moving toward her teacup — an old habit, touching things to know them — and it closed on empty air where the cup wasn't, knocked it, and the cup went off the table and burst on the floorboards, tea spreading dark across the wood, and Maren did not even flinch. She was reaching instead for the girl's hand, finding it, holding it in both of hers with a grip that made the child stiffen in surprise.

"Say it again," Maren whispered. "Child. Please. Again."

The girl said it again.

"Oh," Maren said. It was barely a sound. "Oh, you poor, *terrible*, magnificent—"

"Maren." Edmund heard his own voice go flat and hard, the collector's voice, the one that made debtors sit up straight. "What is it? What did she say?"

Maren turned her blind eyes toward him, and for the first time in the nineteen years Edmund had known her, she looked frightened.

"She said, *the silence is heavy and I am carrying it alone.*" Maren's thumb moved over the girl's knuckles, slow, soothing, though Edmund couldn't tell which of them she was soothing. "That is the First Speech, Edmund. The Tongue of the Binding. The language they used before the Sundering to *command gods* — to bind them, to leash them, to silence them. Every grammar of it was burned. Every speaker was killed or worse. It has been extinct for nine hundred years." Her grip tightened on the girl's hand. "It is not supposed to exist anywhere outside of sealed books that no longer exist. And this child just used it to tell me she's tired."

The fire popped in the grate. Down in the cellar, Edmund thought absurdly, a candle stub sat cold in a niche, and something behind the world had leaned closer to listen.

The girl — Sera, he was calling her in his head already, though no one had given him the name — looked from Maren's face to his, and said something else in the First Speech, brief and questioning, and Maren laughed, a cracked and wondering sound, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her cheeks.

"She asked," Maren translated, "whether the tea is for her too."

Edmund sat down slowly on Maren's crowded bench, among the scrolls and the smell of ink and old paper, and looked at this feral, barefoot, impossible child who drank the tea in both hands and watched them both with her slate-water eyes, and he felt the last door of his ordinary life swing shut behind him with a click as quiet and final as a ledger closing.

Because he had heard it too, in the cellar, underneath her word. The thing that noticed. And he knew — with the certainty of a man who has balanced books his whole life and knows when a column refuses to sum — that sooner or later, everyone in this city who wanted a god's silence was going to want her.

"Nobody hears that language again," he said. "Not in this house. Not where walls are thin. Swear it, Maren."

"I swore oaths to archives my whole life," Maren said softly. "I know how to keep one." She drew the girl's hand into her lap and held it there, possessive as a dragon. "But Edmund — child — whoever taught her even one word of the First Speech is still out there. Tongues like this aren't stumbled into. They're *passed down.*"

Outside, the slush was turning to proper snow, the first of the winter, falling thick and silent over Candlemakers' Row — and somewhere in that silence, Edmund could not stop thinking, something was listening.
