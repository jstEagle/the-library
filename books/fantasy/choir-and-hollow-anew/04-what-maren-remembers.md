## 4. What Maren Remembers

Maren had gone blind the year the plague took the eastern quarters, though no one in the Hollow Ledger Guild ever spoke of the two events in the same breath. The sickness had taken her eyes in a single fevered week; what it left behind was a world made entirely of sound and texture, of paper's grain under her fingertips and the particular hush that settled over a room when someone entered it who did not belong.

She heard Edmund Vasquez come up the archive stairs at half past the ninth bell, and she knew something was wrong before his boot touched the third step.

Edmund was a man whose footsteps had been trained out of him — mercenary first, accountant second, ghost throughout. He moved through the guildhouse the way water moves through cracks, silently, apologetically, as though he were always intruding on some more legitimate life. But he carried things with him. Grief had a weight to it, Maren had learned, and grief walked with a man like a second set of boots. Tonight his grief was loud.

And behind him came a sound she could not name.

Not footsteps. Something lighter. The whisper-scratch of small feet on old stone, quick and irregular, stopping when he stopped, starting when he started. A child, then. But beneath the child's movement there was another thing entirely: a faint vibration in the air, so low Maren felt it in the bones of her inner ear rather than heard it, like the memory of a bell rung long ago in a drowned tower.

She set down her stylus. She smoothed the ledger page she'd been transcribing — winter stores, tallow counts, the endless arithmetic of survival — and turned her face toward the door.

"Edmund," she said. "You've brought something home."

A pause. She imagined him standing in the doorway, one hand still resting on the child's shoulder or not quite reaching for it, caught between two instincts. He was always caught between two instincts. It was what made him trustworthy; honest men decided cleanly, and Edmund never did anything honestly if cleverness would serve.

"I need you to listen to something," he said.

"I'm always listening."

"This is different." His voice dropped, went careful, the way it did when he spoke about money owed to dangerous people. "There's a girl. She speaks a language. Corvane's clerks can't place it. I sent a sample to the scriptorium downriver — they returned it unopened, said it was gibberish, or worse, said it was *nothing*, which is what scholars say when they're frightened."

Maren felt the cold arrive ahead of understanding, the way frost arrives ahead of morning. "Play it for me," she said, which was a strange thing to say about speech, but Edmund understood. He had learned years ago that language, for her, was an instrument to be handled.

Then the girl spoke.

Maren had spent forty years among words. She had catalogued eleven dialects of Low Mercantile, three dead liturgies, the whistled trade-cant of the salt barges, the hand-talk of deaf miners. She believed she had a shape in her mind for every sound human throats could make.

The girl's voice broke that belief in the space of four syllables.

It was not that the language was unfamiliar. It was that it was *older* than familiarity — older than the category into which all languages fit. Each word seemed to arrive already finished, complete, as if it had not been spoken now but merely remembered aloud, dragged up from somewhere very deep and very dark where it had been waiting. And underneath the words ran that low vibration again, and this time Maren recognized what it resembled, because she had read the accounts of those who had stood near cathedral bells when they cracked — how the air itself seemed to grieve.

Her teacup was in her hand. She had forgotten she was holding it. When the girl reached the fifth or sixth syllable, Maren's fingers simply opened, and the cup struck the floor and broke, and the tea spread across the stone in a dark starburst that she would feel with her stocking feet for days afterward, because she would not let anyone clean it up until she had memorized exactly where it lay.

"Maren?" Edmund's voice, sharp with alarm.

"That's not possible," Maren said. Her own voice sounded far away, thin, a child's voice. "That is not *possible*. Who taught her?"

"No one, apparently." He crouched; she heard his knee pop, heard him gathering porcelain. "I found her — she found me, rather. Long story. Debt collection gone sideways. Why? What is it?"

Maren lowered herself from her chair by touch, knelt in the spreading warmth of the spilled tea, and put out her hand in the direction of the voice.

"May I touch your face, little one?" she asked. "I see with my hands. It's only polite to ask."

A hesitation. Then small feet crossed the stone — bare feet, Maren noted, chilled ones — and a small face pressed into her palms.

Thin. All cheekbone and jaw, a bird's architecture. Chapped lips, a scab at the hairline, cold-burned ears. But it was the mouth that stopped her. Maren had held enough dying faces to know the geography of suffering, and this child's mouth bore the particular tension of someone who was holding something behind her teeth and holding it *carefully*, the way one holds a full cup while walking on ice.

"What's your name?" Maren asked.

"Seraphine," the girl said, in ordinary trade-tongue, in a voice roughened by disuse. "Sera."

"Sera. My name is Maren. I keep the guild's oldest books. And I am going to tell you and Edmund Vasquez something that fewer than five living people know, and after I say it, none of our lives will be the same. Do you understand me?"

The girl's chin nodded against her palms.

* * *

They went down to the deep archive, because Maren refused to say it anywhere with thin walls. The deep archive sat below the counting floors, carved into the hill's wet bone, and it held everything the guild had ever been paid to forget: seized deeds, burned contracts' surviving drafts, confession ledgers, the accounting of small atrocities. Edmund lit lamps as they descended. Maren heard him hesitate at the bottom stair — she knew why without asking. There was a niche down there, old and dry, where a candle sometimes burned. His god's candle. He lit it every night, that man, faithful to a silence, and he thought no one knew. She had known for six years and had never once mentioned it, because some devotions are private the way wounds are private.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. Sera — here, on my left. I want to be able to feel you breathe."

She folded her hands in her lap and began.

"Before the Sundering — and I mean *before*, truly before, back when the histories stop being histories and become arguments — there were more gods than there are stars people bother to name. You know this. Everyone knows this the way everyone knows the sea is salty: vaguely, and without consequence. What no one knows — what was cut out of every record, burned, buried, paid to be forgotten — is *how* the old order worked. How gods and mortals arranged themselves toward one another."

"The First Speech," Edmund said quietly.

"You've heard of it?"

"I've heard the phrase used as an insult. In barracks. *Talks like he thinks he speaks First Speech.* Meaning: talks like he expects the world to obey."

"It was no insult," Maren said. "It was a technology." She let the word sit there, ugly and precise. "That is what no one wants written down. Prayer, we are taught, is asking. Sacrifice is trading. Faith is trusting. All of it consensual, all of it humble, mortal kneeling before divine. But the oldest records — the fragments I have catalogued with my fingers, page by rotted page — describe something else entirely. A grammar. A true one. A language in which the gods were not addressed but *instructed*. Where a sentence spoken correctly did not petition a god to act. It compelled. Bindings, Edmund. Chains built out of syntax. The Sundering wasn't a war between gods, whatever the temples claim. It was a war over whether mortals would keep their chains."

Silence, except for the drip of groundwater somewhere in the dark, keeping its own slow count.

"And the tongue died with it," Edmund said.

"The tongue was *killed* with it. Deliberately. The surviving powers hunted speakers the way you hunt rats in a granary — not out of cruelty, but arithmetic. Every speaker alive was a weapon unaccounted for. They killed the children first. Always the children first; children learn fastest." Maren heard her own voice go flat and clinical and could not help it. Some knowledge you carry only by numbing the places it touches. "Within three generations it was gone. Within ten, it was myth. Within thirty — now — it is nothing at all. I have spent my whole life reading everything that survived, and in fifty years I have found perhaps two hundred words of it, most disputed, all broken, like shards of a bell that no longer rings. Scholars fight duels over single syllables. Entire careers have been built on a disputed vowel."

She turned her face toward the child beside her. She reached out and found a small shoulder, and rested her hand there, feeling the tremble in it.

"And just now, in my archive, above us, a child sang four sentences of it as naturally as breathing. Fluent. Unbroken. Not reciting — *speaking*. Living inside it." Maren laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand? It is as if I told you the Sundering never ended. As if I told you the drowned are walking around dry-shod wearing borrowed coats. Everything we believe about why the world is safe is built on the fact that this language is extinct."

"She didn't learn it," Edmund said slowly. "That's what I keep circling back to. She's feral, Maren. Half-starved, unschooled, can't do sums past twenty. Nobody taught her declensions."

"Then where—" Maren stopped. Because there was an answer forming, and she did not want it. Some answers arrive like creditors; you hear them on the stairs long before they knock.

"Ask her," Edmund said.

Maren gathered the girl's cold hands into hers. "Sera. Sweet one. The language you speak — the beautiful, terrible language. Where did it come from?"

A long quiet. The girl's pulse fluttered under Maren's fingers like a moth in a jar.

"I don't speak it," Sera said at last, in trade-tongue, in a whisper. "It speaks *through* me. Sometimes. Mostly when I'm scared. Or asleep." A swallow. "The men who had me before — they wanted to take it out. They said it was inside me. They had knives and chalk and a table with straps."

Maren felt Edmund go very still beside her, the stillness of a man reordering the entire world around a new fact.

"They never got it out," Sera said. "It's still in me. I can feel it when it wants out. It feels like — " she searched, and Maren waited, and waited — "like a word too big for my mouth. Like if I ever say the whole thing, I'll break."

*Like a bell that no longer rings,* Maren thought, *except this one hasn't cracked yet.*

* * *

Afterward — after the tea-stain had been mopped and the lamps trimmed and Sera fed on bread and honey until she drowsed in a chair like a cat in a sunbeam — Maren insisted they go back up to Edmund's rooms. To the shrine niche. She had never once asked to see it. Tonight she asked, and he understood without explanation, because Edmund was many things but never slow.

The candle was already burning. Of course it was. It burned every night; it had burned for six years, through his grief and his bookkeeping and his careful gray life. A stub of gray wax in a dish of black iron, before a shrine with no idol, no inscription, nothing at all — a shrine to a god with no name left to give it. Maren knew the god only as Edmund knew it: as silence. A presence that had once answered him, and then, one day, had not. The priests called such gods *withdrawn*. The ledgers called them *written off*. Edmund called it nothing at all, out loud, and lit the candle anyway.

"Sing for it," Maren said softly.

Sera, curled in the chair, lifted her head. "For the candle?"

"For whatever hears it."

The girl looked at Edmund. Maren heard the fabric of his sleeve shift — a nod, or the ghost of one.

So Sera slid off the chair and knelt, uncertainly, the way children kneel when they have seen adults do it and are imitating the shape of reverence rather than the substance. She clasped her hands. She looked at the small gray flame.

And she sang.

It was only a fragment — seven words, maybe eight, in that deep-remembered tongue, rising and falling in a melody that had no composer, the way rivers have no authors. Maren felt the hairs rise along her arms. Felt that subterranean vibration again, felt it climb through the floor, through the walls, through the wet stone bones of the guildhouse itself, as though the building were an ear pressed to a door.

The candle flared.

Not guttered — *flared*. The flame stood up straight and tall and turned from yellow to a white so pure it hurt, throwing shadows sharp as cuts across the room, and in that light Maren saw — with eyes that had seen nothing for twenty years — one impossible instant of image: a vast dark shape, immense beyond measuring, turning toward them from an unimaginable distance. Not approaching. Just *turning*. The way a sleeper turns toward a sound. The way the drowned turn toward bells.

Then it was gone. The flame sank, shrank, steadied, resumed being a small gray candle doing its small gray work. The room was a room again.

But the silence afterward was different. Maren had spent twenty years learning every species of silence — the silence of empty rooms, of held breaths, of lies. This one was *attentive*. This one was the silence of something listening from very far away, having heard its name in a language it had not heard since before the world was saddled.

Beside her, Edmund made a sound she had never heard him make. Small. Wrecked. The sound of a man whose prayers had been falling into a well for six years hearing, at last, the far-off splash.

"It heard," he whispered. "Maren. It *heard*."

"Yes," Maren said, and her hands were shaking, and she did not try to hide it. Wonder, yes — wonder vast and ringing. But underneath the wonder, cold and patient as groundwater, dread rose to meet it. Because she had read the oldest records. She knew what it meant when the old powers turned toward a voice in the First Speech. She knew what had always followed, in the fragmentary histories, whenever that language surfaced in the world again.

First the wonder. Then the listeners. Then the hunters. Then the war.

"Edmund," she said quietly, "no one can know. No one outside these walls. Not Corvane, not the clerks, not your smuggler friends, no one. Because I promise you — the moment one powerful person hears that sound and understands it, every crown, every cabal, every desperate priest and greedy scholar on this continent will come for that child. Not to protect her." She reached out and found Sera's hand and held it. "To open her. Like a letter."

The girl's small fingers tightened around hers.

Above them, faint through the floors, the guildhouse settled into its night sounds — locks, lamplighters, the last clerks climbing stairs. Ordinary life, continuing on the assumption that the world was safe.

Maren listened to it, and mourned it in advance.

Because far away — farther than the passes, deeper than the drowned places — something had turned over in its sleep. And things that sleep that long, she knew from her oldest books, are always woken by someone.

They would just have to pray it was only them.
