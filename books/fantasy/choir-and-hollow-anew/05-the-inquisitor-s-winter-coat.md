## 5. The Inquisitor's Winter Coat

The frost came to Grayharbor the way debt collectors come — quietly at first, then all at once, and never alone.

Edmund noticed it on the morning the harbor mouth began to smoke. Ice-fog rising off black water, the fishing boats ghosting out of it one by one like things remembered rather than seen. He stood in the guildhouse yard with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hands and watched, and thought about candles.

He had lit the shrine candle again last night. He always did. A stub of tallow no bigger than his thumb, set in a cracked dish before an empty niche where a god used — or had never — lived. He didn't pray. You couldn't pray to silence. But he lit it, and he watched it burn down, and in that hour between midnight and the bell he let himself feel the shape of the absence the way you press your tongue against a missing tooth. Not grief exactly. Accounting. A loss entered and carried forward, year after year, never written off.

"Vasquez." Pell, the door-ward, stuck his head out of the gatehouse, breath steaming. "Rider at the gate. Crown colors."

Edmund's hands did not tighten around the cup. Twenty years of ledger-law had trained that out of him; a man who flinched at bad entries did not survive the counting house. "Expected?"

"No herald, no summons. Just him." Pell hesitated, which Pell never did. "One horse. One servant. He's *polite*, Ed."

That was worse. Edmund drank the tea, cold as it was, because warmth would be useful later and there might not be time to find more. "Show him to the blue room. Tea, the good leaves, small cakes if the kitchen hasn't eaten them all. I'll receive him after I've washed."

"You're dressing up for a tax man?"

"I'm dressing for whatever he actually is." Edmund set the cup on the well-head. "No one rides three days in this cold over unregistered barrels of salt fish."

* * *

Halvard Vess was not what Edmund expected, which was itself a kind of message. Inquisitors of the crown arrived like weather — banners, clerks, a company of pikes to remind everyone whose peace they were keeping. This man came into the blue room trailing cold air and wet wool, a long grey coat beaded with road-melt, and bowed to Edmund as though Edmund were the one with rank.

"Master Vasquez. Halvard Vess, in service to the Office of Inquiry." His voice was soft, unhurried, the voice of a man reading aloud something he has already memorized. He was fifty perhaps, lean, with a scholar's stoop and a face built for forgetting — the kind of face you could describe to a sketch artist for an hour and produce nothing. Only his eyes stayed with you: pale, patient, and quite without mercy, the way winter is without cruelty. Winter isn't cruel. It simply intends to go on longer than you are.

"Inquisitor." Edmund gestured to the chair by the fire. "You've caught us at the start of our winter audit. I hope your business can survive being boring."

"All good business survives audit, Master Vasquez. It's the other kind that fears it." Vess sat, accepted tea, thanked the serving girl by name — *Marta* — and asked after her mother's cough, which he could not possibly have known about. Edmund filed that away with care. The man had been in the city hours at most. Someone had briefed him thoroughly, or he read ledgers the way Edmund read faces.

They spoke for a while about nothing, beautifully. Salt duties. The new wharfage schedule. The guild's excellent reputation for accuracy — *"the cleanest books north of the capital, I'm told,"* said with a smile that gave nothing back. Edmund matched him entry for entry, courtesy for courtesy, and beneath the table his pulse sat steady and cold as coin.

Then Vess set down his cup and said, lightly, "I'm here about persons."

"Persons?"

"The crown maintains, as you know, registries of various sorts. Debts. Licenses. Bonds of service." He produced a slim folio from inside his coat and laid it on his knee without opening it. "And lately, by renewed instruction, a registry of unregistered persons. Individuals present within crown jurisdiction whose names appear in no lawful record. No guild roll. No parish book. No debt-bond. People who exist, Master Vasquez, *off the page.*"

"Audit season," Edmund said. "There are always drifters in winter. Dockside labor without papers, runaways—"

"Of course. The usual sediment." Vess waved this away with two fingers. "But my instruction concerns a particular class. Persons whose lack of registration is not poverty but *design*. Persons who may have been deliberately kept off every list in the city by someone skilled enough to do it." He looked up, and the firelight found those pale eyes and made them briefly luminous. "Skilled enough. Do you know anyone like that?"

The room was very warm. Edmund felt the cold anyway, arriving somewhere behind his sternum, spreading outward with terrible patience, like water finding the seams of a hull.

"I keep accounts, Inquisitor. I don't make people disappear."

"No. Of course not." Vess smiled. "Though I confess the phrase interests me. *Carried debt.* Most unusual construction. I've been reading your guild's filing conventions — a hobby of mine, conventions. Tell me, when a debt is carried rather than held, whom does the law consider its keeper? The one who owes? Or the one who carries?" He turned his cup a slow half-circle on its saucer. "Because someone in this house, some weeks ago, registered a child under that heading. A girl. Age unknown. Name given only as *Sera.* No parentage. No origin. No valuation entered — which, in a guild famous for valuing everything, is almost a confession in itself."

Every instinct Edmund owned screamed at him to look toward the door, toward Maren's tower, toward anywhere Sera might be. He looked instead at the fire, mildly, as a bored man does.

"Guildmaster Corvane approved the entry," Edmund said. "You'd want to raise it with him. Registration irregularities fall under his seal."

"They do. And I will enjoy meeting Guildmaster Corvane tomorrow." Vess closed the folio. "Today, I wanted to meet you first. The registrar of record. The man whose hand wrote the entry." He rose, and Edmund rose with him, and for a moment they stood facing each other across the little table with its cooling tea, two careful men each pretending the other was not holding a knife.

"I'll say this plainly, Master Vasquez, because I respect plainness." Vess buttoned his coat. "I am not here for the girl. Not primarily. Children wander into records and out of them all the time; she is a thread, not the garment. I'm here for what made her worth registering at all. Someone went to great trouble to put that child on your books — the one place in Grayharbor with a legal claim to hold her. That's not paperwork. That's a wall built in a hurry. And walls built in a hurry tell me exactly where the treasure is buried." He inclined his head. "Thank you for the tea. The good leaves. You strike me as a man who spends carefully everywhere except where it matters."

At the door he paused.

"One more thing. An old habit — I collect them, habits." From his pocket he took a coin and set it on the table between the cups, spinning it once with one finger so that it wavered, rang faintly against the wood, and lay still. "For the household. Call it a tip, if tips sit easier than warnings."

Then he was gone, boots quiet on the stairs, and the front door opened and shut, and the cold came in behind him like a dog let off its leash.

* * *

Edmund did not touch the coin for a long time.

He stood in the blue room with the fire ticking and listened to the house breathe — Marta in the kitchen, Pell's rounds overhead, wind testing the shutters the way it did every evening, patiently, certain that eventually something would give. Somewhere above, faint through the floor, he could hear Maren's voice teaching Sera her letters, the old blind archivist's cadence rising and falling like tide against a pier. Safe sounds. Ordinary sounds. He clung to them the way a drowning man is supposed to cling to driftwood, knowing all the while that driftwood does not care.

Then he looked down at the coin.

It was silver, old silver, worn nearly smooth, the milled edge eaten away by centuries of hands. Not any mint Edmund knew — and Edmund knew mints the way other men knew taverns; forgery detection was the first skill the Hollow Ledger taught its children. This was older than milling. Older than milled edges had ever needed to be.

He turned it over once in the lamplight.

A face looked back at him. Not crowned, not haloed — just a face, wide-eyed and open-mouthed, rendered in the shallow worn relief of a people who carved before perspective was invented. The mouth was open in song or speech or scream; the eyes were open too, staring upward past the viewer at something the artist had declined to show. Around the rim, where a legend should run, there were no letters. There was a single continuous line, broken in one place, and the break was deliberate — a gap, a silence cut into the circle of the world.

Edmund knew that face.

He had known it since he was nine years old, kneeling in a shrine that smelled of tallow and river-mud, watching a ragged priest hold up just such a coin and teach a roomful of gutter children the catechism of the Quiet God. *He who speaks not, cannot lie. He who commands not, cannot be commanded. We do not ask him for miracles. We ask him to witness.* The god of the unaccounted — the god who kept no ledger but saw every entry. The faith of dock-hands and debtors and girls sold upriver, the religion of people whose names were written nowhere. It had gone silent in Edmund's fourteenth year, the way a hearth goes out: not extinguished dramatically, just found cold one morning, the priests scattered or dead, the shrines empty, the prayers falling with no one to catch them.

He had lit a candle to that silence every night for twenty years. He had told himself it was habit, then sentiment, then — in the years when honesty came cheaper — grief.

And now a crown inquisitor had carried that face into his house in his coat pocket, warm from his own body, and left it on the table like a calling card.

Edmund sat down slowly.

It meant the hunt had begun long ago. Before Sera. Perhaps before the debtor had offered her at his gate like a sack of turnips — and Edmund felt the floor tilt gently under that thought, because it had never once occurred to him to ask why a starving salt-boiler in the Marsh Ward had a First Tongue child to give away in the first place. He had refused the trade and taken the girl home on pity, and pity had blinded him to the oldest truth of his profession: *nothing arrives unpriced.* Someone had paid to put that child in his path. Someone had spent her like coin.

Or — colder still — someone had spent *him.*

Vess's words rearranged themselves in his memory with the horrible neatness of a balanced column. *I am not here for the girl. Not primarily.* A lie, obviously; everything Vess had said was designed, every sentence a hook dressed as candor. But lies, properly read, were also data. If Vess truly wanted only the girl he would have taken her today, quietly, with writs. Instead he had shown Edmund the coin. He had *told* him, in the language men like Vess used, *I know what you are. I know what you mourn. I know the candle you light.*

Which meant Vess did not need Sera yet. Which meant what Vess wanted first was Edmund's reaction — fear, flight, a letter sent to the wrong person, a favor begged from the wrong smuggler. The inquisitor wasn't hunting a child. He was hunting a network, and he had just dropped a stone into the middle of it to watch which way the ripples ran.

Edmund picked up the coin.

It was lighter than it should have been, the way old grief is lighter than new. He thought of Odo — no, he hadn't met Odo yet, wouldn't for weeks; he thought only of the priests of his boyhood, of the last one he'd seen, dying in a doorway with snow in his beard, whispering the catechism to no one. *We ask him to witness.* Witness what? Witness this: a man grown old in service to numbers, holding the face of his vanished god in his palm, understanding at last that the silence he'd mourned had not been death at all. It had been *taken.* Silenced — the way you silence a witness. And somewhere in his house a child slept who spoke the tongue the silencers had used, or fled it, or *carried* it, and every power in the world was now riding through the snow to collect her.

The candle. He should light the candle early tonight.

No. Edmund closed his fist around the coin and made himself stand, made himself climb the stairs past Maren's door — pausing just long enough to hear Sera laugh at something, a small startled sound like ice cracking on a pond in spring — and continued up to his own cold room, where he knelt at the niche and lit the tallow stub with hands that did not shake, because twenty years of practice counted for something even now.

The flame stood straight. The empty niche received its light and returned nothing, as it always had.

"All right," Edmund said quietly, to the silence, to the god, to whoever in heaven or under it was listening through either. "They've found us. So we start keeping better books."

He set the coin upright on the shrine ledge, the broken circle facing the flame, the open mouth facing him.

Below, faintly, the house creaked — and then stopped creaking, in a way houses don't, as though something had just drawn a long, careful breath.

Edmund blew out the candle and went down to warn the others.
