## 6. Bargains in the Undercroft

Tessaly kept her counting house where the tide could reach it.

Not because she liked the smell of brine and rot — though after twenty years in the trade she'd stopped noticing it, the way a married man stops noticing his wife's snoring — but because water made an honest ledger. Every spring flood took a little of everything: coin tarnished, silk spotted, paper swelled into pulp. Anyone who stored goods below the high-water mark was either a fool or someone who understood that nothing should be held too tightly. Tessaly had been both, once, in that order, and had learned her lesson at the cost of two fingers on her left hand and a brother whose name she no longer said aloud.

So the undercroft beneath the Saltwife's Rest flooded twice a day with the tide, and twice a day drained, and in the hours between, her business ran dry-shod along raised walkways of blackened oak that creaked like old men climbing stairs. Lanterns hung from chains on pulleys so they could be hauled up when the water came. Everything in Tessaly's world could be hauled up when the water came. She'd built her whole life on that principle.

Which is why, on the third evening of the cold snap, when one of her dock-rats came scrambling down the walkway with his eyes too wide and said, "He's here. The ledger-man," she put down her cup of mulled wine without hurry, checked the blade in her boot out of habit rather than need, and smiled.

"Show him down. And Wren — count the crates on tier four again while he's here. I want to know if anything walks off while I'm being nostalgic."

* * *

Edmund Vasquez descended into the undercroft the way he did everything: precisely, without wasted motion, like a man who had audited his own soul and found it overdrawn. He picked his way along the walkway planks with the sureness of someone who'd walked them before — twice before, to be exact, which was how the arithmetic of their acquaintance worked. Two favors spent. Three remaining. Tessaly kept the count the way she kept all counts: carved notches inside her left forearm's leather bracer, where no one could read them but her.

"You look terrible," she said, by way of greeting.

"You look rich."

"One of us is honest." She gestured him onto the stool across the oilcloth-covered barrel she used as a desk, and poured him wine she wouldn't drink herself — the good stuff, plum-dark, from a bottle she kept for people she was pleased to see and slightly worried about. The overlap of those two categories was, historically, small. "To what do I owe the pleasure of your terrible face?"

Edmund wrapped his hands around the cup but didn't drink. That was Edmund. He treated hospitality like a contract clause — acknowledged, pending review.

"I need passage. Quietly. Possibly soon, possibly in the middle of a bad night. One passenger, small. Out of the city or hidden inside it, whichever the situation wants. You'd know better than me."

Tessaly let the silence sit the way she let tide sit, patient and total. Around them the undercroft breathed — drip of water off chain links, the groan of the harbor ice forming out past the pilings, somewhere above the muffled argument of tavern patrons discovering the price of winter ale.

"One passenger, small," she repeated. "A child."

"A child."

"Yours?"

The question landed harder than she'd intended, or maybe exactly as hard as she'd intended. Edmund Vasquez had a face built for ledgers — flat, ruled, every line accounted for — but she watched the line of his jaw do something unaccounted-for. A twitch. A recalculation.

"She's under my protection."

"Mm." Tessaly drank her own wine now, slow, watching him over the rim. In the lamplight the lantern chains threw moving shadows across his shoulders, so that he seemed to sway gently even sitting still, like a man standing on the deck of a ship. "Under your protection. Which means the Hollow Ledger Guild knows about her, since you can't protect a stray cat in this city without Corvane entering it in a book somewhere. Which means the guild has opinions about her. Which means—"

"Tessaly."

"—which means," she continued sweetly, "that whatever you're asking me to carry isn't cargo, it's a live coal, and you're asking me to hold it in my hands and smile about it."

"Yes."

She set down the cup. There it was. Twenty years in the trade and she still admired a man who could say *yes* like that — no ornament, no apology, just the word placed on the table between them like a coin that might be clipped gold.

"And the danger?" she asked. "Be specific. I don't price vague."

"Crown inquisitor came to the guildhouse two days ago. Halvard Vess. Asking politely about unregistered persons." Edmund's voice stayed level, but Tessaly heard what lived underneath the levelness, the way you could hear the river under river-ice. "He left a coin behind. Stamped with a face my god used to wear."

That pulled her up short. Tessaly knew — everyone who'd known Edmund longer than a season knew — that the mercenary keeper of the guild's accounts had once belonged to something else, some silent divinity whose name nobody remembered because there was nothing left to remember *with*. She'd never asked. You didn't ask ledger-men about their debts; you waited for them to surface on their own, usually at inconvenient moments, usually with interest.

"So it's not just the crown," she said slowly. "It's the crown wearing somebody else's glove."

"It's worse than that. But yes."

"Worse how?"

"That part I'm not selling tonight."

* * *

Here was the thing about Tessaly that the merchants of the upper city never understood, the ones who thought smuggling was thievery with better posture: she did not deal in goods. Goods were incidental. What she dealt in was *knowing* — knowing which customs officer drank himself blind on Thursdays, which warehouse had a false wall, which noble's wife needed which forbidden thing delivered discreetly to which wrong address. Knowing was the only currency that didn't rot in the flood.

And right now she wanted very much to know about this child.

She'd already heard whispers, of course — the undercroft had ears above it, beside it, underneath it. A feral girl following Edmund Vasquez home through the slush like a half-drowned gull. Speaking gibberish, the tavern talk said. Speaking *old* gibberish, the archivists' corner of the market said, the kind of whisper that made Maren the blind reader close her doors early and burn extra candles. A girl who talked in a dead language and made a shrine candle behave strangely. Tessaly filed rumors the way other women pressed flowers, and this one she'd been pressing for days, waiting to see what shape it dried into.

Now its shape was sitting on her stool, holding untouched wine, asking her to become an accomplice.

"The price," Edmund said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become a negotiation.

"Ah." She refilled her own cup. "The price. Well. Three favors remain on your account — I keep good books, don't worry, better than yours probably. A favor of this size spends two of them. Maybe all three, depending on how ugly the night gets when we run."

"Fair."

"But—" and she held up one finger, the whole finger, the one with the scar where the old life had taken its tuition payment, "—favors are for moving the girl. Favors don't buy my curiosity. Curiosity is priced separately."

Edmund's eyes narrowed a fraction. "Name it."

"The name." She leaned forward, elbows on the oilcloth, and let her voice drop into the register she saved for contracts and confessions. "Whoever taught her the Tongue — the First Speech, the god-language, whatever we're calling the thing that has a crown inquisitor and half the underworld sniffing after a child. I want the name. Not for sale. For keeping. Because Tessaly Renn does not stand in front of a moving cart blindfolded, Edmund, and right now everyone in this room is blindfolded except you, and I find I resent it."

Something crossed his face then — quick as a fish under ice. She almost missed it. Almost didn't.

"No," he said.

"*No?*"

"Not that. Ask me anything else. Ask me the color of her eyes, where she sleeps, what she eats, whether she's marked, branded, chipped, cursed — ask me anything and I'll answer true. Not that."

Tessaly sat back and studied him, and the studying took a long time, because what she was really studying was the shape of the refusal. People refused for two reasons: because a thing was worthless, or because it was the most valuable thing they had and giving it away would cost blood. Edmund Vasquez did not guard worthless things. She'd seen him hand over guild secrets under knife-point with the serenity of a man reading a shopping list. But this —

This he guarded like the child herself.

"You don't know the name either," she said slowly, watching him. "Do you. Or you know it and saying it out loud makes it real."

Edmund looked at the wine in his cup as though auditing it. "I know what she told me. Parts of it. Men who cut words out of children, she said. Men who harvest speech. If there's a name attached to that, I haven't pried it loose yet, and I won't pry it loose just to pay a toll."

"Even to save her?"

"Especially not to save her. Saving her *with* her own secrets sold off isn't saving her. It's relocating the theft."

Well. Tessaly turned her cup slowly on the oilcloth, one full rotation, thinking. Outside the lantern chains ticked against their hooks in the draft, and far overhead the tavern noise rose and fell like breathing. Somewhere in the dark water below the walkways, something shifted — a crab, a drowned crate, the city's usual sediment settling.

He'd just told her more than he knew. *Men who cut words out of children.* She pressed that flower flat in her mind alongside the others and felt them begin, all together, to make a picture. Crown inquisitor asking polite questions. A coin stamped with a dead god's face. Harvested speech. A cabal-shaped hole in the middle of everything, and Edmund Vasquez standing in front of a seven-year-old with his coat open against the wind.

It was, she reflected, exactly the sort of stupid, doomed, honorable arrangement she had sworn off after her brother died. Exactly the kind. And exactly the kind she had never once managed to walk away from.

"New terms," she said.

Edmund's head came up.

"You keep the name. All of it. When you dig it loose — and you will, because you're you, and because the girl will give it to you herself someday, mark me, children always tell the ones who wait — *then* I get it. Not before. Until then, the passage runs on favors alone: two of the three, and the last one held in reserve for whatever night goes worst." She stuck out her hand across the oilcloth. "But hear the rest, because it's not charity. I'm not doing this for you, ledger-man. I'm doing it because whoever taught that girl the Tongue cut words out of children, and I have a professional interest in making the world smaller for people like that. Consider it an investment."

Edmund took her hand. His grip was dry and careful, a clerk's grip, but he held it a beat longer than the transaction required — which, from Edmund, was practically an embrace.

"When?" he asked. "How fast can you move, if it comes to it?"

"Tonight, if the tide's right. I've got a route through the flooded quarter that doesn't touch a single crown road, and a woman named Brack who pilots a barge so low in the water she's legally a rumor." Tessaly stood, hauling one of the lanterns down on its chain to light the walkway ahead. "Bring the girl tomorrow. Daylight, busy market hour — nobody looks sideways at a man walking with a child when there's fish to haggle over. I'll show her the route myself. If she's going to run these boards, she should learn them from someone who won't drown her by accident."

"She'll bite you."

"Good. I'll know she's clever." Tessaly grinned and started down the walkway, boots ringing on wet oak, and found — somewhat to her irritation, somewhat not — that she meant it. "Anything else I should know about her? Habits? Triggers? Things I shouldn't say?"

Edmund followed, and for a moment he said nothing, and the undercroft dripped around them like a slow clock.

"She flinches at bells," he said finally. "Church bells, ship's bells, any of them. And she doesn't like deep water."

Tessaly stopped walking.

She turned, slowly, and looked back at Edmund Vasquez standing in the lantern-light among the rising tide-chains, and felt the pressed flowers in her mind rearrange themselves into a shape she did not like at all. Bells. Deep water. A silenced god whose old shrine, if the oldest songs were true, had gone under the harbor a century ago and never been dredged.

"Ledger-man," she said quietly, "what exactly is it that girl swallowed?"

And Edmund, who had come down into her flooded counting house to buy passage and had instead paid something rarer, answered her with the first fully honest sentence of the night:

"I think," he said, "she swallowed the reason my god stopped speaking."

Above them, muffled through stone and timber and the bones of the Saltwife's Rest, the harbor bell began to ring the evening watch — and somewhere in the dark water beneath the walkways, Tessaly would have sworn afterward, something vast and patient rolled over in its sleep.
