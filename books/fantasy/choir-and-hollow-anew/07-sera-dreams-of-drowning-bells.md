## 7. Sera Dreams of Drowning Bells

The bells came first, before anything else, the way they always did.

They rang beneath water in her dreams — great drowned bells hanging from a sunken tower, swinging slow and soundless in the green dark, and she knew, the way you know things in dreams, that they were not silent because they had nothing to say. They were silent because someone had taken their voices away and put them somewhere safe. Somewhere small. Somewhere that breathed.

Her.

In the dream she stood on a shore of black glass, and the sea did not move, and above the drowned tower the sky was full of hands — hundreds of them, thousands, reaching down through the clouds like roots growing the wrong direction. The hands did not grab. They only waited. And far below, in the green dark, one bell began to tremble against its rope.

*Sera,* said a voice that was not a voice, coming up through the soles of her feet instead of her ears. *Sera, little vessel, little jar, little mouth. Open.*

She woke with frost on her lips.

Not near her lips. *On* them — a rime of white crusted along the seam of her mouth like breath made solid, and when she licked it away it tasted of iron and old snow. She was lying in the narrow bed in the room above Maren's archive, under three blankets Edmund had brought without being asked about it, and every surface around her was white. Frost ferned across the window glass in feathers. It climbed the bedposts in spirals. It bloomed across the floor in wide cold flowers, each petal edged in silver, beautiful the way some snakes were beautiful, which is to say: from very far away.

Sera lay still and counted, the way she always counted. One for the door. Two for the stairs. Three for the sounds of the guildhouse waking below — boots, a kettle, the scrape of a shutter. Four for the fact that no one had come running yet, which meant they didn't know. Five for how long she had until they knew.

She had learned, in the place with the men who cut words out of children, that it was better to know these numbers than to be surprised by them.

She got up carefully, stepping between the frost-flowers so as not to break any, because breaking them felt like breaking something alive, and pressed her palm flat against the coldest pane of glass. *Go back to sleep,* she whispered at it, in the language that lived under her ribs, the one she hadn't learned but *was*, and the frost shivered, and dimmed, and slid off the window in a thin sheet of meltwater that ran down inside the wall and dripped onto Maren's books below.

Sera closed her eyes.

*I'm sorry,* she told the room, in ordinary speech, because sorry was a word that worked in every tongue. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

The room did not answer. Rooms never did. But sometimes, lately, things answered *for* rooms.

* * *

Maren found out anyway, of course, because Maren found out everything, and she claimed this was not because she was blind but precisely because she was. "Eyes lie," the old archivist liked to say, tapping her temple with one knuckle. "Floors don't."

It was the floors that betrayed Sera. She came down the archive stairs mid-morning and stopped on the last step, her bare feet going cold, her head tilting as though listening — which, in a way, she was. Then she turned her face up toward where Sera stood frozen on the stairwell landing.

"You walked through my workroom," Maren said mildly, sorting papers by feel, "and left footprints."

"I wiped my feet."

"You left footprints in *frost*, child. On a warm floor. In a house with four lit hearths." Maren set down her papers. Her clouded eyes found Sera with unsettling accuracy. "Come here. Sit by me. Don't look ashamed; shame makes it worse. I've read enough treatises on the subject to know."

Sera came. She sat. The bench creaked under the old woman's weight beside her, and smelled of ink and lavender oil and something faintly scorched, like a candle that had been snuffed rather than burned out.

"Now," Maren said. "Tell me what you dreamed."

"How do you know I—"

"Because there's a ring of frost on your bedroom floor shaped exactly like a bell, and because Edmund has been pacing outside your door since dawn pretending to check the locks, and because I am old, not stupid, and those are different conditions entirely." She held out her hand, palm up. "Dreams are just words you don't get to choose. You're carrying a great many words, Sera. Some nights they press against the walls of you. Tell me the shape of the pressure and maybe we can make it fit better."

So Sera told her. About the black-glass shore. About the drowned tower and its voiceless bells. About the hands in the sky, waiting. About the voice that spoke through her feet, calling her a jar, a vessel, a mouth.

Maren went very still when Sera described the hands.

"Roots," the old woman said quietly. "You saw roots growing downward."

"They were hands."

"To you, perhaps. Hands reach for people. Roots reach for soil." Maren's fingers found Sera's wrist and rested there, light as a moth, taking her pulse the way healers did but slower, more thoughtful. "In the First Speech, the word for *to take hold* and the word for *to take root* are the same word. Did you know that? The binders used to say it meant gods could be planted in people. The priests said it meant people could be planted in gods. They fought wars over the difference." A dry, humorless laugh. "Both of them were wrong. It means neither. It means *belonging*, and belonging frightens everyone."

"Am I—" Sera's voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Is something trying to plant itself in me?"

"No." Maren's grip firmed, brief and sure. "Something is trying to *speak* through you, and you keep clenching shut, and clenched things crack. That's all the frost is. That's all the nightmares are. Not a haunting. A held breath." She released Sera's wrist and folded her own hands in her lap. "We're going to play a game."

"A game."

"Mm. Games are how children learn things that would terrify them if you called them lessons. I raised six apprentices on games, and five of them thanked me later." She smiled, and there was something underneath the smile that Sera couldn't name yet — an old grief, maybe, or an old fear wearing kindness's coat. "First game. Name five things you can hear."

"That's easy."

"Do it anyway."

"The kettle downstairs. Boots in the hall. Someone arguing about grain weights. The fire in the big hearth. And..." Sera paused, then admitted it, because lying to Maren felt like lying to a well. "And you breathing."

"Good. Now name five things you can feel."

"The bench. My blanket sleeve. The cold in my toes. Your shoulder touching mine. The... the words." She touched her own sternum, where the pressure lived. "Here. Like stones in a bag."

"Yes," Maren said softly. "There they are. Now — and this is the part that matters — don't push them down. Pushing down is why they come up through the floor. Instead, do this." She lifted Sera's hand and pressed it flat against the armrest of the bench, against the worn wood. "Say the wood. Say it in the Tongue if you can — just the word for wood, just that one. And while you say it, feel the bench. Feel that the bench is real, and warm-ish, and boring, and here. Let the word go *out* of you into something real. Don't let it circle inside."

"It'll break something."

"It might, the first few times. That's why we practice on benches and not on people." Maren's face turned toward her, and even blind, her gaze had weight. "Sera. Listen to me. The words want out. Every night you hold them in, they carve a little more room inside you, and one morning you'll wake up and there won't be enough *you* left to hold them. We give them places to go. Small places. Safe places. Wood and wool and bread and bathwater. We make the magic boring, bit by bit, until it stops being a storm and starts being weather."

Sera looked down at her hand on the bench. The words stirred behind her ribs, uneasy, the way a dog stirs when it hears its name.

"What if I say it wrong?"

"Then we'll sweep up whatever breaks and try again tomorrow," Maren said briskly, and Sera believed her, which was the strangest feeling of all. "Go on. *Wood.*"

Sera took a breath. Found the word — it rose easily, eagerly, almost gratefully, a smooth grey syllable with a root at the end of it — and spoke it into the bench, quiet as a secret.

Nothing shattered. The candles guttered once. And under her palm, the worn wood of the armrest warmed — just slightly, just for a moment — as though something enormous had exhaled through it, far away, without meaning any harm.

"There," Maren murmured. "Wasn't that better than frost?"

Sera didn't answer right away, because her eyes were burning, and she didn't fully understand why. Nobody had ever taught her what to do with the power. Nobody had ever taught her what to do with *kindness*, either. Both of them kept leaking out of her at the wrong times.

* * *

The games became the shape of her days.

There was the naming game in the mornings — five sounds, five touches, five smells — which Maren swore was grounding and which Sera suspected was also just how Maren got the archive dusted. There was the bread game, where Sera kneaded dough and whispered the word for *rise* into it, and if she whispered it gently the loaves came out perfect and golden, and if she was frightened or angry they came out like risen boulders that Edmund ate anyway, solemnly, declaring them "structurally interesting."

There was the counting game on the roof, where Edmund — awkward, patient Edmund, who carried ledgers everywhere and spoke like a man reading terms aloud — taught her to count chimneys and gulls and roof-slates instead of exits and footsteps. "A keeper counts what he protects," he'd told her, the first time he caught her doing her old tally at the window. "Not what threatens him. Try it my way for a week. If you hate it, go back."

She hadn't gone back. Counting chimneys made the city feel less like a trap. Counting gulls made the sky feel less like hands.

And there was the game Sera loved most and trusted least: the whisper-back game, played after dark in the archive, with all the candles lit. Maren would hum a note — any note, a low droning hum — and Sera had to answer it in the First Tongue, one single word, matched to the pitch. Low words for low notes. Bright words for high ones. The trick, Maren said, was choosing the word herself, quickly, before the words chose her.

"Because that's the whole secret, child," Maren said one night, feeding another candle stub to a holder already crowded with melted stalagmites of wax. "The Tongue isn't dangerous because it's powerful. It's dangerous because it's *hungry*. Words that once commanded gods don't like being small. Left alone in you, they'll grow. Used often, used *small*, used for bread and warmth and naming birds — they stay the size you make them. You're not keeping a monster fed by using it. You're keeping it *trained*."

"And if I miss a day? If I'm sick, or scared, and I don't play the games?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. The candle flames leaned in the draft from the window, all together, like a congregation turning heads.

"Then we have worse nights," she said finally. "Like last night."

Sera's stomach dropped. "What happened last night?"

"You don't remember."

"No."

"You woke at the third hour. Standing at the top of the stairs. You were speaking — a long passage, flowing, the way water flows, no pauses. And it wasn't your voice, Sera. It was yours played backward and deeper. Edmund heard it first and came up the steps, and you turned and looked straight through him, and the frost came up the banister under his hand like a tide." Maren reached over and took Sera's cold fingers, folding them into her own warm ones. "He didn't flinch. I want you to know that. He stepped back, slowly, and he said your name — your *name*, not a command, not a counter-word, just Sera, Sera, it's Edmund, you're dreaming — until you came back. Four times. He never raised his voice. When you woke, you cried, and he sat with you till dawn, and this morning he asked me — not you, *me*, because he thought you'd be ashamed — whether the games were working."

"Were they?" Sera whispered. "Working? If I did *that*—"

"They are working the way dams work. Slowly. Against great pressure. And someday, if we do this right—" Maren squeezed her hands. "Someday the pressure won't be a flood. It'll be a river with banks. Rivers can be lovely. People build entire lives beside rivers."

"But what did I *say*?" The question tore out of her before she could soften it. "Last night. What did the voice say?"

Maren hesitated. Sera watched her hesitate, watched the old woman weigh truth against mercy on scales only she could see, and loved her for it and hated her for it in the same breath.

"It named a place," Maren said at last. "Repeatedly. A shrine — a drowned one, south of the city, past the salt flats. And it used a word I haven't heard since I was a girl studying forbidden grammars. The word for *return*. Or possibly the word for *be returned*. In that language, Sera, the distinction is not always yours to make."

Outside, the wind changed, coming off the sea with a rawer edge than it had carried all autumn. Maren tilted her head, listening to it the way she listened to everything.

"Snow soon," she said. "And someone else, if I'm not wrong. Someone walking up the guild road who doesn't walk like a merchant or a debtor or a thief." She rose, joints cracking, and began pinching out candles one by one, plunging the archive into soft successive darknesses. "Off to bed, child. Play the naming game as you climb the stairs. All five senses. And leave your door unlatched tonight — not locked, unlatched. If the river rises again, it should find us easy to reach."

Sera climbed the stairs naming things. Kettle, banked. Wool, scratchy. Lavender, fading. Stair-wood, warm where a hundred years of feet had polished it. And underneath all of it, faint and rising, like bells ringing beneath water:

Salt air. And smoke. And someone — far off down the dark guild road — singing, ragged and cracked, a hymn in a dead god's honor.

She froze at the top step, five senses full, and realized with a lurch that she recognized the melody.

It was the song the drowned bells sang, in her dream, when they still had voices.
