## 8. The Last Priest

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width again.

Edmund noticed it the way he noticed everything wrong in his accounts — not with alarm but with the flat, tired arithmetic of a man who had been subtracting losses for twenty years. The shrine candle, tallow and cheap beeswax mixed, should have lasted until first thaw. It was dying three weeks early. He made a note to buy more, then crossed it out, because there was no more to buy; the chandler on Wick Lane had stopped stocking the god's blend two winters ago, when the last of the faithful stopped coming.

He lit it anyway. He lit it every night, kneeling on the cold flagstone of the little alcove off his room where a bowl of river water and a nub of incense constituted the whole of his devotional life. The god had no name anymore. Or rather — Edmund had given the name back decades ago, at the altar of the Hollow Ledger Guild, when he'd sworn his oath into the guild's great book and felt something in his chest go quiet as a closed door. Men who served the guild served nothing else. That was the price. That was the protection.

*For silence given, silence kept,* the old covenant ran. He had thought it metaphor.

He was thinking about Sera instead. He was always thinking about Sera now, which was its own kind of prayer, and probably a blasphemy. Three days since Maren had dropped her teacup. Three days since the word *First Speech* had entered his house like weather through a broken shutter, impossible to close out. The girl herself remained cheerfully oblivious to what she carried, eating her porridge with too much honey, learning guild-card games from Tessaly's runners, falling asleep mid-sentence in front of Maren's fire as though she had lived among them all her life. As though she had never been afraid.

Edmund knew better. Feral children did not relax like that unless they had decided — with the cold, total certainty of small survivors — that these particular large people were worth the risk. He hadn't earned it. He kept waiting to fail her.

The candle guttered. Outside, the wind came off the harbor carrying the smell of coming snow, and somewhere below, in the undercroft where Tessaly kept her smuggled goods, a door slammed, and men laughed, and the guildhouse went on being the guildhouse: ledgers, debts, the slow grinding commerce of other people's desperation.

A knock at the street door. Three strikes, spaced too evenly. Not the knock of a debtor. Debtors knocked fast and ashamed.

Edmund rose, knees cracking, and took the short cudgel from beside the shrine before he went down. Old habits. Older than his oath.

* * *

The man on the step was rain-soaked, frost-nipped, and dressed in the ruins of what had once been vestments — you could still see the ghost of silver thread at the hem, picked out by scavengers long ago but leaving the stitch-holes like scars. He was perhaps fifty, perhaps sixty; hunger flattens faces and ages them unevenly. His eyes were the problem. They were clear as well-water and utterly without fear, which on this street, at this hour, meant either madness or faith, and Edmund had learned the hard way that the distinction mattered less than people hoped.

"You're Vasquez," the man said. Not a question.

"Guild business is conducted at the counting-house. Sunrise to dusk."

"Guild business." The man smiled, and it was a terrible smile, tender and ruined. "I'm not here about your guild, keeper of accounts. I'm here about the one you keep." He lifted both hands, palms out, empty. A beggar's gesture. A priest's. "My name is Odo. Last of the Order of the Hushed Bell. I've walked four hundred miles to say one thing to you, and then I'll trouble you for soup, if you're the charitable sort, which I suspect you are, underneath."

Edmund did not move. The cudgel stayed loose and ready along his thigh. "Say it."

Odo's gaze went past him, up the stairwell, into the dark of the house above, as if he could see through plaster and floorboards to where a child slept. When he spoke, his voice dropped into a register Edmund had not heard in thirty years — the cadence of liturgy, worn smooth by repetition.

"The Quiet One did not die, Edmund Vasquez. Your god did not fade, did not abandon, was not defeated in any war worth the name. Your god was *silenced.* Bound mouth to throat with a single word, spoken once, at the drowning, and hidden after in flesh so that no binder could ever find it and use it again." His eyes came back and fixed on Edmund's, bright and awful. "And it has been found. It sleeps upstairs in your house tonight, in a girl who speaks the First Tongue in her sleep. You are housing the silencing of your own god, keeper. And I have come to ask you — I have come to *beg* you — to take it from her."

* * *

They sat in the kitchen, because Edmund refused on principle to bring strangers near the shrine, and Odo ate soup like a man who had forgotten food was a thing bodies wanted — slowly, reverently, both hands around the bowl. Steam climbed his face. Frost-melt dripped from his ruined hem onto the flagstones and Edmund found himself watching each drop hit the stone and spread, and thinking, with the dispassion of a lifelong auditor: *this man has come a very long way to cost me something.*

"Maren knows," Odo said. It wasn't a question either. This priest asked very few questions; he arrived at things the way water arrives at low ground. "Blind archivist. Maren Ostrowe, formerly of the Collegium at Vell. She catalogued forbidden grammars before the fire. Of course she knew the moment she heard it. I imagine she hasn't slept."

"You've been watching us."

"I've been watching everyone," Odo said mildly. "It is the whole of my remaining duty. There were nine hundred of us, once. Bells, robes, the drowned shrine at Marlow Deep, all of it. Now there is me, and a hermit in the Greymarshes who may be dead, and a woman in Corvane's own customs office who lights a candle where no one can see. We are what survived of the Hushed Bell." He set down the spoon. "You were dedicated to Him once. Before the guild bought your voice."

The words landed precisely, the way well-aimed blows do, and Edmund hated him a little for the accuracy. "That's not in any record."

"Everything is in some record. I read yours." Odo pushed the bowl away, half-finished, which told Edmund more about his state of mind than anything he'd said. "Novice Edmund Vasquez, age eleven, dedicated at the riverside chapel of Saint-Above-the-Weir, year of the Long Ferry. Your mother's signature is there. She cried when she signed it; the priest noted it, because priests notice. Then the order dissolved, the chapels were sold for quarry-stone, and the Guild of the Hollow Ledger took the orphaned dedicants first, because we were already trained in one kind of service and our vows could be bought cheap." He looked up. "Am I wrong in any particular?"

"No," Edmund said quietly. "You're not wrong in any particular."

Thirty-one years ago. He could still feel it sometimes, the moment of the second oath — not pain, exactly, but subtraction. Something lifted out of him with the gentleness of a surgeon and replaced with the ledger-law: *all debts counted, all debts collected, nothing owed to heaven.* The guild had called it freedom. Most nights, even now, he believed them.

"So take it back," Odo said.

The kitchen was very still. The fire ticked in the grate. Upstairs, faintly, floorboards settled — Sera turning in her sleep, or Maren walking her nightly circuit past the girl's door, checking, the way she had begun to check, every hour, without ever saying why.

"Take what back," Edmund said, though he knew. He had known since the doorstep. That was the obscene genius of it — the request was shaped exactly like the hole in him.

"The word she carries. Take it into yourself. You were His; the dedication never fully washes out, that's doctrine and also physics, for want of a better word. A hollowed vessel can be refilled. If you take the silencing word into your own keeping, the girl becomes only a girl. Strange, gifted, hunted for lesser reasons perhaps — but not *this.* Not the thing the binders' heirs would burn cities to hold." Odo leaned forward, and his ruined face was transfigured, lit from inside by a certainty that Edmund recognized because he had once owned one just like it and sold it. "She keeps her life. You reclaim your god. Everyone is paid. I have done the arithmetic, Master Vasquez — I understand it is your language. Tell me where the sum fails."

And oh, the sum was beautiful. That was the horror. Edmund sat with his hands flat on the table — the same posture he used for balancing columns — and let himself actually do it, actually count, because he was an honest man in his own private way and dishonest arithmetic offended him even when it tempted him.

Sera freed of the word. Himself restored to something he had mourned nightly by candlelight for three decades. Odo's four hundred miles justified. The cabal — and yes, he believed in them now; Vess's coin with the silent god's face had seen to that — deprived of their prize, or at least forced to deal with a hardened guildsman rather than a child. Every column balanced.

Except.

"Where did you learn she has it?" Edmund asked. "Truly. Not the doctrine. The *specifics.* A girl in a port city, three streets from the guildhouse, speaking First Tongue in her sleep — how does the last priest of a dead order four hundred miles away hear of it within weeks?"

Odo's stillness lasted one breath too long.

"There are those who watch," he said. "Fugitives move along old roads. Word travels—"

"Word travels," Edmund agreed. "But not that fast, and not that precisely, unless someone wants it to." He stood, slowly, and began clearing the soup things, needing his hands occupied. "Here's what I know, Father Odo. Someone taught that child the Tongue — or worse, put the word *in* her — and someone cut a trail behind her doing it. Inquisitor Vess didn't find me by luck; he left a coin on my table stamped with my god's face. Whoever is hunting her is using watchers, and watchers talk, and some of what they carry might be true and some of it bait." He set the bowls in the basin with exaggerated care. "So when a ragged pilgrim arrives with a story that fits my oldest wound like a key fits a lock, and asks me to reach into a sleeping child and pull out the one thing keeping her valuable — forgive me if I check the lock twice."

"She would give it," Odo said. "If she understood. Children understand sacrifice better than we do; they haven't been taught to fear it yet."

"You don't know that she'd survive the taking. Neither do I. Neither does *she.*" Edmund turned. "That's the flaw in your sum, father. You wrote it in ink that doesn't exist."

* * *

Odo did not leave. That was the trouble with priests; they had nowhere to go and infinite patience for arriving. Edmund gave him the lean-to off the stable, a blanket, and standing permission for the morning porridge, and told himself it was surveillance rather than charity, and knew he was lying.

For two days the priest was harmless — sweeping the stable unasked, mending harness with clumsy piety, teaching Sera a finger-game involving knotted cord that made her laugh in a way Edmund hadn't heard from her yet, high and startled, like a bird discovering flight. Maren watched Odo across rooms with her blind eyes and said little, except once, catching Edmund's sleeve in the corridor: *"His doctrine is sound. That's what frightens me. Sound doctrine is how the worst things get built."*

On the third night, Edmund woke at the hour of the wolf and knew immediately something had changed. The house had a texture when all within it slept, a grain he'd learned across years of bad nights, and tonight the grain was wrong. He took the cudgel and went up.

Sera's door stood open. Inside, the window was white with frost — not the thin lace of ordinary winter but thick rime, ferned and feathered across the glass in patterns like frozen bells, and the air itself rang faintly, or seemed to, the way a struck glass rings after the sound has technically ended. The girl lay tangled in blankets, thrashing, and the sounds coming out of her were not screams. They were syllables. Deep, layered, ancient, a voice like a bell rung underwater, pouring out of a body far too small for it.

Maren was already there, on her knees by the bed, hands hovering without touching, murmuring the grounding game — *name five blue things, name four soft things* — calm as Sunday while frost crawled the walls around her.

And Odo stood in the doorway beside Edmund, and he was weeping, openly, helplessly, tears freezing in the creases of his ruined face, and he whispered:

"*Listen to it.* Thirty years I prayed for one word from Him. And here it is — here is the mouth of God, stuffed full of its own silencing, crying in its sleep in a child's bed." He gripped Edmund's arm with desperate strength. "Every night she dreams this, keeper. Every night the word works at her from inside, looking for a way out. You can leave her to it. You can. But you will stand here and tell me honestly that your ledger balances, that the comfort of one man's conscience outweighs—"

"That's enough," Edmund said, and his voice came out strange, scraped, because the ringing in the air was getting into his chest, finding the old hollow where his god had lived, and resonating there like a finger on a wound. The frost reached the foot of the bed. Sera arched, and the voice coming through her cracked upward into — briefly, horribly — a child's ordinary sobbing, *don't make me say it, please don't make me say it*, before sinking back into the deep syllables.

Edmund crossed the room, took the girl's ice-cold hands in his, and did the only rite he remembered from a chapel sold for quarry-stone. He pressed her palms together between his own, bowed his head, and spoke the dedication-word — the small one, the safe one, the first prayer a novice learned: *held, held, wholly held.*

The frost stopped growing.

The ringing faded. Sera subsided, breathing ragged, and muttered something in plain trade-tongue about honey and card games, and slept like any child.

Silence in the room. Maren's hand found Edmund's shoulder and rested there, light as ash.

Odo wiped his face with a sleeve. When he spoke, his voice had lost its liturgy and gained something worse: simple, human exhaustion.

"She will not stop having these dreams, Edmund. They will worsen — the word is waking, the binders' heirs are closing in, and the nearer they come the louder it gets in her. I will not pretend otherwise to move you. All I ask—" and here the old priest, last of nine hundred, knelt on the frosted floorboards of a stranger's house and bent his grey head, "—is that you consider it. Truly consider. Not tonight. Just... before the choice is taken from you. Because it *will* be taken. Vess is already in the city. And there are worse things than Vess behind him."

He rose, bowed to the sleeping girl as to an altar, and went out into the dark toward the stable.

Edmund stood a long time in the cold room, holding the shape of two small hands in his memory, listening to the frost melt off the walls drop by drop, like a candle measuring out what remained.

Below, in his alcove, the shrine candle had finally gone out. And in the morning — he was almost sure, standing there in the dark — he was almost sure that when he went down to relight it, he would find he could not remember why he had ever stopped believing the silence answered back.
