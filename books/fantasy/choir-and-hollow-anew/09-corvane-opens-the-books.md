## 9. Corvane Opens the Books

The guildhouse woke that morning to the sound of keys.

Not the ordinary keys — not Tessaly's picklocks whispering in a lock's ear, not the janitor Hobb's great iron ring that jangled like a warning bell when he was drunk. These were the master keys, the ones Corvane kept on a chain against his breastbone the way other men wore relics. They had not sounded in the halls since the winter Edmund had sworn his oath and signed his name into the Hollow Ledger Guild's book with ink mixed from ash and tallow. Seven years of silence, ended at dawn by a man walking his own corridors as though they belonged to someone else.

Edmund heard it from the shrine alcove off the counting room, where he knelt before the candle he lit each night for a god who had never once answered. Wax had pooled so thick around the stub that it looked like a small white mountain, a mountain of all his unanswered prayers. He rose, knees cracking, and went to see what his guildmaster feared.

You could always tell what Corvane feared by how loudly he moved.

* * *

The counting room smelled of paper and cold iron. Corvane stood at the central table with the year-ledgers open before him, all nine of them, their brass clasps undone, their pages fanned like the feathers of anxious birds. Behind him waited two men who did not belong: clerks in crown grey, their sleeves rolled, their faces smooth as eggs, already sorting correspondence into piles that had nothing to do with the guild's business.

"Vasquez." Corvane did not look up. He was a narrow man, Corvane, built like a ledger's spine — all straight lines and stiffness, holding everything together by force of binding. His hair had gone more silver since autumn began. "You'll want to review your collections column. The crown has questions about your winter routes."

"The crown," Edmund said carefully, "has questions about our winter routes."

"The crown," said one of the egg-faced clerks, without looking up, "has questions about everything."

Edmund let the silence sit the way he'd been taught to let a debtor's silence sit — patiently, letting it do his work for him. The candle he'd left burning in the alcove guttered behind him. He thought of Sera upstairs in Maren's archive nook, learning her letters under the pretense of learning *their* letters, the First Tongue hidden inside her like a swallowed coal. He thought of Vess's coin in his pocket, stamped with his god's silent face, and how it had grown no lighter in the week since the inquisitor left it there.

"Guildmaster," Edmund said, "might we speak without the crown's arithmetic in the room?"

Corvane's pen stopped. For one heartbeat — Edmund would have missed it in anyone else, but he had spent seven years reading this man's ledgers and knew every tremor of his hand — the pen hesitated above the page like a diver above black water.

Then Corvane said, "Leave us," and the clerks gathered their piles and withdrew, and the door closed on them soft as snowfall.

"They'll be back," Corvane said. "They're cataloguing the west archive now. Vess arrives within the hour, and he wants the person registers open when he does." He finally raised his eyes. They were red-rimmed. Edmund had never seen Corvane tired before; tiredness, in the guildmaster, had always seemed a thing beneath his dignity, like sentiment or charity. "Including the girl."

"There is no girl in the registers."

"There is a girl in my halls, Vasquez, eating my bread and teaching my archivist to smile, which I had thought anatomically impossible. There is an inquisitor downstairs who can smell unregistered persons the way a hound smells blood, and who has already asked me — politely, in the way of his kind — why my keeper of accounts lights a candle to a dead god every night for seven years." Corvane pressed both palms flat against the open ledger, as if to hold its pages down against a wind only he felt. "I am ordering you, as guildmaster and under the law of the books, to surrender the child's registration. Full entry. Name, origin, valuation. She becomes a guild asset by nightfall, or she is turned out by the same."

The words landed in Edmund's chest and sat there, heavy as coins dropped down a well.

"You know what entry means," Edmund said. An asset entered in the books could be sold, collateralized, transferred, seized. Entry was not paperwork. Entry was ownership written in the guild's own hand, which the crown could then read.

"I know precisely what it means." Corvane drew a breath that shuddered at its edges. "And I know what refusal means, which is that Halvard Vess stops asking polite questions and starts asking the kind that end with the guildhouse sealed and everyone inside it entered into *his* books. You think I want this? I have spent thirty years keeping this house out of the crown's sight, Vasquez. Thirty years of cleverness. And this winter it ends because you took in a feral child who—" He stopped himself. His jaw worked. "Because of what she is. Or carries. Or whatever name we're pretending not to use."

So Vess had told him. Not everything, perhaps — Corvane did not say *the First Tongue*, did not say *the silencing word* — but enough. Enough to be afraid, and afraid men did arithmetic instead of thinking.

"She's a child," Edmund said quietly.

"She's a liability wearing a child."

"And I'm carrying her debt. Registered lawful under the very loophole you approved. Carried debt cannot be transferred without the carrier's consent. You taught me that clause, Guildmaster. Chapter four, the Winter Codex, your own amendment of—"

"I remember what I wrote!" It came out cracked, and Corvane mastered himself with visible effort, smoothing his coat front the way he smoothed a page. When he spoke again his voice was low and even, which was worse. "Consent can be pressured. Consent can be bought. And consent, Vasquez, can be made irrelevant, if the inquisitor decides the law that protects her is inconvenient. Do you understand what is coming up those stairs today? Not a man. A tide. And I will not drown this house for one girl who arrived in a debt collection like a bad coin in a purse."

Edmund held his guildmaster's gaze and gave him the truth he could afford: "She stays carried. That's my answer under ledger-law, and you can enter my refusal however you like."

For a long moment neither moved. Somewhere below them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the crown clerks opening cabinets, drawers, doors — the guildhouse being inventoried like a dead man's estate.

Corvane looked down at the ledgers. "Then we are at an impasse," he said, "which the books will record as a disagreement between partners." And there — Edmund caught it, filed it, turned it over in the dark of his attention — there was something wrong in the way he said *partners*. Something rehearsed. As though he had practiced the sentence, or been given it.

"Understood, Guildmaster," Edmund said, and bowed the precise degree owed, and left the counting room with his heart beating slow and cold.

He did not go upstairs. He went down — down the back stair, down past the strongroom, into the record vault where the guild kept its dead years: closed accounts, dissolved contracts, the ledgers of men who had paid in full or failed entirely. No one audited the vault in winter. No one but him.

He needed to look at something. He didn't yet know what. But seven years of keeping accounts had taught him that when a man's numbers feel wrong, the error is always somewhere real — hiding in a column, waiting to be found.

* * *

The vault lamps burned low and blue. Edmund worked methodically, because method was all he had, pulling the annual abstracts — the summary volumes Corvane compiled each midwinter for the partners' review — and laying them across the reading table in a long pale spine of years.

He started with the current crisis and walked backward. Crown inquiries. There had been crown inquiries before. Small ones, handled quietly, noted in the margins in Corvane's cramped hand: *resolved*, *paid*, *deferred*. A tariff dispute six years ago. A customs seizure five years back, resolved with a fine that appeared nowhere in the expense columns. Three years ago an entire quarter's route schedules requested by the revenue office — and returned, the note said, *with the crown's gratitude*.

Gratitude. Edmund turned pages slowly. Gratitude was not a word crowns used. Crowns took payment and issued receipts.

He found the first thread by lamplight and candle-flicker combined, and once he saw it he could not stop seeing it. Every year, going back — he counted, and recounted, and made himself count again — going back eleven years, there were entries that balanced too neatly. Discrepancies that vanished. Losses recorded on routes that had run clean, offset by gains on routes that had never sailed. Small things, each one defensible alone, each one the kind of rounding a careful man might wave away. Together they formed a channel. A quiet, steady flow of value moving out of the guild's books and disappearing into the margin, the way water disappears into limestone.

Eleven years. Sera was perhaps ten.

Edmund sat back in the vault's cold air and felt something settle in him, patient and terrible, like ash settling after a fire.

It wasn't only money. Money he could have understood — greed was legible, greed was almost honest. He pulled the correspondence copies next, the letterpress duplicates Corvane kept of every official letter sent, and he matched dates. The customs seizure resolved two days after the fine. The revenue office's request for route schedules granted the same week a certain magistrate's brother received a guild contract at suspiciously generous terms. Six years ago, when the crown had nearly licensed its own rival counting house in the river district, the license had died in committee the very month Corvane's private account showed a withdrawal Edmund could find no source for.

The crown had not been pressuring the Hollow Ledger Guild for eleven years.

The Hollow Ledger Guild had been *feeding* the crown for eleven years, and being fed upon in return, and Corvane had kept the arrangement alive the way Edmund kept the shrine candle: nightly, faithfully, out of something that looked from a distance exactly like devotion.

And now Vess had come, and Corvane had opened the master keys at dawn, and ordered Sera surrendered not because he feared the inquisitor's questions about a girl —

but because Vess held the other end of the thread, and one sharp pull would unravel thirty years of Corvane's careful life. Surrendering Sera wasn't panic. It was payment. The guildmaster was settling an account with the crown, and the coin he'd chosen was a child who spoke the language the old gods used to be commanded in.

Edmund closed the last abstract with hands that did not shake, because he would not permit them to.

What frightened him — what he examined honestly, there in the blue lamp-light among the guild's dead years — was how little surprised he felt. Some part of him had always known the house stood on more than paper. Every institution does. He had simply preferred, for seven comfortable years, not to audit the foundation.

Here was the survival math, then, laid out as plainly as a column of figures. If he exposed Corvane, the guild fell — perhaps into Vess's hands outright, an inquisition gifted its prey wrapped in scandal. If he stayed silent, Corvane remained guildmaster, and Corvane had just demonstrated what he would trade and to whom. Either road ran through fire. The only question was who burned.

He thought of Maren dropping her teacup when Sera first spoke, tea spreading across the floorboards like a dark flower. He thought of Tessaly naming her price with a smuggler's grin. He thought of Sera asleep by the archive stove, frost feathering the window near her dreams, her small fists curled around nothing, guarding it.

Betrayal, he decided, was like a coal banked for the night: covered, smothered, made to hold its heat until morning. You did not throw it away. You did not feed it air. You kept it, and you chose the hour.

He copied three entries into his pocket-book — dates, sums, the magistrate's brother's contract — in a cipher of his own devising that no clerk in the crown's employ could read. Then he restored every volume to its shelf, aligned spines to the finger-width, and climbed the back stairs into the noise of the guildhouse being taken apart around him.

* * *

Upstairs, the world had rearranged itself while he was underground. Crown grey filled the corridors. The clerks had reached the second floor and were boxing the partnership records; Hobb stood watch outside Maren's nook with his ring of keys in his fist and an expression of monumental stupidity arranged over something sharper, bless him. Maren herself sat in the doorway of the archive with Sera beside her, the girl's head against the old woman's shoulder, both of them still as furniture while egg-faced men inventoried shelves they couldn't possibly understand.

Sera looked up when Edmund appeared. Her eyes found his the way a compass finds north, and in them was a question she was too wise to ask aloud, in any tongue.

"Not today," he told her, softly, in plain trade-speech. "Today you are carried debt, and the carrier says carry on."

Maren's hand tightened on the girl's shoulder. Her blind eyes turned toward Edmund, and she tilted her head a fraction — she had heard something change in his voice, of course she had; she heard everything — but she only said, "We're learning the letter *H*, Edmund. Sera says it looks like a ladder lying down."

"It does," Edmund agreed. "Mind you don't climb it."

From the stairwell below came footsteps, unhurried and polished, and a voice pitched for courtesy the way knives are pitched for cutting. Halvard Vess had arrived, right on the hour, to read the books.

Edmund stepped into the corridor and put his shoulders against the wall like a man with nowhere better to be, and watched the inquisitor climb toward him with the person register already open in his gloved hands, turning pages, smiling — smiling — as though he already knew what was written there.

And perhaps he did. Because the register Corvane had ordered prepared lay open to the day's fresh entries, and Edmund, who knew every hand in the house, could see that the newest line was not in Corvane's writing at all.

It was in crown grey ink.
