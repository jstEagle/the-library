## 10. Three Favors Called

The first favor Tessaly had ever owed Edmund Vasquez was a rope.

Not a metaphor. An actual rope, tarred and fraying, thrown down into a flooded cistern where she'd been hiding from a harbor master with a knife and a grudge, in the year the river took the Tanner's Quarter. She'd climbed it with her hands bleeding and her satchel of stolen charts soaked through, and Edmund had stood at the rim in the rain, said nothing, and pulled.

He'd never once mentioned it since. That was the currency of their friendship — silence, held in trust, accruing interest.

So when he'd come to her undercroft three nights ago, smelling of candle smoke and fear he was trying to file away like a misentered sum, and said *I need to call a favor*, she had already known it would be bad. She hadn't known it would be *this*.

"This," being a child pressed against her back on a barge pole in the dark, riding the flooded undercity of Vail's Hollow while men with lanterns and crown-stamped warrants walked the streets above.

"Hold on," Tessaly murmured. "Knees tight, hands where I put them, and whatever you hear — and you'll hear things, love, this city sings at night and none of its songs are kind — you don't answer. Not in their tongue, not in yours, not at all."

Sera's arms tightened around her waist. The girl weighed almost nothing. That was the thing that kept snagging in Tessaly's chest, the splinter she couldn't work loose: Edmund had described her as *cargo of a delicate nature*, had used ledger-speak the way he always did when something mattered, and Tessaly had pictured a parcel, a package, a thing to be moved. She had not pictured a child whose fingers were cold as river stones and who gripped like drowning.

"Can you swim?" Tessaly asked.

A pause. Then, in that voice that made the hair on Tessaly's arms rise — not because it was strange, though it was, but because it was *old*, the way a barrow is old — Sera said, "The water remembers being sky."

"Right." Tessaly exhaled slowly. "We're going to work on answers, you and I. But no. Good. Perfect. Don't answer."

She poled them out of the boathouse and into the black.

* * *

The undercity was Vail's Hollow's first draft, the one the river had rejected. Streets drowned to their second stories, bridges that went nowhere, staircases descending into green-black water where eels the length of a man's arm kept their own quiet ledgers. The smugglers called it the Long Wet. Tessaly called it home, in the way you call a knife home — with respect, and never turning your back on it.

She knew every rooftop island, every submerged arch whose keystone still broke the current just enough to pole past, every dry pocket where the old merchant families had sealed their cellars against the flood and then, in the manner of merchant families, sealed their valuables in too and drowned trying to retrieve them. There were things down here that had been underwater for two hundred years and were still, technically, owned. Tessaly had made a modest living on the difference between *owned* and *held*.

Tonight she wasn't hunting. Tonight she was prey with cargo, and the difference sat in her stomach like swallowed riverwater.

Above them, through a grate in a drowned chapel's roof, she heard boots. Four men, maybe five, moving in the paired rhythm of professionals — not guild muscle, not watch. Inquisitor's men. Vess's sweep had started at sundown, Edmund had said, and would work the guildhouse first, then the streets around it, then outward in rings, the way you drain a pond.

"They're looking for me." Sera's voice, very small, against her shoulder blade.

"They're looking for a girl," Tessaly said. "There's a difference, and we're going to be the difference." She poled them under the chapel arch, ducking a beam furred with white fungus that glowed faintly, like the memory of a lamp. "Rule two: nobody knows what you look like. So we make sure of it."

She poled into the flooded nave, where the water stood chest-deep over the pews, and stopped beneath the rotted remnant of a saint's mural. She'd used this room before. She reached into the wall-cavity where she kept her kit — wax, ash, a pot of the grey river-clay that dried the color of drowned skin — and turned to face the girl for the first time in an hour.

In the fungus-light, Sera looked at her with eyes too old for the face, and Tessaly felt the splinter go deeper.

"This will sting a little and itch a lot," Tessaly said. "And when I'm done, you'll be nobody's girl. You'll be my sister's brat, come down from the fen country with a fever, and you'll cough when I cough and complain when I complain. Can you complain?"

Sera's mouth twitched. It was the first almost-smile Tessaly had seen on her. "I can learn."

"Born for it, more like. Everyone from the fens is." She worked the clay into Sera's hair, darkening the white-streaked strands, greying her skin at the temples, giving her the slack-lidded look of a child three days into a bad fever. Sera bore it without flinching, which was its own kind of terrible. Children who flinched at clay and ash were children who hadn't been hurt enough to know when not to bother.

"What's your sister's name?" Sera asked, as Tessaly smoothed the last of it.

"Brannit. Dead eight years, gods keep her, which they don't, but it's polite to say." Tessaly tied the girl's hair back with a strip of sacking. "You're Brannit's by her second husband, who was fen-born and no good, which is why you have the look of nowhere about you. If anyone asks your name, you're Nell. Nell is dull. Nell is a name nobody writes down."

"Nell," Sera repeated. And then, softer, in the First Tongue — a word that made the standing water around their barge tremble, a single ring spreading out from the hull like something breathing beneath.

Tessaly went very still. "What was that."

Sera looked down, ashamed. "It means *kept*. A thing kept safe." She glanced up. "It slipped."

"Love." Tessaly took the girl's clay-dusted chin in her hand, gentle as handling a live coal. "Nothing slips tonight. Nothing. Whatever's in you, you keep it the way I keep charts — folded small, hidden deep, shown to no one. Can you do that?"

"I've done it my whole life," Sera said. "It's why I'm alive."

And there it was again — that splinter, twisting. Tessaly let go of the chin, picked up the pole, and pushed them back out into the current, because it was easier to be steady when she was moving.

* * *

They nearly died at the Weeping Gate.

It was a chokepoint — Tessaly had known that going in, had planned around it, had a dozen alternates. What she hadn't planned around was a lantern hanging from the gate's drowned arch, and beneath it a man in a grey coat, dry, impossibly dry in the middle of the Long Wet, standing on the water as if it were a courtyard floor.

Her first thought was *warden*. Her second was *run*. Her third, arriving late and cold, was that the man wasn't looking at them at all. He was looking at the gate itself, tracing its stones with one gloved finger, and where his finger passed, faint script kindled and died — wards, being read. Being *counted*.

Tessaly backed the barge into the shadow of a collapsed colonnade, slow as syrup, and put her mouth against Sera's ear.

"Don't look at him," she breathed. "He's a reader. They feel attention like a hand on the back of the neck."

But the girl was already looking. Of course she was. And the man in the grey coat went rigid, the way a hound goes rigid when the wind changes, and turned his head — not toward them, not exactly, but toward the *space* they occupied, as if the water had told him something.

Tessaly did the only thing the Long Wet had ever taught her: she made the world boring.

She poled out of the colonnade openly, coughing — a wet, fen-country hack, performed with real commitment — and steered toward the reader with the slouched insolence of a woman who had poled these waters for twenty years and resented every lantern the crown hung in them. Sera slumped against her, slack and feverish, clay-grey and coughing on cue, a masterpiece of misery.

"Gate's warded, is it?" Tessaly called up, before he could speak. "Wonderful. Crown hangs its lights down here now, and us poor devils can't even drown in peace. Which way's clear, then? I've a sick brat and a long pole and no patience."

The reader looked at her. Looked at the girl. Looked at the water.

For three heartbeats, Tessaly's whole life was the sound of dripping.

"South channel," he said at last, in a voice like a door closing. "And woman — if anything passes through here that shouldn't, I'll know."

"Everyone says that," Tessaly said. "Nobody knows."

She poled past beneath his standing feet, close enough to see the script still fading on the gate stones, close enough to see his eyes — pale as a fish's belly, and fixed not on her, not on Sera, but on the *wake* of their barge, reading it, the way Edmund read a ledger. She didn't breathe again until the arch was behind them and the dark had swallowed the lantern.

Only then did she realize Sera's hands had been pressed flat to the barge's hull the whole time, and that the wood beneath them was rimed with a skin of frost, thin as breath on glass, already melting.

"He was counting the wards," Sera whispered. "But they were wrong. Someone had changed them. He was angry about it, underneath. It sounded like — " she searched — "like a bell with a crack in it."

Tessaly thought of Edmund, three nights ago, asking her with elaborate casualness whether she knew anything about the wards on the guildhouse's river doors. Thought of his face when she'd said *why do you ask*. Thought of the way he hadn't answered.

"Someone's been busy," she said, and poled on.

* * *

The safehouse was a sealed merchant's cellar off the drowned Street of Chandlers, dry as a sermon, reached through a submerged stair that only opened if you knew which of the four handholds was false. Tessaly got them inside, barred the inner door, lit one candle — one, never more — and finally, finally let her shoulders come down from around her ears.

Sera stood in the middle of the room, clay half-flaked from her hair, looking at the cellar's one luxury: a child's wooden boat, carved crude and painted blue, sitting on a shelf among the charts. It had been Brannit's. Brannit had never had children. Tessaly had never explained why she kept it, and had never once been able to throw it out.

"That was mine," Tessaly said, catching her looking. "Before it was hers. Things last down here. That's the joke of it — the flood took the city and saved the furniture."

Sera touched the little boat with one finger, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Did you live down here when you were small?"

"Born two streets over, before the streets were over." Tessaly got out bread, hard cheese, the last of the dried plums, and watched the girl eat with the controlled, guilty ferocity of someone who had learned never to trust the next meal. She thought: *Edmund, you absolute ledger-keeper, you didn't tell me she was starving.* She thought: *he knows. He knows and it's eating him, and he stayed behind anyway, because someone has to make the numbers lie.*

"Edmund," Sera said, around the bread. "He stayed."

"He stayed." Tessaly sat down across from her, elbows on knees. "That's what he does. He stays with the numbers so the rest of us can run from them. It's a terrible way to love people, but it's his way, and he does it properly."

"Is it love?"

The question landed in the candlelight like a dropped coin. Tessaly looked at the girl — this feral, word-haunted scrap of a person who had known Edmund for perhaps a month and had already seen through to the load-bearing wall of him.

"That man threw a rope to a stranger and never asked her name," Tessaly said. "He's been asking it ever since, in his way. Every favor, every silence, every time he stands at the edge of something and pulls. Yes, love. The kind that doesn't know how to say so, so it does the books instead."

Sera considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then: "He lights a candle every night. For a god with no name. I've seen him. He thinks no one sees."

"I know," Tessaly said softly. "I've seen him too."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Just slightly — toward the girl, the way a flower leans toward a window. Sera didn't seem to notice. Tessaly noticed, and filed it away in the deep drawer where she kept the things she'd tell Edmund later, if there was a later, if there was an Edmund.

"Sleep," she said. "Nell is very ill and must be unconscious by morning."

Sera lay down on the pallet Tessaly made of sailcloth and old charts, and was asleep in moments — the sleep of the truly spent, the kind Tessaly recognized from her own mirror. Tessaly sat up with the candle and the knife she kept in her boot, and watched the door, and listened to the city above them creak and drip and lie.

Somewhere past the middle of the night, the girl began to dream. Tessaly knew because the candle flame turned blue, and frost feathered across the stone floor in patterns like script, and Sera said, in a voice that was not a child's voice, one word that Tessaly's bones understood even though her ears did not — a word that meant, she was somehow certain, *drown*.

Then Sera rolled over, sighed like any child, and the frost stopped growing, and the candle remembered its color.

Tessaly sat in the dark with her heart going like a barge pole in white water, and understood, finally and completely, what she was carrying. Not cargo. Not a package. A word. A word that had drowned a god, walking around in a girl small enough to fit inside a smuggler's coat, and men in grey coats who could stand on water were already reading the river for her wake.

*Hold on, little Nell,* she thought, absurdly. *Your family is stranger than mine was, but it's a family. I've checked the entries.*

* * *

Dawn came down through the flood as a grey-green shimmer, the Long Wet's idea of morning. Tessaly was up, had been up, repacking the kit, when the knock came — three short, one long, the rhythm she'd given Edmund and no one else, and her whole body unclenched at once.

She opened the inner door, and there he was, soaked to the thighs, smelling of smoke and ink, with soot on his knuckles and a look on his face she had seen only twice before: the night the Tanner's Quarter went under, and the day his god's shrine candle had gone out on its own.

"They took the guildhouse at moonrise," Edmund said. His voice was level. His hands were not. "Vess read the ledgers aloud in the counting hall. All of them. He smiled while he did it." He looked past her, found Sera sitting up on the pallet, and something in his face broke and re-set, quick as a bookkeeper correcting an entry. "Are you hurt?"

"I'm Nell," Sera said. "I have a fever."

"Good girl." And Tessaly saw it — saw the exact moment Edmund Vasquez, keeper of accounts, bound man, silent god's last candle-lighter, decided that the numbers no longer mattered. "Tess. The ledgers are burned. All of them. Every debt, every name, every entry I ever kept. There's no record in Vail's Hollow that Sera exists, or that any of us ever owed anyone anything."

Tessaly let out a low breath. "Corvane?"

"Corvane is in a cell, and Vess thinks the books were a trick, which means he'll start looking for the trick instead of the girl." Edmund's jaw tightened. "It buys us days. Not weeks. And there's something else." He reached into his coat and set a coin on the shelf, next to the little blue boat. It was stamped with a face Tessaly had never seen — a god's face, smooth, mouthless, serene. "This was on the pallet in my room. Vess knows what I was. What I *am*. He's not hunting a girl out of duty."

"He's hunting her for someone," Tessaly said.

"He's hunting her for the people who silence gods." Edmund looked at Sera, and Sera looked back, and between them passed something that had no ledger, no entry, no column — something that was simply owed, and would be paid. "So we move. Tonight. North road, Tessaly's contacts, before the reader counts his way to this door."

Tessaly was already reaching for her charts. "Then rest while you can, keeper. Because the Long Wet only has one rule, and you're about to learn it the hard way."

"Which is?"

She rolled up the map of the north road and slapped it against his chest.

"Everything runs downhill," she said, "and the crown is uphill."
