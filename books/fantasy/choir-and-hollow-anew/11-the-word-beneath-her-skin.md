## 11. The Word Beneath Her Skin

The lighthouse had not lit a lamp in forty years, Tessaly said, and the sea had taken its revenge by filling the keeper's rooms with salt and gull-quiet. Sera loved it anyway. It stood at the end of a drowned road like an old man wading out to meet the tide, and inside it smelled of cold stone and bird fat and, beneath everything, of iron — old iron, the kind that remembered being forged.

She climbed the spiral stair each morning to watch for boats that were not supposed to come. None came. The horizon lay flat as hammered pewter, and the wind worried at the broken lantern room overhead with a sound like someone patiently unbraiding hair.

Three days now since Tessaly had carried her through the flooded undercity, through water to the waist and dark to the wrist, while men with lanterns and long knives swept the guildhouse streets calling her name in a language she almost understood. Three days since Maren had found them at the salt-gate, blind hands finding Sera's shoulder first, always first, as if blindness were a compass and Sera was north.

Now they waited. Edmund was still behind them, in the counting house, among the ledgers. Nobody said this aloud. Everybody thought it constantly; Sera could feel it coming off the others like heat off a hearthstone.

She sat on the third-floor landing where a window faced the open water, knees pulled up, watching winter assemble itself over the sea. The frost came when she was afraid or dreaming or sometimes just very quiet — it bloomed from her fingertips now without asking, fern-patterns on the stone, feather-patterns on the glass. She had stopped apologizing for it. That was new. That was Maren's doing.

Maren sat below her on the stair, three steps down so their heads would be level when Sera looked back, which Maren knew Sera would do, because Maren knew everything about where people put their eyes even though she could not see any of it.

"You're chewing your braid again," Maren said.

"I'm not."

"Salt and spit, child. I can smell it." A pause. "And you've been sitting there breathing like someone carrying a stone upstairs who keeps hoping the stairs will end."

Sera let go of her braid. Below, the tide came in over the drowned road, filling the wheel-ruts, erasing the way they'd come.

"The nightmares are worse here," she said.

"Because the sea is close," Maren said. "Drowning bells dream loudest near their own water."

That was what Maren called them — drowning bells — the dreams that had been leaking frost across the floorboards of the guildhouse before Tessaly moved her. In them Sera stood in a flooded chapel with water rising past her ribs, past her chin, and all around her hung bells made of silence, and they tolled without sound, and every toll took something out of her chest and gave something back that wasn't hers. She woke speaking. Not her voice. An older one, vast and patient and terribly sad, saying things in the First Tongue that made the candle flames lie flat and made Maren's face go white around the mouth even though Maren's face couldn't go white anywhere else.

"Maren." Her own voice came out small. "I have to tell you something. About how I learned it. The Tongue."

Maren turned her face up toward her. Blind eyes were strange to look into — they didn't look *at* you, they looked through you at some farther country — but right now they seemed to look exactly at her, and kindly, and waiting.

"I'm listening," Maren said. "I'll listen until the sea comes up these stairs if that's how long you need."

* * *

It began, Sera said, with a house that smelled of tallow and copper.

She had been born — if Odo's arithmetic was right, and he'd counted on his fingers twice — in a river town whose name she had never learned, because names were the first thing they took. She remembered only fragments before the house: her mother's hands smelling of fish-scale and lye, a song sung off-key on purpose to make her laugh, the particular grey of a northern dawn. Then fever, then carts, then the house.

The men in the house wore grey robes with no sigils, because sigils could be traced, and they spoke softly, always softly, the way people speak in sickrooms and slaughterhouses. They called themselves teachers. There were other children there, eleven or twelve of them, in a hall with no windows, and the lessons were simple and terrible: words written on slate, words pronounced syllable by syllable, and after each word, a test.

Not a recitation. A demonstration.

The word for *open*, spoken correctly, would unlatch any lock in the building. The word for *burn* would light the tallow candles without flame touching wick. The word for *obey* —

Sera stopped. On the window beside her, the frost had begun to spread without her permission, crawling out from under her palm in black-edged feathers. She watched it happen the way you watch blood leave your own body: from somewhere outside yourself, with a strange clinical horror.

Maren reached back without looking and laid her hand on Sera's ankle. Warm. Human. Anchored.

"Keep going," Maren said gently. "Or stop, and we sit here instead. Both are allowed."

"They kept a boy called Wren," Sera said. "He was older than me. He could say the word for *still* — the one that stops a heart, if you say it at a person instead of at the air. They were very proud of Wren. He got a blanket. We slept on straw and he slept on a blanket, and I used to think, when I grow up I will learn the word for *still* and I will get a blanket too." Her laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I was six. I wanted a blanket."

The frost feathers reached the edge of the pane. Beyond the glass, the sea went on grinding the shore, indifferent, ancient, the oldest sound in the world.

"One night Wren said the word for *still* at himself," she went on, quieter. "They told us he'd been ill. But I saw the mat they carried him out on, and there was frost on the mat, in the shape of a hand, pressed flat, like he'd held on to something invisible while it happened. Like he'd been holding the word back with both hands and it went anyway." She swallowed. "That was the day I understood the lessons weren't teaching us the Tongue. They were *harvesting* us. You don't teach a field to grow wheat because you love wheat."

Maren's hand tightened on her ankle. She said nothing. She had been an archivist for thirty years; she knew when the next line of a text had to be read in silence.

"There was a night when the soft-voiced men argued in the passage," Sera said. "One of them said the guildmasters in the city had gotten suspicious, that the crown was asking about shipments of children, that they should move the school north before spring. The other said no — first the *extraction*. First they would take the Tongue out of the oldest children, the ones who'd mastered whole sentences, and bottle it, bind it, whatever they did — I heard the word *vessels* and I heard the word *empty* and I understood both." Her fingers found her own throat without meaning to. Rested there. "Wren had been empty for two days. Empty children don't wake up. They breathe and blink and follow instructions, and their eyes are like the eyes of the fish my mother sold, and I decided I would rather be dead than emptied, and I was seven years old and I had no plan except a drain."

She laughed again, that broken laugh. "A drain. In the washroom floor. Big enough for a cat, maybe. I am not a cat. I got stuck. And while I was stuck, half-in and half-out, in the dark under the floor, listening to them search the halls, I did the only thing I could think of. I took the Tongue — all of it, every word they'd ever put in me, four years of lessons — and I did what they were going to do to me. I did it to myself first."

The wind found the broken lantern room above and moaned down the stairwell like a mourner walking a corridor.

"How?" Maren whispered. It was not an archivist's question. It was a human one.

"I don't know the word for it," Sera said. "Isn't that funny? I know words for *open* and *burn* and *bind* and *unmake*, and I don't know the word for what I did. I think — I think I told the language itself to *hide*. Inside me. Where they cut. Because they cut, Maren. The extraction isn't gentle. Wren — before — he showed me the scar at the base of his skull and said it felt like being unpicked stitch by stitch, and that the worst part was that you stayed awake for the last of it, because the last stitches are the ones holding your name." Her hand was still at her throat. She forced it down. "So I hid the whole tongue under my own name, underneath everything, folded small, the way you fold a stolen letter into your boot. And I crawled out the drain, and I ran, and I have been running for two winters, and every place I stopped, within a month, someone came asking about a girl. Because they can't harvest what I carry unless they find me. And I cannot give it back unless I empty myself. And so I ran, and I stole, and I bit a man's thumb to the bone once, and I trusted nobody, until—"

She stopped. Looked down the stairwell, at the blind woman sitting three steps below her in the salt-grey light, patient as stone, warm as bread.

"Until a debtor offered me to a mercenary like a sack of flour," Sera said, "and the mercenary said *no*, and I followed him home through the slush like a dog because he was the first person who ever refused to buy me."

The frost on the window had stopped growing. It hung there, feathered and still, beautiful and terrible, and Sera realized her cheeks were wet and she hadn't noticed herself crying, and that was its own kind of frightening — that her body could do things, speak things, leak things, without consulting her.

"I never told anyone," she said. "Not the truth. In the river towns I said I was apprenticed to a dead singer. Here I said nothing at all. Because if I say it out loud, it's real, and if it's real, then everyone in this lighthouse is in the same danger I am, and I have been *so tired*, Maren, I have been so tired of being the only person who knows what is sleeping under my skin—"

"Sera." Maren's voice was low and even, the voice she used on frightened apprentices and, apparently, on frightened ex-archivists of the impossible. "Come down here. Sit where I can reach you properly. If I'm going to hold you together, I'd rather not do it by the ankle."

Sera came down. Sat. Was gathered in — Maren's arms were thin but they closed around her with the certainty of a lock accepting the only key — and she cried the way she hadn't cried since the house, since before the house, ugly and loud and graceless, into wool that smelled of tea and ink and cold air.

When she could speak again, Maren said: "Do you know what I did, the day I dropped my teacup?"

"Heard the Tongue."

"I heard the Tongue," Maren agreed. "But do you know why I dropped the cup? Not surprise. Before the Sundering, the archives taught that the First Speech could not be *learned* the way you learn trade-talk — it had to be given, bound into the speaker, and the binding left marks. I dropped the cup because your accent was perfect. Four thousand years dead, and your accent was *perfect*, and no living teacher alive could have produced that. Only a source. Only the well itself." She smoothed Sera's hair back from her forehead with a hand that trembled slightly and did not withdraw. "I have suspected since the first week that you didn't learn this language. I have been waiting for you to tell me, because a secret offered is a different thing than a secret taken, and you have had enough taken."

Sera pulled back enough to see her face. "You knew? And you let me — you taught me the grounding games, you sat with me through the nightmares — knowing what I might be?"

"I knew you were a child," Maren said, "carrying a weight that has crushed empires, and doing it standing up. Knowing changed nothing except that I stopped sleeping well." A dry smile. "I never slept well anyway. Ask anyone."

Then the smile faded, and the archivist surfaced in her, the woman who had spent her life among dangerous texts, and she took both of Sera's hands in hers and held them up between them, palms open, the way you'd hold a book you were afraid to open and more afraid not to.

"Now listen to me, Sera-of-no-town, keeper of the hidden tongue. What those men built, they built to take. What you did in the dark under that floor, you built to *keep*. Do you understand the difference? They wanted to pour the god-language out of children into vessels and weapons. You told the language to hide — and languages are older and cleverer than men, and I think it chose to hide in you, I think it *agreed* to hide in you, because you asked it as a refuge asks and not as a thief asks." Her grip tightened. "Which means the word sleeping under your skin is not a wound, child. It is a covenant. And covenants can be renegotiated. Remember that. Whatever they tell you at the drowned shrine, whatever Odo believes, whatever the cabal bleeds for — a thing that *agreed* to hide can also agree to change."

Sera looked at her hands in Maren's hands. Small hands, chapped, bitten nails, a scar across the left knuckle from a knife she'd stolen and used badly. Ordinary hands. Hands that held a language older than kingdoms, folded small, waiting.

"I'm tired of hiding it," she said slowly, and heard her own voice steady as she said it, steadier than it had been in two winters. "I'm tired of it deciding things while I sleep. If it's a covenant — if it agreed — then I want to be the one who speaks for us. Both of us. I want—" she searched for the word and found, of all things, a guild word, Edmund's word, "—I want to *enter it in the ledger*. My terms. My hand."

Maren laughed — a short, startled, delighted sound — and kissed her forehead. "You sound like him already."

Above them, in the broken lantern room, the wind changed. Sera felt it before she heard it: a pressure shift, a wrongness, the particular hush that had preceded every bad arrival of her life. The frost on the window flared white, every feather brightening at once, and far out on the pewter sea, where the horizon met the falling snow, she saw it — a light where no light should be. Not a fishing boat's lamp. A chain of lights. Moving along the coast road.

Toward the lighthouse.

Toward the drowned shrine beyond it.

Toward Odo, who had slipped away in the night, taking his certainty and his sermon and, God forgive them all, the girl's location, copied from Tessaly's charts while the rest of them slept.

"Maren," Sera said, rising, the First Tongue rising with her, unbidden, humming under her skin like a struck bell. "Someone's come for the word. And I think Odo invited them."
