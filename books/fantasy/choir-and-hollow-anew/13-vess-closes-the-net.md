## 13. Vess Closes the Net

The snow came in the night like a debt collector — quietly at first, then all at once.

Edmund knew it before he opened his eyes. The silence of snowfall has a texture all its own, a hush that presses against the windows and swallows the usual sounds of the guildhouse: the cart-wheels on the cobbles below, the coughing of the night clerk, the groan of the old building settling its bones against the cold. None of it came. Only the soft, ceaseless whisper of flakes finding the world, and beneath it, faint as a pulse, the memory of the shrine candle guttering in its draft.

He had lit it again last night. He always did. A candle for a god who had never once answered, not in thirty years of oaths and offerings and the slow erosion of everything Edmund had been willing to give. He lit it anyway, because a man who kept accounts understood that some debts were not meant to be collected — only acknowledged, nightly, with a small flame and a bowed head.

Sera's door was closed when he passed it. From behind it came no sound at all, which was either sleep or listening, and with Sera it was impossible to say which. Maren had taken to leaving a plate of honeyed bread outside her room each morning, arranged with the fussy precision of a woman who had spent forty years cataloguing things she could not touch. This morning the plate was already empty when Edmund went down, the bread gone, a single crumb left dead center on the ceramic like a signature.

*She is still here,* he told himself. *Tessaly moved her through the undercity four nights ago. She is safe in the lighthouse with Maren, and Vess's agents swept the guildhouse twice and found nothing but ledgers and lies.*

Both of those were true. Both of those were his own work.

He settled into the counting room with the ledger open before him and did what he had done every morning for twenty years: he counted. Barley in the third storehouse, salted pork in barrels whose staves he knew by their knots, lamp oil measured down to the finger-width. The numbers behaved themselves. Numbers always did. It was people who wrote themselves into columns and then refused to stay there — Corvane with his neat treason, Tessaly with her three favors spent, Sera with her page that read *carried debt* in his own hand and meant, in truth, something no ledger had ever held.

Outside, the snow kept falling. Somewhere in it, he was almost certain, men were walking.

* * *

They came at the ninth bell, and they did not knock.

Edmund heard them first as a wrongness in the courtyard — boots too many, moving too evenly, the particular discipline of soldiers trying to be quiet and failing at scale. Then the front doors opened without complaint, which told him everything: someone inside had unlocked them. Someone inside had been waiting for this morning as patiently as Edmund waited for quarterly inventories.

Inquisitor Halvard Vess entered the counting room the way winter enters a house — through every gap at once, courteous and total. He had traded his traveling coat for one of grey wool trimmed in white fox, and the crown's seal hung at his breast on a chain so fine it seemed less worn than grown there. Behind him came six men in plain dark livery, and behind them, hands bound in silk rather than rope — a courtesy, or a message — came Guildmaster Aldous Corvane.

Corvane looked smaller than Edmund had ever seen him. Twenty-two years of running the Hollow Ledger Guild had built the man out of angles and authority, sharp as a folded document, and now all of it had gone soft at once, like wax held too near a flame. His eyes found Edmund's across the counting room and there was something in them Edmund had not expected. Not accusation. Not even fear.

*Gratitude.* That was the worst of it. Corvane looked at him with gratitude, as if Edmund's silence over the years had been a kindness and not simply the arithmetic of a man who could not afford enemies above him.

"Master Vasquez." Vess removed one glove, finger by finger, and warmed his bare hand at the counting room's little stove as though he owned it. Perhaps, after today, he would. "I hope you'll forgive the hour. Snow makes me impatient. Everything arrives late or not at all, and I am a man who dislikes waiting for either."

"You've arrested my guildmaster," Edmund said. "On what charge?"

"Obstruction." Vess said it lightly, the way one names a weather condition. "The crown requested certain records. The records provided were—" he paused, savoring it, "—inaccurate."

"They're audited quarterly," Edmund said. "By me. They're accurate."

"Yes." Vess smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind a man practices in mirrors until it fits him like the coat. "That's what I told them. I said, whoever falsified these entries is either very clever or very loyal, and in my experience the two are rarely separable." He drew off the second glove. "Shall we read together? I do love being read to."

What followed took an hour, and Edmund endured it standing, because sitting would have meant leaning on something, and he would not lean while they watched.

Vess read aloud. That was the cruelty of it — he read *well*, in a clear pleasant voice, turning pages with fingers that never smudged the ink, and he read the falsifications exactly as Edmund had written them. Sera's entry appeared nowhere. Her name had been struck from every list, her registration dissolved into a fictional transfer of goods, a consignment of northern wool that had never existed, routed through a factor who had died two winters past and could not object. Edmund had erased her the way you erase anything properly: by burying her in true things.

And Vess read each buried lie aloud with the relish of a man unwrapping gifts.

"Wool, forty bales, consigned to Master Hettick — deceased, I believe, Master Vasquez? How industrious of him, shipping from the grave." Page-turn. "Barley, twelve sacks, transferred to the coastal annex — which, curiously, has no coastal annex." Page-turn. "One entry in your hand, undated, reading simply *carried*, with no debtor named. How poetic. Ledgers are not meant for poetry, Master Vasquez. They are meant for truth. That is their whole beauty — that unlike people, unlike kingdoms, unlike gods—" and here his voice did something almost gentle, "—they cannot lie."

"They can," Edmund said. "I've spent my life proving it."

"I know," said Vess, and the smile widened by a single tooth. "It's why I'm here."

* * *

They emptied the guildhouse the way water empties a hull — systematically, from the bottom up. The clerks were marched into the great hall and made to sit in rows while gray-liveried men went shelf by shelf, chest by chest, carrying out not silver or deeds but *paper*. Contracts. Bonds. Thirty years of the guild's quiet architecture, stacked into crates and carried into the snow.

Edmund watched a young clerk named Peth weep silently over a strongbox of family sureties — farmers' collateral, widows' pensions, the small desperate paper lives of people who had trusted the Hollow Ledger to hold their weight. Every one of those documents would now be read by the crown. Every debt, every secret, every shameful little arrangement that kept the poor fed and the powerful patient, laid open on an inquisitor's table.

*You did this,* said a voice in him that sounded like Odo, ragged and righteous. *You stayed to falsify the books instead of going with her. You chose paper over people, one last time, and the paper burned everyone.*

*And if I'd gone with her,* answered the older voice, the ledger-voice, the one that had kept him alive through wars and famines and the long silence of his god, *they would have torn the city apart looking for her, and found her, because I was beside her. The math does not forgive. The math only balances.*

But the math had a new term in it now, and the term was Peth's face, wet in the firelight, and the math could not carry it.

Toward midday, Vess found him in the counting room, which had become an island — the one room the soldiers had not stripped, as if by some unspoken agreement to leave the heart intact until the very end. Vess stood in the doorway with snow melting on his shoulders, and for a moment neither spoke. The stove ticked. Outside, the light was doing that terrible northern thing where the snow made the afternoon brighter than noon, everything washed white and merciless.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Master Vasquez?" Vess said at last. He came in and sat on the corner of the counting desk itself, an informality so precise it was clearly rehearsed. "Most men who hide something valuable grow careless in one direction or another. They guard the thing and neglect the perimeter, or they guard the perimeter and grow careless with the thing. You did neither. You hid a child inside an institution's blind spot and then you blinded the institution. Textbook. I have trained inquisitors for eleven years and I could not have taught it better."

"She was a debtor's daughter given in payment," Edmund said. "I refused the trade. She followed me home. I registered her per guild law. After that she left the city. That is the whole of it, and it's all in whatever papers you haven't burned yet."

"Mm." Vess examined his bare hands, turning them palm up, palm down, as though checking them for ink. "And the First Tongue? Is that in the papers as well?"

The cold that went through Edmund then had nothing to do with the season. He kept his face still — twenty years of negotiating with creditors had taught him that — but inside, something large and load-bearing shifted, and he heard the crack.

"I don't know the phrase."

"No," Vess agreed pleasantly. "Almost no one does. That's rather the point. But you keep a shrine, Master Vasquez. A candle, nightly, to a god with no name in any living rite. Blind archivist Maren — oh, don't look surprised, we've watched her for years; a woman who can read dead languages is a national resource — Maren identified the language your little ward speaks within a teacup's fall of hearing it. And a defrocked priest named Odo has been asking after drowned shrines from here to the coast, asking after *you*." He slid off the desk and straightened his coat. "You are not a man hiding a stray, Master Vasquez. You are the last account-holder of a closed divine bank, and I have come to collect its assets."

"And if I tell you nothing?"

"Then nothing happens to you at all." Vess said it with such sincerity that it took a moment to arrive as a threat. "You misunderstand the shape of this. I don't need your confession. I need only your presence. A man who will burn his life's work to protect a child — that man is worth more than any testimony. Where you go, the others gather. You are not the net, Master Vasquez. You are the bait."

He paused at the door, and put his gloves back on, one finger at a time, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world because the world was already his.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "the falsifications were beautiful. Whoever taught you should be proud." A beat. "Whoever taught you *is* proud. We keep track of our graduates."

Then the soldiers came for Edmund, and bound his hands in silk rather than rope — a courtesy, or a message — and walked him out into the falling snow, past the empty shelves, past Peth's red eyes, past the counting room where the stove still ticked to itself like a heart that hadn't yet been told.

At the door he stopped, one last indulgence, and looked back at the shrine alcove where his candle had burned every night for thirty years. It was out. Of course it was out. Someone had pinched it between thumb and forefinger, tidy as bookkeeping.

But the wick was not cold. When Edmund passed close enough to feel it, the smoke rose straight up through the frozen air, thin and black and patient, like a line of ink drawn upward toward a hand that might still be waiting to write.

They took him south through the snow, toward the cells under the counting house, and the last thing he saw of the guildhouse was its roofline dissolving into white — everything he had built, everything he had betrayed, everything he had tried to save, going out one flake at a time.

Somewhere north, in a lighthouse full of salt and lamplight, a girl was dreaming. He prayed — oathless, godless, out of pure habit — that she was dreaming of anything else.
