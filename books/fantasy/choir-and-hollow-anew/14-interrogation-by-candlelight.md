## 14. Interrogation by Candlelight

They took his boots first.

Edmund noticed it the way he noticed everything about a room — inventory before fear. Boots, belt, the iron stylus he'd carried for nineteen years, the little brass weights from his coat pockets that had no business being comforting and were anyway. A man without boots was a man who could not run, and Vess wanted him to understand that this was not an accident of procedure but a sentence, pronounced in advance.

The cell they gave him was not a cell. That was the first lie, and Edmund had spent enough years among ledgers to know that every lie has a shape, and the shape of this one was *comfort*. A chair with arms. A table of real walnut, not guild pine. A candle of white wax, beeswax by the smell, burning with the steady, expensive calm of a flame that had never once guttered for want of tallow. Somewhere below, water moved through the counting house's old veins — the undercity cisterns, the flooded vaults Tessaly knew like her own scars — and the sound came up through the floor like breathing.

He sat. He did not touch the chair's arms, because a man who grips his chair is a man who expects to be shaken.

The door opened without sound. Halvard Vess entered the way winter enters a valley: no drama, no announcement, just a slow change in the quality of the air. His gray coat was immaculate. His hands were bare, and warm-looking, and folded loosely at his back as though they had never gripped anything harder than a pen.

"Master Vasquez." He said it like a man greeting a colleague across a negotiating table, which, Edmund supposed, was exactly what this was. "I apologize for the accommodations. The guildhouse is in some disorder tonight."

"You arrested its master."

"I arrested a man who obstructed a crown inquiry." Vess drew out the chair opposite and sat, unhurried, arranging his coat with two small precise gestures. "Corvane will be treated according to his station. Which is to say, poorly, but with paperwork."

"And the ledgers you read aloud on the stairs."

Vess smiled. It was a good smile. It was built to be a good smile, the way a fishhook is built to look like food. "Ah yes. Your arithmetic. You erased the girl very thoroughly, Master Vasquez. Three days of entries, expunged and rewritten in your own hand — and I'll say, the forgery of yourself is exquisite. You even got your own fatigue into it. The slight tremor in the downstrokes after midnight. Very human. Very convincing."

Edmund said nothing. In the guild they taught you that silence was a number too — zero, the most powerful figure in any column, the one that made all others reveal their sums.

"But here is my difficulty," Vess went on, leaning forward, candlelight pooling in the hollows of his face. "A ledger that lies tells me more than a ledger that tells the truth. Because a man only falsifies accounts around one thing: the thing he cannot afford to lose." He let that sit between them like a coin on a table. "So I don't need you to confess, Edmund. The confession is already written, in erasures. What I need is the girl."

* * *

The candle burned. Down in the walls, the water breathed.

"Let us speak plainly," Vess said, "because I have read your file, and you are a plain man wearing a complicated trade. You were bound at nineteen. Sworn to the Quiet God — forgive me, we both know He has no name left to be quiet about. Nineteen years of service. Oath-kept, tithe-paid, shrine-candle lit every night of your life even after the prayers stopped answering. You are the last functioning vow in a dead religion, and you have kept it the way other men keep a grave." He tilted his head. "That is not criticism. I find it admirable. Loyalty without hope is the rarest currency there is, and I am, above all things, a collector of rare currencies."

"What do you want."

"The word." No coyness now. The smile was gone, and what remained underneath was worse — hunger, old and disciplined, like a furnace banked so long it had learned patience. "The silencing word. The one that unmade Him. The one the child carries in her blood and bone and breath."

"She doesn't carry any such thing."

"Master Vasquez." Vess's voice softened into something almost tender. "I have been hunting the First Speech for eleven years. I have dug through the Sundering archives in Vael, through the drowned scriptoriums off the Salt Coast, through the memories of three men so old they remembered when remembering was still safe. And four months ago, in a fishing village called Greyhollow, men in my employ found a woman who had escaped a place where children were taught to swallow language whole. She told us — before she died of what they had done to her — that one child got out. One child who didn't learn the Tongue." His eyes were very steady. "She *ate* it."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"So," Vess said, folding his hands on the walnut table. "Here is the bargain. The crown — or rather, the office I represent, which you may think of as the crown's long arm — offers full amnesty for the Hollow Ledger Guild. Every debt forgiven. Every falsified entry absolved. Corvane released. Your people walk free of this winter untouched. In exchange: the girl, delivered alive and undamaged, into my custody. You have my oath on it."

"Your oath."

"Yes."

"I've seen what your oaths are worth. I watched you arrest a man on a stairway while smiling at him."

"Corvane sold secrets to the crown for six years," Vess said mildly. "I simply stopped pretending not to know. Consider it a professional courtesy — I let him believe he was valuable right up until he wasn't. You, at least, I respect enough to threaten honestly."

Edmund looked at the candle. Beeswax, white, steady. Nineteen years he had lit a stub of tallow before a shrine whose god never answered, and here was a man with a perfect flame and a voice like oiled hinges offering him everything, and the price was a child who had once, in the slush behind a debtor's door, looked up at him with eyes like a frozen river — surface intact, depths moving.

"You're not the crown," Edmund said.

It was not a question, and Vess knew it was not a question, and for a moment — a single, hairline crack — the inquisitor's composure shifted, the way a ledger shifts when you find the entry that doesn't balance.

"Excuse me?"

"The crown wants unregistered persons registered. The crown wants taxes and order and its name spoken correctly in provincial churches. The crown does not want a word of power." Edmund leaned forward now, slowly, matching the man's earlier posture, giving him back his own weight. "I've done collections for nineteen years, Vess. I know what a creditor looks like. And I know what a *principal* looks like. You're not collecting for the crown. You're collecting for someone the crown has never heard of. Someone who taught you that hunger you're hiding behind your teeth."

Silence. The water breathed below. And then Halvard Vess laughed — quietly, genuinely, a short exhalation of surprise that seemed to cost him something.

"Well," he said. "Maren always said you were sharper than your paperwork."

The name landed in Edmund's chest like a dropped stone. "You know Maren."

"I *trained* Maren." Vess's face did something complicated and brief — grief, or the memory of grief, worn smooth by handling. "Thirty years ago, in the archive halls of the Chancellery of Sacred Accounts. Before she blinded herself reading texts that were never meant for living eyes. Before she ran away to hide among money-counters." He shook his head slowly. "She was the best of us. Which is why she fled. The rest of us stayed, and staying changes what you serve."

"Who do you serve?"

And here Vess leaned back, and the candlelight caught him fully, and Edmund saw — really saw, the way he saw a forged signature, the way he saw the tremor in a debtor's hands — the truth of the man. Not cruelty. Conviction. The most dangerous substance in the world.

"There was a time," Vess said, "when gods walked openly, and mortals spoke to them in the First Tongue, and the world burned every third generation because gods are children with infinite hands. The Sundering ended that. Or it was meant to. But the ones who ended it — the binders, the silencers, the men and women who wrote the words that put the old gods to sleep — they understood something the priests never did. That divinity is not worship. Divinity is *leverage*. A god who can be commanded is a tool. A god who can be silenced is a vault." His voice dropped, reverent as any priest's. "There is a circle of us, Edmund. Old families. Old vows. We have spent centuries gathering the scattered fragments of the First Speech, and we are one word short of complete. One word. The word that silenced the Quiet God Himself — the master-key, the proof that even the oldest powers kneel to a well-spoken syllable. And a feral child in a fishing village swallowed it whole."

"She's not a vault."

"Everything is a vault. Some are merely locked badly." Vess spread his hands, reasonable, almost kind. "We don't wish her harm. Truly. Once the word is extracted — and extraction is a refined art, we have methods that leave the vessel breathing — she will be compensated. Educated. Kept. She would want for nothing."

"She's nine."

"She's an heirloom."

The word hung in the air, and Edmund felt something in himself go very quiet and very cold, the way a ledger goes quiet before the final sum. He thought of Sera asleep on Maren's floor with frost blooming on the window glass in shapes like bells. He thought of her singing a fragment of the First Speech and the shrine candle flaring like a struck match. He thought of her small hand gripping his sleeve in the slush, the day he refused to buy her and she followed him home anyway, because even then, even feral and wordless in the common tongue, she had known how to choose her people.

"Nineteen years ago," Edmund said, "I swore an oath to a silent god. Kneeling in a shrine that smelled of tallow and river water. And the god never answered — not once, not ever — and I kept the oath anyway, because an oath isn't a transaction, it's a spine." He stood. Without boots he was shorter than Vess expected, and he watched the man recalibrate, and he did not care. "You just offered me amnesty in exchange for a child, and you called her an heirloom, and you think the problem is that I'll refuse. The problem isn't refusal, Vess. Refusal is easy. I refused Corvane for six years. The problem is that you've shown me your ledger. And now I know your principals, your assets, your methods, and your one missing line. And I am going to spend the rest of my life closing your account."

Vess regarded him for a long moment. Then he sighed, and rose, and buttoned his gray coat with those warm bare hands, and Edmund saw that the disappointment on his face was genuine, and that beneath the disappointment was something patient and geological.

"I hoped you'd be sensible," Vess said. "Sensible men are so much easier to bury respectfully." He paused at the door. "For what it's worth — and it is worth very little — Maren loved you all. She wrote to me once, years ago, and asked me to leave the guild alone. I did, for a while. Sentiment." He opened the door onto torchlight and the sound of boots. "But sentiment is a debt, Edmund, and debts come due. Yours comes due at dawn. Think about the girl's face when they drag her out of whatever hole Tessaly Rook has hidden her in — because we will find her, we always find her — and ask yourself whether your spine was worth her ribs."

The door closed. The candle burned on, white and steady and expensive.

Edmund sat alone in the comfortable room that was not a cell, and he did the only thing left to a man with no boots, no stylus, and no allies inside these walls.

He counted.

The guards changed at intervals — he timed them by the candle, four hundred breaths, then a rotation, two sets of footsteps, one bored, one not. The water in the walls breathed on a rhythm too, swelling faintly at the turn of each hour, and he marked it, and marked it again, because Tessaly had once told him, drunk on stolen brandy in the undercroft, that the cisterns sang differently near the flooded vaults where the old guild had sealed its forbidden accounts — and that if you pressed your ear to the right stone at the right hour, you could hear which walls were hollow.

At the turn of the second hour, the water swelled, and Edmund pressed his palm flat against the floor, and beneath the breathing of the cisterns he heard it: a knock. Three, then two, then three. The guild's oldest signal, older than Corvane, older than the ledgers. *Counting continues. We are coming.*

Tessaly. Bleeding, half-broken, hunted — and already moving.

Edmund closed his eyes and let the cold rise through his palm, and for the first time since the stairs, since the reading of the false ledgers, since the snap of irons, he smiled.

"Dawn," he murmured to the empty room, to the perfect candle, to the man who served vaults and called them gods. "Keep the light burning. I intend to be rude to you in person."

Above, far north beyond the sealed passes, snow was falling on a lighthouse where a girl slept with a word beneath her skin. Below, in the drowned dark, the old guild's forgotten vaults waited, hollow and uncounted.

And somewhere between the two, the family that wasn't on paper began, in whispers and lamplight, to break him out.
