## 17. Corvane's Last Entry

The counting house smelled of ash and old ink when Edmund came back to it, and both smells were lies.

The ash was from the burned ledgers — his own handiwork, three nights of feeding the guild's true books into the grate while Tessaly moved Sera through the drowned dark of the undercity. The ink was Corvane's, and it was fresh. That was the lie: that nothing had changed, that the Guildmaster still sat in his high-backed chair with his silver pen-knife and his ledger-law, that the world still ran on entries and balances and debts paid in full.

Edmund stood in the doorway a long moment before entering. Habit. A collector's habit — you never crossed a debtor's threshold until you'd read what the threshold could tell you. The snow on the steps was scuffed in two directions, boot-prints going out and coming back, and the coming-back prints were heavier than they should have been, as though the man who made them had been carrying something. Or being carried by something.

Inside, every candle was lit.

Corvane never wasted candles. In twenty years of service under him, Edmund had watched the man work by daylight alone, winter or no, because light was an expense and expenses were sins. Now the whole long room burned yellow, twenty tallow stubbles guttering in their iron dishes, and the shadows of the shelved ledgers swayed on the walls like reeds in current.

Corvane sat at the great desk with his back half-turned, writing.

"You're late," he said, without looking up. His voice was steady. His hand, Edmund noticed, was not; the pen scratched and stuttered, scratched and stuttered, like a bird with a broken wing trying to fly anyway. "I've been keeping your accounts myself. Do you know how much you cost this guild, Vasquez? A smuggler's favor. An archivist's silence. A girl who is worth more than everything I have ever written down."

"Guildmaster." Edmund closed the door behind him. Softly. He'd learned long ago that loud doors announced intentions, and he had none yet — only a cold knot in his stomach that had been there since Tessaly's runner brought word at dawn: *the Guildmaster's been asking after the north road. Asking after passes. Asking after lighthouses.*

Only four people alive knew about the lighthouse.

"The guild is seized," Edmund said. "Vess holds it. Your seal means nothing now."

"My seal." Corvane laughed — a small, dry sound, like paper tearing. He set down the pen at last and turned, and Edmund saw him properly for the first time and had to fight not to show it. Twenty years of careful, gray, immaculate Corvane, and now: coat unbuttoned, collar open, eyes rimmed red as raw meat, cheeks hollowed as if something inside him had been eating its way out for days. "My seal bought me a cell, Vasquez. Did you know that? Vess read my ledgers aloud to me. Aloud. Every entry. Every name. He has such a pleasant voice for it."

"He knows they're false."

"He knows *I* know they're false, which is worse." Corvane rose, and one hand went into his coat pocket, and stayed there, and Edmund marked it without marking it. "He offered me amnesty too. Did he offer you? Sera for the guild's forgiveness. A simple trade. One child against forty lives."

"I don't trade children."

"No." Corvane smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room, worse than the candles, worse than the shaking hand. "No, you burn thirty years of bookkeeping instead. You give away my favors. You make me a ruin, Edmund, and then you walk north with your little found family singing songs in dead languages, and you think the story ends there."

*He knows,* Edmund thought. *He knows about the north. About the lighthouse.* And beneath the thought, colder: *who told him.* But he already knew the answer, and the answer was standing in front of him, breathing candle-smoke.

"Who did you write to?" Edmund asked quietly.

* * *

Corvane didn't answer at once. He walked instead along the shelf-wall, trailing one finger over the spines of the ledgers — the false ones now, the ones Vess had read aloud, filled with doctored sums and erased names. Sera's name wasn't in any of them anymore. Edmund had cut her out with a razor blade and a steady hand, page by page, until she existed nowhere but in flesh and memory. He had thought that would be enough. He had thought paper was the only place a person could be hunted from.

"There was a man," Corvane said. "Came to me eight days ago. Before Vess took the house. Gray cloak, gray gloves, face like a coin left too long in a fire — melted smooth. He didn't threaten me. You understand? He didn't have to. He simply opened a ledger on my desk. My ledger. The real one, the one I kept in the floor safe, the one recording every crown I ever passed to the crown's men — and he began to read it. Backward. From the last entry to the first. And as he read, Edmund—"

Corvane's finger stopped on a spine.

"—my hands remembered every signature. I could feel them. All twenty years of them, crawling up through my arms like ants made of ice. When he stopped reading, they stopped. He said—" and here Corvane's voice did something Edmund had never heard it do; it cracked, "—he said, *the cabal collects all debts, Guildmaster. Even yours.*"

The word *cabal* dropped into the room like a stone into well-water. Edmund had heard it only once before, from Vess's own mouth, in the candlelit interrogation cell, spoken with the offhand contempt of a man naming a thing he served. God-binders. The ones who wanted the silencing word pulled out of a child and set into a weapon.

"And so you sold her," Edmund said.

"I *tried* to buy my hands back!" It burst out of him, ugly and loud, and the candles all leaned sideways at once though there was no draft. "You don't know what it is, Vasquez, to feel your own body turned against you, to be a debt walking around wearing your skin! I gave them what they asked. A location. A description. The blind woman, the smuggler, the route north. It's done. It was done before Vess ever put you in chains, and if you'd given the girl up when I first ordered—"

Edmund moved. Not fast — he hadn't been fast in ten years — but certain, the way a closing door is certain, and suddenly he was across the room with his forearm across Corvane's chest and the Guildmaster's back flat against the shelf-wall, ledgers trembling above them like a roost of startled birds.

"Where did you tell them she'd be."

"The drowned coast." Corvane's breath came shallow against Edmund's arm. For all his ruin, he didn't struggle. Perhaps he'd already spent the strength for struggling. "The shrine. She'll go to the shrine, won't she? They all go to the shrine eventually. The priest will take her. Priests always do." His eyes, up close, were terrible — not cruel, which Edmund could have understood, but *empty*, scraped hollow, a man's insides scooped out and replaced with arithmetic. "They promised me release, Edmund. They said if I gave them the girl, the man in gray would close my account. Burn my ledger. Let my hands be mine again."

"They lied."

"They always lie." Corvane laughed again, and this time there was nothing dry in it at all; it was wet, and close, and awful. "But you'd have taken the bargain. Don't look at me like that. Thirty years ago you'd have taken it. I made you, Vasquez. I taught you that everything is owed, everyone is owed, and the only sin is leaving a balance unsettled. Where do you think your ledger-law came from? I wrote it. I wrote it and then it wrote me, and now—"

His free hand came out of his pocket.

* * *

Edmund had seen desperate magic perhaps five times in his life, and four of those times it had killed someone other than the caster. Desperation was a poor teacher. It taught shortcuts, and shortcuts in the First Crafts — the small binding-arts the guild's collectors used for locks and lies and the occasional quieting of a witness — were how men lost fingers, voices, faces.

What Corvane held was a scrap of parchment, folded small, dense with writing so fine it looked like a hairline crack across the paper. A contract. Edmund recognized the form even upside-down: a self-binding, the kind guildmen swore on their ledgers, *my oath against my life*, except this one was written backward, inward, a debt collected rather than pledged. The gray-gloved man's parting gift, Edmund realized. Not a promise of release. A leash.

"What did you do?" he whispered.

"I called in my last favor," Corvane said. "From the only party that ever answered." And he began to speak the words on the scrap aloud.

Edmund hit him. Dropped the man, drove a knee onto his wrist, felt bones grind, snatched for the parchment — and it was gone, not torn but *unwritten*, dissolving between his fingers like frost in a warm palm, because that was how self-bindings worked, the words passed out of the paper and into the air and then into the blood, and once they were in the blood no one could take them back. Not the caster. Not anyone.

"Corvane. *Listen to me.* There are ways to break—"

"There are no ways." The Guildmaster lay on the floor among the fallen candles, wax pooling and hardening against his cheek, and he was smiling up at the ceiling with tears running into his ears. "That's the elegance of it. He told me. He said, *sign this and whatever you ask will be granted, and the price will be whatever remains.* I asked to be free of debt. All of it. Every entry, every balance, every name I ever wrote down." His voice was going strange now, resonant, doubled, as though something enormous spoke just behind his throat, using him as a mask uses a face. "Do you see, Vasquez? Do you finally see? If I am freed of every debt—"

The candles died. All twenty, at once, not blown out but *drunk*, their flames vanishing like water down a drain, and the darkness that replaced them was total and thick and hungry.

"—then I must be freed of everyone I owe. And everyone who owes *me*."

Edmund had sworn his oath to a silent god. He knew what it was to carry a bond in the marrow. So he felt it when the pulling started — a dragging sensation behind his sternum, gentle at first, like a hook testing line, like something reading the shape of what he was before deciding whether to keep him.

It stopped.

It stopped because Corvane, dying on the floor of his own counting house, reached out in the dark and caught Edmund's sleeve with the last of his ruined strength, and said, in his ordinary human voice, small and cracked and almost boyish:

"Not you. I wrote you out. Twenty years ago — there was an entry, the night you swore your oath, I entered you as *paid in full*, remember? You asked me to. You said you never wanted to belong to the books." A wet, bubbling laugh. "Best mistake I ever made, you ungrateful wretch. It's the only reason it can't touch you. Get out of here. Go north. Go be somebody's family, since you're so determined—"

The rest of it was not words.

Edmund held his hand anyway. It was what you did; it was the whole of what anyone could do. He held the hand of the man who had taught him that everything was owed and watched the debt collect itself, and Corvane's body arched and settled, arched and settled, like a bell being rung very slowly, and somewhere in the middle of it the gray-gloved man's contract finished its arithmetic, and what remained of Guildmaster Aldous Corvane was a lightness, an absence, a coat and bones and a faint smell of extinguished candles.

The relit dark lasted another minute. Then the candles came back one by one, apologetically, as though embarrassed by what they'd witnessed, and the room was only a room again.

* * *

Edmund did what he had always done. It was the only prayer he knew.

There was a ledger on the desk — Corvane's personal book, the daily one, its last entry half-finished in that stuttering hand. Edmund dipped the pen. His fingers shook. He noticed them shaking with a distant, scholarly interest, the way you notice weather in a country you're leaving, and he waited, and they did not stop, and so he wrote anyway.

*Final entry, the account of A. Corvane. Settled.*

He wrote the date. He wrote *cause: his own hand, his own terms*. He considered writing more — some verdict, some curse, some absolution — and found that he had no words that were true. Justice outside the law was supposed to feel clean. The ballads said so. Instead it tasted like the slush outside, gray and salted and going nowhere.

Underneath the entry, in the margin, where Corvane had taught him twenty years ago that marginalia was for weaklings and romantics, Edmund wrote one more line:

*He was my teacher. I am not his student anymore.*

Then he closed the book, and it was done, and he stood in the burning room with his shaking hand pressed flat against the cover as though taking a pulse.

Somewhere south of the city, past the frozen marshes, a drowned shrine waited under black water, and a ragged priest was leading a child toward it believing sacrifice was mercy, and a cabal of god-binders marched with melted faces and borrowed hands, and every hour he spent here was an hour they gained.

But he allowed himself sixty seconds. He owed the dead man that much, and — for the first time in his life — Edmund Vasquez decided some debts were worth keeping.

Then he took the ledger, tucked it inside his coat against his heart where the old oath used to sit, and went out into the falling snow, north, toward the storm, toward the water, toward whatever was left to save.
