## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The ewe was dying in the blue hour before dawn, and Wendell Marchetti knelt in the wet straw beside her because no one else would.

He had heard her crying since midnight — a thin, fraying sound that carried across the frozen paddocks the way grief carries, not loudly but insistently, finding every gap in a sleeping valley. The other ewes had moved away from her. They always did. Animals understood something about death that people preferred not to, and they gave it room, the way you'd give room to a fire.

Wendell did not give it room. He had pulled on his boots without lacing them, taken the jar of rendered tallow from the shelf by the door, and crossed his grandmother's frost-stiff garden with his breath smoking in front of him like a small ghost going ahead to announce him.

Now he crouched in the lambing shed, and the cold came up through the straw and into his knees, and the ewe looked at him with one amber eye rolled nearly white.

*Easy,* he said — but not with his mouth. Not exactly. It was a thing that had no good name, this speaking. His mouth made ordinary sounds: *there now, easy, old girl.* But underneath the words, beneath the human noise of them, he let out the other thing, the borrowed language, the grammar of breath and weight and warmth that sheep used among themselves when they thought no one was listening. He had been stealing it since he was six years old, word by word, from the flocks on the high pasture, from the geese arguing over the millpond, from the barn cats who spoke mostly in insults. He had never told anyone. There was no one to tell.

*I'm here,* he said in their tongue. *You're not alone.*

The ewe's breathing changed. It didn't slow, exactly. It became less like drowning.

She was old — seven years, maybe eight, which in a sheep is a long marriage to winter. Her fleece was yellowed at the flanks, and there was a hardness in her belly that Wendell had felt once with his palm and known immediately, the way you know a struck bell is cracked. Something inside her had turned wrong. No shepherd in Hollowvale could have done anything; no shepherd would have tried. A sick ewe was a ledger entry, and ledgers did not weep.

But Wendell knelt in the straw and put his hands on her — one at the hinge of her jaw, one over the hard place in her belly — and he listened.

This was the part no one knew about, the part even Sable only half believed when she saw it: that listening went both ways. When he opened himself to the beast-tongue, the world came pouring in. He felt the ewe's pain as a gray pressure behind his own ribs. He felt her confusion — not fear, exactly, but the deep animal outrage of a body failing its own instructions. And underneath all of it, faint as a psalm heard through a stone wall, he felt what she remembered: spring grass, the wet noses of lambs born three springs ago, the particular slant of light in this shed in April.

*That's real,* he told her. *All of that happened. I'll hold it for you.*

Outside, the sky was going from black to the color of a bruise healing. Frost furred the shed's single window. Somewhere up the valley, the first cock crowed, and the sound came back off the western cliffs in a long, thin echo, the way sounds did in Hollowvale, where the walls of the world were close enough to answer.

Hollowvale answered everything. That was the trouble with it.

Wendell had lived all nineteen years of his life inside the valley's cupped hands and had never once seen the top of the rim except in cloud. The road out existed — everyone knew where it went, the switchbacks up past the Wardstone and over the shoulder of the mountain to the low country — but nobody took it. Nobody needed to. The valley grew barley and turnips and wool enough. The valley kept its own weather, mild when the passes were buried, green when the lowlands burned. Grandmother said it was God's favor. The priest at the chapel said it was the wards, though he said the word *wards* the way you might say *the roof* — a fact of the house, not a miracle. Three generations since anything strange had happened inside Hollowvale. Three generations of prayers going up like smoke and coming back as nothing, which everyone agreed was proper and right. Magic was for the godless country beyond the rim. In here, a man's life was his own, plain and accounted for.

And in here, a boy who spoke to beasts would have been — Wendell had never finished the thought. He turned it over sometimes at night like a coin he couldn't spend. *Feared.* That was the honest word. Old Marta Corvi still told the story of her grandfather, two generations back, who claimed a wolf had walked beside him a whole day on the high pasture, and how the valley had looked at that man differently ever after, politely, the way you look at a door left open. He had died alone. Everyone said so quietly, as if it were a weather report.

So Wendell kept the coin in his pocket. He soothed calving cows in the dark and told no one. He untangled kestrels from the church eaves and told no one. He sat with dying animals because someone should, and if what he gave them was words, then the words were between him and God, and God, apparently, was not answering anyone these days anyway.

Except that the ewe answered.

*Warm,* she said, in the way sheep say things — not a word but a weight, a meaning shaped like warmth. *Cold going away. Small light staying.*

*I know,* Wendell said. *I've got you.*

And then, because he was nineteen and tired and the straw smelled of iron and milk, he said the true thing, the thing he had never said to any living creature:

*I wish somebody talked to me like this.*

The ewe blinked her amber eye slowly. Sheep cannot pity. That is one of their virtues. But something passed between them in the gray light — an acknowledgment, plain as bread — that they were both creatures keeping vigil, both waiting on a birth or a death neither of them had chosen.

Her breathing slowed. The gray pressure behind his ribs gentled, gathered, and went out like a lamp carried into another room. He stayed kneeling a long while after, his hands still on her cooling wool, because letting go first felt like a lie.

* * *

"You smell of the lambing shed," Sable said, "and you're late, and you have straw in your hair."

"Good morning to you too."

They were walking the millrace path with the morning's eggs in a basket between them, delivered to Sable's father's kitchen on Wendell's way to the forge, an arrangement so old neither of them could remember inventing it. Frost still clung to the north sides of the drystone walls. The race ran fast and brown with snowmelt, loud enough that they had to lean toward each other to talk, which was possibly why the arrangement had survived so long.

Sable Dune was the shepherd Brannic's daughter, which in Hollowvale meant she had been raised half-wild on the high pasture and half-caged in her father's kitchen, and had opinions about both. She was seventeen, quick-eyed, with a scar splitting one eyebrow where a ram had taught her humility at age nine. She walked like the ground owed her nothing.

"I asked what happened," she said. "The old ewe. The black-faced one."

Wendell watched the water instead of her. This was the ritual, the little liturgy of it: she asked, he deflected, she needled, he surrendered a piece. Neither of them named what the pieces added up to.

"She died," he said. "Peacefully. Before dawn."

"And you were with her."

"The gate was open."

"Wendell." She stopped walking. The basket swung between them. "The gate was *not* open. I checked it at moonset because the dog was barking at nothing, which he does now, every night, have you noticed? Every night this week. And it was shut. And this morning it was open, and there are knee-prints in the straw, and you come down the hill smelling of tallow and secrets."

He looked at her. Her eyes were the brown of turned earth, and they did not blink.

"They'd have left her," he said. "Alone, in the dark, crying. For hours."

"So you talk to them." It wasn't quite a question anymore. It hadn't been a question since the summer he'd calmed the stallion that had thrown the farrier, since the winter the crows brought him things — buttons, a brass buckle, once a silver thimble — and he'd sworn Sable to silence with a look rather than a word. "You actually talk to them. Not — whistle and guess. Talk."

"Sable—"

"No. I'm not saying it's wrong. I'm saying I want to hear it. Just once. What do they—" She glanced around, at the empty path, the loud water, the indifferent valley. "What do they say? About us. About here."

Wendell hesitated. Then, because it was Sable, and because the morning was cold and beautiful and the secret weighed what it had always weighed, he said: "Mostly they don't talk about us at all. We're weather to them. Walls and weather." He paused. "But lately—"

"Lately?"

"Lately the dog barks at nothing. Lately the geese won't settle on the millpond. Lately everything with four legs stands facing the same direction at dusk, and I can't get any of them to tell me why, because they say it's *humming*, Sable. They say the ground hums, up by the rim, under the Wardstone. Like a struck bell that won't finish."

The basket handle creaked in her grip.

"That's not funny," she said.

"It's not meant to be."

Above them, beyond the millroof, the western cliffs stood in the morning sun, and somewhere along their crown, invisible from the valley floor, the old wardstones ran in their broken necklace around the rim of Hollowvale. Wendell had climbed to the nearest one exactly once, at fourteen, and touched it, and felt nothing — a dead thing, a standing rock, moss and lichen and pigeon droppings. Grandmother had boxed his ears for a week over it. *The stones are not for touching,* she'd said, which was a strange thing to say about stones that did nothing.

"They say it's getting louder," Wendell said. "Whatever it is. The humming. The mule — you know the blind mule up at Tarn Farm, the ancient one? He told me last night. Well. He doesn't tell, exactly. He *remembers* at me. And what he remembers at me lately is a word I don't know. An old word. He keeps saying it like it's a name."

"What word?"

"*Vale-hollow.* Or maybe hollow-vale. Like the valley's name, but backwards. Inside out." Wendell shrugged, uncomfortable suddenly, the way you get when a dream follows you into daylight. "Probably just an old mule's grumbling."

Sable wasn't laughing. She was looking up at the cliffs, at the invisible stones, and her hand had gone to the scar on her brow the way it did when she was thinking hard.

"My aunt wrote again," she said, too casually. "From outside. Maren. Father burned the letter, but I read the corner that didn't catch. She said—" Sable stopped, started again. "She said the wards were waking up. Out there, they can feel it. She said something was *coming home*."

The millrace roared. Somewhere above, a kestrel screamed once and went silent, mid-cry, as if something had put a hand over its mouth.

That night, lying awake in the loft above his grandmother's kitchen, Wendell heard it for himself.

Not the dog barking. Not the geese. Underneath the ordinary night noises of the valley — wind in the barley, the creek, his grandmother's slow snoring — there was a tone. Faint. Patient. Rising out of the ground itself, out of the bones of the valley, a hum like the lowest string of some enormous instrument being tuned by hands he could not see.

The beasts felt it. He could feel them feeling it, all across the dark valley, hundreds of small minds turning toward the rim like flowers toward a sun that hadn't risen yet. Waiting. Afraid. Awake.

And threaded through the hum, so faint he might have dreamed it, came a voice — not a beast's voice, not any voice he knew — old and dry and glad, saying a word he almost recognized:

*Soon.*
