## 2. A Prayer Answered

The morning the traveler came up the valley road, Wendell was in the lower pasture teaching a lame ewe how to stand.

Not teaching, exactly. There was no word for what he did, not in any language his grandmother would let him speak aloud. It was more like lending. He lent the ewe his balance, his certainty that legs were things that could be trusted, and she borrowed it the way she'd once borrowed milk from her mother's teat — greedily, tremblingly, with her dark eyes fixed on his face as though he were something holy and slightly frightening. Her name was Sorrel. She was dying of a rot in the hip that the shepherd's salves could not touch, and all Wendell could give her was company for it.

*It hurts less when you're here,* she said, in the way sheep say things, which is mostly warmth and weight and very few words. *Why does it hurt less when you're here?*

"Because I'm listening," he murmured, so quietly that even the wind off the fells couldn't carry it. "That's all anybody needs, most days."

He was crouched with his palm flat against her wool when he heard the cart.

Nobody came to Hollowvale. That was the whole architecture of the place — the valley sat at the end of a road nobody traveled, walled by fells that discouraged climbing and by a reputation that discouraged everything else. The mapmakers wrote it down as *unremarkable* and left a blank space where the people should have been, which suited Hollowvale fine. His grandmother said the blankness was a blessing. His grandmother said a great many things were blessings: silence, smallness, the habit of looking at one's boots in company. Wendell had spent nineteen years learning which of her blessings were blessings and which were walls.

The cart came on. He stood, brushed straw from his knees, and watched it lurch along the river track toward the gate — the old stone archway where the valley narrowed to a throat, where his great-grandfather's generation had set the wards into the rock itself. He could not see them, the wards. Nobody in living memory had seen them work. They were like the keel of a boat: invisible, essential, spoken of only when something went wrong.

Something was wrong now. The air over the gate shimmered the way water shimmers over stones in August, a heat-haze in a cold month, and the beasts felt it before the men did. Every head in every pasture turned toward the gate at once. Forty sheep, six cows, the plow horses, the geese — all facing the same direction with the same terrible attention, like a congregation hearing the first syllable of an unfamiliar hymn.

*Sorrel* said, *What is that? What is that, what is that—*

"Hush," Wendell told her, though his own heart had begun to hammer. "Hush now."

The traveler was a man alone, driving a cart with a broken wheel-rim and a mule that looked half-starved. He wore the dust of many roads and the particular gray exhaustion of someone who has been praying for a long time without being answered. Wendell knew that look. He saw it sometimes in his own reflection.

At the gate the cart stopped. The man climbed down, and Wendell saw him sway, and saw him put his hand against the wardstone — just rest his hand there, the way travelers do, touching the boundary of a place they hope will be kind — and Wendell saw the man's lips move.

He was too far away to hear words. But he heard the prayer anyway.

That was the thing about his gift that no one in Hollowvale knew, the thing he kept folded away beneath his ribs like contraband: prayer and beast-speech ran in the same channel. When a creature opened itself — sheep or man or anything between — Wendell heard it, whether he wanted to or not, whether it was meant for him or for heaven. Most prayers sounded like weather. This one did not.

*Please,* the man prayed, and the prayer went through Wendell like a hook through wool. *I have carried her ashes three hundred miles. I promised her the sea but I haven't the strength for the sea. Let there be somewhere I can stop. Please. Let me stop.*

And the valley — the valley answered.

Wendell felt it happen in his teeth first, then in the soles of his feet, then everywhere at once. The wards did not fail the way a rope fails, fraying and parting. They failed the way river ice fails: a single report like a gunshot, then a long rippling crack that ran outward through the ground, through the bedrock, through the roots of the fells themselves, a sound he heard with his skin rather than his ears. The shimmer over the gate shattered into a thousand bright fragments that hung in the air for one impossible heartbeat — and then sank into the stone and were gone.

The traveler staggered. Then he straightened, blinking, and took a breath that went all the way down for the first time in three hundred miles. He looked at the gate, and the gate was open — not barred, not shimmering, just open, the way a door stands open in ordinary houses in ordinary places.

*Oh,* the man prayed, weeping now, guiding his mule through. *Oh, thank you. Thank you, thank you, thank you.*

The valley let him in.

Wendell stood in the pasture with Sorrel's wool still warm under his hand and understood, with the cold clarity that comes in the moment after lightning, that nothing in his life would ever again be arranged the way it had been arranged that morning. Because the wards had been quiet for three generations. Because his grandmother had told him a hundred times that magic did not enter Hollowvale, could not, that this was the covenant's one mercy to them — and because he had just watched a stranger's desperate prayer punch through sixty years of silence like a fist through wet paper.

The beasts were screaming.

Not loudly — beasts in Hollowvale didn't scream loudly, they hadn't in living memory — but every animal on the valley floor was speaking at once, urgent, overlapping, afraid. The horses at their rails saying *the sky cracked, the sky cracked*. The geese rising in a white ragged cloud and saying nothing intelligible at all, just alarm, pure alarm. And under all of it, older and deeper, Sorrel's voice going thin and strange:

*They stopped listening. For sixty years they stopped listening, and now something is answering, and Wendell, Wendell, the answers are coming from underneath.*

He did not ask from underneath what. Some questions you know better than to ask while the sun is still up.

* * *

His grandmother met him at the gate of the house, which meant she had felt it too, which meant everyone had felt it, which meant by nightfall there would not be a soul in Hollowvale who hadn't felt it. She was seventy-nine years old and built like the hawthorn at the corner of the sheepfold: short, dense, thorned, immovable. Her name was Aggie Marchetti and she was holding a kitchen knife, which told Wendell precisely how frightened she was.

"Inside," she said.

"The wards—"

"*Inside.*"

Inside smelled of bread and lamp oil and fear. She bolted the door, which was a performance — doors in Hollowvale hadn't been bolted since before Wendell was born — and then she stood in the middle of her own kitchen with the knife held wrong, grip too high, blade angled at nothing, and looked at her grandson as though measuring him for a coffin.

"You felt it," she said. Not a question.

"I was in the pasture. A traveler came. He was praying at the gate, and the valley—" Wendell stopped. Started again. "Grandmother. The valley answered him."

She closed her eyes. For a moment she looked every year of her age and several she had not yet reached. When she opened them, the fear had been folded away and hidden somewhere deep, the way she hid money in flour jars, and what remained was the flat practical voice she used for lambing deaths and flooded fields.

"What did you hear?" she asked. "Of the prayer. What exactly did you hear?"

The question dropped through Wendell like a stone through still water.

Because she had asked *what did you hear*, not *did you see*. She had asked about his ears, his particular ears, the inheritance nobody in the Marchetti line spoke about. She knew. She had always known — not the details, perhaps, not the shape of it, but the fact of it, the blood of it, and she had spent nineteen years building her silence around his like a second set of walls.

"You know," he said. It came out barely above a whisper. "You've always known."

"I know that you talk to animals. I have known since you were four years old and told me the cat's opinion of my cooking." She set the knife down on the table with exaggerated care, as if it might go off. "I hoped you'd grow out of it. Your father grew out of it. Or learned to bury it, which amounts to the same thing. And I know what happens in families where the gift stays loud." She turned to face him fully. "So you'll swear me an oath, Wendell Marchetti, here and now, with salt in your hand."

"An oath of what?"

"Silence. Total silence. Whatever you hear now — whatever starts speaking back — you tell no one. Not Sable, not the priest, not me unless I ask. The beasts are waking up. Something out there has remembered us, and the worst thing you can be, in a valley full of frightened people and waking animals, is the boy who hears both sides." She fetched the salt cellar and pressed it into his palm; the crystals were cold and coarse. "Swear it."

"Grandmother—"

"*Swear it.*" And then, quieter, with something in her voice he had never heard before and would spend the rest of his life identifying as terror: "Wendell. Sixty years ago your grandfather's brother swore the same oath, and broke it, and the last anyone saw of him was his boots on the church step and the wards humming for a week. I am asking you to survive. That's all I have ever asked of anyone in this family. Swear."

He swore. The salt bit into the creases of his palm and he said the old formula, *by bread and by blood and by the ground I stand on*, and his grandmother exhaled for perhaps the first time since morning, and began, absurdly, terribly, to make supper.

Routine as a fortress. Routine as a spell against the end of the world. She kneaded dough and he sat with salt still stinging his hand, and outside the window the light went amber and then blue, and the beasts of Hollowvale did not fall silent at dusk the way they had fallen silent every dusk of his life. They stayed awake. They stayed talking. Low, ceaseless, urgent, a tide that would not go out.

*Underneath,* Sorrel had said. *The answers are coming from underneath.*

And near midnight, when the bread had risen and his grandmother had finally gone to her bed and the house was dark except for the banked fire, Wendell heard the new voice for the first time.

It came up through the floorboards. Through the foundations. Through the bedrock and whatever slept beneath the bedrock. It was vast and slow and patient as erosion, and it did not speak in words so much as in pressure, the way deep water speaks to a drowning man, and what it said was this:

*I remember you, little speaker. I have waited a long time to be heard. Do not let them salt your voice shut.*

Wendell sat in the dark with his oath sour on his tongue and his hand curled around its cold grains, and understood that he had sworn silence to his grandmother at exactly the moment the world had decided to start speaking.

Outside, far off toward the gate, a mule brayed — long, ragged, human with grief — and every beast in the valley answered it at once.
