## 3. The Priestess of Ash

The stranger came up the valley road on the third morning after the traveler died, and the birds told Wendell before anyone else did.

*A woman,* said the wren from the eaves of the byre, its voice a bright needle in his skull. *Walking. Alone. She smells of smoke that isn't woodsmoke.*

He was mucking out the lambing pen when he heard it, and he stood very still with the fork half-lifted, letting the wren's knowing pour into him like water into a dry channel. Three days since the prayer at the gate had been answered — three days since the wards had cracked like river ice and something vast had leaned down through the gap to speak a dead man's name — and the valley had spent those days pretending nothing had happened. His grandmother had made him swear silence. The elders had swept the gate clean, burned the traveler's belongings, and declared the crack in the air a trick of weather. Hollowvale was very good at forgetting. It had practiced for three generations.

But the beasts had not forgotten. They had not stopped speaking back.

*She smells of ash,* the wren insisted. *Old ash. Altar ash.*

Wendell set down the fork and went to find Sable.

* * *

They watched her come from the wall above the sheepfold, the two of them shoulder to shoulder among the cold stones, as they had watched everything together since they were children. Sable's breath fogged in the early cold. Below, the road unspooled from the valley mouth, pale as a scar through the brown grass, and on it the woman walked without hurry, wrapped in a traveling cloak the color of wet slate.

"She's not dressed like a pilgrim," Sable murmured. "Pilgrims carry everything they own. She carries almost nothing."

She was right. The stranger bore only a staff of blackened wood and a satchel flat against her spine, and she walked the way water walks downhill — certain of her course, unhurried about reaching it. As she drew closer Wendell saw she was perhaps fifty, gray threading her dark hair, her face lined in deep vertical creases between the brows as though she had spent years squinting into fires. And around her neck, half-hidden by her collar, hung something that made his stomach drop: a pendant of bone carved into the shape of an open eye.

No one wore the open eye anymore. No one had worn it since his grandmother was a girl. It was the sign of the old priesthood — the keepers of the godstone, the speakers at the altar — the order that had scattered when the gods went silent and the valley sealed itself and everyone agreed, all at once and forever, that there had never been any such things.

"A priestess," Wendell breathed. "Sable, that's—"

"I know what it is." Sable's voice had gone strange and flat. "I've seen that face before. Older. In my mother's locket." Her hands tightened on the wall. "That's my aunt."

"Your aunt is dead. Your mother said—"

"My mother says a great many things." Sable was already climbing down from the wall. "Come on. If we don't meet her at the gate, the elders will, and the elders will lie to her, and she'll see them doing it, and then no one will learn anything from anyone."

Wendell hesitated. Every instinct the valley had planted in him said *stay small, stay silent, stay home.* But the wren was still speaking in his ear — *ash, altar-ash, she has knelt where the fire burned longest* — and the beasts had been urgent and afraid for three days now, pressing their knowledge against the inside of his skull like faces against a window. He wanted answers the way a drowning man wants air.

He followed Sable down.

* * *

Maren received them in the yard of the waystation inn, where the elders had grudgingly permitted her to wait while they "considered her petition." She stood apart from the cluster of gawking villagers, and when she turned at their approach, Wendell felt her attention land on him like a hand on his chest.

"You'll be Sable," she said to Sable first, and her voice was low and roughened, a voice used to speaking over wind and flame. "You have your mother's jaw and none of her caution. I'd know you anywhere." Something moved behind her eyes — grief, quickly shuttered. Then she looked at Wendell, and everything else in the world went quiet.

She looked at him for a long time. Not at his face, exactly. At some point just beside it, or within it. The yard noise — chickens, argument, the creak of the inn sign — receded. He became aware, horribly, of every beast within a hundred paces: the inn's dog gone rigid and attentive, the sparrows frozen on the roofline, the old plow horse at the far rail turning its blind attention toward him like a compass needle. All of them looking at him. All of them, in their wordless ways, *answering* him.

And Maren watching him watch them.

"Well," she said softly. "Well. Three generations, and here you are anyway, growing up wild in the dark like a mushroom." She laughed — a short, disbelieving sound. "Do you have any idea what you are, boy?"

"He's a farmhand," said Elder Coram, arriving red-faced and breathless. "Nephew of the Marchetti family. Good worker. Now, madam, regarding your petition—"

"His grandmother is Halda Marchetti," Maren said, not taking her eyes off Wendell. "Of course he is. Of course they kept the bloodline here, penned in the middle of nowhere, telling it stories about weather." Her gaze finally shifted, and Wendell watched Elder Coram wilt under it. "I'm not here for your petition, elder. I'm here for the stone your grandparents buried under the chapel floor when they thought the gods would sleep forever. And I'm leaving with it. Whether this valley burns or not depends rather a lot on whether I do."

A murmur ran through the gathered villagers. Someone spat. Someone else made the old warding gesture, thumb pressed to sternum, that children used against nightmares — and hadn't been needed in living memory until this week.

"The chapel floor is our business," Coram said stiffly.

"It's everyone's business now. That's rather the point." Maren turned back to Wendell, and her voice dropped, pitched for him alone beneath the crowd's noise. "The wards are failing. You've heard it — don't look surprised, everyone in this valley has heard it, they've just agreed not to. And when they finish failing, everything your ancestors locked out comes walking home. Including the thing that ate the last beast-speaker."

The words went through him like cold water. *The last beast-speaker.* Not a story. A person. A person who had been eaten.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, and hated how thin it sounded.

"No," Maren agreed. "You don't. But your bones do. Look at your hands."

He looked. He couldn't help it. His hands were trembling, and he hadn't noticed when they'd started.

* * *

She found him that evening at the sheepfold, because of course she did; the dog had told her where he'd be, and the dog had told her because Wendell had asked it to mind her, without thinking, the way he asked animals things a hundred times a day. He was beginning to understand that his gift left tracks everywhere, that he had never once in his life been truly hidden, only politely ignored.

Sable was with him. He was glad of it. Alone, he might have believed he dreamed her.

Maren sat down on the fold's low wall across from them, laid her black staff in the grass, and pulled a twist of paper from her cloak. From it she shook dried leaves that smelled of resin and something older, burnt-sweet, like the memory of incense.

"Altar leaf," she said. "There are four plants left in the world that grow on consecrated ground. This is one. I've carried it twenty years waiting for a reason." She did not light it. She only held it, letting the scent drift. "I'm going to tell you a story, Wendell Marchetti, and you're going to listen, because no one in this valley will ever tell it to you, and because I only have the strength to tell it once.

"Before Hollowvale had a name, it had an altar. A godstone, cut from the root of the world, and upon it the covenant was spoken — the bargain between gods, beasts, and mortals. Each answered to each. Mortals prayed, gods listened. Beasts spoke, mortals understood. It was a choir, do you see? Every voice bound to every other voice. And the ones who could hear the whole choir — who could stand in the middle of it and translate — were rare. Precious. Beast-speakers. Covenant-keepers. Your bloodline."

The evening had gone very still. Even the wind seemed to be listening.

"When the gods fell silent — and they fell silent because someone broke the covenant, though the how of it is a longer story than tonight — the beast-speakers were the only proof anything had ever been real. So the frightened men of that age did what frightened men do. They built walls. They warded the valley, buried the godstone, and hid the bloodline inside it like a jewel in a strongbox. They called it a refuge." Her mouth twisted. "It was a cage with a garden in it. And you were born in the cage, and they taught you the bars were the sky."

"That's not—" Wendell started, and stopped, because the ewe from three nights ago rose in his memory, the dying ewe he had soothed with borrowed words, and the strange rightness of those words in his mouth, words he had never learned anywhere, words that had simply *arrived*, the way water arrives in a well. His throat closed.

"Maren." Sable's voice was careful. "My mother said you were cast out. That you desecrated the shrine at Greyford. That you—"

"That I kept praying after the prayers stopped working? Yes." Maren smiled without warmth. "Your mother chose the cage and called it wisdom. I chose the wilderness and called it faith. We've both had twenty years to regret it." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and fixed Wendell with eyes like banked coals. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it once. Come with me. I can teach you what you are — the old tongue, the rites, the shape of the covenant and how it broke. I can take you to people who won't flinch when a sparrow speaks through you. And when the wards fail — not if, *when* — you'll be standing in the open, armed, instead of trapped in a box with the lid coming off."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I dig up the godstone alone, and you stay here and be a farmhand until the night the sky cracks open, and I truly hope you're quick on your feet." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not threatening you, boy. I'm describing Tuesday."

Wendell looked at Sable. Sable looked at the ground. Between them lay everything unspoken — the ewe, the gate, the dead traveler's name spoken out of the air, the three days of beasts pressing their fear against his skull. Home. The whole warm weight of it, the lambs and the kitchen smoke and his grandmother's hands, all of it balanced on one side of a scale. On the other side, a word he didn't yet know how to carry: *purpose.*

"I need to think," he said.

"You have until dawn," Maren said. "Then I go to the chapel, with you or without you."

* * *

His grandmother was waiting in the kitchen when he came home, which meant she already knew. Halda Marchetti sat by the banked fire with her knitting untouched in her lap, and she was smaller than he remembered her being, though she could not have shrunk since breakfast.

"Maren Ash," she said, using the name like a blade. "Sable's aunt. The priestess who wouldn't stop praying." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once. "Whatever she offered you, Wendell. Whatever truth she dangled. The answer is no. You swear it to me now, on this house, on your mother's grave. You swear you will not leave this valley."

Outside, beyond the window, the whole valley held its breath — the dog, the horses, the owls waking in the elms, all of them turning their attention toward the small lit kitchen where an old woman demanded a vow from a boy who had just learned his whole life was a cage.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, beneath the soil, beneath the chapel floor, something ancient and patient began — very faintly, like a heart remembering its work — to hum.
