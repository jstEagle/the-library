## 4. What the Beasts Remember

The ewe died at dawn, and the valley pretended nothing had happened.

Wendell buried her behind the shepherd's wall, where the ground was soft from three days of rain, and he did not sing the burial words aloud because there was no one left to pretend for. Sable stood beside him with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hands black with grave-earth, and neither of them spoke. There was a language between them now that needed no breath — the language of two people who had watched a stranger's prayer crack the sky over Hollowvale and had not run screaming into the hills.

The wards were still humming. He could feel them in his teeth, in the fillings of old fences, in the water barrel that shivered without wind. Three nights since the traveler died at the gate, and the sound had not stopped. It had only changed — grown lower, sadder, like a hymn sung by throats too tired to remember the verses.

That morning, the beasts stopped eating.

Not all at once. That was what made it terrible. Wendell noticed it first in the hens, who left their grain untouched and stood in a silent brown row along the coop fence, facing the hills. Then the dairy cows, gathered at the far corner of their pasture, heads low, tails still. Then the dogs, who would not bark, who pressed themselves flat to the earth when anyone passed as though sound itself had become dangerous.

By noon, every animal in the lower valley had turned its face toward the standing stones above Marchetti land — toward the godstone, though Wendell did not yet know that name — and waited.

"They want you," Sable said. She had come over the wall at midday, mud to her knees, hair escaping its braid in pale wisps. Her grandmother thought she was at the mill. Everyone in Hollowvale was very good lately at not asking where people were.

"Who wants me?"

"The beasts." She said it plainly, the way she said everything. "All of them. I've never seen them do this. Grandmother says animals go strange before earthquakes." She looked at him, and her gray eyes held something that was not fear but lived next door to it. "But there isn't going to be an earthquake, is there, Wendell."

It was not a question, so he did not answer it. He wiped his hands on his trousers, took up the crook he no longer needed to speak with beasts but carried anyway, the way another man might carry a cross, and started up the sheep path toward the high pasture. Behind him — he heard it with that other sense, the one that lived below hearing — the animals began to move. All of them. A slow tide of hooves and paws and wings flowing uphill after him through the wet grass.

Sable walked beside him. She had never once asked him to leave her out of anything, and he loved her for it in the way he loved breathing: constantly, and without deciding to.

* * *

The blind mule was waiting at the top.

She had been on the Marchetti farm longer than Wendell had been alive; his grandmother claimed the mule had been old when she was a girl, and his grandmother lied about very little. She stood in the ruined fold above the stones, gray as the granite around her, her milky eyes turned precisely to where Wendell's footsteps stopped. Moss hung from her like a garment. Her breath came slow and tidal.

They called her Old Nettle, but the name felt wrong in his mouth today, the way a borrowed coat feels wrong.

*"Speaker,"* she said.

Her voice was not a voice. It arrived the way weather arrives — vast, unhurried, already inside the valley before he perceived it coming. Every beast-speech Wendell had ever traded had been small talk: a dog's opinion of thunder, a horse's grudge against a particular gate. This was different. This was the difference between rain on a window and standing in the sea.

"I'm here," he said aloud, because his gift worked both ways, and because his throat needed something ordinary to do.

*"You have spoken our tongues since you could stand,"* the mule said. *"Now you will hear ours. All of it. We have kept it long enough."*

The animals had filled the fold behind him — cattle in the gateway, sheep spilling down the slope, the dogs lying flat with their ears pinned, crows lining the wall like mourners on a pew. Sable stood close enough that her shoulder touched his arm. He drew strength from that small weight.

"Then tell me."

The mule lowered her great head until her gray lips nearly brushed the turf. When she spoke again, the whole herd breathed with her, a hundred lungs moving as one bellows, and the words came up out of the earth itself.

*"Your valley is not a refuge, Wendell Marchetti. It is a lock. And you are the thing it locks away."*

The wind moved through the grass. Somewhere below, the wards hummed their tired hymn.

*"Before your grandmother's grandmother, there was a covenant,"* the mule said. *"Gods answered, beasts spoke, mortals kept the terms. One law bound all three kinds together, and the law was this: every prayer must be answerable, and every answer must be borne. The gods spoke through beasts to mortals, and mortals spoke back through the same channel. The channel had a name among us. The Speaker's line."*

Wendell's mouth had gone dry. "Beast-speakers."

*"One in a generation. Fewer, in the lean centuries. The voice that could carry between kinds without breaking. Your blood, child. Your mother's blood, and hers before her, back to the first woman who understood the speech of wolves."* The mule's nostrils flared, drawing in his scent, cataloguing him. *"A covenant needs a keystone. Yours was it. And keystones are dangerous things — pull them out, and everything falls."*

"But the covenant *did* fall," Sable said quietly. "Three generations ago. The gods went silent."

*"Because your ancestors ran,"* the mule said, without cruelty and without mercy. *"There was a breaking — we remember it as a scream that lasted nine days. What caused it, we do not know; the gods themselves went dark with grief or guilt. But the Speaker line survived it, and those who remained feared what the line might draw to it. Feared what might come looking for the last keystone of a broken law. So they built walls."*

The mule turned her blind face upward, toward the ridgeline where the ward-stones stood like sentries pretending to be scenery.

*"They told the world they sought shelter here. They told their children they were keeping magic out. Both lies were kind, and both were lies. The wards were not raised against what walks outside, Speaker. They were raised against what sleeps inside — against you, and your mother, and every drop of the old blood. A cage dressed as a hearth. We watched them build it. We watched them teach your line to be ashamed of the very gift that once held heaven and earth in conversation. Shame is an excellent chain. It does not need iron."*

Wendell sat down. His legs simply declined to hold him, and the wet turf soaked through his trousers, cold as a hand pressing him into the ground. He thought of his mother, who had died quiet and young and afraid of open doors. Of his grandmother's oath, forced on him three nights ago: *stay silent, stay small, stay.* He thought of every year he had swallowed words like stones, every ewe soothed in secret, every kindness done in hiding — and how he had believed, right up until this moment, that the hiding was his own cowardice.

It had been architecture. It had been blueprints drawn before his birth.

"Wendell." Sable knelt beside him. Her hand found his and gripped hard. He was distantly aware that she was watching the animals watch him, that she alone in this valley had nothing to hide and therefore nothing to fear from the truth being poured out on the hilltop.

"Why tell me now?" His voice came out cracked. "Why have none of you ever—"

*"We tried,"* the mule said, gentler now, the way one is gentle with the newly wounded. *"Every speaker before you was warned the same way, in the same hour: when the wards begin to fail. Your mother heard these words on this hill. She went home and never spoke to us again. Grief can close a gift like frost closes a gate."* A pause, vast and slow. *"We do not wish to watch that happen twice."*

"The wards are failing." He said it flatly, testing the shape of it. "The traveler's prayer. The cracking. That's the cage breaking."

*"No,"* said the mule. *"That is the world calling the keystone home. The covenant broke, but it did not die — broken things still hunger to be whole, and a broken law reaches for the piece that once held it. Something is coming, Speaker. It has followed your bloodline's scent for three generations and could not reach you through the walls. Now the walls are thinning, and it has begun to walk."*

The crows lifted off the wall all at once, a single dark exhalation against the white sky. Down in the valley, the hum of the wards stuttered — actually stuttered, a skipped beat like a frightened heart — and every animal in the fold flinched in unison.

"What is it?" Sable asked. "What's coming?"

The mule was silent a long moment. Rain began to fall, fine and cold, beading on her gray hide, and she did not blink against it because she had no lids worth blinking and had made her peace with weather centuries ago.

*"The ones who broke it,"* she said at last. *"Or what remains of them. We smell unanswered prayers on the wind, and under the prayers, something older, walking. Ask your aunt's sister — the ash-priestess. She knows its name. She has been running from it her whole life, which is why she has come back to the last stone of the old law."*

"My mother didn't have a sister," Wendell said, but even as he said it he remembered Maren — Sable's aunt, the disgraced priestess who had arrived two days ago with road dust in her hair and recognition in her eyes, who had looked at him across the kitchen fire and said, *So the valley kept you after all.*

*"Go down, Speaker,"* the mule said, turning her blind face back toward the valley, toward home, toward the lock. *"Ask her about the godstone your family has spent four generations pretending is just an unusual rock. Ask her what it opens."*

And then, as Wendell rose on shaking legs with Sable's hand still locked in his, the old mule added one thing more — softly, so softly it might have been meant only for the rain to hear:

*"Ask her why your grandmother still kneels to it every full moon. And ask yourself why she never let you touch it."*
