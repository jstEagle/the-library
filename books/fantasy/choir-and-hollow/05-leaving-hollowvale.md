## 5. Leaving Hollowvale

Sable had always thought she would know the end of the world by its noise. That was what the elders promised at every winter fire: when the wards fail, you will hear it — a crack like the sky splitting, a sound to stop the heart in the chest. Instead the world ended quietly, the way a rope ends, not with a snap but with a slow unraveling of fibers until there is nothing left to hold.

She felt it first in her hands. She was milking her father's cows in the blue hour before dawn, and the milk came warm and ordinary into the pail, but her palms were singing. Not pain. Something older than pain. It felt like standing on a bridge and feeling the boards begin to give beneath your feet — that flutter in the soles, the body knowing before the mind consents to know.

The wards of Hollowvale had been humming for three nights now. Everyone heard it. They pretended they didn't, the way a family pretends not to hear an argument behind a closed door. Her mother had taken to leaving salt at the windowsills. Her father had sharpened his scythe twice without needing to. And Sable — Sable had spent those three nights with her ear pressed to the ground in the sheep pasture, listening to the hum come up through the soil like a hymn sung underwater, and thinking not *we are doomed* but *so it was true all along.*

That was her shame, if it was shame: under the fear, under the grief, some small bright coal in her had caught flame and would not be put out. Magic was real. The world outside the valley was real, and awake, and coming in.

The pail slipped from her fingers and did not fall.

It hung in the air beside her knee, tilted, milk catching the first gray light, turning very slowly as if suspended in water. Sable watched one white drop rise out of the pail's mouth and hang there like a pearl.

"Ah," she said, to no one, and then, because her aunt Maren had once told her that words were the oldest magic and should never be wasted on silence: "Well. Ah."

* * *

She found Wendell at the godstone.

Of course he was at the godstone. The whole valley would converge there by full light — she could see lanterns bobbing down from the upper farms, hear dogs barking their confusion, hear old Tam Wicker shouting at his chickens, who had all flown up into the alders and refused, apparently on principle, to come down. But Wendell had gotten there first, the way he got everywhere first, quiet as fog off the river, and he stood with one hand flat against the stone as though taking the pulse of something enormous.

The godstone did not look like much. It never had — a gray fang of rock taller than two men, crouched in the bracken above the eastern path, worn smooth on one side where generations of superstitious hands had stroked it for luck and then gone home embarrassed to have done so. But this morning it was *lit*, faintly, the way a banked stove is lit: no flame, only a deep orange glow living inside the grain of the stone, pulsing in time — she realized with a lurch — in time with her own heart.

"It's waking up," Wendell said without turning. "They're all waking up. There are eleven more of these buried around the valley and every one of them is—" He stopped. His shoulders moved with a breath that wanted to be a laugh and couldn't quite afford it. "Sable. The milk didn't spill."

"I held it," she said. "With my mind. Badly."

"With your mind." Now he turned, and the look on his face undid her a little: wonder and terror braided together, and underneath both, aimed at her specifically, a fierce gladness that made her feel seen in a way she hadn't known she was starving for. "You don't have the gift. You've never had the gift. Your whole line's been deaf to it since—"

"Since my aunt was cast out for having too much of it, yes, I've heard the story. Apparently the blood bides its time." She looked down at her hands. They looked like her hands. They felt like doors thrown open in an empty house. "Wendell. The beasts are speaking to me. All night. The ewes won't shut up about the sky."

"What do they say?"

"That it's opening."

He nodded slowly, and told her the rest: the traveler dead at the gate whose prayer had been answered aloud; the cracks running through the warding like frost through a windowpane; what the blind mule had told him in the dark of the stable, word by terrible word. Prison. Keystone. Bloodline. His voice stayed level all the way through, which she knew cost him, because she had seen him weep over a dying lamb at eleven years old and he had never once learned to hide anything that mattered.

When he finished, the lanterns were closer. She could pick out her mother's voice among the distant crowd, high and frightened, calling her name across the fields.

"They'll want to put you under the stone," Sable said. "Or send you out of it. Or pray over you. They'll do something, and it will be stupid, because they've had three generations to practice being helpless and they're very good at it."

"Sable—"

"Maren's coming back for you. I know she is. She'd have reached the pass by yesterday." She took his hand off the godstone and held it, and felt the hum of the waking world travel between their palms like current. "I'm coming with you."

"You don't know what's out there."

"No," she agreed. "And I don't know what's in here, either, anymore. At least out there the not-knowing is honest." She laughed, and heard how ragged it came out, grief and exhilaration cut from the same cloth and impossible to tell apart. "My grandmother used to say the valley keeps us safe. She never said safe *from*, and I was too young to ask. I'm asking now. Whatever's coming through those wards tonight or tomorrow or the day after — it's coming because of you, or for you, and I will not spend the rest of my life in a prison built around my favorite person. I'd rather be unsafe where you are than safe where you aren't. That's the whole speech. I worked on it in the milking shed."

For a moment he just looked at her, and the light of the godstone moved in his eyes, and she thought: *oh, there it is, we're going to have to talk about that too, someday when we're not fleeing.* Then he said, "Your mother is going to curse my name."

"She already does. It'll be practice for the outside world." 

* * *

They went at dawn, while the valley was still arguing with itself.

Her mother found them at the edge of the home pasture. Of course she did; Sable had counted on it, and had rehearsed nothing, because there was nothing to rehearse that wouldn't be a lie. Brigid Almeida came across the wet grass with her shawl clutched at her throat and her face white as the milk Sable had left hanging in the air of the byre, and Sable saw in one terrible glance everything her mother would never say: *not you too, not you, everyone leaves, the gift took my sister and now it takes my daughter.*

"Mother," Sable said. "I have to."

"He'll be eaten by the world," her mother said. "Or worshipped by it. Both are dying, Sable, I watched them take Maren apart piece by piece and I could do nothing, and you want to walk into the same mouth—"

"Yes."

Just that. Sable heard herself say it and marveled at how small the word was for how much it weighed. Her mother stared at her, and then at Wendell, who stood with his head bowed like a boy at a funeral, and something moved across Brigid Almeida's face that Sable had never seen there before — not surrender, nothing so clean, but the look of a woman measuring a wall she cannot climb and deciding, furiously, to build a door instead.

She unpinned her shawl and pushed it into Sable's hands. It smelled of woodsmoke and lavender and thirty years of kitchen work.

"If you die out there," her mother said, "I will be so angry with you. Do you understand? So angry. For the rest of my life."

"Yes, Mother."

"And you—" She jabbed a finger at Wendell, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "You keep her. You keep her better than we kept you. That is not a blessing. I am not giving you a blessing. I'm giving you an order."

Then she turned and walked back toward the house and did not look at the sky, which had begun, very quietly, to shimmer.

* * *

The gate was not a gate anymore. It was a wound.

Maren stood in the middle of it. The eastern pass narrowed between two shoulders of granite, and where the last ward had hung — Sable had never seen the wards, none of them had, that was the point of wards — the air now tore and flickered like heat over a forge, and through the tear Sable could see *wrongness*. Colors that hurt. A darkness with texture, with appetite, pressing against the gap the way floodwater presses at a door. Something on the other side knew they were there. Something on the other side had always known.

"Aunt," Sable said.

"Don't 'aunt' me, girl, get behind the godstone." Maren's robes were travel-stained, her cropped hair gray at the temples now where it hadn't been a season ago, and her hands were raised and burning with a soft ash-colored fire that threw no heat Sable could feel. She looked magnificent and half-consumed, a candle burning from both ends by choice. "Wendell. Look at me. Past the stone, down the pilgrim road, three days to the drowned chapel — you do not stop, you do not answer any voice that calls you by name, and you do not, *under any circumstances*, look back at the valley before the ridge takes it from view. Grief can follow you. Regret can follow you. What's behind me can only follow you if you invite it. Understood?"

"Maren, you can't hold that alone," Wendell said.

"I held worse alone for twenty years. It's why they unfrocked me." She grinned, and it was her sister's grin, the whole family's grin, sudden and crooked and bright. "Go. Both of you. And girl—" her eyes found Sable's, ash-fire to ash-fire, "—the blood wakes late but it wakes strong. When it comes, don't fight it and don't hoard it. Spend it. Everything I did wrong, I did by holding back."

Sable kissed her cheek, quick and hard, tasting salt and smoke. "Come find us at the chapel."

"Oh, I intend to be insufferably alive," Maren said. "Now run."

They ran. Past the godstone, glowing now like an ember blown upon, past the boundary oaks where the wards' last threads snapped overhead with sounds like harp strings breaking, down onto the pilgrim road that no feet had kept for sixty years. The world beyond the valley opened before them — hills the color of unbleached wool, a sky vast and trembling with new light, rivers running silver with things moving in them that rivers had not held since her great-grandmother's day. Sable's legs shook. Her lungs burned. The shawl her mother gave her streamed out behind her like a banner for a country that didn't exist yet, and she was crying, and she was laughing, and both were entirely true.

Behind them, from the pass, came a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and Maren's ash-fire flared white against the tearing dark, and whatever hunted from the god-side struck the gate she held.

Wendell's hand found hers and gripped it.

"Don't look back," he said.

"I'm not looking back," said Sable, running toward the wide and dangerous world with her heart hammering like wings. "I'm looking forward. Pray whatever's ahead of us has better manners than what's behind."

Ahead of them, on the far hilltop, the drowned chapel's bell began — impossibly, distantly, sweetly — to ring.
