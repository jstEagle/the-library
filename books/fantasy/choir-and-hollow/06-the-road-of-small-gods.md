## 6. The Road of Small Gods

The pilgrim road out of the highlands was older than any map Wendell had ever seen, and it showed. The paving stones had been laid by hands that believed travelers would come forever, and now they heaved and cracked like old teeth, shouldered apart by moss and the roots of rowan trees that no one had planted on purpose. In Hollowvale, roads went somewhere. This one seemed to go everywhere at once, braiding itself through valleys and over ridgelines, worn deep in places by centuries of knees.

They had been walking for six days when they came to the first village with a god in it.

Wendell heard it before he saw anything — a sound like a bell rung underwater, coming from the direction of the village well. He stopped so abruptly that Sable walked into his back.

"What is it?" she asked, hand going to the knife at her belt. She had taken to carrying it openly since the valley; her family's disapproval could not reach her here, and she wore the freedom like a second cloak.

"It's singing," Wendell said. "Or — praying. Something is praying."

Maren came back down the road to them, her ash-grey robes stiff with dust, her face unreadable as ever. "Thornhollow," she said, reading the crooked sign at the crossroads. "There used to be nothing here but sheep and a shrine to a river spirit nobody remembered the name of." Her mouth thinned. "That was thirty years ago. Apparently things have changed."

They hadn't changed. That was the trouble. As they came down into the village, Wendell saw that everything was exactly as it must have been for generations — the same slate roofs, the same drover's inn, the same geese arguing in the lane — except that around the well, a crowd had gathered, and the well itself was *listening*.

He felt it the way he felt weather coming in his bad knee, which was to say, all over. The water below the stones was awake. Not fully — not the way the beasts were alive, warm and particular and full of their own opinions — but awake the way a sleeper is awake when you say their name: turning toward the sound, hungry for more of it.

At the well's edge, an old woman knelt with her forehead nearly touching the stone. "Please," she was saying, and her voice had the raw, reused quality of someone who had said this many times. "Please, she's my granddaughter, she's fevered three days, please, I'll give you the blue beads, I'll give you the first cut of hay, I'll—"

The water shivered. A smell rose from the well like crushed mint and pond-bottom, and somewhere beneath the village, something vast and half-formed turned its attention toward the words, the way a starving man turns toward the smell of bread.

And then it answered. Not in words. In a *pulling*. Wendell watched the woman's offering — a string of blue glass beads — slide off her palm and vanish down between the stones, and he felt the moment the god took hold of the fever the way a man takes hold of a rope: clumsily, with strength but no skill, dragging the sickness out of the child and leaving something else behind in its place. He couldn't have said what. But the crowd sighed, and the old woman wept, and somewhere a door opened and a girl's voice called weakly for water, and everyone agreed it was a miracle.

Wendell stood very still in the lane, and the geese came over to look at him, and one of them said, in the flat, unimpressed voice of geese everywhere, *"You're the one they'll want. Best keep walking."*

He kept walking. It took him three steps to realize what the goose had said, and another three to understand why his hands were shaking.

* * *

They took a room at the inn because Maren insisted they needed information more than speed, and because Sable's feet had begun to bleed inside her new boots. The inn was called the Drowned Lamb, which struck Wendell as either very honest or very grim, and its common room was full of pilgrims — real ones, with staffs and scallop badges and the thousand-yard stare of people who had walked a long way to ask for something.

"They're queuing up," Sable said quietly, watching from their corner table. A one-eyed man was telling anyone who'd listen about the god at Millbeck Bridge, who would mend a lame horse if you fed it your left shoe. "Like it's a market."

"It is a market," Maren said. She turned her cup slowly on the table, not drinking. "That's what happens when the covenant rots. Prayer was never supposed to be a transaction. It was supposed to be a conversation — two voices, answering each other, bound by the old agreements. A god gave because a mortal gave, and both knew the shape of the debt, and neither could take more than the covenant allowed." She looked up, and her eyes were the flat grey of wood ash. "Now there are no agreements. Just hunger on both sides. These little gods wake up starving, and mortals wake up desperate, and every prayer is a small theft — a scrap of power pulled loose from the dying covenant, spent on fevers and lame horses and lost wedding rings. And every theft makes the unraveling faster."

"Then stop them," Sable said. "You're a priestess. Tell them."

"I am a *disgraced* priestess," Maren said, without self-pity, the way you'd state the weather. "I told the truth in a place that wanted lies, and they burned my order's records and struck my name from the rolls. No one on this road will listen to me. They'd listen to—" She stopped. Her gaze moved to Wendell, and stayed there, and Wendell felt the weight of it settle on him like a yoke.

"No," he said.

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"You were going to say they'd listen to me." He kept his voice low. Around them the pilgrims murmured their shopping lists of prayers, and under the floorboards, in the well-water, in the roots of the rowans along the road, he could feel the small gods stirring like fish under ice. "I've been listening to them since the well. They're all saying the same thing. Did you know that? Every one of them, all day, like gulls over a fishing boat. They're saying *come here. Come here. Come here.*"

Maren didn't deny it. That was worse than denial.

"Why?" Sable asked. She had put her hand over Wendell's on the table, and he held on.

"Because of what he is," Maren said. "Because the beast-speakers were the covenant's voice. When the covenant was whole, a speaker could call a god to heel the way a shepherd calls a dog — not with force, with *authority*. The gods were made to answer that voice. And right now every half-awake godling on this road is starving, and stealing, and tearing the world a little more loose with every bargain, and somewhere in the marrow of each of them is an old, old instruction: *when the voice comes, obey it.*" She leaned forward. "You could quiet them, Wendell. All of them. You could walk this road from end to end and hush every thieving godling back to sleep, and the villages would call you a saint, and the unraveling would slow, and—"

"And?" Wendell said. Because there was an *and*. There was always an *and*, and he was learning to hear it coming.

Maren held his gaze. "And you'd be spending yourself to do it. Each command costs. And the gods would learn your voice, Wendell. They'd learn it the way these villagers have learned the well-god's price. And the day would come when you spoke and something vast answered, something that remembers being commanded, and it would not be grateful." She sat back. "But it would buy time. Time to reach the Altar. Which is what we need. So I'm asking you to consider it. That's all. Consider it."

Wendell looked at his hand under Sable's. He thought of the ewe, years ago, dying in the cold barn, and how her pain had poured into him and he had poured back the only thing he had — borrowed words, soft ones, *easy now, easy, you're not alone* — and how she had quieted, not because the words fixed anything, but because being heard was its own mercy.

"I'll think about it," he said, and Maren nodded once, satisfied, and Wendell understood with a cold, slow clarity that he had just been managed — gently, expertly, by a woman who loved a purpose more than she loved people — and that this was only the first time.

* * *

Millbeck Bridge confirmed everything and made it worse.

The god there lived in the river under the bridge, and it had grown fat on shoes. Pilgrims came from miles around to feed it — left shoes, always left shoes, because some forgotten bargain had fixed the price and no one dared vary it — and the bridge's far bank was hung with hundreds of single right shoes drying on ropes like strange fruit. In exchange, the god mended horses, found lost children, sweetened sour wells. Small favors. Small thefts. The river ran slower than it should have, Wendell noticed, thickened with everything it swallowed, and the fish had gone strange and silent, and when he leaned over the parapet and listened, the god underneath said, in a voice like wet gravel learning language:

*"Voice. Voice. You're the Voice. We smelled you coming for two days. Come down. Come down and tell us what to be."*

"I'm not commanding you," Wendell said under his breath.

*"Please."*

It was the *please* that undid him. He gripped the cold stone of the parapet and made himself listen to the whole of it — the god's hunger, yes, but under the hunger, terror. It had woken up alone in a world with no instructions, no covenant, no shape, and all it knew how to do was eat what was offered and dread what came next. It was a dog born after the fall of man, waiting at a door that no longer opened.

"You're hurting the river," Wendell whispered. "You're hurting yourselves. All of you, all down the road. You have to stop taking. You have to wait."

*"Wait for what?"*

"For the covenant to be mended. For the Altar. For—" He didn't know how to finish. But the god went still beneath him, the whole river gone quiet as held breath, and Wendell realized that his voice had done it — not a command, just a speaking-to, plain and human, and the god had *obeyed* anyway, because obedience was stitched into its oldest fibers. He felt sick. He felt powerful. Both at once, and neither clean.

Behind him, he heard Maren murmur to Sable, low and urgent: "...if he can do that with a river-godling, imagine what he could do at the Altar. Imagine what the old gods would pay for him. We need to keep him moving, keep him focused. Whatever it takes."

He didn't turn around. He stood on the bridge with his hands on the stone and let the words sink into him like rain into dry ground, and beside his fear, something harder began to set, the way mud sets.

That night, camped in a hollow off the road with the fire low, Sable rolled her blanket out next to his and said, "You heard them. On the bridge."

"Yes."

"She's not wrong, though. That's the worst part. We do need to move fast. And you *can* quiet them." She poked the fire. "I just need to know whose plan you're following, Wendell. Hers? Or yours?"

Above the hollow, past the black teeth of the moorline, the sky did the thing it had been doing every night since the wards failed — shivered, like a curtain with someone behind it. And far off, at the edge of hearing, down in the drowned places where the old chapels slept, something that was not a small god at all stirred in its long watching, and turned its attention, patient as sediment, toward the boy on the road who could speak.

Wendell lay back and listened to the night, and the night listened back, and somewhere in the dark a voice older than the valley said, almost kindly:

*"I know you, little speaker. I have always known you. Come to the water."*
