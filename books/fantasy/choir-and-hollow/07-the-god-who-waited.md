## 7. The God Who Waited

The chapel had drowned a century before either of them was born, and still the water remembered it was holy.

Wendell felt it first as a hush — the particular silence of a place where even frogs declined to sing. The road of small gods had led them down out of the moorland through a birch wood gone silver in the dusk, and then the trees simply stopped, as if politely declining to intrude, and there it was: a stone roof rising from black water like the back of a sleeping whale. A bell tower leaned at an angle no honest mason would have permitted, its bell long since stolen or sunk, and around the walls the water lay flat as poured pewter, holding the first stars.

"It's flooded," Sable said, stating the obvious the way she always did when she was afraid — plainly, stubbornly, daring the world to argue.

"The water's shallow near the door," Wendell said. He could hear it, the way he heard everything now: not words exactly, but the water's slow contentment, the fish moving beneath it like thoughts too shy to be spoken aloud. "And something's keeping it warm."

"That should frighten me more than it does."

"Everything about you frightens me more than it does," he said, and she laughed — a short, startled sound that the hush swallowed whole — and they waded in.

The water came to their knees inside, then their shins, then, in the central nave, only their ankles, which was wrong and they both knew it. The chapel floor should have been deepest at its heart. Instead the water parted there, drawing back from a circle of dry flagstone as if the flood itself were kneeling.

Candle stubs floated on the surface. As Wendell watched, each one lit — softly, without fuss, flames no larger than thumbnails, so that the whole drowned nave filled with small warm lights drifting like seeds on a pond.

"I hate when gods do that," Sable whispered.

"You've met many?"

"Only the ones who light things without hands." She had her knife out. It was a farmer's knife, honest and dull, and she held it anyway. Wendell loved her for it and did not say so, because saying so had become, over the weeks of the road, a thing he could barely keep himself from doing at all hours, and this did not seem the moment.

*It is exactly the moment,* said the water, in a voice like cold bells rung underwater. *But take your time. I have waited longer than your valley has had a name.*

He came up slowly between the drifting candles. Not rising from the water but *of* it — a figure assembled from reflection, tall and thin, wearing robes that moved like current. Where a face should have been there was a smoothness like the underside of ice, and in it, faintly, features swam: eyes, a mouth, surfacing and submerging again, never quite committing to being seen.

"You're Vesper," Wendell said.

*I am.* The god inclined his head, and the candleflames all bent toward Wendell at once, like grass before wind. *You knew my name.*

"Maren said it. On the road. She said—" He stopped, recalibrating, because Maren had said it the way you'd say *careful, that dog bites.* "She said you were the god who remembers."

*Your priestess flatters me by halves.* Vesper drifted closer, and where he passed, the candleflames guttered and righted themselves, apologetic. *I remember everything. That is my whole domain, and my whole punishment. Come. Sit. The water will hold you.*

The water did hold them — a gentle pressure at their backs, guiding them down onto two dry flagstones that might have been carved as pews a thousand years ago. Sable sat close enough that her shoulder pressed his, and neither of them pretended it was accidental.

"Tell me about the covenant," Wendell said. "The real version. Not Maren's. Not the beasts'."

Vesper was quiet for a long moment. Around him the candles drifted, rearranging themselves into patterns Wendell almost recognized — constellations, maybe, or the shape of some older alphabet.

*Maren told you the covenant bound gods and beasts and mortals into mutual answerability,* Vesper said at last. *That is true, and it is the smallest true thing I can say. Here is a larger one. When the world was young and loud, every voice answered every other voice. Gods spoke to worms. Wolves debated theology with sheep. Mortals prayed and were answered so immediately that prayer and conversation had not yet split apart as practices.* A ripple crossed the water; the candles rocked. *It was too much. Do you understand? Every voice answering every voice is not harmony. It is a scream sustained forever. So the covenant was forged as a mercy: not silence, but *order*. Each realm would speak in turn. Beasts would carry mortals' prayers. Gods would hear them and answer through beast and weather and dream. And one bloodline would stand at the center of it, the tongue between tongues, the keystone voice.*

"My bloodline."

*Yours.* The swimming face surfaced, and for a moment Wendell saw eyes there, ancient and unbearably kind. *Beast-speakers. The altar at Hollowvale's heart was not built to cage you, whatever the mule told you — though from inside the cage, I grant you, it would feel the same. It was built to be *held*. A covenant needs a living voice at its center, willing to speak for all three realms and willing to pay what speaking costs.*

"And the cost," Wendell said quietly, "is being silenced."

*Yes.*

Sable's hand found his. Her fingers were cold from the wade and rough from the road, and he held on.

"But it broke," she said. "Before us. Someone came before."

*One came before.* Vesper's voice dropped until Wendell felt it more than heard it, a vibration in the floodwater against their ankles. *The last of your line before you. A woman named Ilsette, who stood where you are sitting, in this same chapel, when it was only damp and not yet drowned. She was brave and clever and tired, as you are. And when the Altar showed her the price — a voice given over, a self surrendered, the gift that made her herself poured out forever — she refused.*

"She just... walked away?"

*She ran.* No judgment in it. Only grief, worn smooth as riverstone. *And the covenant, left without its keystone, began to rot. Slowly, at first. Prayers took longer to arrive. Answers grew garbled. Gods who went unprayed-for grew hungry, and hungry gods grow strange. And from all those unanswered prayers — every plea that rose and found no ear, every cry that fell back heavy and unheard — something condensed. Something with an appetite.*

"The Hollow One."

*The Hollow One. My brother, once, in the way old things are brothers.* The candleflames nearest Vesper went out, one by one, and he did not relight them. *He does not hate you, Wendell. That is what Maren will not understand. He *wants* you. You are the last unbroken vessel of the voice he starves without. If you die refusing the Altar, he drinks what spills. If you live refusing it, he waits. He has been waiting since Ilsette. He is very good at waiting.*

* * *

Afterward — after Vesper had shown them, in the drifting candlelight, visions of the Altar and the rite and the threefold price — the god withdrew to the far end of the nave and became indistinguishable from the water, courteous as a host leaving a room. Whether he slept or listened, Wendell couldn't tell. He suspected listening. Gods, in his limited experience, were mostly listening.

They dried themselves by a fire Sable built from pew-wood, in the chapel's one corner the flood had spared. The drowned nave breathed around them, dark and starred with candlelight, and somewhere overhead a hole in the roof let the actual stars look in on the false ones floating below.

"He wants one of us," Sable said, to the fire.

"He wants me."

"He wants someone to break." She fed a splinter of pew to the flames. "Maren thinks it'll be you. The mule thinks it'll be you. Even the *god* thinks it'll be you — did you see how he looked at you when he talked about the price? Like a man describing a debt he expects you to honor."

"Sable—"

"And nobody," she said, and her voice cracked and she pushed through it, furious at herself, "nobody has once asked what happens to *me*. If you pour yourself out at that Altar. If you walk away like Ilsette and get drunk like rain. What am I supposed to do afterward, Wendell? Go home?" She laughed, awful and bright. "There's no home. There's a valley full of magic flooding in and a family that watched me leave and a gate held shut behind us by an aunt I've spoken four words to in my life. I have nothing except—"

She stopped. The fire popped.

"Except you," Wendell finished.

"Don't." But she turned toward him, and the word had no weight in it at all.

He reached across the fire and took both her hands. They were shaking. So were his.

"When I was twelve," he said, "there was a cart horse in Hollowvale with a bone splinter in her hoof, and no one could touch her, and I sat with her for two days talking nonsense until she let me pull it out. And the whole time I kept thinking — this is it, this is the best thing I will ever do, and no one will ever know, because knowing would ruin it." His throat tightened. "I spent my whole life loving a place that couldn't know me. And then I met you, and you knew me inside a week — the gift, the fear, all of it — and you weren't afraid. Do you understand what that did to me? I've been yours since the gate, Sable. Maybe since the ewe. I just didn't have the courage to spend words on it, because everything I love gets taken to an altar eventually."

"Then don't give anything to any altar," she whispered. "Give it to me."

He kissed her, and the fire leaned, and above them through the broken roof the stars wheeled on indifferent, and below them on the black water the hundred small flames drifted and drifted — and among them, Wendell noticed with the last rational part of his mind, the candles had stopped drifting. All hundred of them had arranged themselves into a single line, pointing out through the chapel door, into the night.

Toward the moors.

Toward something coming.

*He knows where you are now,* said the water gently, all around them, everywhere at once. *My brother has always known. Rest while you can, keystone. Dawn will bring the hunt, and you must reach the Altar before the moon turns — or choose, before witnesses, which of you the Hollow One will drink instead.*

The candleflames guttered, all together, like a drawn breath.

Outside, far off across the drowned fields, something began to sing.
