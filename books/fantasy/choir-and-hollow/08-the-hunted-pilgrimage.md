## 8. The Hunted Pilgrimage

Sable learned the Hollow One's coming by the silence of birds.

Not their fleeing — she had grown up in a shepherd's household and knew how a hawk emptied a field, the sudden hush, then the scatter. This was different. The birds did not scatter. They simply stopped, mid-song, mid-wingbeat, as though someone had drawn a line through the air and everything on one side of it had been erased from the world's hearing.

The first time it happened, three days out from the drowned chapel, she thought her ears had failed her. The moor went on making its small sounds — wind combing the heather, peat-water sucking at Wendell's boots, Maren's rosary clicking between her fingers like a beetle turning over. Then the birdsong cut off all at once, a seam ripped clean, and Sable felt the absence move across the hillside toward them like cold water finding its level.

"It's hunting," Maren said. She didn't stop walking. Her voice was flat as slate. "It hunts by listening for answered prayers. Every prayer that gets an answer leaves a trail — warmth on a cold road. We're not being followed, girl. We're *smelled*."

"Then we don't pray," Sable said.

Maren laughed, and there was no kindness in it. "You've never been afraid enough to pray, then. Wait."

She was right, of course. That night, sheltering under a leaning slab of granite while sleet needled sideways, Sable caught herself with her lips moving — *keep him safe, keep him safe* — before she remembered. She pressed her mouth shut until it hurt. Beside her, Wendell slept with his head against a ewe he'd talked into lying down beside them, some borrowed word of comfort still working in its old bones. His gift hummed off him even in sleep. She could feel it now, faintly, like heat off a hearthstone. The whole world could feel it soon, if the gods kept waking. And every hungry thing out here in the dark was learning what he was.

She lay awake counting his breaths and hating the story they were walking inside of. Because she had read the shape of it already, the way you read weather in a sheep's coat. Stories like this one wanted a sacrifice. Stories like this one always reached first for the one who could speak to gods — and everyone around him became either an obstacle or a stepping-stone. Maren, who had given her whole life to a church that burned her name off its rolls. Vesper, who had waited centuries and spoke of Wendell in a voice like a man describing the last light he'd ever see. Even the covenant itself, that great rotten thing they were hauling toward repair, seemed to regard Sable Almeida as furniture.

Well. She had been furniture all her life. The shepherd's daughter who stayed. The niece nobody wrote to. She knew exactly how much weight furniture carried, and she had decided, somewhere on the wet black moor with her mouth pressed shut against prayer, that she would not be it any longer.

* * *

They crossed the River Sorrel on the fourth day, where it ran shallow and loud over red stones, and that was where the moor began to fight them.

Sable noticed it before the others: the path bending. Not visibly — nothing so kind as visible. But an hour after the crossing she realized they were walking downhill when the map in her head said up, and the heather had changed color twice without her seeing it change, and the sun stood in the wrong quarter of the sky and had done so, apparently, for some time.

"Maren."

"I see it." Maren had gone very still, her face tilted like a woman listening to a room she suspected held someone breathing. "It's not chasing us anymore. It's herding us." She spat, an old soldier's habit incongruous in a priestess. "Unanswered prayers are its food, do you understand? It wants us afraid. It wants us begging at a god who won't come. There's a shrine ahead — I can feel it, dead and dry as a well — and it's steering us there so we can stand in front of it and ask and ask and be *refused*, and it will drink every drop of that."

"So we don't go to the shrine," Sable said.

"The shrine is the only way across the Fells before dark." Maren's jaw worked. "It knows the ground better than we do. Everything out here is its ground now."

Wendell had stopped walking. He was looking east, at nothing Sable could see, and his hand was half-raised, palm open, the way he held it toward frightened animals. "There's something else on the moor," he said quietly. "Something moving. Not it. Smaller. A lot of smaller things, running together, like—" He frowned, listening to whatever channel the rest of them were deaf in. "Like a flock. But wrong. They're singing."

"Singing," Maren said.

"Praying," Wendell corrected himself, and Sable watched the color leave his face. "Maren. The animals are praying. To me."

The wind came down off the Fells then, and it smelled wrong — scorched, mineral, like the air after lightning, though no storm showed anywhere. Far behind them, back along their own trail, the birdsong died again. Closer than before. Much closer.

Maren made her decision the way she seemed to make all of them, instantly and without consulting anyone whose opinion she hadn't already dismissed. "The flock goes north to the standing stones; the stones are old enough to hold a little holiness even now. You'll take the animals' prayer there — that's your work, that's what you're *for*. I'll take the shrine road."

"No," Sable said.

"It's not a request, girl—"

"You're not doing this." Sable heard her own voice come out low and hard, a stranger's voice, and found she didn't mind. "I know what you're doing. I've watched you do it since the chapel. Every mile you've been spending yourself like coin because you think that's all a disgraced priestess is good for — buying time for the real chosen one. Well, I'm not the chosen one either, and I'm telling you: it doesn't get fed today. Not with you. Not with anyone."

Maren turned on her, ash-grey eyes furious. "And what would you have us do, shepherdess? Stand in the open and sing hymns?"

"Yes," Sable said.

The word surprised all three of them. But once it was out, she saw the shape of it, the way she saw weather. "It eats unanswered prayers. So we answer each other." She grabbed Wendell's arm. "When I pray — when any of us prays — someone answers. Out loud. Here. Now. It can't drink what doesn't go unanswered. We make ourselves into—" she groped for it, and Wendell gave it to her, eyes going wide—

"—a choir," he breathed. "A covenant in miniature."

Maren stared at them both, and something moved behind the fury in her face, something older and hungrier and terribly like hope. "That's either the bravest thing I've ever heard," she said, "or the stupidest."

"We're alive," Sable said. "So far it's been both."

* * *

They sang on the open moor with the dark thing coming, and Sable would remember it all her life the way you remember a birth or a burning.

It was not beautiful. Maren's voice was a cracked bell; Wendell sang like a man who had spent his life whispering to beasts and didn't know his own volume. Sable herself couldn't carry a tune in a bucket. But they prayed aloud, turn about, and answered each prayer aloud, plainly, immediately, and the answering made a sound in the world that the Hollow One could not swallow. *Keep us safe* — *you are safe, I'm beside you.* *Let us cross the Fells* — *the path is here, take my hand.* Small prayers. Poor answers. A human covenant, stitched together out of breath and stubbornness.

The darkness came anyway. Of course it came. It broke over the ridge like a tide of unbeing, and Sable saw it then — not a shape but an absence shaped like a hunger, a hole wearing the memory of wings — and it fell upon their choir and found

nothing.

No unanswered prayer to drink. Their words kept landing, kept being caught, kept passing hand to hand like embers in a gale, and the Hollow One circled them shrieking — a soundless shriek, a shriek in the negative, felt in the teeth and the marrow — and could not feed.

But it could still take.

It struck Maren as she stepped between it and Wendell, because of course she did, because she was Maren, because she had been walking toward her own sacrifice since the drowned chapel and Sable had simply delayed her, not saved her. One moment the priestess was singing, voice cracking on an answered psalm; the next the absence closed around her like a fist, and the last thing she did — the very last — was smile at Sable, fierce and approving, and throw her rosary.

It landed at Sable's feet. The beads glowed like coals. Somewhere inside them, Sable understood, was everything Maren had ever believed, hoarded against this hour.

"*Run,*" Maren's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, stretched thin as wire. "North. The stones. Finish it. Girl — *shepherdess* — you were never furniture. None of us ever were. Make them see it—"

Then the dark took her, folded her up like a letter, and was gone south with its prize, and the moor rang with ordinary wind again, and Sable stood holding warm beads in a cold rain and refused, absolutely refused, to weep.

"We follow it," she said.

"Sable—" Wendell's face was wrecked with it, guilt and grief and the terrible arithmetic already starting behind his eyes — *one of us will pay, one of us must* — and she cut it off at the root.

"Don't." She took his face in her hands. His skin was freezing. "Don't start deciding what you'll give up. I know that look. Vesper taught it to you, the story taught it to you, every god and priest and prophecy has been teaching it to you since the valley gate: the speaker gives his voice, the lover gives her heartbreak, everyone lines up to be noble and I'm supposed to hold the coats." Her thumb traced his cheekbone. "I love you. Hear me. I am not holding anyone's coats. We walk north together, we finish this together, and when the bill comes due at that altar, we *negotiate*."

Behind them, far off across the darkening moor, the blind mule's flock crested a hill — a hundred beasts, maybe more, drawn by the pull of a beast-speaker's grief, standing silent in a row like mourners at a graveside. Waiting for him to speak.

Waiting, Sable realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the rain, for *her* too. The mule's milky eyes were fixed not on Wendell at all.

On the rosary in her fist.

"They want you to lead," Wendell whispered.

"No," Sable said, and closed her hand around the glowing beads, and felt Maren's faith pulse against her palm like a second heartbeat. "They want what's in these. And I think—" She looked north, where the standing stones waited grey against the last light, and beyond them, at the horizon's edge, a shape of deeper dark was already circling back. Hunting. Patient. Hungry.

"—I think the Hollow One made a mistake taking a priestess," she said. "Because priests come with prayers attached. And I intend to find out exactly what she left behind in my hand."

The flock began to move. North. Toward the stones. Toward the altar where the bill waited, and the dark thing wheeling above it like a hole in the sky.

Sable walked first.
