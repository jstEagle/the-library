## 9. The Hollow Altar

They smelled it before they saw it — the Hollow Altar, breathing out its long exhalation of cold stone and old blood and something sweeter beneath, like apples rotting in a cellar no one had opened in a hundred years. The path up the mountain had been silent for three days. No birds. Even the wind moved carefully, as though it too were being hunted.

Wendell climbed with Sable's hand in his, and Vesper walked behind them like a shadow that had learned to carry weight.

The mountain's crown was a bowl of black granite worn smooth as a tongue. And there at its center lay the Altar itself: not the raised slab Wendell had imagined from Maren's stories, but a hollow — a great cup-shaped depression in the living rock, wide enough to hold a man, deep enough to swallow him. Rainwater had gathered in it over centuries, black and perfectly still, a mirror turned up at the sky.

The sky did not reflect in it.

That was the first wrongness. The water held only darkness, though dawn was breaking gold and merciless over the eastern peaks, gilding every stone but this one.

"It drinks light," Vesper said quietly. "It always has. That is what altars are for."

Wendell knelt at the rim and looked down into the stillness. His own face should have been there. Instead he saw — faintly, as if through deep water — a choir. Shapes standing in ranks that receded farther than the hollow could possibly be deep, gods and beasts and burning mortal silhouettes, all of them leaning toward the surface as if listening.

"They're waiting," Sable whispered. "All of them. They've been waiting since before my grandmother's grandmother."

"For a voice," Wendell said. The words came out of him flat and strange, as if the mountain were speaking through his mouth already. "For someone willing to stand where the silence is."

* * *

Vesper told them the truth of it while the sun climbed and refused to touch the altar's water.

A covenant is not a contract written down. It is sung — continuously, endlessly, by a voice placed at the hinge between realms. The first voice had been a god who grew tired of godhood and chose to become the song instead. When that voice frayed, another took it up. And when the last beast-speaker stood on this stone, three generations ago, she heard the full price of the singing.

"To keep the covenant," Vesper said, "the voice must be willing to be silenced. Not mute. Silenced — unmade as a self, poured out into the answering of prayers forever. She would never again be anyone. She would only be *yes*. She looked into the hollow and saw herself dissolving into usefulness, and she said no. She fled. She built walls around a valley and called them mercy." The old god's voice cracked like drought earth. "I have never blamed her. But her refusal is why your gift is a secret, why the beasts went quiet, why prayers fell into the dark and fed the thing that waits below."

Sable's grip tightened on Wendell's hand until the bones ground together. "And if he refuses too?"

"Then the Hollow One finishes eating the world one unanswered prayer at a time," Vesper said simply. "Or we find another way, which I do not have. Or—" and here the god looked at Wendell with an expression so ancient and so tender it hurt worse than any threat, "—or someone else pays."

"I pay," Wendell said.

"No," said Sable.

"Sable—"

"*No.*" She stepped between him and the hollow, small and mortal and blazing. "You've been chosen your whole life, Wendell Marchetti. Chosen by blood, chosen by beasts, chosen by a god. Everyone keeps deciding what you're for. Well, I'm deciding too, and I decide you don't get to disappear." She turned to face the altar, jaw set the way it set when she argued with her father about the winter slaughter. "I'm mortal. My life ends anyway — sixty years, seventy if I'm stubborn, and then dark. What's mortality against forever? Take mine. Let me be the silence. At least I'd know what I was buying with it."

The water in the hollow trembled. Somewhere far below, something vast shifted its attention toward her like a wolf turning its head.

"No," Vesper said, and the word came out of him with the weight of mountains settling. "There is a third price, and I have carried it up this mountain hoping I would be brave enough to name it." He spread his hands — hands that had held drowned chapels and dead pilgrims' last breaths, hands older than the valley's walls. "A god's ending. All of me. Not death — gods don't die — but *dissolution*. Poured into the hinge the way the first voice was. I remember everything, Wendell. Ten thousand years of remembering, and most of it grief, because I remembered your bloodline when even your bloodline forgot itself." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Wendell had ever seen. "Let me stop remembering. Let me be the song. It is the only rest I will ever be offered, and I find — now that I stand here — that I want it."

Three sacrifices, standing in the dawn. A boy's self. A girl's life. A god's eternity.

And the hollow waited, drinking the light, patient as hunger.

* * *

Wendell walked to the rim of the altar and looked down into the choir of waiting shapes, and he understood — finally, fully — what everyone had been asking of him. Not courage. Surrender. Every version of the bargain ended the same way: someone beloved poured out, erased, made into an instrument. Sable's warmth gone from the world. Vesper's memory scattered into mindless harmony. Himself, emptied into eternal usefulness, a yes that had once been a boy who talked to sheep.

The valley had taught him this lesson his whole life without ever saying it aloud. *Hide what you are. Shrink. Survive.* And now the whole world had grown up around the same lesson, a covenant built on the idea that love meant finding someone else to feed to the silence.

"No," Wendell said.

The word fell into the hollow and the water shivered across its whole black surface.

"I won't take Sable's life. I won't take Vesper's memory. I won't give up myself. Every bargain on this table costs someone everything, and that's not a covenant, that's just the valley writ large — safety bought by burying somebody." His voice was shaking, and he let it shake. "You're all offering to be sacrificed because none of you can imagine a covenant that doesn't require it. So I'll write a new one."

"You can't," Vesper said gently. "The rite requires—"

"The rite requires a voice willing to be silenced. Fine." Wendell stepped up onto the altar's rim, barefoot, the cold of the granite climbing his shins like rising water. "But it doesn't say whose voice. And it doesn't say *one* voice."

He looked back at Sable — at her fierce, terrified, mortal face — and at Vesper, ancient and grieving and ready to end.

"You told me once the covenant bound gods, beasts, and mortals in mutual answerability. Then it rotted into hierarchy — one voice above, everyone else begging below. That's how it broke. Not with her refusal. With the loneliness built into it." He crouched, laid his palm flat against the black water. It was warm. It had been waiting so long it had gone feverish. "So here is my price: I'll stand at the hinge. But I won't be silenced alone. I'll be answered. Gods answering mortals. Mortals answering beasts. Beasts answering gods. A choir, not a throne — and every voice in it gets to say *no*, sometimes, or it isn't a covenant, it's a cage."

The water began to rise.

"Wendell—" Sable started forward.

"Take my hand," he said. "Both of you. If we sing, we sing together or not at all."

* * *

He never learned whether it would have worked.

Because the mountain screamed.

It came up through the rock itself, a sound like ten thousand prayers dying mid-word, and the sky over the eastern peaks tore open along a seam that had always been there, hidden in the blue. Out of the tear poured the Hollow One — not a shape but an absence wearing shapes, a hunger that borrowed the outlines of everything it had eaten: a shepherd's coat, a child's silhouette, the suggestion of wings it had stripped from some minor god of sparrows. It flowed down the mountainside faster than anything living could run, and where it passed, the granite grayed and crumbled into ash that smelled of unanswered hope.

It hit the altar's edge and stopped — stopped as if striking glass — and Wendell felt the barrier shudder under his feet. The hollow's power, the waiting choir, holding it back. For now.

"*Little voice,*" it said, in a chorus of every prayer no one had ever answered. "*Come away from the water. Come and be quiet. I have been so hungry, and you are the loudest thing left.*"

Wendell stood his ground on the rim, Sable's hand clamped white-knuckled around his left, Vesper's around his right. Below, the choir of shapes pressed closer to the surface, and he could feel them now, really feel them — thousands upon thousands of voices, gods and beasts and burning mortals, all of them straining toward the hinge, all of them waiting to see what kind of covenant would finally be sung.

The Hollow One pressed against the barrier, and the barrier bowed inward like ice under a boot heel.

"Vesper," Wendell said. "How long do we have?"

The god looked at the thing that had eaten his siblings, his temples, ten thousand years of worship — and smiled the smile of a creature who has finally found the fight he was made for.

"Long enough," Vesper said, "if you start singing *now*."
