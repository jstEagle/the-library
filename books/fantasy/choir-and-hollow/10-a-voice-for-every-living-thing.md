## 10. A Voice for Every Living Thing

The Hollow Altar was not hollow at all.

That was the first thing Wendell understood when the light of Vesper's dying radiance finally settled over it — a slab of black stone veined with silver, worn smooth by centuries of kneeling pilgrims, and beneath it, when he pressed his palm flat against its cold face, a hum like a hive of sleeping bees. The old covenant lived in there still. Rotten, threadbare, but alive. Waiting to be rewritten by whatever voice was brave enough or foolish enough to try.

The price had been laid out before him like cards on a table. His voice. Sable's mortality. Vesper's godhood. Three bargains, three ways to end himself, and he stood at the altar with blood on his knuckles from the climb and ash from Maren's pyre still in his hair, and he said no.

Not the defiant *no* of a boy slamming a door. A quieter thing. The *no* of someone who had spent twenty-three years listening and had finally learned what listening was for.

"No," Wendell said again, aloud, so that every power gathered on that mountaintop could hear it — Vesper fading at the chapel's broken edge, the Hollow One coiled in the storm-dark like a hunger given shape, the beasts of three realms pressed silent into the treeline below. "You're all asking who pays. You've been asking that since before the valley was built. And the answer is: everyone has been paying, a little, for so long that none of you remember what it was for."

The Hollow One stirred. Its voice was the sound of prayers going unanswered — dry leaves, empty wells, a child calling a name that no longer answered back. *"One voice must be silenced. That is the covenant's shape. Break the shape and everything unravels."*

"Then we'll weave a new shape," Wendell said.

He looked at Sable. She stood beside him on the wind-scoured stone with her shepherd's crook — her grandmother's crook, carried out of a valley she would never enter the same way twice — and she did not flinch, did not bargain, did not offer herself up as sacrifice the way she'd tried to a moment before. She just nodded. *Say it,* her eyes said. *I'm here.*

He put his hand on the altar. He opened his gift the way he'd never dared open it in Hollowvale — wide, unguarded, spilling out of him like water through a breached weir — and he began to speak.

Not to one beast. Not to one god. To *all* of them.

*Hear me,* he said in the borrowed grammar of wolves and wrens, in the slow stone-speech of mules, in the high silver frequencies that only gods could parse. *The old covenant asked one voice to be silent so the rest could be safe. I am the last of those voices, and I refuse. Instead: let every voice answer to every other. Let the gods hear the sheep. Let the sheep question their shepherds. Let mortals hear what they have done to the world and be unable to pretend otherwise. No more silence purchased with one throat. Only this — that none of us may close our ears again.*

The altar drank the words. Silver climbed the black stone like frost blooming in fast time, and the hum beneath his palm became a chord, became a choir, became —

Became everything answering at once.

Wendell had spent his life speaking to beasts one at a time, a private conversation in a hidden language, an ache with no audience. Now the audience arrived all together. The moor grass sang in voices too small for human throats but audible now, suddenly, to every mortal ear within a hundred miles. Somewhere far below, a river god woke confused and asked its first question in eight hundred years: *who is upstream of me now?* The ewes of Hollowvale, three valleys away, lifted their heads at dawn and spoke to their shepherd in plain words, and the old man dropped his crook and sat down hard in the frost and wept without knowing why.

And the Hollow One —

The Hollow One screamed. It was made of unanswered prayer, and Wendell's covenant answered *it.*

*"No,"* it howled, thrashing against the storm. *"I was promised exile. I was promised the dark. That is my portion, that is the shape—"*

"That was the old shape," Wendell said, and his voice cracked with exhaustion and something dangerously close to pity. "You fed on silence because silence was all anyone gave you. Come to the choir instead."

It came. Not gently — nothing about the Hollow One was gentle, and it descended on the altar like a landslide of grief, and for one terrible moment Wendell felt it trying to hollow *him*, reaching for the quiet places in his chest where every unanswered prayer of his own life had pooled. But Sable's hand found his. Vesper's last light wrapped them both. And the Hollow One, meeting for the first time a voice that refused to look away from it, faltered.

It was very small, in the end. Small and ragged and starving, the way abandoned things are. It folded itself into the new covenant the way a feral animal finally enters a lit doorway — braced for the blow that didn't come.

The wards around Hollowvale, three valleys away, did not crack that night. They simply opened, like hands unclenching after holding something too long, and did not close again.

* * *

They went back, of course. Once. In spring, when the mountain passes thawed.

Hollowvale had changed less than Wendell feared and more than he expected. The same slate roofs. The same terraces climbing the walls of the valley like green staircases. But the gate — the old warded gate where a traveler had died and a prayer had been audibly answered, cracking three generations of silence like river ice — stood open. Stood *unlocked*. Wildflowers grew through the threshold stones, and a hedgehog had taken up residence beneath the lintel and greeted them, unprompted, in a voice like dry leaves: *about time somebody came home.*

His grandmother met them at the door of the family house. She was smaller than Wendell remembered, or perhaps he had grown. She looked at him a long time — at the silver threaded now through his irises, a mark the covenant left on its makers — and then she did the thing he had never once seen her do.

She bowed her head.

"You swore me to silence," Wendell said. His voice was rough.

"I swore you to survival," she corrected. "There's a difference, and I was too frightened, all those years, to teach it to you." Her gaze went to Sable, standing at his shoulder with dirt on her boots and Maren's crook slung across her back. "Your aunt would have liked this," she told Sable. "She always thought the wards were a cage. They called her mad for it." She stepped aside. "Come in. Your mother kept your room."

They stayed nine days. Long enough for the ewes to tell Wendell which fields were going fallow. Long enough for his grandmother to sit by the fire and listen — truly listen, for the first time — to a wren explain the weather. Long enough for Wendell to understand that the valley had never stopped being home; it had only ever stopped being honest.

On the ninth day, a delegation arrived from the road of small gods — villagers from the bargaining towns, led by a girl of maybe twelve who could now hear her late father's voice in the apple trees he'd planted. They wanted to learn. Word had spread, as word does, of the beast-speaker who rewrote the covenant, of the priestess who died holding a gate against the dark, of the god who chose to end so that mortals could begin.

"We can't stay here," Wendell told Sable that night, lying in his narrow childhood bed with the ceiling he'd stared at through a hundred sleepless years. "They'll keep coming. And the valley —"

"— was built to hide things," Sable finished. "It doesn't know how to hold a school." She turned her head against the pillow to look at him. In the firelight her eyes were the brown of turned earth, of beginnings. "So we build one somewhere else. Between the realms. Where the roads cross."

"The drowned chapel," Wendell said slowly. "Vesper's chapel. It's still standing. And Vesper — what's left of Vesper — sleeps under the floorstones. There are worse neighbors for a school than a god learning to be small."

"There are no better ones," Sable said, and laughed, and pulled him close.

* * *

The school of the crossing took three years to build and would take three lifetimes to finish, which was, Sable observed, rather the point of a school.

They raised it on the ruins of the drowned chapel, draining the holy floodwater into gardens where the fish-gods' descendants taught children to breathe prayer like air. The walls were stone from the Hollow Altar's mountain, donated by a mountain that had, to everyone's alarm, developed opinions on architecture. The bell in the tower was cast from Maren's crook, melted down with her name spoken into the metal, so that every morning it rang out across the moors and every morning, faintly, it sounded like her voice saying *get up, you lazy miracle, there's work to do.*

Students came from everywhere. Shepherd-children who could suddenly hear their flocks and didn't know whether to rejoice or run. Young gods, newly diminished and newly curious, learning to walk among mortals without demanding worship. One solemn badger who had opinions on ethics. Even, once a season, a gray ragged figure that smelled of empty wells and cold hearths, who sat at the back of the class and said nothing and was never turned away, because the covenant's first lesson — Wendell's lesson, paid for with a valley's safety — was that no voice gets silenced, not even the hungry ones.

Some nights, Wendell walked the moors alone, the way he used to walk the valley paths, and listened to the world talking. All of it talking. Grass and granite, god and goat, the wind arguing theology with itself. The noise of it should have been unbearable. Instead it was the sound he'd been aching toward his whole life: belonging, vast and unsanitized and loud.

Sable found him on one such night, up on the ridge above the school, where you could see the lights of Hollowvale far off in its open, unwarded bowl — no longer a refuge, no longer a prison, just a place among places, its people trading freely now with the road towns, afraid some mornings and glad most of them.

"They're lighting the festival fires early," she said, settling against his shoulder.

"Mm. The ewes told me. They want the whole valley to come sing to them this year. Apparently last year's singing was, quote, 'adequate.'"

"Adequate," Sable repeated gravely. "Criticism from livestock. This is the world you made, Wendell Marchetti."

"This is the world we made," he said, "and yes, and I'd pay it again — the safety, the silence, all of it. Every day. Gladly."

Below them the school windows glowed gold. Inside, somewhere, a young god was probably failing arithmetic while a badger tutored her in humility. The bell that was also Maren waited for morning. The Hollow One's hearth-fire burned low and steady in the east wing, tended by students who had learned that feeding a hunger with company works better than feeding it with fear.

Far away, under the floorstones of what was now a library, something ancient and kind dreamed its small dreams, and if you pressed your ear to the stone you could hear it humming along with the choir.

The valley gates stood open behind them. The road ran open ahead.

Neither of them, ever again, closed a door.
