## 1. The Smuggler's Ledger

Winter came to the Hollow Gate the way debt comes to a poor man — slowly, then all at once. By the first week of Frostmoon the pass had closed behind Rosalind Dravenmoor like a held breath finally released, and the world narrowed to what she could see from her window: black stone, white snow, and the door.

The door was forty feet of seamless basalt set into the mountainside, without handle, hinge, or keyhole, and it had not opened in fifteen years. She knew this the way she knew her own hands. She had counted its silence the way other women counted beads.

Below her, in the keeper's house that clung to the cliff like a barnacle, her deputy Tam Voss was doing the accounts. She could hear him through the floorboards — the scratch of his nib, the small grunt he made when a number displeased him. He was a good boy, Tam. Twenty-four, honest in a way that occasionally inconvenienced her, and devoted to the ledger the way novices are devoted to scripture: faithfully, and without understanding what any of it meant.

The ledger was the whole of her office now. Once the keepers of the Hollow Gate had been priests, soldiers, scholars of the sealed thing behind the stone. Now they were customs agents for a road that went nowhere. Three times a year the wool merchants of the northern valleys tried to run their bales through the pass because the mountain route saved nine days over the coast road, and three times a year Rosalind turned them back, took her duty-tax, and wrote the sums in a book no one would ever read.

*Entry 214: two wagons, felt and tallow, turned at the gate. Duty paid: four crowns, one bottle of plum brandy (accepted in lieu of coin).*

Tam thought the brandy entries were a joke on the merchants. They were, but not the one he imagined. The brandy went down the well, poured out over the water, and Rosalind never asked herself too closely why she still performed the rite, only that some mornings the not-asking was the hardest work she did.

She wrapped her cloak around her shoulders and went down to make breakfast, because dread is easier to carry when your hands are busy, and she had woken with dread sitting on her chest like a cat. Not the ordinary winter dread — the low gray animal mood of short days and longer nights — but something sharper underneath, the feeling of a word forgotten on the tip of the tongue. The mountain had been quiet all night. Too quiet. Even the wind, which usually came down the pass at dusk like an argumentative relative, had failed to arrive.

"More snow coming," Tam said when she came into the kitchen. He had the ledger open beside his porridge, which she tolerated because the alternative was taking it away, and a deputy who loved his ledger was worth a spilled bowl or two. "The mule drivers at the waystation say the sky over the peak's gone the color of a bruise."

"The mule drivers," said Rosalind, "would know." She set the kettle on and looked out the window at the gate, veiled in falling flakes. "Any traffic yesterday?"

"One peddler, half-frozen, going the wrong direction. I sent him back to Thornhollow with a warning about the pass." Tam licked his thumb and turned a page. "He kept looking at the door like it might look back."

"It does that," Rosalind said, and was pleased at how light she made it sound.

That was the trick of the place, the trick she had perfected over fifteen years: everything true could be made to sound like a joke, if you said it flatly enough. *The gate hums before storms. The snow near the door never settles right. I have not dreamed in fifteen years.* Say them like inventory and people laughed, uneasily, and stopped asking. Competence was a kind of armor. You wore it until people mistook it for you.

The kettle screamed. She poured the water over the leaves and stood with her back to the window, and that was why she saw Tam's face change before she heard anything at all.

"Keeper," he said. Not *Rosalind*, not the familiar *mum* he'd slipped into sometime last spring. *Keeper.* His spoon had stopped halfway to his mouth. "There's someone out there."

She turned.

The snow had thickened while her back was turned, the world beyond the window reduced to gray-on-gray, and against that blankness stood a figure by the gate itself — small, dark, impossibly still. A child's silhouette, Rosalind thought first, though the thought made no sense; there were no children within thirty miles of the Hollow Gate, no reason at all for one to be standing bare-headed in a blizzard at the sealed end of the world.

"Fetch the blankets," Rosalind said. Her voice came out steady. Good. Let it be steady all the way through whatever this was. "And the brandy from the third shelf — the medicinal bottle, not the good one. Cold that deep kills from the feet up."

They went out into the white together, Tam stumbling, Rosalind walking the way she always walked toward the gate — as if approaching a sleeping animal, weight even, breath shallow. The cold bit through her gloves. The child did not move.

Eight years old, perhaps. A girl, thin as a winter branch, in a shift the color of old milk that no blizzard should have left so dry. Barefoot on the snow. And here was the thing Rosalind would try, later, to explain and fail: the girl's feet had left no prints. Behind her the snow lay smooth as fresh paper, and around her ankles it lay undisturbed, and she stood on the surface of the drifts as though snow were simply another kind of floor.

Her eyes were open and calm. She looked at Rosalind without fear, without wonder, with the mild attention of someone checking an appointment against a memory — and then she began to sing.

The voice was thin, a child's untrained pipe, and it should have been nothing. It should have been a lost girl humming against the cold, one more piece of misfortune for the ledger. But the melody rose and fell in a pattern Rosalind's body knew before her mind caught up, the way a scar aches before rain: four notes ascending, a suspension held past comfort, then the fall into the minor third where the second voice should enter, except there was no second voice, there had been no choir in —

Fifteen years.

Fifteen years, and the snow falling upward for half a heartbeat — she would swear it later, only to herself, only once — and the taste of candle smoke in her mouth, and her mother's hand heavy between her shoulder blades, pushing her through a side door, saying words she had spent a decade and a half refusing to remember.

The anthem. The unfinished anthem. The song that stopped mid-phrase because everyone who knew the ending had died in a single burning night.

The girl sang the phrase and let it hang unresolved, exactly as it had hung for fifteen years, and smiled at Rosalind as though passing her a gift.

Rosalind moved. Later she would not remember deciding to; her body simply executed fifteen years of discipline in a single motion — she crossed the drifts, stripped off her own cloak, and wrapped it around the child, gathering her up, one arm under her knees and one around her back, lifting her clean off the snow. The girl weighed almost nothing. She smelled of nothing at all, no woodsmoke, no sweat, no fear — the absence of smell, the absence of prints, the absence of everything except the song still dying sweetly on her lips.

"Hush now," Rosalind said, in the common tongue, loud enough for Tam to hear and understand. "Hush, you're frozen through, we'll warm you." Loud enough, and shaped carefully, so that what she carried inside sounded like rescue and nothing else.

But her heart was saying, in a language of falling thirds and suspended fourths: *No. No, no, it burned, I watched it burn, you are not possible.*

* * *

The kitchen filled with steam and the small efficient chaos of warming a chilled child: blankets from the cedar chest, boots of Tam's to swallow her bare feet, broth laced with the medicinal brandy. The girl submitted to all of it with the same mild patience, watching Rosalind's hands work, and said nothing. When Rosalind pressed two fingers gently under the girl's jaw she found a pulse, slow and even, like a sleeper's.

"She can't have walked here," Tam said, keeping his voice low across the table. "No tracks. Keeper, there weren't tracks anywhere on the pass, I checked twice on my rounds yesterday and there's been no one since the peddler—"

"Lost parties wander onto the glacier road sometimes," Rosalind said. "The ice field plays tricks. She may have come further than we can guess and remembers less." She heard herself say it, smooth as a merchant's patter, and marveled a little at the craft of it, the fluency of a lie told to a face that trusted her. Fifteen years of practice. "We'll send word to Thornhollow when the weather lifts. Someone will come for her."

"Should I write her in the ledger?"

The question hung there. *Entry 215: one child, arrived out of impossible snow, singing the funeral music of a country that does not exist.*

"Note her as a foundling, recovered from the pass," Rosalind said. "Condition: hypothermia, exposure. No possessions. Mute, so far as we can tell."

The girl looked up at that — directly at her, with those calm gray eyes, and the corner of her mouth curved very slightly, and Rosalind understood, with a certainty that dropped through her like a stone through still water, that the child was not mute at all. That she was choosing silence. That she had heard every word.

"And the singing?" Tam said. "I heard her, out there. Couldn't make out the words. Some southern dialect, maybe? Sounded old."

Older than south, Tam. Older than the maps.

"Fell hymnody," Rosalind said. "Mountain villages still use bits of it for lullabies. My mother sang fragments of it; I'd know the style anywhere." True, every word, and arranged like glass shards in a wall to keep him from reaching what lay behind them. "It means nothing. It means she came from somewhere in the high valleys, and someone taught her the old songs, and the cold addled her wits. Write it down."

Tam wrote it down. He had beautiful penmanship and no idea, none at all, that he was recording the beginning of the end of fifteen years of quiet, and Rosalind watched his nib move and felt the dread in her chest finally resolve into its true shape, which was not dread at all but recognition — the terrible relief of a hunted animal hearing the hounds, because now, at last, she knew which direction to run.

The girl finished her broth. Set down the bowl precisely. And then, looking at the ledger, at Tam's neat descending columns, she reached out one small finger and touched the page — and where her fingertip rested, for just a moment before she withdrew it, the ink shimmered as though seen through water.

On the far side of the room, unnoticed, unremarked, the black basalt of the Hollow Gate gave off a single low tone, like a bell struck once under the sea.

Rosalind alone heard it. She kept her face still, and poured the tea, and began — calmly, competently, lying with every stroke — to plan.
