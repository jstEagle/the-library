## 2. What the Child Said

The snow kept falling for three days after the girl arrived, and Rosalind kept her in the gatehouse all three days, telling herself it was policy. Strays in winter were a hazard. They froze on the pass, or they wandered onto the ice of the Hollowmere, or they were bait — and the Hollow Gate had seen enough bait in fifteen years that Rosalind had stopped counting. You fed them, warmed them, sent them down to the valley mission with a coin in their pocket and a warning in their ears. That was the procedure. Procedure was how you survived a posting like this without going strange.

She fed the girl barley porridge with honey she could not afford. She warmed her in the deputy's spare blanket, the good one, wool from the southern looms. And on the second morning, when she set a slate and a stub of chalk in front of her and said, slowly, the way you talk to anyone the words won't reach — *can you tell me your name* — the girl had looked at the chalk the way other children looked at fire.

Then she had drawn a street.

Rosalind stood in the doorway of the gatehouse kitchen with a kettle going cold in her hand and watched the chalk move. The girl drew fast, sure, the way a person draws a thing they have walked a thousand times and never had to think about. A street, wide, cobbled in a fan pattern. Buildings leaning in over it, upper storeys jutting, timber-framed, with iron brackets where lanterns would hang. A fountain at the far end. Arches. A bridge beyond the arches with a tower on it, and the tower was wrong — no, the tower was *right*, and that was the wrongness, because Rosalind knew that tower, knew the way its shadow fell across the water at evening, knew the bell in it and the man who rang the bell and the smell of the tanneries downstream when the wind came off the hills, and she knew all of it from a city that had been ash for fifteen years.

The kettle was burning her hand. She set it down without looking, and the girl glanced up at her — quick, bird-quick, those grey eyes — and went back to drawing. A second street off the first. A stair cut into a wall. A door, and above the door a sign: a wren in a circle of thorns.

*The Wren and Thorn.* A tavern on Candlemakers' Row, three streets from the Choir Square. Rosalind had drunk elderflower wine there the night before her voice broke, the night her mother first let her sing the descant, and the tavern-keeper's wife had pressed a copper into her palm and said *for luck, little bird*, and the copper had been warm from her apron.

The chalk squeaked. Rosalind made herself breathe.

"Where did you see this?" she said, and her voice came out steady, which cost her something. "Who taught you these streets?"

The girl shook her head. Not *I don't know* — Rosalind had raised a deputy's daughter through her terrible threes and knew every flavor of a child's headshake. This was *you are asking the wrong question*. The girl tapped the fountain with one chalk-white fingertip, then touched her own chest.

"You live there."

The girl nodded.

"It's snowing there."

The girl looked at the window, at the real snow falling past it, and then back at the slate, and her small face did something complicated — a shrug, almost, but with grief in it, the way you shrug when someone asks about weather in a country you can't go home to. She drew snow on the slate. Then, after a moment, she drew it falling *upward*, flakes rising off the cobbles into the grey sky like sparks off a fire run backward, and looked at Rosalind with an expression of perfect, patient reasonableness, as if to say: *well, obviously.*

Rosalind sat down across from her because her legs had opinions of their own.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She sat in her narrow room above the gatehouse with a single candle and let the memories come the way you let a dog come back to you after it has run off — carefully, with your hand out flat, ready for it to be a wolf instead.

She had been nineteen. She had been the choir-keeper's daughter, which in Vaelith meant something; the choirs were not decoration there, they were *infrastructure*, they held the harvest blessings and the bridge-oaths and the long anthem that was sung once a generation to renew the wards along the borders, and her mother had trained her from the cradle to take her place in it. She remembered the cathedral's acoustic the way other people remembered their mother's heartbeat. She remembered the smell of the resin they rubbed on the bow-strings. She remembered the last night — she had spent fifteen years not remembering it and now it came up her throat like floodwater — the last night, the sky the color of a bruise, the anthem started and *not finished*, the high voices cut off mid-phrase when the towers came down, and her mother's hand shoving her toward a postern door in the dark, and the words her mother had said, the bargain Rosalind had made with a man whose face she had never clearly seen, *you will live, and you will not look back, and you will not speak of it, and the gate will hold because you will hold it* —

She remembered burning. That was the thing she had kept, all these years, the one stone she'd carried out of the rubble: the capital burned, and she had watched it burn from the far side of the pass, and she had not gone back. She could still see the light of it on the snow.

And the girl had drawn that city's streets from memory, in a hand that knew them, and drawn snow falling upward in it.

Rosalind got the knife out of the bottom of her trunk. It was a gate-warden's knife, plain, single-edged, the kind you used for cutting tack and kindling and, once in a great and ugly while, something that had needed cutting. She had not slept with it since her first winter here, when she'd been twenty-two and still flinching at wind-sounds. She put it under the edge of her mattress, handle out, and lay down, and listened to the girl breathing in the room next door — a small, even, untroubled sound, the breathing of a child who was not afraid of anything, which was its own kind of terrifying.

Children were afraid of the dark. Children were afraid of loud noises and strange dogs and the space under beds. This child had walked out of a blizzard barefoot and unafraid, had sung in a dead language in the yard of a fortified gate, and now drew impossible streets with the calm of a cartographer.

*The gate is leaking,* said a voice in Rosalind that was not her mother's and not her own, or maybe was her own, fifteen years older and colder. *And something came through.*

She slept at last near dawn, and dreamed of the fountain on Candlemakers' Row, and in the dream the water in it was singing.

* * *

On the third day the weather broke, and with it came traffic. The pass south opened for the first time in a week, and the first sledges through were the salt merchants out of Grayharbor, four of them, drivers wrapped to the eyes, runners hissing on the packed snow. Rosalind met them at the gate with the ledger, because that was the job: tolls, tariffs, the manifest, the small necessary graft of a border post in winter. The girl sat in the corner of the gatehouse by the stove with the slate, drawing, and Rosalind had meant to move her before the merchants came in, had meant to and then — she told herself — had simply been busy.

Marek of Grayharbor came in first, stamping snow, unwinding his scarf, a broad comfortable man Rosalind had known for a decade, who paid his tolls without argument and always brought a twist of dried figs for the deputy's daughter. He was halfway through his greeting when his eyes went past her shoulder and stopped.

Rosalind turned.

The girl had drawn the bridge. The bridge with the tower, and she had drawn the tower in detail now, every arch of its belfry, and she had drawn figures on the bridge — small chalk people, dozens of them, all facing the same direction, all with their mouths open — and above them, rising off the page in a spiral of chalk dust and smudge, she had drawn music. Not notes; she was too young for notation, or it was too old. She had drawn it as a shape, a ribbon of sound winding up into the sky, and she had drawn it the way you draw something you can *see*.

Marek had gone the color of old tallow.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out thin, and he cleared his throat and tried again. "Where did she get that picture."

"You know it?" Rosalind said, and heard her own voice do the same thing his had done.

"No." He was already backing toward the door, hands up, the gesture of a man declining a gift or a fight. "No, I don't know it. Nobody knows it. It's — my grandmother used to hum a tune. She said it was a mourning song. She said we don't sing it. She said the last people who sang it, their whole country went into the ground." He was at the door now, and the other merchants were staring, and the girl was looking up at him with that patient grey gaze, and Rosalind saw that Marek was not looking at the slate anymore. He was looking at the girl. At her feet. Her bare, blue-rimmed, unscarred feet, which had walked out of a blizzard.

"Your toll," Rosalind said. "Marek. Your toll, the manifest —"

"Keep it." The coins hit the floor, not her hand. "Keep the whole winter's take. I'll settle at midwinter. I'm — I have to go."

The door banged. Through the frost-furred window Rosalind watched him fumble his sledge team together with shaking hands, watched the runners bite the snow, watched the salt merchant of Grayharbor — a man who haggled over a half-copper the way other men haggled over their lives — flee his own profit down the pass like a rabbit with the hawk's shadow on it.

The stove ticked. The girl went back to her drawing.

Rosalind stood a long moment with the ledger open in her hands and the unpaid toll scattered on the floorboards, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that Marek's grandmother's mourning song was a song she herself knew every note of. That the country that went into the ground had a name, and she was the only person on this side of the mountains who remembered it, and the child drawing its streets by her stove had never once, in all likelihood, been cold.

She picked up the coins. She banked the stove. And when the deputy came in at dusk, whistling, shaking snow off his coat, asking after the stray and the salt sledges, Rosalind lied to him — smoothly, easily, the way you lie when the muscle is fifteen years strong — and went up to her room, and took the knife from under the mattress, and held it in her lap while the candle burned down, listening to the child next door breathe.

Near midnight, faint through the wall, the girl began to hum.

It was the anthem. The unfinished one. And she was humming the part that came *after* the place where the singing had stopped.
