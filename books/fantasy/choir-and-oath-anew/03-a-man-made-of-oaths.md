## 3. A Man Made of Oaths

He came at the hour when the light goes thin and gray, the hour Rosalind had always privately called the seam of the day, when winter pressed hardest against the gatehouse windows and the world seemed to be holding its breath between one moment and the next.

"Rider on the north road," Pell called from the wall, and there was something in the deputy's voice that made Rosalind set down her pen mid-ledger. "Alone. Carrying a parley banner."

"Smugglers don't carry parley banners," she said, already moving.

"No," Pell agreed. "They don't."

She climbed the wall stairs with the unhurried tread she had practiced for fifteen years, the tread of a woman who had never in her life been afraid of anything that arrived on a road. Below, the rider came on through the falling snow at a walking pace, unhurried as a man going to church. The banner he carried was white, unmarked, and very clean, and somehow that cleanliness was the most frightening thing about him. Nothing in the borderlands stayed clean. Nothing here stayed white. The snow itself arrived gray within an hour of falling, stained by the smoke of the pass and the soot of a hundred small compromises. But the banner was white as a shroud, and the rider beneath it sat his horse as though the cold were a rumor he had declined to believe.

He was a large man, broad through the shoulders, wearing a long coat of dark green so deep it was nearly black, cut in a style Rosalind had not seen since she was a girl — high-collared, silver-clasped, the sleeves embroidered at the cuff with a pattern of interlocking chains. His hair was iron-gray, his face long and composed, and when he drew rein below the wall and looked up, she saw that his eyes were the color of a frozen lake just before it gives way.

"Keeper of the Hollow Gate," he said. His voice carried without effort, pitched as though he had spent a lifetime speaking to rooms built for kings. "I ask parley. I ask hospitality. I ask to be heard."

"Names first," Rosalind called down. "The border answers to names."

Something moved across his face that was almost, but not quite, a smile. "Corvane," he said. "Warden of the Silent Court, in the service of the Crown of Vaelith."

The name landed on the wall like a dropped stone. Rosalind heard Pell's breath catch beside her. She kept her own face still through an act of will that cost her more than she would ever admit, and said, in the flat voice of a border official reciting procedure, "There is no Crown of Vaelith. There is no Vaelith. You're a long way from anywhere, and the gate is closed to all traffic. State your business or ride on."

"I will state it," Corvane said, "inside, by your fire, with your people safe behind your walls. What I carry is not for open air."

* * *

She should have turned him away. She knew it even as she gave the order to lower the gate, knew it the way one knows the shape of a mistake while still making it — the way, she thought with a cold twist of the old guilt, one knows the shape of an open door. But parley was law, older than her office, older than the kingdom that had burned, and to refuse it would be to admit that she had something to hide. She had spent fifteen years being a woman with nothing to hide. The performance had to be flawless now, or not at all.

So she sat him at her own table, in her own hall, with Pell and two of the garrison standing armed along the wall, and she set bread and salted meat before him because hospitality demanded it, and watched him not touch any of it.

"You have a child," Corvane said.

It was not a question, and it was not the opening she had expected. She had braced for accusations, for the word *traitor*, for the old names of old crimes. Instead he sat with his gloved hands folded on the table and regarded her with the patient courtesy of a man reading a document he had already memorized.

"I have a garrison of nine," Rosalind said. "A ledger of bribes I'm not proud of, and a stove that smokes. Children pass through in the spring, with the caravans. None now."

"The child came to you in the snow," Corvane said. "Barefoot. Eight years old, or thereabouts. She does not speak your tongue. She sings." He let the last word sit in the air between them like a blade laid flat on a table. "You heard her sing, Keeper. And you knew the song. I would very much like to hear you deny that."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, the wind found a gap and worried at it. Rosalind became aware of Pell's eyes on her, and of how still she had gone, and she made herself lean back in her chair, make her voice bored, make her shoulders say *what nonsense is this*.

"I don't know what you think you'll find at the end of the world," she said. "But this is it. Snow, smugglers, and a stove that smokes. If a child came through, she'd be in my ledger like everything else. She isn't."

"May I see the ledger?"

"You may see the gate," Rosalind said. "From the outside. Parley's honored, Warden. Bread's on the table. The road's behind you."

For a long moment Corvane did not move. Then, slowly, he unfolded his hands and drew one glove off, finger by finger, and Rosalind found herself watching the small ritual with an attention that felt dangerously like recognition. She knew that gesture. She had seen it across a different table, in a different life, from men who had come to collect debts.

"You swore an oath," he said quietly. "Fifteen years ago. In a garden that no longer exists, before a throne that no longer stands, you put your hand on the Book of Hours and you swore to keep the borders of Vaelith against all comers, in all directions, until released by the Crown. I have come to release you, Keeper. That is my mercy, and I did not have to bring it." He turned his bare hand over, palm up, an offering or a summons. "The oath did not die with the kingdom. Oaths outlive the tongues that spoke them. I have spent fifteen years collecting the ones that remain, and yours is among the last, and it is *loud*. It has been pulling at you for fifteen years like a fishhook in the soft part of the throat. Don't tell me you haven't felt it. Don't tell me you haven't woken some mornings facing east."

Rosalind's mouth was dry. She thought of all the mornings — and she had told herself it was habit, the way a body learns a wall it has slept beside — of waking with her face turned toward the mountains, toward the pass, toward the drowned country she had climbed out of with her hands bleeding and her mouth full of someone else's name.

"You're mad," she said. "Or you're a very good liar. Vaelith is ash and story. I was born in the Marches. Ask anyone."

"I have asked everyone," Corvane said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than cruelty. "I have spent fifteen years asking. Do you know what I have learned, Keeper? That the kingdom did not fall the way your histories say. That the plague that took it was not a plague. That a choir was singing when the end came, and the song was not finished, and unfinished things do not die — they *wait*. And that a child has walked out of that waiting, barefoot through the snow, singing the anthem that should have died with everyone who ever learned it." His eyes, lake-gray and terrible, did not leave her face. "She is royal property, Keeper. Born of the Silent Court, marked by the Crown. And you are keeping her. Which means that either you are a liar — or the kingdom is not as dead as we were promised, and everything I have broken myself to do has been for nothing. I confess I no longer know which answer I am praying for."

"Get out," Rosalind said.

* * *

He went, in the end. He rose with that same terrible courtesy, bowed — actually bowed, the old court bow, low and precise, a gesture she had not seen since she was nine years old watching her mother curtsy to a chamberlain — and drew his glove back on, finger by finger.

At the door of the hall he stopped.

"I will take her," he said, not turning around. "Not today. Perhaps not this month. But I will take her, Keeper, because I must, and you will give her to me, because you must, and the only question left between us is how much it costs us both when we do." He looked back at her then, and for one unguarded moment she saw what lived underneath the courtesy: a weariness so vast and so old that it seemed geological, a man who had been carrying something enormous for so long that he had forgotten what it was to stand upright without it. "I was a kinder man once. I want you to know that. Whatever you decide, I want you to know that there was a time when I would have wept at this table instead of threatening you."

Then he was gone, into the gray, and the gate was shut behind him, and Rosalind stood in her own hall with her hands flat on her own table and discovered that they were shaking.

"Rosalind." Pell's voice, carefully neutral. "What was all that? Vaelith? Oaths? You looked at him like —" The deputy stopped, started again. "You *knew* him. Or you knew someone like him."

"I knew his coat," Rosalind said. It was even true. "The Marches used to trade with the east, before the war. I saw courtiers in coats like that when I was a girl. It doesn't mean anything."

Pell looked at her for a long moment, and Rosalind watched the deputy decide — watched the trust and the doubt weigh themselves against each other behind those young eyes — and knew that she had not entirely won.

* * *

She found the token after he was gone.

It sat in the exact center of the table, where the bread had been, where his folded hands had rested, and she could not have said when he had set it down. It was a disc of black iron, small enough to close in her fist, and it was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather — a cold that climbed her arm and settled behind her breastbone like a swallowed stone. On one face was stamped the sigil she had spent fifteen years not dreaming about: the crowned tower of Vaelith, its gate shut, its single window dark. On the other, in the old script, in letters worn nearly smooth by a thumb that had worried them for years, was a single word.

*Owes.*

She stood holding it while the fire burned down and the snow came on thick outside and somewhere above them, in the room where Wren slept, the child began — softly, in her sleep, in the language of a dead country — to sing.

Rosalind climbed the stairs and stood in the doorway and listened, and the anthem rose and rose toward the line where it had always broken off, the line no living voice had ever finished, and Rosalind's lips moved with it without her permission, shaping the words her mother had taught her, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Corvane had not come for the child at all.

He had come to see whether the keeper of the Hollow Gate still remembered the song.

She did. She remembered every note. And the black iron token in her hand had begun, very faintly, to warm.
