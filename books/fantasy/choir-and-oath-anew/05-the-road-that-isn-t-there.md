## 5. The Road That Isn't There

The soldiers came at dusk, three days after the token, and Rosalind knew it before her deputy's boy came pounding up the stairs — knew it in the way the wind changed, in the way the gate behind its wall of stone seemed to lean toward the sound of hooves like a dog hearing its name.

"Riders on the north road," the boy gasped. "Six. Maybe more behind. Flying no colors."

No colors meant Corvane. A flag would have been a courtesy; he had already spent his.

Rosalind had packed in the night, in the hours after she'd stopped pretending she wouldn't run. One satchel: bread, dried apples, a skin of brandy for wounds or courage, flint, the knife she now slept beside, and a coil of her mother's old choir-rope, silk-braided and absurdly precious, kept fifteen years for no reason she'd ever admitted aloud. She'd stood over Wren's pallet while packing, watching the child sleep with her small fists curled under her chin, and had felt the terrible arithmetic of it settle in her chest: one life she could carry, and nothing else.

"Wren." She touched the girl's shoulder. "Up. Quietly."

Wren woke the way she did everything — without fear, eyes opening black and calm as still water. She'd learned perhaps a dozen common words in the weeks since the snow had given her to Rosalind. *Bread. Cold. No.* She used them sparingly, as if rationing a language she hadn't asked for. Now she looked at Rosalind's face, read it the way children read, and reached for her boots without being told twice.

They went out through the coal cellar, past the ledger room where the black iron token still sat on the table where Corvane had left it. Rosalind had meant to throw it in the ravine. She hadn't. It rode in her pocket now, cold as a coin minted from midnight, and she hated herself for carrying it the way you hate an ache you've decided to walk on.

Behind the gate station the mountain dropped away into pine and shadow, and threaded through that shadow ran the smuggler roads — the unmarked ways her father's generation had cut to move untaxed wool, fugitives, worse. Rosalind had walked them all once, as a girl, before Vaelith burned. She knew where they went.

Or she had known.

* * *

By full dark they were deep enough in the pines that the riders' torches were only a rumor of orange between trunks. Rosalind moved by memory and moon-glint, hand on Wren's collar, choosing forks in the trail by landmarks she named to herself like a litany: the split boulder, the fallen birch bridge, the cairn shaped like a kneeling man.

It was at the third fork that the wrongness began.

She wanted the left-hand path. The left-hand path led south, down to the river villages, to coaches and anonymity and a hundred miles of distance bought with sore feet. She turned right instead — and only realized she'd done it forty strides later, when the cairns began appearing out of order, stones stacked in patterns no wool-smuggler had ever laid.

She stopped. Wren bumped gently against her back.

"That's not the way," Rosalind said aloud, to herself, to the trees. "That is *not* the way."

But the right-hand path lay open and inviting, snow untouched, and when she turned to retrace her steps the forest behind them had arranged itself into something less like woods and more like a corridor. The pines stood too straight. The dark between them was deeper than dark should be, and at its far end — impossibly, faintly — there was light. Not torchlight. Lamplight, warm gold, the color of windows in a city she remembered burning.

Wren tugged her sleeve. Pointed down the right-hand path. Said, in Old Vaelish, a word Rosalind knew and had hoped never to hear from a child's mouth: *Home.*

"No," Rosalind said. Her breath came out white and shaking. "No home. There is no home that way, do you understand? There's a drowned country and a sealed door and I am never—" She stopped, because the child was looking at her with such patient, terrible certainty that the sentence died somewhere in her throat.

The country was pulling them. It had been pulling her for fifteen years, she understood now — every unexplained detour, every winter she'd chosen to stay at this frozen posting rather than take a soft billet in the capital. She had thought she was guarding the gate. Perhaps the gate had been guarding its chance at her.

"We go south," she said firmly, and turned left, and walked, and within a quarter mile found herself walking right, and could not for the life of her say when the trail had turned beneath her feet.

* * *

They walked through the night. There was no point fighting it anymore; every path bent toward the light, the way water bends toward the sea, and the soldiers behind them — she heard dogs now, faint and rhythmic — made stopping a slower death than walking. So Rosalind walked, furious and frightened, holding Wren's hand across the bad ground, and somewhere in the fourth hour the fury wore itself thin and what was underneath it showed through: exhaustion, and grief, and an unwilling tenderness that startled her every time she caught it.

The child didn't complain. Eight years old, barefoot in boots two sizes too large, fleeing armed men through a haunted wood, and she hummed.

Rosaland recognized the tune before she let herself recognize it. Of course she did. She'd been humming it all her life, the way you breathe — the anthem's first movement, the part every Vaelish child absorbed before speech. Hearing it come back to her out of this small stranger's mouth was like hearing her own heartbeat played on someone else's ribs.

"Where did you learn that?" she asked, though she knew. She'd known since the snow.

Wren shrugged, the elaborate indifference of children who have been asked impossible questions before.

"The choir," Rosalind said. "You sang with the choir." And then, because the dark made her honest: "So did my mother. Before the end. She was the keeper of it — the songs, I mean. Kept them like bees. Said a kingdom wasn't its walls, it was its music, and walls fall but a song can be carried out in the mouth of anyone who knows it." She laughed, short and bitter, breath smoking. "Everyone laughed at her. Then the walls fell, and here we are, two women and half a song between us, walking home."

Wren's hand tightened in hers. Small fingers, chapped at the knuckles, gripping like an anchor-line.

They crossed a stream on stones furred with ice. Rosalind lifted the girl over the worst of it, and Wren — instead of letting go — slid her arms around Rosalind's neck and stayed there, cheek pressed to her shoulder, breathing her sleep-warm breath against Rosalind's jaw. It lasted perhaps ten steps. It undid something in Rosalind that fifteen years of vigil had built careful and high.

"Don't," she said quietly, to no one. To the child. To herself. "Don't get attached to me, little bird. People attached to me end up on the wrong side of doors."

Wren, who could not have understood the words, understood the tone, and tightened her grip.

* * *

Dawn found them in a valley that did not exist on any map Rosalind had ever drawn or memorized, which by itself proved whose valley it was. The snow here fell upward — slow flakes rising from the white ground into a sky the color of pewter, unhurried, as if the morning had been played backward and no one had told the light. Ahead, the lamplight glow had resolved into a pass between two hills, and beyond the pass, faint as a memory of a memory, rose towers.

Rosalind sat down hard on a boulder and let herself shake.

Wren stood before her, watching, then dug in the satchel, produced the last of the bread, broke it, and held half out with both hands — an offering, grave and formal, the gesture of someone sharing sacrament rather than supper.

"I can't eat your bread," Rosalind started, and then couldn't finish, because the girl's face held such absolute seriousness that refusing became unthinkable. She took it. It was stale and tasted of nothing and she ate every crumb.

"You know what's down there," she said. Wren nodded. "You know what's following us." Another nod, and this time a flicker crossed the calm black eyes — the first fear Rosalind had ever seen in her, gone almost before it arrived. "And you're going anyway. Back toward it. Toward him."

Wren considered. Then she pointed at Rosalind, and then at herself, and pressed both fists together — joined, bound, one shape.

"Yes, well." Rosalind looked at the upward-falling snow so she wouldn't have to look at the child. "One of us has to survive long enough to bury the other. That's how these things work."

* * *

It happened at the mouth of the pass, where the first true stones of the old road began — Vaelish-cut, jointed so finely a knife blade couldn't slip between them, work no living mason remembered how to do. The dogs were closer now. Once, between the hills, a horn sounded, single and patient, the note Corvane used instead of threats.

Rosalind drew Wren into the lee of the hill and knelt to check the boot-straps, needing something small and fixable, needing her hands to have a task. "Listen to me," she said. "If they take me, you run down this road and don't stop. The country wants you. It will keep you. Do you hear me? Whatever else happens—"

"*Ros-a-lin-dra.*"

The sound stopped her heart.

Not *Rosalind*. The word came out in the old shapes, the old weight — four syllables where the common tongue made three, the vowels rounded the way her mother had said them, the way no one alive had said them in fifteen years except ghosts. *Ros-alin-dra.* Keeper-of-the-door. Light-under-the-hill. Her name, meaning something, in a language that was supposed to be dead.

Wren watched her with those still-water eyes, hands folded, waiting — the way you wait after setting down something heavy you've been carrying a long way.

"That's my name," Rosalind managed. Her voice cracked in the middle of it. "My real one. Who taught you—"

But she knew the answer to that too, and the knowing arrived like ice water: the choir. The ghost-choir she hadn't yet admitted existed, rehearsing beyond the pass in a city frozen mid-moment, singing to whoever could still hear them, and the smallest listener in all the world had learned the anthem, and the anthem had apparently learned *her*.

The horn sounded again, nearer. Down the pass, the upward-falling snow thickened, and through it the towers stood waiting, patient as the drowned.

Rosalind rose. Took the child's hand. Found, to her surprise, that it steadied her more than it shook her.

"All right, Wren," she said. "Ros-alin-dra. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly."

Hand in hand, they walked down the road that wasn't there, toward the gates of the country that had eaten her life — and far behind them, cresting the valley's rim, the first of Corvane's riders came over the hill with the dawn at their backs, and did not slow.
