## 6. The Drowned Capital

They crossed at the place where the smuggler road gave up pretending to be a road.

Rosalind had known it would happen. She had walked these paths for fifteen years, and she knew the moment the ground stopped belonging to the world of ledgers and bribes and closed winter roads — the moment the stones beneath her boots began to hum. Not sound, exactly. A vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone, like standing too near a struck bell. Wren felt it first. The girl stopped mid-stride, bare feet on the frozen mud, and tilted her head the way a bird listens, and then she laughed — a small, startled sound, her first laugh since the snow — and pointed ahead.

The fog ahead was not fog. It was a wall of held breath, grey-white and faintly luminous, standing between two leaning pines like a curtain someone had forgotten to take down.

"Stay behind me," Rosalind said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. Fifteen years of steady. It was the one thing she had built that had never cracked.

She stepped through.

* * *

The city of Vaelith was drowning in air.

That was the only way Rosalind could think of it, standing on the threshold of the capital she remembered burning. The streets were there — the Serpentine Way, the Weavers' Stair, the long processional avenue called the Oathroad — but everything was suspended, mid-moment, as though the city had been caught between one heartbeat and the next and had never been permitted the second. Snow fell upward. It rose from the cobbles in slow spirals, drifting past shuttered windows and up past the spires, returning to a sky the color of pewter. Somewhere a fountain stood frozen in the act of spilling, its water a sculpture of itself, silver and unmoving.

A baker's cart stood abandoned in the middle of the Oathroad, its loaves still warm — she could smell them, impossibly, yeast and woodsmoke — and its horse stood mid-toss of its head, mane flung up like a wave, one hoof lifted and never set down. A child's ball rested on a windowsill where it had rolled to a stop. A door hung open. No one had closed it.

Rosalind's legs nearly failed her. She put a hand against a lamppost — the iron cold and real and furred with frost that was not frost — and breathed.

"I burned," she whispered. "I watched this burn."

But it hadn't burned, had it. That was the thing she had never let herself think all the way through. The fires she remembered were the fires of the siege outside the walls, the burning of the farms, the refugee camps, the world that had come to swallow Vaelith whole. The city itself had never burned. The city had been *closed* — shut away, sealed behind the Hollow Gate and the hundred lesser gates like a letter folded and never sent — and everything inside it had simply stopped. The plague-god's conquest had been interrupted mid-stride. The kingdom had been preserved like a fly in amber, and the amber was grief, and she was standing in it.

Wren walked out ahead of her, unafraid, her bare feet making no prints in the upward-drifting snow. She turned in a slow circle in the middle of the Oathroad, arms out, face lifted, and the rising snowflakes bent around her — not avoiding her, Rosalind thought, but *recognizing* her. Greeting her. The girl's mouth moved, and though no sound came, Rosalind knew the shape of the word. She had known it since the night the child appeared in the snow singing a language she had sworn she'd buried.

*Home.*

* * *

They heard the choir before they saw it.

It came from the direction of the cathedral — the Cathedral of the Nine Voices, its twin towers still standing, its great rose window dark — a sound like many throats breathing in unison, rising and falling, never resolving. Rosalind followed it through streets she had walked as a girl, past the chandler's shop where she had spent her first coins, past the schoolhouse where the choir-keeper's daughter had been both envied and pitied. The doors of the cathedral stood open. Inside, in the nave, the ghosts were rehearsing.

There were hundreds of them. They stood in the choir stalls and in the aisles and on the steps of the altar, translucent as breath on glass, their faces turned toward a conductor's podium that stood empty. They were singing the Anthem of the Unbroken Circle — the anthem every Vaelish child learned before they learned their own name — and they were singing it beautifully, and they were singing it *wrong*, because they always stopped at the same place. The anthem broke off three measures from its end, the way a sentence breaks off when the speaker dies, and then it began again, and broke again, and began. Over and over. A loop of longing. Fifteen years, Rosalind understood, or a hundred, or a thousand — however long it had been in this place where time was a held breath — they had been rehearsing the last three measures they would never reach.

"Why do they stop?" Wren asked, and Rosalind started — the girl's common tongue had been coming back to her in fragments all through the smuggler roads, surfacing like drowned things.

"Because it isn't finished," Rosalind said. "The anthem was never finished. The last composer died before—" She stopped. She knew why it was unfinished. Everyone in Vaelith had known why, in the last days. The final movement required nine voices of a specific lineage, the Nine Voices of the founding, and the plague had taken them one by one, and the kingdom had fallen with its song still open like a wound.

Wren was looking at the empty podium.

"No," Rosalind said, before the girl could say anything. "No. Whatever you're thinking—"

But the ghosts had seen them.

It happened all at once, the way recognition moves through a crowd. A woman in the second row of the alto stalls turned her head, and her translucent face changed, and then the woman beside her turned, and then the whole choir was turning, the anthem collapsing into silence, hundreds of dead faces fixing on the living woman in the doorway. The silence was worse than the singing. In the silence Rosalind could hear the snow rising against the windows, could hear her own heart, could hear Wren's small hand finding hers.

And then the crowd of ghosts parted, the way water parts, the way it always parts, and a woman came down the aisle of the Cathedral of the Nine Voices with her hands folded and her spine straight and her face like a verdict.

Rosalind's mother.

* * *

She had imagined this meeting for fifteen years, in the bad hours, the hours between midnight and the bell. She had imagined her mother angry, imagined her weeping, imagined her turned away in silence. She had imagined every possible cruelty and every possible mercy and had never once imagined this: that Maren Dravenmoor, choir-keeper of Vaelith, would simply stop in front of her daughter and look at her with an expression of terrible, patient sorrow, as if Rosalind were a question her mother had been working at for a very long time and still could not solve.

"You came back," her mother said. Her voice was the voice of the anthem — many-voiced, faintly harmonized with itself, a chord in the shape of a woman.

"I had to. They're hunting her." Rosalind drew Wren forward. "Mother, this is Wren. She came through the Gate. She's—"

"I know what she is." Her mother's gaze moved to the child, and something in it softened and then hardened again, like wax cooling. "I know everything that happens at the Gate, Rosalind. We feel it. Every year you light the lamp. Every year you sweep the threshold. Every year you do not sing." The chord-voice trembled. "Do you know what it is, to be a choir-keeper's daughter and to hear your mother's song stop at the same three measures for fifteen years, and to know that the one person who could finish it chooses not to?"

"I was nine years old." The words came out of Rosalind raw, unsteady, nothing like her gate-keeper's voice. "I was nine, and you told me to open the side door, and I opened it, and forty-one people lived. Forty-one people, Mother. I have counted them every night for fifteen years. I have named them. I have—" Her breath caught. "I did what you told me to do."

"You did more than I told you." Her mother's folded hands tightened. "I told you to open the door and close it behind them. You went through it yourself. You walked out of the city while it was being sealed, and you carried the Gate-key in your pocket, and you let them fold us up like a letter and never sent us. You survived, Rosalind. That was the price they paid you. Survival and silence. And you took it."

"I was nine."

"You were the choir-keeper's daughter." Her mother's voice dropped to a single thread, almost human, almost gentle, and that was the worst of it. "You knew what the anthem needed. You knew the Nine Voices were dying. You could have stayed. You could have sung. One voice of the lineage, holding the last measure open — the seal would have had a flaw, a door, a *hope*. Instead you ran, and the song broke, and we have been standing in the broken place ever since." She turned her translucent face toward the empty podium, toward the hundreds of ghosts who had not moved, who were listening the way the dead listen — completely, without mercy. "I do not forgive you, my love. I want you to hear it plainly, because everyone else in your life has been too afraid to say it. I do not forgive you. But I am glad you are alive. Both of those things are true, and you will have to carry them, because I cannot carry them for you anymore."

The snow rose past the rose window. Somewhere far away, at the edge of the drowned city, Rosalind felt — actually felt, in the humming stones, in her teeth — a pressure. A tread. Oath-bound boots on a road that bent toward Vaelith whether its walkers willed it or not.

Corvane was coming. And Wren had let go of her hand and was walking toward the empty conductor's podium, and the ghosts, all of them, were beginning — very softly, very terribly — to sing.
