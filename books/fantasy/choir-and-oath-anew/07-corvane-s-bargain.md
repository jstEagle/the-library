## 7. Corvane's Bargain

The oath did not sleep. That was the first thing they never understood, the ones who called him monster in the market squares of the borderlands, the ones who spat when his banner passed. An oath was not a promise made and set aside like a coin in a drawer. It was a fire banked in the chest, always burning, and it warmed him on nights like this one when the snow came down sideways and the soldiers around the cookfire could not meet his eyes.

Corvane sat apart from them, as he always did, with his back against a dead elm and the black iron token's twin resting in his palm. He had carried its brother for fifteen years. The one he had left on Rosalind Dravenmoor's table had been hers to claim all along; she simply hadn't known it yet.

He turned the token over. On one face, a crown. On the other, a closed eye.

*Watch until it opens,* the oath said, in the voice of a dying king who had not been a king for years before he died. *Watch until it opens, and then close your hand.*

His hand was closed now. It had been closed since Vaelith burned.

* * *

They thought he remembered nothing but duty. Even his own soldiers believed it — the story was cleaner that way, and stories had to be clean or they curdled. The truth was that Corvane remembered everything, and that was precisely the shape of his damnation.

He had been young when they brought him before King Aldous the Third, younger than the crown prince whose face he would learn to fear. A warden of the outer gates, unremarkable, third son of a house too poor to matter and too loyal to be dangerous. The king had looked at him with eyes like tarnished silver and said, *You do not love me. Good. I cannot afford to be loved by the man I am about to ask this of.*

What he asked was simple. What he asked was impossible.

*A thing wears my son's face,* the king said. *It came out of the deep country beyond the maps, where the old gods drowned when we built our churches on their graves. It smiles at my councilors. It laughs at my fools. Last week it stood in the nursery and watched my granddaughter sleep, and when I entered, it thanked me for coming — in my own voice. I have prayed to every saint in the calendar and none of them answers, which tells me this is older than saints. So I will bind you, Warden, with the oldest law I have: the Oath of the Sealed Door. When I give the word, you will help me shut my kingdom away from the world — every gate, every road, every memory. And you will stand outside it, forever, and make certain that whatever I seal inside stays sealed.*

Corvane had asked the only question a man could ask. *And the people, Majesty? The ones who are only people?*

The king's face had done something terrible then — cracked down the middle like river ice, and beneath the crack Corvane saw not a tyrant but a father drowning slowly.

*There is no way to cut the rot out without cutting the limb,* Aldous said. *I have looked. God knows how I have looked. If there were a door small enough for the innocent and too small for it, I would have found it.* He paused. *Perhaps there is one such door. There is a choir-keeper's daughter who keeps the under-gate by the river. She has been asking after refugees.*

And so the bargain had been arranged that Corvane had spent fifteen years pretending he had no part in: the girl Rosalind, seventeen years old and terrified, would open her side door for three nights and let the innocent flee into the world of maps. In exchange she would carry silence like a second skin and never speak the name of her country again. The king would have his narrow door. The warden would have his seal.

Three nights became two when the thing wearing the prince's face grew impatient. Two became one. And on the last night, standing at the Hollow Gate with the king's blood on the binding-stones because the thing had found him first and worn him while he died, Corvane spoke the words that pulled Vaelith down behind the veil of the world — and heard, faint and rising through the closing dark, a choir singing an anthem that had never been finished.

He had told himself for fifteen years that the anthem did not haunt him. He had lied very well, even to himself. That was what oaths trained you for.

* * *

Now the camp slept, mostly, and Corvane walked the perimeter because sleep had left him years ago and walking was the next best thing to prayer. The snow squeaked under his boots. Beyond the tree line, the land bent in ways cartography refused to admit — roads that curved toward Vaelith whether you walked them forward or back. He felt the pull in his teeth, like a tuning fork struck somewhere below hearing.

The child was pulling. Or the thing inside the child was.

He found himself, as he often did, at the edge of the sentries' ring, looking north toward a gate he could not see and feeling the seam of the world like a scar under frost. Fifteen years he had kept this vigil. Fifteen years of watching the hollow countries of other men — the places grief hollowed in living faces, the spaces where names used to be. His soldiers thought him tireless. He was merely haunted in a disciplined way.

And here was the confession he had never spoken aloud, not to the king's ghost, not to his own reflection, not to the god he no longer believed in: he did not know anymore whether the thing in Vaelith still lived.

The oath told him it did. The oath was a wound that throbbed whenever its cause stirred, and it had throbbed for eight years now — softly at first, like a far-off drum, then louder, insistent, until three months ago in a border village he had doubled over in the street with his hand pressed to his breastbone while farmers stared. Something had crossed. Something small. Something that hummed in Old Vaelish in its sleep, if the reports were true, and drew maps of streets that should not exist, and sang songs that made grown merchants flee without their coin.

A seed, the oath whispered. It has planted a seed. Close your hand.

But seeds, Corvane knew — he had been a boy on a farm once, before the walls took him — seeds do not choose what they grow into. He had seen the sketches his scouts described. Streets drawn with a child's careful crayon, and in the corner of each page, flowers. Not plague-flowers. Just flowers, lopsided, lovingly rendered.

That was the problem. That was the whole unbearable problem. If the seed were monstrous, his hand could close without shaking.

* * *

At dawn he went to her, alone, as he had promised himself he would — though he had brought forty soldiers within a half-mile, because promises to oneself were the easiest to keep dishonestly.

Rosalind Dravenmoor met him at the gatehouse door with her arms crossed and a knife visible at her belt, which pleased him in a grim way. Fear sharpened people into honesty.

"You came back," she said. "Parley's over, warden."

"Parley," he said, "was theater. This is the truth, and then I will leave you to decide." The snow had stopped; the morning was the color of pewter, and their breath mingled and vanished between them. "You believe I serve a tyrant's ghost. You believe I burned a kingdom to keep a throne safe, or to punish treason, or for love of the sound of doors closing. I have heard every version, because I have needed to hear them. It is easier to be hated for grand reasons."

"I don't hate you," Rosalind said. "Hating takes energy I need for winter."

Something in him almost laughed. He throttled it. "Then hear me plainly. Your king — my king — discovered, too late, that his son and heir had been replaced. Something from the deep country, something the founders of Vaelith buried rather than killed, because it could not be killed. It wore the prince's face. It wore his voice, his memories, his smile. By the end it was wearing his body into the council chamber while the true prince's corpse cooled somewhere I will not describe. It wanted a kingdom, Dravenmoor. Not to rule. To wear. Kingdoms are the largest garments it knows how to put on."

She had gone very still. He watched her try to disbelieve him and fail, because she had been there; she had seen the last days; some part of her had always known the fall had a cause uglier than war.

"The sealing did not kill it," Corvane continued. "Sealing never kills. It starves, and waits, and dreams. And eight years ago it dreamed something out. A child. Yours — no. Not yours. Everyone's. Found at a shrine, they say, humming songs no living tongue teaches." He held up the second token, the one he had carried across fifteen winters, and let her see both faces: the crown, the closed eye. "I swore to watch until it opened. It is opening. The child is the hinge."

"And you want me to give her to you."

"I want to offer you mercy, which no one offered me." He said it flatly, because ornament would have been obscene. "Here is the whole of it, keeper of the gate. What grows inside that girl will finish what its sire began unless it is ended first. I do not relish it. I have spent three weeks within sight of her, if your scouts are worth their wages, and I have watched her feed birds and stumble in snowdrifts and laugh, and every hour of it has been a knife between my ribs. But I buried a kingdom once because hesitation cost me a night, and I have sworn — *sworn*, Dravenmoor, in words that burn — that I will not bury a world the same way. Surrender the child. Stand aside. And I swear to you by the same fire that binds me: you will live. Your deputy will live. No soldier of mine touches this gate or anyone behind it. Mercy, freely given, for the first and last time in my long ugly service."

The wind moved through the pass, and far below, in the smuggler roads that bent toward a drowned country, he could feel the seam straining like a stitch in old cloth.

Rosalind looked at him for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was quiet enough to be crueler than shouting.

"You said the king asked you to seal the innocent in with the rot. Three nights of door, cut to one. I know. I was the door." Her eyes did not blink. "So tell me, warden — when you closed your hand on that oath, did it ever once occur to you that the child in front of you might be the small door? The one small enough for innocence and too small for the god? Because I've watched her for a season. I've held her when the nightmares come. Whatever is growing in her, it grows the way a song grows — wanting to be finished, not to devour. And if you take her from me, I will open the Hollow Gate myself, and I will go down into Vaelith, and I will find whatever is left of your king's ghost, and I will teach it a better oath than yours."

For the space of three heartbeats, Corvane could not answer — because beneath her fury, in the old pronunciation of her vowels, he had heard the anthem. Rising. Rehearsing. As if the dead kingdom had already chosen its conductor.

Behind him, faint as a struck bell, the oath in his chest began to sing back.
