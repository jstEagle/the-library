## 8. The Choir Assembles

The cathedral had been waiting for them.

Rosalind understood that now, standing beneath the broken rose window with Wren's cold hand in hers. The building had not collapsed so much as paused — its ribs of pale stone still holding the shape of a breath, its pillars furred with frost that had never finished falling. Snow drifted upward here, slow as ash in reverse, and through the gaps in the vaulted ceiling she could see the drowned sky of Vaelith, that strange pewter light that never quite became morning.

The ghosts were already in the seats.

They had been gathering since the moment they crossed into the capital — slipping out of doorways, out of the spaces between falling snow, taking their places in the pews with the patience of people who had been waiting fifteen years for a rehearsal to resume. They did not look at Rosalind. They looked at Wren.

The girl's hand tightened in hers.

"I know," Rosalind said softly, in Old Vaelish. "I see them too."

Wren had not spoken since the road. She had spoken only once in the common tongue — Rosalind's name, in the old pronunciation, *Rhos-ah-lind*, with the vowels rounded the way her mother would have said them — and since then she had gone quiet in a way that frightened Rosalind more than any army. The child had stopped humming. That was the worst of it. For eight days on the smuggler roads, Wren had hummed the anthem under her breath the way children hum, thoughtlessly, constantly, a thread of melody that Rosalind had told herself was harmless. Then, two nights ago, at a fire in the ruins of a waystation, the humming had stopped mid-phrase.

And the fire had leaned toward the girl.

Not blown. *Leaned*. Every flame in the hearth bending like wheat toward Wren's silence, and the shadows behind her deepening into something with structure, something almost like a face, and Rosalind had thrown salt on the coals and pulled the child close and pretended not to see.

Now, in the cathedral, Wren pulled her hand free and walked three steps forward into the aisle. The upward snow parted around her. When she turned back, her eyes were wet, and when she spoke, her voice came out in both languages at once, layered, the Old Vaelish and something underneath it that made the frost on the pillars shiver.

"I can hear it growing," she said. "In me. It sings when I sleep. It knows the anthem better than I do — it knows the *end* of it, and the end isn't a song, Rosalind. The end is a door." Her small fists clenched. "I don't want to be a door. I want to be a girl."

Rosalind crossed the aisle and knelt, so that they were eye to eye, so that the ghosts in the pews became a blur at the edge of her vision. Up close, Wren's pupils had a faint silver ring around them now, like frost forming on glass. It hadn't been there a week ago.

"Then we finish it first," Rosalind said. "The anthem. Before it finishes you."

"Finish it how?" Wren's voice cracked. "It's not finished because the singers died. Mama told me — before — she said the choir was singing the sealing when the city fell, and they never got to the last verse, and that's why everything here is stuck. Stuck like me." She swallowed. "If I sing the last verse with *it* in my mouth, I open everything. But if I never sing it—"

"Then you become it. Yes." Rosalind took the girl's hands. They were small and chapped and human, and she held them the way she had once held a ledger she was afraid to open. "So we don't let you sing it alone, and we don't let *it* sing it at all. We sing it the way it was meant to be sung — by a choir. Every voice carrying a part, so no single throat holds the whole. That's why anthems are written for choirs, Wren. Not for beauty. For safety. A song split among many mouths cannot be owned by one."

Wren searched her face. "Do you know it? All of it?"

The question went through Rosalind like a hook through cloth.

She had been the choir-keeper's daughter. She had grown up beneath this very window, or one like it, in a cathedral that stood in a city that had not yet burned. She had copied the anthem out in her father's hand a hundred times, had sung the alto line before she could write her own name, had stood in this nave — or its living echo — while two hundred voices lifted the sealing-song that kept the plague-god bound beneath the palace. She knew it the way she knew the shape of her own hands.

"I know it," she said. "Every part."

"Then teach me." Wren's chin came up, and for a moment she looked less like a frightened child and more like what the ghosts apparently believed she was: a daughter of Vaelith, standing in her own cathedral. "Not to open. To close. You said a song split among many mouths can't be owned. So split it with me. Teach me to give the ending away."

Outside, very faint, came the sound of iron on stone. Rhythmic. Patient. A column of soldiers marching through streets where nothing else had moved in fifteen years.

Rosalind did not turn her head. "We have until they reach the doors," she said. "So we begin now."

* * *

She taught her the way her father had taught her: standing, hands free, breath first.

"From the belly," Rosalind said, pressing her palm flat against the girl's sternum. "Not the throat. The throat is where *it* lives. The belly is where you live. When you feel the song trying to climb into your mouth and take over, you send it down. You make it pass through you, not out of you. Can you feel the difference?"

Wren nodded, eyes shut, small chest rising.

"Again. The second verse. And this time when it reaches for the high phrase — the one that sounds like breaking glass — you *give it away*. Point it at the pews. There are two hundred ghosts in this room who have been holding their part of this song for fifteen years. Let them hold it."

The first attempt was a disaster. The second was worse. The silver ring around Wren's pupils flared each time the melody approached the broken-glass phrase, and the frost on the pillars crept, and somewhere in the dark beneath the cathedral floor something turned over in its sleep and murmured a harmony that made Rosalind's teeth ache. Twice Rosalind clapped her hands and made the girl stop and drink water and breathe, and once Wren burst into tears and said she could hear the god singing her own name inside the melody, and Rosalind held her until the shaking stopped and said, with a steadiness she did not feel:

"Then we'll sing your name louder. That's all an anthem is, in the end. A name sung louder than the dark."

On the fourth attempt, the ghosts began to hum.

It started in the back pews — a low, wavering drone, like wind through a keyhole — and then it spread, row by row, the dead of Vaelith picking up the alto line as if they had only set it down that morning. Their voices were thin and cold and imperfect, cracked with fifteen years of silence, and they were the most beautiful thing Rosalind had ever heard. Wren's eyes flew open. The silver ring in them flickered — and steadied.

"They're helping me," she whispered.

"They were always going to help you," Rosalind said. "You're theirs as much as mine. More, if we're honest." She said it lightly. It did not come out lightly.

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached up and took Rosalind's hand again, and did not let go, and Rosalind found that she did not want her to, and thought: *ah. So this is what it is. Fifteen years guarding a door, and the door turns out to have been guarding me from this.*

The anthem rose through the nave, incomplete, imperfect, the dead carrying the middle verses and Wren's small voice threading the descant and Rosalind's lower register holding the ground beneath them both. The upward snow swirled faster. The frost on the pillars bloomed into patterns — roses, thorns, the interlaced knotwork of the old royal seal. And in the deepest dark under the floor, the other voice fell silent, listening, hungry, waiting for the verse that would not come.

The doors at the back of the cathedral boomed.

Once. Twice. The iron-on-stone rhythm had reached them, and it had not stopped to knock politely. Corvane's soldiers were at the doors, and Corvane's soldiers did not parley twice.

The singing faltered.

"No." Rosalind stepped forward, raising both arms the way her father had, the way conductors had raised them in this nave for four hundred years. "No one stops. Dead of Vaelith — you waited fifteen years. You can wait one verse more. *Sing.*"

The choir sang.

The doors boomed again, and this time the hinges screamed, and through the widening gap came the grey light of the drowned sky and the black silhouettes of soldiers in Corvane's oath-grey livery, and at their center, tall and terrible and courteous even now, a man made of oaths with a black iron token glinting at his throat.

Wren's hand crushed Rosalind's fingers. "Rosalind," she said, in the old pronunciation, in the common tongue, in both at once. "I'm scared."

Rosalind did not take her eyes from the doors. She lifted her chin, found the pitch of the final verse — the verse no one had ever sung, the verse that lived only in her memory and, now, in the mouth of a god wearing a child's face — and began, very quietly, to conduct.

"Then sing louder than the fear," she said. "It's time to finish the song."
