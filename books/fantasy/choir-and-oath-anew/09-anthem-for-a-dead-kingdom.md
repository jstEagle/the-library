## 9. Anthem for a Dead Kingdom

The cathedral of Vaelith had no roof anymore, and so the snow fell upward through it, rising past the broken ribs of the vaulting like prayers returning to whatever had refused to answer them. Rosalind stood in the crossing where the nave met the transepts, where the choir-keeper's podium had been, where her mother's hands had once lifted and fallen like a bird deciding whether to stay. The stone under her feet was warm. That frightened her more than anything else — that after fifteen years of drowning, the heart of the drowned country should still be warm.

Wren stood beside her on the podium step, small hands folded the way choir children fold them, the way Rosalind had folded hers at eight years old in this exact spot. The girl's eyes were wide and dark and full of something growing. She could feel it too. They both could: beneath the flagstones, beneath the frozen city, a seed turning over in its sleep, dreaming of roots.

Behind them, at the far end of the nave, the great doors shuddered.

Corvane had come.

* * *

The doors were bronze, cast with the faces of the first kings, and they had held against the fall of Vaelith when nothing else did. Now they rang like a bell struck by a patient fist. Each blow sent a tremor up through the floor, and each tremor woke the ghosts a little further from their long rehearsal.

They stood in the ranks of the pews — hundreds of them, translucent as breath on glass, wearing the clothes they had died in or the clothes they had loved best. Courtiers and fishwives, soldiers with half their faces missing, children holding hands with children. For fifteen years they had gathered here every night to sing an anthem they could never finish, because its last movement required voices that remembered why it was written, and memory was the one thing Vaelith's dead had been permitted to keep only in fragments.

Rosalind had all of it. Every street. Every name. Every bargain.

That was what the anthem wanted. She understood it now, standing on her mother's podium with Wren's cold hand in hers. The song was not a weapon. It was a ledger. And ledgers must be paid.

The doors rang again. A seam split down the bronze, and through it came not light but absence — a darkness shaped like certainty, like a man who had never once doubted his own oath. Corvane's voice came through the gap, and it was gentle, which was the worst thing about him.

"Keeper Dravenmoor. You are standing on ground that is not yours, protecting a child who is not a child. Step aside, and I will let you walk out of here with your life, as I let you walk out of Vaelith fifteen years ago."

"You didn't let me," Rosalind said. Her voice sounded thin in the vast dark. "I opened a door. You just didn't think I mattered enough to stop."

"I am thinking about you now." Another blow. The bronze groaned. Somewhere in the ranks of ghosts, a woman began to weep without sound. "The thing inside that girl ate a kingdom once. It will eat yours next if I fail. Give her to me, Rosalind. Be the keeper of a gate instead of the keeper of a grave."

Wren's grip tightened. The child looked up at her, and her mouth moved — not words yet, not quite, but the shape of the anthem's first line, the one every Vaelish child learned before they learned their own name.

*Rise, O country, rise and remember.*

Rosalind turned back to the ruined nave, to the upward-falling snow, to the dead waiting in their pews like a congregation that had outlasted its god. She raised her hands the way her mother had taught her, palms open, fingers soft, as if holding water she meant to give away.

"Sing," she said, in Old Vaelish. "All of you. Sing me the beginning."

* * *

The anthem began the way rivers begin — somewhere unseen, quietly, and then everywhere at once.

It rose out of the ghost-ranks in a hundred broken voices, and where the voices broke, the song filled the cracks with Rosalind's memory. She felt it leave her in pieces. The smell of bread from the ovens on Lantern Row — gone, paid. The name of her first singing teacher, Master Ilven, who smelled of resin and wore one blue glove — gone. The way the harbor bells sounded at midwinter, three notes and then a fourth that only children could hear —

Gone. Paid into the song. And the song grew fat and bright on it, climbing the broken pillars, pouring through the empty windows, and the snow fell upward faster, and the darkness pressing through the split bronze doors slowed as though wading into deep water.

Beside her, Wren began to sing. Not the seed-voice, not the thing that wore the king's face in Corvane's telling — but the child's voice, thin and true, carrying the second line. *We were a lamp lit on the water's edge.* The ghosts turned toward her, and Rosalind saw that they were not afraid of her. They were grieving for her, gently, the way you grieve for someone still alive.

The doors burst.

Not shattered — opened, swung wide by Corvane's hand, and he came up the nave alone, because his soldiers' courage had failed them at the threshold of a place where snow defied the sky. He walked through the anthem as though through rain, and where it touched him, his outline flickered, and Rosalind saw what was underneath: not a man made of oaths, but a man hollowed out by one, a vessel so full of duty there had been no room left in him for anything else. His black iron token burned in his fist. His eyes found Wren, and in them was not hatred. It was exhaustion. Fifteen years of it. Thirty. However long he had carried the oath.

"Last chance," he said, and the anthem swallowed half the words.

Rosalind did not answer him. She answered the song.

She gave it the fall of Vaelith. She gave it the night sky over the palace turning the color of a bruise, the plague-god walking the streets in the king's body, smiling, touching doorframes and leaving rot. She gave it the side door in the choir crypt, and her own young hands on the bar, and the refugees streaming past her — and the price whispered to her by something in the dark that she had agreed to and never spoken aloud since. The anthem took it all. It took the guilt and the geography alike, and it grew so bright that the ghosts themselves began to thin, offering their last fragments voice by voice, a fishwife giving up her daughter's laugh, a soldier giving up the taste of homecoming, each payment dissolving them a little more into light.

And the seed beneath the cathedral stirred, and rose, and Wren cried out — a sound with harmonics in it that no child's throat should hold — and Rosalind understood that the anthem was not destroying the god-seed. It was teaching it. Giving it, note by note, everything Vaelith had been, so that it might become a requiem instead of a conquest. So that it might choose music.

"Stop," Corvane said, and for the first time his voice cracked. Because he was hearing it too. He was hearing the anthem reach its final movement, the one that had never been sung, the one that named — 

It named him.

His true name, the one before the oath, the one before the iron token, sung back to him by a dead country in a chord of perfect absolution. *Corvane, who kept faith when faith was all he had. Corvane, who was forgiven.* The ghosts sang it. The anthem sang it. Wren, tears freezing on her cheeks, sang it.

He stopped walking.

Rosalind watched the oath break in him the way ice breaks on a river in spring — not all at once, but in long groaning seams, from the middle outward. The black iron token glowed white-hot in his fist and then simply came apart, falling as ash. He sank to his knees in the nave of the kingdom he had helped unmake, and he did not beg, and he did not resist. He opened his hands.

"Finish it," he said. "Please. Let me hear the end."

* * *

The last payment was hers.

Rosalind felt the anthem turn toward her like a tide, patient, immense, asking. Everything. All of it. The capital and the burning and the mother who would not forgive her; the smuggler roads and the fifteen winters of bribes and closed roads; the reason she hummed; the reason she flinched at bells. If she paid, she would stand in this cathedral a stranger to herself, keeping nothing but the girl beside her and a tune with no name.

She looked down at Wren. The child looked up, and her mouth shaped the old pronunciation of Rosalind's name — the only common word she knew — and that, Rosalind thought, that at least the song could not take, because it belonged to the living now.

"I keep her," Rosalind said aloud, to the anthem, to the dead, to whatever bargained in the dark. "Everything else. Take everything else."

She opened her memory like a door.

Vaelith poured out of her — whole, blazing, complete for one final moment, every street and every voice and every grief — and the anthem caught it and rose to its ending, the ending fifteen years deferred, and the ghosts came off their pews in a single wave of light to sing it, and Corvane sang it kneeling, and Wren sang it with her arms around Rosalind's waist, and the god-seed rose through the warm stone and unfolded — not wings, not crowns, but sound itself given shape, a chord the size of a country — and then, gently, like a lamp being carried out of a room at the end of a long day, it dissolved into the music rather than through it.

The seal closed like a book.

Snow stopped falling upward. Began, instead, to fall the ordinary way, down through the roofless cathedral, settling on the pews where no ghosts sat anymore, on the ashes where a token had been, on the shoulders of a man who wept without shame, and on two figures at the podium — one tall, one small — who remembered almost nothing at all.

Rosalind lowered her hands. The silence afterward was enormous and clean.

She looked at the child beside her and did not know her name, and did not need to. She looked at the man kneeling in the nave and felt nothing she could explain. She hummed, without knowing why, four notes and then a fifth that only children could hear.

Wren smiled through her tears, took her hand, and said, in a language Rosalind no longer spoke but somehow still understood:

"Home now. Both of us. Home now."
