## 10. Keeper of Nothing

The thaw came late that year, and all at once.

Rosalind stood at the gatehouse window on the morning the ice broke, listening to the Hollow River groan and crack like old timber settling. Below her, the water ran brown and triumphant through channels it had carved in a single night, carrying winter's wreckage south. Somewhere in that flood — she did not know how she knew this, only that the knowledge sat in her chest like a swallowed stone — was the last of something. A country's worth of snowmelt. A kingdom's worth of forgetting.

She hummed while she watched it go.

Three notes, then four, rising into a phrase that turned back on itself the way rivers turn back toward their sources. She had been humming it for weeks now, maybe months. Her deputy, Maren, had taken to smiling when she heard it through the door of the ledger room — *there goes Rosalind again* — the way one smiles at a cat washing its face or a clock striking the hour. It was simply something Rosalind Dravenmoor did, the way she drank her tea bitter and slept with the window cracked even in frost. A habit without a history. A door with no room behind it.

She could not have said where the tune came from. When she reached for its origin, her fingers closed on smooth blank stone, warm as skin.

That should have frightened her. Once — she had the sense of *once*, though not of its contents — it might have. Now it felt like standing in a house where every window has been bricked over but the light comes anyway, sourceless and sufficient.

"Keeper." Maren's boots on the stair, two at a time. She came into the room carrying the ledger under one arm and a steaming mug in each hand, and set them both down on the table between them with the ceremony of a woman laying out cards for a reading. "Roads are open to the ford. The wool merchants will want passage by week's end, which means bribes by week's end, which means you letting me handle the bribes because you still pretend we don't take them."

"We don't take bribes," Rosalind said. "We accept contributions to gate maintenance."

"Mm." Maren opened the ledger across her knees. Fifteen years of winters were bound in that book — tolls and tariffs, names crossed out and written in, the small gray arithmetic of keeping a dead road alive. "Also, there's a line item from last autumn I can't reconcile. The night you took the girl up-mountain. There's an entry here in your hand — 'payment owed, see that it is never collected' — and no amount beside it."

Rosalind looked at the entry. The ink was hers. The words meant nothing to her, and everything, both at once, the way a scar means nothing and everything.

"It stays," she said.

Maren studied her a moment. Maren was good at studying people; it was why Rosalind kept her. "You came back down that mountain different," she said. "You and the girl. I've never asked."

"I know."

"I'm asking now. Not what happened." Maren turned the ledger around so Rosalind could see the empty column. "Just whether it's finished."

Rosalind thought about the question longer than she expected to. Finished. The gate below them stood sealed — she knew this the way she knew her own hands, without remembering learning it. She had walked its length yesterday on her rounds, palm flat against stone that hummed faintly, faintly, like a struck bell long after the sound has left the range of hearing. Sealed so thoroughly that even she could find no seam in it, no hinge, no keyhole. Whatever had been on the other side of it was on the other side of it forever, and she had put it there, and she could not tell herself why, or for whom, or what it had cost.

Only that when she pressed her ear to the stone, the humming in her chest answered it.

"It's finished," she said. And found, to her mild surprise, that it was true.

* * *

Wren learned fast, the way spring learns.

By April she had common speech enough to argue — mostly with Maren, mostly about whether the hens' eggs belonged to the hens. By May she could read her letters, tracing them in spilled flour on the table while Rosalind cooked. She was tall for eight, or perhaps nine; she had arrived with no age attached, the way she'd arrived with no shoes and no fear and no name anyone could pronounce until Rosalind gave her one off a weathercock.

She still drew sometimes. Not maps — Rosalind had checked, early on, with her heart in her teeth, and found only pictures: goats, boats, a woman at a window with her hair coming loose. But once, in the margins of her letter practice, Wren had drawn a choir. Rows of small robed figures, mouths open, and above them a shape like a bird made of notes.

"What's this?" Rosalind had asked.

Wren had looked at the drawing a long time, her brow furrowing the way it did when she reached for something just past her fingers.

"Home," she'd said finally. Then, uncertain: "I think. I don't remember it either."

They had sat together with that for a while — two people holding the same absence from opposite sides, like mourners at a grave neither could read. Then Wren had leaned her head against Rosalind's arm, and Rosalind had begun to hum without deciding to, three notes then four, and the child had hummed back, and somewhere far beneath them the sealed stone had seemed to sigh.

Now, on the first truly warm morning, they walked the wall together. Wren went ahead along the parapet with her arms out like a tightrope walker, fearless as ever, and Rosalind followed at a keeper's pace, nodding to the two soldiers on the north tower who would be relieved at noon. The valley below had gone green with an indecency that only happened in mountains, all at once and everywhere. Wildflowers in the ruins of the old tollhouse. Bees working the rosemary Maren insisted on growing despite the climate.

"You're doing the song," Wren said, dropping back to walk beside her.

"Am I?"

"You do it when you're happy. You didn't used to. Before the mountain you didn't do it at all." Wren kicked a loose pebble off the parapet and listened for it to land; it never did, just fell into green silence. "Maren says you've always done it. But I remember before. A little."

"Do you." Rosalind kept her voice easy. "What else do you remember?"

Wren considered this with the terrible honesty of children. "That you were scared," she said. "Of me. In the beginning. And then you weren't, and I don't know what changed, but I think it cost you something big." She looked up. Her eyes were dark, unremarkable, entirely a child's eyes, and yet Rosalind's chest tightened looking into them, grief and love arriving together with no explanation for either. "I'm sorry. If it was me it cost."

Rosalind crouched, which put them nearly eye to eye, which is where she preferred to conduct the important conversations. "Listen to me," she said. "Whatever it was, wherever I paid it — I'd pay it again. Do you understand? Twice over. That isn't kindness talking. That's arithmetic."

Wren's mouth quirked, unconvinced the way children are unconvinced, but she nodded, and threw her arms around Rosalind's neck, and smelled of woodsmoke and goat, and Rosalind held on until the girl wriggled free to chase a butterfly along the wall, because childhood keeps its own appointments.

* * *

In June the merchant caravan came up from the lowlands, and among the muleteers was an old man who stopped dead in the courtyard when he saw Rosalind, and stared, and crossed himself with a shaking hand.

"You," he said. "The keeper. You're the one from—" He stopped himself. Looked around. Lowered his voice as if the walls might be taking dictation. "I knew a woman once, years back. Said she'd come through the Hollow Gate in the worst storm of the century, walking out of country that wasn't on any map, carrying a baby wrapped in a choir robe. Said the gatekeeper took them in and asked nothing." His eyes searched Rosalind's face. "Said she never learned the gatekeeper's name. Said the gatekeeper told her that was better. For everyone."

Rosalind felt the words land somewhere deep, in the warm blank place where her memories used to live, and echo without meaning.

"That sounds like a story someone invented," she said gently. "To make sense of a hard road."

"Aye," the old man agreed, too quickly. "Aye, likely." But he paid his toll in full, overpaid it, refused the change, and would not meet her eye again.

That night Rosalind sat alone in the ledger room after Maren had gone to bed, the lamp turned low, the summer insects ticking against the shutters. She opened the book to the autumn entries and read the line in her own handwriting again: *payment owed, see that it is never collected.*

She tried to remember making it. She tried to remember anything — a mountain pass, a storm, a flag of parley, a man made of oaths, a cathedral, a song. She reached and reached, and the smooth warm stone of her forgetting gave back nothing, and underneath the nothing, faint as a pulse, the tune rose again. Three notes, then four, turning home.

And here is what Rosalind Dravenmoor understood, sitting in her lamplit room with her missing past folded around her like a coat: she did not need the sum. She knew she had paid it. She knew it had been worth more than she could count — that was the whole shape of the feeling, vast and settled, like a debt discharged so thoroughly that even the creditor was released. Somewhere behind a sealed door, something she loved had been saved, and something she feared had ended, and she had bought both with the only coin that mattered, and the purchase was complete.

Grief without content. Peace without proof. She had heard, once — or would have said she'd heard, if asked to account for the certainty — that some songs demand payment in memory, voice by voice, until only the melody remains. If so, she had sung well. She had sung to the end.

She dipped her pen and wrote beneath the unreconciled line, in a firm keeper's hand:

*Account closed. Nothing further owed.*

Then she blew out the lamp, climbed the stairs to where Wren slept with one arm flung over her eyes and the blankets already kicked half away, and tucked her in, humming. Three notes, then four.

Outside, the Hollow Gate stood black and seamless under the summer stars, holding its silence like a held breath — and if the stone hummed back, very faintly, in answer to the tune, no one woke to hear it, and no one ever needed to know.
