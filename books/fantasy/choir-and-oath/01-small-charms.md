## 1. Small Charms

The market of Vellum's Reach woke the way it always did: grudgingly, and with complaints about the weather.

Casper Brightwater had been there for the waking, as they were every morning, arranging their little folding table between the pie cart and the woman who sold secondhand boots with an honesty that bordered on cruelty. ("They'll last you a year, maybe two, and your left foot will hate you the whole time.") The table's legs wobbled on the cobblestones no matter how many times Casper wedged them, so the first charm of the day was not, strictly speaking, magic—just a folded bit of leather and a stubborn refusal to let physics win.

The other charms were magic. Very small magic. Magic so small it barely qualified, the way a kitten barely qualifies as a predator.

"Luck-charms!" Casper called, in the easy market voice they'd spent three years perfecting. "Gentle luck! Nothing dramatic! You will not find treasure, you will not win the lottery of the Guild of Cooperage, but your bread will not burn, and your mother-in-law will arrive in a *good* mood!"

That got a laugh from a passing fishwife, which was the point. In Vellum's Reach, humor was legal currency. Magic was not. The Bell Wardens had seen to that for a hundred years—the great bronze bells in the Warden towers rang at dawn and dusk, and everyone said it was just to mark the hours, but everyone also knew someone whose cousin had been taken in for questioning after a candle flame bent the wrong way.

So Casper sold luck that could plausibly be coincidence. A copper button polished smooth and whispered over: carry it, and you'd never lose your keys. A knotted thread in the color of whatever the customer wanted: red for courage, blue for calm, green for finding things you'd misplaced. The whisper took almost nothing out of Casper—less than walking up a hill—and left behind almost nothing anyone could detect. It was like sneaking crumbs past a sleeping dog. Casper had gotten very good at sneaking crumbs.

The oath-scar on their palm itched, the way it did every morning, and every morning Casper pressed it flat against their apron and pretended it didn't exist.

Ten years since they'd sworn it away. Ten years since the night they'd stood in their grandmother's empty house with blood on their hand and grief in their throat and spoken the Unbinding—a swordspeaker's oath turned against their own gift, folding all that terrible inherited power down into something the size of a whisper. Small charms. Harmless ones. Enough to pay for bread and rent on a room above a cobbler's shop, and not one syllable more.

It had worked. That was the miracle nobody applauded: it had simply *worked*, for ten uneventful years, and Casper had learned to be grateful for uneventful the way other people were grateful for wealth.

"Morning, Casper," rumbled Bramwell, the pie man, handing over the usual: one meat pie, slightly crushed, half price, because "presentation matters and this one fell."

"Bramwell, this is artistry."

"It's a pie what fell."

"A pie with a history." Casper paid, ate standing up, and kept one eye on the crowd the way they always did—not looking for danger, exactly. Just practicing invisibility. It was a skill like any other. You kept your voice pleasant and your eyes moving and your sleeves down over your wrists, and after ten years you could stand in the middle of a crowd and feel reasonably sure that no one would remember you five minutes later.

Which was why it was so infuriating when the woman at the map stall across the aisle looked directly at Casper and smiled like she'd known them for years.

She wasn't from the market. Casper knew everyone in this row by now, and she wasn't one of them. She'd set up late—a folding table like Casper's own, covered in maps, weighted down at the corners with ink bottles and one defiantly decorative rock—and she stood behind it with ink-stained fingers and a smudge of blue on her jaw where she'd pushed her hair back one too many times. Her maps were good. Genuinely good, not market-fair good. Casper could see the hatching on the hills from here, the careful curl of rivers, the little annotations in handwriting so tidy it looked printed.

And she was looking at Casper. Not glancing. Looking, with her head tipped slightly, like Casper was a landmark she'd read about and finally found.

Casper looked away first. Practiced. Smooth. Invisible.

When Casper looked back—because they were weak, apparently—she was still watching, and her smile had widened into something frankly unfair.

*Don't*, Casper told themselves. *You know how this goes. People get curious. Curious people ask questions. Questions are how Wardens end up in your shop going through your drawers.*

But the morning went on, and the curiosity did not go away. She sold a river chart to a barge captain. She argued cheerfully with the boot woman about whether the Saltmarsh road had moved or merely "developed opinions." She laughed at something Bramwell said, and Casper caught themselves leaning toward the sound before catching their own lean and straightening up, mortified, thirty-eight years old and hiding from a smile.

Around noon, the crowd thickened, and she crossed the aisle.

Casper busied their hands immediately, straightening buttons that did not need straightening.

"You're the charm seller," she said.

"I'm a charm seller. There's a meaningful difference. There's a boot seller over there; you wouldn't say 'you're the boot seller' to all of them."

"There's only one of me."

"And yet." Casper looked up and immediately regretted it, because up close her eyes were brown-gold, warm as lamplight, and the ink stain on her jaw had a companion on her wrist, and there was something—

There was something *familiar* about her. Not I've-met-you familiar. Older than that. Deeper. Like hearing a song you couldn't name, hummed in a room you couldn't place. The itch in Casper's scarred palm flared once and settled, like a cat turning over in its sleep.

"Wren Halloway," she said, wiping her right hand on her apron before offering it. Ink came off on everything, apparently; her palm was silver-gray at the creases. "Cartographer. Well, surveyor, technically, until the Guild accepts my petition, which they will not, because the Guild is a museum with fees."

"Casper." No surname. The omission was automatic, worn smooth by use.

"Just Casper? Mysterious. I like it. Very market-appropriate." Wren turned to the table, studying the wares with genuine interest rather than polite flickers. "What does the blue thread do?"

"Calm. Blue for calm."

"And if I want to be less calm?"

"Why would anyone want that?"

"Because calm is what people do instead of brave, sometimes." She said it lightly, but it landed somewhere under Casper's ribs and sat there. "The buttons?"

"Never lose your keys."

"That's not luck, that's responsibility."

"The finest kind of luck." Casper found they were smiling and couldn't remember starting. They stopped, then started again, because stopping felt rude and continuing felt dangerous and Casper had been making safe choices for ten years and was suddenly, violently tired of it. "One copper each. Two for three. The knot-work takes longer, so those run higher."

"I'll take a blue thread." Wren counted out the copper, then paused, thread in hand, and looked at Casper with that same head-tilted attention. "Can I ask a strange question?"

"I have a feeling you'll ask it either way."

"Do you ever sell maps? Or want one? Because I noticed—" she tapped the edge of Casper's table, casual as anything, "—that your stall faces north. Every morning. And you keep your left hand tucked when you handle coin, and you flinch, very slightly, whenever the bells ring. I mapped the old mining roads out past the Reach last spring, and I spent a month learning to notice which way things face. Things face *away* from what they're avoiding." She held up the blue thread. "You, Casper-Just-Casper, are facing away from something very consistently."

The market noise carried on around them—pies, boots, bartering, a child shrieking joyfully about a dog—but for Casper the sound went distant, the way it did when an oath stirred in its sleep.

"I have a bad shoulder," Casper said.

"On your left hand?"

"Shoulder-hand syndrome. Rare. Tragic."

Wren laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and something in Casper's chest did an unauthorized maneuver. "Fine," she said. "Keep your secrets. Everyone in this city has at least three; it's part of the local charm." She wound the blue thread twice around her wrist and tied it off with quick, certain fingers. "I'm at the archive most days. The old one, off Chandler's Row, the one everyone thinks is abandoned. If you ever want a map made, or a friend made, either comes cheap."

She turned back toward her stall, and Casper should have let her go. Should have let the moment close like water over a stone, the way a thousand moments had closed over ten years.

"Casper Brightwater," they heard themselves say.

Wren stopped. Turned. And the look on her face was wrong—was *strange*—not surprise, nothing so simple as surprise. Recognition. The deep kind. The kind that has a shape already waiting, like a box built years ago for exactly this thing.

"I know," she said quietly.

"You—what?"

"Brightwater," Wren said, and her ink-stained fingers touched her wrist, right over the little blue knot, and her warm lamplight eyes had gone very still. "I need to show you a book."

* * *

Later, Casper could never say why they followed her. Only that the word *Brightwater*, spoken aloud in daylight in Vellum's Reach for the first time in ten years, had sounded less like a name and more like a door unlocking—and that some part of them, the part they'd sworn quiet, had been waiting a decade to hear it anyway.

She led them through lanes Casper knew and one alley Casper didn't, to a narrow door off Chandler's Row with a rusted lock that opened at her touch. Inside: dust, tall windows, and shelves upon shelves of books breathing the particular smell of paper that remembers things.

Wren went straight to a lectern in the corner, where a heavy volume lay open under a cloth. She pulled the cloth back with the care of someone uncovering a sleeping face.

"This came in with a bequest last month," she said. "A century old, at least. Nobody's catalogued it. I started reading it Tuesday." She turned the book toward Casper. "I haven't slept properly since."

The page showed a woman drawn in fine brown ink. Standing at a market stall. Selling small charms. Head tipped, smiling, ink on her fingers.

It was Wren. To the life. Dated ninety-seven years before she was born.

"Turn the page," Wren whispered.

Casper did. And there, beneath a drawing of a figure with sleeves rolled up and one palm marked in careful lines, written in the same steady century-old hand, were four words:

*the Brightwater debt follows.*
