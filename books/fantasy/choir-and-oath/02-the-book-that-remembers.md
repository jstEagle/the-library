## 2. The Book That Remembers

The trouble with following someone through Vellum's Reach was that the city had been designed by people who hated being followed.

Casper had learned this the hard way over ten years of practice. The streets kinked at odd angles, as if a cartographer had sneezed mid-draft and no one had bothered to fix it. Alleys doubled back on themselves out of what Casper could only assume was spite. And every third corner held a neighbor who knew your face, your business, and — in the case of Mrs. Oduya from the chandlery — your approximate weekly income down to the copper.

So when Wren Halloway turned left onto Lamplighter's Row instead of right toward the residential terraces where any sensible person went after a first date, Casper had to make a series of rapid decisions about who they were willing to become in pursuit of an answer.

A person browsing dried herbs they did not need. A person deeply invested in the window display of the cobbler's. A person, briefly, buying a meat pie solely to have something to hold while loitering outside a building marked *The Reach Archive & Records Office — Quiet Please*.

The pie was actually quite good. Small mercies.

Through the archive's tall windows, Casper watched Wren hang her coat on a peg like she owned the peg, nod to a night clerk, and disappear into the stacks beyond. It was nearly eight o'clock. Nobody went to work at their archive job at eight o'clock at night after a date unless they were either devoted or haunted.

Casper knew haunted. Casper *was* haunted, professionally speaking.

They stood in the lamplight eating their pie and having a very reasonable internal argument.

*Go home,* said the sensible voice, the one that had kept them alive for a decade. *You had a lovely evening. She laughed at your jokes. You told her exactly nothing true about yourself, which is the whole point of you now. Go home, sleep, and never think about how she knew your grandmother's lullaby.*

*Or,* said the other voice, the one that sounded uncomfortably like Maren, *you could walk through that door and find out why a cartographer's hands smell like old ink and older secrets.*

Casper finished the pie. Crumpled the paper. Told both voices that they were going home, and then walked through the door.

* * *

The archive smelled the way all archives smelled: dust, leather, lamp oil, and the faint sweet rot of paper slowly giving up. The night clerk looked up from a crossword, took in Casper's face, and apparently decided they looked like someone with legitimate research needs.

"Reading room's open till ten," he said. "No candles. No ink near the folios. No singing."

"That last one comes up often?"

"You'd be surprised." He waved toward the stacks. "Wren! You've got a visitor."

From somewhere deep among the shelves came a pause — the particular quality of silence made by a person going very still. Then Wren appeared around a corner with a book in each arm and an expression carefully arranged into casual surprise, which fooled no one, least of all Casper.

"Oh," she said. "Hello. I just — I forgot I needed to check something. For work."

"At eight at night," Casper said.

"I'm dedicated."

"You walked past your own street to get here."

"I'm *very* dedicated." She set the books down on a reading table, and Casper saw the titles upside down: *Hydrographic Surveys of the Upper Reaches*, and beneath it, a plain grey volume with no title at all. Her fingers rested on the grey one the way people rest fingers on things they're afraid will move.

"Wren." Casper sat down across from her. "On the walk back from the market tonight, you hummed something. You said it was nothing. But it wasn't nothing. It was *Sleeper in the Stone*, and there are maybe four people left alive who know it, and two of them are dead."

The lamp between them hissed softly. Somewhere in the stacks, a page turned — or didn't; old buildings make sounds like breathing, and you learn not to trust them.

"My grandfather sang it," Wren said quietly. "He learned it from his mother, who learned it from hers. It was a family song. We thought it was just a family song." She pushed the grey volume an inch toward Casper. "Until I started working here, and found out my family kept records. Detailed ones. About songs. About scars. About—"

She stopped. Swallowed.

"About you, Casper Brightwater."

The name landed in Casper's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They hadn't heard it spoken aloud in years — not the full name, not the surname, the surname above all. In Vellum's Reach they were Casper, charm-seller, quiet tenant, nobody. Hearing *Brightwater* spoken in a warm lamplit room felt like hearing a door open somewhere in a house you'd nailed shut.

"That's not my name," Casper said, too fast.

"It's written in this book."

"A lot of things are written in books. Books lie. I sell little wooden charms, Wren. I carve them myself. My biggest seller is a rabbit that supposedly helps with stubborn jars."

"I know," Wren said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "I bought three. I don't even have stubborn jars." She opened the grey cover. "But this book doesn't lie, because this book isn't really a book. It's a ledger. And ledgers only do one thing: they remember."

* * *

The pages were vellum, thick and cream-colored, and the handwriting was small and precise — a surveyor's hand, or a priest's. Dates marched down the margins in a century of ink. Casper read sideways at first, politely, the way you read a stranger's letters. Then they read properly, and politeness died.

It was a prophecy book. Not the riddling, smoke-and-metaphor kind peddled by fortune-tellers in the market — Casper sold those customers nicer lies for two coppers — but the other kind. The kind written before, so that everything after was merely fulfillment.

*In the hundredth year of the Reach's founding, the debt comes due,* the opening entry read. *The court beneath keeps its accounts. Two souls are named. One is promised; one is owed.*

Below, in fresher ink — added later, Casper realized with a creeping coldness, in a different hand — a name:

*Wren Halloway, of the line Halloway. The promised soul. Marked by ink-stained hands and a song half-remembered. She will be found beside the river, bargaining for luck she does not believe in.*

Casper looked up. "The luck-charm stall. By the river. That's—" Their mouth went dry. "That's where we met. Three weeks ago. You bargained me down on a windcharm and then bought it anyway at full price because you 'felt bad.'"

"I felt bad," Wren confirmed faintly.

"And your hands—"

Wren lifted them. Ink in the creases, under the nails, stained deep the way ink stains when it has been a lifetime companion. Casper stared at them and felt the floor tilt gently, the way it does when the world admits it has been lying to you about something fundamental.

"This book is a hundred years old," Casper said. "This entry describes a woman who wasn't born yet. Describes her meeting me. At my stall. On a specific day." They laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and high. "Someone wrote fan fiction about my life before I lived it. That's — fine. That's fine. People write strange things. There's a whole shelf in my shop about prophesied fish."

"Casper."

"The fish prophecy is at least internally consistent—"

"*Casper.* Turn the page."

There was a reason, Casper would think later, that they hadn't turned the page themselves. Some part of them, some animal cleverness buried under ten years of careful dullness, had known what waited on the next page the way you know a storm is coming from the taste of the air. So they let Wren turn it. Cowardice, perhaps. Or mercy. With Wren it would always be hard to tell the difference.

The next page held a single entry, centered, written large enough that whoever added it had wanted it seen.

*Beneath the promised soul stands the owing soul: the last Brightwater. The debt passes as eye color passes, as height passes. It cannot be refused, only carried. When the court calls, the Brightwater answers — or the promised soul pays in their place.*

And below that, in ink so fresh it still shone faintly in the lamplight, as if written hours ago rather than decades:

*Casper Brightwater. The Brightwater debt. Currently residing: Vellum's Reach, Lantern Street, above the chandlery. Occupation: charm-seller.*

The room got very small and very loud and very silent all at once.

"They know my address," Casper said. Their voice sounded like someone else's. "Wren. Someone updated this. Recently. Someone knows where I sleep, what I do for a living, and they wrote it in a book that's been waiting for a hundred years, and—" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "I moved twice. I changed my name. I spent a decade being *boring*. I became a man whose most exciting trait is jar-related merchandise."

"I know," Wren said.

"You can't know."

"My family hunted people like you." She said it flatly, quickly, like pulling a splinter. "For generations. Swordspeakers, oath-speakers, Brightwaters. The Guild kept records so thorough that when the hunting ended, we couldn't stop keeping them. We just... watched. Waited. Wrote things down." Her hand hovered over the fresh ink without touching it. "I didn't write this entry. I swear I didn't. But I found it yesterday, and I've been coming back every night since, trying to decide whether to show you, and then tonight you followed me here like a suspiciously charming stalker, and I thought — well. Fate's doing the introductions whether I like it or not."

Casper lowered their hands. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if it, too, was waiting to see what happened next.

Ten years ago, Casper had sworn away the family gift — spoken the unbinding with their palm bleeding on their grandmother's kitchen table, felt the power drain out of them like warmth from a cooling cup, and walked away from everything the name Brightwater meant. Debts included. They had believed, genuinely believed, that oaths ended when they were broken.

But debts weren't oaths. Debts were inheritance. And inheritance, as the book so patiently reminded them, passed as eye color passed.

"My grandmother," Casper said slowly, "sold something. To the court beneath the mountain. She never said what. She used to say the family safety was *paid for*, and paid for things stay paid for, and I was young and stupid and didn't ask what the currency was." They traced the fresh ink with one fingertip, not quite touching. "She died four years ago, Wren. And this entry was written after."

"Then who wrote it?"

The question hung between them. Outside, the city bells began the hour — nine slow tolls rolling across the rooftops of Vellum's Reach, the sound everyone in the anti-magical city trusted more than scripture, because bells were honest, bells were only bronze and physics, bells could not lie.

Except that on the ninth toll, Casper heard — or imagined, or remembered, memory being its own unreliable ledger — a note underneath the bell-note. A thin, sweet, patient tone, like a finger run around a glass rim. Like something humming *Sleeper in the Stone* far underground, keeping time, keeping accounts.

Keeping an address.

"The clerk said no singing," Casper murmured, staring at the page that bore their name. "I'm starting to understand why."

Wren closed the book — gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing — and put her ink-stained hand flat on the cover, as if holding it down.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we need to talk about what your grandmother swore."
