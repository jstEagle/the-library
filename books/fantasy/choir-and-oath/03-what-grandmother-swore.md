## 3. What Grandmother Swore

The night after the archive, Casper did not sleep. They sat at the kitchen table in the flat above the charm shop with the kettle going cold for the third time, turning a copper coin over and over between their fingers—the one they kept in their pocket for luck, though luck had nothing to do with it. It was a coin from the mountain. It had a hole through its center, and through that hole, if you held it to the light just so, you could see a sliver of the room behind you, warped and small.

Wren Halloway's name, written a hundred years ago. *A cartographer with river-grey eyes and ink to the second knuckle.* And beneath it, in the same patient hand:

*Casper Brightwater. The debt.*

They had not told Wren about the second page. They weren't sure why. Cowardice, probably. Or the old instinct: *don't show them the wound, they'll use it.* Ten years of practice did not unlearn itself in a single evening, no matter how kind the eyes across the archive table had been.

The kettle clicked as it cooled. Casper gave up on tea and went instead to the wardrobe in the back bedroom, the one that didn't open onto clothes.

It opened onto the past, which was worse.

* * *

Casper was seven years old the first time Maren Brightwater taught them the first oath, and it was raining, because it was always raining in the house Maren kept at the edge of the Reach, back when the Reach still tolerated the family it would later ban.

"Sit," Maren said, and Casper sat, on the cold flagstone of the cellar, knees together, hands out.

Maren was not a tall woman. She had hands like tree roots and hair going silver in patches, as though the grey couldn't decide where to start and had simply begun everywhere at once. She smelled of lamp oil and dried yarrow and, faintly, of the deep stone smell that Casper would not be able to name for years—the smell of the under-mountain, carried home in the folds of a coat.

"This is the smallest oath there is," Maren said. "The one all the others grow from. Say it wrong and nothing happens. Say it right and—" she smiled, and her smile was like a door opening in a wall Casper hadn't known was there, "—and the world listens."

"What does it do?" Casper asked, seven years old and impatient, feet swinging above the cellar floor.

"It doesn't *do*. That's the first lesson. It *asks*." Maren took Casper's small hands in her root-knuckled ones and turned them palm up. "Everything else—the fire-oaths, the binding-oaths, the terrible ones your great-grandfather bragged about—all of that is just shouting. This is asking. Ready?"

Casper nodded so hard their teeth clicked.

Maren spoke, and her voice changed. Not louder. *Truer*, somehow, the way a bell is truer than a shout. Casper never afterward remembered the words themselves—the first oath did not survive being written down, and Maren made them swear never to try, which Casper, being seven, took as a marvelous invitation to try constantly and fail constantly—but they remembered the shape of it. A rising line, a turn, a falling line, like a bird taking off and landing again in the same hand.

The rain outside the cellar window stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped*, mid-fall, a hundred grey beads hanging in the air like beads on a string, and the light through them went strange and golden, and seven-year-old Casper made a sound that was not a word at all.

"Careful," Maren said, warm and dry. "That's the second lesson. When the world listens, it listens to *everything*. Your fear. Your wanting. Especially your wanting." She let the rain fall again, all at once, a soft drumroll on the flagstones, and laughed at Casper's face. "You'll want things, little bright one. Everyone does. A swordspeaker has to learn what they want *before* they open their mouth, or the oath will hand them the wanting instead of the truth."

"Did you ever ask for something wrong?"

Maren went quiet in a way that Casper, at seven, did not notice and, at thirty-one, could not stop hearing. The lamplight caught the scar on her palm—the same place Casper's own scar would one day sit, a thin white line like a sentence that had been struck through but not erased.

"Once or twice," she said. "Come along. Your mother's making honey bread and if we're late she'll swear an oath of her own, and hers don't work, which means she just yells."

That was the memory the wardrobe gave up first, always. The rain hanging in the air. The door of the world swinging open. Maren's laugh.

It was not the last memory. It was only the one Casper could bear.

* * *

The second memory took longer to come, the way a splinter takes longer to surface than to go in.

Casper was seventeen. Maren had been gone three days—gone down, the family said, *gone down to settle accounts*—and when she came back she came back *less*. Casper couldn't have said how, at seventeen. She walked the same, laughed the same, made the same terrible jokes about the Bell Wardens' hats. But the lamplight didn't sit on her the same way. It was as if she had been a window and now she was a painting of a window: same frame, same view, but nothing behind it moving.

She called Casper to the cellar again. The same flagstones. The same lamp.

"You're of age," she said, without preamble. "Time you knew what the Brightwater name costs."

"I know what it costs," Casper said, with the magnificent confidence of seventeen. "Wardens. Curfews. Having to pretend at market that the charms are just—"

"Not that." Maren sat down across from them, and Casper saw that her hands were shaking, and seventeen-year-old Casper's whole world tilted, because Maren's hands did not shake. Maren's hands had held back rain. "Sit still, little bright one. This is the last lesson, and it's the one I'd give anything not to teach."

And she told them.

She told them about the court beneath the mountain, the goblin court, old as the stone itself, with its ledger of names and its long patience. She told them about the year the Reach's mines broke into the court's outer halls—about the famine that followed, the sickness, the way the city above had begun to fail, and the way the court below had offered terms: *a soul, freely given, and the city lives.*

"And you said yes," Casper said. It wasn't a question. The shape of it was already in Maren's face.

"I said yes." Maren's voice was steady. Her hands were not. "I was the speaker of my generation. It had to be a speaker—a soul that knows how to *ask* is worth ten that only know how to take, or so the herald told me, and the herald was very polite, which is the worst thing about them. I gave my name to the ledger and I bought your city another hundred years, and your family another hundred years of being left alone by things that do not leave people alone."

Casper sat with it. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, their mother dropped a pan and swore.

"But—" Casper's voice came out wrong, young, cracked. "You *came back*. You're here."

"Most of me." Maren smiled, and it was the door-in-the-wall smile, and it broke Casper's heart even then, even at seventeen, because now they could see the wall behind the door, and the wall was load-bearing. "The court keeps what it's owed. It keeps it *kindly*, which is worse than keeping it cruelly. I still dream. I still laugh. But some part of me is down there in a ledger, in a hand very like the one that wrote your name, and when I die, that part doesn't go where parts go. It stays. It's *theirs*."

"And it passes down." Casper's own hands had gone cold. They looked at their palm, at nothing yet, at the smooth unmarked skin that would one day carry the scar. "The debt."

"It passes down like eye color," Maren said. "Like the silver in my hair. I'm sorry, little bright one. I have been sorry since before you could walk. I hoped—" and here her voice did shake, finally, all the way through, "—I hoped it would pass to someone with better luck than me. Or better sense. Or both."

"Why didn't you *tell* me? All those years of lessons—"

"Because knowing it early makes it true early," Maren said, "and I wanted you to have a childhood that wasn't a countdown." She reached across and took Casper's hands, palm to palm, the same as the first lesson, and for one moment the lamplight bent toward them, the old habit of the world leaning in to listen to a Brightwater. "One more thing. The most important thing. A debt is a kind of oath, and every oath can be *spoken differently*. Remember that. Whatever they tell you down there—whatever the ledger says—a binding is just words that someone chose. And words that someone chose can be chosen otherwise."

She said it like a gift. Casper, at seventeen, heard only the terror in it.

* * *

Casper came back to themselves at the kitchen table, the copper coin biting into their palm, night fully arrived outside the window over the sink. The wardrobe door stood open. It always stood open, on the nights they were foolish enough to go to it. They had never been able to bring themselves to board it shut. Grief and anger wore the same face in Casper's chest, and they had long ago stopped trying to tell which one had opened the door.

*She lied to me,* thought the anger. *Or the ledger lied through her. Either way, someone wrote Wren's name a century ago and wrote mine beneath it, and the hand was patient, and the hand knew the address.*

*She bought us a hundred years,* thought the grief. *She paid with the best part of herself and smiled about it, and she'd be furious with me for crying, so don't cry, don't you dare—*

The coin slipped from their fingers and rang on the table, and through its little hole Casper caught a warped sliver of the kitchen, and in the sliver, for half a heartbeat, the lamplight stood up straight and still, like rain refusing to fall.

The world, listening.

Casper closed their hand around the coin and made themselves breathe. There was only one place the ledger could be answered from, and it wasn't here. It was under the mountain, in a court where a polite herald kept a piece of Maren Brightwater in a book, and where—according to a prophecy a century old—Wren Halloway's soul was already entered in a fine and waiting hand.

Down in the shop, the bell above the door chimed.

Casper went very still. The bell did not chime at midnight. The bell did not chime for wind. And when they crept to the top of the stairs and looked down, there was a figure standing among the luck-charms, small and grey and courteous, with the deep stone smell of the under-mountain rising from its coat like a memory of rain.

"Apologies for the hour," said the goblin herald, looking up with terrible politeness. "But the court understands you've been reading ahead in the family book."
