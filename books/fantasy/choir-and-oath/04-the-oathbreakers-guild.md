## 4. The Oathbreakers' Guild

Wren Halloway had inherited three things from her family: a long nose she'd grown to like, ink-stained fingers no amount of scrubbing would fix, and a cellar full of things the city had decided never happened.

The cellar was under the archive. Officially it was "flooded storage." Unofficially it was dry as an old promise and held the Oathbreakers' Guild entire: ledgers, blades, bounty notices, and—this was the part that made her stomach turn on slow evenings—a book of techniques for unmaking spoken oaths. Her great-great-great-grandmothers had been very good at unmaking. They'd called themselves oathbreakers the way butchers call themselves meat-cutters: proudly, practically, without asking the meat how it felt.

She thought about all of this while Casper Brightwater stood in her archive workroom pretending very hard to be a person who had simply wandered in off the street.

"You followed me," Wren said.

"I was walking," Casper said. "In your direction. Repeatedly."

"For six blocks."

"The city is laid out poorly."

"The city is laid out exactly the way I mapped it." Wren tapped the survey pinned to the wall behind her, the one with her name inked in the corner in her own unmistakable hand. She was enjoying this more than she should. There was something delightful about watching a person who had spent ten years being invisible fail at it so completely. Casper stood in doorways like they were apologizing to them. They flinched when the lamplight caught their face, as if being seen were a bill arriving early.

"Fine," Casper said. "I followed you. The book you showed me—the prophecy—I haven't slept. My name's in it, Wren. Under yours. *The Brightwater debt.* You don't just get to show someone that and then go home to your maps."

"No," Wren agreed. "You're right. That was cruel of me, actually. I've been feeling bad about it since Tuesday."

Casper blinked. Whatever speech they'd prepared, that wasn't the response it was built for. "You've been... feeling bad?"

"Professionally. It's part of my process." She pulled the ring of keys from her belt and jingled them once, deliberately, like a curtain rising. "I didn't show you the book by accident, Casper. I knew what it was before you touched it. And I know what you are."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the archive clock ticked through the hour with its usual indifference.

"Swordspeaker," Wren said softly. "Last one. Grandmother Maren Brightwater's grandchild. The scar on your palm isn't from a kitchen accident, unless your kitchen contained a burning oath."

Casper's hand closed slowly around the scarred palm, an old habit, Wren guessed—one of those gestures people make when they've spent years holding a door shut from the inside. "Who told you?"

"My family," Wren said. "A hundred years of my family, in a manner of speaking. Come downstairs. There's something you need to see, and I'd rather you saw it sitting down, because I watched my cousin faint over these records and he's a blacksmith."

* * *

The cellar smelled of cedar and cold iron. Wren lit the lamps one by one, and Casper's face changed with each flame—not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. That was worse, Wren thought. Fear you could run from. Recognition meant the past had already found you and was waiting politely with its hat in its hands.

Shelves lined every wall. Ledgers with cracked spines. A rack of swords wrapped in oilcloth, each hilt stamped with the guild's mark: a broken circle. Bounty notices, yellowed and curling, bearing names and sums. And in the center of the room, on a reading table worn smooth by generations of hands, an open ledger with columns of names.

"What is this place?" Casper asked, though Wren could see they already knew. People always knew. That was the cruelty of guilt; it recognized its own handwriting.

"The Oathbreakers' Guild," Wren said. "Founded in Vellum's Reach about a hundred and ten years ago. Their charter was simple: swordspeakers' oaths reshape reality, reality shouldn't be reshaped by anyone, therefore swordspeakers must be found and their oaths broken. By force if necessary." She let that sit. "By force was usually necessary."

Casper walked the shelves slowly, trailing fingers along the spines without touching. "These are hunting records."

"These are hunting records," Wren confirmed. "Techniques for severing an oath from its speaker. Interrogation protocols. Lists." She turned the open ledger toward them. "Names of everyone the guild ever caught. What oath they carried. Whether they gave it up quietly." She paused. "Whether they didn't."

Casper looked down at the page. Wren watched them read, watched their lips move slightly, and then watched the color leave their face one shade at a time, like a lamp running out of oil.

"This name," Casper said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "Tobias Brightwater. That's—that's my grandfather's brother. He disappeared before I was born. Grandmother said he went to sea."

"He didn't go to sea." Wren kept her voice gentle and steady, the way you speak to someone standing on ice. "He was brought here. He refused to surrender his oath. The guild broke it out of him anyway. The technique is described—" she hated herself for knowing the page number, "—on the facing page. He survived. Many didn't. But he stopped being a swordspeaker, which for the guild counted as success, and for Tobias Brightwater I imagine counted as nothing at all."

Casper sat down. Not gracefully—more like a marionette whose strings had been cut mid-sentence. They stared at the ledger for a long time.

"Your family did this," they said finally. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't shouted either, which somehow made it heavier. "You invited me here. You showed me the prophecy. You let me follow you around like a lost dog for six blocks, and the whole time your family's cellar was full of—" their hand swept the room, the blades, the lists, the neat, meticulous, lovingly maintained record of everything done to people like them. "Did you know who I were when you asked me to dinner?"

"Yes."

"*Yes?*"

"I wanted to meet you first," Wren said. "Before you knew. Because I knew what you'd see when you found out. You'd stop seeing me and start seeing them." She gestured at the shelves. "And I couldn't bear that yet. Which was selfish. I'm aware."

Casper laughed, and it was not a kind laugh—it was the laugh of someone who has stepped on a stair that wasn't there. "Selfish. Right. My grandmother sold our souls to the goblin court, apparently, and your family tortured mine for a century, and we're both standing here calling things selfish. This whole city is built on top of a graveyard and everyone's just *mapping the streets*—"

"Don't," Wren said, sharper than she intended.

Casper stopped.

"Don't say everyone. My mother burned half these ledgers before I was born. She wouldn't tell me why until I was sixteen, and then she handed me the key to this room and said, *Somebody in this family has to remember what we did, or we'll do it again thinking it was new.*" Wren's hands had curled into fists without her permission; she made herself open them, finger by finger. "I didn't hunt anyone, Casper. Neither did she. But I carry the guild mark on my family name whether I want it or not. Every time I sign a map, I'm signing with the same hand that wrote those lists. So yes. I know exactly what inherited guilt looks like. I dust it every week."

The silence that followed was different from the silence before. Before, it had been a wall. Now it was more like a bridge that neither of them had quite tested.

Casper looked at her for a long moment. "You said you have records. Beyond the hunting ones."

"The guild kept everything," Wren said, grateful for solid ground. "Including things they never understood. Prophecies, court-treaties, correspondence with the Bell Wardens before the Wardens went official. And—" she pulled a slim volume from a locked drawer, bound in green leather gone soft with age, "—everything anyone ever wrote about the court beneath the mountain. If there's a way to get two souls back out of a goblin ledger, the first draft of the answer is probably in here."

Casper took the book like it might bite, which, given everything, was reasonable. "Why help me? Honestly. Not the noble answer."

Wren considered lying, decided against it, and heard herself say: "Because I've read these records my whole life and every single person in them is a name and a number, and I am so tired of names and numbers. Because you came into my archive and argued with me about the city layout and stole two biscuits off my desk and apologized to a chair when you bumped it, and I thought, *there's a person. An actual person.* And because—" she stopped, started again, pushed through, "because somebody in my family owes your family a debt that doesn't fit in any ledger, and I'd like to start paying it down."

Casper was quiet. Then, slowly, they held up their scarred palm, the oath-scar catching lamplight like a seam of silver.

"When I was nine," they said, "my grandmother taught me my first oath. She held my hands and she cried while she did it, and I never understood why until this week. Love and betrayal wearing the same face." They exhaled. "I'm not saying I forgive your ancestors. I don't even know how to hold that yet. But you dusted the guilt instead of hiding it. That counts for something. That counts for—" they searched, "—a lot, actually."

"It's the only thing my mother ever taught me that I liked learning."

"Gentle humor runs in your family too, then."

"On my father's side. They were cartographers. Very dry people. They drew swamps."

That got a real laugh out of Casper—short, surprised, like a bird startled off a wire—and Wren felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched. Saying it aloud had changed the shape of the room. Both of them carrying things they hadn't chosen, set down side by side on the table next to the green leather book, lighter than either had been alone.

They worked until the lamps burned low, heads bent together over the court-records, shoulders almost touching. Wren found herself cataloguing small things: the way Casper hummed when they read, badly; the way they said *sorry* to furniture; the way their eyes went distant and hungry whenever the ledger mentioned souls.

It was near midnight when Casper went still over a page near the back of the green book—an inventory, centuries old, written in a hand that shook.

"Wren," they said. "This entry. *One soul held in trust, Maren Brightwater, condition: living.*"

"That's your grandmother. We knew that."

"No." Casper turned the book around, and their finger trembled against the parchment. "Look at the date. Wren, this entry is *current*. The ink's fresh. Someone updated it last month." They looked up, and their eyes were wide and terrified and lit with something that might, under other circumstances, have been hope. "My grandmother didn't sell her soul a hundred years ago. She's alive. She's *down there*. Right now."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the archive clock began to strike twelve—and beneath its twelfth bell, Wren would swear forever after, came a second sound. Far away. Underground. Like a bell answering.
