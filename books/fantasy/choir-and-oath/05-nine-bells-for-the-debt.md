## 5. Nine Bells for the Debt

The trouble with selling luck-charms in a city that had outlawed magic was that eventually someone bought one that worked.

Casper had spent ten years making sure it wouldn't happen. Every charm in the shop—*Brightwater's Small Blessings*, hand-painted sign and all—was honest junk. Dried lavender for calm. A copper coin on a red string for fortune, which worked exactly as well as any copper coin. Knotwork bracelets that held nothing but knots. Casper charged fair prices, kept the ledgers boring, and smiled at customers with the particular blandness of a man who wanted very much to be forgotten.

It had almost worked, too. Ten years of almost.

The morning of the raid began like any other. Casper swept the step, watered the stubborn little rowan tree in its pot by the door—it wasn't magical, it was just a rowan that liked neglect—and opened the shutters onto Copperlane Market, where the fishmongers were shouting about herring and the baker's boy was stealing an apple with more ambition than skill.

Wren Halloway arrived at half past nine, carrying two meat pies and a look that meant she'd found something.

"You're up early," Casper said, which was a lie; Wren was always early. It was one of the things Casper liked about her, catalogued carefully and never spoken aloud, because liking things was how you got caught.

"I couldn't sleep." She set the pies on the counter. "I've been reading the margins."

"The margins of what?"

"Everything." She said it the way other people said *the sea* or *the gods*. "Prophecy books don't end where the writing ends, Casper. They end where the reader stops asking questions. And somebody stopped asking questions about your family a hundred years ago, right when it got interesting."

"Wren." Casper kept their voice light. "We agreed. Slowly. You were going to be mysterious about it for at least another week so I could pretend I wasn't terrified."

That earned the smile—the crooked one, ink-stained fingers drumming the counter, eyes too sharp for anyone's comfort. "Fine. Mysterious. I'll be mysterious over a pie." She pushed one across. "Eat. You look like a man who's about to have a bad day."

Casper would spend years afterward wondering if Wren had known. If some cartographer's instinct had read the streets of Copperlane that morning and seen the shape of what was coming, the way she read coastlines in the grain of old paper.

Because at ten minutes past ten, the bells began.

* * *

Nine of them. Everyone in Vellum's Reach knew the count. One bell meant a fire. Three meant a flood. But nine bells, rung from the Warden tower in slow, deliberate sequence, meant only one thing: *sanctioned entry*. The Bell Wardens were coming, and they were coming legally, which was worse than coming angry.

Casper's hands went still over the pie.

"Nine," Wren said quietly.

"I heard."

"Casper. What did you sell?"

"Nothing. I sell *nothing*. That's the entire business model." But even as they said it, Casper's mind was racing backward through the week. The charm for old Mistress Odile's grandson—knots and lavender, nothing else. The coin-string for the ferryman. The woman on Tuesday, the nervous one, who'd asked twice whether the charms were *real*, and Casper had given the practiced laugh and said real enough to part a fool from her coin—

And she'd laughed too. A beat too late. A beat too long.

"Oh," Casper said softly. "Oh, you absolute idiot. Someone tipped them."

"Back door?" Wren was already moving, cartographer-quick, reading the shop's geometry.

"There is no back door. There's a wall, and behind the wall there's a tanner's yard, and between me and the yard there's a wall." Casper was pulling the floorboard loose by the window seat anyway, out of pure habit, though all that was under it was a tin of buttons and ten years of pretending. "Ten years, Wren. Ten years of being aggressively unremarkable, and it takes one nervous woman and nine bells."

Boots in the lane. Not running—Wardens never ran; running suggested urgency, and urgency suggested fear, and fear was not sanctioned. They walked. Six of them, by the sound, and the creak of a warden-captain's ceremonial coat, stiff with iron thread.

Wren grabbed Casper's sleeve. "Listen to me. Whatever happens, don't do anything stupid."

"That's my whole personality."

"I mean it. Don't be clever. Clever gets people taken to the Bell Tower, and nobody comes down from the Bell Tower talking."

The door didn't open so much as decide to stop existing as an obstacle. Captain Vess Harrow filled the frame—a broad, gray woman with a face like a closed ledger and a voice pitched precisely for courtrooms.

"Casper Brightwater." Not a question. The name landed in Casper's chest like a dropped stone. Nobody had said it aloud in ten years. Casper had made sure of it. "By sanction of the Council of Bells, this premises is entered and searched. You will stand there, keep your hands visible, and speak only if spoken to."

"Technically you spoke first," Casper said, "so does that open a conversational window, or—"

"Hands."

Casper raised them. Wren, wisely, stood by the counter looking like a customer with nowhere better to be, which was its own kind of genius.

They searched badly, which was the frightening part. Wardens weren't looking for magic—they couldn't find magic, not really; the ban had been built on the assumption that magic left residue, and swordspeaker work left none. They were looking for *reasons*. A suspicious book. A suspicious knot. A suspicious amount of lavender. Reasons could be manufactured after the fact; everyone knew the Bell Tower had a room where reasons were written.

Captain Harrow herself drifted along the shelves, touching nothing, missing nothing. She paused at the rowan tree.

"Lovely," she said. "Rowan. Old warding wood, they say. Superstition says it turns away ill intent." Her eyes came up. "You'd know about superstition, Brightwater."

"It's a tree, Captain. It likes neglect. We have that in common."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "Your grandmother sold charms in this market too. Did you know that? Before the Reckoning. Maren Brightwater, luck-witch of Copperlane. My mother bought a charm off her once." She turned a dried lavender bundle over in her fingers. "Said it was the only honest magic she ever met. Said it never promised more than it gave."

"She sounds like a remarkable saleswoman."

"She burned for it."

The words fell into the shop and lay there. Casper heard their own heartbeat, slow and enormous. *She burned for it.* Yes. Casper knew. Casper had been eleven years old and standing in the crowd, holding a hand that smelled of lavender, while the city that grandmother had sworn to protect decided what she was worth.

"We found something," called a young Warden from the back room, and Casper's stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the cellar, into the mountain itself.

Harrow crossed the shop. In the young Warden's gloved hands: the prophecy book. Wren's copy—the archive copy, the one with the margins full of questions, the one with *Brightwater debt* underlined three times in a dead woman's careful hand.

Wren had brought it. Of course she had. She'd come straight from a sleepless night of reading, pie in hand, book under her arm, because she was Wren Halloway and caution was a country she'd never bothered mapping.

"This," said Harrow, "is a century-old text naming a living citizen as party to a soul-debt. This is *evidence*." She looked up, and for the first time her ledger-face showed something underneath: appetite. Ten years of quiet, and here was a catch big enough to make a career. "Take him."

Two Wardens stepped forward. Casper's vision narrowed to a bright, ringing point. Ten years. Ten years of smallness, of the practiced laugh, of watering a tree and charging fair prices and dying by inches in a shop the size of a coffin. And now hands were going to close around their arms and walk them to the Bell Tower, and the oath—the oath grandmother had passed down like eye color, the one Casper had buried under a decade of silence—would either rot unused in some iron cell, or be torn out of them in a room with hooks.

*Or,* said a voice that sounded uncomfortably like Maren, *you could stop hiding.*

The Wardens reached for Casper's arms.

Casper spoke.

Not the great oath. Not anything the old books would recognize. Just four words, small as a coin on a string, aimed at the floor beneath six pairs of boots:

"*Floor, remember being honey.*"

For one glorious second, nothing happened. Then the floorboards of Brightwater's Small Blessings remembered, with the enthusiasm of anything long-denied, exactly what honey was. Six Wardens sank to the ankles in amber-gold stickiness that had absolutely no business existing. Captain Harrow went down with a sound of pure bureaucratic outrage. The young Warden dropped the prophecy book; Wren caught it out of the air without appearing to move.

"What—" Harrow began, and then her boots made a sound like a kiss leaving a cheek, and she abandoned dignity entirely. "SEIZE HIM!"

Casper was already through the window, over the sill, rowan tree tucked under one arm like a stolen infant, Wren's hand locked in theirs. Behind them the shop filled with shouting and the wet tearing sound of boots losing arguments with floors.

They ran.

* * *

They ran through Copperlane with the whole market turning to watch, which was the point at which Casper understood, with a giddy, terrible clarity, that it was done. Finished. Ten years over. Every face in the crowd would carry the description home tonight: *ran past the fish stalls, the Brightwater one, the charm-seller, glowing faintly gold at the heels.* By tomorrow the story would have grown wings. By next week, someone would swear the floor had sung.

And the astonishing thing—the thing that made Casper laugh out loud, breathless, dodging a cart of herring—was that it didn't feel like terror anymore. It felt like air. Like a splint cut off a broken limb. Like the first deep breath after ten years of breathing through a straw.

"Left!" Wren shouted, hauling them down a alley she clearly knew from some map no one else had ever drawn. "There's a culvert under the tannery, it comes out past the Bell Wardens' checkpoint—"

"You've mapped my escape routes?"

"I mapped *everyone's* escape routes. It's called being thorough." She glanced back, wild-haired, triumphant, the prophecy book clutched to her chest. "Also I may have hoped you'd need them."

Behind them, distantly, the bells began again—but wrong. Not nine. Not any sanctioned count. A ragged, urgent peal, the sound of a tower whose schedule had been interrupted, and beneath it, threading through the alleys like smoke, came a smell of cold stone and old copper, and a voice—polite, unhurried, entirely unhurried in a way that made the skin crawl—

"Well struck, heir of Brightwater. Grandmother Maren sends her regards, and a request: *do try to run toward the mountain.*"

Casper skidded to a stop in the mouth of the culvert. Sitting on the drainage grate, legs crossed, tail curled neatly around one boot, was a goblin in a herald's gray livery, holding a lantern and an envelope sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"Sess," the goblin said, bowing slightly. "Herald of the Court Beneath. Do come in out of the light. We have *such* a lot to discuss."
