## 6. The Road Under

Casper

The Wardens reached the shop before the ink on Casper's apology note had dried.

They came at dusk in their grey coats and brass bells—the Bell Wardens, who wore the city's silence like a uniform—and Casper watched them from the roof of the chandlery next door while Wren crouched beside them, breathing hard, her satchel of maps clutched to her chest like a child.

"You set fire to your own shop?" Wren whispered.

"Charmed it to burn slow. Very slow. It'll smolder politely until morning, then put itself out." Casper kept their eyes on the street. "It seemed dramatic. I've been told I have a flair for it."

"By whom?"

"No one. That's rather the point."

Below, a Warden captain held up Casper's sign—*Brightwater Charms: Small Luck, Fair Prices*—and turned it over twice as though luck might be written on the back. The bell at his hip gave a single flat toll, the sound that meant *magic used here*, and half the market flinched. Ten years of small charms, harmless as bread crusts, and now every one of those customers would remember where they'd bought their fortune.

"I'm sorry," Wren said quietly.

"Don't be. You didn't tip them off." Casper had thought about it, actually, in the long cold hour after finding her name in the prophecy book. Had lain awake cataloguing everyone who might want them found. But Wren Halloway had cried when she'd read her own name aloud, and liars didn't usually cry about their own handwriting. "Someone did, though. Someone who knew what I was before I did."

"The ledger said you were coming," Wren said. "Maybe it wasn't only us reading it."

That was an unpleasant thought, so Casper declined to have it. "The mining roads go under the mountain from the old quarry gate. Two days' walk if we don't die. Three if we do it properly."

"And the Wardens?"

"They'll follow us to the gate and then stop." Casper stood, knees cracking. "The Reach's authority ends where Vellum ends. Past the boundary stones, the mountain keeps its own law. Which is why my family never should have made promises down there. Promises need witnesses, and the mountain has nothing but appetite."

Wren looked at them for a long moment. In the last light she looked less like a stranger than ever, and more like something out of the prophecy book—ink-stained, determined, doomed by someone else's pen. "You could still run the other way," she said. "Alone. Faster."

"I could," Casper agreed. "But I've spent ten years being no one, and it turns out no one is a terrible conversationalist." They offered their hand, palm down, scar hidden. "Come on, cartographer. Draw me a way under the world."

* * *

Wren

The old mining roads smelled of wet stone and older iron, and they echoed wrong—not the clean echo of empty space but a listening kind of quiet, as though the tunnels were holding their breath between sentences.

Wren had mapped three of these passages as an apprentice, working from guild records her grandmother had forbidden her to touch. She'd assumed the prohibition was boredom. Now, walking them by lamplight with a fugitive swordspeaker, she revised her assumptions considerably.

"Left here," she said, at a junction that wasn't marked on any living map.

"How do you know?"

"Because the right fork collapses in about forty paces. My great-grandmother collapsed it." Wren heard herself say it and winced. "Guild work. They were thorough about sealing things away. Swordspeakers mostly."

Casper stopped walking. The lamp threw their shadow huge against the tunnel wall, and for a moment neither of them moved.

"So when you offered me your help," Casper said, very evenly, "you were offering me the help of the people who hunted my family."

"My ancestors hunted your ancestors. Four generations ago. I've never—" Wren's hands wanted to draw something, anything, and there was no paper worth the truth, so she pressed them flat against her thighs. "I'm not going to pretend that doesn't count. It counts. It counts all the way down. But I brought you the records anyway, didn't I? I broke into my own family's vault to find that prophecy book because I thought—I hoped—if someone wrote your name down as a debt, maybe I could be the one who crosses it out."

The tunnel dripped somewhere behind them. Casper studied her the way she imagined one studies a map for errors—patiently, looking for the place where the terrain and the paper disagree.

"My grandmother sold her soul to buy our safety," Casper said at last. "So I know something about inheritance being a bill you didn't sign." They started walking again, slower. "Keep mapping, Halloway. And when this is over, if we're both still alive, you can teach me which parts of your family to be angry at. I find it helps to be specific."

It wasn't forgiveness. Wren hadn't expected it yet. But it was an invitation to keep walking beside them, and she took it, and the road went down.

* * *

Casper

Sess appeared at the third milestone, materializing out of a side gallery with the unhurried air of a host arriving at their own dinner party.

Goblin heralds were supposed to be rags and teeth and rattling bones, according to the stories. Sess was instead immaculate: a long coat of grey-green velvet, buttons of river pearl, and manners so polished they gleamed.

"Caspar Brightwater," Sess said, bowing exactly the correct depth for addressing a creditor. "And the promised guest, Miss Halloway. What a pleasure. The court has been expecting you since before either of you was born, which I understand is considered rude in some circles, and we do apologize."

"It's Casper," Casper said.

"Of course. Casper. The court regrets the error. It was made approximately one hundred years ago and errors, once entered, are terribly difficult to amend." Sess produced, from nowhere, a small silver tray bearing three cups of tea. Steam rose from them. Casper did not take one. "Your grandmother sends her regards."

Casper's heart performed a maneuver it had not rehearsed. "My grandmother is dead."

"She is collateral," Sess corrected gently, as if correcting a pronunciation. "There is a difference, though I grant it is subtle from above ground. She is alive, well, extremely sharp-tongued, and held in the finest comfort the court can provide. As security for the debt. The court finds living collateral far more motivating than gold. Gold does not write letters." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "She writes many letters. She asked me to tell you—" the herald consulted some inner document, or perhaps simply enjoyed the theater of it— "*'Tell the child the oath was mine, and the mistake was mine, and the way out is theirs to make. Also tell them to wear a coat.'*"

Wren made a small strangled sound that might have been laughter or grief. Casper stared at the tea until the steam blurred.

"She always did think weather was a moral issue," Casper said finally, and their voice cracked on the second word, and they let it, because Wren was watching and hiding had already cost them ten years.

Sess inclined their head. "I am instructed to guide you to the court. Not to hurry you, not to hinder you. Merely to see you arrive." Their smile was courteous and showed too many teeth, all of them neat. "The road is dangerous. There are Wardens behind you and older things ahead. May I walk with you?"

"Why would you help us?" Wren asked.

"Help?" Sess looked genuinely delighted by the word. "Oh, Miss Halloway. I am not helping you. I am delivering you. There is a difference, though I grant it is subtle from above ground."

* * *

Wren

On the second night, in a dry gallery where the lamplight reached both walls, Wren couldn't sleep and found Casper sitting up, turning their left hand over and over in the light.

The scar ran across the palm like a river on a map—a long pale seam where an oath had been cut out. Wren had seen scars before. She'd never seen one shaped so deliberately, so much like writing.

"What does it say?" she asked. "What it used to say. Before you swore it away."

Casper didn't look up. "It was a binding oath. The oldest form. *My blade for my blood, my blood for my word.* You speak it once and everything you say afterward becomes true, whether you mean it or not." A dry laugh. "Do you know what that's like? Sneezing and accidentally curing someone's fever. Arguing about the price of turnips and making the price true. Ten years old, and I told my grandmother I hated her, and for three days she believed it so completely she couldn't hear anything else."

"Oh," Wren said softly.

"I cut it out myself. Well. Grandmother held my hand steady. We both cried. Then I came here, where magic doesn't work, and became a person who sells lucky buttons." Casper flexed the scarred hand. "And it worked. Mostly. Except that the whole time, some book under the mountain had my name in it, waiting. Like the oath didn't care that I'd returned it. Like debts don't have receipts."

Wren sat down beside them. Close enough that their shoulders touched, which was either an accident or a decision, and she decided not to decide which.

"When I was little," she said, "my grandmother told me our family had done a terrible thing once, to keep everyone safe, and that the punishment was remembering. I used to think that was just how she explained guilt to children." She pulled her knees up. "But I remember, Casper. All of it. Every record. Every name the guild crossed out. If remembering is the punishment, then maybe remembering is also the payment. Maybe I've been paying it my whole life without knowing what for."

Casper was quiet a long time. The lamp flame leaned and straightened.

"Maren used to say love and debt look identical from the inside," they said. "I used to think it was wisdom. Now I think it was a confession." They glanced sideways at Wren, and in the low light their expression was unguarded in a way Wren suspected few people had ever seen. "For what it's worth—whatever your family did—I'm glad it was your name next to mine in that book. If I had to be somebody's doom, I'd rather it be someone who cries about handwriting and knows which tunnels collapse."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me," Wren said, "and it's deeply alarming."

"Welcome to knowing me."

Somewhere far off, through miles of stone, a bell tolled—one flat note, then another. Nine of them. Counting down.

Sess stepped out of the dark with their pearl buttons glinting, and even the herald's courtesy had thinned at the edges.

"Ah," Sess said. "The Wardens have reached the boundary stone. They will stop there, as promised. But the court has heard the ninth bell, and so has everything else sleeping under this mountain." They gestured down the gallery, toward a darkness that breathed. "If we could resume walking? My mistress dislikes waiting, and my mistress is not me."

Casper got to their feet and offered Wren their scarred hand, palm open this time, the old wound plain in the lamplight. She took it.

"Lead on," Casper told Sess. "And when we get there—tell the court the Brightwater debt is calling in a visit."

Down the tunnel, in the deep beneath everything, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the first bell began to ring again.
