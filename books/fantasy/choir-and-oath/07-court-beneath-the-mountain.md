## 7. Court Beneath the Mountain

The road under the mountain did not end so much as *admit* us.

One moment Sess was leading Wren and me down a mining shaft so old the support beams had turned to something like stone, and the next there was a doorway carved out of the dark itself, tall and narrow and hung with bells that made no sound at all.

"Announce yourselves quietly," Sess said. "The court is asleep until dusk, and it is rude to wake a court."

"What happens if we're rude?" Wren whispered.

"Nothing terrible," said Sess, in the tone of someone who had watched something terrible happen many times and found it dull through repetition.

I looked at Wren. She looked at me. Ten days on the road had taught us a whole language of glances, and this one meant: *we've come this far, and also I'm terrified, and also if you die I will be very annoyed.*

"After you," I said, because my grandmother had raised me to be polite, and apparently that training survived everything, even the end of the world I'd built for myself.

The bells stirred as we passed beneath them—not ringing, but humming, like they remembered what sound was and missed it.

* * *

The goblin court was not what the fireside stories promised. There were no mountains of bones, no rivers of fire. Instead there was a vast cavern roofed with glowworms arranged in constellations no surface astronomer had ever named, and beneath them a city of copper and paper. Goblin courts, it turns out, run on paperwork the way human cities run on gossip. Bureaus rose in tiers down the cavern walls, thousands of drawers, thousands of ledgers, and goblins everywhere—small ones on ladders, large ones at desks with lamps made from bottled lightning—recording things.

Recording *everything*, from the look of it. A goblin near the entrance was writing down, in a careful hand, the fact that three travelers had entered, what they were wearing, and—I leaned sideways to check—the exact number of blisters on Wren's left heel.

"Seven," Wren confirmed, catching my eye. "It's seven."

"They're very thorough," Sess said, with unmistakable pride.

The court was quieter than any court had a right to be. The goblins spoke in whispers and pen-scratches. Somewhere far below, deeper than the bureaus, deeper than the light, something breathed. Slowly. Patiently. I felt it in my teeth, in the oath-scar on my palm, which had woken on the road and hadn't slept since—a warm pulse, like a second heartbeat that had opinions about my life choices.

"The Brightwater debt," said a voice.

The herald who came toward us was old even by goblin measure, silver-skinned, dressed in robes covered in marginalia. She looked at me the way an archivist looks at a misfiled book: with deep personal offense.

"Casper Brightwater," she said. "Grandchild of Maren. You are one hundred and eleven years late."

"I was busy," I said. "Hiding. It's sort of my whole thing."

"Eleven years," she corrected. "Your grandmother's contract specified ten. We have been keeping accounts." She turned, robes whispering. "Come. She asked that you be brought to her the moment you arrived. She has been asking for eleven years."

* * *

They kept Maren Brightwater in a garden.

That was the detail that undid me—not chains, not a cell, but a small hanging garden off one of the upper bureaus, all moss and pale mushrooms and flowers that bloomed underground out of pure stubbornness, tended with tiny copper tools. And in the middle of it, kneeling in the dirt with dirt to her elbows, was my grandmother.

She was smaller. That was the first thing. The woman who had taught me my first oath had seemed carved from mountain rock, and now she was thin as a winter branch, her hair gone entirely white, her hands shaking slightly as she worked the soil. But when she looked up and saw me, her spine straightened like a drawn sword, and her eyes were exactly the same: bright, fierce, and absolutely unapologetic.

"Well," she said. "You look terrible. Road food?"

"Gran." My voice cracked on the word. Eleven years of rehearsed speeches, and all I managed was that.

"Come here, child, and mind the moonwort, it's delicate."

I crossed the garden in three strides and knelt in the dirt and held her, this small fierce diminished woman, and felt her grip my shoulders with hands much stronger than they looked. Behind us, Wren stood awkwardly at the garden's edge. Sess had vanished—announcing us, presumably, or recording us, or both.

"You shouldn't have come," Maren said into my hair. "I spent eleven years making sure you wouldn't."

"You could have just told me where you went."

"And you'd have followed me in a week. You were always a stubborn idiot. It's genetic." She pulled back and studied my face, then my hand—the scarred palm, glowing faintly gold now, betraying me. Her expression flickered through grief and pride so fast I nearly missed both. "You took it back. Your oath."

"Some of it. Enough to run with."

"That's how everyone starts." She turned her head. "And you're the Halloway girl."

Wren stiffened. "You know me?"

"I know your name. It's written downstairs in a ledger with my grandchild's underneath it." Maren said it plainly, without cruelty, the way she'd once told me the stove was hot. "I'm sorry about that. If I could tear the page out myself I'd have done it decades ago."

* * *

We sat among the mushrooms while the court's dusk gathered—glowworms dimming, lamps being lit, goblins filing home with their pens capped. Maren poured tea from a dented pot that must have been forty years old, and it tasted like every winter morning of my childhood, and I had to blink hard before I could listen.

"Tell me the truth," I said. "All of it. Not the story you gave me when I was nine."

Maren wrapped her shaking hands around her cup. "You mean the story where I sold my soul to buy the family's safety? The one I let you believe, because a coward's bargain was easier for a child to carry than the truth?"

"Yes. That one."

She laughed—a dry, real laugh. "Good. I hate repeating lies. Here it is, then, Casper. I never sold my soul. I *pledged* it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." She nodded downward, toward whatever breathed below. "There is something under this mountain older than the goblins, older than oaths, maybe older than words themselves. The First Speaker. The thing that first learned language could shape the world—and learned it the way a forest fire learns fuel. Every swordspeaker line descends from its first students. Including ours."

The oath-scar pulsed, and I pressed my palm flat against my knee. "The oaths bind it."

"The oaths are the only cage it has. Generations of Brightwaters renewed the binding with their own souls as collateral—pledged, not sold. Our souls stay ours; we keep living, loving, gardening, all of it. But when we die, the debt comes due, and the court holds our pledge as surety against the day the binding fails." She sipped her tea. "I renewed it thirty years ago. My mother renewed it before me, and her mother before her, back eleven generations. It was never a sale, child. It was a watch. The most important watch there is."

Something cold moved through me. "Then why hide it? Why let me grow up thinking you were a monster who traded souls like coins?"

"Because the binding weakens each time it passes down. Fewer speakers, thinner oaths, less belief in the world to draw on." Her eyes found mine, steady and awful. "When I pledged, the court's scholars told me the truth: the chain would likely break within two generations. And whoever carried the debt last would face a choice no one should have to make. I wanted you to have twenty good years believing your grandmother was merely wicked, rather than five frightened years knowing you were chosen. Cowardice," she added briskly, "but I recommend it. Kept me young."

"It didn't keep you young at all," I said. "Look at you."

"Underground air," she said. "Terrible for the skin."

Wren had gone very still beside me, teacup untouched. "The ledger," she said slowly. "My name. If the binding is collateral—if the debts are held against the cage failing—then why is *my* name in it? I'm not a Brightwater. I'm not anything. I draw maps."

Maren's face did something complicated. Pity and apology and old anger, all wearing the same expression.

"The cage needs feeding," she said quietly. "Not just pledging. A binding that old drinks. The court keeps it fed on borrowed souls—souls lent willingly, mostly, by people who owed the court other debts, people who never quite understood the terms. It's the part of this place I hate most, and I've argued about it with the archivists for eleven years." She reached out and took Wren's hand. "A century ago, a Halloway—an Oathbreaker guildsman, back when your family still believed in breaking instead of hunting—made a bargain here. He lent his line's names to the court as payment. Yours came due this year. It was never about you, girl. You were born into a bill."

The silence that followed was the kind that has weight. Above us the glowworm constellations wheeled slowly, indifferent. Below us, the breathing continued, and I realized with a lurch that I could feel it more clearly than before—as though the scar on my palm were a window, and something behind the glass had noticed the light change.

"So the ledger," I said. "Our names. What happens when the court calls them?"

Maren set down her cup. For the first time since I'd arrived, her steadiness slipped, and I saw eleven years of fear looking out through my grandmother's eyes.

"When the First Speaker wakes fully—and it is waking, Casper, faster every month—the court will call every pledged soul to renew the binding at once. Yours, mine, hers." She nodded at Wren. "The renewal requires a speaker strong enough to hold the whole oath alone. There hasn't been one in three hundred years. They believe you might be. Which is why your name sat under hers in that book, waiting. You're not a debtor, my love. You're their last hope, and they intend to spend you."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the cage breaks, and everything the First Speaker touches becomes a word in its mouth." She smiled then, small and crooked, the smile she used to give me before lessons. "Unless someone writes a better sentence. But that's a conversation for after you sleep. You'll want your strength."

Before I could answer, the garden door opened. Sess stood there, courteous as ever, but for the first time their courtesy had an edge to it, like a blade wrapped in a napkin.

"Forgive the interruption," Sess said. "The archivists report a disturbance in the records hall. Someone is in the archive. Someone appears to be—" a pause, the goblin equivalent of wincing, "—attempting to erase a page."

Wren's chair was already empty.

She'd been sitting beside me the entire time, quiet as ink, and I hadn't seen her leave.
