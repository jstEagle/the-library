## 8. The Ledger of Names

The goblin court had a library. Of course it did. Wren had been underground for two days and had already learned the first law of goblin society: everything was a ledger, and every ledger was kept somewhere.

She found it by accident, which was a lie she told herself later. In truth she'd spent most of the afternoon drifting away from Sess's courteous tour, marking turns in her head — left at the bone chandelier, right past the hall where something enormous and slow breathed behind a door of black iron, straight on where the torchlight went from green to honest yellow. Cartographers didn't get lost. They just pretended to wander.

"You are going the wrong way," Sess had said, not unkindly, an hour ago.

"I'm exploring."

"Ah." The herald's ears had twitched in what Wren suspected was delight. "The Brightwater has that same look. It runs in the family, this walking-toward-danger-while-calling-it-curiosity."

Wren had smiled and kept walking, and Sess had let her go, which was either courtesy or a trap, and with goblins it was honestly hard to say.

Now she stood before a door of grey stone carved with a single word in a script older than any map she'd ever copied. She couldn't read it, but she didn't need to. She could feel it, the way you can feel a storm through a wall: *records live here.*

"Right," she whispered, to no one, because saying things out loud made them real and she needed this one to be very real indeed. "Let's see what your books have to say about me."

* * *

The archive was vast and cold and organized in a way that made her heart ache with professional longing. Scrolls hung from ceiling racks like cured fish. Books were shelved spine-inward, their titles written on the fore-edges in glowing ink. And everywhere, everywhere, ledgers. Rows of them. Centuries of them.

A goblin archivist sat at a desk near the entrance, ancient and tiny, wearing spectacles with four lenses apiece. It looked up at Wren without surprise.

"Human," it said. "Halloway branch?"

Wren's stomach dropped. "How did you—"

"Ink under the fingernails, paper cuts on three fingers, and you're standing like someone who alphabetizes for pleasure." The archivist turned a page. "Also your name is in the pending acquisitions ledger. Third shelf, second case. We do read our own records."

"Of course you do," Wren said faintly. Then, because her grandmother's training was louder than her fear: "Is there an index?"

The archivist looked at her over all eight lenses, and something that might have been respect moved across its face like weather. "Case nine. Red spine. Don't dog-ear anything or I'll know, and I am very old and have nothing but time."

* * *

The index led her down, and down again, into a lower gallery where the air tasted of iron and old promises. Here the ledgers changed. Above, the books had recorded debts — names traded, souls pawned, interest accruing in decades. But these books were different. These were *bindings*. Their covers were stitched shut with wire, and each one hummed faintly against her fingers when she touched it, like a struck bell still deciding whether to ring.

And there, on a lectern beneath a hooded lamp, lay the current volume. Open. Waiting.

Her name was on the first page.

Not just her name. Her whole line, in a column descending like a family tree drawn by someone who thought people were roots. *HALLOWAY, descended of the Oathbreakers' Guild, given in payment of the Brightwater debt, per agreement of the court and the guild's founding oath.* Beneath it, in fresher ink: a date. Nine days hence.

Nine days. She'd been a line item for a century and hadn't known until a man with a luck-charm stall and a scarred palm bought her tea.

"No," she said out loud, and the word came out steadier than she felt. "No, absolutely not."

She took out her knife — the good one, guild steel, the one heirloom nobody had ever explained to her until last week — and set the blade against the page.

The ledger *screamed*.

Not aloud. In her bones. The ink writhed away from the steel like water from oil, and her name swam deeper into the parchment, sinking as if the page were a pond. She pressed harder. The knife sang a high, thin note. The ink sank further.

"Oh, you absolute *book*," she snarled, which was not the language of heroic defiance, but it was honest. "Fine. Fine! We'll do this properly."

Because here was the thing about cartographers, the thing Casper had understood when they'd watched her work and gone quiet in that particular way of theirs: maps weren't pictures. Maps were arguments. Every border was a claim, every name a small act of power. You didn't erase a river by scratching it out. You redrew the country around it.

She couldn't strike her name. So she would make the ledger agree it didn't belong.

* * *

It took her four hours, two false starts, and one genuinely alarming moment when the book tried to add her blood to its margins and she had to slap it shut on her own thumb. (The archivist, passing by with a scroll, had said only, "Don't fight it, redirect it," which turned out to be the single most useful piece of advice anyone had given her underground.)

The oaths binding her name were old ones — guild-oaths, human-made, sold to the court long ago by ancestors who'd thought they were saving their children. But old oaths had grammar. Grammar had seams. And Wren Halloway had spent ten years correcting other people's maps, which is to say, finding where the world had been described wrongly and insisting, in careful ink, on the truth.

She wrote in the margin, small and precise:

*Given in payment of the Brightwater debt* — *given freely? No. Given in ignorance, by the dead, of the living. A promise made over my head binds my hair, not me.*

Then, after a moment, she added:

*Amended by the named party. Consent withheld.*

The ledger shuddered. The ink around her name went grey, then pale, then — slowly, grudgingly, like frost giving up on a window — faded to a ghost of itself. Not gone. Not yet. But *contestable*. A claim disputed rather than owned. She stared at it and laughed, a shaky, delighted sound that echoed strangely in the cold gallery.

"I'm not a page," she told the book. "I'm the cartographer. Get used to it."

That was when she noticed what her lamp had been illuminating all along, at the back of the lectern, half-hidden by the open cover of the binding-ledger.

A second book. Older. Its wire stitching had rusted through in a dozen places, and through the gaps, the pages breathed.

* * *

She should have run. Later, she would think about how clearly she should have run. But the breathing book had a title, and the title was in a script she *could* read, because her grandmother had taught it to her as a game when she was seven — *guild-hand, little wren, the letters that unmade the speakers* — and the title said:

*THE FIRST WORD.*

Wren touched the cover. The humming here wasn't bell-like. It was low and vast, a note held so long it had become the room itself.

Through the largest gap in the wires she saw a page, and on the page, writing that moved — not wriggling like the ledger's defensive ink, but moving the way weather moves, slow and continental. Words reorganizing themselves. Sentences coming apart at the joints.

And beneath them, in a hand she recognized with a lurch — the same hand, the same slanted B, the same crossed letters, that wrote *Casper Brightwater* on the flyleaf of every book in that shop above — a marginal note, decades old:

*Maren Brightwater, visiting. The bindings weaken. Each generation swears less and means it less. The words are fraying like an old rope. Something must be renewed, or —*

The rest was torn away.

Fraying. Wren stood very still, her heart doing arithmetic. The oaths that held the thing behind the black iron door — the enormous slow breathing she'd heard on her way down — those oaths were made of sworn words, generation after generation of swordspeakers feeding strength into the cage with every oath they spoke. And the Brightwaters had stopped. Maren had sold herself to keep the lines going from inside. Casper had walked away entirely. Ten years of silence. Ten years of a rope losing strands.

The First Speaker wasn't waking because it was strong.

It was waking because *they had all been trying so hard to be free of it* that nobody was holding the door anymore.

"Oh," Wren said quietly. "Oh, that's horrible. That's the most horrible fair thing I've ever heard."

Because it was fair, in the way old debts are fair. Casper's gift wasn't just a gift. It had never been just a gift. It was maintenance duty, inherited like eye color, and everyone in the family had mistaken the chain for the treasure and tried to be rid of it, and now the door was opening.

She looked at her own name, greyed and contested on the ledger beside it. She looked at the breathing book.

She could take the knowledge and run. Find Casper, tell them, let them decide — it was their inheritance, their rope, their door. That would be the sensible thing. The kind thing, even.

But Wren had just spent four hours teaching a century-old ledger that she was not a line item, and some lessons, once learned, apply to everything.

She tore a blank leaf from the back of the binding-ledger — the archivist would know, the archivist always knew, but this seemed like the sort of crime one confessed eventually — and copied down everything she could read through the gaps: the fraying counts, the dates, the structure of the old binding-oaths, the terrible elegant shape of the cage. If Casper was going to face whatever was behind that iron door, they deserved more than a warning.

They deserved a map.

She was folding the pages into her coat when the whole archive went silent. Not quiet — *silent*, the way a forest goes silent, every hummed ledger, every whispering scroll, all of it stopping at once like a choir holding its breath.

Far above, muffled through miles of stone, a bell began to ring. Not one of the court's bells. This one came from the direction of the throne hall, where Casper had gone to confront a grandmother who was supposed to be dead.

One ring. Two. Three.

Sess had told her, on the first day, with heraldic precision: *"If ever you hear bells from the throne hall, come running. It will mean the First Speaker stirs. It will mean there is very little time."*

Four. Five.

Wren ran.

Behind her, in the dark of the lower gallery, the old book breathed out — long, and slow, and satisfied — and the first strand of the rope snapped with a sound like a distant harp string breaking, and somewhere in the throne hall, Casper Brightwater turned toward the sound with their scarred palm burning like a brand.
