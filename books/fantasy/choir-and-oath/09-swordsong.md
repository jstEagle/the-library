## 9. Swordsong

The First Speaker did not look like a monster. That was the worst part.

Casper had expected something with too many teeth, too many joints, a shape that argued with the eye. Instead the thing that rose from the cracked ledger-stone at the heart of the court looked like *language given a body*—a figure made of overlapping voices, tall as the cavern's ceiling, its edges flickering between faces. Some of those faces Casper knew from portraits in grandmother's stories. Some of them were their own.

"Well," said the First Speaker, in a voice like ten thousand people agreeing at once. "The Brightwater debt comes home."

Around them, the goblin court was in chaos—or what passed for chaos in a place that prized etiquette above oxygen. Courtiers scattered like startled moths. Sess stood his ground near the great doors, one hand pressed flat against them as if he could hold back the mountain itself by good manners alone. Wren was somewhere in the archive galleries above, and Casper's whole chest ached toward her like a compass needle.

And Maren—Maren Brightwater, diminished to a wisp of herself after decades as collateral, stood at Casper's shoulder with her chin up and her knuckles white around nothing.

"You shouldn't have come," she murmured.

"You taught me to finish what I start," Casper said. "You just didn't teach me when to stop listening."

The First Speaker spread hands that were more echo than flesh. Where it stepped, the fraying oaths that had held it for centuries smoked off its skin like frost in sunlight. Casper could feel the old bindings snapping one by one—not heard so much as *remembered*, each one a thread their bloodline had tied and retied for generations until the tying was all anyone knew how to do.

"I offer you a bargain, last swordspeaker," the First Speaker said. "As I offered your grandmother. As she refused."

Casper blinked. "She told me she traded her soul. She never said she refused you."

"She refused to *renew* me," the creature said. "There is a difference. She gave her soul to the court instead—to keep the bindings fed, to buy time. A coward's arithmetic. But then, love makes cowards of us all." Its many mouths smiled with one mouth. "I offer better terms. Renew the binding yourself. Your soul instead of hers. Yours instead of the girl cartographer's. Yours instead of every borrowed name in this court's ledger. In exchange—" the cavern leaned closer, hungry, delighted— "*everything*. Every word you speak will be true. Every truth will be law. You will not hide in a market selling luck-charms, Casper Brightwater. You will be luck itself. Fate itself. The world will hold still when you clear your throat."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Casper thought about the market. About the little charms they'd carved—acorns and buttons and bent nails—and the customers who'd bought them not because they worked but because Casper smiled while handing them over. Ten years of being small on purpose. Ten years of practicing invisibility like scales on an instrument.

And underneath that, quieter: ten years of wanting, so badly it felt like a wound, to be seen.

"Say yes," whispered a courtier. Then another. Then a dozen voices, then hundreds, the whole court caught in the undertow of the offer, because who among them hadn't dreamed of mattering?

Maren grabbed Casper's sleeve. Her hand weighed almost nothing. "Don't listen. It lies in perfect grammar."

"It isn't lying," Casper said slowly. "That's the horrible thing. It's offering exactly what it says." They looked at their palm—the oath-scar, the pale seam where a decade ago they had spoken the renunciation that hollowed them out. *I am no one's blade.* It had been the bravest lie of their life, because they'd meant it and it hadn't taken, because oaths don't die just because you stop feeding them.

Grandmother's voice, from a kitchen twenty years gone: *An oath is a door, little one. You can lock it, bar it, board it over—but the doorway remains. The only way to unmake a door is to stop building houses.*

"Casper," Maren said, urgent now, frightened. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking you built a house out of locked doors," Casper said. "So did the court. So did the guild. So did everyone who ever picked up a sword and called it a promise." They turned to face the First Speaker fully, and something in their chest—the gift, the curse, the family inheritance nobody asked for—rose up singing.

They drew their sword.

Not the court-forged blade they'd carried down the mining roads. The old one. Their grandmother's, which Sess had returned to them at the gates with a bow and the words *"Property of the estate; the estate is present."* The steel hummed under their grip like a struck tuning fork, and every oath their ancestors had ever spoken woke up inside it.

"No," said the First Speaker.

"Yes," Casper agreed pleasantly. "But not the way you mean."

* * *

Wren came skidding into the chamber at that exact moment, ink-stained fingers black to the wrist, clutching a torn page like it was a live coal. Later, Casper would learn she'd cut her own name out of the ledger with a letter opener and a great deal of profanity. For now, all Casper saw was her—alive, furious, *there*—and the sight steadied them the way a fixed star steadies a ship.

"The bindings are fraying because they were badly written!" she shouted across the chaos, holding up the page. "I found the original draft! Maren's grandmother's draft—it's got margin notes! It was never supposed to last forever, it was supposed to be *rewritten every generation*, and nobody did, because—" she jabbed a finger at the First Speaker— "because *you* told them a permanent cage was safer than a working hinge!"

"A hinge swings both ways," the First Speaker said, and its voice cracked—for the first time in centuries, it sounded tired. Old. Afraid, maybe, in the way only ancient things can be afraid: of endings.

"So does a door," Casper said. "I've been thinking about doors."

They planted the sword point-down on the ledger-stone, put both hands on the pommel, and did the thing no swordspeaker had ever done in the entire history of swordspeaking.

They spoke an oath with no one bound in it.

"I am Casper Brightwater," they said, and the words rang off the cavern walls and came back changed, "last of a long line of people who mistook duty for destiny. I inherit the debts, and I decline the terms. Hear me, First Speaker: I do not bind you. I do not cage you. I *release* you—from the prophecy, from the ledger, from the hunger we fed you because feeding you was easier than asking what you were hungry *for*."

The court went absolutely silent.

"You cannot release me," the First Speaker breathed. "I am hunger. Hunger cannot end."

"Hunger ends every time someone eats," Casper said. "That's the whole trick. Nobody ever tried it because eating requires *sharing*, and binding requires only fear, and fear is so much cheaper." They looked down at their scarred palm. "So here's my price. Not a soul. Not mine, not Wren's, not my grandmother's. My gift. All of it. Every oath my blood can ever speak, burned as fuel for this one. No Brightwater after me will ever bind anything to anything again. Take the power instead of the person. Take the *sword* instead of the hand."

"That is not how bargains work," the First Speaker said.

"It's exactly how gifts work," said Maren, and her old voice came back strong for one sentence, the voice from the kitchen, the voice that had taught a child her first oath. "You wouldn't know. Everything you've ever gotten was taken."

The First Speaker looked at her for a long moment. Then at Casper. Then—at last, and this was the moment Casper would remember for the rest of their life—at the sword humming under their hands, waiting.

"Do it, then," it said quietly. "Sing."

Casper sang.

It wasn't music, quite. It was the sound an oath makes when it stops being a chain and becomes a chord—all the inherited words rising together: the first oath Maren taught them, the renunciation, the debt, the prophecy, the guild's hunting-creed, the court's ledgers, a century of names written down by people who believed a name was a leash. Casper gathered all of it the way you gather kindling, held it against the gift in their chest, and let go.

Light took the cavern. Not violent light—warm light, hearth-light, the light of a lamp left burning in a window for someone coming home. The prophecy book in Wren's arms went blank page by blank page with a sound like snow sliding off a roof. The ledger-stone crumbled to ordinary rock. All through the goblin court, souls that had been borrowed for decades slipped their ledgers like birds off a wire, and the courtiers—who had expected terror, who had braced for terror—stood blinking in the warmth with tears running down their grey cheeks, because none of them, apparently, had ever been released from anything before either.

The First Speaker shrank as the light touched it. Shrank and softened, its thousand borrowed faces settling into one—old, plain, human-shaped, exhausted beyond measure. When it finally spoke, it was just a voice. One voice. Cracked and small.

"What am I, now?"

Casper's gift went with the answer—a final flare, bright as a forge, and then ash, and then silence where the singing had been. Empty. Permanently, irrevocably empty. They would spend years learning to live with that quiet, the way you learn to live with a missing limb, touching the absence at odd hours. But that was later.

"Now?" Casper said, kneeling to the small grey figure huddled where a god had been. "Now you're somebody with nowhere to be. I know exactly how that feels. It's terrible for about a year, and then it's the best thing that ever happened to you." They held out their hand. "Come up to the city. There's soup."

The former First Speaker stared at the offered hand. "Soup," it repeated, in the tone of a scholar encountering a new branch of mathematics.

"It's very good soup," said Wren, appearing at Casper's side, ink-blackened and radiant. "Also, if you ever write anything down again, I'm confiscating the pen."

* * *

They climbed out of the mountain in the grey hour before dawn, when Vellum's Reach was just beginning to smell like bread. Maren walked between them, humanly aged, humanly slow, leaning on Sess's arm and complaining about the stairs with the enthusiasm of a woman who had decades of complaining saved up and intended to spend every coin of it.

Casper's sword-hand ached. Their palm was smooth where the scar had been—no mark at all now, nothing to hide, nothing to show. Just skin. Just a hand, good for carrying things.

Wren laced her fingers through theirs without asking, the way she'd started doing somewhere on the road, and Casper realized they weren't hiding anymore. Hadn't been since the bells. Might never manage it again.

"Everyone's going to know," Wren said quietly. "What you did. What you are. What you're not anymore."

"Mm," Casper said, watching the sun come up over the market where they'd sold luck-charms to people who deserved better luck than buttons and bent nails. "Any ideas what a retired miracle does for a living?"

Wren grinned. "I hear there's a building for rent near the archive. Good light. Terrible acoustics."

"Terrible acoustics," Casper repeated, and laughed until Maren told them both to hush, some of them were elderly and owed peace and quiet, and the four of them came down the mountain road into the city together, loud as a choir.
