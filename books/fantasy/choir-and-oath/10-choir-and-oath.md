## 10. Choir and Oath

The school had been a bakery once, and it still smelled faintly of bread, which Casper considered the finest possible foundation for an education. The oven was gone, but the great brick arch where it had stood remained, and Wren had declared it perfect for the chalkboard.

"Perfect," Casper had said, "except that children will assume a witch lives in there."

"A retired witch," Wren said. "One who only curses people who don't finish their sums."

They had laughed about that for longer than the joke deserved, because that was what freedom did to people. It made them silly in the good way, like dogs let off the lead.

Now, on a bright morning in late spring, Casper stood at the front of the room and watched eleven children attempt their first charm.

Not a real one. There were no real ones anymore—not from Casper's hands. The gift had burned out of them like a star burning out of a lamp, leaving nothing but warmth behind. But words still mattered. Words had always mattered more than magic; magic was only what happened when someone meant a word so completely that the world agreed to listen. So they taught meaning. They taught breath and intention and the difference between saying *please* and *meaning* please, which turned out to be a lesson even the grown-ups in Vellum's Reach kept quietly auditing through the windows.

"Again," said Pip Tarrow, who was seven and bossy as a magpie. "You have to say it like you're not scared of it."

"I'm not scared of it," said little Odette, who absolutely was.

"Everyone's scared of it the first time," Casper said, crouching by her desk. "That's how you know it matters. Now. *Lantern.* Say it like the dark is listening."

Odette squeezed her eyes shut. "*Lantern.*"

Nothing happened, because nothing was supposed to happen. But Odette opened her eyes with the astonished expression of someone who has shouted into a canyon and heard her own voice come back, and Casper felt something settle in their chest that no oath had ever given them.

"That's it," they said. "That's the whole trick. You just told the truth."

* * *

Wren came at midday, as she always did, with ink on her fingers and dust on her boots and a rolled map under her arm that she handled more gently than she handled most people.

She'd been mapping the roads beneath the mountain all winter. It was slow work—Sess served as her guide, appearing out of side tunnels with unnerving courtesy and comments like *"The third fork is dishonest this season, madam, I would take the fourth"*—and the maps she produced were unlike anything the surface guilds had ever seen. She drew the goblin court itself: the vaulted bone-and-lantern halls, the ledger archive with its ten thousand names, the small warm cell where Maren Brightwater had spent sixty years as a lock rather than a prisoner.

The Wardens had wanted copies. Wren had sold them exactly one, at a price designed to insult them, and donated the money to the school.

"How many today?" she asked, unrolling the newest sheet across Casper's desk.

Casper looked. Wren's cartography had a quality Casper had never seen in anyone else's work: she drew places the way you'd draw a person you loved, every crooked passage rendered with affectionate precision. At the bottom corner, in her small neat hand, she'd written: *No one is lost here who is remembered.*

"Eleven," Casper said. "And Odette spoke her first word-truth. She said *lantern* like the dark owed her money."

Wren grinned. "That girl's going to be trouble."

"The best kind." Casper traced the map's edge. "Any change below?"

"One." Wren's voice went quiet in the particular way it did when she was choosing words carefully, which was rare enough that Casper gave her their full attention. "The First Speaker's prison—it's not a prison anymore. It's a garden now. Sess says things are growing there. Small things. Kind ones." She hesitated. "He says the court doesn't know what to do with kindness. They keep visiting it like tourists."

Casper laughed until they had to sit down.

It had been a year since Swordsong. A year since Casper had stood before the thing older than oaths and refused it—not with power, which they no longer had, but with a sentence spoken so plainly it had unmade a century of binding:

*I release you. I release her. I release us all. Let no name be a debt again.*

The prophecy book in the court's archive had gone blank, page after page, like snow melting upward. Maren had walked out of her cell upright, blinking at the lantern-light, sixty years old in spirit and suddenly eighty in body, aging humanly at last, complaining cheerfully about her knees the entire road home.

And Casper had come above ground with empty hands and a full chest, and discovered that empty hands could hold quite a lot.

* * *

Maren visited on Thursdays. She arrived by the market route so she could inspect the luck-charm stalls—still operating, though Casper no longer made them; a former apprentice named Doss had taken over, charging honest prices—and she arrived loudly, announcing herself from the street so no child would startle.

"Grandmother," Casper said, "you don't have to announce yourself. This is a school. Nothing here bites."

"Everything bites if you're my age." She settled onto the bench by the window, which had become *her* bench, the way certain cats own certain chairs. "What are they learning?"

"Today? That being afraid of your own voice is optional."

"Mm." Maren watched the children in the yard, chasing each other around the old bread-arch. "I taught you your first oath in a cellar with the shutters drawn. I thought I was protecting you." Her hands, spotted and steady, folded in her lap. "I was teaching you to hide. Sixty years underground bought me time to understand the difference, and I'd have traded every year of it to have understood sooner."

"You understood eventually," Casper said. "That's more than most people manage."

"It's not forgiveness I'm fishing for, child."

"I know." Casper sat beside her on the bench. "But you can have some anyway. Interest accrued over a year of Thursdays."

Maren snorted—a very undignified sound for a woman who had once held a demon in a cage with her soul—and patted Casper's knee. On the yard, Odette had climbed atop the bread-arch and was shouting *lantern* at the sky with tremendous authority, and several smaller children were shouting it back, and it sounded, honestly, like a choir.

"There," Maren said softly. "There's the thing I swore away my whole life trying to buy."

* * *

The sign had been Wren's idea, though she denied it.

Vellum's Reach businesses hung shingles, per city custom: *Tallow & Wick*, *The Gilded Scale*, *Halloway Cartography* (new, bold, hanging proud over her shop door). But schools, in the Reach, traditionally hung nothing, because teachers were meant to be humble and also because nobody had ever bothered to make it a rule worth breaking.

Casper had ordered the board in autumn. It had sat behind the shop counter all winter while they sandpapered it on slow evenings, Wren reading aloud from adventure serials, both of them pretending the board wasn't waiting for something.

In spring, on the first warm morning, Casper carried it outside along with a pot of black paint and the good brush.

The children gathered immediately, because children can smell an event the way sharks smell blood. Pip Tarrow appointed herself supervisor. Odette stood closest, hands clasped, vibrating slightly.

"What's it going to say?" she demanded.

"My name," Casper said.

"You have a name? We thought you were just *Teacher*."

"I have several. Most of them I spent ten years pretending weren't mine." Casper dipped the brush. "This one's my favorite."

They painted slowly, letting each letter dry true. *Choir and Oath School of Words.* And beneath it, in letters larger than modesty would have permitted a year ago, larger than fear had ever allowed:

*Casper Brightwater.*

The paint gleamed wet in the sun. Somewhere down the street, a Bell Warden on morning rounds stopped, read the sign, and—this was the miracle, the small ordinary miracle the whole city had been growing into for a year—simply nodded and walked on. The ban had not been repealed. It had simply, quietly, become beside the point, the way old laws do when everyone agrees to outgrow them.

"Well?" Pip said. "Aren't you going to say something clever?"

Casper considered. Ten years ago, they would have said nothing. Five years ago, they would have said something safe. Today, they set down the brush, wiped their hand on their apron, and spoke the way Odette had spoken—like the dark was listening, like the truth owed them nothing and they owed it everything.

"My name is Casper Brightwater," they said, "and this is my school, and you are all welcome in it. Every one of you. Exactly as you are."

The words didn't glow. They didn't reshape reality. They did something better: they landed, in eleven young hearts and one old one and one ink-stained cartographer leaning in the doorway with her arms crossed and her eyes suspiciously bright, and they stayed.

Wren crossed the street, took Casper's paint-spotted hand, and laced her fingers through it in front of God, the Wardens, and the entire Tarrow family.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"I was pacing myself."

"For ten years?"

"It's called craftsmanship."

Maren laughed from the bench, and Odette cheered, and down in the warm dark beneath the mountain something kind and small bloomed in a garden that used to be a cage, and above it all the sign swung gently on its new hinges, catching the light.

Casper Brightwater looked at their own name written plain where anyone could read it, and found—to their lasting surprise—that it felt nothing like exposure.

It felt like coming home.
