## 1. The Quiet Market

The trick to selling luck in Correnholt was to never look like you were selling anything at all.

Silas Brightwater had learned that in her first winter here, twelve years and a lifetime ago, when she'd still flinched at every gray cloak in a crowd. You didn't set out a stall with charms hung from hooks like a fortune-teller at a fair. You set out a table of ordinary things—candles, salt, bootlaces, bundles of dried herbs for stew—and you let the right customers find the right things among them. A knotted red cord buried at the bottom of the twine basket. A river-stone with a hole through it, sitting innocently in the tray of buttons. A scrap of parchment folded eight times and slipped inside a candle's wrapping paper, which only worked if you knew to look for the slight thickening at the fold.

Harmless things. That was the point. That had always been the point.

None of them did anything. Silas hadn't been able to charm so much as a hangnail since the Saltfen, since the morning Veyra's voice cut out mid-prayer like a singer struck dumb between one note and the next. The knots she tied were just knots. The river-stones were just stones. What she sold, when she sold to those who knew to ask, was the shape of hope—an old ritual performed by someone who remembered how it went, even if the god it addressed had stopped listening.

It turned out there was a brisk market in the shape of hope. In a city where magic would get you a cell beneath the Choir of the Silent and a set of questions you couldn't answer without damning yourself, people still needed *something* to hold when the nets came up empty and the fevers climbed and the letters from distant children went unanswered. They needed something small enough to hide in a pocket.

Silas understood that need the way a fish understands water.

"Three coppers," she said, "and I'll throw in the candle."

Maren Ostrey—the fisherman's widow, though everyone in Salt Row called her that as if widowhood were her given name—turned the knotted cord over in her cracked hands. Her husband's boat had gone down off the Teeth in autumn, and now her son was talking about taking the *Perseverance* out himself come spring, because grief made boys stupid and hunger made mothers desperate.

"This is the same knot you sold Joss's mother," Maren said. "Before his first deep run."

"It is."

"And he came back."

"He came back." Silas kept her voice flat, unhurried. She never oversold. Overselling was how charlatans died; under Correnholt law, fraud was a fine, but sorcery was a trial. "The knot doesn't keep him safe, Maren. The knot reminds him somebody wants him home. That's most of what safety is."

Something moved behind the widow's eyes—not quite belief, but its cousin. She counted out three coppers, then pushed a fourth across the trestle table anyway. "For the candle."

"The candle's two."

"Then light it for me sometime. When you hear how he does on his first run."

Silas took the fourth copper because refusing it would have meant explaining herself, and explanations were the one luxury she could no longer afford. "I will," she said, and meant it, which surprised her a little.

Maren tucked the cord into her shawl and shuffled off toward the fish stalls, and Silas exhaled slowly and looked out over the gray market the way she did every morning: cataloging exits, noting uniforms, measuring the crowd against the day's appetite for trouble.

Correnholt's market district didn't deserve the name *gray* for its weather, though the weather helped—a permanent low ceiling of cloud the color of dishwater, pressing down on the slate roofs and the crooked bell towers. It earned the name honestly. Everything here was the color of things that had survived: gray wool coats patched at the elbows, gray bread wrapped in gray paper, gray-faced clerks hurrying with their collars up, past the shuttered front of a chapel whose bells had been removed by Choir decree eleven years ago. Even the gulls wheeled overhead looking like scraps of ash.

But the market itself was stubbornly alive underneath all that grayness. Crates of oranges from the southern river trade, shocking as lanterns against the stone. A flower-seller's bucket of yellow crocuses. Children running between the legs of the crowd, shrieking, alive, unafraid in the way children are until someone teaches them better.

Silas watched the crowd the way she always did, and the crowd ignored her the way they always did, and this was the whole architecture of her life: a woman of forty-one years who looked, dressed, bargained, and haggled exactly like every other vendor on Tallow Street, indistinguishable, invisible, safe.

Under her left sleeve, beneath three wraps of oiled leather, the oathmark sat quiet as it had for twelve years. Quiet as a grave.

She checked it anyway. She always checked it. Not because she expected anything—twelve years had cured her of expecting anything—but because checking had become a prayer, and prayers were habits that outlived their gods.

She pressed her thumb briefly against her forearm through the leather. Cold skin. Cold scar. The mark itself was old work, older than her: a spiral of interlocking waves inked into the skin with salt and silver and a sworn vow, the badge of Veyra's company. Once it had glowed faintly blue when she prayed, warm as a banked coal. Mercenaries crossed the known world to fight beside marked soldiers of the Tide-Bearer, because a company with Veyra's favor did not break, did not drown, did not lose.

Twelve years ago, on the mud flats of the Saltfen, every mark in the company had gone dark at once, mid-battle, mid-prayer, and the invincible company had learned in one terrible hour what it was without its god.

Silas pulled her sleeve back down and arranged her candles.

*You're still here,* she reminded herself, which was the only liturgy she kept anymore. *They're not. You're still here.*

* *

The morning passed in the small commerce of staying unnoticed.

A cooper bought wick-cord and complained about the price of pitch. Two Choir novices walked the row in their gray-and-white, checking vendor licenses, and Silas sold them each a beeswax taper at an honest price and wished them a dull afternoon, which was the only kind of blessing she dared give anyone in uniform. An old man she half-recognized from the dockside chapels paused too long at her table, and she felt the familiar cold crawl up her spine—*did he know her, had he seen her before, somewhere, some year*—but he only bought a box of matches and left.

That was the rhythm. Twelve years of it. She had gotten so good at being nobody that some mornings she had to remind herself there had ever been anybody to hide.

Her name had not always been Brightwater, for one thing. And her hands had not always smelled of candle wax. There had been a time when they smelled of salt spray and sword oil, when she'd stood third rank in the shield line of the Wavebound Company with thirty pounds of steel on her body and a god's voice in her ear like sunlight poured directly into the skull. *Steady, little wave,* Veyra used to say, in the moment before contact, and the word *steady* would land in Silas's chest and become literally true, her heartbeat slowing, her grip iron, her fear folding itself up small and polite like a guest who knew when to leave.

No voice had said anything to her in twelve years. She'd stopped keeping count of the days around year six.

At midday she ate bread and hard cheese standing behind her table, and watched a pickpocket work the crowd with real professional admiration—he was maybe fourteen and very smooth—and decided not to catch his eye, because catching anyone's eye was against her religion now, if she'd ever had one besides Veyra, which she sometimes doubted.

And then, in the way that catastrophe arrives in ordinary lives—sideways, wearing the clothes of an ordinary afternoon—it happened.

It started with the sound.

Not loud. That was the thing she'd realize later, turning it over and over in the dark: not thunder, not a choir, nothing the stories promised. Just a sound like a wet finger on a wineglass rim, high and clear, coming from the alley that ran along the butcher's row. A sound that most of the market stepped over without noticing, the way you step over a crack in the pavement.

Silas did not step over it. Silas's whole body turned toward it like a compass needle, because she had spent seven years of her life listening for exactly that pitch—that thin, ringing, resonant frequency at the top edge of hearing—and she had heard it exactly twice before in her life: once at her own oath-taking, and once at the Saltfen, a half-second before everything went silent forever.

A child's voice came with it. Small, ragged, pleading. Words she couldn't make out at first over the market noise, and then could.

"*Please.* Please, please, wake up, please, somebody—I'll be good, I'll do whatever—*please wake up.*"

Silas was moving before she'd decided to move. She rounded the corner of the butcher's row into the alley's narrow gloom, hand already reaching for a voice she hadn't used in over a decade, the old command-forms of the company rising up out of her memory like drowned bodies surfacing—

—and stopped.

In the mouth of the alley, beside a heap of sacking where a ragged boy of six or seven knelt over a smaller figure—a girl, a toddler, gray-lipped and still, some fever or fall or worse—light answered him.

Not lamplight. Not reflected sun off a window. Light with no source, a soft gold-white bloom rising out of the air above the two children like heat-shimmer made visible, like the first inch of dawn arriving in the wrong place. It had weight. Silas felt it press against her face, warm as a hand.

The boy wasn't doing anything. That was the part that broke something open in her chest. He wasn't casting, wasn't chanting, wasn't holding a focus or drawing a circle. He was just a child kneeling in filth, praying with the naked desperation that adults learn to disguise, and something in the world had simply *answered*.

"No," Silas said. Quietly. To the light, to the alley, to the last twelve years.

Because she knew what answered prayer looked like. She had lived inside it for twenty-two years. And this—this unbidden grace, this god reaching down through the cracks of the world without being summoned through any lawful rite—this was impossible. This was the thing the Choir of the Silent existed to prevent. This was the thing that had supposedly ended everywhere, all at once, on a mud flat twelve years ago.

The toddler coughed. Color flooded back into her face like ink dropped in water. The boy made a sound Silas had only heard from soldiers pulling friends out of the surf at the Saltfen—a sound that had no name and needed none—and grabbed his sister and held her, and the light folded itself away as gently as it had come, leaving only the ordinary gray alley and the ordinary gray afternoon.

Silas stood frozen at the alley's mouth, and the market flowed on behind her, unaware. Nobody else had seen it. Or if they had, they'd done what everyone in Correnholt did when the impossible happened in public: looked very hard at something else and walked faster.

She should walk away. That was the training. Twelve years of it, self-administered: see nothing, know nothing, be nobody. A child's prayer answered in an alley was a Choir matter now, and the Choir would come asking questions of everyone nearby, and the only correct answer was *I sell candles, officer, I sell salt and bootlaces, I've never seen anything in my life.*

Silas turned to go.

And her arm caught fire.

Not the leather wraps. Underneath them. The oathmark—cold, dead, silent for twelve years, mourned like a grave and checked like a wound—*moved*. A slow pulse of heat uncoiled along the spiral of waves, warm as breath, warm as a banked coal waking, and for one staggering half-second Silas felt something on the far end of it. Not a voice. Not yet. More like the pressure change before a storm door opens—a vast attention swinging toward her across an unthinkable distance, the way a lighthouse beam swings across a ship's deck, touching her and passing on.

Then it was gone. The warmth faded. The mark lay dead and cold under its wraps, and Silas Brightwater stood in a gray market in a gray city with her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out.

Around her, the market went on selling. A woman laughed at something a fruit-seller said. Somewhere, distantly, the state bells of the Cathedral rang the hour—one low tone, then another, the only bells Correnholt was permitted—and the sound rolled over the rooftops like a warning that nobody heeded because it rang every hour of every day.

Silas looked down at her left arm.

Twelve years. Twelve years of knots that were only knots and stones that were only stones and a silence she had learned to call peace. Twelve years of careful invisibility, and it had taken one child's desperate prayer less than a minute to put a crack through all of it.

Prayers were answering themselves in Correnholt. And her god's mark—the mark of a god twelve years dead, or fled, or worse—had just reached out and touched her back.

Down the row, two gray cloaks detached themselves from the crowd near the cathedral steps and began walking toward the butcher's row. Not hurrying. Choir novices never hurried; urgency was for people who believed the world could still surprise them. But they were walking her way, and one of them carried the long brass resonance-fork the Choir used to listen for what shouldn't exist.

Silas picked up her coin-box, pulled her scarf higher, and became exactly as memorable as a Tuesday.

Whatever was waking up in the world, she had perhaps an hour before the Choir started asking the city questions. And for the first time in twelve years, Silas Brightwater had no idea what her answer would be.
