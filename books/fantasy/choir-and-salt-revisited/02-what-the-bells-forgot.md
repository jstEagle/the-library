## 2. What the Bells Forgot

By morning, Correnholt had forgotten how to be quiet.

Silas heard it before she opened her shutters: the sound of a city talking all at once. Voices stacking on voices in the lane below her window, and underneath them, rising like steam off wet stone, something she hadn't heard in twelve years—people praying out loud. Not the furtive, mumbled kind. Not the careful prayers of people who knew better. Full-voiced, desperate, greedy prayers, spat into the gray air like coins into a fountain.

"—healed my boy's hand, I saw it, the bone set itself—"

"—the widow Vetch prayed for rain and it came sideways up the harbor road—"

"—the Choir can't stop it, they don't know how—"

She stood at the window with her shirt still unbuttoned and watched the market square fill with people who should have known better. Fishwives with their hands raised. A lamplighter on his knees in front of the fountain, lips moving fast. Two children skipping rope to a rhyme about the Lady Under the Waves, and no one telling them to hush.

Twelve years. Twelve years of learning exactly how small a person could make herself. And now the whole city was shouting her secret into the sky.

The mark on her forearm pulsed.

It had been doing that since last night—since the alley, since the child's prayer, since light had poured out of nowhere like water through a cracked dam. She pushed her sleeve back and looked at it for the thousandth time, but this time it looked back. The oathmark was a stylized wave cresting over a broken anchor, inked not by any needle but burned into her skin at nineteen by a god's finger, back when gods still touched things. For twelve years it had been a scar. Pale. Dead. A receipt for services no longer rendered.

Now the lines of it glowed faint gold, dimming when she looked away, brightening when she despaired of it. It throbbed in time with nothing—not her heartbeat, not the tide. Some other rhythm. Something far off, turning over in its sleep.

"Stop," she told it.

It pulsed again, insolent as a dog that had learned a new trick.

She buttoned her sleeve, pinned her cuff tight, and went downstairs to burn her life.

* * *

The charms went into the brazier one at a time, because Silas Brightwater had never in her life done a sloppy job of anything, including destroying it.

Each one had taken hours. Knotted cord, river salt, tallow, a word whispered over the work that wasn't quite a prayer because she'd stopped deserving prayers years ago—just a wish, pressed into the fibers like a flower into a book. Luck-charms mostly. A fever-knot or two. The little woven anchors she sold to sailors' wives, guaranteed to do absolutely nothing, which was the entire point. Harmless. Every charm harmless by design, because the Choir of the Silent hanged people for resonance, and Silas had survived this long by being precisely, perfectly nothing.

She fed the luck-charms to the coals and watched the cords blacken and curl. The tallow smell was wrong for the season, thick and funereal.

There was a knock at her door. Three raps, then a pause, then two—the fisherman's widow, Maren, from the rhythm of it.

She shouldn't open it. Every instinct she'd sharpened over twelve years said don't, stay dark, be furniture. But Maren had paid her three coppers last week for a charm against storms, and Maren had fed her soup through the winter fever, and there were maybe four people in Correnholt who would notice if Silas Brightwater simply stopped existing, and Maren was one.

She opened the door on the chain.

Maren looked like a woman who hadn't slept. Good news had that effect in Correnholt; it always arrived dressed as danger. "They're saying the old gods are waking." Her voice was low, urgent. "Saying prayers are answering themselves. Silas—my sister-in-law lit a candle for her husband's boat and the candle wouldn't go out. Burned three days. Three days, in this wind."

"That's a story."

"It's a hundred stories. It's a thousand." Maren gripped the doorframe, and Silas saw her knuckles whiten. "You know things. You always knew things. What's happening?"

What was happening was that her god—who had gone silent mid-prayer twelve years ago on a salt marsh full of dying men—was stirring in whatever prison held her, and every sleeping faithful thing in Correnholt was feeling the tremor through its bones. What was happening was that the life Silas had built out of salt and caution was ending, and there was nothing she could do about it except be somewhere else when it finished.

"I don't know," Silas said. "But if I were you, I'd put that candle out."

"They'll search now. The Choir." Maren's eyes flicked down, once, to Silas's sleeved forearm—an old habit between them, an old courtesy, never acknowledged—and came back up. "House to house. They started in Tanners' Row at dawn."

"I know."

"Do you?" Maren's voice dropped further. "Because I've seen you at the stall, Silas. Seen how you watch the gray-cloaks. Whatever you're carrying—" She stopped herself, shook her head. "Just don't be here when they come. That's all. Don't be here."

Then she was gone down the stair, and Silas closed the door and leaned her forehead against it and let herself feel, for exactly ten breaths, the full weight of what was ending. The stall. The regulars. The room above the chandlery with its good lock and its view of the water and its floorboard that squeaked only when she stepped on it, which she had stepped on deliberately every night for six years so the pattern would stay familiar. Twelve years of building a person out of routine, and routine was ash by breakfast.

She gave herself the ten breaths. Then she stopped.

Burn the rest, pack light, take the south stairs, be gone before the bells rang noon. Simple arithmetic. She'd done harder math with arrows in the air.

The last charm in the box was different from the others. Older. A knot of gray cord shot through with one thread of blue, worked in a pattern she hadn't used since the Saltfen—the company knot, the one every soldier sworn to Veyra wore braided into their sword-grip. She'd kept it in the bottom of the box under the harmless stock for twelve years without selling it, without burning it, without looking at it directly, the way you keep a letter from someone dead.

The mark flared when her fingers touched it. Actually flared—gold light leaking through her cuff seams, bright enough to throw shadows in the little room.

Silas stood very still and listened to the sounds of the city outside: the voices, the footsteps, somewhere a bell tolling wrong, arrhythmic, like the Choir's own instruments were confused. And underneath everything, faint as a pulse through a wall, that other rhythm again. Turning over. Reaching.

"Not yet," she whispered, and she didn't know if she meant it to the mark, or the god behind it, or herself.

She threw the company knot into the brazier last, and made herself watch it burn.

* * *

Inquisitor Dain Merek had a theory about rumors, which was that they were smoke, and his job was to walk toward the fire without pretending the smoke wasn't there.

He stood in the courtyard of the Choir of the Silent's chapterhouse and watched his acolytes bring out the resonance engines' morning readings on slate tablets. The numbers were bad. The numbers had been bad for three days, and getting worse. Every engine in the city—a hundred tuned brass instruments, each calibrated to detect divine resonance the way a compass detects north—was drifting off true, all in the same direction, all pointing inward like weathervanes in a cyclone.

"Southside engine reads four degrees off bearing, Inquisitor. Harbor engine nine. The cathedral engine—" The young acolyte hesitated.

"Say it."

"The cathedral engine is spinning, Brother. Freely. We've locked the gimbal."

Spinning freely. Merek turned the phrase over the way he turned everything over, slowly, looking for the crack in it. He was not a dramatic man. People mistook his quietness for zealotry, which suited him fine, because zealots were predictable and predictable men died of their predictions. He had been a harbor clerk's son in Vellsmarsh before the god-wars ended. He had buried a wife and two daughters in the same week, and he had not prayed at their graves, because he had learned exactly what prayers were worth in the years when the gods walked abroad and needed feeding.

Divinity returning was not a miracle to Dain Merek. It was weather coming. And you did not stand in a field admiring weather that had already killed you once.

"Sweep rotation," he said. "Door-to-door, starting southside. Pairs only. Any engine reading above threshold, mark the house, move on—no seizures without my signature. I want names first, bodies later."

"Yes, Inquisitor." The acolyte paused. "Brother Cathal says—forgive me—he says we should be asking whether the engines are failing rather than the faithful resonating. He says a hundred needles don't all drift the same way unless the compass rose itself is broken."

"Brother Cathal is welcome to test his theory by standing in the harbor with his hands in the water, praying for fish." Merek took the slates, stacked them, tapped their edges straight. "The rose isn't broken. Something is moving under it. Find me who it's moving through—that's your work today."

The acolyte hurried off, and Merek stood alone in the courtyard with the slates under his arm and let himself think the thought he'd been not-thinking for three days.

The resonance wasn't random anymore. That was new. For years the engines had caught stray sparks—one frightened soul in ten thousand whose prayers accidentally rang true, enough to hang quietly and keep the ledgers balanced. Scattered noise. This was signal. Coherent, strengthening, centered. Somewhere in his city, something was amplifying. Or someone.

And there was a second thing, smaller, filed at the back of his mind where he kept the facts that didn't yet have drawers: among the captured resonants of the past week, questioned and released because their sparks were negligible, three had said versions of the same strange thing. *It feels like it's looking for someone.* Not generic. Specific. A god waking was catastrophe enough. A god waking *on purpose*, with a name on its lips—

Merek dismissed the thought for later and went in to sharpen his pens. Smoke first. Fire after.

* * *

Silas was on the roof of the chandlery with a travel bag she couldn't finish packing when the bells forgot themselves.

That was the only way to describe it. Noon in Correnholt meant the Cathedral of the Silence tolling eight measured strokes—the city's daily reminder that noise was suspect and volume was sin. Silas had lived under those bells for six years. They were as reliable as tide tables.

Today the first stroke came out clean and deep, and on the second stroke the note bent. Slid. Rose half a tone and kept rising, swelling, until the bronze was singing instead of tolling, a high clear harmonic that had never been cast into it, and every other bell in the city caught it up—the harbor chapel, the market clock, the little cracked thing on Tanners' Row—until Correnholt was full of ringing that no hand had rung, harmonizing, ascending, and Silas stood on the rooftop with her bag hanging forgotten from her fist and felt the oathmark blaze like a brand fresh from the forge.

Below, the city lost its mind politely. People came out into the streets and looked up. Some wept. Some cheered. Down in the square, a gray-cloaked acolyte tore off his hood and ran, just ran, directionless, while his brothers stood frozen around a resonance engine whose needle she could almost see spinning free.

And Silas understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, two things at once.

The first: this wasn't random awakening. This was a summons. All that rising harmony, aimed like an arrow—and she could feel the aim. At the alley child's prayer the mark had stirred. Now it was *straining*, pulling toward something east of the city with a force she could lean against, like wind. Veyra wasn't just turning in her sleep. Veyra was calling. Calling through every bell and candle and desperate throat in Correnholt, calling the way a drowning woman calls, and there was exactly one person alive carrying the ear to hear it.

The second thing: she had perhaps a day before the Choir traced the resonance's focus and found the same arrowhead she'd just felt, and the arrowhead was her.

She got off the roof. Down the south stairs, out the coal chute, through the alleys with her collar up and her sleeve pinned and the mark beating against her arm like a second heart trying to escape. There was one man in Correnholt who moved cargo the Choir didn't weigh, one man who'd run relics for the faithful back when running relics was survivable, and one name that might still open his door: Brannoc Hale. Old debt. Old trust, or near enough.

Behind her, the false bells rang on, gorgeous and terrible, and Silas Brightwater ran toward the harbor with her god's voice in her bones and her whole life burning down to embers at her back.

She did not look back. Looking back was for people with something left to lose.

She had, she realized somewhere around the fish-quay, exactly one thing left—and it was glowing through her cuff, pulling east, and it would not, could not, be left behind.
