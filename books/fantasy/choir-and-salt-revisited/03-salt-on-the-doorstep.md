## 3. Salt on the Doorstep

The river district smelled of tar and low tide, and Silas walked through it with her hood up and her mark burning like a coal sewn under her skin.

Three days since the child in the alley. Three days of light spilling out of a desperate little prayer, and of the oathmark on her forearm waking from its twelve-year sleep with a heat that no amount of leather wrapping could smother. She'd taken to wearing two wraps now, and a bracer over them, and she still caught herself glancing down at intervals, half expecting to see gold leaking through the stitching.

*Stop it,* she told herself. *You're a charm-seller. You're nobody. Nobody is safe.*

But nobody didn't have a god's fingerprint glowing through her sleeve.

Brannoc Hale kept his boat three piers past the customs house, where the pilings went soft and the harbor watch stopped caring. The *Kindly Tide* sat low in the water, unloading nothing, which meant she was loading something, which meant Hale was home. Silas had done business with him twice before the Choir came down hard on relic-running—once for a saint's knucklebone, once for a vial of blessed oil that turned out to be lamp fuel and a good story. He'd been older then. Everyone had been.

She found him on deck arguing with a winch, gray beard braided at the chin, hands like knotted rope. He saw her coming before she reached the gangplank. Of course he did. Men who'd spent thirty years dodging the law developed eyes in the backs of their heads and everywhere else besides.

"No," he said.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were going to." He set down the winch handle. "I can smell a favor coming off you like fish off a warm dock. No, Brightwater. Whatever it is, the answer's no."

"It's a ride."

"Where?"

"Upstream."

Hale laughed, short and ugly. "Upstream is the Saltfen road, and everything upstream is either Choir patrol or worse. You know what they're doing door to door? My own daughter got questioned yesterday. They asked if she knew any 'resonants.' That's the word now. Resonants." He spat over the rail. "Twelve years I've kept my nose clean. Ran my last relic before you were even marked, girl. Whatever's burning in your arm—and don't tell me nothing's burning, I can see you holding that arm like it's a broken wing—whatever it is, it's not my problem, and I'll thank you to keep it off my deck."

Silas had rehearsed arguments the whole walk down. Money. Leverage. The old debt from the knucklebone job. She deployed none of them, because she could see in his face that none of them would land. Fear landed. Fear was the only coin the Choir accepted these days.

"Brannoc," she said quietly. "They're going to find me whether or not you help me. The only question is whether they find me near your pier."

His jaw worked. "That's not persuasion. That's a threat."

"It's arithmetic. I hate it as much as you do."

He looked at her a long moment—looked at the hood, the wrapped arm, the twelve years of careful gray invisibility standing on his gangplank asking to be smuggled like contraband. Then he shook his head, slow and final.

"Off my boat, Silas. Please. I've got grandchildren."

There it was. She nodded, throat tight, and turned—

And heard the screaming start below decks.

* * *

It came up through the hold grating like steam: a woman's voice, cracked raw, and beneath it a smaller sound, a child crying in the thin, whistling way children cry when breathing costs more than they have. Hale went past Silas like a man struck, shouldering down the hatch without a glance backward.

She should have left. Every instinct she'd honed for twelve years said *leave, leave, leave*—you are a lit fuse walking into a powder store, and the Choir is sniffing the air for smoke. She stood on the gangplank with the harbor wind pulling at her hood and counted to five.

Then she followed him down.

The cabin was low and close and smelled of camphor and fear. A girl of maybe six lay on a bunk, small body arched against coughing, skin flushed dark red, lips gone an alarming blue-gray. A woman—Hale's daughter, presumably, same stubborn jaw—knelt beside her wringing a cloth that had long since gone warm.

"Fever's four days," the woman said, seeing her father and then Silas, and not caring anymore who strangers were. "The physiker wants nine crowns a visit and nine more for the bitterbark tincture. We don't—we sold the—" Her voice folded in on itself.

Hale stood at the foot of the bunk with his big hands hanging empty, and Silas watched a man who had stared down harbor guards and river pirates come apart completely, quietly, in front of his dying granddaughter.

"Nine crowns," he said stupidly. "I've got four."

Something in Silas moved—not thought, not decision, something older and lower than either. Later she would try to reconstruct it and fail. All she knew was that she crossed the cabin, knelt by the bunk, and the words were already leaving her mouth before she understood she was saying them.

"Veyra of the Saltfen," she whispered, "who kept watch when watch was hopeless—who held the line so the small ones behind it could sleep—"

The mark ignited.

Not the dull coal-heat of the last three days but a white-gold blaze, and light came *through* the leather wrap, through the bracer, through her sleeve, painting the low cabin beams in dawn colors. The girl's fevered body arched once—and settled. The whistling breath eased, deepened, eased again. Color crept back into her lips like tide returning over mudflats.

The mother made a sound Silas had never heard from an adult—a sound with no dignity in it at all, pure relief with nowhere to go.

Silas knelt there, arm blazing, heart hammering, and felt along the bond something she hadn't felt in twelve years: attention. Not a voice. Not words. Just a vast, tired, *directional* awareness turning toward her the way a sunflower turns toward light, and then—fainter, fainter, like a lamp carried away down a long corridor—it withdrew.

"Veyra?" she breathed.

Nothing. But the silence had changed shape. Before, it had been a wall. Now it was a door, locked, with someone standing on the other side.

"Brightwater." Hale's voice. She looked up. He was staring at her arm—at the glow already fading, the leather scorched brown in the shape of something like a feather or a flame. His face did several things quickly and settled on grim. "Get upstairs. Now."

* * *

He gave her tea he clearly couldn't afford and told her to drink it, which she did, because refusing Brannoc Hale's hospitality twice in one day seemed unwise. The girl—Pell, her name was Pell—slept in the cabin below, breathing easy. The mother hadn't stopped touching her.

"That prayer," Hale said. "The one you said. Where'd you learn it?"

"Saltfen company liturgy. Every mercenary sworn to Veyra had it beaten into them before their first campaign." She turned the cup in her hands. "I haven't said it aloud in twelve years. It just... came out."

"And your arm's been doing that since when?"

"Since a child prayed in an alley three days ago and something answered. Not me. Something else. And my mark woke up."

Hale sat back. Outside, gulls argued over the pilings. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—the bluster burned off, leaving something older and wearier underneath.

"I ran relics for eleven years, girl. Saints' bones, altar cloths, one time a font that wept real tears—I never learned how, and I sold it fast. And in all that time, do you know what I learned about the gods?" He held up one rope-knuckled finger. "They're quiet. Quiet as stones. I figured that was just how it was—faith's a one-way street, always has been, and the priests sell you the scenery." Another finger. "Then the Choir comes in, says the old gods are dead or dangerous, burns the relics, hangs the resonants, and everyone nods along because what's faith against a crossbow?" He leaned forward. "And now prayers are answering themselves all over Correnholt, and the Choir's tearing apart doors looking for why, and a woman walks up my gangplank with a burning brand on her arm and heals my granddaughter with a soldier's prayer." He shook his head slowly. "So here's my question, and think hard before you answer, because Pell's life touched whatever that was, and that makes it my business now. What did you pray *to*, exactly?"

"A god who went silent twelve years ago mid-battle, with forty of my comrades dying around me believing they'd been abandoned." The words came out flat, worn smooth by years of not saying them. "I prayed to Veyra. And for the first time since the Saltfen, I think somebody heard."

Hale was quiet for a long time. Down in the cabin, faintly, Pell laughed at something her mother said—a small, impossible sound, a sound that shouldn't exist, bought with a scrap of borrowed divinity.

"If the Choir finds out what you are," Hale said finally, "they won't hang you. Hangings are for smugglers and heretics. For resonants they've got the resonance engines, and I've heard things about those engines I don't repeat sober." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I'm too old for this. I want that stated for the record. I am too old, too tired, and too fond of my neck for this."

"Noted for the record."

"But." He exhaled through his nose, a man watching his own good sense drown. "You healed my Pell. There's a debt in this family now, and Hales pay debts. So." He pointed a thick finger at her. "One run. Upstream, night navigation, my crew doesn't know what you are and never will. You keep that arm wrapped, you keep that mouth shut, and the moment this business of yours is finished, you're off my boat and out of my life. Agreed?"

Silas felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched. Hope, maybe—or its cheaper cousin, a plan. Either way, it was the first thing in three days that wasn't dread.

"Agreed."

"Don't thank me." He stood, joints cracking like rigging in a storm. "Thanking implies I had a choice, and I'd rather keep pretending I didn't." At the hatch he paused, back to her. "One more thing, Brightwater. That feeling on your arm, when the prayer landed. Like something noticed you."

Silas went still. "How did you know—"

"Because I've seen that look before. On the faithful, back when there were faithful." He glanced over his shoulder, and his eyes were old and sad and certain. "Whatever's on the other side of your silence, girl—it's not done with you. Gods never are. That's rather the point of them."

He climbed out into the daylight, and Silas sat alone with her cooling tea and her scorch-marked arm, doing new arithmetic.

She couldn't outrun what she was. The mark would glow, the prayers would answer, the Choir would come. Running was over; it had been over since the alley. But a thing that answers could be called. A thing that notices could be aimed. She was a mercenary, after all, and mercenaries didn't pray—they negotiated.

Maybe that was the trick no one had tried in twelve years. Not faith. Terms.

She was still turning it over when the hatch banged open and Hale's head reappeared, expression gone strange.

"There's a stowaway in the forward hold," he said. "Skinny little thing in Choir boots. Says—if you can believe it—that she's been looking for you."
