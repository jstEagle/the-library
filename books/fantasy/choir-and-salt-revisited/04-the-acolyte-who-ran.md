## 4. The Acolyte Who Ran

The barge smelled of tar, wet hemp, and Brannoc Hale's pipe smoke, and Silas had been awake on it for three hours without finding sleep.

They'd cast off from the Salt Quay an hour past midnight, riding the ebb down the Corren with the running lamps doused. Hale's granddaughter—Pell, all elbows and eleven years of opinions—had the tiller while her grandfather dozed against the cargo netting, and Silas sat amidships in the dark with her hood up and her mark wrapped twice in leather, watching the city she'd spent twelve years hiding in slide away behind her like something she owed money to.

The mark hadn't stopped glowing since the alley. She could feel it through the wraps now, a low warmth like a coin held too long in the palm, and twice tonight it had pulsed when she wasn't praying—once when Pell laughed at something, once for no reason at all. She didn't like what that suggested about what it was reaching for.

Which was why she heard the sound.

Small. Under the creak of hull and water. A breath drawn too carefully—the specific discipline of someone trying not to make noise and failing by exactly one degree. Twelve years of being hunted taught you that sound. Twelve years of hunting others had taught her how to move toward it.

She rose without a word, crossed the deck on the balls of her feet, and put her knife to the gap between the cargo crates where two eyes caught the starlight.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Move."

The eyes went wide. Then, absurdly, they squeezed shut—like a child who believed darkness worked both ways.

Silas flipped back the tarpaulin.

A girl. Young—younger than Silas had expected, maybe nineteen, maybe twenty, hard to tell under the grime and the hollows of her cheeks. She wore a Choir novice's gray robe cut off at the knees, ruined boots, and around her throat, on a cord, the little tin badge of an acolyte of the Silent—a circle with no face inside it. Her lips were cracked. Her hands were bone over sinew. And she was holding a stolen kitchen knife in a grip that said she'd been taught the theory of blades by men who'd never bled.

"I haven't taken anything," the girl whispered. "I just needed to get downriver. I'll get off at the first lock, I swear it, I—"

"Choir." Silas said it like spitting a pit. Every instinct she owned turned edge-up. A Choir acolyte on Hale's barge, three days after the searches started, three days after answered prayers lit up half of Correnholt like festival lamps. Either the girl was a deserter or she was bait, and Silas had watched enough Inquisitor work to know bait looked exactly like this: starving, trembling, harmless. "How long have you been under there?"

"Since the quay." The girl swallowed. "Please. I'm not—I ran. They're looking for me too."

"They're looking for everyone." Silas pressed the flat of the knife lightly against the girl's collarbone, just enough pressure to make the point. "Name."

"Tamsin."

"Tamsin what?"

"Just Tamsin. We give up our surnames when we take the gray. Everyone does."

"How convenient." Silas leaned closer. The mark under her wraps had gone strange—not warm anymore but *attentive*, straining toward the girl like a hound toward a scent, and that was impossible, that was wrong, marks didn't care about people, they cared about gods, and this was a girl of the god-killers' own household. "You're going to answer a question for me, and you're going to answer it honestly, because I will know if you lie. Have you ever heard the name Veyra?"

He should have thrown her over the side at the lock. That was Brannoc Hale's position on the matter, delivered at dawn on the aft deck with his arms folded and his granddaughter conspicuously listening from the tiller, which meant the argument would go badly for him.

Hale was a mountain going soft, sixty years of river work in shoulders like a dock crane, a beard gone to iron wool, and eyes that had priced everything he'd ever seen. He'd run relics for the faithful before the Proscription—censer-silver, saint-fingers, whole altars floated out ahead of the Choir's bonfires—and he'd buried that trade along with most of his customers. He did not want it resurrected on his deck.

"Acolytes talk," he said. "Acolytes break. You know what they do to breakers. You've seen the resonance engines work on a person. So either we put her ashore at Marlow's Landing, or every patrol boat on this river knows our draft and our heading by Thursday."

"She was under a tarp in my cargo hold for six hours," Silas said. "If she were bait, she'd have signaled by now. There are ways."

"And if she's just stupid?"

"Then she's stupid and starving and she heard Veyra's name." Silas kept her voice level, but the words came out of her like something pulled. She still hadn't told Hale everything the girl had said last night, kneeling in the hold with a blade at her throat and tears cutting clean lines through the grime on her face. *I heard it in choir-watch. The silence—that's what we listen to, the great silence, it's holy—but three weeks ago the silence spoke. One name. They said it was corruption. They cast me out for asking whose name it was.* "You felt your granddaughter's fever break. You know what's moving out there. If she heard His name—if she's marked somehow—we can't just leave her for Merek's people to find."

"So it's mercy now." Hale chewed his pipe stem unlit; he only smoked at anchor. "Twelve years you've been careful as frost, Brightwater. Careful as frost. Now there's a god waking up, an Inquisitor pulling the city apart nail by nail, and you want me to sail straight down the middle of it with a runaway nun in my hold."

"Yes."

He looked at her a long moment. Behind him the river ran brown and patient, and the morning fog was burning off the reed banks in slow rags.

"Pell eats first," he said finally. "And she sleeps in the cabin, not the hold, and if she so much as hums a hymn I put her ashore wherever we happen to be. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And you tell me what she actually said to you. All of it. Before noon."

Tamsin ate like someone who had forgotten food was allowed to be a regular event—carefully at first, then with a ferocity that made Pell giggle and made something in Silas's chest twist sideways. Cabin bread, salt pork, tea brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in. Tamsin got through all of it and then sat very still with her hands around the empty bowl, as if afraid it might be taken back.

"You gave up your name," Silas said. They were alone in the cabin; Hale had the watch. "When you took the gray. What did you give it up *from*?"

"Tamsin Wells. My mother keeps a candle-shop in Harrowgate." A ghost of a smile. "She thinks I'm at the Cloister studying harmonics. She doesn't know I—" The smile died. "They don't write home after third year. It's considered worldly attachment."

"Your mother doesn't know you're gone."

"The Choir posted my dismissal to the diocesan office in Harrowgate. It goes in a ledger." Tamsin said it flatly, reciting doctrine, but her thumb worried the tin badge at her throat and wouldn't stop. "There's a form."

Silas almost laughed. Almost. "Why haven't you thrown that in the river?"

Tamsin's hand closed over the badge. For a moment Silas thought the girl wouldn't answer—she'd spent the day watching Tamsin flinch from loud noises and doors opening fast, cataloguing the fear like inventory—but then the chin came up, and underneath the starvation and the terror there was something with a spine in it.

"Because it's mine," Tamsin said. "It's the only thing I chose. Everything else—the rule, the silence, the hours—they assigned me. But I asked the question nobody wanted asked. I got cast out for *asking*. This badge is proof I was right to ask." She met Silas's eyes, and her voice dropped. "You know something. When you said His name last night, you said it like a wound. Who is He to you?"

The cabin lantern guttered. Silas found she was gripping the table edge.

"He was mine," she said. "The way the Choir means it and worse. I bore his oathmark. My whole company did. We were his—" She stopped. Twelve years, and the sentence still wouldn't finish itself. "We were sworn. And then he stopped answering, and the Choir called it the Proof of the Silence, and everything I was burned down to nothing."

"That's not what happened."

Silas went cold. "What did you say?"

"In the silence." Tamsin's voice shook, but she didn't look away. "Three weeks ago. When it spoke. It wasn't just a name, it was—it was aimed. Like an arrow. I hear things, that's why they made me an acolyte, I'm a listener, and I know what prayer sounds like when it's *for* someone, and this—" Her hands came up, shaping something in the air between them. "It wasn't for me. It was addressed to someone else, and I was just standing in the way of it. A woman. A soldier. A mark like burning wire up the left arm. He was calling her, and she couldn't hear him, and he was—" Her face crumpled. "He was grieving. That's what came through. Not power. Grief. Twelve years of it, all at once, and the name Veyra, and *she can't hear me.*"

The leather wrap on Silas's forearm had begun to glow through both layers, gold bleeding through brown, and Tamsin stared at it and went white as the cabin crockery.

"It's you," she breathed. "The vision was you."

Later—much later, after Tamsin had cried herself silent and Pell had brought blankets without being asked and pretended not to notice anything, because that was a family skill—Silas stood the middle watch alone and let the river decide her thoughts for her.

Called. He'd been calling her. Twelve years of silence and the first thing Veyra did on the far side of it was reach for her, and she'd never known, and some mercenary girl had caught the message meant for her like a stray arrow taking a bystander. Silas turned that fact over and couldn't decide whether it was a miracle or an indictment. Both. It was both, everything was both now, the mark glowed and the world was dangerous and the god she'd mourned like a dead father was alive behind stone somewhere, saying her name into the dark.

Footsteps on the deck. Soft ones. Tamsin settled onto the cargo netting an arm's length away, wrapped in a blanket, badge still around her neck.

"I can't sleep on boats," Tamsin said. "The rocking."

"Liar. You slept until noon."

"That was collapse. Different thing." A pause. The water talked to itself under the bow. "Are you going to the Saltfen? Hale's heading downriver, and there's nothing downriver except—"

"You know the Saltfen?"

"Every acolyte knows it. Where the voice went out. Where the Proof began." Tamsin pulled the blanket tighter. "Where your company died."

"Not all of it." Silas watched the dark water. "I'm the residue."

"I'm sorry."

"For what? You didn't—"

"For standing in front of his message." Tamsin's voice was very small. "For hearing what was yours."

Silas turned to look at her. Starlight, river-noise, a girl in a ruined robe holding a tin god-killer's badge like a talisman, and the mark on Silas's arm glowing steady and warm, pointed at her like a compass needle, and Silas understood suddenly that she was never going to be rid of this person. Whatever happened at the Saltfen, whatever the Choir did, this girl had been caught in the same net, snagged by the same absent hand, and there was no version of the road ahead that didn't have her in it.

"Don't apologize for that," Silas said roughly. "Ever."

"Why not?"

"Because you're the first person in twelve years who heard him at all."

Tamsin smiled then—tired, cracked-lipped, real—and leaned back against the netting, and neither of them moved away, and the river carried them down through the dark toward the place where the voice had died.

It was near dawn, the sky just graying, when Pell's shout came forward from the tiller.

"Lanterns! Astern—three of them—gray hulls!"

Silas was on her feet with her knife out before she was fully awake. Tamsin scrambled up beside her, and downriver behind them, sliding out of the fog with the silence of things that mean business, came the Choir's patrol boats.

And Tamsin said, in a voice that didn't shake at all:

"I know a hymn that will help. Pray you don't hate me for it."
