## 5. Twelve Years of Silence

The barge was tied up at a rotting pier three miles downriver from Correnholt, where the reeds grew tall enough to swallow a man and the Choir's lanterns couldn't reach. Hale called it a layover. Silas knew a hiding place when she stood in one.

Tamsin had the first watch. Silas couldn't sleep, so she climbed up to join her, and the girl didn't object—just shifted along the cargo rail to make room, which was more courtesy than Silas had earned from her yet.

They sat in silence for a while. The river moved beneath them, black and patient. Somewhere across the water, a night bird made its one note and stopped.

"You don't have to talk," Tamsin said eventually. "I just—when I can't sleep, silence makes it worse. The kind of silence that has something under it."

Silas almost laughed. "You'd know. You lived in the Choir."

"Twelve years." Tamsin's voice went flat. "You learn the difference between quiet and *quiet*."

Silas looked at her. The girl was nineteen, maybe twenty, all elbows and hollow cheeks and eyes that had seen too much of the inside of a cloister and not enough of anything else. She had run from the only institution in the world more disciplined than a mercenary company, and she sat watch like she'd been doing it her whole life.

Silas had been deciding, for three days now, whether to tell her. Not the story everyone in Correnholt could piece together from rumor—the mercenary who'd lost her god, the charm-seller with the burned-out mark. The other story. The true one, with the names in it.

The river kept moving. Silas heard herself say, "There were forty-one of us."

* * *

The company had been called the Brightwater Spears, after her—no. That wasn't true, and she'd promised herself she'd stop telling it that way. The company came first. The Brightwaters had held the Saltfen marches for six generations, sworn to Veyra since before the Choir had a name, and Silas had been born into the oath the way other children were born into a family trade. Her mother had carried the mark. Her grandmother. Her great-grandmother, who had stood at Veyra's altar when the goddess still spoke in full sentences instead of the warm wordless pressure Silas had known all her life.

Veyra was not a loud god. That was what people never understood, the ones who thought the old gods were storms and fire. Veyra was the god of the tide-line, of the moment the wave decides to come in or go back. She spoke the way salt speaks—slowly, evenly, in a language you didn't hear so much as absorb. When Silas prayed, she didn't get words. She got certainty. A direction, a weight, a *yes* or a *no* that settled into the bones like ballast.

Forty-one of them, the night they marched for the Saltfen. She could still name them. She made herself name them, once a year, on the anniversary, in the dark of whatever room she'd rented, moving her lips so no one would hear. Dree, who cooked eels badly and sang beautifully. The Voss twins, who finished each other's sentences and, in the end, each other's deaths. Captain Orsen Hale—no relation to Brannoc, though Silas had checked, the first week, with a desperate kind of hope—Orsen who had carried a wooden token of Veyra's wave on a cord around his neck for thirty years.

They'd been hired to hold the Saltfen causeway against a rival company in the pay of the Kestrel Compact. Ordinary work. The kind of work they'd done a hundred times. The Choir had been tightening its grip on the coast for a decade by then, but out in the salt marshes the old oaths still counted for something, and Veyra's mark on Silas's arm was worth more than armor. It had never failed her. Not once.

The battle went wrong in the way battles go wrong—slowly, then all at once. The Compact had bought a Choir-sanctioned resonance engine, one of the first, a thing like a cathedral bell turned inside out, and when they rang it over the marsh the air itself had screamed. Silas remembered the sound. She would always remember the sound. Not because it was loud—because it was *empty*. It reached into her chest and took the ballast out.

She'd been in the middle of the morning prayer. Forty-one voices, the old words, the ones her grandmother had taught her: *Wave, decide. Tide, turn. We stand at your line, Veyra, and we are not afraid.*

She got as far as *we are not*—

And the god was gone.

Not faded. Not distant. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from your mouth—present in its absence, a hole with edges. Silas had stood in the middle of a battle with her arm raised and her mouth open and nothing coming out of the world at all, and the resonance engine had kept screaming, and the Compact's pikes had kept coming, and forty men and women had looked to her for the certainty that was no longer there.

"We held the causeway," Silas said, out loud, to Tamsin, because she had promised herself that if she ever told it she would tell it true. "That's the joke. We won. We held the line for six hours with no god and no prayer, on nothing but training and stubbornness, and when it was over, nineteen of us were alive."

Tamsin had gone very still beside her. She didn't say *I'm sorry*. Silas was grateful. She'd have stopped talking if anyone had said *I'm sorry*.

* * *

The worst part hadn't been the battle. She'd learned that later, the way you learn the worst part of anything is never the moment itself.

The worst part was Dree, three days after, sitting on the riverbank with her hands in the water, asking Silas—begging her, actually begging, this woman who'd fought beside her for nine years—*ask her again. Ask her if we did something wrong. Ask her if we're forgiven.*

And Silas had prayed until her knees bled on the stones, and the hole in the world had stayed a hole, and Dree had looked at her with an expression Silas had never seen on a living face before and said, "Then she was never there at all."

Dree had walked into the marsh that night. The Voss twins had gone a week later, together, and everyone understood. Orsen Hale had lasted a year. He'd died at a ferry crossing in the hill country, in a bar fight, over an insult to Veyra that the insulter probably hadn't even meant as one. Silas had buried the wooden wave-token with him. She'd kept the cord.

"Twelve years," she said. "I've had twelve years to decide whether she abandoned us or died or never existed, and I never picked one, because picking one meant the others were lies. So I sold charms. Little ones. Luck-wards, fever-knots, things that work because people believe they work. And I kept the mark wrapped, and I kept my head down, and I told myself the silence was just silence."

The river made its slow sound against the pilings.

"And then a child prayed in an alley," Tamsin said quietly, "and the light came."

"And my arm woke up." Silas pulled back the leather wrap. The oathmark glowed faintly even now, even in the dark, a wave-shape of pale gold that hadn't faded in three days no matter how she willed it. "She's not dead, Tamsin. Whatever happened at the Saltfen—whatever that engine did—she's not dead. She's *elsewhere*. And something's changed, and she's close enough now that my blood can hear her."

"Do you hate her?"

The question landed like a stone in still water. Silas watched the ripples.

"I did," she said. "For about six years. Then I was too tired to hate her, and I was just sad, and sad's worse, because hate at least gives you something to do with your hands." She wrapped the mark again. "I don't know what I feel now. She was my god before she was anything else. You don't stop—"

She stopped. Tamsin was watching her with an expression Silas recognized from the mirror, from twelve years of mirrors: the look of someone hearing their own grief spoken by another mouth.

"You prayed to her," Silas said. "In the Choir. By name."

Tamsin's jaw tightened. "That's not a story for the night watch."

"No," Silas agreed. "But it's the same story. That's what I'm trying to tell you. Whatever they did to you in there, whatever made you run—it's the same hole. Same silence. We're both standing at the tide-line waiting for the wave to decide."

* * *

They sat with that for a long time. Tamsin broke the silence first, and when she spoke, it was in the careful voice of someone handling something fragile.

"At the Saltfen. When the voice cut out. Did it hurt?"

Silas thought about lying. Decided against it. "Yes. Like a bone breaking somewhere I didn't have bones. And then after, nothing. That was the cruel part. No wound you could point to. The Choir's healers examined me twice—before I ran—and found nothing. A perfectly healthy woman with a scar on her arm that didn't glow. They let me go because I was useless. The marked ones who still had their gods, those were the ones they burned."

"And you've been running for twelve years."

"I've been *hiding* for twelve years. Running's what you do when you think there's somewhere to get to." Silas looked downriver, toward the dark line of the marshes, toward the Saltfen, though from here it was just darkness. "I stopped believing there was somewhere to get to. That was easier."

"And now?"

"Now there's a light in an alley and a mark that won't go dark and an acolyte who ran from the Choir with my god's name in her mouth." She huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Now I'm on a smuggler's barge with a man who owes me a favor he doesn't want to owe, and I'm thinking about going back to the one place in the world I swore I'd never stand again."

"The Saltfen."

"The Saltfen."

Tamsin pulled her knees up. "I had a vision," she said, and then pressed her lips together, and Silas had the sudden, sharp impression of a door opening one inch and being pushed shut again by someone stronger than the door.

"Of what?"

"Of you." Tamsin's eyes were fixed on the river. "Of the altar. And I can't— I'm not ready to tell you the rest. Don't ask me yet. Please."

Everything in Silas wanted to ask. Twelve years of swallowing grief had taught her, at least, when someone else was doing the same thing. She nodded.

"Then tell me something else," she said. "Tell me why you ran. Not the whole story. Just the first inch of it."

Tamsin was quiet so long that Silas thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Because I prayed, and something answered. And in twelve years in that cloister, praying the sanctioned prayers to the sanctioned silence, nothing had ever answered me. And I realized the Choir doesn't keep the gods out." Her voice cracked, just slightly. "They keep the *answers* out. And I couldn't stay in a house built on that."

Silas looked at her—this half-starved runaway with Choir calluses still on her knees from a decade of enforced prayer—and felt something in her chest that wasn't the mark, wasn't the god, wasn't grief. Something newer. Something she didn't have a name for yet and wasn't sure she wanted one.

"Get some sleep," she said. "I'll take the watch."

Tamsin didn't move. "The man who owns this barge. Hale. He's taking us downriver, isn't he? Not away from the marshes. Toward them."

Silas said nothing.

"I heard him arguing with his pilot," Tamsin said. "Saltfen. You're going back."

"I'm thinking about it."

"You've been thinking about it since the alley. You've been thinking about it for twelve years. You just didn't know it until the light came." Tamsin stood, brushed straw from her trousers, and paused at the hatch. "For what it's worth—I'm glad it's you. Whoever the bearer is. Whoever she answers to." A beat. "I'm glad it's you."

She went below. Silas sat alone with the river and the wrapped mark and the names, all thirty-one of them, running through her head like a tide coming in.

She was still naming them when she saw the lantern.

It was a long way off—a single point of light on the eastern bank, moving without bobbing, steady as a procession. Too steady for a fisherman. Too slow for a patrol skiff under oars. Silas had spent twelve years learning the difference between lights on a river at night, and this one said *Choir* the way a bell says *Choir*.

She was below decks and shaking Hale awake before the thought finished forming.

"Brannoc. We have a lantern on the east bank, moving procession-steady, and I need you to get this barge moving *now*."

Hale sat up in the dark, gray-haired and grim, and Silas saw the exact moment the old smuggler decided that the favor he owed her had just gotten more expensive.

"Saltfen it is, then," he muttered. "God help us all."

"Working on it," Silas said, and went to wake the others.
