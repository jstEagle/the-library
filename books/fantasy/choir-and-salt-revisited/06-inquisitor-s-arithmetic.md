## 6. Inquisitor's Arithmetic

The resonant's name was Odile Ferrin, and she had been a laundress before the light found her.

Inquisitor Dain Merek knew this because he had read her file twice, and because he made it a discipline to know the name of everyone who sat across the table from him. Names mattered. A name was proof that the person in front of you was a person, and a person could be reasoned with, and a person who could be reasoned with was a person who might yet be saved from what was waking up inside them.

She sat with her wrists bound in lead-thread cord—standard issue, ugly, effective—and she would not stop shivering. Not from cold. The interrogation room in the Choir's chapterhouse kept a temperature that Merek himself had once complained about, back when he was junior enough to complain about anything. The room was warm. The woman shook as though the river ran through her veins.

"Odile Ferrin," Merek said. He set her file down on the table between them, squared its corners to the edge. "Laundry house on Tanner's Row. Eleven years employed. Two children. Your husband drowned six years ago; your mother keeps the children when you work nights." He folded his hands. "I'm not here to hurt you."

"You already have," she said. Her voice came out scraped thin. "You burned my prayer."

"We confiscated a written prayer. That is not the same thing."

"It was for my daughter. She has the lung-rot. I wrote it out so I wouldn't forget the words at night, and your people took it off my sill like it was—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "It was a prayer, Inquisitor. It was just a prayer."

"And it answered itself," Merek said.

Her mouth closed. He watched her decide whether to lie, watched the lie die behind her eyes, watched her arrive at the truth the way people always did eventually: alone, and too late for it to help.

"Yes," she said. "Three nights ago. I knelt by her bed and I said the words and nothing happened, same as every night for eleven years. And then—" Her hands turned over in her lap, palms up, as if presenting something. There were faint marks there, pale as old scars, in shapes that almost resolved into letters if you let your eyes go soft. "Then the words said themselves back."

"What shape was the voice?"

"There wasn't a voice. That's the thing. Everyone thinks there's a voice, thunder, a burning bush, whatever the old stories say. There wasn't anything like that. It was more like—" She frowned, hunting. "Like when you hang washing in winter and it freezes stiff, and then you bring it inside and hold it near the fire, and slowly it remembers how to be cloth again. It was like that. Like something frozen was thawing, somewhere far away, and everything in the world that had ever prayed got a little warmer."

Merek wrote that down. He wrote most things down; it gave people time to say the second true thing after the first true thing, and the second was always the one that mattered.

* * *

He had been an Inquisitor of the Silent Choir for nine years, and before that a Novice for four, and before that—he did not often go this far back, but tonight the room was warm and the woman's shivering reminded him of something—before that he had been a boy of nine in a town called Vell's Crossing, with two sisters and a father who sang off-key and a mother who kept a shrine to no god at all because Vell's Crossing had been founded by people who remembered what gods cost.

He had been nine years old when the war's last ember drifted east and caught the town.

People called them the god-wars now, in the way people call a storm by a woman's name—as if naming it made it a single thing instead of a hundred years of horror. The histories taught in the Choir's schools were careful histories. They spoke of the Sundering Accord, of the great pact where the surviving gods agreed to withdraw, to go quiet, to let mortalkind govern itself without divine patronage and all the slaughter that patronage required. What the careful histories did not dwell on was the order of events. The gods withdrew first. Then their faithful—the companies, the orders, the sworn bands who had built their whole identities around a god's favor—discovered they had been abandoned mid-prayer, and discovered also that each other still existed, still armed, still marked.

The last three years of the god-wars had involved no gods whatsoever. Only men with empty marks on their arms, killing each other over the memory of voices that no longer answered.

Vell's Crossing had stood between two such companies retreating from a battle neither had won. The Brightwater company—that name surfaced sometimes in old records, and Merek had learned to note it without flinching—had passed through first, buying provisions, leaving coin. The Ashen Vigil had come through a day later, looking for them, and had decided Vell's Crossing must have aided their enemies, or might have, or simply existed along the road.

His mother had pushed him down the well shaft. He never understood why the well. Perhaps she thought the water would hide him. He had stood ankle-deep in the dark listening to the sounds above for a length of time he refused even now to measure, and when he finally climbed out, the town was smoke and the sky was the color of a bruise going bad.

He was the only reason he knew his mother's last act had been an act of love rather than panic. Someone had to know it. So he knew it.

* * *

"Tell me about the others," he said to Odile Ferrin. "The ones who've been thawing, as you put it. You've heard talk. Market talk. Confession talk—you worked the laundry houses; you heard everything."

"I don't want anyone else to get in trouble."

"Odile." He kept his voice level, almost gentle. "Do you know why the Choir exists? Not the schoolbook answer. The real one."

She looked at him with red-rimmed eyes. "To keep magic outlawed."

"To keep the gods asleep," Merek said. "Magic is just the word we use for what leaks through when they stir. And when gods stir, Odile, people burn. Not metaphorically. I have walked through towns where the burning took eleven days to finish. I was in one of those towns. I am the reason the Choir exists, in a sense—I'm what's left over when the Choir arrives too late." He leaned forward. "So when I ask you about the others, understand: every hour I spend finding them gently is an hour someone harsher doesn't spend finding them at all. Give me names, and I promise you visits, not pyres. I give you my word on it, and my word is the only currency I have left."

Something in her face shifted—not trust, exactly. Recognition. People always recognized grief, even dressed as authority.

"There's a chandler on Salt Lane," she said slowly. "Marlow. His wife says he talks in his sleep now, but not in his own voice. Old-fashioned words. Words like from a hymnal." She hesitated. "There's a woman down by the fish market. Sells charms—little luck-knots, salt-charms, harmless things. But Marta—Marta who does the nets—says she saw the woman's wrist glow through her glove when a child fell in the alley behind the stalls. Saw light come up out of the ground, like the child's crying pulled it up."

Merek's pen stopped moving.

"A charm-seller," he said. "By the fish market."

"Gray market. Off Cooper's Stair. She keeps her arms wrapped. Marta says leather wraps, even in summer, and everyone assumed it was scarring, or shame, or—"

"What's her name?"

"I don't know it. Truly. She's just—the charm-woman. Quiet. Polite. Buys her bread from the baker on the corner and never argues about the price." Odile's bound hands tightened in her lap. "Inquisitor, she's nobody. Whatever's happening in this city, it isn't her. She sells knots and says kind things to widows. If the Choir takes her, take me instead, I'll—"

"The mark," Merek interrupted, because his heart had begun to beat in a way he distrusted. "When Marta saw the light. Describe the wrist. Exactly."

"Glowing," Odile said, bewildered. "Like coals under skin."

"Was there a shape? A sigil?"

"I didn't see it. Marta only glimpsed. But she said—" Odile paused, dredging. "She said it looked like a wave breaking. Or a bird diving. Something curved, with a point. She couldn't say better than that."

A wave breaking. A curved line with a point.

Merek had spent nine years memorizing the heraldry of dead divinity. It was part of the training: every sworn mark of every god of the old pantheon, catalogued and cross-referenced, so that any fragment recovered from the wars could be identified, contained, studied—or, in the early years, burned. Most marks were geometric. Circles, interlocking triangles, the vertical bars of the Ashen Vigil's grim standard.

But there had been water-gods. Fewer, and quieter, and mostly gone before the end—and one of them had kept a mercenary company whose members bore a mark shaped like a wave about to break. The records called the goddess *the Voice Below Tide*. Common soldiers had called her something else. Merek had read the court transcripts from the trials of the company's survivors, twenty years ago, filed and dusty, and one phrase recurred across forty depositions, given by men and women who wept as they said it:

*We served Veyra. We served Veyra, and she went silent at the Saltfen.*

Saltfen. The battlefield where the last loyal company had stood in formation awaiting an order that never came, while their sister-companies slaughtered each other around them. The place where, according to every account, the goddess's voice had cut out mid-sentence—mid-*blessing*—and never returned.

And now prayers across Correnholt were thawing. And a charm-seller with hidden wrists had lit up like a coal when a child prayed in desperation.

Merek closed the file. His hand was steady. He had trained it to be steady through worse than this.

"Odile Ferrin," he said, "you're going to be released today. Your fine is commuted; your prayer will be returned to you. Your daughter will receive treatment at the Choir's infirmary for the lung-rot—we have treatments the city hospitals don't, and I'd rather she owe her breathing to us than to a thawing god. Consider it an exchange: your cooperation, freely given, for her care."

"That's not an exchange, that's a bribe."

"Yes," Merek said. "Most exchanges are. Take it anyway."

* * *

He walked the long corridor to the archives alone, as he preferred. The chapterhouse at night had a particular silence—not the Silence the Choir preached, which was meant to be reverent and full, but the ordinary human silence of stone and lamplight and distant footsteps. Merek had come to prefer it to the other kind. Reverence had always struck him as a door held open for something to walk through.

In the archive he requested the Saltfen deposition folios and the company muster rolls. The archivist—a young man with ink-stained fingers named Pell—brought them without comment, though his eyes lingered on the request slip. Merek supposed rumors moved fast in a building like this. Rumors of answered prayers. Rumors that the Inquisitor himself had been seen interviewing laundresses at midnight.

He spread the muster roll across the reading table and began the arithmetic.

Forty-one sworn members of Veyra's company recorded present at the Saltfen. Of these, nineteen confirmed dead in the fighting that followed the Silence—the desperate weeks when companies without gods tore at each other like dogs over an empty bowl. Twelve unaccounted for, presumed dead. Seven captured and tried; of those, five executed, two sentenced to the penitential labor camps, both dead within the decade.

That left three. Three marked survivors of the Saltfen company unaccounted for in any record Merek could find.

One was listed as *Brightwater, S.* — no further entry, no trial record, no death certificate, no camp intake. A hole in the ledger shaped exactly like a person.

He cross-referenced the gray-market reports. Charm-seller, Cooper's Stair, twelve years established—twelve years, and the Silence was thirteen years past, and people who needed to disappear needed a year or two to find a place to be invisible in. Leather wraps worn constantly. No recorded family. No registered guild membership, no temple affiliation, no militia service—in Correnholt, where every soul was counted in some ledger or other, a woman with no entries at all was not a nobody. She was a somebody performing nobodiness, and doing it well enough that only a glowing wrist in an alley had ever cracked the performance.

*Brightwater, S.*

Silas. He tried the name against the glimpse Marta had reported and found it fit with a horrible neatness.

Merek sat back in the archive chair and looked at the lamp flame for a long moment.

Here was the thing about arithmetic: it was honest. It did not care what you wanted. Nine plus nineteen plus seven left three, and one of the three had a name that matched a woman selling luck-charms twelve streets from the chapterhouse, and none of that changed because he wished it otherwise.

And here was the other thing about arithmetic: it was incomplete. Numbers could not tell him whether Silas Brightwater had spent twelve years building a quiet life out of knots and kindness, or twelve years waiting. They could not tell him whether the light in the alley had been an accident of proximity or the first crack in a dam. They could not tell him what was thawing beneath the world, or how fast, or whether it could be re-frozen at all.

For that, he needed the woman herself.

He thought of his sisters. He did this rarely—it was a discipline, like everything—but he permitted it now, because the decision ahead deserved the weight. Lissa, who had been seven, who used to braid grass crowns and crown him with them and declare him king of the well. Anni, who was four, who had cried at loud noises until their father taught her that thunder was just clouds talking. Clouds talking, Anni. Nothing to fear.

Clouds talking. Gods answering. Where was the line, really? When had it ever held?

He had been nine years old, standing in well-water, learning that faith did not save towns. Companies did. Companies with marks on their arms and gods in their ears, and when the gods went quiet the companies burned everything anyway, because the killing had never actually been about the gods. It had been about the certainty. Faith was certainty wearing vestments, and certainty was the fire itself, and the god was only the match.

Now matches were striking themselves across Correnholt. A laundress thawing. A chandler hymning in his sleep. A charm-woman glowing at a child's cry. Each one small. Each one a coal. And coals, given air and time and the great bellows of human hope, became conflagrations, and conflagrations did not check the muster rolls of the towns they ate.

If he moved softly—if he took Brightwater quietly, questioned her kindly, studied what leaked through her mark and sealed it—perhaps the coal stayed a coal. Perhaps the Choir learned enough about the thaw to stop it at the source. Perhaps the gods stayed asleep, and Vell's Crossings stayed unburned, and some other nine-year-old in some other town grew up never needing to know what the well was for.

Or perhaps he moved softly, and she slipped him, and the thaw accelerated, and by the time harsher men than he arrived—and there were always harsher men; he had signed the transfer orders himself, he knew the names—there would be nothing left to manage but ash and blame.

Softly, then. Softly, and soon, and alone. He would take no squad to Cooper's Stair. Squads frightened people, and frightened resonants did unpredictable things with light. One man, plain-cloaked, a neighbor's face, a knock at an ordinary door.

He returned the folios to Pell, who asked no questions, and walked out into the Correnholt night, where the fog came off the river in slow grey shoulders and the lamps along Cooper's Stair burned in halos of moisture. Somewhere below him, in the tangle of the gray market, a woman was probably closing her stall for the night. Counting her coins. Wrapping her wrists. Going home to a narrow bed in a narrow room, believing herself invisible.

Twelve years of invisibility, Merek thought, descending the stair. That was an achievement. He almost respected it.

But arithmetic was arithmetic. Three survivors, one name, one glowing wrist, and above it all a frozen god beginning, inch by inch, to thaw.

Tomorrow, he would knock on her door.

Behind him, in the dark of the archive, the lamp he'd left burning guttered once—though every window in the chapterhouse was shut—and steadied, and burned on.
