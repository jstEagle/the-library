## 7. The Relic Road

The barge was called *Marta's Regret*, which Silas thought was either very funny or very sad, and Brannoc Hale refused to say which.

They cast off from the salt-dock at moonset, when the tide turned and the harbor watch changed hands and every lantern along the Correnholt waterfront burned down to its last inch of oil. Hale had timed it the way he timed everything—by bribes, by habit, by thirty years of knowing exactly how lazy a man gets in the hour before dawn. The pilot was a woman named Osk with a face like a closed door. There were four deckhands who didn't ask questions because asking questions cost extra and Hale didn't pay for extras. And there was cargo under the tarp amidships that Silas was not supposed to look at, and did not look at, mostly.

"You'll ride below," Hale said, jerking his chin at the hold hatch. "You and the choir-girl."

"I can pull an oar."

"You can glow in the dark is what you can do." He said it quietly, but not gently. "Every time your heart rate goes up, that arm of yours lights like a festival lamp. I've got six people on this boat whose necks are worth more than your feelings. Below. Hatch shut. That's the price of passage."

Silas looked at him. Hale looked back with the flat patience of a man who had buried two wives and outlasted three customs regimes and would bury her too if she made him dig.

"Below," she said.

Tamsin was already down the ladder. Of course she was. The girl had the survival instincts of a cat and twice the sense about when to stop arguing. Silas followed her into the dark, and Osk swung the hatch shut above them, and the bolts went home with a sound like a small, polite prison.

* * *

The hold smelled of tar, salt pork, and old rope. A single storm-lantern hung from a beam, shuttered to a coin of light. Tamsin sat on a crate with her knees drawn up, and when Silas settled across from her, the girl watched her the way she'd watched her for three days now—openly, without apology, like reading a book she'd already decided to finish.

"Your arm's doing it again," Tamsin said.

Silas glanced down. Through the gap between her sleeve and her leather wrap, a thread of pale gold light pulsed in time with her pulse. She pressed her palm over it. "It does that."

"It's been doing it since we left the dock."

"It knows we're going the right way." She said it before she could decide whether to say it, and then hated how it sounded—like faith, like hope, like all the soft things she'd spent twelve years amputating. "Or it's just excited. Gods get excited. It doesn't mean anything."

Tamsin tilted her head. In the coin of lantern light her face was all edges—cheekbones sharp from three weeks of bad eating, eyes too big for them. "You talk about her like she's a dog."

"She was never a *her* until she needed a shape to talk to me in." Silas leaned her head back against the hull. The river was already moving under them; she could feel the barge's slow roll, the creak of timber taking the current. "Gods are weather, mostly. Pressure systems. Veyra wore a shape because soldiers are stupid and we'd have prayed at nothing otherwise. A woman with a spear. Grey eyes. Voice like—" She stopped.

"Like what?"

"Like the moment after you decide to live."

Tamsin was quiet for a while. The river talked to itself outside the hull.

"In the seminary," she said finally, "they taught us Veyra died at the Saltfen. Killed by the other gods for warmongering. They made us sing the *Requiem for the Broken Blade* every year. Four verses. Very mournful." Her voice went strange. "But that's not what happened, is it. Because she's not dead. You're not carrying a ghost."

"No."

"So the Choir has been singing a funeral for someone who isn't dead." Tamsin unfolded slowly, like something uncurling. "And they've been hunting people like me for hearing her name in visions that weren't supposed to exist. Which means they know she's not dead too." Her eyes came up, bright and hard. "Silas. What are they actually afraid of?"

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Three days ago this girl had been a stowaway stealing their bread, and Silas had put a knife to her throat, and Tamsin had said, *you're Brightwater, aren't you*, with a look on her face like a pilgrim finding a relic in a ditch. Three days. It felt like longer. It felt like the girl had been on the boat since the beginning.

"They're afraid of waking up," Silas said. "Go to sleep. We've got a long night."

* * *

They didn't sleep.

An hour past the harbor mouth, the river widened into the Graywater estuary, and the barge's rhythm changed—Osk running them close to the reed banks where the channel narrowed and the patrol boats couldn't turn. Silas felt it through the hull before she heard anything: the crew moving differently overhead, softer steps, no voices. War-shipping. She'd been on enough night crossings to know the grammar.

Then the lantern above them went dark, and the hatch bolts slid, and Osk's voice came down the ladder in a whisper flattened to almost nothing: "*Light on the water. Two points astern.*"

Silas was up the ladder before the words finished.

On deck, the world was black and silver. The reeds stood along both banks like a crowd holding its breath, and behind them, maybe half a mile back, a lantern burned low and green—the Choir's color, the color of silence, chosen so that everyone on the river knew exactly whose boat it was. As Silas watched, a second green light kindled further out, wider in the channel, cutting off the deep water. Pincer formation. Textbook.

Hale crouched at the tiller beside Osk, and his face in the dark was older than it had been on the dock.

"How fast?" he murmured.

"Patrol boats run two knots better than us loaded," Osk said. "Twenty minutes if they're keen. Less if they've a resonator aboard."

"A what?" Silas said.

Hale didn't answer. He was looking at her arm.

She followed his gaze. Under her sleeve, through the leather wrap, gold light was leaking—thin threads of it, brightening with each beat of her heart, and worse than that, it was *moving*, crawling up toward her wrist like ink climbing wet paper. Twelve years of nothing, and now the mark behaved like a struck bell whenever another bell rang nearby.

"A resonator," Hale said grimly. "Choir engine. Sings a note that makes god-stuff ring back. Used to be they used them to find relics. Now they use them to find *you*."

"Can they hear us yet?"

"Not yet. But they will, and when they do, they won't need the lanterns." He spat over the side. "There's a branch channel two miles up, the Old Salt Cut. Shallows, silted, no patrol boat bigger than a skiff can follow. If we make it before they close—"

"And if we don't?"

"Then we're a sermon, love. An object lesson about what happens to smugglers who carry marked cargo." His jaw worked. "I knew this cargo would hang me someday. I just hoped it'd be slower."

Silas turned to look astern. The green lights were closer. Not rushing—they didn't have to rush. The estuary only went one way.

"We need to go quiet," she said. "No engine noise, no wake—"

"We're a *barge*, Brightwater. Quiet is not among our gifts."

"Then we need them blind."

"With what? You going to glow at them?"

And that was when Tamsin stepped out of the hold.

Silas hadn't heard the hatch. The girl moved quiet as spilled water, barefoot, hair loose, and there was something in her stance that stopped both Hale and Osk mid-word—an acolyte's stance, spine straight, hands loose, the posture of someone about to do a thing they'd been trained to do since childhood and punished for wanting to do ever since.

"Hymnals," Tamsin said. "Do you have any hymnals aboard? Or—no. No, I don't need the book. I need everyone below deck. Now."

"Tamsin—" Silas started.

"*Now*," the girl said, and her voice cracked like a whip, and for one second Silas heard something underneath it—a resonance, a doubled tone, like two girls speaking in perfect unison—and the hair rose on her arms, and the mark on her wrist flared so hot she bit down on a cry.

Hale stared at her. "What in the drowned deep is she?"

"She's the reason we're alive in about four minutes," Silas said. "Everyone below. Go."

* * *

Later, Silas would try to describe it, and fail, and keep trying.

What she saw: Tamsin standing alone at the stern of a dark barge in the middle of a black estuary, facing two green lanterns and whatever rowed behind them. What she heard, crouched at the hatch with the bolt cracked one inch: a hymn.

Not the *Requiem*. Not anything the Choir sang—those were songs about gods, safe and embalmed, sung at gods the way you'd speak politely over a grave. This was different. This was old. The melody walked downward, verse after verse, each line lower than the last, a stair descending into dark water, and Tamsin's voice carried over the river with impossible ease—not loud, exactly, but *everywhere*, the way thunder is everywhere, the way cold is everywhere. And beneath her voice there was the other voice, the doubled tone, vast and patient and grieving, and Silas knew that voice.

Knew it the way you know a scar.

"*Veyra,*" she breathed, and her mark blazed through wrap and sleeve and leather, pouring gold light across the deck, and Tamsin—without turning, without breaking the hymn—said, clearly, conversationally, mid-verse: "*Wrap it in the sail canvas. Please.*"

Silas wrapped it in sail canvas.

The hymn descended. On the water astern, things began to happen. First the green lanterns guttered—both of them, together, like candles pinched by the same breath. Then, from the direction of the nearer patrol boat, came shouting, then the shouts dissolved into something worse: confusion, men calling to each other in ordinary voices gone ragged with fear, the sound of an oar snapping against a thole, the wet crunch of a hull finding a sandbar it had crossed a hundred times before. Blind. Not blinded in the eyes—in the *charts*. The river itself had forgotten where it was, or the boats had forgotten where the river was; either way, the lead patrol boat ran aground with a groan that carried a mile across the flat water, and the second one sheered away hard, oars backing water, its crew choosing the open estuary over a channel that had started singing.

Tamsin sang the last verse down to silence.

Then she swayed, and Silas was across the deck before she finished falling, catching her under the arms, lowering her against the rail. The girl's eyes were unfocused, her lips gray, her hands shaking so hard her knuckles clicked.

"That," Silas said, "was the single most forbidden thing I have ever seen anyone do."

"Mm." Tamsin blinked slowly. "It gets worse. That wasn't the whole hymn. The Choir cut the last three verses out of the canon four hundred years ago. They're—mmmm." Her eyes rolled back.

"Tamsin." Silas shook her shoulder. "*Tamsin.*"

The girl's eyes refocused with visible effort. She smiled—lopsided, exhausted, absolutely insufferable. "I'm fine. Ask me tomorrow." Her hand found Silas's wrist, found the sail-canvas wrap, and pressed. Beneath the cloth, the mark had gone quiet for the first time in days. "See? Better. She liked it. She *really* liked it."

"How do you know that hymn?" Silas kept her voice low. On the water astern, the grounded patrol boat was still shouting at itself. "The Choir doesn't teach it. Nobody teaches it. Where did—"

"My grandmother." Tamsin closed her eyes. "Before the seminary took me. She used to hum it when she baked. Said it was a luck-song, didn't even know it was Veyra's. The Choir burned her for less, later, so I suppose it's good she never learned what she was humming." A pause. Her breathing evened. "I remembered it wrong for years. Tonight it just... corrected itself. Like she was holding the book."

Silas sat back on her heels. The estuary stretched ahead, silver-black and empty, the Old Salt Cut somewhere in the dark ahead of them, and behind them the Choir floundered in a river that had turned traitor. Above, Hale was leaning over the rail staring down at the two of them with the expression of a man recalculating every debt he'd ever tallied.

"The Cut," Osk called softly from the tiller. "Coming up on the Cut."

"Take it," Hale said. Then, to Silas: "Get her below. And Brightwater—"

"Yeah."

"When this cargo's delivered, I'm renegotiating." But there was something in his voice that hadn't been there on the dock. Something that might, in a generous light, have been the beginning of respect. "Nobody sings my boat out of a Choir pincer. Nobody. Whatever you two are, you're worth more than I quoted."

He turned back to the tiller. Silas gathered Tamsin up—the girl was already half-asleep, boneless with spent power—and carried her to the hatch.

At the top of the ladder, Tamsin stirred against her shoulder and murmured, barely audible:

"She says thank you."

Silas froze. "Who does."

"You know who." A breath of a laugh, gone almost before it arrived. "Grey eyes. Spear. Terrible manners."

Silas stood in the hatchway a long moment, listening to the river take them into the Old Salt Cut, into silt and shadow and the country beyond the Choir's maps. The mark under the canvas was warm and steady as a sleeping animal.

Twelve years of silence. And now, in the space of one night, her god had a voice again—and the voice belonged, somehow, to a starving runaway who hummed forbidden hymns and fell asleep in her arms.

Below decks, in the dark, Tamsin smiled in her sleep and whispered a word Silas hadn't heard spoken aloud since the Saltfen: a soldier's word, a company-word, the word Veyra's sworn had used for *home*.

Silas went down the ladder, and shut the hatch, and did not let herself think about what it meant that her hands were shaking.
