## 8. Where the Voice Went Out

The Saltfen didn't look like a graveyard. That was the first lie of the place.

It looked like nothing at all—a broad gray flat where the river silted out into marsh, stitched with reeds and the black ribs of old pilings. Gulls argued over the mudflats. Somewhere a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of a thing that had never once been lied to.

Silas stood at the rail of Hale's barge and looked at the water and felt twelve years fold up like a map.

"You're sure this is it?" Hale asked behind her. He'd said little since they left Correnholt, since the patrol boats, since Tamsin stood at the prow and sang a hymn that turned the Choir's lanterns blind. He was a man recalculating everything he'd believed about the girl he'd fed dinner to.

"I'm sure."

"You don't look sure."

"I look like a woman standing on the grave of everyone she ever loved, Brannoc. Forgive me if my face is doing something you don't approve of."

He grunted and let it go. Behind him, Tamsin sat on a coil of rope with her knees drawn up, watching Silas with that steady, unblinking attention that still made her want to check her pockets. The acolyte had barely slept. Neither of them had. But Tamsin had said, before they made the last bend of the river, one thing only:

"It's louder here."

Silas hadn't asked what she meant. She hadn't needed to. Since Correnholt, since the child in the alley and the light that answered, Silas had been hearing it too—not a voice, not yet, but a pressure. A hum beneath the world, like a note held by someone with enormous patience and no intention of stopping.

The mark on her arm hadn't stopped burning since the market. She'd wrapped it in linen and leather, and the linen had scorch-marks now, faint brown blooms like pressed flowers.

They beached the barge where the old landing had been. There was almost nothing left of it—a few pilings, a rotted cleat. Twelve years of tide had done its work. The mercenary company that had made camp here had left nothing but the land's indifference, and the land had been thorough.

Silas went first. Of course she went first. She had always gone first; it was the one prayer Veyra had ever asked of her, and she had never stopped saying it with her feet.

* * *

The battlefield announced itself slowly.

First the ground changed—softer, giving, the silt holding the memory of what had been churned into it. Then the reeds thinned, and the mud began to give things up: a buckle green with age. A boot sole. A helm, split clean down the crest, half-swallowed by the earth. Silas stepped around a shape in the mud that might once have been a shield and did not let herself think about whose arm had held it.

She had buried eleven of them herself. She remembered the arithmetic of it with a horrible precision: eleven graves dug by one pair of hands, in ground that fought her, in the hour after the voice stopped. She remembered deciding not to mark them, because markers could be found, and the Choir was already coming. She remembered telling herself that Veyra would know her own dead without stones.

She remembered, most of all, the last thing Veyra had said.

Not the silence. The words before it. Mid-prayer, mid-battle, mid-breath—Veyra's voice had simply stopped, like a door closing in a house where someone had been singing. And the company had heard it. All of them. Forty-one soldiers sworn to a goddess of the hearth-flame and the harbor-light, and they had all heard their god go quiet in the middle of a sentence.

*Hold the line, Brightwater. I am—*

And then nothing.

Twelve years, and Silas still flinched at the dash.

"Silas." Tamsin's voice, soft, behind her. "There. Do you see it?"

She saw it.

The altar stood on a low rise at the center of the flat, and it was wrong, the way a scar is wrong. It was a single stone, taller than a man, black as cooled tar, and it did not belong to the marsh. Nothing grew near it. The reeds bent away from it in a wide ring, as if the ground itself had leaned back. The gulls wouldn't cross the ring. Silas watched one bank and turn and flap off along the shoreline rather than pass over that circle of bare gray earth.

The company had raised it in the first week of the campaign. She remembered the masons singing while they worked. She remembered Veyra's presence in it, warm as a hearthstone, and how the whole company had slept well that first night with their backs against it.

It looked like a tombstone now. It had always looked like a tombstone. She had just been too young to read it.

"You don't have to," Tamsin said.

"Yes, I do."

"That's not what I mean." Tamsin came up beside her, close enough that Silas could smell woodsmoke and river water on her. "I mean you don't have to be the one. Whatever's in there, it's been waiting twelve years. It can wait another hour while you eat something, or sleep, or—"

"Tamsin."

"You're shaking."

"I've been shaking for twelve years. I'm just doing it standing up now." Silas looked at the stone. "Stay back. Whatever happens—stay back until I call for you."

"Will you call for me?"

The question landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there. "Yes," Silas said. "I'll call for you."

* * *

She crossed the ring alone.

The ground inside it was bare and hard, packed smooth as a threshing floor, and the silence changed the moment her boots touched it. Not quiet—silence. The difference between an empty room and a held breath. The hum she'd been hearing since Correnholt dropped away all at once, and in its absence she heard her own heart, fast and stupid, and the lap of the river, and nothing else.

The altar was warm.

She noticed it before she touched it—felt the heat coming off the stone like heat off a hearth in a cold room, the kind you lean toward without deciding to. Twelve years of river weather, and the stone was warm. She put her bare hand flat against it, and the leather wrap on her arm grew hot enough that she smelled it scorching, and she did not pull away.

"Hello," she said. Her voice came out cracked. She tried again. "Veyra. It's Silas. Silas Brightwater, Third Company, oath-sworn at Correnholt, in the year of—" She stopped, because she'd rehearsed this speech on the river for two days and it was nonsense, all of it, a soldier's formalities laid like flowers on a grave. She pressed her forehead against the warm stone instead. "You went quiet. In the middle of a sentence. You went quiet and I buried eleven of my people and I have spent twelve years not knowing if you were dead, or gone, or if I was—"

*If you were what?*

The voice filled the flat.

It didn't come from the stone. It came from everywhere—the reeds, the water, the ground under her knees, the air in her own lungs. Veyra's voice had always been like that, less a sound than a weather. Silas's legs went out from under her and she knelt in the gray dirt with her hand still on the stone, and she wept like a recruit, ugly and loud, because the voice was real, it had always been real, she had not been praying into a well for twelve years.

*If you were mad,* Veyra finished, gently. *You were not mad, Brightwater. I am sorry. I am so sorry. I could not answer.*

"Where were you?" It came out as a sob. She hated it. She let it. "Where did you go?"

*Here.* A pause, and Silas felt the whole marsh attend the pause, felt the weight of what was being gathered to be said. *Here, under this. Under everything. They buried me, Silas.*

"Who buried you?"

*The others. The old ones. The Choir of heaven, before your Choir of the Silent borrowed the name.* The warmth of the stone deepened; Silas felt it against her palm like a pulse. *When the last war came, every god was called to the field. Every one was asked to spend their people like coin. I refused. I told them my flame was for hearths, not for forges of war. I told them I would not burn my sworn ones to buy a throne of clouds.*

"And they—"

*They broke me and they buried me here, where I had made my stand, so that my own altar would be my stone. And they silenced my voice, so that no prayer of mine could reach the world, and no prayer of yours could reach me. What you have heard since the market—that is not my strength. That is my voice, leaking through a crack in the seal. The prayers across Correnholt, the answered ones—they are not me giving. They are me calling through the crack, and the world answering on its own. I am a prisoner tapping on a wall.*

Silas knelt with the mud soaking her knees and the warmth of her god under her hand, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that the silence had never been abandonment. That the eleven graves were not evidence of a god who had fled. That the thing she had hated Veyra for, quietly, in the small hours, for twelve years—the going, the leaving, the not answering—had been a chaining.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "We thought—Gods, Veyra, we thought you'd left us."

*I know.* And the voice did something it had never done in all the years Silas had borne the mark. It broke. *I heard you dig. Eleven graves. I heard every spadeful and I could not so much as warm your hands. You think I do not know what that silence cost? You think I have not counted it, every day, in the dark?*

Silas pressed her forehead to the stone again and stayed there a long time, and the stone held her the way a hearth holds a cold room, and when she finally spoke her voice was steady again, because she was a mercenary and grief, for her, had always been something you carried while walking.

"How do I get you out?"

*Ah.* The warmth flickered. *There it is.*

"There what is?"

*You were always the fastest of my sworn ones to the point, Brightwater. I have missed it. I have missed you.* The marsh seemed to lean in. *There is a way. The seal was made by gods, but it was keyed to a bearer—to the oathmark, to the spark that lives in the one who carries my mark. The seal is a lock, and your bloodline of oath is its key. A bearer can unmake it.*

"Then I'll unmake it." She was already reaching for her knife, already turning her wrist to bare the mark. "Tell me what to—"

*Freely given,* Veyra said, and the word landed like an axe.

Silas went still.

*The seal cannot be cut or forced or stolen. It was made to be opened only by surrender. A bearer's divine spark—the fragment of me that lives in you, that I put in your line when I swore the first Brightwater—must be given back. All of it. Freely. Willingly. With a whole heart.* The voice was very quiet now. *You would give me back everything of me that is in you. You would be unmarked. Ungodded. Mortal in the way that other mortals are mortal, and I would rise, and the seal would break, and I would be free.*

The river lapped. The gulls argued somewhere far away, beyond the ring where they wouldn't cross.

"And the other gods," Silas said. "The ones who buried you. If the seal breaks—"

*Then they will know I am loose. And they will remember that I refused them. And there will be a reckoning, one way or another, because I will not be gentle and they will not be kind.* A long pause. *I am not going to lie to you, Silas Brightwater. You have earned better than lies. If I rise, the war may rise with me. That is the truth of it. That is the price on the other side of the coin.*

Silas looked at her hand on the stone. At the mark beneath the scorched leather, burning like a coal. Twelve years she had wanted one thing: the voice back. The warmth back. Her god back. She had built a whole gray little life around the absence of it—charms and salt and careful invisibility—and every hour of it had been a waiting room, and here at last was the door, and the door was open, and the price was written on it in her own blood.

Everything she had left.

Not her life. Her life was cheap; she'd have paid it gladly and called it a fair wage. Her spark. The divine fragment, the oath, the thing that made her Veyra's and not merely a woman who had once been Veyra's. Give it all, and there would be no mark, no warmth, no voice ever again—she would free her god into a sky that might burn the world for it, and then she would stand in the ashes of that transaction as nothing but a woman, deaf to the divine for the rest of her mortal days.

And Tamsin.

The thought arrived without permission and refused to leave. Tamsin, who had run from the Choir with a vision in her head. Tamsin, who had looked at Silas on the barge like Silas was a page in a book she'd already read to the end. If Veyra rose, if the old gods stirred, if the war came back to Correnholt—Tamsin would be standing in it. They all would. Hale and his grandchildren. The widow and her boy. Every answered prayer in the city was a crack in a dam, and Silas was holding the only key.

*You do not have to decide now,* Veyra said. *I have waited twelve years under this stone. I can wait while you think.*

"No," Silas said. "You can't. Every day you're down there, the Choir is hunting people like Tamsin. Every day the crack gets wider and the prayers get louder and Merek gets closer. You can't wait, and I can't pretend otherwise." She got to her feet. Her knees were wet with marsh. Her voice was level. "But I'm not giving it to you here, alone, on your word alone. I have people now. I didn't have people when you went quiet. I have them now, and I don't spend them without telling them the price."

*Silas—*

"You waited twelve years in the dark without lying to me. I can wait three days without lying to them. That's the deal, Veyra. That's the oath I'm swearing back."

The silence stretched. Then the warmth of the stone rose, and it was not the warmth of a prisoner tapping a wall—it was the warmth of a hearth, of a harbor-light, of the thing she had sworn to in the first place, and Silas felt it like a hand on her head, the way a mother blesses a child who has just become more stubborn than she is.

*Three days,* Veyra said. *And Brightwater—when you come back to this stone, come knowing. Come with a whole heart or not at all. The seal will take nothing less, and it will take everything you bring.*

"I know."

*And the acolyte.* The voice gentled further, and Silas went cold. *She hears me, even through the seal. Did you know that? She is the first in three hundred years who hears me unmarked. Ask her what she heard, Silas. Ask her what I said to her the night she ran.*

The warmth faded. The hum came back—the pressure beneath the world, the note held with enormous patience. The gulls crossed the ring again as if nothing had happened, and the marsh went back to being a marsh, and the altar went back to being a black stone in the mud.

Except it wasn't. It was a door, and Silas was the key, and the price was everything she had left.

She walked back across the ring on legs that mostly worked. Tamsin stood at the edge of it, wrapped in her own arms against the river wind, and her eyes were wet, and she was looking at Silas the way you look at someone you have watched walk into a fire you saw coming.

"You heard," Silas said. Not a question.

"Some of it." Tamsin's voice was rough. "She said—she told me to ask you what she said to me. The night I ran."

"Then tell me."

Tamsin looked out at the black stone, and the wind came off the water, and when she spoke it was barely above the marsh.

"She told me a bearer would die at this altar."
