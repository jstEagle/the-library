## 9. Terms of an Old Oath

Tamsin had known the ending for eleven days.

She had counted them the way you count stitches in a wound—each one a small proof you're still paying attention, still awake, still carrying it. Eleven days since she'd stood in the Choir's dormitory at Vespers and heard a name no one was supposed to hear anymore, and understood that the voice in her head had not been hers, and had run before they could take the hearing away from her too.

Eleven days of knowing that Silas Brightwater would die at an altar of black stone.

And Silas had walked back across that ring of salt and asked *then tell me* as if Tamsin were delivering news about the weather.

So when the argument came—and it came that same night, because Tamsin had never once in her life been good at holding her tongue—it did not arrive politely. It arrived with her boots in the sand of the ruined chapel where Hale's crew had made camp, her finger less than a hand's width from Silas's sternum, and her voice cracking on the first word like river ice underfoot.

"You knew."

Silas didn't step back. That was the thing about her that drove Tamsin half-mad: she absorbed confrontation the way stone absorbs rain. "Knew what?"

"Don't." Tamsin's hands were shaking. She watched them shake and hated them for it. "Don't do the market-stall voice. Don't sell me something small and harmless. You stood in front of your god today and she told you the price, and you walked back across that ring with your face arranged like you were quoting me a price on luck-charms, and you have not said one word since—not one—about what it's going to cost."

The camp had gone quiet around them. Hale's people were pretending very hard to be asleep by their cookfire. The barge sat low on the black water behind them, moored against the ruins' edge, its single lantern turning the marsh grass to a fringe of dull gold. Somewhere out in the fen a bird cried once and gave up.

"Tamsin," Silas said quietly.

"No. No, you don't get my name right now, you don't get to make this gentle." Her eyes were burning. She realized distantly that she was crying, which was humiliating, which made her angrier, which made her cry harder—the old acolyte's curse, weeping as evidence, tears as testimony. "I heard her too, all right? I've been hearing her since before I ever met you. And I know what a freely given spark means, because Mother Verity taught us the old catechisms before they burned them, and I know what happens to bearers who give everything, and so do you. So do *you*, Silas."

For a moment Silas said nothing. The lantern light moved on her face, and Tamsin watched the mask try to hold and fail by degrees—a hairline fracture running from the set of her jaw to her eyes, which were suddenly, terribly tired.

"Keep your voice down," Silas said.

"Or what? Or the Inquisitor will find us faster?" Tamsin laughed, and it came out ugly. "Let him come. Let him hear it. Maybe he'll arrest you and save you from yourself."

That landed. She saw it land—a flinch, quick and involuntary, like a blink against a blow. And then, worse than the flinch, Silas turned away. Walked two steps toward the water and stopped, arms folded, shoulders squared toward the fen like a woman standing watch over nothing.

Tamsin stood in the cold with her heart slamming and realized she had two choices: scream at the broad, stubborn, infuriating back until the whole camp woke up, or follow her down to the mudflat where the truth could be spoken at a volume that wouldn't get anyone killed.

She followed. Of course she followed. She'd been following this woman since a dormitory in Correnholt; she simply hadn't known it yet.

* * *

The mudflat below the chapel was ribbed with tide-lines, silvered where the moon caught the wet. Silas stood at the margin where water met land, and the oathmark on her forearm glowed faintly through its leather wrap—a pulse, slow as breathing, like something sleeping just under the skin.

"I wasn't hiding it," Silas said finally. "From you."

"That's a lie."

"It's a lie," Silas agreed, without turning. "But it's a specific lie, and I want you to understand it before you decide how much to hate me."

Tamsin wrapped her arms around herself. The wind came off the Saltfen carrying brine and old ash—the whole battlefield smelled of it, she'd noticed, even after three hundred years, as if the ground itself remembered burning. "Then say it plainly. Please. I'm begging you. Just once, say a thing plainly."

Silas turned. In the dark her eyes were almost colorless, and there was something in them Tamsin hadn't seen before—not the weariness of the market, not the grim competence of the river run. Fear. Plain, unarmored fear, worn openly for maybe the first time since Tamsin had known her.

"She said a spark, freely given," Silas said. "Hers is gone—imprisoned, starved, whatever word you want. What's left of her is what lives in me. Twelve years of it, sitting quiet in my blood while I sold charms to widows and tried not to exist. If I give it back, she wakes. Whole. Furious. Three hundred years of being buried under that altar, Tamsin, and every god that put her there still holding the stones down. You saw what she is even muffled. Now imagine her unmuffled, waking angry, with a war already half-started above her."

"So you refuse."

"So I—" Silas stopped. Her jaw worked. When she spoke again, each word came out like something pried loose with tools. "When I was nineteen years old, I swore an oath on my blood that I would carry her spark and keep it safe until she needed it. Not spend it. Not trade it. Keep it. That was the whole shape of my life, Tamsin. I built everything on top of it—the silence, the hiding, the little harmless life—because keeping it meant staying alive, staying hidden, staying small. And now she needs it. And if I give it, I might start a war that kills everyone in Correnholt, everyone on that barge, everyone whose name I know. And if I don't give it—"

Her voice failed. Not dramatically. It just stopped, like a door closing on a room.

"If you don't give it," Tamsin finished, barely above a whisper, "you break your oath. And your god goes back to sleep in the dark forever, and you live."

"And I live." Silas looked down at the mark on her arm. The glow pulsed once beneath the leather, slow and patient, and Tamsin thought: *she can hear us.* The thought should have frightened her. Instead it made her furious all over again. "Twelve years ago I lost forty-one people because my god went silent mid-prayer and I told myself she'd abandoned us, and I built a whole religion of abandonment to survive it. Today I learned she never left. She was taken. Buried alive for refusing to fight. And every hour I stand here choosing my own skin, she's under that stone, awake, knowing exactly what I'm deciding."

"Oh, Silas." All the anger went out of Tamsin at once, and what replaced it was so much worse. "You were going to do it. You were going to walk into that ring tomorrow and give her everything and never tell us why."

"I was going to tell Hale. Someone had to know where the body was."

"*The body—*" Tamsin's voice broke clean in half. She pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth, breathed through her fingers, and lowered it. "You were going to die. At this altar. And you were going to arrange it like a delivery."

"The vision says a bearer dies here. Yours or mine or whoever's. The terms don't change because I don't like them."

"Yes, they do!" Tamsin stepped forward and grabbed Silas by the wrap on her arm, right over the mark, and felt the heat of it through the leather—felt Silas flinch, not from pain but from contact, as if the touch went all the way down to something raw. "Terms are negotiated. That's what terms *are*. You of all people—you spent twelve years learning every loophole in Correnholt's gray market, every way to make a small life inside a big prohibition, and now suddenly the rules are fixed and holy and you just have to obey them? Where's that woman? Give her back. I liked her better than this martyr."

Silas's breath came out ragged. For a long moment neither of them moved, Tamsin's fist knotted in the leather wrap, the mark pulsing between their hands like a second heartbeat they were both holding.

"I'm afraid," Silas said at last.

It was such a small sentence for such an enormous room. Tamsin let go of her arm.

"I've been afraid since I was nineteen," Silas went on, and now the words came faster, looser, like a knot slipping. "Afraid of the dark under the altar. Afraid of the war. Afraid of dying badly on a battlefield again, except this time alone, except this time it would be my choice, which is either better or infinitely worse, I genuinely don't know. But do you want to know what I'm actually afraid of? The real answer, underneath?"

"Tell me."

"I'm afraid that if I stop moving toward that altar, I'll find out I don't want to live more than I want anything else." Her voice dropped. "Forty-one people, Tamsin. I carried that number for twelve years. Every night. And some part of me decided a long time ago that the debt only gets paid one way, and every road since has just been me walking toward it in a circle, calling it duty, calling it oath, calling it anything except what it is."

The wind moved the marsh grass. Out on the black stone altar, far across the ring, nothing stirred—but Tamsin felt it anyway, that listening quality the whole ruin had, the sense of a vast attention held just below the surface, patient as sediment.

"What is it, then?" she asked. "What is it actually?"

Silas looked at her for a long time. And Tamsin watched her decide—watched the exact moment the last door inside her opened, because Silas Brightwater's face had always been easy to read if you knew what to look for, and what to look for was the way she stopped arranging herself.

"It's you," Silas said. "It's been you since the barge. Since you pulled a knife on me with shaking hands and lied about not being hungry. I have wanted, for the first time in twelve years, to see tomorrow. Do you understand what that does to a plan like mine? A sacrifice needs a person who wants nothing. And I—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Tamsin closed the distance between them and kissed her, there on the tide-line, with the dead god's altar watching and the mark burning warm between them, and Silas's hand came up to cradle the back of her head like something breakable, and Tamsin thought, with a clarity that felt almost cruel: *this is why she couldn't tell me. Because telling me makes it real, and real things can be argued with.*

When they broke apart, Silas rested her forehead against Tamsin's. "This doesn't change the arithmetic," she murmured.

"No," Tamsin agreed. "But it changes who gets to do the sums."

* * *

They did not go back to camp. They sat instead on a fallen lintel at the ruin's edge, shoulder to shoulder, watching the fen breathe. Tamsin had produced, from somewhere inside her coat, half a loaf of stolen ship's bread, and they passed it back and forth in silence for a while—an intimacy older than any vow, two people eating in the dark together.

"Tell me about the night you ran," Silas said eventually. "All of it. You've given me pieces. I want the whole shape."

Tamsin chewed, swallowed, looked out at the water. "Vespers. Third bell. We sang the Litany of Quiet—same words every night, six years, until the words stopped meaning anything. Except that night the words *moved*. There was a name in them that isn't in any version of the litany. Veyra. I said it out loud before I could stop myself. One word, in the middle of four hundred voices." She smiled, without much humor. "You know what happened next."

"They took you to the reflection cells."

"Three days. No mirrors, no voices, just the arithmetic of silence—they call it 'clarification.' On the third day Mother Verity came in, and I thought, finally, someone will ask me what I heard. Instead she told me I'd be transferred to the resonance engines. They're building them under the cathedral, Silas. Great brass throats, tuned to catch returning gods and squeeze them dry. They've been waiting twelve years for the prayers to start answering, and now they have, and they intend to harvest whatever comes back." She hugged her knees. "I ran that night. Through the cisterns. I'd planned nothing—I just ran, and stole aboard the first barge heading out of the city, and it happened to be his." She nodded back toward the camp, toward the sound of Hale snoring like distant surf. "And the whole time, in my head, her voice. Saying: *a bearer dies at the altar. Tell her. Make her choose knowing.*"

"And you've known eleven days."

"Twelve now." Tamsin glanced sideways. "I could ask you the same question, you know. Why didn't *you* tell me? First night on the barge, when you caught me with the knife—you looked at me like you recognized me. Did you hear her too?"

Silas was quiet long enough that Tamsin thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "The mark woke the day the child prayed in the alley. Light out of nowhere. And that night I dreamed in a language I haven't spoken since the Saltfen, and the dream had a shape, and the shape was this place. So yes. I think we were both being summoned." A pause. "I didn't tell you because telling you made you a target. The Choir burns resonants. And because—" her mouth twisted, "—because I'm a coward in exactly the ways I accuse myself of being brave."

"We're a matched set, then."

"Worse. We're a congregation."

Tamsin laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and bright in the dark—and leaned her head against Silas's shoulder. The mark's glow had steadied to a low ember between them. For a while there was only the fen and the bread and the warmth of another person, which Tamsin had learned, in six years of communal dormitories, to value above nearly everything.

"Here's what I believe," she said at last. "Not doctrine. Belief. I believe your god refused a war and got buried for it, and I believe that makes her the only god worth praying to. I believe the terms are real but not final—nothing the gods agree to stays final, that's the entire history of religion. And I believe that if you walk into that ring tomorrow to die, I will physically attach myself to you, and we will both look extremely stupid, and I am trained in scripture, not wrestling, so it will be undignified for everyone."

"Tamsin—"

"I'm not finished. My point is not that I can stop you. Nobody can stop you, that's the whole tragedy of you. My point is that you spent one evening telling me the truth and the plan already changed—you said it yourself, the sacrifice needs a person who wants nothing, and you want something now. *Me.* So the old terms are void, and new ones are open, and I am telling you formally, as a defrocked acolyte of the Choir of the Silent: I am entering negotiations."

Silas turned her head. Their faces were very close. "On behalf of whom?"

"On behalf of both of us." Tamsin reached over and laid her palm flat on the leather wrap, over the mark, deliberately this time. The glow answered—brightened, warmed, a pulse like recognition. "Because I think she can hear me. And I think she'd rather have a bearer who fights for a third path than one who marches obediently into the ground. You told me she was imprisoned for refusing to fight. Then refuse, Silas. Refuse beautifully."

Under Tamsin's hand, the mark flared—once, hard, bright enough to throw their shadows long across the mudflat—and in the flare, for half a heartbeat, Tamsin heard it, clear as a bell across water:

*Yes.*

They stared at each other.

"She's listening," Tamsin whispered.

"She's always been listening." Silas sounded stunned, and young, and hopeful, and Tamsin fell a little further in love with her right there in the mud. "Gods. Twelve years and I forgot what that's like."

"Then we have work to do, because—" Tamsin stopped.

Because there was a light out on the water. Far off, at the fen's southern edge where the river narrowed—a lantern signal, three short flashes, pause, three more. Then another light answering it from the east. Then a third, farther off, and the pattern repeating, patient and methodical, sweeping the dark the way you sweep a field for something you have lost and fully intend to find.

Silas was on her feet before Tamsin finished speaking. "Search grid. That's a search grid."

"How close?"

Silas shaded her eyes, counting lights, counting pauses, doing mercenary math with her jaw tight. When she answered, her voice had gone flat and professional, and her hand had found Tamsin's and gripped it hard.

"Close enough that they found the barge's wake. Close enough that by morning, this altar won't be ours anymore."

Behind them, across the camp, Brannoc Hale's snores stopped abruptly. In the sudden silence, the old smuggler's voice carried flat across the ruins:

"Everyone up. We're leaving."
