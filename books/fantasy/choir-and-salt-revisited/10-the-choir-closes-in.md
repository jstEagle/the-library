## 10. The Choir Closes In

The night broke wrong.

Silas felt it before she heard it—the mark on her arm waking like a struck bell, humming through the leather wrap, through the blanket, through the ground itself. She came up out of her bedroll with a knife already in her hand and the taste of iron in her mouth.

Beside her, Tamsin stirred. "What—"

"Quiet."

The Saltfen ruins at midnight should have been silent except for wind and the mutter of marsh water finding its way through old bones of stone. Instead Silas heard oars. Many oars, muffled with cloth, and beneath them the low sustained note she'd learned to dread in the last three days: resonance engines, Choir-built, singing their flat dead hymn across the water.

They had found them. Or they had found the place and were sweeping it, which amounted to the same thing.

She shook Tamsin's shoulder. "Up. Now. Wake Ives. Get Hale's people to the boats."

Tamsin was moving before Silas finished speaking, no questions, no fumbling—a month ago she'd been a starving stowaway, and now she moved through the dark camp like someone who had done this all her life. Maybe she had. The Choir taught its children discipline if nothing else.

Silas crossed to the altar side of the camp where Brannoc Hale stood at the edge of the ruin wall, one hand shading his eyes toward the river.

"How many?" he said.

"Four boats. Maybe five." She could see them now as her eyes adjusted—dark shapes against the gray water, lanterns hooded, the engines' song making her teeth ache. "We can't outrun them loaded."

"We don't need to outrun them." Hale's voice was calm in the way that only frightened men and very old smugglers could manage. "We need to be somewhere they aren't looking. The eastern channel. Silted up since the war, but my hull's shallow enough."

"They'll have lookouts on the banks."

"Aye." He turned to her then, and in the starlight his face was older than it had been that morning, or maybe she was finally seeing it true. "So somebody needs to give them something to look at."

Silas understood one heartbeat before he said it, and her stomach dropped through the marsh mud into somewhere cold.

"No."

"You've got your light," Hale said. "Show it on the west bank, loud and bright, and every eye on those boats goes there. My crew takes the east channel in the dark. That's arithmetic even a priest can follow."

"I'm not spending myself so you can—" 

"You're not spending anything." He smiled, gap-toothed and unhurried, the smile of a man who had cheated death so many times they'd stopped keeping score. "I'm going to stand here with a lamp and a loud mouth and make them think an old man is alone in these ruins, and by the time they work out their mistake I'll be gone over the back wall like a rumor. I've done it before. Younger men than me have died doing less."

"Hale—"

"Brannoc." He corrected her gently, the way he always did, as though being on first-name terms with her were the whole of his reward for everything. "You gave my granddaughter back her fever-free nights. You gave my crew a reason to hope, which is worth more than gold and harder to carry. Let me pay some of it down."

Behind them the engines' hymn swelled. The lead boat was turning toward the west bank. Toward him.

Silas's hands were shaking. Twelve years of running had taught her exactly one lesson: never be the bright thing. Never be seen. And now an old man was asking her to burn like a signal fire because it was the smart play, and it *was* the smart play, and she hated it with her whole chest.

"The moment we're clear," she said, "you run."

"The moment you're clear," he agreed, "I run. Go on, girl. The tide won't wait and neither will the Choir."

She went. It was the hardest ten steps of her life.

* * *

Inquisitor Dain Merek stood in the bow of the lead boat and watched the west bank of the Saltfen ruins, where a single lamp burned beside the silhouette of a man who was either a fool or bait.

"Fool," said Brother Cassel beside him, but Merek wasn't sure.

He had taken this river at speed when the resonants' trail warmed—three days of half-truths from river folk, a bribed toll-keeper, a barge with fresh scrape-marks on its hull. He had expected flight. Flight made sense. Flight was what guilty things did.

Instead the man on the bank stood still, lamp raised, and called out in a cracked, carrying voice: *"Ho there! Ho! Travelers! You're come to the wrong landing—nothing here but stones and gulls!"*

"Nothing here but stones," Merek repeated softly.

The resonance engine at his shoulder hummed its flat note. He had learned to read it the way sailors read weather. Right now it sang steady—no spike, no flare. Whatever divinity had been moving through Correnholt like a fever, it was not standing next to that lamp.

Which meant the old man was either genuinely nothing, or he was very good at standing between the Choir and what it wanted.

Merek had been good at arithmetic his whole life. His family had died in the god-wars' last spasm—father, mother, sister, in a border town that had the misfortune to sit between two warring faiths—and the sums had never once come out any way but this: wherever gods woke, ordinary people paid. So he had given his life to the Choir of the Silent, not out of love for its doctrine but out of respect for its purpose. Silence. Stillness. A world where no child ever again watched the sky catch fire over a question of worship.

And yet.

Three days ago a captured resonant—a ferryman's daughter, sixteen, whose prayers had started answering themselves—had looked at him with terror and pity in equal measure and said: *You think you hate the gods. But you hate being small. There's a difference, Inquisitor.*

He had ordered her cell sealed and thought about it for a long time afterward.

"Lamp party ashore," Cassel said. "Twelve men."

"Send eight. Keep four on the boat." Merek watched the old man lower his lamp, set it down on a stone, and begin—of all things—to sit, arranging his coat with the fussiness of a man settling in for a long wait. "And Cassel. If it's a decoy, I want the real prize found, not chased. Where would you run from this shore?"

Cassel considered the black outline of the ruins. "East channel. If it holds water."

"It doesn't hold water," Merek said. "It holds silt. Which is why nobody watches it." He lifted two fingers. "Second boat to the east channel mouth. Quietly. No lamps."

"And the old man?"

Merek hesitated. Something about the set of the man's shoulders—the patience of him, the terrible willingness—made Merek want to shout across the water: *Go home. Whatever they promised you, whatever you owe them, go home.* But the Choir did not employ men who shouted warnings, and besides, he knew better. Men like that didn't go home. They went where they'd decided to go, and no arithmetic of his would move them.

"Take him alive," Merek said. "Gently, if he allows it."

* * *

The eastern channel took the barge like a secret.

Silas crouched low among the cargo as the hull whispered through reeds that scraped the gunwales on both sides, close enough to touch, close enough to hide behind. Behind them, growing smaller, the west bank glowed with Hale's lone lamp and the torches of eight Choir soldiers wading ashore toward him.

Ives sat opposite her, hands folded, lips moving in a prayer so old the Choir would have burned her for it twice over. Tamsin knelt at the stern, one hand trailing the cold water, watching the lights recede.

"He'll make the wall," Silas said. It was meant to reassure herself more than anyone.

"He'll make the wall," Tamsin echoed.

Then the barge shuddered, and the reeds ahead lit with lantern-light.

Silas came up fast enough to tear something in her neck. At the channel mouth, sliding out of the darkness with no lamps until the last possible moment, was a second Choir boat, and standing in its bow with a hooked pole was a soldier whose face she couldn't see and didn't need to. The pole swung. Grappling irons followed it, biting into the barge's rail with a sound like teeth.

"*Down!*"

The world became noise. Crossbows from the boat—two bolts buried themselves in the cargo crates a hand's width from Ives's head. The smugglers returned fire wildly. Someone screamed. The barge swung sideways, momentum dying, and hands began hauling it toward the patrol boat, and Silas understood with total clarity: they were boarded. They were caught. Everything—the escape, Hale's sacrifice, Tamsin's freedom—ended here in a ditch of a channel because the Choir's Inquisitor was smarter than she was.

A Choir soldier vaulted the rail.

He landed three feet from Tamsin.

Later, Silas would try to reconstruct the decision and fail. There wasn't one. There was no weighing, no arithmetic, none of the careful accounting that had kept her alive for twelve years. There was only the sight of a masked man reaching for Tamsin's collar while Tamsin—foolish, brave, hopeless Tamsin—swung an oar at him instead of diving for cover.

The oathmark tore free of its wrappings.

Silas didn't unwrap it. It unwrapped itself, leather splitting along seams that hadn't existed a moment before, and light poured out—not the fitful glow of the last weeks but a column, a pillar, a thing with weight and temperature. The marsh reeds within arm's reach of her ignited and were not consumed, burning green-white without smoke. The grappling irons glowed cherry-red and the ropes holding them parted. The soldier reaching for Tamsin was lifted off his feet and set down—set down, gently, unconscious—in the shallows.

And every resonance engine on the river, four boats' worth, changed key at once.

The note they sang went from flat to ravenous.

"There!" someone on the patrol boat screamed. "*There*—the marked one—the Brightwater woman—it's *her*—"

Silas stood in the stern of the barge wrapped in a god's leftover light and knew, with the same total clarity as before, that she had just spent twelve years of careful invisibility in roughly four seconds.

Fine.

If she was going to be visible, she would be *visible*.

She raised her hand and pushed. Not at the soldiers—at the boat. The patrol vessel heeled away as if shoved by a giant's palm, shipping water, oars tangling, the grappling lines snapping one after another like harp strings breaking. The barge lurched free. The eastern channel opened ahead of them, black and empty, and Hale's steersman needed no telling; the hull surged forward through the reeds while behind them the Choir boat wallowed and its soldiers fired bolts that fell short, then shorter, then not at all.

Tamsin grabbed her arm—her bare, blazing arm—and didn't flinch. "Your wrappings."

"Gone."

"Does it hurt?"

Silas looked down at the light coming off her own skin. It didn't hurt. That was the strange part. It felt like being *remembered*.

"It will," she said. "Get down before another boat finds us."

* * *

Merek stood at the rail of his boat and watched the light recede up the eastern channel, and for a long moment he could not speak.

He had seen divinity before. Once, as a boy, from a hilltop, during the last flare of the wars: a saint-king's army wreathed in borrowed radiance, beautiful and distant, right before it burned his town's name off the map. He had spent thirty years building a life on the certainty that such light belonged far away or nowhere.

Now it had been close enough to warm his face. And the woman wearing it had knocked his boat aside without killing a single man. Eight of his soldiers were in the shallows, coughing, alive. The sentry nearest the marked woman had been set down like a sleeping child.

"That was her," Cassel said hoarsely. "That was the Brightwater woman. We could have had her. One volley—"

"One volley against whatever that was?" Merek shook his head slowly. "No."

He turned aft, where his men were bringing up the other prisoner—the old man from the west bank, wrists bound, coat torn, one eye swelling shut where a soldier's enthusiasm had outrun the Inquisitor's orders. Brannoc Hale surveyed the boat, the soldiers, the receding channel, with the air of a man checking figures on a ledger and finding them mostly acceptable.

"You knew," Merek said to him.

"I hoped," Hale said. His voice was thickening—he'd taken a blow to the throat—but the insolence underneath it was intact. "Hoping's cheaper than knowing."

"You sat under a lamp to draw my men while a god-touched woman carried everyone you love past my boat. You gambled your life on her light." Merek studied him. "Why? What does she pay you?"

Hale laughed, and it cost him—the laugh turned into a cough—but he finished it anyway. "Pay me. Boy, do you know what it's like, watching folk kneel to nothing for thirty years? Watching them pray into a hole in the world and call it piety?" He leaned closer, bound hands and all. "That woman makes the hole sing. You can lock me in whatever cellar you like. You'll never get me to call that a crime."

Merek looked at him for a long time.

Then he said, quietly, to Cassel: "Put him in the hold. Separate cell. He doesn't speak to anyone but me." And to himself, watching the dark channel where the light had gone: *Sixteen years old, on a hilltop, and the light was beautiful even then. That was the worst part. It was always beautiful first.*

The engines sang their hungry note across the water, hunting, and for the first time in his career Dain Merek was not entirely sure which side of the song he stood on.

* * *

They ran the silted channel until dawn, then hid the barge in a dead-cut oxbow under cut brush, and there Silas finally let herself stop.

Her arm had stopped glowing an hour after the fight. What remained was worse: the mark lay quiet again, but different now, like a door that had been kicked open once and would never quite shut. She could feel the river behind it. She could feel the Choir's engines behind *that*, faint and constant, searching.

Tamsin sat down beside her without asking, and put a blanket around both their shoulders, and said nothing for a long time.

"When they take Hale's release terms," Silas said finally—because she had been doing arithmetic of her own, all night, and the sum kept coming out the same way—"when they offer to trade him for me. I'm going to take it."

Tamsin went very still.

"Don't," she said.

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "I've seen how this ends, Silas. I told you—I *told* you what I saw at the altar. Don't you dare walk into it with your eyes open and call it arithmetic."

Silas looked at her—at the chapped lips and the sleepless eyes and the fury that was only fear wearing armor—and felt the truth rise in her like floodwater: that the choice had never really been between her god and her oath. It was between the light and this. The light had had twelve years to claim her. This had had one month, and it had already won.

"Hale is in a cell because of me," she said quietly. "Everyone on this barge is hunted because of me. I'm done buying safety with other people's blood."

"Then we get him back," Tamsin said. "All of us. Together. That's what a company *is*, Silas—you of all people should know."

And Silas, who had watched her last company die around a silent altar, who had sworn never to belong to anything that could be taken from her, heard herself say:

"Mother Ives knows a rite. For splitting what's in me. If it works, no one death can end this—one bearer becomes many, and no one person can free Veyra or doom her." She paused. "No one's survived it in three hundred years."

Tamsin stared at her. Then, horribly, she laughed—a small wet disbelieving sound. "That's your alternative? Gamble all our lives on a heresy?"

"It's the only option where I don't lose you."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere across the marsh, faint as memory, the resonance engines were still singing—and Silas realized the note had changed again, subtly, and that she could feel it through her bare arm like a finger tracing a map.

"They've stopped searching the river," she said slowly. "They're pulling back toward the city."

"Why?"

Silas closed her eyes and listened to the door in her arm, and what came through it was not Veyra's voice, not yet, but something older and colder beneath it—impatient, vast, and waking.

"Because they don't need to find me anymore," she said. "They need the altar. And something under it is starting to push."
