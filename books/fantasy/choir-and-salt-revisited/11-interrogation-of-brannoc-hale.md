## 11. Interrogation of Brannoc Hale

They took his boots first.

Brannoc Hale decided, as they marched him barefoot down the white stairs of the Choir's cathedral-prison, that this was deliberate. Not cruelty for its own sake—though there was plenty of that in Correnholt these days—but arithmetic. A man without boots was a man being prepared for a long stay. A man with boots might still believe he was leaving.

Hale had run cargo on the Mirrow for forty years, and he knew when someone was reading him. So he read them back. Six escorts, not four—they'd expected trouble. Spears with the points ground blunt, which meant they wanted him talking, not bleeding. And at the bottom of the stairs, waiting by a door of black iron, the Inquisitor himself, Dain Merek, holding a clay cup of something steaming like he'd brought it as a gift.

"Master Hale." Merek handed over the cup. It was broth, salted, still hot. "You've been on a boat for two days. Eat."

Hale looked at the broth, then at the Inquisitor's face—a plain face, tired around the eyes, the kind of face you'd trust to sell you a horse honestly and then rob you at the crossroads. He took the cup. If it was drugged, it was drugged well enough that refusing would only insult them both. He drank. It was good broth. That annoyed him more than anything else so far.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Hale said. "You know my name."

"I know everything about you," Merek said. "Brannoc Hale, licensed lighterman, unlicensed relic-runner during the Purification, pardoned under the Concord, widower, grandfather of three. Your granddaughter Sera had a fever last month. I'm told she's recovered."

The broth turned to lead in Hale's stomach. He kept his face still, the way you keep a boat steady in chop—not by fighting the water, but by giving it nothing to push against.

"Children get fevers," he said.

"They do." Merek opened the black door. "Come in. Sit. This doesn't have to be unpleasant. Unpleasantness is a tool I keep for people who make it necessary."

* * *

The room was nothing like Hale expected. No chains bolted to walls, no iron chairs, no drains in the floor—the things the old stories promised. Instead: a table, two chairs, a lamp of ordinary oil, a pitcher of water, two cups. The room of a man who wanted witnesses to be able to say, later, that no harm was done.

Merek sat across from him and folded his hands like a merchant closing ledgers.

"A barge was stopped above the Weir two nights ago," Merek said. "Your barge. Registered to carry salt. Instead we found—" he consulted a paper he did not need to consult, "—wardstones from the old shrine at Dell's Landing. Nine of them. Resonant artifacts, banned under the Concord. Your manifest said firewood."

"Firewood's dear this time of year," Hale said.

"Inquisitor Hale—"

"Brannoc."

"Master Hale." Something flickered at the corner of Merek's mouth—not quite amusement, but a cousin of it. "Nine wardstones is smuggling. Smuggling is a hanging offense, suspended only because the Choir has larger concerns. Those concerns concern me, and you are going to help me with them, because the alternative is that your grandchildren grow up visiting their grandfather through a grate. Do you understand the shape of our conversation?"

"I understand you've practiced it," Hale said. "How many times this week?"

That got him a longer look. Merek leaned back.

"Here is what interests me," Merek said. "Wardstones are dead stone unless someone wakes them. They were warm when we took them, Master Hale. Warm, and singing, faintly, in a voice my resonance-readers identified as—" he paused, and Hale watched him decide to say it plainly, which told Hale the man was more dangerous than his plainness suggested, "—Veyra. The Brightwater god. The Silent Lady. The one that died twelve years ago at a place called the Saltfen, along with every mercenary who carried her mark." He let that sit. "Except, apparently, one."

Hale drank water. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Forty years of river work taught you that panic is a luxury you purchase after the storm, never during.

"I'm a salt-hauler," he said. "Stones sing if you stack them wrong. Ask any mason."

"Ask any mason," Merek repeated softly. He reached into his coat and set something on the table between them: a strip of leather, cracked and salt-stained, buckled for a forearm. Hale recognized it the way you recognize your own front door. Silas Brightwater had worn that wrap every day for twelve years, and twice she'd left it aboard his barge and he'd kept it safe both times without ever once asking why.

"We found it in a cabin you scrubbed with lye," Merek said. "Lye, Master Hale. On a salt barge, where everything smells of salt anyway. You went to a great deal of trouble to erase a woman from your boat. Which means she existed. Which means she mattered." He tapped the wrap. "This belonged to a marked survivor of the Saltfen company. Where is she?"

"She isn't," Hale said.

"Isn't where?"

"Isn't. Isn't anything. That's a dead woman's armband, Inquisitor. I haul relics sometimes for folk who like old things. Old things come off dead people. That's the whole trade."

Merek studied him for a long moment. Then, quietly, without heat: "I lost my wife and son in the Godswar. Third year, the siege of Anselm's Ford. A god walked past the walls and everything inside them caught fire. Not attacked. Walked past. Like weather." He said it the way other men recite an inventory. "I don't hunt the faithful out of hatred, Master Hale. I hunt them because every prayer that gets answered is a fuse, and fuses end in places like Anselm's Ford. Help me put this one out and I will personally see your pardon written in gold ink. Stand in my way, and I will treat you as a man carrying flame through a dry city. Which is exactly what you're doing."

"That's a fine speech," Hale said. "You give it often?"

"Every time." Merek gathered the leather wrap, rose, and signaled the door. "We'll continue tomorrow. Think about your granddaughter's fever, and how quickly children sicken again when their families fall out of the Choir's protection."

At the threshold Hale stopped, because stubbornness was the only cargo he'd never once lost overboard.

"One thing, Inquisitor. Free advice, no charge." He turned. "You said the stones were singing. Warm and singing. If your gods are all safely dead and buried, who do you suppose woke them up?" He let his grin show, gap-toothed and river-broad. "Because it wasn't me. I just hauled 'em."

He didn't see Merek's face as the door closed. He didn't need to. He'd been hauling dangerous freight his whole life, and he knew the sound a hold makes when something inside shifts against the lashings.

* * *

The cell was clean, warm, and had a window too high to reach, which was somehow worse than no window at all. Hale lay on the cot and counted what he actually owned: his nerve, his name, and roughly two days.

Two days before Merek tired of patience. Two days before the arithmetic of threats moved from grandchildren's fevers to harder goods. Two days, if Silas had any sense—and she had sense like a knife has edge, when she chose to use it—to get herself and the girl and Mother Ives and the rest of his poor doomed crew so deep into the undercity that even the Choir's resonance-engines couldn't hear them breathing.

Because that was the trick of stalling, Hale knew. You weren't buying freedom. You were buying distance. Every hour Merek spent on a stubborn old lighterman was an hour not spent shaking the piers, questioning the fish-wives, dragging the gray market lamp by lamp. Hale intended to be worth every hour. He'd spent thirty years lying to customs men in three cities; one tired Inquisitor, however reasonable, was going to earn every half-truth the hard way.

The trick, he'd decided, was to tell almost everything.

A lie was a single plank—you found it, you broke it, done. But a structure of truths arranged to point the wrong way, that was a bridge, and men would march halfway across a bad bridge before they thought to test it. So: yes, he ran relics. Yes, the wardstones were his. Yes, he knew a marked survivor—past tense, mind, a dead woman's leavings, grief worn as cover. Let Merek spend his days digging through grave-registers and pardon-rolls looking for a Brightwater mercenary officially listed among the Saltfen dead. Let him chase paperwork. Paperwork was slow, and slow was the whole religion of Brannoc Hale.

There came footsteps in the corridor near evening—a different tread, lighter, hesitant—and then the observation slot slid open and a young Choirsman's eyes appeared, no older than Tamsin, with the same starved look the faithful got when the faith stopped feeding them.

"Brought you supper," the boy whispered. "And—" a pause, a glance down the empty corridor, "—they're saying below decks you gave the Inquisitor nothing. Whole cell block's talking about it."

"Tell them an old man's got nothing worth taking," Hale said.

"They're also saying—" the boy hesitated again, and lowered his voice further, "—there was a raid tonight. Piers and the salt-market. Hundreds questioned. They found nobody. Nobody at all, and the resonometers just sang static, like the whole riverfront went deaf at once. Some are saying it's sorcery. Some are saying the Silent Lady's cursing her own hunters." His eyes were bright and frightened and alive. "Some of us are saying other things. Quietly. In the dormitories."

Hale sat up slowly. Somewhere above him, under the city's skin, his crew was doing exactly what he'd hoped—moving, hiding, being smoke. And somewhere behind that boy's frightened eyes, the Choir itself was beginning to rot from questions like damp gets into stone.

"Boy," Hale said gravely, "what's your name?"

"Osser."

"When you bring breakfast tomorrow, Osser, bring it slow. Take the long way. Give a tired old man time to miss the sound of the river." He lay back down and folded his hands on his chest, a lighterman at anchor. "I'll want to hear all about those dormitory conversations."

* * *

The second session came at midmorning, and Merek had changed.

Hale saw it at once—the way you see weather coming off the water before the shore feels it. The Inquisitor's patience had a crack in it now, hairline-thin, and everything he did was built carefully over the top of it. He brought no broth today. He brought a folder, thick with papers, and he laid it open like a surgeon laying out tools.

"Grave-register, Saltfen company, honor roll of the fallen," Merek said. "Forty-one names. Cross-referenced against the pardon rolls, the guild manifests, the harbor taxes for twelve years." He turned the folder around. One page, one name, circled in ink gone brown with handling. "Brightwater, Silas. Listed dead. Except a woman answering her description bought ointment for a burn at the Lantern Row apothecary nine years ago, and the apothecary remembers, because the coin she paid with was old Saltfen campaign scrip. Dead women spend strange money, Master Hale."

Hale looked at the name a long time. Twelve years of a young woman wrapping her arm in leather and keeping her voice low, and here it all was in a circle of ink.

"You've done a lot of work," he said.

"For nine years it went nowhere, because there was nothing to find. A survivor who wants to stay dead is easy to lose." Merek's voice stayed level, but Hale could hear the effort in it now, the cost of the levelness. "Then three weeks ago prayers started answering themselves across this city, and every ghost I ever filed away stood up in its grave. So here is where we are, plainly: I know she lives. I know she was on your barge. I know the resonance-engines registered a bearer-class surge at the Weir the night you were taken, powerful enough to blind a patrol flotilla—which my readers tell me requires either an artifact of the first order or a living oathmark at full burn." He leaned forward. "She's not hiding anymore, Master Hale. She can't. Every hour she uses that power to survive, she burns brighter, and every hour she burns brighter, the things beneath the Saltfen wake a little more. This stopped being about one smuggler and one apostate the moment her mark lit up. It is now about whether this city burns. So I ask you one last time, and I am begging you, man to man: where is Silas Brightwater?"

And there it was—the crack, wide open. Not zealotry. Fear. Hale had carried enough gunpowder to know the difference between a man guarding a flame and a man terrified of one, and Dain Merek was terrified, and terrified men with authority were the most honest men alive and the most dangerous.

Hale thought of Sera's fever breaking. Of Silas standing in his galley with salt on her sleeves, saying *I don't want your faith, Brannoc, I just need a river.* Of Tamsin's thin wrists and stubborn eyes.

"No," Hale said.

Merek closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, the Inquisitor was back, the crack papered over, and Hale understood he'd just watched the last of the man's mercy spend itself.

"Then we'll try other doors," Merek said, gathering his papers. "Your first mate is already in custody. Your daughter-in-law was questioned this morning. There's a defrocked priestess living in the undercity—we've known about her for years, an old heretic named Ives who used to preach about the transference of bonds. Curious thing, Master Hale: your crew vanished off the face of the river, and within a day, someone was seen at Ives's door. Someone carrying supplies. Someone who knew exactly which drain to disappear down." He stood. "You've been stalling beautifully. I want you to know that I know. But every door I open costs me hours I don't have, and eventually I will stop opening doors politely. Think on it."

The door shut. The lock turned. Hale sat very still on the cot, listening to his own heart.

*Ives.* They had the name. Maybe not the door yet, not the face, not the drain—but the name, and Merek was a man who followed names like blood in water.

Hale lay back and stared at the ceiling, at the high window's thin bar of light, and did the only mathematics left to an ordinary man in a stone box: how much time he'd bought, and whether it would be enough, and what in the name of every silent god a defrocked priestess could possibly teach a hunted woman that the Choir would consider worth killing a city for.

Above him, faint through forty feet of stone, a bell began to toll—and then, impossibly, wavered, split, and answered itself.
