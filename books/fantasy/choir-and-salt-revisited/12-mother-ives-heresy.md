## 12. Mother Ives Heresy

The undercity of Correnholt smelled of tallow, river rot, and candle smoke, and Silas had never been so glad to breathe anything in her life.

Mother Ives's chapel was a cellar beneath a shuttered dyeworks, its ceiling low enough that Hale's tallest crewman, Osk, had to walk with his head bowed like a penitent. The pews were mismatched crates. The altar was a plank laid across two barrels, and on it sat the only holy thing in the room: a chipped ceramic bowl that Ives claimed had once held the tears of a saint, and which everyone else suspected had once held onions.

There were nine of them crammed into the space. Hale's people—Osk, the Ferrow twins, old Marta who could gut a man with a fish hook and had, once, in Silas's presence, without apology. Tamsin sat close enough to Silas that their shoulders touched, and did not move away. And Mother Ives stood at her barrel-altar with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms bare, showing them the faded scar tissue where her own oathmark had been cut out thirty years ago.

"You want to know how to unmake a god-bond," Ives said. "That's what you came down those stairs to ask, even if none of you has said it yet. So let me tell you first what I know, and then let me tell you what nobody wants to hear."

Silas kept her arm still. The mark burned under its leather wrap anyway; it had burned for three days now, ever since the Saltfen, ever since Veyra's voice had come up through the stone like water through a cracked dam. *Bearer.* The word was still lodged in her chest. *A bearer's spark, freely given.*

She'd told no one but Tamsin, and she regretted it every time she caught Tamsin watching her with those too-careful acolyte eyes.

"Speak," Silas said.

Ives picked up a stub of candle and turned it in her fingers. "Before the god-wars, there were thousands of bearers across the Nine Valleys. Mercenary companies like yours, farming villages with their own little harvest gods, city temples with a dozen sworn souls apiece. And there was a problem the histories don't like to talk about: gods die. Gods get killed. And when a god dies, every bearer tied to her dies too—or worse."

"Worse how?" Marta asked.

"There are records." Ives set the candle down. "Bearers who outlived their gods as hollow things. Walking, eating, speaking, and nothing home behind the eyes. The Church called them the Empty Blessed and put them down like dogs, and they were right to. A spark with nowhere to go doesn't just fade. It festers."

Silas felt Tamsin go very still beside her.

"So the old faithful developed a rite," Ives went on. "The Rite of Splitting. If a god is dying—if the bond must be severed before the god drags her bearers down with her—the spark can be divided. Split among many hands instead of one. Diluted past the point where any single death or any single failure can do harm. It was mercy work. Priests performed it in plague camps so that dying soldiers wouldn't take their whole companies into the dark."

"And the cost," Silas said. Her voice came out flat. She made it flat on purpose. "You're building up to the cost."

"The rite requires the bearer to hold the spark open while it leaves her." Ives met her eyes. "Every bearer who performed it in the last three hundred years died on the altar. The last recorded success was in the reign of Queen Alenna the Second, and even that one—" she paused, and something moved behind her face, some old grief worn smooth by time, "—even that one took four days to die. She spent them dictating letters. Very moving. Utterly useless to her."

The cellar was quiet. Somewhere above them, through the floorboards, Correnholt went about its evening—carts, gulls, a drunk singing something about a girl from Vessany. The ordinary world, going on, oblivious.

"That's not an option," Tamsin said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't spoken much since the Saltfen; she'd gone quiet and watchful the way new soldiers did, cataloguing dangers. Now her voice cracked out of the silence like a whip.

"You're not doing it. That's not—we didn't come here for that. We came here for another way."

"Tamsin." Silas reached for her wrist.

"No." Tamsin pulled free and stood, and Silas saw the acolyte in her suddenly, the trained spine, the trained voice, the girl who had stood in a Choir classroom at nine years old and recited the catechism of the Silent until her knees bled. "Tell them the other part, Mother. Tell them why the splitting works. Tell them what happens to the spark after."

Ives's expression gentled. "The spark doesn't vanish. It distributes. In theory, if the rite were performed with living co-bearers—people willing to receive shares of the divine fragment—then no single person would carry enough to anchor Veyra or to free her. No single death would release anything. The god would remain... diluted. Sleeping. Harmless."

"In theory," Osk repeated.

"In theory," Ives agreed. "Because no one has attempted it with co-bearers in three centuries, and the historical record of co-bearing is—" she smiled thinly, "—not encouraging. Divine sparks burn. They burn out weak vessels. They burn out strong ones. They burn out anyone who isn't built to hold them, and no one is built to hold them, because mortals aren't meant to carry divinity any more than clay jars are meant to carry the sea."

"So we'd be asking five people—" Silas counted, hating herself for counting, "—five, maybe six people to hold pieces of a god in bodies that might cook from the inside."

"Yes," Ives said simply. "And I would be lying to you, Silas Brightwater, if I told you the odds were better than terrible."

* * *

They argued until the candles burned down to puddles.

Marta wanted to run. Take the barge south past the toll-locks, disappear into the marsh country where the Choir's writ didn't reach. "You can outrun a god," she said. "Gods don't have legs."

"The mark does," Silas said, and pulled back her sleeve, and let them all see it glowing.

It was worse than before. Three days since the Saltfen, and the oathmark had spread—a thin bright filigree now climbing past her elbow, branching like frost across her skin, each branch a hairline of gold light. When she flexed her hand, the light pulsed. She could feel it pulling, faint and constant, like a hook set behind her sternum. Veyra, below the ruins of the Saltfen, feeling along the thread of her own imprisoned power toward the one mortal soul still holding a piece of it.

"It's growing," Silas said. "Every day it climbs a little further. Ives thinks when it reaches my heart, the choice gets made whether I choose or not."

"Then we cut it out," said Ferrow—one of the twins; Silas had never learned which. "Ives cut out her own mark. Cut hers."

"I tried," Ives said quietly. "The night I left the Choir. I got as far as the knife." She held up her forearm again, the old surgical scars stark white against the cellar dimness. "A bond isn't in the skin, child. It's in the soul. Cutting the skin only teaches the spark that you're willing to bleed. Mine stayed with me for twenty more years, quiet, until the day the gods went silent and it finally starved. That's the mercy your generation never had to learn about: most bonds die with their gods. Yours didn't, because yours isn't dead. Yours is *down there*, and it's awake, and it knows your name."

Tamsin hadn't sat back down. She stood with her arms crossed tight, and when Silas looked at her, she looked away.

Which was answer enough.

* * *

Later—much later, when the others had curled up on pallets among the crates and the chapel had gone dark except for one candle—Ives found Silas sitting alone at the barrel-altar, turning the chipped bowl in her hands.

"Ask me," Ives said, sitting down across from her.

"Why did you really leave the Choir?"

Ives laughed, short and dry. "Not the question I expected. But fair." She was quiet a moment. "I was a resonance-tender. Before the engines, before the Silence. Back when the Choir still admitted the gods existed, and built machines to listen for them. My order served a small god. A river goddess, name of Senn. Nothing grand. Fishermen's offerings, blessings for safe crossing. I was her bearer for eleven years."

"What happened?"

"The war came to our valley. Both armies wanted the ford. Senn refused to fight—she was a river, not a sword—and the other side's god didn't care. He broke her on the stones upstream and her body backed up the water and drowned six villages." Ives's voice never changed register. It was the flattest thing Silas had ever heard. "Four thousand people. I carried nineteen of them out myself. On the twentieth day I walked into the Cathedral of the Silent, put my hands on their resonance engine, and swore that if the gods ever started coming back, I would be ready. Not to welcome them. To *manage* them."

"And the Rite of Splitting is how you manage this one."

"The Rite of Splitting is the only tool I have." Ives leaned forward. "Listen to me, mercenary. You think your choice is between freeing Veyra and killing her. It isn't. There's a third door, and it's ugly, and I won't pretend otherwise. But consider what's behind the other doors. Door one: you give your spark freely, fully, at that altar. Veyra rises. Maybe she's grateful. Maybe she's reasonable. Or maybe she wakes up after twelve years in the dark with the taste of betrayal in her mouth and decides the world that caged her owes her a war. You knew her as a soldier's god. Ask yourself what twelve years of solitary confinement does to a soldier."

Silas's stomach turned over. She had heard Veyra's voice at the Saltfen—weak, cracked, furious, *bearer, bearer, free me*—and she had told herself the fury was pain. She had not let herself ask what the fury was aimed at.

"Door two," Ives continued. "You do nothing. The mark climbs. It reaches your heart. And then either the bond takes you—makes you a doorway whether you consent or not—or the Choir finds you first, and Merek puts you on an engine, and they siphon the spark out of you slowly, in a cage, and use it to build themselves a weapon. You've seen what their machines do to resonants. You've seen the faces."

She had. The memory of the Choir's holding-house—the resonants strapped to copper frames, mouths open, eyes rolled back, being *drained*—rose in her throat like bile.

"And door three," Silas said.

"Door three, we split you. We split the spark so fine that Veyra stays asleep forever, harmless, a rumor, a story. And we do it with volunteers, so that no one's death—not yours, not anyone's—can ever be the key that opens her prison. The war ends before it starts. Nobody rises. Nobody burns." Ives paused. "And the odds are that it kills most of the people brave enough to try."

Silas looked down at the bowl in her hands. Saint's tears. Onion water. Thirty years of a defrocked priest pretending the holy still lived in ordinary things.

"How many volunteers?" she asked. "How many do you need?"

"For a stable split? Seven, ideally. The number has to divide clean. Fewer vessels means deeper shares per vessel means more burning." Ives's voice softened. "Silas. Look at the room you're standing in. Count the people sleeping in it. Then tell me you can ask this of them with a clear conscience."

Silas counted anyway. Osk. Marta. The twins. Hale, wherever he was—still in a Choir cell, still stalling Merek with half-truths and a smuggler's gift for arithmetic. Tamsin. Herself. Ives, if Ives chose to stand inside the circle, and Silas knew—I knew it the way you know a load-bearing wall—that she would.

Seven.

Seven people she loved, or was learning to, or owed.

"I can't," she said.

"No," Ives agreed. "You can't. That's what love does to leadership. It makes cowards of us exactly when courage matters most." The old priestess rose, joints cracking, and put a hand briefly on Silas's head like a benediction. "So don't decide tonight. Decide tomorrow. And when you decide, remember: every door is monstrous. Your job is not to find the clean option. Your job is to pick the monster that leaves the most people alive."

* * *

Tamsin found her an hour before dawn, up on the loading dock above the dyeworks, where the gray light was starting to show the river.

For a long time neither of them spoke. Below them, Correnholt woke: smoke from a hundred chimneys, fishing boats putting out with the tide, the bells of the Silent Choir ringing matins over a city that didn't know yet how close it had come to burning.

"Ives told me the numbers," Tamsin said finally. "Seven vessels. Terrible odds."

"She had no right."

"She had every right. It's our lives she's spending, not yours." Tamsin came to stand beside her, close, their elbows touching on the rail. "You've been carrying this alone since the Saltfen. The terms. The price. You told me a piece of it and then you shut the door and I've watched you walk around with it for three days like a knife you're afraid to show anyone."

"If I ask them—"

"You're afraid they'll say yes." Tamsin's voice was gentle and merciless at once, the way it always was. "That's the real thing. You could live with them saying no. What's killing you is knowing they'll say yes. Marta will say yes. Osk will say yes. The twins will probably argue over who goes first. Because that's what this family does, Silas. We keep choosing each other. And you can't stand it, because you've spent twelve years believing that everything you love dies when you turn your back."

Silas said nothing. The mark pulsed under its wrap, patient as a tide.

"So here's what I'm going to do," Tamsin said. "I'm going to go downstairs, and I'm going to tell them. All of it. The altar, the price, the rite, the odds. And then I'm going to volunteer first, and you are going to have to watch me do it, and you are going to hate every second, and afterward we are going to be even—you spied on me, I outvoted you—and then we are going to save Brannoc Hale, because he's sitting in a Choir cell buying us time with nothing but nerve, and we are not leaving him there."

Silas turned to look at her. Gray dawn light, river wind, the runaway acolyte with the stubborn chin and the steady eyes.

"When did you become the dangerous one?" Silas asked.

"The day I prayed to Veyra by name and got an answer." Tamsin kissed her—brief, fierce, tasting of salt and candle smoke—and then stepped back toward the stairs. "Come down when you're done being a coward, Brightwater. The family's waiting."

Silas stood alone on the dock a moment longer, watching the boats go out.

Then the mark flared—sudden, searing, brighter than it had ever been—and beneath the river-smell and the gull-cries and the distant bells, she felt it again, threading up through stone and water and years:

*Bearer. They are coming for you. Choose before they choose for you.*

Down the street, at the mouth of the undercity stairs, lanterns were lighting. Many lanterns. Moving in formation.

The Choir had found the dyeworks.
