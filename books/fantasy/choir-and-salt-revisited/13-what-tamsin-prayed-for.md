## 13. What Tamsin Prayed For

Tamsin had been carrying the secret for eleven days.

She knew because she counted. Every morning since the Saltfen, she woke in the narrow bunk aboard Hale's barge and lay still with her eyes open, listening to Silas breathe two feet away, and asked herself the same question: *today?* And every morning the answer came back the same: *not yet.* Not on the river, with patrol boats burning lanterns along the banks. Not at the ruins, with the altar stone humming under their palms. Not during the flight back downriver, not through the fever that swept the crew after three nights of rain, not while Mother Ives was teaching them the geometry of a rite that had killed everyone who ever attempted it.

There was always a reason. There would always be a reason. That was how secrets survived—not by being hidden well, but by never finding a good moment to stop hiding.

But tonight the barge was moored in the undercity, tied off beneath the old tannery arches where the water ran slow and black and nobody official came. Hale was ashore buying rope. Ives was asleep with her prayer book open on her chest. The splitting rite was set for tomorrow night, and tomorrow night, if it worked, no single one of them could free Veyra or doom her—and if it failed, they'd all be dead anyway, which meant there was no version of tomorrow worth keeping a secret for.

So tonight.

Tamsin climbed out of her bunk without waking Silas, took the oil lamp, and went up to the deck. The rain had stopped an hour ago. The sky over Correnholt's rooftops was the color of wet slate, and somewhere above them, distantly, the Choir's bells were ringing the hour—nine strokes, muffled by distance into something almost gentle. Almost like a memory of what bells were supposed to mean.

She sat on the cargo hatch and waited. She didn't have to wait long. Silas slept badly these days; Tamsin had learned the rhythm of it, the way she'd once learned the rhythm of the Choir's night offices—the turn, the pause, the half-waking murmur. Eleven minutes later the hold ladder creaked and Silas came up into the lamplight, hair unbound, leather wraps loose around her forearm where the mark lived.

"You're watching me sleep," Silas said. "That's either very sweet or very grim."

"Both." Tamsin turned the lamp wick up a little. "Sit down."

Silas sat. She looked tired in a way that sleep didn't touch anymore—a tiredness that had moved in twelve years ago and paid rent ever since. Tamsin loved her face for exactly that. Loved the gray at her temple that hadn't been there in the spring. Loved the scar through her eyebrow and the way she rubbed her thumb across her knuckles when she was thinking, a habit from sword-work that had outlived any sword.

"I need to tell you something," Tamsin said. "And I need you to hear all of it before you say anything. Because if you interrupt me halfway through, you're going to do something reckless, and we don't have time for reckless."

Silas's mouth twitched. "You've met me."

"I've met you," Tamsin agreed. "That's why I'm asking."

* * *

She started with the part Silas already suspected, because starting anywhere else would feel like lying by arrangement.

"I wasn't running when you found me on your barge."

Silas said nothing. Her thumb stopped moving across her knuckles.

"The Choir didn't lose me. They threw me out. Formally. With paperwork." Tamsin heard herself say it and flinched at how small it sounded, how administrative. A girl's whole faith, reduced to a line in a ledger. "There was a hearing. Three canons, a stenographer, a room that smelled of ink and cold stone. They asked me one question: did I pray to Veyra. I said yes. They asked if I understood what I'd done. I said yes. And then they took my habit, my name in the rolls, my seat in the choir loft—all of it—and they walked me out the west door before dawn so the novices wouldn't see."

"Why?" Silas said quietly. "The Choir prays to silence itself. To the absence. Praying to a specific god—"

"—is worse than praying to any of them," Tamsin finished. "I know. I argued it at the hearing. I said: we sing to the empty chair every day, Canon, what difference does it make whose chair we pretend is empty? And Canon Verel looked at me with such patience, such terrible kind patience, and he said: because the empty chair keeps the peace. A named god has a face. A face has enemies. An enemy has a war. And then he signed the paper."

Tamsin's hands were shaking. She put them flat on her knees and kept talking, because stopping now would be the same as never starting.

"But here's the thing I couldn't explain to them, and couldn't explain to you, and have barely been able to explain to myself. I prayed to Veyra because I heard her first. Before I ever saw your mark. Before Correnholt, before the market, before any of it—I was sixteen, in the choir loft, mid-psalm, and the psalm is about the silence of heaven, and in the middle of it something answered. Not words. Not at first. A pressure. Like a hand laid flat against the inside of my ribs. And underneath the pressure, a name. *Veyra.* Just the name, once, the way you'd hear someone knock on a door from the far side."

Silas leaned forward. "When?"

"Four years ago." Tamsin watched her face change as the arithmetic landed. Four years ago was eight years after the Saltfen. Eight years after the voice cut out mid-prayer and a company of soldiers died believing they'd been abandoned. "It took me two more years to understand what I'd heard. Two years of digging through the restricted archives, reading accounts of the Saltfen campaign, cross-referencing company rosters against casualty lists. I wasn't looking for a person. I was looking for a door. Something the name could be knocking *on*."

"And you found me."

"I found a roster," Tamsin said. "Brightwater, S. Oathbearer, First Company. Status: survivor. And then I found a tax record, and a charm-seller's license, and a street address in Correnholt, and I did the thing you're never supposed to do with a vision, which is go find it." She laughed, short and unhappy. "The canons were right about one thing. Named gods have faces. Mine just happened to be yours."

* * *

Silas was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way people speak when they're holding something breakable.

"The night we met. On the barge. You had a knife at my throat and you said—" she stopped, and Tamsin watched her reconstruct it, word by word, "—you said you'd heard her name in a vision. I thought you meant recently. I thought you meant you'd run because the Choir found out you were a resonant, same as me. Same trouble, different door."

"No."

"How long did you know about me? Before the barge?"

"A year." Tamsin said it and felt the weight of it settle, finally, outside her body. "I came to Correnholt eight months before I ever let you see me. I bought charms from you twice. You sold me a luck-knot for a copper and told me the knot was harmless but the blessing was real, and you laughed after, like you'd caught yourself saying too much. I stood on the far side of the market with my hood up and my heart going like a drum and I thought: *she doesn't remember what she is. She thinks she's selling ash.*"

"I remember that sale," Silas said slowly. "Gray cloak. Kept your sleeve pulled down."

"You noticed the sleeve. You didn't notice me. Which—" Tamsin shook her head. "Which was the point. I was good at not being noticed. Four years of training."

"So the vision. The one that gave you her name. What else was in it?"

And this was the place. This was the wall she'd been standing in front of for eleven days, and there was no way around it, only through, and everything on the other side of it was going to hurt Silas, and Tamsin had decided—tonight, on the hatch, under the wet slate sky—that hurting her now was kinder than letting her walk into it blind tomorrow.

"I'm going to tell you," Tamsin said, "and then I'm going to tell you what I did about it, and the second part matters more. Okay?"

"Okay."

* * *

"It wasn't one vision. It was one vision, repeated. That's how Veyra comes to me—not sentences, not pictures exactly. More like... weather. A pressure that means *yes*, a pressure that means *no*, and once, only once, a full thing, beginning to end, the way a storm arrives complete even though you watch it arrive piece by piece."

"What was the storm?"

Tamsin made herself look at Silas's eyes while she said it. Not away. Away would be mercy, and mercy right now would be a lie.

"There's an altar. Black stone, salt-crusted, standing in a drowned field. There's a light coming up out of the ground beneath it—gold-white, enormous, angry, wounded. And there's a woman walking into the light. Gray at the temples. Leather wraps on her arm, and the wraps are glowing through, burning off her like paper. She walks in and the light takes her and the light goes out. All of it. At once. Like a candle pinched between fingers." She paused. "You're the woman. You've been the woman since the first time I saw it. I didn't know your name yet, but I knew your hands—I'd watched them tie that luck-knot. I knew your laugh. I knew the way you stand with your weight on your left heel."

Silas's face didn't move. That was the worst part, Tamsin thought afterward. Not tears, not anger—stillness. The stillness of a soldier receiving a report she already, somewhere below the surface, believed.

"When?" Silas said.

"At the altar. At the Saltfen. In the rising light." Tamsin swallowed. "Two days ago you touched that stone and heard her, and she named her price, and you didn't tell us what it was. You hid it. And I understood why you hid it, and I understood what it was, because I've known since I was twenty years old. A bearer's spark, freely given. Your spark. All of it. You walk into her and she goes back to sleep and the world gets to keep its peace, and you—" her voice cracked and she forced it level, "—you get to be the candle."

Silence. The river lapped against the hull. Somewhere above, the last of the bell-strokes faded.

"You've known the ending," Silas said at last, "since before you knew me."

"Yes."

"And you came anyway. Bought a charm. Found the barge. Stayed."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And Tamsin almost laughed, because the honest answer was so much smaller and stranger than anything she'd rehearsed. "Because the vision didn't show me whether it was worth it. It showed me *that*. Only that. And I lay awake for a year deciding that if the ending was fixed—if she was really going to take you—then somebody should be there who loved you first. Somebody who chose you with their eyes open. Not fate. Not prophecy. A person." She looked down at her hands. "I couldn't save you. I knew that going in. But I could refuse to let it happen to you alone."

* * *

Silas got up. For a moment Tamsin thought she was leaving—that the confession had broken something, that the woman who'd spent twelve years surviving abandonment couldn't bear to be loved by someone who'd already buried her. Silas crossed the deck, lifted the lamp, and set it down beside Tamsin so the light fell full on her face.

Then she sat back down, closer, and took Tamsin's hands.

"Say the rest," Silas said. "You said there was a second part. What you did about it."

"I tried to change it." Tamsin's grip tightened on hers. "Every single day since the Saltfen, I've been trying to change it. Ives's rite—the splitting—that was my idea. Did you know that? I went to her before you did. I sat in her kitchen and told her about the vision and asked her if there was any way, any forbidden heretical way, to make sure the price couldn't be paid by one person. She told me about splitting the spark. She told me nobody survives it. I told her to teach it to us anyway." She shook her head. "Tomorrow night, when we attempt it, that's not leadership. That's not tactics. That's me, spending every favor I have, betting all of your lives, trying to make a liar out of my own god."

Silas exhaled. Long and slow, like a woman setting down something heavy.

"Tamsin. If the rite works—if the spark scatters—then no one can free her and no one can bind her. She stays caged. The war stays buried. We live."

"That's the theory."

"And if it fails?"

"Then I was wrong about everything," Tamsin said, "and the vision was always true, and I'll be standing next to you when it comes due. That's the whole of my plan. It's not clever. It's just—" she stopped, searching for the word, and found it, and it embarrassed her, "—it's just *staying*. Twelve years ago a god went silent and a company died of it, and you've been alone ever since, and I have watched you be alone, and I am telling you that whatever happens tomorrow, at the altar or before it, you are not doing it alone. That's my vow. I never took the Choir's vows properly anyway. I was always better at writing my own."

Silas bowed her head until her forehead touched Tamsin's joined hands. She stayed there. Tamsin felt her shoulders shake once, twice—not crying, or not only crying; something older than crying, some grief that had been swallowed for twelve years finally meeting air.

"You should have told me," Silas said into her hands. "At the barge. The knife. Any time in the last month."

"If I'd told you at the barge, you'd have put me off the boat."

"Yes," Silas said, without shame. "I would have."

"I know. That's why I didn't. And I'm not sorry." Tamsin pulled back enough to look at her. "I'm sorry for the hiding. I'm not sorry for the staying. Those are different sins and I'll answer for them separately."

Silas laughed—wet, cracked, real. "You sound like Ives."

"Somebody has to."

They stayed like that, foreheads together, while the river breathed under the hull and the city dripped its rain from ten thousand eaves. And Tamsin felt the thing she'd been afraid to feel since the market stall, since the luck-knot, since the first time she'd watched those hands move: allowed. Chosen back. Whatever the storm-vision held, it did not hold this. This—lamp-oil and river-smell and Silas Brightwater's callused hands around hers—this was the part the vision never showed, and therefore the part that was theirs.

Then Silas stiffened.

Not much. A fraction. But Tamsin had spent months learning her body's language, and she read it instantly: the small lock of the jaw, the sudden stillness of the breathing. She looked up. Silas was staring past her, toward the dark mouth of the tannery arch, where the water reflected nothing.

"Lamp out," Silas said softly. "Now."

Tamsin cupped the flame dead. In the black, far off across the undercity's channels, she heard it: oars. Muffled. Many. Moving in the disciplined rhythm she'd known since childhood—the stroke cadence of Choir boats.

"They found the barge," Silas whispered.

Tamsin reached in the dark and found her hand again, and held on.

"Not yet," she said. "We're not done."
