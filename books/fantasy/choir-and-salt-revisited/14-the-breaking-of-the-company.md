## 14. The Breaking of the Company

The Choir boats passed within thirty feet of the tannery arch and kept going, oars biting the dark in that patient, terrible rhythm. Nobody in the undercity spoke until the sound faded to nothing. Then Mother Ives lit her lamp again and said, "They know we're here. They're just deciding how much of this tunnel they want to flood."

"They're not going to flood it," Silas said. Her arm ached under its wraps. It had been aching for three days, ever since the rite at Ives's chapel had scattered the spark through five bodies and left her feeling like a bell struck too hard—still ringing, but cracked. "Merek doesn't waste men. He'll come down the channels one at a time and take us apart politely."

"Cheerful," said Wick.

"He's thorough," Silas said. "It's worse."

They had regrouped badly. The undercity safehouse was a drained cistern behind a collapsed dye-works, damp as a grave and twice as cold, and everyone in it looked like what they were: people running. Hale was gone—that was the wound they kept not looking at. Captured on the barge buying them their escape, four days now in whatever cells the Choir maintained beneath the cathedral. Four days of silence from a man who talked for a living.

Tamsin sat close enough to Silas that their shoulders touched, which she did without seeming to notice she did it, which was how Silas knew it wasn't accidental. Wick was sharpening a knife he'd sharpened yesterday. Osten and Merrit, Hale's two deckhands, played cards with no interest in the game. Ives mended a cassock she would never wear again in public.

The knock came at the third hour past midnight. Three taps, a pause, two more—the signal Osten had arranged with a sewer-rat named Fenn who sold information to anyone whose coin didn't smell of the Choir.

Fenn's coin smelled of the Choir tonight. But he came anyway, wet to his knees, and handed Silas a folded paper sealed with gray wax.

"Man in a plain coat gave me a whole mark to bring it to the woman with the wrapped arm," Fenn said. "Didn't even haggle. That scared me more than the coat did."

They waited until he'd gone before breaking the seal. Inside, one page. A fair hand, unhurried:

*Mercenary Brightwater. Brannoc Hale eats bread and drinks water and is treated civilly, and will continue to be so long as you are free, and will hang from the cathedral bridge at noon on Diesday if you still are. You have touched forty-one lives since the bells began answering. I have forty-one names. Surrender yourself at the Salt Gate at dusk on Diesday eve, unarmed, marked openly, and every name comes off my list—including his. Come armed, run, or send someone else, and I proceed as arithmetic requires. You know by now that I do not bluff. You also know, I think, that I do not enjoy this. — Inquisitor Dain Merek*

Silas read it twice. Then she read it aloud, because a thing like this didn't belong to one person, and because her voice might shake less than her hands.

When she finished, the cistern was very quiet. Water dripped somewhere. Wick stopped sharpening his knife.

* * *

"Well," said Osten, into the silence. "That's that, then. She goes."

"Wick first." Wick's voice came out flat. "You go through him like water through a net, Osten, and then what? He's got forty-one names because he's been doing his reading. He's got mine. He's got Merrit's. He's probably got Fenn's, the little traitor. She turns herself in and we all die a bit slower, that's all."

"She turns herself in and *Hale doesn't hang*," Osten said. "He's your captain same as mine."

"He's not my captain. He's my captain's grandfather." Wick stood, and the lamplight made his shadow climb the cistern wall like something drowning. "And I'm not feeding the woman I—" He caught himself. Looked at Tamsin. Didn't finish. "I'm not walking her to the gate myself, is what I'm saying."

"You think I haven't thought about it?" Silas said.

Everyone turned. She hadn't meant to speak yet. But the argument was going where arguments went, circling the drain of the obvious, and she was tired of being the subject instead of a voice.

"Merek's right about one thing—I know him a little now. He doesn't bluff and he doesn't enjoy it. Which means the offer is real, and it means the threat is real, and it means both of those things can be true while the deal is still a lie." She held up the letter. "Read it again. *Surrender yourself.* Not: surrender and stand trial. Not: surrender and be questioned. He wants me at the Salt Gate at dusk, alone. And then what? Forty-one names come off a list written in ink I've never seen. He could burn the list and take us all anyway. Or—" she felt the shape of it as she said it, the way you feel a stair in the dark, "—or he doesn't need me alive at all. Maybe what he needs is what I'm carrying. Maybe the Choir's engines eat sparks, and I'm a meal walking itself to the table."

"The resonance engines," Ives said quietly. "The girl told me what she saw in the cathedral undercroft. If they're siphoning the returning gods—if that's what the Choir has become—then yes. They'd want a bearer delivered intact." She set down her mending. "Delivered willing, even better. A spark given up freely burns brighter than one taken. Old doctrine. Very old. Heretical, now, but the engineers remember."

"So the deal's poison," Merrit said. He'd said maybe ten words all night. "Good. Then we run."

"Run where?" Wick demanded. "Name one place. Go on. The bells answer prayers in *Correnholt*, Merrit—they're answering in half the world by now if the rumors hold. There's no corner far enough. There never was. We should've put her off the barge at Kell's Reach."

"That's enough," Tamsin said.

It was the first time anyone had heard her raise her voice. It didn't get loud. It got *cold*, the acolyte's voice, the one drilled into children who sang hymns at dawn, and it stopped Wick mid-breath.

"She pulled your brother out of the river at Kell's Reach," Tamsin said. "You were too busy bleeding to notice. So we don't put anybody off barges. We're past that. What we are is stuck, and shouting won't unstitch us."

Wick sat down. His jaw worked. He didn't apologize, and Silas didn't need him to. Fear talked like that when it had nowhere else to go. She'd spent twelve years listening to her own fear make the same speech.

"Then fight," Osten said. "Four days to Diesday. We hit the cells, pull Hale out, and scatter before the city wakes."

"With eleven people and one defrocked priest against the entire Choir of the Silent," Ives said. "In their own house."

"Eleven people took the barge past three patrol boats," Osten said. "Eleven people broke into an Inquisitor's evidence vault."

"And lost Hale doing it," Silas said. "Every time we fight, the bill gets bigger. That's how Merek counts. He doesn't need to beat us. He needs to keep charging."

She looked around the cistern—at Osten's anger, Merrit's flat pragmatism, Wick staring at the floor like it owed him money, Ives folding her hands the way she did when she was praying to nothing, Tamsin watching Silas with those eyes that saw too much.

This was the moment, she understood. This was the thing the songs never mentioned because the songs were written by survivors of companies that never had to face it: the moment the company broke. Not in battle. Battles held you together the way storms hold a crew. It was the quiet after, the arithmetic after, the offer on the table—*your life for theirs, theirs for yours, choose*—that split people into factions over a fire. Every sworn band she'd ever served in had died this way or survived this way, and there was no third option, and the choosing was done in lamplight by tired people who loved each other.

Duty's oldest trap: making love into a ledger.

"Here's what I see," Silas said, and kept her voice level. "Option one, I take the deal. Best case, Merek keeps his word, forty-one people sleep easy, and whatever the engines do to me, I did it with my eyes open. Worst case, he takes my spark, uses it to fuel whatever god-cage the Choir's building, and then we've fed the wolf and it's still a wolf."

Nobody argued. Even Tamsin's face had gone carefully still.

"Option two, we run. Split up, scatter, hope the dark holds. Worst case, he hangs Hale on Diesday and starts working down the list, and we spend the rest of our lives watching doors. Best case—we're alive. Running. For good."

"Option three," Osten said.

"Option three, we fight, and I've already told you the price of fighting. It compounds."

"And option four," Tamsin said softly.

Silas looked at her.

"There's always an option four," Tamsin said. "You taught me that. At the Saltfen you told me Veyra's company survived the god-war's worst year because Brightwater found the ford nobody else saw. There's a ford here."

"I was telling you a story."

"You were telling me the truth and calling it a story so I wouldn't be frightened." Tamsin's voice gentled. "Find the ford."

Silas wanted to say there isn't one. She wanted it so badly that she recognized the wanting for what it was—not caution but exhaustion, twelve years of exhaustion, finally asking to be done. And underneath the wanting, faint as the mark under her wraps, something else. A thread. Frayed almost to nothing since the splitting rite, but there: the sense of a vast attention turned toward her from very far below, patient, wounded, waiting.

Veyra had refused to fight in the last war. That was why the other gods had buried her. That was the whole crime—a god who chose a third path.

"Nobody decides anything tonight," Silas said. "Osten, double the watch. Wick, get word to Fenn—I want everything he knows about the Salt Gate's approaches and I want it before dawn, and tell him if he sells us to Merek I will personally haunt him. Merrit, inventory everything we own and pretend we're going to use it. Mother Ives—"

"I know," Ives said. "I'll pray."

"To whom?"

"To anyone listening," she said. "These days, that's a long list."

* * *

The others slept in shifts. Tamsin didn't. Silas found her at the mouth of the cistern's outflow channel, sitting where the lamplight couldn't reach, watching the black water.

"He's going to hang Hale whether you come or not," Tamsin said, without turning. "You've worked that out."

"Yes."

"And the deal is poison."

"Yes."

"And you're going to take it anyway."

Silas sat down beside her. The stone was cold through her trousers. Somewhere above them, Correnholt slept its uneasy sleep, bells silent for once, as if the whole city were holding its breath along with her.

"I'm going to *consider* taking it," Silas said. "Out loud. Where everyone can hear. Because if I don't, Osten does something brave and stupid, and Wick follows him, and then I've buried this family twice."

"And then?"

"Then I write a letter."

Tamsin went very still beside her. Twelve years of reading mercenaries had taught Silas to read one runaway acolyte very well indeed, and she watched the understanding arrive in the set of her shoulders, the slow turn of her head.

"No," Tamsin said.

"Tamsin—"

"*No.* Whatever you've built in your head—the noble walk to the gate, the clean trade, your life for ours like coins on a table—it doesn't work. You said it yourself. He doesn't need you alive. He needs what's *in* you. You walk through that gate and you're not saving forty-one people, you're delivering the last piece of a god to the people building a cage for gods, and then there is nothing left in the world strong enough to break Hale out or burn those engines down. Your sacrifice buys nothing. It just spends you."

"It buys Hale's rope coming off his neck."

"It buys Hale's rope staying on until *our* necks," Tamsin said, and her voice cracked finally, the acolyte's frost shattering, and underneath it was just a girl from a small town who had prayed herself out of paradise. "You don't get to decide alone. That's the whole point of—of us. Of this. I didn't run across half of Correnholt with stolen visions in my head so you could hand yourself to an Inquisitor in a *letter*."

Silas reached out and took her hand. Tamsin let her. Her fingers were cold and they gripped back hard enough to hurt.

"If anything happens to me," Silas said quietly, "the spark threads go to you and the others. Ives confirmed it. The bond transfers. You'd feel her—Veyra—faintly, the way I do now. You'd know what she knows. You could find the ford I can't see."

"Don't." Tamsin's eyes were bright and furious in the dark. "Don't you dare give me your god as a consolation prize."

"I'm giving you a *chance*. There's a difference."

"There isn't," Tamsin said. "Not between people like us. That's what frightens me. You and I both grew up learning that love is a debt and somebody has to pay, and we will spend the rest of our lives outbidding each other's sacrifices unless one of us learns another way to live." She laughed, wetly, without humor. "Listen to me. Three months ago I was scrubbing cathedral floors. Now I'm lecturing a mercenary on theology and I mean every word."

Silas pulled her close. Tamsin came, stiff at first and then not, her forehead against Silas's shoulder, breathing unevenly.

"Four days," Tamsin said into the leather of Silas's jacket. "Tell me you have a plan. Lie if you have to."

"I have the start of one," Silas said.

It was even true. It had assembled itself in the dark of the last hour, ugly and lopsided and dangerous, and it required exactly one thing: that nobody else in the cistern know until it was too late to stop her. Because the ford nobody else saw only worked if nobody else saw it—not the enemy, and not the people who loved you either.

Above them, far off across the sleeping city, the cathedral bell tolled once—the change of the watch, the sound Merek's people moved by. Somewhere under that bell, Hale sat in the dark counting days. Somewhere above him, an Inquisitor sat with a list of forty-one names and a conscience he claimed not to enjoy using.

Silas kissed the top of Tamsin's head and said, "Sleep. I'll take the watch."

And when Tamsin had finally, grudgingly, gone—watching her the whole way back down the channel—Silas took out Merek's letter, read it one last time, and began composing her reply in her head.

*Dusk, Diesday eve, Salt Gate. Alone. Agreed.*

She had one condition to add, and it wasn't Hale's freedom.

It was the engine room.
