## 15. Night of Small Treasons

The letter took Silas three tries to write.

The first attempt she burned because it sounded like an apology, and Merek would smell apology the way a shark smelled blood. The second she burned because her hand shook so badly the ink looked like something written by a dying woman, and she refused to give him even that satisfaction on paper. The third she wrote in the cold hours before dawn, sitting on the upturned crate that served as her desk in Mother Ives's undercity chapel, with Tamsin asleep on the pallet behind her and the candle turned low so its light wouldn't touch her face.

*Inquisitor Merek. You offered terms. I accept them.*

That was all. Four words of substance. She read it back six times, looking for any way to make it mean less than it did, and found none. A surrender was a surrender no matter how you spelled it.

Behind her, Tamsin murmured in her sleep and rolled toward the warmth where Silas had been. Silas watched the shape of her breathing for a long moment—the small hitch at the top of each inhale, the way one hand stayed curled near the edge of the pallet as if reaching for someone who kept leaving. Twelve days since the Saltfen. Ten since Hale had gone into the Choir's cells wearing his stubbornness like armor. Nine since Silas had stood over his empty bedroll and understood exactly what her invisibility had cost, and what more it was going to cost if she didn't end this soon.

Merek's offer had come folded inside a hymnal left in a dead-drop they'd used once, months ago, when Silas still thought she could be a merchant instead of a fugitive. *Surrender yourself and the spark you carry. The city spares everyone you've touched. The smuggler walks free within the week. The acolyte is returned to the Choir's protection—or released, if she wishes it. No one else need burn.*

She'd read it standing in the rain outside Ives's door, and the terrible thing, the thing she couldn't stop turning over since, was that she believed him. Not because Merek was kind—she'd seen what his arithmetic did to people—but because he was honest the way a blade is honest. It cuts or it doesn't. There's no third thing.

*Ives says the rite might work,* she'd told herself. *Ives says we can split the spark, dilute it, make sure no single death frees Veyra or dooms the city.* And Ives believed it, with the fervent certainty of a woman who'd built her whole heresy around one forbidden page. But Ives also said *no one has survived the splitting in three hundred years*, and said it in the flat voice people use for facts they can't soften. Six bearers. Six chances for the spark to tear them apart from the inside. Hale was in a cell. The twins were barely more than children. And Tamsin—

Tamsin had already died once in a vision. Silas wasn't going to help the prophecy along.

She sealed the letter with plain tallow, no mark, and tucked it inside her coat against her ribs, right over the place where the oathmark had lived before the rite scattered it into threads. Even now, half-ruined as it was, she could feel those threads humming under her skin like wires after a storm. Faint. Failing. Enough.

The handoff was set for tomorrow night. The old salt warehouse on Tanner's Slip, ninth bell, alone. Merek's terms, Merek's ground. She'd spent the day learning the approaches: the water gate with its rotted pilings, the catwalk over the render-yard, the two Choir patrol routes that crossed within sight of the doors. If he meant treachery, she'd know before she was through the threshold, and she'd take as many of them down as her fraying spark allowed before it went out entirely.

It wasn't much of a plan. It was, however, hers, and it kept everyone she loved out of it, which made it better than every other plan on the table.

She banked the candle and lay down beside Tamsin without touching her, the letter a small hard rectangle against her heart, and stared at the ceiling beams until exhaustion took her like a debt collector.

* * *

Tamsin woke to an empty pallet and knew, before her eyes were fully open, before her feet found the cold floor, that something had changed in the shape of the room.

Silas had been sleeping badly for nine days—not tossing, nothing so honest as tossing, but lying rigid as a plank with her eyes closed, breathing like a woman pretending. Tamsin had learned to read her the way sailors read weather. Last night the rigidity had been different. Resolved. A decision wearing sleep like a mask.

She found the candle stub cold. She found the ink pot stoppered. And she found, caught in the seam of the crate Silas used as a desk, a curl of wax the color of nothing—plain tallow, unmarked, the seal of someone who didn't want to be identified.

Tamsin stood very still in the gray light coming through the chapel's high slit windows and let the fear move through her the way Ives had taught her to let prayers move through: completely, without flinching.

Then she started searching.

It took until midmorning. She went through Silas's gear with hands that wanted to shake and didn't allow it—bedroll, boot heels, the false bottom of the charm-case she hadn't used since burning her stock. Nothing. It was only when she sat down on the crate itself, defeated, that she felt the stiff edge pressing through her skirt against the wood, and lifted the loose board beneath, and there it was.

A letter. Sealed in plain tallow. Addressed in Silas's careful mercenary's hand: *Inquisitor Dain Merek, Cathedral of the Quiet.*

Tamsin read it twice. It was four lines long. It didn't need to be longer.

She should have been surprised. She wasn't. That was the worst part—that some part of her, the part that had carried a vision of Silas dying at an altar across half the country, had known this was coming the way you know a storm is coming from the taste of the air. Silas Brightwater did not run from sacrifices. She collected them. She'd given up her god's voice, her name, her company, twelve years of her life, and now, when the ledger finally showed a surplus—when she had a crew, a cause, a woman who loved her—she meant to pay out the whole account at once and call it fair.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, to the empty chapel. Her voice cracked on it. She said it again, steadier. "No."

Mother Ives found her an hour later, sitting where she'd sat, the letter in her lap, and didn't ask. Ives had been defrocked for asking too few of the wrong questions and too many of the right ones; she simply poured tea, sat opposite, and waited.

"She'll go alone," Tamsin said. "Ninth bell, Tanner's Slip. She thinks none of us will know."

"Will you tell the others?"

"And have Renna try to talk her out of it? Oskar lock her in a cellar?" Tamsin turned the letter over. Plain wax. Honest as a blade. "She'd just go anyway. She's Silas." She looked up. "But if someone else were already at the warehouse when she arrived—if the meeting happened before she got there—"

Ives set down her cup. For a long moment the only sound was the undercity dripping somewhere below the floor, the chapel's eternal heartbeat.

"You're proposing to trade yourself for her," Ives said. "Knowing what the Choir does to resonants. Knowing what Merek knows about you."

"I know what Merek knows about me." Tamsin had thought about very little else since dawn. "He knows I was cast out for praying to Veyra by name. He knows I heard a vision meant for another. He doesn't know—" she touched her own forearm, where no mark had ever burned, where the vision lived instead behind her eyes, "—that the spark isn't only in her anymore. After the rite, it's in all of us. Threads. If he takes me, he gets a thread. One thread, torn from six. Ives, you said it yourself—a split spark can't free her and can't be used. I'm the safest person in this room to put in chains."

"That is not the argument of someone who has thought clearly."

"It's the argument of someone who has thought about nothing else." Tamsin stood. The letter felt heavy as a stone in her pocket. "She wrote to him offering her life for ours. I'm going to write back offering mine for hers. And then I'm going to be at Tanner's Slip at ninth bell, and when Silas arrives—and she will arrive, because she'd walk into the Quiet's own throat before she let a promise go unkept—she's going to find the meeting already finished, and me gone, and you're going to keep her here."

Ives studied her for a long time. Whatever she saw seemed to settle something, because she rose, went to her writing desk, and took out paper.

"If I help you," she said, "and she learns of it, she will never forgive either of us."

"I know." Tamsin almost smiled. "That's how I know it's the right thing. She forgives everything else."

* * *

Silas spent the day of the handoff saying goodbye without saying it.

She fixed the hinge on the chapel's storeroom door, which had squealed for a week. She taught Renna the river-signs for the eastern channels, casually, as though it were idle knowledge, watching the girl absorb them the way she absorbed everything. She ate Oskar's terrible stew and asked for seconds and meant it. She let Ives catch her eye across the afternoon and held the look a beat longer than necessary, and Ives nodded once, slow, and said nothing, and Silas chose to believe the nod meant *go with whatever gods are left.*

At dusk she stood at the mouth of the tunnel that led up toward the Slip and let herself look back, just once, at the lamplit chapel and the sound of Renna laughing at something Oskar had said.

Tamsin was nowhere in sight. That was good. That was mercy. Silas had rehearsed a dozen farewells in the night and abandoned all of them; anything she said aloud, Tamsin would hear the truth in, and the truth would make her do something reckless, and Silas needed her safe more than she needed to be known.

*You'll understand later,* she told the dark. *Or you won't, and you'll hate me, and you'll be alive to do it. That's the trade. That's always been the trade.*

She walked up into the evening fog with the letter against her ribs and the threads of a dying god whispering under her skin, and she did not look back again.

* * *

Tanner's Slip at ninth bell was all black water and lamplight bleeding yellow through fog. Silas came in along the render-yard catwalk as planned, counted the guards—four visible, two surely hidden, Merek's economy of force—and dropped to the cobbles at the warehouse door with her hands open and empty.

The door opened before she knocked.

Not Merek. A Choir sister, young, expressionless, holding a lantern. Behind her, in the warehouse's cavernous dark between stacked salt barrels, a table had been set with two chairs and a single lamp, and Inquisitor Dain Merek sat at it with his hands folded, looking older than he had at the Saltfen, looking like a man whose sums had stopped adding up and who blamed the numbers.

"Brightwater." He gestured to the far chair. "You came alone."

"I came," Silas said, and walked toward the lamp.

And stopped.

Because the chair facing Merek was not empty. A figure sat in it, slight, hooded back, hands flat on the table in deliberate surrender—and even before the hood turned, even before the lamp caught the line of a jaw Silas knew better than her own heartbeat, she knew. Some knowledge arrives ahead of the senses, the way thunder arrives ahead of the explanation.

"Tamsin." Her voice came out wrecked. "What have you done?"

"What you taught me." Tamsin pushed the hood back. Her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "You wrote him a letter trading your life for ours. I wrote him one trading mine for yours. Mine arrived first." A breath that was almost a laugh. "I've always been faster than you at the things that matter."

Merek watched them both with the stillness of a man witnessing a proof he hadn't asked for. "Two letters," he said quietly. "Same terms, same hour, same sacrifice. Each addressed to spare the other." He unfolded his hands. "Do you know what I lost to the god-wars, Brightwater? Everyone. And I told myself returning divinity meant returning slaughter, because slaughter is what divinity has ever meant. I built my whole life on that arithmetic." He looked from Silas to Tamsin and back, and something in his face gave, hairline-cracked, like ice under weight. "I never accounted for this. Two people racing each other to die."

"Then let us both go," Silas said, "and take the spark's threads as they fail on their own—"

"The terms stand," Merek said. "One bearer. Freely given. The smuggler walks, the city stands down." His jaw tightened. "Those were my terms. I am bound by my own word, God help me, and one of you has met them."

Silas moved. Later she would not remember deciding to—only that Tamsin was rising from the chair, and the Choir sister was reaching for her arm, and Silas's body simply refused, with the whole furious weight of everything she was, to let it happen. She crossed the space in three strides, and the threads of Veyra in her blood answered her desperation the way they always had, flaring silver-bright, and the lanterns guttered, and the nearest guard staggered back clutching eyes full of light.

"Tamsin." She caught her wrists. Up close, Tamsin's composure was a thin shell over terror, and she wore it anyway, and Silas loved her so violently it felt like injury. "Take it back. Take the letter back. You don't get to do this. You don't get to *out-sacrifice* me—"

"Someone has to," Tamsin whispered. "You'd never choose yourself. So I chose for you." Her voice broke and held. "You told me once, on the barge, that an oath is just love with the fear taken out. Well. I'm terrified, Silas. And I'm staying."

The light in the warehouse steadied. Somewhere beyond the walls, bells began to mark the quarter hour, and Silas felt, through the fraying threads of her god, a vast and distant stirring—as if something imprisoned beneath the Saltfen had turned over in its sleep and begun, slowly, to listen.

Merek rose from his chair. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that only the three of them heard it.

"Choose," he said. "Before my superiors choose for you. You have until the bells finish."

* * *

Outside, in the fog above Tanner's Slip, a Choir signal-lantern blinked twice—*bearer secured*—and began, deliberately, to blink a third message that no one had ordered:

*Both of them.*
