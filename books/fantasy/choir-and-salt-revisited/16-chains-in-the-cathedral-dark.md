## 16. Chains in the Cathedral Dark

The girl had not stopped singing.

Dain Merek stood outside the resonance cell and listened to it bleed through three inches of blessed iron: a hymn, low and unhurried, in an old dialect the Choir had spent two centuries burying. The acolytes who escorted her down had reported she went quietly. That was the part that troubled him. He had expected screaming. He had expected bargaining. He had built his whole life on the assumption that people begged when the arithmetic finally caught up with them.

She hadn't even asked what would happen to her.

He signaled the wardens to open the door, and the singing stopped mid-verse, as if she'd been waiting for exactly this.

Tamsin sat on the cell's stone bench with her wrists unbound—there'd been no need; she hadn't fought—and her hands folded in her lap like a student awaiting a lesson. Up close she was younger than her file suggested. Underfed. There was a healing cut along her jaw from the handoff at the docks, where she had stepped into a meeting meant for another woman and said, *I'm the one you want,* with a steadiness Merek had only ever heard from men about to do something unforgivable.

"You're Inquisitor Merek," she said. Not a question.

"And you're the one who took Silas Brightwater's place." He pulled the second chair to face her and sat, elbows on knees, the way he did for interrogations that were really conversations. "Why?"

"Because she would have come anyway." Tamsin's eyes were clear. No tears, no performance. "And if she came, your people would have killed her, or worse, she'd have killed hers way through yours, and then we're all just doing the god-war again with smaller numbers."

"That's not an answer to why *you*."

"It is." She tilted her head. "You think love is a weakness because it makes people predictable. It does make them predictable. I knew she'd trade herself. So I traded myself first. Love isn't a flaw in the arithmetic, Inquisitor. It's just arithmetic you haven't done yet."

Merek kept his face still. Twelve years of practice. But something behind his ribs shifted, the way ice shifts on a river before it goes.

"Your file says you were cast out of the Choir," he said. "For praying to Veyra by name."

"For praying honestly." A ghost of a smile. "There's a difference. Your Choir prays to nothing, you know that? All those silent bells, all that incense. You pray to the absence of gods and call it peace. I prayed to one, and she answered, and for that I'm in a cage."

"She answered you with a vision of Silas Brightwater dying."

"Yes."

"And you still followed her. Still—" he gestured vaguely, at the cell, at everything, "—loved her. Knowing how it ends."

"I don't know how it ends." For the first time, something moved under the clarity—fear, maybe, or faith, they looked identical from certain angles. "I know one picture. One frame. Visions aren't books, Inquisitor. They're windows, and someone else decides which window opens. Maybe Silas dies at the altar. Maybe she dies there so that no one else has to die anywhere. I've read both those endings in the same image. Haven't you? A thing that means ruin and salvation depending on which side of it you stand?"

Merek thought of his wife's hands. Of the ash that had been Ashenford. Of the arithmetic he'd carried for thirty years: *any returning divinity means slaughter*, because he had stood on the wrong side of a rising god once and buried what it left behind.

"The last time a god rose," he said quietly, "it didn't matter which side anyone stood on. The fire didn't check allegiances."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "It didn't. And your answer was to build machines to strangle anything holy before it could breathe. And now the gods are answering prayers again, everywhere, all at once, and your engines are full, and you're drowning in the very thing you tried to prevent. Tell me, Inquisitor—when the dam breaks, is it better to have spent thirty years learning to hate the water?"

He left without answering. That was the discipline. You never let them see the crack.

But in the corridor, walking up out of the dark toward the bells that never rang, Dain Merek caught himself doing something he had not done since he was a boy: he counted the cost of the girl's death, itemized it the way he itemized everything, and found the column did not balance. She was a resonant. She was a heretic. She was, by every entry in his ledger, a threat to be processed.

And she had walked into chains so that a mercenary wouldn't have to, and sung hymns to the god in the iron, and called his hatred *arithmetic he hadn't finished*.

His superiors wanted the girl bled into the engines by week's end. The resonance siphons needed bearers, living ones, and Tamsin's connection to Veyra was strong enough to make her valuable past the point of usefulness. He signed the transfer order because the order required signing. His hand did not shake.

It simply felt, for the first time in thirty years, like someone else's hand.

* * *

They moved her deeper that night.

Silas heard it from Mother Ives, who heard it from a laundress who heard it from a warden with gambling debts: the acolyte had been taken alive at the docks, and the Choir had taken her below the cathedral, into the old resonance vaults where the siphon-engines drank.

Silas stood in Ives's cellar sanctuary among the candle stubs and smuggled relics and did not speak for a long time. Brannoc Hale sat on a crate with his bandaged knuckles and his granddaughter's carved wooden bird in his fist, and waited. He'd been back from the cells four days, exchanged for nothing, released without explanation—which meant Merek had wanted him released, which meant Merek was playing some game none of them could read.

"They'll put her in the engines," Ives said. Not cruelly. Just factually, the way she delivered most truths. "A bearer that strong will keep a siphon lit for years. They won't kill her, Silas. They'll do something I've only seen described in books that were burned for describing it."

"Then we go tonight."

"We are six," Hale said. "They are four hundred. The cathedral sits on its own island of stone with two bridges, both warded, both watched."

"I know the math, Brannoc."

"I know you know the math." He set the wooden bird down carefully, the way he handled everything he loved. "I'm saying it out loud so we all agree we're fools before we start. My crew's in. Wren's in—she hasn't stopped sharpening things since the docks. Even young Pip says he's in, and Pip is fifteen and lies about his age, so weigh that accordingly."

Silas turned to Ives. "The rite worked. Partially. The spark scattered. What's left in me—is it enough to get us through their wards?"

Ives was quiet a moment. "It's enough to be noticed," she said. "Which is either our key or our funeral bell. The wards are tuned to resonate. Walk in carrying even a thread of Veyra, and every engine in that building will feel you coming."

"Good," Silas said. "Let them feel me. While they're looking at me, Hale takes the water-gate. Wren takes the bell-tower rope. Ives—" she hesitated. "I need the doors held. When we come out, whatever's chasing us, I need those doors held."

"With borrowed prayer?" Ives smiled thinly. "Child, I was defrocked for teaching that prayer belongs to everyone. Holding a door against the Choir with it is the least they can arrest me for twice."

Silas looked around the cellar—at the smuggler with ruined knuckles, the priest without a church, the crew of thieves and runaways who had no reason on earth to march into the teeth of the Choir except that a runaway acolyte had once shared her bread with them below decks.

"I'm not asking you to die for me," she said. "I'm asking you to help me not let her die for me. There's a difference, and I intend to keep it."

Hale grunted—the sound he made instead of agreement. Wren grinned like a knife. Somewhere upstairs, the city bells tolled the hour, silent as always, and Silas thought: *not for much longer.*

"One more thing," Ives said, as they gathered cloaks and blades and the small terrible inventory of a hopeless plan. "Merek released Hale without terms. Ask yourself why an inquisitor gives back his prisoner and waits. Either he's baiting us—and we walk into exactly the trap he's built—or he's a man whose certainty has started to leak. Both roads lead to him, Silas. Be ready for either."

Silas buckled her sword, and beneath the leather wrap, the frayed thread of her mark pulsed once, like a heartbeat remembering itself.

"Then let's go meet him," she said.

* * *

Merek stood in the resonance vault at midnight and watched the engines drink.

They had wired Tamsin into the smallest of the siphons—not the great central machine but a subsidiary coil, a testing rig—and already the brass rings around her wrists glowed with harvested light. She had stopped singing. That frightened him more than the singing had. The technicians said subjects often went quiet at this stage, that silence indicated efficient capture.

He thought: *or it indicates a person deciding where to spend her strength.*

"Extraction rates exceed projection," said Sub-Inquisitor Corvane beside him, young and eager and smelling of ink. "At this yield, we could light the main array within the month. The Grand Director sends her compliments, and her instruction that the subject's processing continue uninterrupted."

"The subject has a name."

Corvane blinked. "Inquisitor?"

"Tamsin." He heard his own voice and didn't recognize it. "Her name is Tamsin. Write it in the log. Names go in the log. That's how we stay different from the things we fight."

Corvane wrote it, bewildered, and Merek turned away before the boy could see his face.

Above them, far above, through forty feet of stone and blessed iron, the cathedral bells hung silent in their towers as they had hung for two hundred years. Merek had always found comfort in that silence—the whole architecture of his belief was built on it, on the proposition that quiet was safety, that unanswered heavens were kind heavens, that a city with no gods in it was a city where children did not burn.

He had been a child in a city with a god in it. He remembered the burning. He remembered his mother pushing him into a root cellar and the door going orange, and he remembered that he had never once, in thirty years, allowed himself to ask the question that had been waiting behind all his arithmetic like a debtor at a funeral:

*If the gods are the fire, who taught the fire to burn?*

The alarm began as a shiver in the floor.

He felt it before he heard it—one thread of familiar resonance threading up through the foundations, faint and frayed but unmistakable, a signature he had catalogued years ago and never forgotten. The wards sang. The watch-bells, dead for two centuries, trembled in their frames with a sound like a held breath.

"She's coming," Corvane breathed, half terror, half awe. "Brightwater. She's actually—"

"Muster the guard on the bridges. Seal the water-gate. Wake the—" Merek stopped. Stood very still in the humming dark, listening to the signature rise, and understood, with the calm of a man reading his own death warrant, that it was too clean. Too loud. A beacon, not an approach.

"She wants us looking at the front door," he said slowly. "Which means—" 

The water-gate alarm shrieked.

Corvane ran. Merek did not. He stood alone in the vault, beside the girl wired into the machine that was drinking her light, and did the arithmetic he had avoided for thirty years.

Four hundred defenders. Six attackers. Every number favored the Choir, and every number was worthless, because numbers had never accounted for a woman trading her freedom for a stranger's, or a girl stepping into a noose with a hymn in her mouth, or an old smuggler with ruined knuckles who had gone into Choir cells and come out owing nothing and giving everything anyway.

Love, Tamsin had called it. Arithmetic he hadn't finished.

Merek looked at Tamsin—quiet, dimming, her light being poured into brass rings to power the machinery of a silence that had never saved anyone—and made the first entry in a new ledger.

He drew his blade, and turned it on the lock of the engine housing.

"Hold on, acolyte," he said. "Your arithmetic is about to get worse before it gets better."

Above them, the first of the silent bells began, very softly, to ring.
