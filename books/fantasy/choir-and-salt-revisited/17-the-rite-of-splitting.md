## 17. The Rite of Splitting

Mother Ives drew the circle in salt at midnight, and Silas thought, absurdly, of her market stall.

Same salt. Same careful lines. Different purpose. For twelve years she'd used that white grain to ward off bad luck and worse attention, pouring it into paper twists for fishermen's wives and nervous dockhands, selling them nothing but the shape of safety. Now Hale's granddaughter was sweeping the same salt into a ring wide enough for five people to kneel inside, and the price had changed.

"You understand what I'm about to do," Ives said. Not a question. The old priest never asked questions she didn't want answered.

"Split the spark." Silas pulled her sleeve down past her elbow. The oathmark burned beneath the skin, a brightwater-blue thread of light coiling from wrist to shoulder, brighter every hour since Saltfen. "Five bearers instead of one. No single death can free her or doom her."

"And if it fails?"

"Then I'm dead, and you're all standing in a cellar with a dead mercenary and a lot of spilled salt."

Ives's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "That's not the failure mode I meant, girl."

"I know what you meant." Silas knelt at the circle's edge and set her bare arm on the cold stone floor. Beneath them, the undercity cistern hummed with runoff water; above them, the Choir's cathedral squatted on its hill like a toad made of architecture, holding Tamsin somewhere in its dark. "If it fails the other way, Veyra wakes up scattered across five people who can't hold her, tears loose, and takes the city apart looking for her enemies. I know."

"Say it anyway." Ives crouched opposite her, silver hair pinned back with what looked suspiciously like a fishhook. "The rite works on truth. It always has. That's why the Choir buried it."

Silas looked around the circle. Hale stood at the north point, arms crossed over his barrel chest, still moving stiff from three days of healing after the Choir cells—three days Merek had given back to her, along with an apology nobody had asked for and nobody had accepted. His granddaughter Wren took the east point, thirteen years old and refusing to be sent away, chin up in a way that made Silas's chest hurt. Mother Ives at the west. And the south point—

Empty. Tamsin's point.

"She should be here," Silas said.

"She is where she is because she loves you stupidly," Ives said. "Which is why we do this tonight. Say the truth, Silas. All of it."

Silas closed her hand into a fist. The mark pulsed against her palm like a second heartbeat, out of rhythm with her own, the way it had been out of rhythm for twelve years—silent, then suddenly, horribly, present again. A god's attention. A god's need.

"The truth," Silas said, and her voice came out lower than she intended. "Veyra didn't refuse to fight because she was gentle. She refused because she'd already seen how it ends. And the other gods buried her for cowardice, and I've spent twelve years calling it mercy because the alternative was calling my whole life a lie." The mark flared. She kept talking, fast, before the truth could choke her. "She wants out. Every prayer that gets answered in this city is her pulling on the bars. And if she gets out through me—if I die wrong, or break wrong—she comes up swinging, and Correnholt burns first, and everyone in this circle dies second. That's the truth. I am not a vessel. I'm a fuse. And I want—" Her voice cracked. She let it. "I want to stop being the only thing standing between her and everything I love."

The salt ring drank the words. Ives nodded once, satisfied, terrible.

"Good. Now hold still. This part I can't make gentle."

* * *

The rite, as Ives described it, was simple the way drowning is simple.

A spark of divinity is not a soul. It has no self, no loyalties—it is pure *answer*, the raw capacity to respond to prayer. What makes it dangerous is concentration: one bearer, one point of failure, one human heart asked to hold an ocean in a cup. Split it, and each fragment becomes too small to open the prison door alone. Dilute it until no single death—not Silas's, not Tamsin's, not anyone the Choir could put on an altar—could ever give Veyra enough to rise.

"No one has survived it in three hundred years," Ives had said, when she first taught it. "Not because it kills. Because it *unmakes*. The bearer's bond to their god is a rope. Cut it into pieces and you have to hope the pieces remember they were ever a rope."

Silas lay back against the cold stone, arm extended across the salt, and thought about ropes.

Hale took her right hand. Wren, small fingers trembling, took her left. Ives set a knife—a real knife, consecrated once, defrocked now, like its owner—at the center of the oathmark, where the blue light burned brightest.

"When I cut," Ives said, "you'll feel it leave. Don't fight it. Don't chase it. The instinct will be to hold on, and if you hold on, it tears instead of splits." Her old eyes were very steady. "Let go, Silas. Even though every part of you will scream not to."

"What happens to what's left?" Silas asked. "In me."

Ives didn't answer. That was answer enough.

"Do it," Silas said. "Tamsin doesn't have until dawn."

The knife came down.

There was no pain at first. There was *silence*—a silence so total it had texture, the silence of the twelve years compressed into one endless heartbeat, and Silas understood with sick clarity that this was what Veyra felt in her prison, this was the inside of the god's abandonment, this was—

Then the spark moved.

It didn't flow. It *tore*, the way cloth tears, along a line Ives's blade guided but did not choose, and Silas felt the first piece go into Hale's weathered hand and the second into Wren's small one and the third into Ives, and each departure was a sound she heard with her bones rather than her ears—a note cut from a chord, the chord stumbling on without it. Her body arched. Someone was shouting. The salt ring blazed white, then blue, then a gray that had never been any color of fire before, and through it all Silas held to the one instruction Ives had given her:

Let go. Let go. Let go.

She let go.

And the last of it—the deepest part, the part that had lived behind her sternum since she was nineteen years old and swore on the Saltfen altar—came loose like a tooth, like a name, like a language she'd spoken fluently and would never speak again, and went into the four hands holding hers, and the circle went dark.

Silas lay on the stone floor of a smuggler's cistern and listened for her god.

Nothing.

Not the silence she knew—the loaded silence, the held-breath silence, the silence with a god behind it. Just quiet. Water dripping. Wren crying softly. Hale breathing hard, both fists pressed to his chest as if he could feel the fragment lodged there, and maybe he could; his eyes had gone faintly, terribly bright, twin candle flames guttering low.

"It worked," Wren whispered. "Did it work?"

Ives was kneeling over Silas, two fingers pressed to her throat, and her face was doing something complicated. "She's alive. Heart's strong." A pause Silas heard but couldn't interpret. "The rite held. The spark scattered."

"Then why does she look like that?" Hale asked.

Silas raised her own arm.

The oathmark was still there—but changed. Where it had burned solid, a rope of light, it now ran in broken threads, thin blue filaments spaced along her skin like a river seen from very high up, like stitches half-pulled from a wound. Between the threads: nothing. Gaps. Holes in something that had once been whole.

She reached for Veyra the way she'd reached for her every day for fifteen years—the way a soldier reaches for a weapon, the way a child reaches for a lamp in the dark—and found only threads. Faint ones. Frayed ends. When she followed them inward, they dissolved before reaching anything, trailing off into gray static, into a distance that hadn't been there before.

"I can't hear her," Silas said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else reporting from another room. "It's not—I'm not sealed off. The connection's just—" She flexed her hand. The threads shimmered weakly. "Frayed. Like the rite took most of the rope and left me the memory of holding it."

Ives sat back on her heels. For a long moment the old woman said nothing, and Silas watched her decide whether to lie, and watched her decide against it—because truth was the rite's currency, and Ives had just spent a great deal of it.

"That's the cost," Ives said quietly. "The texts warned of it, in words I didn't understand until now. The splitting doesn't dilute the spark alone. It dilutes the *bearer*. You gave most of your bond away, and what remains—" She touched one of the frayed threads, featherlight, and Silas felt it like a hand brushed over an old scar. "You may carry prayers again someday, Silas Brightwater. But you will never again be the vessel you were. Whatever you were saving—" Her voice caught, just slightly. "Whatever you were keeping, all these years, for her or for yourself. It's spent."

The cistern dripped. Wren wiped her face with her sleeve, trying to be brave about it. Hale sat down heavily against the wall, bright-eyed and shaking, alive, carrying a piece of a god he'd run relics for and never believed in.

Silas stared at her ruined mark and waited for grief, and grief came, but it came strangely—arriving alongside something else, something colder and cleaner. Relief. Twelve years of being the fuse, the key, the single point of failure, the one death that could end the world—and now there were four of them, scattered thin, none sufficient, none destroyable in a way that mattered. Tamsin couldn't be used to free Veyra anymore. No altar could take what was left in Silas and open the prison door. The Choir's engines had nothing left to siphon but scraps.

She was safe. Everyone she loved was safe, in the only way that arithmetic allowed.

So why did the quiet feel like dying?

"Get up," she said, and was surprised the voice was hers—rough, level, command-voice, the voice that had once led forty sworn spears at the Saltfen. She pushed herself upright on the wet stone. The threads along her arm flickered, held. "We've got maybe an hour before whatever we just did shows up on the Choir's resonance instruments. Maybe less. Merek knows something happened—he'll have felt it in his teeth." She looked at Hale. "Your cell block. Which entrance?"

Hale blinked the brightness out of his eyes and grinned, and it was the grin of a man who'd just discovered he was carrying divine fire and found it fit in his hands easier than expected. "The corpse gate. Nobody watches a corpse gate."

"Then we go get her." Silas pulled her sleeve down over the frayed threads, over the holes where her god used to live, and stood. "Tonight. Before dawn. Before anyone decides those fragments are worth collecting."

They moved to gather cloaks and blades and lamps. Ives lingered, one hand still resting on the dead salt circle.

"Silas." Her voice stopped her at the circle's edge. "One more truth, since the rite likes them. What you're feeling right now—that hollow place? It isn't only the bond." The old priest's eyes were wet and fierce. "You split the spark so no single sacrifice could ever be enough. Do you understand what you've done? You haven't just protected her from the Choir. You've taken the ending out of Tamsin's vision. She saw you die at the altar. And now there is no altar that can kill you."

Silas stood very still in the dripping dark.

Because Ives was right, and the truth of it landed harder than any blade: Tamsin had known the ending for weeks. Had chosen her anyway. Had let herself be taken wearing that knowledge like a stone under her ribs. And somewhere up in the cathedral dark, in whatever chains the Choir had made, Tamsin was still waiting for the vision to come true—still braced for a death that Silas had just, at terrible cost, made impossible.

*She doesn't know,* Silas thought, and the hollow in her chest filled with something that was almost laughter, almost weeping. *She's down there expecting to lose me, and I've already spent the part of me she was afraid for.*

She picked up her sword.

"Then someone had better tell her," Silas said, and led them toward the corpse gate.
