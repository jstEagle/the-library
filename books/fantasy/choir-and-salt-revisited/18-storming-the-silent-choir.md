## 18. Storming the Silent Choir

**HALE**

They came for him at the changing of the watch, which told Hale two things: his captors were disciplined, and someone outside was not.

He heard it before he understood it—a low thrum through the cell floor, like a bowstring drawn across the whole city. Then the torches in the corridor guttered blue, and the lock on his door wept frost.

Brannoc Hale had spent forty years running cargo on dark rivers. He knew a diversion when he heard one. He also knew that when a diversion arrived, you moved *now* and asked questions in the boat.

The door swung open on its own hinges—warped by cold—and Mother Ives stood in the corridor with her hands raised and her eyes shut, murmuring something old. She looked smaller than he remembered from the undercity sermons, grayer, but her voice filled the stone like water filling a hull.

"Can you walk?" she said without opening her eyes.

"I can run," Hale said. "Badly."

"Then do it badly and quickly." Her murmur deepened. Somewhere above them, a bell began to ring—not the Choir's measured hours but a panicked, arrhythmic clangor. "The borrowed prayer won't hold past dawn, Brannoc. And I've promised the doors to Silas Brightwater, so I intend to be standing between them when she arrives."

"Between the doors?"

"Every door in this cathedral," Ives said, "is about to matter very much."

Hale ran badly and quickly, and behind him the frost crept up the walls of the cells, sealing each empty room in silence.

* * *

**IVES**

The great doors of the Cathedral of the Quiet were oak and iron, three men tall, and had not been forced open in two hundred years.

Mother Ives stood before them at the bottom of the processional stair with salt poured in a line at her feet and a prayer in her mouth that no living congregation had sung since the god-wars ended. It was not her prayer. That was what made it *borrowed*. It belonged to a woman named Sera Vane who had died at the Saltfen with Veyra's name half-spoken, and Ives had carried the words thirty years in a locked box in her chest, waiting for a night worth spending them on.

The first Inquisitors found her within minutes. Of course they did—the stair was the one road in.

"Hold the line," she told the smugglers and defrocked acolytes and one extremely nervous river-rat named Pip who had volunteered because Silas had once fixed his sister's cough. "Hold it, and mean it."

"Inquisitor's coming," someone hissed. "Not Merek. The other one. Prior Cassian."

Ives felt the prior's presence before she saw him—the particular pressure of a man who had never prayed in his life and resented everyone who had. He descended the stair flanked by six blade-wardens, and his voice, when it came, was almost gentle.

"Mother Ives. You were expelled for lesser heresies than this."

"This isn't heresy," Ives said. "Heresy is a wrong belief. I believe exactly what your own archives say, Prior. You built engines to catch gods in bottles. I'm just here to break the bottles."

"You are here to die on a stairway."

"Yes," Ives agreed pleasantly. "That too. Eventually. But not—" she pressed her palm flat against the air, and the borrowed prayer went out of her like a wave off a harbor wall, "—*tonight.*"

The wave took the six wardens off their feet. It took Cassian to one knee. It did not kill anyone, because Ives had spent her whole life learning prayers, and the ones worth borrowing were never the killing kind.

But she felt the cost immediately, the way a spendthrift feels the purse thin. The words were going out of her faster than she'd planned. Saltfen prayers were not meant for living mouths.

She straightened her spine and kept singing.

Above her, faint through the vaulted dark, she could hear the resonance engines beginning to scream.

* * *

**TAMSIN**

They had strapped Tamsin into the fourth engine because the fourth engine was failing, and failed siphons needed a living resonant to prime.

She had stopped screaming an hour ago. Screaming gave them satisfaction, or data—one of the two, and she refused to provide either. Instead she hung in the brass harness with her wrists bleeding where the clamps held her and did the only thing left: she prayed. Silently. Stubbornly. Not for rescue.

For Silas.

*Let her be afraid,* Tamsin prayed, because she knew Silas, and Silas's fear always came out sideways as fury, and fury would carry her up the river steps faster than hope ever could. *Let her be so angry she forgets to be careful.*

The engine drank the prayer out of her mid-thought. That was the horror of the thing—it didn't block the gods' channel, it *tapped* it. Every word Tamsin sent toward heaven went into the Choir's reservoirs instead, and she could feel her own devotion being weighed, measured, decanted into copper tanks by technicians who wouldn't look at her face.

Then the lights died.

All of them, all at once, the hundred lamps of the engine hall snuffed as if pinched by a single enormous hand, and in the sudden dark Tamsin heard the reservoirs begin to sing—high, glassy, rising—and then burst. One after another down the row, copper splitting like chestnuts in a fire, and whatever had been bottled inside them poured back out into the world, and the world *shivered*.

"The bearer," said a technician, in a voice gone thin with terror. "The bearer is in the building. Something answered her—"

"No," Tamsin said. Something loosened in her chest, warm and furious and unmistakable, the way a hearth is unmistakable to a person freezing in a field. "Something answered *me*."

The harness clamps froze. Frost climbed the brass. And through the shattered far wall of the engine hall—through the wall, not the door, because of course Silas Brightwater had never in her life used a door when a wall would do—came a light the color of oathmarks.

Silas walked into the engine hall trailing sparks from both hands like a woman wading through shallow fire, and behind her came Pip with a stolen pike and three smugglers with knives and absolutely no plan, and none of it mattered, because Silas crossed the floor in ten strides and tore the clamps off Tamsin's arms with her bare hands, and the metal didn't even burn her.

"You're late," Tamsin croaked.

"You prayed me here," Silas said. Her voice cracked on it. "I felt every word. Don't ever stop."

"Wasn't planning to."

Silas got her shoulder under Tamsin's arm, and the sparks around them flared bright enough to throw shadows on the broken ceiling—two women, leaning together, lit from within.

That was when Prior Cassian's voice rang through the cathedral, amplified by the dead engines' last resonance, cold and carrying:

*"Inquisitor Merek. The traitors are in the engine hall. Kill the resonant girl and the bearer will surrender herself. That is an order."*

And Tamsin, half-carrying Silas now, felt the other woman go rigid against her side.

"Merek," Silas breathed. "Where's Merek."

* * *

**MEREK**

Dain Merek stood in the reliquary gallery above the engine hall with his sword drawn, and discovered that after twenty years of service he had finally been given an order he could not do arithmetic on.

Kill the resonant girl. Save the city. Simple. Clean. The same equation he'd balanced a thousand times in twenty years: one life against many, the individual against the whole. He had buried his wife and daughters under that equation and called the burial necessary, and he had never once doubted the sum.

Except.

Except the girl had taken Silas Brightwater's place at a handoff meant for a marked woman, knowing what the meeting was, walking into it with her chin up like a soldier reporting for a duty she'd invented herself. Except the mercenary Hale, who under interrogation had given away nothing for three days except a running commentary on the quality of Choir tea. Except the old priestess currently holding the cathedral's front doors alone with a dead woman's prayer, singing hymns at armed wardens like a grandmother scolding weather.

Everyone Silas Brightwater touched stood in front of her like a wall.

Merek had spent his career believing that faith made people stupid—that gods were the original lie, the first and worst con, and the god-wars the proof. But standing here, watching a smuggler's granddaughter sing to a frozen harness and a runaway acolyte bleed willingly into a machine, he was forced to consider an ugly alternative:

Maybe it wasn't faith that made people stupid.

Maybe it was whatever *he* had instead.

"Cassian's ordered the girl killed," said Warden Ostric beside him. "Your blade, Inquisitor. You're closest."

Merek looked down into the engine hall. Silas Brightwater stood below him with lightning in her fists and a dying god's name on her lips, and she was looking up—not at him, at the reservoirs, calculating something, and he realized with a strange lurch that she wasn't calculating how to escape.

She was calculating how to make sure no one else died doing this.

Twenty years. Twenty years of arithmetic, and it had never once occurred to him that the enemy might be doing sums too.

"No," Merek said.

Ostric turned. "Inquisitor—"

"I said *no*." Merek's voice came out level, which surprised him; he'd expected it to shake. "Cassian's order stands unexecuted. Report me if you like. Here's my countering order: every warden in this gallery stands down. Now."

"You'll hang for this."

"I know exactly what I'll hang for." Merek stepped forward, put himself bodily between Ostric's crossbow and the engine hall floor, and drew his sword—not at the intruders below, but at the winch mechanism that held the gallery's resonance amplifier over the hall. One cut. The amplifier crashed down onto the remaining machinery, and the engines' scream cut off mid-note, and the silence that followed was the first honest silence the Cathedral of the Quiet had held in two hundred years.

"Get the girl out," Merek called down to Silas Brightwater, not looking at her, looking at the stairs where Prior Cassian was ascending with every remaining blade-warden in the cathedral. "Your priestess can't hold the doors much longer, and I've just become a traitor, so I suggest we all leave together."

Silas stared up at him for one long heartbeat.

"You're serious."

"I'm a man who lost everything to a war started by people certain they were right," Merek said. "I don't intend to help start another. Move."

Below, Tamsin laughed—a wild, cracked sound. Above, Cassian's voice rose in command. And somewhere beneath all of it, deeper than the foundations, deeper than the reservoirs, something vast and imprisoned shifted in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

* * *

**SILAS**

They fought their way to the doors because there was nowhere else to fight to.

Ives met them at the base of the stair, gray-faced, blood at the corner of her mouth, still singing. The moment Silas's hand closed on the old woman's shoulder the borrowed prayer guttered out like a candle in wind, and Ives sagged against her with a groan of pure relief.

"That's spent," Ives rasped. "Thirty years in a box. Worth it. Go, girl—go, all of you, the river gate—"

"Not yet." Silas planted herself in the center of the processional hall, Tamsin braced against her side, Hale limping up behind with a warden's stolen mace, Merek standing apart with his sword still drawn and his face like a man reading his own death warrant and finding it legible.

Because the floor was humming.

Not the engines—they were wreckage now. This was older. It came up through the flagstones, through the salt lines, through the soles of Silas's boots and into the mark on her arm, which burned so brightly she could see its glow through her sleeve. Twelve years of silence, and underneath the cathedral, underneath the prison the other gods had built, Veyra was pressing upward like a tide against a door.

"She felt the reservoirs break," Silas said. "She felt *me*. She's coming up."

"Then we run," Hale said.

"If she comes up unanchored—" Silas's voice caught. She thought of the Saltfen, of the last prayer cut off mid-word, of the war the gods had nearly reignited over a single insult. "If she rises angry, with two hundred years of imprisonment behind her and the Choir's machines still smoking, there won't be a Correnholt to save. There may not be anything else either."

"So what do we do?" Tamsin asked quietly. Her hand found Silas's. Her wrist was still bleeding. "Silas. What do we do?"

Silas looked down at the glowing floor, and the answer rose in her the way the answers always had, in Veyra's cadence, in the old battle-prayer rhythm—and this time it wasn't a voice from outside. It was her own voice, arriving from somewhere ahead of her, from a choice she hadn't made yet but already understood.

"I stay," Silas said. "Someone has to meet her when she comes. Someone she knows."

"And I stay with you," Tamsin said instantly.

"Tamsin—"

"You prayed me here, remember? Your turn to feel every word."

The flagstones began to crack. Light came up through the seams like dawn through storm-clouds, gold-white and enormous, and Silas Brightwater tightened her grip on the hand of the woman she loved, faced the rising of her god, and stepped forward into the breaking of the world.

Above them, Merek raised his sword against his own former brothers-in-arms. Behind them, Hale barred the doors with a smuggler's timber brace. Beside them, Ives began—hoarse, defiant, alive—to sing again.

And beneath them, patient as salt, Veyra began to rise.
