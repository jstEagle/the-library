## 19. Veyra Rising

The world broke open like a struck bell.

Silas felt it before she heard it—a pressure behind her sternum, the threads of the spark scattered among her friends all pulling taut at once, six fishing lines hooked into something enormous on the far side of the stone. She was halfway up the cathedral's processional stair with Tamsin's hand in hers when the resonance engines beneath the nave began to scream.

Not metal screaming. Not steam. A voice.

"Down!" Mother Ives shouted from the doors, arms spread, prayer-light guttering around her like a candle in a gale. "Everyone down, she's—"

The floor split.

* * *

Later, Silas would try to describe it and fail every time. What came up through the rupture in the cathedral floor was not a shape. It was a season. It was salt air and the smell of rain on hot stone, it was the specific gray of dawn over a mercenary camp, it was twelve years of silence ending so suddenly that every mortal thing within a mile felt its own heartbeat stagger.

Veyra rose through the wreck of the Choir's great engine like a diver surfacing after too long underwater, and the first thing she did was make a sound that Silas had spent a decade believing she'd never hear again: her god, saying her name.

*SILAS.*

The word went through her like a spear of warm iron. Around her, Hale's crew dropped to their knees—not in worship, Silas thought, but because standing had stopped being an option. Merek was still upright somewhere behind her, blade drawn, face bloodied from turning it on his own superiors. Tamsin's grip on her hand crushed bone.

The engine housing tore loose and fell in pieces the size of ox-carts. Through the wound in the floor poured light—not white, nothing so clean—the color of sea-foam at dusk, green-gray and restless, and in it a vast suggestion of shoulders, of wings folded wrong from lying too long in a narrow grave, of a head turning on a neck like a river changing course.

The Choir had built their cathedral directly over the Saltfen altar. Of course they had. You cage the wolf in the middle of the sheepfold and call it vigilance.

*TWELVE YEARS,* Veyra said, and her voice was everywhere, in the stone and the smoke and the marrow of Silas's bones. *THEY HELD ME UNDER THE WATER AND CALLED IT PEACE.*

"I know," Silas said. Her voice came out small and human and absurd against that vastness, but she said it anyway, the way you'd speak to a wounded animal twice your weight. "I know. I'm here."

*YOU ARE HERE.* The great head swung toward her, and where the eyes should have been there were only deeps—tide-pools, dark water, the place light goes to die. *MY BEARER. MY LAST BRIGHT THING. THEY TOOK MY VOICE AND GAVE ME A HUNDRED YEARS OF LISTENING TO THEM PRAY TO THEIR EMPTY SKY, AND NOW—*

The cathedral shuddered. Cracks ran up pillars like lightning frozen mid-strike. Somewhere beyond the walls, Correnholt screamed—bells toppling, glass going, dogs howling across nine districts—and Silas understood with sick clarity what she was watching. This was not a god emerging. This was a war resuming. Veyra had been imprisoned for refusing to fight the last one, and the first act of her freedom was to find out whether she'd been right to refuse, or whether twelve years under the stone had taught her the only lesson captivity ever teaches:

That the ones who caged you must burn.

* * *

"She's going to breach the wards," Ives gasped. The old priestess was on her knees at the door, holding the last of the cathedral's containment liturgies together with both hands and sheer spite. The prayer-light around her had gone thin as morning frost. "If she gets out into the city in this state, the other gods will feel it by nightfall. They'll come to finish what they started. Every god who ever slept will wake to watch or to join, and this city—" she coughed, "—this city is standing on the battlefield."

"Then we hold her here," Silas said.

"With what, child? Look at her."

Silas looked.

Her god filled the ruined nave like floodwater fills a cellar. And underneath the fury, visible now that Silas made herself look past the light and the size and the terror of her, Veyra was wounded in a way gods are wounded—not bleeding but *thin*, stretched translucent in places, the way an old banner goes thin before it tears. Twelve years siphoned through the Choir's engines. They'd been drinking her slowly, converting divinity into their warding power, their silence, their surveillance. The whole gray machine of Correnholt's peace was built out of stolen god.

And now the debt had come due, with interest.

The spark inside Silas—what remained of it after the rite of splitting, frayed to threads running out to Hale and Ives and the others—pulled toward that vastness like water finding its level. She could feel Veyra feeling it. Could feel the god's attention sharpen on her the way a drowning creature sharpens on driftwood.

*BEARER.* Softer now. Terribly soft. *I AM COMING APART. FREE ME ENTIRELY AND I WILL DO WHAT WOUNDED GODS DO. GIVE ME YOUR SPARK AND I WILL BE WHOLE, AND WHOLENESS IN ME MEANS WAR. YOU KNOW THIS. YOU HAVE ALWAYS KNOWN THIS.*

"Yes," Silas said.

Tamsin's hand tightened. "Silas. *Silas.* Whatever you're thinking—"

"You already know what I'm thinking." Silas turned to her. Tamsin's face was streaked with soot and blood not her own, and her eyes were wide and wet and full of the thing she'd been carrying since before they met—the vision, the altar, the ending she'd confessed on a cold night with the words falling out of her like stones. *A bearer walking into the light and not walking back out.*

"That was three chapters of my life ago," Silas said, which wasn't fair, because it had been eleven days, and also because you can't say *chapters* to someone, life isn't written yet. She tried again. "You told me and I decided then I'd prove you wrong."

"You didn't."

"Not yet. Watch me."

* * *

Merek came up the stair at a run, skidding on rubble, sword forgotten or discarded—he looked strange without it, unarmored in every sense, a man whose entire architecture of certainty had collapsed and left him standing in the open. "Inquisitor," Silas said, before he could speak. "Good timing. I need someone to argue with while I think."

"Your god is going to level the city."

"My god is going to level the city unless I do something, yes, thank you, that's the argument I was hoping for." She looked back at Veyra. The light pulsed. Beneath them the broken engine groaned, its siphon-chambers cracked, still greedily drinking even now, dragging at the god like tar at a soldier's boots. "Merek. Your engines. Can Ives shut them down?"

"They're not machines you shut down," Merek said hoarsely. "They're liturgies. They're built out of a hymn I learned as a boy. You'd need—"

"A bearer," Ives said quietly, from the door. "To unmake a god-siphon you need divine fire applied at the source. Or you need three hundred singers and a week, and we have neither."

Divine fire at the source. Silas did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the floor opened. The spark was scattered among six bearers; no single death could free or doom Veyra anymore—that had been the point of the rite, the gamble, the half-success that had cost her most of her connection and bought them all a kind of safety. But safety from what? From the terms of an old oath. Not from this. If any one of them walked into that light alone, they'd carry a sixth of a spark, and Veyra would take it, and it wouldn't be enough to anchor her—it would be enough to *anger* her, a sip where she needed a meal.

But all of them. All six threads gathered back into one place, freely given—

No. That was the old math too. That was Tamsin's vision wearing different clothes.

Unless.

*I can hear you scheming,* Veyra said, and there was something almost like the old humor in it, the dry tide-pull voice that had once talked Silas to sleep after battles. *You always schemed in the same cadence. Your mother schemed the same way.*

"Don't," Silas said. "Don't you dare be gentle with me right now. Gentle is a trick. I know every trick you have, I learned them all at your knee."

*Then you know I cannot hold myself back much longer. The engines drink me even now. Every moment I spend being careful is a moment they grow stronger and I grow thinner, and thin gods do terrible desperate things, Brightwater. You've seen thin soldiers.*

She had. She'd buried them.

Tamsin stepped forward, putting herself between Silas and the light, which was so perfectly, so maddeningly like her. "There has to be another way. There's always—Mother Ives, the splitting rite, couldn't we—"

"The splitting rite divides," Ives said. "It doesn't give. Dividing further would just scatter her thinner until she gutters out, and a god guttering out takes everything she's touched down with her. Every marked soul. Every blessing ever given by her hand." Her eyes found Silas. "Which includes everyone in this room, child, and half the company you used to run with, and likely a few strangers in this city you charmed on a bad Tuesday."

So that was the ledger. Give everything and bind her gently—or divide again and watch her starve destructively—or stand here doing nothing while the engines ate her and the war woke up on its own.

Every option monstrous. Love making each one heavier than the last.

*Bearer,* Veyra said, and now there was no humor in it at all, only the vast tired patience of a thing that had waited twelve years in the dark and could wait exactly as long as it took Silas to decide and not one moment more. *I release you from your oath. Hear me. I release every soldier who ever swore to me, every widow who kept my feast days, every frightened child who prayed my name in alleys. You owe me nothing. Walk away. Live your small bright mortal life. I will manage my own dying.*

"No," Silas said.

*SILAS—*

"You don't get to do that. You don't get to be noble at me. I've watched men refuse help so they could die clean and I hated every one of them, and you're not going to be the biggest example of it in my life." Her voice cracked and she let it. "You went silent rather than fight. Twelve years ago, at the Saltfen, they ordered you to drown a city and you chose silence over slaughter and let us think you dead rather than tell us why. That's the truth, isn't it? That's what you've never said."

The great head bowed. Light moved across the ruins like water finding cracks.

*IT IS,* Veyra said.

"Then you're the only god I've ever heard of who chose peace when it cost her everything," Silas said, "and I am not letting the Choir's engines turn you into the monster they always said you were. Not for anything. Not for anyone."

She turned to Tamsin last.

Tamsin was crying without noise about it, the way she did everything—thoroughly and privately. "I saw this," she whispered. "Eleven days ago I told you and you held my hands and you said *then we'll change the ending*, and Silas, this isn't changed, this is exactly—"

"It's not." Silas took her face in both hands. Behind Tamsin the god-light surged and the engine shrieked and Hale shouted something, and Silas ignored all of it for the length of one breath. "In your vision, a bearer walks into the light to free her god, and the freed god burns the world. That's the ending. I'm not walking in to free her, Tam. I'm walking in to put her gently back to bed. Nobody's ever written that version. We get to be first."

"And you don't walk back out either way."

Silas didn't lie to her. That was the whole foundation of them, laid down in a barge's cargo hold with knives drawn: no lies between us, not even kind ones.

"Come with me, then," Tamsin said, and her voice broke wide open. "Split it with me. Take me in with you, half and half, I'm a bearer too now, Ives's rite put a thread in me, if we—"

"A thread. Tam. You've got a thread and I've got the frayed end of a rope." Silas pressed her forehead to Tamsin's. "And if you came with me and we both burned, who tells Hale's grandchildren about today? Who keeps Ives honest? Who makes Merek into a decent human being? He'll need constant supervision."

Tamsin laughed through the tears, one broken sound, and gripped Silas's wrists like she could hold her here by force. Like love was a rope with friction.

"I prayed to Veyra by name and it ruined my life," Tamsin said. "Best prayer I ever made."

"Second best," Silas said. "The best one answered itself."

* * *

She let go and turned to face her god.

The spark rose in her at last—not the blaze she'd carried before the splitting rite, but enough. Six threads came willingly when she called: Hale swearing in three languages and offering his anyway, Ives chanting the old terms, the others pressing their hands to Silas's shoulders, and the threads gathered home along her arm where the oathmark had burned twelve years ago and would burn for ten more minutes and then never again.

The mark flared white.

*Bearwater,* said Veyra, getting her name wrong on purpose, the way she had the day they met, twenty years and two lifetimes ago. *LAST BRIGHT THING. ARE YOU CERTAIN?*

"No," said Silas Brightwater, and walked into the light.

The light closed over her like the sea.
