## 20. The Last Brightwater Prayer

The light came up out of the Saltfen like a tide that had forgotten which way water was supposed to run.

Silas stood at the edge of it with her boots sinking into salt-crusted mud and her heart doing something she hadn't let it do in twelve years: breaking open. Behind her—far behind her, past the shattered gates of the resonance engines, past Mother Ives still holding a warding prayer against the cathedral's collapse like a woman holding shut a door in a storm—Tamsin was screaming her name.

She didn't turn around. If she turned around, she would stop walking, and if she stopped walking, everything they had bled for on this cursed ground would curdle into nothing.

*Veyra,* she thought, and for the first time since the Saltfen had gone silent mid-prayer, twelve years and a lifetime ago, the name didn't taste like ash.

It tasted like an answer.

---

The god rose out of the battlefield the way grief rises out of a body that has held it too long—all at once, and ruinous, and impossible to contain. Silas had seen Veyra once before, properly seen her, the day the company swore their oaths on the fen's edge: a figure made of dawn-light and sea-spray, tall as a lighthouse, kind as a hand on a fevered brow. That was what she expected to see now.

That was not what she saw.

What rose from the ruptured engines was vast and wrong-edged, a shape that kept trying to be a woman and couldn't quite remember how. Light poured off her in sheets, and where it touched the ground the old battle-marks flared—the rusted spearheads, the half-buried shields, the bones of three armies that had died here believing their gods would catch them. The air screamed. Not metaphorically. The air itself made a sound like a struck bell that would not stop ringing, and Silas felt it in her teeth, in the oathmark that no longer sat quietly under her skin but blazed up her arm like a brand fresh from the forge.

*WHO,* said the god, and the word went through Silas like a spear through wet canvas, *HAS WAKENED ME TO A WORLD STILL AT WAR.*

"You were never woken," Silas said. Her voice came out small and human and hers. "You were siphoned. The Choir built machines to drink you, one drop at a time, for twelve years. And when we broke them—" she gestured at the smoking wreckage, at Ives on her knees at the gate, at Hale limping free of his chains with Tamsin's knife still in his fist, "—you came up all at once. Like blood from a cut vein."

The great head turned toward her. There were no eyes in it, exactly. There were two places where eyes should have been, and looking into them was like looking into the moment before a wave breaks.

*BRIGHTWATER.* The name landed soft, almost wondering. *MY LAST BEARER. MY LAST OATH. I FELT YOU DIE HERE ONCE.*

"I felt me die here once too," Silas said. "Took years to be sure I hadn't."

Behind her, footsteps in the mud—running, stumbling, refusing to stop. She knew that rhythm. She'd know it blind, deaf, dead. *Don't*, she thought desperately. *Tamsin, don't.*

But Tamsin had never once listened to her when it mattered, and that, Silas suspected, was the whole reason either of them was still alive.

---

*Tamsin reached her first, mud to her knees, hair torn loose, one eye swelling shut where a piece of engine-casing had caught her during the assault. She grabbed Silas's arm—the marked arm—and the contact sent light skittering between them like static, and she didn't even flinch.*

*"Whatever you're about to do," Tamsin gasped, "I saw it. Three months ago, in the vision they cast me out for. You walk into the light and you don't walk back out."*

*"I know."*

*"Then don't—"*

*"Tamsin."* Silas turned then, finally, because there was no more use pretending. She took Tamsin's face in both hands. Her palms were shaking. Twelve years of mercenary discipline, and her hands shook like a girl's. *"Look at her. Really look. That isn't my god anymore—that's a wound wearing her shape. If she comes fully awake like this, furious, half-mad from being drunk on for a decade, every god who imprisoned her will come down out of their high places to finish the job. And they won't be careful this time. They'll burn the world down to get to her, and us standing in front of her."*

*"Ives said the rite could split the spark—"*

*"Ives's rite frayed me to threads. It bought us hours, not answers. There's only one anchor strong enough left, and it's standing right here asking you to let go of her arm."*

*Tamsin's grip tightened. Her knuckles went white. "There has to be another way. There's always—I found you, Silas. I ran across half the country on a rumor and a hymn and I found you, and you're telling me the ending is the ending I saw?"*

*"No," Silas said gently. "The ending you saw was me dying at the altar. That's not what this is."*

*And it wasn't. That was the miracle of it, the terrible arithmetic of it. Dying was easy. Soldiers did it every day. What Silas was about to do was harder than dying, and she understood that now, standing in the shadow of her god's grief: she was going to live. Mortal. Unmarked. Deaf to the divine for the rest of her days. She was going to give away the last bright thing in her blood and walk out of this light ordinary, and spend whatever years remained learning to be a person instead of a weapon.*

*She had spent so long afraid of silence. She had built her whole careful invisible life around the fear of it. But silence wasn't the thing she'd feared after all. Silence was just a room. What mattered was who was in it with you.*

---

*YOU INTEND THE BINDING,* Veyra said. It was not a question. Gods, apparently, could read intent the way Silas read weather. *THE OTHERS SEALED ME WITH CHAINS AND CALLED IT PEACE. YOU WOULD SEAL ME WITH GIFT AND CALL IT SLEEP. WHY SHOULD I ACCEPT EITHER?*

"Because the chains were fear," Silas said, "and this is love, and you taught the company the difference on our first day of swearing. You stood on this fen and you told us: *a god bound by force will always be straining at the leash; a god bound by choice can rest.* You said it about oaths. You meant it about yourself."

*I WAS YOUNG THEN. ARROGANT. I REFUSED THEIR WAR AND THEY BURIED ME FOR IT.*

"And you've been down there counting the years and getting angrier, and anger is exactly what they wanted, because it proves them right." Silas stepped forward, one step, into the outer edge of the light. It didn't burn. It *knew* her—it poured into the mark on her arm like water finding a channel, and for one vertiginous second she felt all of Veyra at once: twelve years of dark, the slow horror of being drained, the desperate animal joy of hearing a bearer's voice again. She staggered. Tamsin caught her elbow and did not let go.

"I'm not offering you a cage," Silas said, steadying. "I'm offering you what you gave us. An oath. Freely sworn, freely kept. Sleep until the world deserves you. Wake—if you ever wake—to a choir that sings your name out of love instead of terror. I can't promise that day comes. But I can promise nobody will ever put engines in your light again."

The god was quiet. The bell-scream of the air softened by degrees, the way a storm decides.

*AND WHAT DO YOU LOSE, LITTLE BEARER?*

"Everything divine I have left. Which, after the splitting rite, is mostly scar tissue and stubbornness." Silas laughed—a cracked, real sound. "But you should know what I'm keeping. I'm keeping her." She squeezed Tamsin's hand without looking away from the light. "And a smuggler with bad knees. And a defrocked priest who swears worse than my old sergeant. And a city full of people who prayed to you last month without knowing your name, because something in them remembered kindness and reached for it. You didn't come back to nothing, Veyra. You came back to *us*. Let that be enough."

---

The light gathered.

Silas felt it gather in herself too—in the mark, in the marrow, in the place behind her sternum where the spark had lived since the day she swore her oath at nineteen and thought she'd been handed a purpose instead of a fuse. The god drew it up out of her the way dawn draws color out of a dark field, slowly, inevitably, and with each pulse Silas felt something go quiet in her that had been noisy for twelve years.

The fear went first. Then the hunger—the awful, unspoken hunger to hear a voice answer her again, any voice, even a mad one. Then, strangest of all, the loneliness. It lifted off her like fog burning off the fen, and underneath it she discovered she had never actually been alone; she had simply been refusing to count the people who were there.

*Silas.* Veyra's voice, and suddenly it was small—not small, but *sized for her*, the voice of the lighthouse-keeper rather than the storm. *Silas Brightwater. When they buried me, I believed the world had chosen war over mercy. I intended to prove it right.*

"I know."

*You are offering me the mercy I refused to believe in. From my own blood. From the last of my own company.* A pause, and in it Silas heard something she had waited twelve years to hear and had stopped letting herself want: her god, weeping. *I bless you, then. Not as a bearer. As a daughter of the fen. May your hands build what mine defended. May your silence be full. May everyone you call answer.*

The spark left her.

It didn't hurt. That was the part she would try, later, to explain to Ives and fail: it didn't hurt, it *emptied*, gently, like a tide going out, and what rushed in behind it was not darkness but plain morning—the smell of salt and mud, the weight of Tamsin's shoulder against hers, the distant sound of Hale laughing his broken laugh because he was alive and couldn't believe it.

Above her, the vast wounded shape folded inward, folding and folding, storm becoming woman becoming light becoming a single point of white—

—and then, softly, like a lamp carried down a stair, it went out.

The Saltfen was silent. Truly silent, for the first time in twelve years. No hum in the stones. No whisper in the mark. Silas looked down at her bare forearm, where the oathmark had been since she was nineteen, and found smooth unmarked skin, pale where the rest of her was sun-browned, like a door bricked over so neatly you'd never know a room had been there.

She felt nothing where the god had been.

She felt everything else, everywhere else, all at once.

"Tamsin," she said, and her voice cracked clean in half on the second syllable.

"I'm here." Tamsin's arms came around her, filthy and shaking and certain. "I'm here, I'm here, you absolute mule, I'm here."

Over Tamsin's shoulder, Silas saw the others coming across the mud—Hale leaning on a broken spear-shaft like a pilgrim's staff, grinning through the tears he'd deny to his grave; Mother Ives standing at last from her ruined warding, muttering a prayer that was, for once, purely thanksgiving. The sky over Correnholt, far off, was turning the ordinary gray-pink of a normal dawn. Somewhere in the city, bells were ringing, and for the first time in living memory nobody flinched at the sound.

Silas held on to Tamsin and breathed.

The gods were quiet now. All of them, forever, or at least for a good long age—and the silence where Veyra had been was not an absence. It was a room. And the room was full.

"Hale," she called, without letting go. "How many grandchildren do you have, and how many of them need teaching to fish?"

His laugh rolled across the dead battlefield like the first tide of a new sea. "All of them, Brightwater. Every last one. Starting tomorrow."

Starting tomorrow. She closed her eyes, and for the first time in twelve years, Silas Brightwater slept without dreaming of the dark.
