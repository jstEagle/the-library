## 21. Aftermath of Ash and Song

The silence was the first thing Tamsin noticed when she woke.

Not the cathedral's silence—that had been full of screaming and stone-dust and the wet sound of Merek turning his blade against his own. Not the silence after, either, when the resonance engines ruptured and Veyra's light poured out of the broken machinery like water from a split cask, filling every crack in the world for one impossible minute.

No. This silence was small and domestic and wrong: the absence of Silas's breathing beside her.

Tamsin sat up too fast and paid for it—her ribs flared where a falling rafter had caught her, and her head swam with the particular dizziness that came from three days of bad sleep and worse fear. The room resolved around her by degrees. A low-ceilinged chamber under Mother Ives's rebuilt chapel in the undercity, candlelight, the smell of rendered fat and dried yarrow. And there, on the cot across the narrow room—

Silas.

She lay on her side, blanket to her chin, dark hair spread on the pillow like something spilled. Her chest moved. Slowly, shallowly, but it moved, and Tamsin let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the moment Silas had walked into that rising light and hadn't walked back out of it.

Three days. Ives said three days. Hale had come down once to sit by the cot, gruff as ever, saying nothing useful, just being there the way old soldiers are. Tamsin had barely left. She'd slept in the chair, or tried to, waking every hour to check the rise and fall of that chest like a woman counting coins she couldn't afford to lose.

Now Silas's eyes were open.

Not looking at anything. Just open, fixed on the far wall, on the water-stain shaped like nothing at all, and Tamsin's heart went sideways before she remembered—Ives had warned her about this. *She'll wake slow,* the old priestess had said, wrapping Tamsin's burned hands. *What she gave away doesn't grow back. Some mornings the body forgets what's missing and grieves all over again.*

"Silas," Tamsin said softly. Then, louder, when the eyes didn't move: "Silas Brightwater."

Something shifted behind the grey-green of them. Focus arriving like a tide coming in.

"Tamsin." Her voice was rust and river-mud. "You're supposed to be resting."

"So are you."

"I've been resting three days. It's very boring." She started to push herself up and stopped halfway, arm trembling, and Tamsin crossed the room in four steps and got a shoulder under her before pride could argue. Together they arranged her against the wall and the headboard, and Silas breathed through the cost of it, jaw tight, until the shaking passed.

Then she looked at her own forearm.

Tamsin watched her look. Watched her turn the wrist over, once, twice, the way you'd check a pocket for keys you knew weren't there. The oathmark was gone—not scarred over, not faded, but *gone*, the skin smooth and ordinary as any dockworker's. Twelve years of hiding it under leather wraps, and now there was nothing to hide.

Silas stared at the bare arm for a long time.

"It's quiet," she said at last.

"Yes."

"Not sleeping-quiet. Not the twelve-years quiet. Just—" She flexed her fingers. "Quiet. Like a room after everyone's gone home." Her voice didn't break, exactly. It thinned, the way thread does before it parts. "I can't hear her, Tamsin. At all. Even asleep, even praying, which I apparently still do, because habits are bastards. There's nothing there."

"I know." Tamsin sat on the edge of the cot, careful of her ribs. "Ives says the bond didn't break. It completed. Like—" she groped for it, "—like a hymn ending. Not cut off. Finished."

"That's a very priestly way to describe losing your god."

"I've been hanging around defrocked clergy. It rubs off."

Silas huffed—almost a laugh—and closed her eyes. In the candlelight Tamsin could see everything the last weeks had written on her face: new lines at the corners, shadows beneath, the raw pink of healing burns along her jaw where Veyra's light had kissed her on the way out. She looked older. She looked lighter. Both things at once, impossibly.

"The city?" Silas asked.

"Burying its dead." Tamsin kept her voice level. "Forty-one at the cathedral. Eleven more from the patrol boats on the river. The Choir published the names yesterday—all of them, theirs and ours both, in the same list. First time they've ever done that."

"And Merek?"

"Inquisitor no longer. He resigned the title in front of the whole conclave—handed his seal to a junior clerk and said the arithmetic was wrong." Tamsin smiled despite herself. "Half the Choir wants him excommunicated for it. The other half wants him running the place. They compromised: he's reforming it instead. Coexistence, he calls it. Marked folk registered, not hunted. Prayers heard, not silenced. He stood on the cathedral steps and apologized to the city. Out loud. I don't think anyone knew an Inquisitor *could* do that."

Silas was quiet for a moment.

"He turned his blade on his own superiors to save you," she said. "I keep trying to fit that into the man who interrogated Hale. I can't make both men true."

"They're both true. That's what makes him worth being afraid of. Or not afraid of. I haven't decided." Tamsin reached out and took Silas's hand—the bare hand, the unmarked one—and Silas's fingers closed around hers with a grip that was weaker than it should have been and held on anyway. "Hale's home, by the way. His granddaughter wouldn't let go of him for an hour. He cried. He'll deny it. Ives has already started holding services again—out loud, with singing, in a room with windows. Half the undercity comes. Half the *upper* city, too, now. They stand in the back and pretend they're not listening."

"And the god?" Silas asked it carefully, the way you'd touch a burn to see how deep it goes. "Is she—is the sealing—"

"Holding. Ives checked the old rites' signs, such as they are. No surges. No answered prayers anywhere in the city since that day. Whatever you did, it worked. She's asleep, and this time nobody built engines to siphon her dreams." Tamsin hesitated. "There's talk, though. Pilgrims coming to the Saltfen. Leaving offerings at the altar. Little ones—salt, bread, river-flowers. They say a brightwater sleeps there."

"A what?"

"That's what they're calling you. Brightwaters. People who gave something up so the rest of us could keep what we have." Tamsin shrugged, embarrassed. "It's spreading. There's a song already. It's terrible. Hale's granddaughter made me learn it."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed—a real one, cracked and hoarse and startled out of her—and winced immediately at the cost, and Tamsin laughed too, and for a little while neither of them stopped, and the grief in the room had to wait its turn.

* * *

They buried the dead of Correnholt over six days, and Tamsin went to every grave.

It wasn't duty. It was something closer to penance she couldn't have named, except that Ives told her once, gently, that penance was just love wearing work clothes, and after that she stopped questioning it.

The Choir's dead went first—eleven patrolmen drowned in the river chase, their names read by clerks in gray robes while their families stood rigid with a grief that didn't know yet whether it was allowed to be angry. Then the forty-one from the cathedral, most of them Choir servants and wardens caught in the collapse, some of them prisoners who'd never gotten out of the resonance cells. Merek attended each burial personally. He didn't speak much. He carried dirt in his bare hands and put it in the graves himself, every single time, and Tamsin watched him do it and understood that whatever the man had been building in himself for twenty years had come down with the engines, and what he was laying in the ground was the last of it.

On the sixth day they buried the crew's dead.

Only two. Tamsin counted herself lucky and hated herself for counting. Wren, the boy who'd run the lamp-lines on the barge, killed in the assault's first minutes by a warden's crossbow he never saw. And old Sefa, Ives's deacon, who'd held the cathedral doors with borrowed prayer until the doors came down on top of her.

They buried them on the hillside above the river, where you could see the water. Hale dug the graves himself, refusing help, his shoulders working like a man punishing the earth. When it was done he stood at the head of Wren's grave with his cap in his fists and said, "He was fast and he was funny and he was seventeen, and none of those things should have been spent here," and then he put the cap back on and didn't say anything else, and nobody asked him to.

Tamsin sang.

She didn't plan it. The hole opened up the way it always did—at the amen, in the space where the response should come—and before she could stop herself the hymn was out of her, the one she'd used on the river to blind the patrol boats, except slower now, stripped of power, just a girl's voice carrying over two mounds of fresh-turned earth. Nobody joined in. Everybody listened. When it ended, Ives pressed something into her hand: a small clay lamp, plain, unfired.

"For the house," the old woman said. "A light you lit yourself means more than one you prayed for. That's the whole heresy, if you're wondering. That's all of it."

That night Tamsin set the lamp on the sill of the room where Silas slept, and lit it, and watched the flame steady itself, and thought: *this is what we build instead.*

* * *

Silas walked on the ninth day.

Tamsin found her in the chapel yard at dawn, standing among Ives's winter kale beds in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, looking up at the slot of gray sky between the undercity's roofs like a woman reading weather.

"You'll catch your death," Tamsin said.

"Been closer to it lately. It hardly counts." But Silas took the offered arm anyway, and they walked—slowly, past the chapel, up the stair that climbed toward the market district, because Silas said she wanted to see it and Tamsin didn't ask why, because she already knew.

Correnholt in the morning was a city pretending very hard to be normal, and mostly succeeding. Carts in the streets. Fish off the boats. Children running messages for coins. But everywhere there were the marks of that week: scaffolded walls, scorch stains no one had scrubbed yet, and—every few streets—a small shrine. Not the Choir's austere alcoves. Homemade things. Ribbons on a doorpost. A dish of salt on a step. A child's wooden bird nailed above a lintel. Offerings to nobody in particular, or to everybody, or to the idea that someone had been watching after all, and had chosen, at the end, to be kind about it.

At the mouth of the gray market, Silas stopped.

Her stall was gone. Of course it was—confiscated in the second week, when the searches began, when her life had burned like paper. The spot was occupied now by a cobbler with loud opinions about leather oil. But Silas stood there anyway, in the crowd's edge, looking at the place where twelve years of careful invisibility had lived, and her face did something complicated and settled.

"I used to stand right there," she said, "and watch the fisherman's widow count out coppers she couldn't spare, and lie to her about what the charm would do, and hate myself a little more each time. And I told myself it was survival. And it was. But underneath it—" she shook her head. "Underneath it I was waiting. Twelve years of waiting for her voice to come back and tell me what I was for. You know what I hear now? When I pray?"

"No."

"Nothing. Same as everyone else." She turned to Tamsin, and there it was—that thing in her eyes that Tamsin had been cataloguing since the first night on the barge, sharpened now by fire and loss into something with edges. "And it turns out that was the answer. We were always going to have to decide for ourselves. She just finally stopped letting me pretend otherwise."

"Silas—"

"I'm all right." And strange to say, she seemed to mean it. "Come on. I want bread. Real bread, from a real oven, bought with money I intend to earn honestly, doing something that helps people and lies to no one."

"What, exactly, is that?"

"No idea yet. That's the best part." She was smiling as she said it, tired and true, and Tamsin felt her chest ache with a feeling too large for the word *love* and refused to find a better one.

They bought bread. They ate it on the harbor wall, feet dangling, watching the fishing fleet come in. Somewhere behind them a street singer had picked up the Brightwater song, the terrible one, and was murdering it cheerfully for coppers, and Silas listened to the whole verse about her own imagined death and pronounced it "dramatically inaccurate" and "catchy as rot," and Tamsin laughed until her ribs hurt, and it was, absurdly, a good morning.

* * *

Merek came to the chapel at dusk on the eleventh day.

He came alone and unarmed, which in Correnholt was practically a confession of weakness, and Ives met him in the yard with her arms folded and her opinion of him worn openly on her face. Tamsin watched from the doorway, Silas leaning on her shoulder inside.

"Inquisitor," Ives said.

"Not anymore. Just Merek. I've brought documents." He held up a satchel. "Charters. Registrations. The new framework—it needs signatures from people the old Choir wronged, or it's just words on vellum. Your congregation qualifies. So do you, Mother Ives, though I suspect you'll enjoy telling me no."

"I will."

"I assumed." Something almost like warmth crossed his face, quickly mastered. "And I need to speak with Silas Brightwater. About the Saltfen. There are questions the conclave keeps asking that only she can close."

"She's resting."

"Merek." Silas's voice, from the doorway. He turned, and Tamsin felt her stiffen slightly—old reflexes, old fears—but Silas stepped forward into the yard, bare-armed in the cold, and faced him.

For a long moment the two of them just looked at each other: the mercenary who'd given away a god and the inquisitor who'd spent his life making sure gods stayed dead, standing in a heretic's garden between rows of winter kale.

"You should know," Merek said quietly, "that my superiors wanted you executed. Before the engines broke. Afterward, they wanted you canonized. I told them both no. I told them you were neither a criminal nor a saint—you were a soldier who did her duty, and the difference matters, because saints are excuses and soldiers are examples." He bowed his head slightly, and it was the hardest thing Tamsin had ever seen a proud man do. "I lost my family to a god's war. I built twenty years of certainty on that grief. You broke it in one afternoon. I haven't decided if I thank you yet. But the city stands, and my arithmetic includes love now, however poorly I calculate it, and that began with what you did. So. Thank you. Provisionally."

Silas studied him.

"When the conclave asks its questions," she said, "tell them this: the gods aren't coming back, and that's not a tragedy. What's left of the divine is sleeping, and we put it there ourselves, together, which means we can also choose—every day, freely—to leave it there. Tell them the miracle isn't that a god answered. It's that people did." She glanced back through the doorway, briefly, at Tamsin. "Tell them I checked."

Merek's mouth twitched—the ghost of a smile, quickly disciplined. "They'll want that in writing."

"They can want a lot of things."

* * *

That night, Tamsin woke to find Silas sitting up in bed, awake, watching the little clay lamp on the sill.

Tamsin slid over until their shoulders touched. For a while neither spoke. Outside, the undercity murmured its nighttime murmur—footsteps, a drunk singing, a dog with ambitions—and the lamp flame leaned and steadied, leaned and steadied.

"Do you miss it?" Tamsin asked at last. "Really. Not the polite answer."

Silas was quiet long enough that Tamsin thought she wouldn't answer.

"Every day," she said finally. "Some part of me will always be standing at that altar reaching for her voice. That doesn't heal. It just gets company." She turned her head, and in the lamplight her eyes were clear and unhurried. "But here's what I know that I didn't know at twenty-two with a mark on my arm. When I called for Veyra, sometimes she answered. When I call for you—" she lifted their joined hands, laced fingers, held them up in the small warm light— "you answer. Every time. Before I finish asking. Do you understand what that's worth? Do you understand that I traded a maybe for a certainty, and got the better bargain?"

Tamsin's throat closed. She managed, eventually: "Even when I'm wrong?"

"*Especially* when you're wrong. Wrong is how I know you're real. Gods are always right and it's unbearable."

Tamsin laughed wetly and put her forehead against Silas's shoulder, and Silas's cheek came down on her hair, and they sat like that while the lamp burned and the city murmured and the divine slept its long sealed sleep beneath the Saltfen, undisturbed, unneeded.

Later, half-asleep, Silas murmured something Tamsin almost missed.

"Hmm?"

"I said—" a yawn— "Hale wants us at the market at dawn. Says he's found us a stall. Says we're going to sell salt and bread, and that if I try to give anything away free he'll dock my wages." Another yawn. "Apparently I have wages now. Apparently I have a *stall*. Twelve years a mercenary, and my reward is retail."

Tamsin smiled into the dark. "Will you be good at it?"

"I'll be terrible at it. You'll do the sums." Silas's breathing was already lengthening toward sleep. "That's rather the point of all this, isn't it. Nobody carries it alone anymore."

Tamsin lay awake a while longer, listening to her breathe—counting the rise and fall one last time out of habit, and then, deliberately, letting the habit go. The lamp guttered low. Somewhere beyond the walls, dawn was assembling itself over a city of the living, and tomorrow there would be bread to sell and a stall to build and a family that answered when she called.

She blew out the light, and in the dark she reached for Silas's hand, and found it already reaching for hers.
