## 22. Choir and Salt

The morning bell in Correnholt rang seven times, and for the first time in a year, Silas Brightwater was awake to hear all of them.

She counted them out of habit more than need. The old habit had been different—counting the bells to know how long she had before the gray market wardens made their rounds, before she had to fold up her table of charms and melt into the fish-crowd like smoke. Now she counted because Tamsin slept through anything short of a fire, and if Silas didn't rise first, they'd lose the good corner by the fountain to the candle-seller who got there at dawn with the ruthless punctuality of a woman who had once run contraband candles through three blockades.

Silas dressed in the dark. Her hands knew the work: wool stockings, the good boots, the long apron with the deep pockets. She paused at the washbasin and looked at her left forearm in the gray light.

Bare skin. Nothing else. No mark, no glow, no thread of gold beneath the surface. A year, and she still checked. Maybe she'd check forever. Some mornings it felt like grief; most mornings it felt like taking off armor after a long march—the ache of absence, yes, but also the lightness underneath.

She rolled down her sleeve and went downstairs.

The stall was hers now—not rented, *hers*, which in Correnholt meant Hale owned half of it on paper and pretended otherwise. It sat on Sable Row between a mender of kettles and a woman who sold songbirds she refused to keep caged overnight, so that every dawn began with a small riot of wings. Silas hauled out the crates one by one: salt in coarse sacks from the Saltfen flats themselves—Hale's joke, and then Hale's genuine enterprise, because the man could smell a market where other men smelled only a battlefield—and bread from Mother Ives's ovens, still warm enough that steam curled off the loaves when Silas stacked them in wicker.

Salt and bread. The two oldest prayers there were. Feed me. Preserve me. She'd sold luck once, sold protection, sold little knotted charms that did nothing and cost a widow her winter coin. She wasn't proud of those years, though she understood them. When your god goes silent, you either stop believing in gods or you start believing in the small mercies people will pay for.

Now she sold salt, and people paid an honest price for it, and nobody crossed the square to avoid her eyes.

* * *

By mid-morning Sable Row was loud the way only Correnholt could be loud—a working racket, gulls and cartwheels and the kettle-minder arguing with a customer about whether dents were character or damage. Hale arrived with his grandchildren swarming around him like pilot fish, the twins first, then the baby riding his shoulders with the serene entitlement of an empress.

"Uncle Brannoc," Silas said, "your granddaughter is eating my display."

"She's sampling," Hale said gravely, setting the child down among the loaves. "There's a difference. In trade law, I mean. Ask any smuggler." He surveyed the stall with his hands on his hips, this enormous gray-bearded man who had once stared down an Inquisitor in a cell and given away nothing but jokes. He still had a faint scar at his temple from the Choir's questions. He wore it like a medal he declined to explain.

"How's the river?" Silas asked.

"High and honest." He grinned. "First time in my life I've been able to say that about either."

That was the strange arithmetic of the year since the cathedral: the Choir reformed rather than dissolved, Merek at its head, and the smuggling routes that used to carry relics and fugitives now carried mostly salt and letters and, once memorably, forty barrels of festival wine that no one asked too many questions about. Old habits. Hale said a man should never waste a talent.

The twins—Pella and dark-eyed Sorrel—had appointed themselves Silas's sales force, a job they performed with the merciless efficiency of children who had learned arithmetic from a smuggler. Pella announced prices. Sorrel collected coins and bit each one, which was theater, since no one had counterfeited in Correnholt since the Choir stopped burning people and started listening to them.

"Two loaves," said a voice, "and whatever the small tyrants recommend."

Tamsin came around the corner of the stall with her hood back and her hair escaping its braid in every direction, ink on her fingers—she still wrote letters for the harbor folk, petitions and love notes, sometimes both in the same letter—and a basket over her arm containing, Silas knew without looking, something Tamsin had bought for her and would pretend was practical.

"You're late," Silas said.

"I'm exactly on time. You're early. You're always early. You wake before the bells like the city owes you money." Tamsin leaned across the salt sacks and kissed her, unhurried, in front of the whole row, and Pella made a theatrical gagging sound and Sorrel charged them a loaf for the privilege of witnessing.

A year ago Silas had lain on the cathedral steps with Veyra's last light going out of her like heat leaving iron, deaf to everything except one heartbeat above her own—Tamsin's, furious, refusing to stop. She had traded the voice of a god for that heartbeat. There were nights she still woke reaching for the silence where Veyra had lived in her, the vast warm attention of being *known* by something older than mountains.

And there were mornings like this one, when a runaway acolyte stole her bread and her breath in the same motion, and Silas thought: *fair trade. Fair trade, and I'd make it again before the words finished leaving my mouth.*

* * *

Mother Ives came at noon, as she always did, walking the Row with her congregation's troubles folded under her arm in the form of ledgers. The rebuilt congregation met in a sailmaker's loft, no pews, no resonance engines, no enforced silence—just singing, which the new Choir had ruled was legal as long as it stayed on key, a ruling Ives publicly thanked them for and privately called "the least inspiring miracle of our age."

She bought nothing and never had. She inspected the bread instead, tore a corner of a loaf, chewed it slowly, and pronounced judgment.

"Too much faith in the yeast," she said. "Which is to say it's perfect. Put aside four for tonight."

"Who's feeding tonight?"

"The loft. The Harrow family got their boat back—compensation, finally, from the Choir treasury, took Merek a year to shake it loose. They're singing about it whether anyone wants them to or not." Ives's eyes were bright and tired, the way they always were now; she had taken on the work of three defrocked priests and made it look like a hobby. "You'll come? You always say you'll come and then you stand in the doorway like a woman guarding a gate."

"I stand in doorways," Silas said, "because I'm tall."

"You stand in doorways because for twelve years standing in open rooms could get you killed, and no one has told your body the war is over." Ives said it kindly, without pity, the way she said everything. Then she patted the salt sacks. "You know what I love about you, Brightwater? Every prayer has an answer. Yours just turned out to be bread."

* * *

It was late afternoon when the stranger came.

He wasn't remarkable. Gray cloak, road dust, the careful walk of a man who'd walked a long way and wasn't sure of his welcome. He stood at the edge of the stall while Sorrel finished cheating a fishwife out of a fair price for two pounds of coarse salt, and he waited, and when Silas looked up he flinched—just slightly, the way people still sometimes did when they recognized her face from the broadsheets. THE BRIGHTWATER OF CORRENHOLT, one had called her, before she'd made Hale buy up every copy in the district and burn them in his stove.

"You're her," the man said. "The one the god spoke to."

"I'm the one who sells salt," Silas said. "How many loaves?"

He didn't answer right away. His eyes went to her sleeve, to the arm beneath it, and she let him look. There was nothing to find.

"They say you gave it up," he said. "Your spark. That you put the god back to sleep with it. They say—" he hesitated, and now she heard it, under the road dust and the caution: hunger. The particular hunger of someone who had prayed into silence and wanted to know if silence was all there was. "They say you can still hear her. That she lives in you like an ember."

"The gods are quiet," Silas said.

Something in his face fell, and she hated being the cause of it, and knew she couldn't be anything else. That was the truth she'd paid everything to learn: there was no ember. What she had given, she had given entirely, freely, and Veyra—wounded, vast, understanding at the last—had blessed her and gone down into the deep places of the world like a tide going out, and the tide would not come back in. Not for her. Not for anyone. That had been the point.

But the man was still standing there with his hunger showing, and Tamsin had gone still at the far end of the stall, watching, and Silas found she had more than one true thing to say after all.

"But the people aren't."

The stranger blinked.

"Listen." She tipped her chin toward the Row, toward the noise of it. The kettle-minder had won his argument and was hammering out dents in celebration. The songbird woman was teaching a sparrow to take crumbs from Pella's hand. Somewhere behind the fish-stalls, the Harrow family was already practicing the song they'd inflict on the sailmaker's loft tonight, badly, joyfully. Down at the harbor, the new Choir's patrol boats went by with their lanterns unlit, their horns silent, doing nothing more menacing than counting seals. "A year ago this city was afraid of its own voice. Now listen to it. Nobody answered their prayers. They answered each other's. Turns out that was the answer all along—it just needed the quiet to hear itself."

The stranger stood a long moment. Then he set a coin on the boards—too much, Silas noted—and took a loaf and a sack of salt, and held them like something received rather than purchased.

"My daughter's sick," he said quietly. "Fever. The physicians are done. I walked nine days because they said—" his jaw worked. "They said the Brightwater once healed a smuggler's grandchild with a spoken prayer."

"That was instinct and dumb luck," Silas said, "and I couldn't do it again if you held a knife to me. But Mother Ives holds clinic at the sailmaker's loft on Bell Street, every evening, and she knows more medicine than any god I've met. Tell her Silas sent you and she'll pretend to be annoyed and then she won't leave your daughter's side till the fever breaks or she does." She slid his coin back across the boards. "Nine days is a long walk. Don't make it longer than it has to be."

He looked at the coin, at her bare wrist, at her face. Whatever he'd come looking for, he hadn't found it—but maybe he'd found the next thing down, which was a person pointing him toward help with no price attached, and maybe that was worth nine days of road.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it, and went.

Tamsin appeared at her shoulder. "You know Ives will make you sing tonight now. That's how she repays referrals. Cruelly."

"Worth it."

"Mm." Tamsin hooked her chin over Silas's shoulder, arms coming around her waist, and for a moment they just watched the Row together—the twins herding customers, Hale haggling with a man he'd known for thirty years over a price they'd agreed on yesterday, the light going long and gold across the salt sacks. "You okay? After ones like him?"

Silas considered it honestly, the way she'd learned to. "I keep waiting for it to hurt worse than it does. Missing her. And then some morning I'm stacking bread and I realize an hour's gone by and I haven't thought about it at all, and I feel like a traitor." She turned her head, kissed Tamsin's temple. "Ives says that's just what healing is. Forgetting to bleed."

"And the rest of it?" Tamsin's voice was light, but her fingers weren't; they pressed against Silas's ribs like she could hold her to the world by grip alone. Sometimes, in the dark, Tamsin still asked the question her vision had taught her to dread—that Silas regretted, that she'd do it differently, that the altar was waiting in every dream. "No regrets? None at all?"

"Tamsin." Silas covered her hand with her own. "I gave up the voice of a god and in exchange I get to hear you complain about my baking, my posture, and my taste in hats, every day, until we're old. No god ever loved me as specifically as that."

Tamsin laughed—wetly, a little—and hid her face against Silas's back, and Silas let her, and the bells rang six o'clock over Correnholt, and Silas counted every one.

* * *

They closed the stall at dusk. The twins carried crates with the staggering dignity of dockworkers; Hale locked the strongbox and pronounced the day's profit "respectable, which is an insult, so tomorrow we'll do better." The lamps came along Sable Row one by one, and the songbirds settled into their cages for the night with small contented mutters, and Silas stood a moment in the emptying square with her hands full of nothing, breathing salt air.

Somewhere out past the harbor bar, under cold water and older stone, something vast slept the sleep Veyra had chosen—bound not by chains now but by a gift, kept not by fear but by a blessing, quiet at last, and at peace. Silas sent the thought down to her the way she did most evenings, wordless, less a prayer than a letter to a comrade who'd survived the war: *Still here. Still standing. The company's good.*

Nothing answered. Nothing needed to.

"Are you coming?" Tamsin called from the mouth of the Row, haloed in lamplight, impatient, beloved. "They'll start without us, and Ives will blame you, and she'll be right."

Silas smiled, shouldered the last crate, and went to her.

Down the Row, the city sang.
