## 1. The Ledger of Small Sins

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle by the time Dominic Quintana reached the duchess's grief.

He noted it the way he noted everything: in a fine, level hand, no flourishes, no sentiment. *Duchess Amaranthe Vey, one grief, maternal, eleven years old, weight heavy as riverstone.* The guild bought it from her in the spring, when she'd decided that mourning her dead son was interfering with her remarriage to a man who found tears vulgar. She'd wept for an afternoon at the Saltwork Guild's counting table while Odile's appraisers held the memory up to the light like a jewel, and then she'd stood, dry-eyed and lighter, and thanked them.

Dominic entered the figure. Balanced the column. Moved on.

Around him the back room of the guildhouse settled into its night noises—the tick of the ledger-room clock that ran slow because nobody had ever bothered to steal the hours to wind it, the drip of harbor water somewhere behind the plaster, the creak of old salt-rotted beams remembering the weight they used to carry. Candlelight moved across the shelves of bottled memories that lined the walls: thousands of little glass vessels, each stoppered with wax, each holding something somebody had once been unable or unwilling to keep.

Years, mostly. Years were the currency of the trade—clean, divisible, easy to price. But the small things came through too, the things people sold out of desperation or spite or simple weariness. First kisses. The smell of a specific kitchen on a specific morning. The name of someone you'd sworn never to forget. A fisherman's terror of drowning, which he'd sold so he could go back out on the water. A soldier's guilt, purchased at a discount because it was water-damaged—he'd been drinking when he sold it.

Dominic catalogued them all. That was his work, his particular genius, the thing Odile kept him for. Anyone could carry a bottle. It took a thief like Dominic to *enter* a memory—to step inside it, walk its length, learn its true weight—and come out again without leaving anything of himself behind.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole profession, boiled down to one sentence: get in, get out, take nothing with you.

Most thieves failed at the last part. They came out of other people's memories carrying crumbs—a stray affection here, a borrowed fear there—until their own selves got crowded out and they became patchwork people, walking around wearing strangers' feelings like coats they couldn't take off. Dominic had watched three of Odile's appraisers go that way. One of them now lived in a chair by the harbor and greeted everyone as her son.

So Dominic had built rules. Rules were load-bearing stone; you didn't move them.

Rule one: enter clean, exit clean. Nothing stays.

Rule two: never hold a memory longer than the work requires.

Rule three—

Rule three was the door.

He paused over the ledger, quill hovering, and made himself not think about rule three. Thinking about it was how you ended up opening it, and he did not open it. In thirty-one years of life and seventeen years of theft, he had opened it exactly zero times, and he intended to keep that record intact until the day some luckier arrangement than death carried him off.

But the candle was low, and low candles made him honest, and honesty made the door visible again, standing there at the back of his mind where he'd bricked it in long ago. He could feel it the way you feel a tooth you've decided not to bite with. Behind it was a single memory—one of his own, the only thing he'd ever stolen from himself—and he had done it so long ago that he no longer remembered what was behind it. That was the design. That was the point. He'd taken the key, walked away, and swallowed the shape of the key along with everything else.

A man who keeps a door locked against himself is a man who knows what he'd find if he looked.

Dominic dipped his quill and wrote the night's final entry: *Inventory reconciled. Discrepancy: none.*

There was always a discrepancy. There had been a discrepancy every quarter for seventeen years, small enough to hide in the rounding, invisible unless you knew the ledgers the way only he knew them—which was to say, completely. A year here, shaved thin. A kiss there, entered at half-weight. It wasn't greed. He didn't sell the skim, didn't spend it, didn't even look at it. He simply took it, because taking it proved he still could, and because a thief who stops stealing is just a clerk, and clerks get replaced.

Also—and this he admitted to no one, least of all the ledger—also because the guild owed him. Not in coin. In something older. He'd been paid once, long ago, in a way he couldn't remember being paid, and some part of him, the part behind the door, understood that the books had never balanced since.

* * *

The bell at the salt door rang twice past midnight.

Nobody used the salt door at night except couriers, smugglers, and people running from something, and the sound it made—two flat iron knocks, no handle, no voice—told Dominic which kind this was before he'd finished climbing the stairs. He blew out the candle, set the ledger under the false bottom of the ink cabinet, and went down through the dark with the ease of a man who had memorized every stair in the building precisely so he would never need light.

He lifted the bar. Opened the door six inches.

The courier was already falling toward him.

Dominic caught him—an armful of wet wool and blood, heavier than he looked, the way dying men are always heavier—and hauled him inside and barred the door behind them, all in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Habit. The guild trained you fast or it buried you in the foundation, and Dominic had been trained at nine.

"Easy," he said. "You're at the Saltwork Guild. If you've come to trade, you're late—we're closed."

The courier laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up blood, black in the dark. "Not—" He grabbed Dominic's collar with a fist that shook. His other hand pressed something against Dominic's chest: a scroll case, sealed in gray wax stamped with a sigil Dominic didn't recognize—a mountain, split down the middle. "Take it."

"I don't take deliveries off the books."

"You'll want this one." The courier's eyes were wide and fixed and terrible, the eyes of a man who has seen the thing he's dying of. "Mount Verity. The court beneath. They have my soul, thief—they have it *filed*. I sold it as collateral and I went to buy it back and they wouldn't release it, they said the debt wasn't mine alone, they said—" His grip tightened. "They said the original payment was made years ago. A child's memories. Sealed in the vault. You know whose."

Something cold walked up Dominic's spine and sat down.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"No," the courier whispered. "Of course you don't." He smiled then, and it was the worst thing Dominic had seen all year, because it was pitying. "That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

The courier pressed the scroll case into Dominic's hands and folded the thief's fingers shut around it with both of his own bloody ones, like a priest administering something.

"The Regent keeps what's mine," he said. "And yours. And she keeps the map to prove she can."

Then his eyes rolled back, and his weight changed—the specific, unmistakable change, the moment the tenant leaves the house—and Dominic Quintana sat on the floor of the Saltwork Guild's receiving room holding a dead stranger's message and listening to his own heart behave very badly indeed.

Outside, faint but coming closer, boots on cobblestones. More than two pairs. Moving with the unhurried coordination of guild enforcers who had been told where to be.

Dominic had perhaps four minutes.

He did what he was made to do. He didn't run—running leaves tracks, and tracks leave questions, and questions leave Odile asking them. He arranged the courier decently, closed the man's eyes, wiped his own prints from the scroll case with a rag and burned the rag in the cold hearth. Then he sat down in the dark, put his back against the wall, cracked open the seal with his thumbnail, and unrolled just enough of the map to see what he was holding.

Ink on vellum. Fine work. Stairways drawn in cross-section, descending and descending beneath Mount Verity into chambers marked in a hand that made his skin crawl—not because it was unfamiliar, but because it was *precise*, the hand of a professional archivist, the kind of hand that files souls.

And beside one deep chamber, small and neat, a notation:

*Vault. Collateral, assorted. Original payment: childhood, male, age seven. Paid in full.*

Age seven.

The cold thing on his spine spread outward, into his chest, into his hands. He thought of the door. The bricked-over door at the back of his mind, locked since before he could remember locking it, the one memory he had ever stolen from himself, and he thought—with a clarity that felt less like thinking and more like falling—*I was seven when Odile took me in.*

Boots on the stones outside. Thirty seconds, maybe less.

There was no time to hide a scroll case anywhere a searcher wouldn't look. Every cache in the guildhouse was known; caches were the first thing enforcers learned. Which left only one hiding place in the world that no thief would ever search, because no thief was stupid enough to store contraband there.

Dominic Quintana broke rule one.

He laid the map open across his mind, weighted it down with intent, and stole it from himself—folded the vellum into the dark behind his own eyes, into the vaulted place where he kept nothing, where he had always kept nothing, right beside the door he never opens. Then he sealed it, the way he sealed everything: clean, complete, no seam showing. Even he would not know it was there. Especially he would not know it was there.

He was re-barred and composed and reaching for the courier's body to check the pockets—professional diligence, a reason to be found kneeling over the corpse—when the salt door shuddered under the first knock.

Two flat iron strikes.

Then a voice through the wood, pleasant and cold as harbor water: "Quintana. Open the door. We know he came in."

Dominic rose, smoothed his coat, and became—for the benefit of whoever waited beyond—exactly what he had spent seventeen years becoming: a forgettable man in a forgettable room, holding a ledger full of small sins, with no memory of anything worth stealing.

Even, if the night demanded it, from himself.

He lifted the bar.
