## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Door

The guild's back room smelled of tallow and brine, the way everything in Saltreach smelled eventually. Dominic Quintana sat alone at the long table with three candles burning down to their wicks and the night's ledger open before him, entering the day's takings in a hand so small and even it might have been printed.

He liked this hour best. The enforcers had gone home to their beds or their bottles, Odile was shut behind her door on the upper floor doing whatever Guildmasters did in the dark, and the vaults below were locked and humming faintly, like a cellar full of sleeping wasps. For a few hours nobody wanted anything from him, and he wanted nothing from anybody. A forgettable man in a forgettable room. It was the closest thing to peace the world had ever offered him.

The entry before him read: *One (1) summer afternoon, age six, belonging to Marseau, Isabet—sold to the Fenwick estate for the settling of debts.* Below that: *One (1) first kiss, unclaimed, provenance uncertain—held in inventory.* Below that, a line he'd written twice over the years and never gotten used to: *One (1) grief, duchess-grade, still wet.*

That one had come in that morning. The Duchess of Vail had sold her mourning—not her dead son, never that, but the eleven years of grieving him, the whole gray weight of it—and she had walked out of the guild lighter than air, smiling, patting Dominic's hand as if he'd pulled a tooth. He'd catalogued the memory himself, because grief that size needed careful handling, and when he pressed his palm to the wax seal of its jar he had felt it through the glass like heat off a stove.

He did not feel other people's memories. That was the rule he'd built his life on. He took them, he shelved them, he forgot them by morning. A thief who kept nothing was a thief who could pass through any door.

Except the one.

His eyes went, as they always did at this hour, to the small iron-banded box set into the wall behind the third candle. It looked like a strongbox and was not. It held a single memory, sealed in black wax, unlabeled. His own. Or what remained of one. He had taken it from himself years ago—theft being the only privacy he trusted—and he had never once opened the box since.

*Door you don't open,* he told himself, the way he told himself every night. *Doors you don't open keep houses standing.*

He dipped his pen.

That was when the door came apart.

Not opened—came apart, hinges shrieking, wood splitting at the jamb as a body hit it from outside and kept going. Dominic was on his feet with his sleeve knocked through the inkwell before his mind caught up to his body, which had already done the arithmetic: one person, heavy, falling, dying.

A man lay in the doorway with the harbor's fog rolling in around him. He was dressed for fast travel and bad roads, dust to the knee, and the front of him was dark and glistening. Blood, a lot of it, spreading on the salt-white floorboards in a shape Dominic would later think of, absurdly, as a map of its own.

"Bar the door," the man said. His voice was cracked gravel. "Behind me. They're behind me."

Dominic didn't move. Every instinct said run, say nothing, be no one—but the man's hand had already found his ankle, and it gripped with the terrible strength of someone spending the last coin in his purse.

"You're Quintana." Not a question. "The ledger-keeper. I know your name."

*Nobody knows my name.* That was the whole architecture of his survival. He felt the crack go through him like ice in a hull.

"You're mistaken," Dominic said. "I'm nobody."

"Yes," the courier said, and something that might have been laughter turned into coughing, red at the lips. "So was I. Once."

He dragged himself forward another foot. Up close Dominic could see the wound properly now—a thrust under the ribs, deep, angled upward by someone who knew where to put steel. Enforcers' work. Guild work, maybe. Or work that wanted to look like it.

"They took my soul," the man said. "Years ago. I signed a piece of paper in this room, do you understand? I was broke and my daughter was hungry and they paid well, they always pay well, and the paper said *collateral*, and collateral is such a gentle word for—" He stopped. Breathed. "It's held beneath Mount Verity. In the court there. The Regent keeps what's mine."

"The Regent," Dominic repeated, flat. He knew the word the way everyone in the trade knew it—a rumor with teeth, a buyer so far above the Saltwork Guild's ceiling that guild men spoke of it the way sailors spoke of the sea floor. Nobody sold to the court beneath Mount Verity directly. You went through too many hands. Your soul changed ledgers so many times that by the end no one could say whose name it sat under.

"I went to buy it back." The courier's mouth bent. "Fool. They wouldn't deal. So I ran, and they chased, and here I am, and here—" His hand left Dominic's ankle and went into his coat, and came out with something wrapped in oilcloth, stained dark now at one corner. "Here is how."

He pressed it into Dominic's hands.

Dominic tried to give it back. That was the truth he would carry afterward, in whatever self he ended up owning: he tried. "Take it to Odile. Take it anywhere but me. I keep books. I don't—"

"You keep *everything*," the courier said. "That's what they say about you. Down in the low rooms, among the couriers and the mules. There's a keeper who keeps all, who forgets nothing he touches and is touched by nothing he keeps. If anyone can carry this without it carrying him—" His breath whistled. "It has to be someone with clean hands. Yours are the cleanest dirty hands in Saltreach."

"That's not a compliment."

"No," the man agreed. "It's an epitaph. Mine, probably." He sagged back against the table leg. Under the blood and dust he was younger than his voice, forty maybe, with a stubble coming in gray and a burn scar on the back of his wrist shaped like a crescent moon. "Her name is Wren," he said. "My daughter. She doesn't know what I sold. When I didn't come home she'll think—" The sentence failed him. He looked up at Dominic with the ferocious clarity of the nearly dead. "Tell her nothing. Better a father who vanished than a father who gambled her soul and lost. Swear it."

"I don't swear things," Dominic said. "Swearing is collateral."

The courier laughed then, a real laugh, awful with blood in it. "Ledger-keeper. You're exactly what they said." He closed his eyes. "The Regent keeps what's mine. And yours too, though you don't know it yet. We were both bought with the same coin. Ask Odile what a boy's childhood fetches, these days. Ask her what *you* fetched."

"What?"

But the man's eyes stayed closed, and his breathing went slow and shallow and then, between one of Dominic's questions and the next, stopped altogether. The fog curled in around him. The candles leaned in the draft, righted themselves, burned on.

Dominic stood very still for a long moment, holding the oilcloth bundle against his chest, listening to the silence of the guild the way a sailor listens for water in the hold.

Then he heard footsteps. More than one pair. Coming fast through the alley behind the counting house, boots on cobbles, unhurried the way men are unhurried when they are certain of what they'll find.

* * *

Fear, Dominic had learned, was mostly logistics.

There was no time to be afraid. There was time to move. He rolled the dead man—not gently; gentleness was expensive—behind the ledger cabinet, out of the sightline of the door, and looked down at the blood on the floorboards. Too much. Salt from the bin by the wall, thrown down thick, would crust it gray and pass in candlelight for a spill. He did it in two scoops, heart slamming, hands steady. Hands steady; that was the one thing the guild had ever taught him that he was grateful for.

The bundle. Where?

Under a floorboard—he knew every board, and every board was known to Sera Voss, who knew everything. In the vault—no, the vault was the first place anyone would look and the last place anything stayed secret. Out the window—too late, the footsteps had reached the alley mouth and slowed, which meant they'd seen something, which meant they were sure.

He stood in the middle of the room holding a dead man's map while the guild's enforcers counted down his invisibility, and the thought arrived with the calm of all his worst thoughts:

*A thief hides stolen goods in the last place another thief would look.*

No shelf. No safe. No floor, no wall, no false book. There was only one place in the world Dominic Quintana had ever kept anything, and he had sworn never to use it again.

The iron box behind the third candle.

And deeper than the box. The box was a hiding place only if you never touched what was inside. To hide the map where no thief would look—not Voss, not Odile, not even himself on a greedy day—he would have to do the thing he had trained himself out of doing for fifteen years. He would have to steal from himself.

The boots reached the alley door.

Dominic unwrapped one corner of the oilcloth. Inside was paper—a sheet folded twice, sealed along the fold with a blob of green wax stamped with a sigil he didn't recognize: a mountain, upside down, roots above peaks. He didn't study it. Studying was remembering, and remembering was carrying, and he needed it *carried* now, whole, unseen.

He put two fingers to his own temple, the way he had done a thousand times to others, and for the first time since he was a boy he turned the thief's attention inward.

Every theft had a grammar. Object, vessel, seal. He was object and vessel both, and the seal—

The seal was the door he never opened.

He went to it the way you go to a room in a dream, knowing the house, not knowing why. Behind the box, behind the black wax, there was a memory with his name on it—one of the few—and he had kept it locked not because it was precious but because it was *hers*, whoever she was, a woman's voice singing somewhere in the sealed dark of his seventh year, and he could not bear to know what the rest of the song remembered.

He didn't open it. God help him, he didn't open it. He only unlocked it—an inch, a hair's width, a key turned in a door he'd painted shut with years—and slid the folded map through the gap, into the dark on the other side, and locked it again.

The cost hit him immediately, like stepping into cold water. The memory knew it had been disturbed. Something in his chest he had no name for began to ache, low and continuous, the way a tooth aches before you admit it's a tooth. He sealed the box. He set the third candle back in front of it with a hand that was almost steady.

The alley door shook once, twice, and gave.

Three of them came in: Brannoc, wide as a doorframe; a boy called Finch whose job was climbing and listening; and behind them, wrapped in a gray traveling cloak against the fog, Sera Voss.

Sera was the guild's charm, its smile, its knife kept warm. She looked at Dominic first—she always looked at Dominic first, briefly, dismissively, filing him under *furniture*—and then at the salt-crusted stain on the floorboards, and stopped.

"Furniture" had one advantage: it got asked fewer questions.

"Dominic." Her voice was warm honey over a blade's edge. "Working late."

"Books don't balance themselves." He kept his ink-stained sleeve visible, his posture apologetic, his eyes appropriately dull. "Someone's spilled salt. Again. I've told Odile we need—"

"Where is he?" Brannoc said. He was already moving through the room, kicking open the storage closet, the cold pantry.

"Who?"

"The courier." Finch had gone to the window, was scanning the fog over the harbor with the particular hunger of a boy who earned by the find. "Gray coat, half a head shorter than Brannoc, limp on the left. Came down Rope Walk like a cut cat. Bled on three doorways getting here."

"Here," Dominic repeated. "Why would a courier come *here* at this hour? Deliveries are morning work. Everyone knows—"

"We know he came here." Sera crossed the room slowly, trailing a fingertip along the ledger table, reading it the way Dominic read ledgers. She stopped at the inkwell. At his knocked-over ink bottle. At the black pool of it soaking into the cloth of his sleeve, which he realized only now he had never changed, and which was evidence of exactly the kind she collected. Her eyes came up to his and stayed there, pleasantly, the way a cat watches a gap in the wainscoting.

"You've inked yourself," she said.

"Candles. Clumsy. It's late."

"Mm." She smiled at him with perfect warmth. "You should wash that before it sets. Ink holds memories, my grandmother used to say. Everything it's touched, it remembers." She let that sit in the air between them like a coin tossed to see what sound it makes. Then, brightly: "Well. If a bleeding man staggered in here, he's not here now. Dead men don't walk, and living ones don't bleed on salt without a trace." She turned for the door, then paused, half-smiling over her shoulder. "Odile wants you at the ten o'clock reckoning tomorrow, Dominic. Don't be forgettable."

The joke landed the way she intended, a needle dressed as kindness. They filed out—Brannoc last, giving the room one more sweep with those flat gray eyes—and the alley door hung open on fog and lamplight and the sound of their boots diminishing toward the harbor.

Dominic waited until the fog had swallowed the last of it. Then he sat down at the ledger table, took up his pen with its ruined, ink-clotted nib, and wrote nothing at all for a very long time.

*Ask Odile what a boy's childhood fetches, these days. Ask her what you fetched.*

*We were both bought with the same coin.*

He pressed his free hand flat against his chest, against the ache behind the sternum where the sealed door sat a hair less shut than it had been an hour ago. Through iron and black wax and his own careful forgetting, he could almost feel the shape of the folded paper resting in the dark beside a woman's unfinished song.

Somewhere across the city, a girl named Wren was waiting for a father who wasn't coming home. Somewhere beneath a mountain, a Regent kept a soul with his name in its margins. And tomorrow at ten o'clock, Odile—who had raised him, paid him, made him, *bought* him, said a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like the dead courier—wanted him at the reckoning.

Dominic snuffed the third candle last, leaving the little iron box in the dark where it belonged.

He had hidden the map in the one place no thief would look.

He just wasn't sure anymore whether he counted.
