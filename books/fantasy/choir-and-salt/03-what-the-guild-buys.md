## 3. What the Guild Buys

The dead courier had been in the ground for six days when Odile summoned Dominic to the vault.

That was the thing about Guildmaster Odile Quintana—no relation, whatever the story she told at her own expense suggested—that she never did anything quickly. She let a body rot first. She let the harbor gulls finish their argument over it. She let the fear ripen in everyone who'd seen the man die, because fear was a preservative and she was, above all things, a woman who understood preservation.

The summons came by a child with salt-white hands who didn't know his own name. They all worked like that, the guild's errants: children scrubbed blank and cheap to keep, running messages they couldn't be tortured into repeating because there was nothing left to torture. Dominic had been one of them once. He knew this the way you know a fact about a stranger in a book.

*You were an errant. You were bought young.* The thought arrived without weather, without grief, just the flat tone of a ledger line. He wrote it down mentally and filed it and felt nothing, and the nothing was the point. The nothing was the craft.

He dressed carefully—not too fine, not too poor—and went down through the guild's bones.

The Saltwork Guild kept its offices above a decommissioned brine-house on the Gray Quay, all sagging beams and the smell of old sea. But the real business happened below, where the miners had once carved their living out of the earth's salt veins, and where the guild now carved its living out of something softer. Dominic descended three flights of wet stone steps, past the counting rooms, past the interrogation cells that were officially storage, down to the iron door with its lock of seven tumblers that only answered to blood and boredom together.

Odile was waiting inside, holding a lamp the color of weak tea.

"You look well, bookkeeper," she said. Which meant: *I am watching you.*

"Guildmaster." He bowed the precise degree a subordinate bows—enough respect to be unremarkable, not enough to seem eager. Eager men got noticed. Noticing was death.

"Walk with me." She turned and moved into the dark, and the dark moved around her like water around a keel.

* * *

The vault of the Saltwork Guild did not look like a treasure house. It looked like what it had once been: a mine. Shafts branched off the main gallery into darkness, each one racked floor to ceiling with shelves, and on the shelves stood bottles.

Not jars. Bottles. Long-necked, stoppered with wax and lead seals, each one faintly luminous, each one humming so low you felt it in your back teeth rather than heard it. Some glowed amber—the sweet stock, first kisses and summer afternoons, sold to nobles who wanted to feel something they'd never earned. Some burned a sullen red: rage stock, sold to soldiers and duellists. Some were gray as harbor water and gave off no light at all, and those were the ones Dominic walked fastest past.

Grief stock. Sold mostly back to the people who'd paid to have it taken, once the price of forgetting had compounded enough. Grief was the guild's most reliable commodity. It always grew back.

"Third-quarter inventory," Odile said, trailing her fingers along a shelf without touching anything. "Two hundred years, give or take, of a duchess's mourning for a son lost at sea. We'll get maybe forty years of use out of it before it thins. There's a buyer in Vell waiting."

"There's always a buyer in Vell," Dominic said.

She smiled at that. It was not, entirely, an unpleasant smile. That was the trap of Odile—you could work beside her for twenty years and still catch yourself liking her, the way you might like a well-made knife.

"You're wondering why you're here," she said.

"The thought crossed my mind."

"Crossed. Ha." She stopped at a junction where four shafts met, and held up the lamp. "A courier died on our doorstep last week. You processed the incident report. Very thorough. Very you."

"He bled out before I finished the header."

"Mm." Her eyes in the lamplight were the pale yellow of old wax. "His name was Corvin Hale. Do you remember it?"

"I remember everything I enter in the ledgers," Dominic said, which was true, and also the safest lie he owned.

"Corvin Hale ran the Verity route for eleven years. Do you know what the Verity route carries?"

Dominic kept his breathing even. Somewhere under his ribs, the wax-sealed map he had hidden six days ago seemed to warm against his memory—which is to say, against nothing, because he had hidden it in the one place even he could not casually browse: folded into a stolen memory, sealed and shelved inside his own skull like any other piece of guild stock.

"Salt," he said. "Preserved goods. Guild correspondence."

"Soul-collateral instruments," Odile said pleasantly. "Debt instruments. We buy from the desperate, bookkeeper, and we sell to the powerful, and sometimes—" she tapped the shelf, and a hundred bottles shivered and hummed— "we sell to the powerful *beneath*. Mount Verity has a court in it. Old place. Older than the guild, older than most names. And courts, even stone courts, run on credit."

She began walking again, deeper, and Dominic followed because not following would have been more remarkable than following.

"Corvin Hale," Odile went on, "sold his soul to us thirty years ago for his daughter's medicine. Standard terms: we hold it, he buys it back at interest, and if he defaults, we may dispose of the asset as we see fit. Two months ago he defaulted. And two weeks ago the court beneath Mount Verity called in its collateral—a debt of ours dating back some years—and so we settled it." She glanced back at him, mild as milk. "We sent them Hale's soul. He learned of it. He came here to steal the paperwork that might have saved him, and instead he died in our doorway. A tidy tragedy. The kind we don't even need to write down."

Dominic heard every word and gave away nothing, and behind his face he was doing arithmetic at knife-speed:

*I have the map. The map shows the court beneath Mount Verity. The dying man said the Regent keeps what's mine. If Hale's soul went south as payment, then the map isn't a guild document—it's a debtor's map, drawn by someone who'd been inside. Someone who wanted out.*

"And the older debt?" he asked, keeping his voice dry as sand. "The original collateral. What does a court like that take as payment from the Saltwork Guild?"

Odile stopped.

They had reached the deepest shaft, a dead-end gallery where the oldest stock was kept—the bottles that predated even her tenure, glowing faintly through decades of dust. She raised the lamp, and the light fell across a single shelf, and on that shelf sat one bottle unlike the others.

It was small. Child-sized, you'd say, if you said such things. Its glow was not amber or red or gray but a color Dominic had no ledger word for—a green like the underside of leaves, like light coming up through water, like a summer he could almost—

He shut the feeling down. Professional habit. You did not reach for things in the vault; reaching was how thieves got caught.

"This," Odile said softly, "is the oldest instrument we hold. Purchased twenty-six years ago from a woman in the fishing districts who needed debts settled in a way that didn't leave a body. The price she offered wasn't hers to sell." The lamp swung, gently. "A boy's childhood. Complete. Age nought to seven. Clean extraction—we used our best hand, even then. The court beneath Verity accepted it as the down payment on a much larger arrangement, and we've held it in escrow ever since, because the contract specified *held*, not *delivered*. There are reasons, bookkeeper, why certain assets sit in escrow for decades, and none of them are sentiment."

The bottle glowed its impossible green.

Twenty-six years. Nought to seven. A boy's childhood, complete.

Dominic stood very still, the way a man stands on ice when he hears the first crack, and somewhere far below the surface of himself, something he did not have a name for put its hand flat against the underside of the floor.

"What was the buyer's name?" he asked. "The woman."

"Does it matter?"

"For the ledger," he said. "Everything matters for the ledger."

Odile studied him for a long moment, and then she laughed—one short syllable, genuinely amused, which was worse than suspicion.

"You want to know the funny thing?" she said. "I've told this story a dozen times over brandy and no one ever asks about her. The mother. Everyone asks about the price. Fine. Her name was Mirren Ansel. She sang at the Tidewife chapel. She died within the year—grief stock withdrawal, the physicians said, though between us, I think she simply had nothing left worth staying for." She lifted the bottle from its shelf and held it up so the green light washed her face. "And here is the funnier thing, the thing I've never told anyone, because it stops being funny and starts being fate."

She turned the bottle. On its base, scratched into the glass by the extracting hand—a professional hand, a clean hand—was a mark. A maker's mark. The little cipher of the thief who'd done the taking.

It was Dominic's mark. His own private sigil, the one he cut into every extraction he performed, the one no other soul alive could draw.

"That's impossible," he said, and his voice sounded like someone else's voice, very far away.

"It is, isn't it," Odile agreed cheerfully. "Because this bottle was extracted twenty-six years ago, and you, my dear bookkeeper, are twenty-seven." She weighed it in her palm like a plum. "You were the best hand we ever trained. We made sure of it—we started early. So early, in fact, that your very first extraction is right here. The guild's finest work. A seven-year-old boy, cutting out his own childhood, guided by masters, because that was the price of your apprenticeship and your mother's debts, and because a child will agree to anything if the adults in the room call it *destiny*."

The vault hummed around them. Hundreds of bottles. Thousands of years of other people's lives, and Dominic stood among them hearing his own pulse like surf in his ears, and the crack in the ice spread all the way across.

*The Regent keeps what's mine.*

*Held, not delivered.*

The bottle in escrow. Twenty-six years of escrow, owed to a court beneath a mountain, and the dying courier's map showing the way down, and a voice saying *mine* with a boy's mouth.

"The court's called-in debt," Dominic said. Each word placed like a coin on a counter. "The one Hale's soul settled the interest on. What was the principal?"

"Now there," Odile said, setting the green bottle back on its shelf with the tenderness of a woman handling her own heart, "is the interesting question. Because I've asked it myself, lately. Why would a court that buys souls keep a single childhood in escrow for twenty-six years instead of consuming it? What are they waiting for?" She picked up the lamp. "Unless, of course, it isn't the childhood they're holding. Unless it's bait."

* * *

That night, in his narrow room above the counting house, Dominic lit no candle. He sat on the edge of his cot in the dark and tried to do what he had done every night of his adult life: open the ledger of himself, verify the entries, close it again.

The entries wouldn't hold.

He reached inward for the hidden thing—the map, folded into stolen stock, tucked behind a false memory of a rainy market day—and found it exactly where he'd left it. Fine. Good. The map was safe.

But next to it, in the filing system of his own mind, he now saw a gap. A clean-edged absence, professionally cut, running from his earliest recall to his seventh year. He had always known it was there the way you know the shape of a missing tooth. He had assumed it was small. It was not small. It was a whole country of himself, and he had lived his entire life on its coastline, telling himself the interior was empty.

*Nought to seven. Clean extraction. The guild's finest work.*

He had cut it out himself. His own hand, guided by theirs. He had been the instrument of his own hollowing before he was old enough to know there was anything to hollow, and then they had shelved him in a bottle and sold the rights to his beginning to a court under a mountain, and he had spent twenty-seven years balancing books full of other people's losses without once checking the entry that bore his own name.

Dominic put his head in his hands.

Somewhere below his feet, three flights down behind iron, the green bottle sat in escrow, glowing like the underside of leaves, humming its small note into the salt. And far to the south, under Mount Verity, something ancient held the deed to his first seven years and had held it for twenty-six years, patient as stone, waiting for the boy who cut it out to come looking for it.

*Come and see,* the silence said, in a voice that might have been his mother's, if he'd had one left to imagine.

He lay back on the cot and stared at the black ceiling and understood, with the terrible clarity of a balanced column, that he was no longer the keeper of the ledger.

He was an entry in it. Line item, pending delivery. And every road he might walk now led south, through the guild's teeth, toward the mountain that already owned the best part of him—and toward the woman who'd just shown him the bottle with a smile, knowing exactly what it would do to him, which meant Odile hadn't warned him.

Odile had baited him. Same as the court.

In the morning, he knew, there would be a shadow assigned to follow him. There always was, after Odile showed you your own price.

He began planning, that same dark hour, how to lose her.
