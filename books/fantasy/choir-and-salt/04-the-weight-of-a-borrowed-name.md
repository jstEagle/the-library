## 4. The Weight of a Borrowed Name

The summons came at dawn, folded inside a fishmonger's receipt, which was how Odile liked to deliver things that weren't about fish.

*My office. An hour. Wear something forgettable.*

Dominic read it twice over his tea, then fed it to the coals and watched the words curl to ash. *Wear something forgettable.* As if he owned anything else. His whole wardrobe was the color of unpaid debts. His face was the kind that made bartenders ask if they'd met before and then decide it didn't matter. He had spent twenty years sanding himself down into a man nobody described twice, and Odile knew it, which meant the note wasn't advice.

It was a test.

He dressed in his grayest coat and walked to the guildhouse through streets still slick with night, taking a longer route than necessary so he could check his own shadow for followers. He'd hidden the courier's map three days ago—folded small, sealed in wax, and pushed deep into a memory he'd carved out of his own head: an afternoon of no consequence whatsoever, a rain-shined street, a woman laughing at something Dominic couldn't name anymore because he'd taken the laugh and buried the map beneath it. A thief's hiding place. No thief would search there, because no thief could. The door only opened from the inside, and he had swallowed the key.

Mostly he didn't think about it. Mostly he was good at not thinking about it.

Odile's office sat above the counting room, where the guild's true business hummed through the floor like a hive under the boards. She was at her window when he entered, a small straight-backed woman with white hair cut brutally short and hands that never stopped moving, sorting, tapping, folding. People thought her power was the vault. It wasn't. Her power was arithmetic. She could look at any man and tell you exactly what he cost and what he'd fetch.

"Dominic." She didn't turn. "You slept badly."

"I slept."

"You slept badly," she repeated, "because you've been carrying something heavy, and you haven't entered it in the books. Unregistered weight is my least favorite kind." Now she turned, and her eyes were the pale gray of ledger paper. "A courier died on our doorstep Tuesday night. Bleeding. Whispering. And you, my meticulous bookkeeper, filed no report."

"He died before he said anything worth filing."

"Everyone says something worth filing before they die. It's practically the definition of dying." She came around the desk and adjusted his collar with two brisk tugs, a gesture that should have been maternal and was instead the way a woman straightens merchandise. "I'm not accusing you, Dominic. Accusation is for people whose guilt I haven't confirmed. I'm simply assigning you company."

The door opened behind him.

She was tall, dark-haired, dressed like someone who had dressed quickly but well, and she wore the particular smile of a person who has already decided to like you and finds this convenient. "Sera Voss," she said, extending a hand. "I've read your ledgers. You have beautiful handwriting."

"Thank you," Dominic said, and shook it. Her grip was warm and entirely without warmth.

"Sera will be assisting you with collections," Odile said. "Harbor district. There are several accounts overdue, and I find I like the idea of two sets of eyes on the books these days. The world's gotten slippery." She smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "Enjoy the market. Buy something. It's good for the guild when people see us spending."

Dominic bowed slightly and left with Sera Voss at his elbow, and he understood the arrangement precisely: he had been given a leash with excellent manners.

* * *

The harbor market opened early and lied all day—about the age of its fish, the origin of its silks, the honesty of its scales. Dominic moved through it the way he moved through everything, unremarkable, half-heard, while Sera Voss walked beside him like a bell that had learned to walk.

She was good. He had to give her that. She didn't crowd him or interrogate him. She bought roasted chestnuts and made him take some. She haggled over a scarf with a ferocity that got them both a better price and possibly made two friends of the vendor's children. She asked questions that sounded like weather.

"So," she said, around a chestnut, "how long have you kept the ledgers?"

"Eleven years."

"That's a long time to write down what other people sold. Doesn't it itch? All those years and kisses and griefs going by on the page, and none of them yours?"

"They're mine to count," Dominic said. "That's enough."

"It isn't, though." She said it lightly, tossing the shell into the harbor, where the gulls argued over it. "I've seen you write. You press too hard. Men who are content don't press that hard."

"And what do you trade in, Voss? Since you're so fluent in wanting."

"Me?" She smiled at him sideways, and the smile was genuinely lovely, which he suspected was load-bearing. "Loyalty. That's the guild's quiet line of business. Memory gets all the songs written about it, but loyalty is the steadier commodity. A man will sell you his childhood and still turn on you. Sell his devotion, and he's yours past reason." She plucked another chestnut and offered it to him with her fingers, an intimacy calculated to within an inch of its life. "I broker it. I know every man on this dock who'd die for somebody, and exactly who paid for that."

"That's a filthy trade," Dominic said.

"It's a clean one. Nobody lies to me. That's more than memory-thieves can say." She let that sit between them, pleasant as a knife on the table. "Speaking of which. The dead courier. Corvo. You were seen with him before the enforcers arrived. Two minutes, maybe three. A long time to spend with a dying man who said nothing worth filing."

The harbor noise seemed to thin. Dominic kept his face doing nothing at all, which was his finest craft. "I turned him over. Checked his pockets for guild property."

"What did he feel like? Dying men. I've always wondered. Warm or cold?"

"Warm," Dominic said. "They spend their last heat telling you things."

"Mm." She looked out at the water, unhurried, and he understood that she had just told him she knew he was lying, and he had just told her he knew she knew, and neither of them had spent a coin doing it. "There's a rumor," she went on, conversational, "that Corvo was carrying something to the mountain court. Something that got him killed on our doorstep. Odile wants it found. She's offered a very generous finder's fee, and she's asked me to keep my eyes open." She turned the full force of her attention back to him, and it was a lot of attention; it had weight, like standing too close to a fire. "Your eyes are open, aren't they, Dominic?"

"Always."

"Good." She patted his arm. "Because here's the thing about betrayal. It never looks like betrayal. It looks like a man being careful. It looks like extra routes home. It looks—" she tapped the lapel of his gray coat, gently, "—like wearing something forgettable."

* * *

They parted at the fish-stalls, or pretended to. Dominic took three turns through the market's guts, doubled back through the rope-sellers' alley, and stood five minutes in the doorway of a chandler's shop watching reflections in stacked brass pots until he was satisfied—or as satisfied as he could be, which was never—that Sera Voss had truly gone.

She hadn't. He caught her once, far down the quay, talking to a crab-boat captain, and the captain nodded toward the city, toward the guild, and Sera pressed something small into his palm, and Dominic felt the cold arithmetic of it settle into his bones. She wasn't following him today. She was building a web. Every contact, every favor, every man on the docks who owed the guild his own devotion—and now they would all be watching for a gray-coated bookkeeper who took wrong turns home.

He bought a paper of salted plums he didn't want, for cover, and ate one, and it was excellent, and he hated it.

Here was the shape of his problem, laid out as cleanly as a column of figures: the map was hidden in the only vault no one could crack, which also meant it was hidden in the one vault he could barely afford to open. Opening it in front of Sera Voss was impossible. Opening it alone grew more dangerous daily, because each time he touched that buried afternoon—the laughing woman, the wet street—he risked fraying it, and a frayed hiding place spills. And meanwhile everyone wanted what he carried. The guild wanted it. Whatever had hunted Corvo through the night wanted it. The court beneath Mount Verity, wherever it was, presumably wanted it back, since courts always want their maps returned. And now Sera Voss wanted it, or wanted proof he had it, and her method of wanting was patience, and patience was the one weapon he had no defense against.

He finished the plums. He walked home by his longest route, past the chandlery, past the brass pots, and just before the last corner he felt it—a lightness in his coat pocket, quick fingers, gone before his hand could close.

He spun. The quay was gulls and crates and ordinary morning.

In his hand, where the paper of plums had been, was a folded note in beautiful handwriting:

*You press too hard. — S.*

And beneath the note, tucked with a lover's care, the plums themselves, returned.

He laughed once, short and involuntary, because it was either that or run. She had picked his pocket to tell him she could pick his pocket. The message was the theft. If she ever found something worth taking, he wouldn't see her coming twice.

Which meant the map could not stay where it was. Not because she could reach it—no one could reach it—but because he could feel the shape of her patience now, patient as tide, and a man carrying an unregistered weight starts sleeping worse, and sleeping worse starts showing, and showing was death.

That evening he lit his candle, uncapped his ink, and balanced the day's accounts the way he balanced every account: precisely, completely, leaving nothing off the books. Then, in the smallest entry at the bottom of the smallest column, in handwriting only he could read, he recorded a single line.

*One debt, hidden. Interest accruing.*

He blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the counting room hum below his floor, and thought about the laughing woman on the wet street whose laugh he had stolen to bury a map in, and realized with a cold, slow clarity that he could no longer remember why she'd been laughing, or whether the memory had been real at all, or whether some earlier version of himself had put it there for exactly this purpose, and that whatever he had hidden inside his own head, he was no longer certain he was the one who had hidden it.

Somewhere across the city, a mountain hummed.

And somewhere closer, much closer, Sera Voss was buying loyalty by the pint, and every drop of it was looking for him.
