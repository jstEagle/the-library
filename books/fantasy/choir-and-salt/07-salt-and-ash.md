## 7. Salt and Ash

The summons came at dawn, which in the Saltwork Guild meant it was already too late.

Dominic knew this because dawn was when Odile did her accounting, and Odile only called a man to her office at accounting hours if his name had appeared somewhere in her ledgers. Nobody's name ever appeared in her ledgers by accident. He'd kept those ledgers himself for eleven years. He knew exactly how many ways a name could be written down.

He dressed carefully. Not well—well would be suspicious—but with the deliberate shabbiness of a man who had nothing to hide and had never thought about it twice. He left the map where he'd hidden it: folded small inside the memory of a rainy Tuesday, tucked behind the image of a woman with an umbrella he'd stolen off a banker three winters ago. No thief would look there. No one looked at Tuesdays at all. That was the whole point of them.

The walk up through the guildhouse took four minutes. He spent all four rehearsing nothing, keeping his mind smooth as still water, because Sera Voss fell into step beside him on the second landing without a word, and she was reading him the way he read ledgers—line by line, looking for the entry that didn't balance.

"Sleep?" she asked.

"Badly."

"Liar," she said pleasantly, and opened Odile's door for him.

* * *

Odile Vane kept her office cold. That wasn't temperament; it was preservation. Bottled memories kept better in chill, and the shelves along her walls held more of them than some vaults Dominic could name—a hundred small amber lights, each one a life someone had decided they could live without. He had catalogued every one of them. He had never once been tempted. Temptation was for men who wanted things back.

She sat behind a desk bare except for an inkwell, a ledger, and a single sheet of paper laid face-down, the way you lay out a body.

"Dominic." She didn't ask him to sit. "You've been with us how long?"

"Eleven years."

"And in eleven years, what have I never once had cause to do?"

He let the silence answer. She smiled, thin as a ledger line.

"I've never had cause to audit you." She turned the paper over.

It was a receipt. Not guild stock—the wax was wrong, the seal was a mountain, a stylized peak pressed deep into dark red wax—and the hand that had written the entries was not guildhand but something older, angular, written in ink that looked brown in the morning light. A court document. A bill of sale.

He read it the way he read everything: fast, complete, permanent. Three lines. The first named a courier, one Tomas Wren, deceased, soul held as collateral against debt. The second named the creditor: *the Court Beneath Mount Verity, in perpetuity.* The third line—

The third line named the original payment. *Paid in full, seven years prior, by transfer of one childhood entire.* And beneath that, in a different ink, added later like a footnote in a crueler hand: *seller of record: O. Vane.*

His face did nothing. Eleven years of practice made sure of that. But something behind his ribs went very quiet, the way a room goes quiet when you realize the floor has been gone for years and you've been walking on air the whole time.

"You recognize it," Odile said. It wasn't a question either.

"It's a court bill."

"It's *my* court bill," she said, "which is mine to hold, and which has gone missing from my strongroom along with—" she paused delicately, "—a map. Sealed in wax. Carried here by a dying man who bled on my back-room floor two nights ago and whose death, I note, no one reported to me. You reported no deaths, Dominic. You always report deaths. It's in your nature. You're a man who files things."

"The man died before I could—"

"Before you could what? Save him? Call the enforcers who arrived, what, minutes after? Before you could report a sealed map delivered into the hands of my ledger-keeper by a courier running from the court beneath Mount Verity?" She leaned back. The cold light from the shelf-lamps made her face younger and older at once. "I'm not angry, Dominic. Anger is for surprises. Nothing about you surprises me anymore."

* * *

Here is what he thought, standing there while she spoke: she had sold his childhood.

Not the guild. Not some faceless buyer in the dark. Her. Her hand on the seller's line. Seven years ago—he did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way you can't stop touching a broken tooth—seven years ago he had been nineteen, newly indentured, freshly emptied of whatever the guild had taken in its standard tithe, and Odile had stood in a room beneath a mountain and sold the rest. His seventh year. His mother's voice. Whatever a boy keeps in the pockets of a childhood: the names of clouds, the smell of a particular kitchen, the reason he flinched at certain songs.

And then she had wiped the transaction from her own memory of it, or paid someone to, because when he searched her face now there was nothing there but the pleasant blankness of a woman doing routine business.

"—so the question," she was saying, "is not whether you took the map. Of course you took the map. You're the only person in this building capable of hiding anything from me, which is precisely why I pay you. The question is what you intend to do with it, and whether your intentions are worth more to me than your silence."

"And if they're not?"

"Then you'll die in a way that takes three days and I'll have Sera write it up as a fall in the harbor." She said it the way she said everything, mildly, as though discussing freight rates. "But I don't think we'll need that. Because I'm going to offer you a bargain, and you're going to take it, because it's the best bargain anyone has ever offered you."

She slid a second paper across the desk. This one was guildstock, clean wax, clean hand. A contract of passage.

"The court wants the map. The court wants the courier's debts settled. The court, as it happens, wants several things, and the guild is in a position to provide delivery services. You will go to Mount Verity. You will carry the map. You will present yourself at the court as the guild's agent—which, conveniently, you are—and you will conduct the negotiation for the return of collateral assets."

"Whose collateral assets?"

"The courier's soul. One item, currently held." Her eyes were steady and kind and absolutely empty. "You'll secure its release. The guild takes its fee. The trail that leads from that dead man to this room—to *you*—gets erased. Every record, every whisper, every ledger line. As far as the world will know, you were never touched by any of it. Clean hands, Dominic. For the first time in your career."

It was elegant. That was the thing about Odile; he'd admired it for eleven years and hated himself for admiring it. The offer gave him exactly what he wanted—the road under the world, the court, the way in—and wrapped it in absolution so thick he might miss what was underneath.

Except he was the guild's ledger-keeper. He read contracts the way priests read scripture, looking for the sin in the margins.

"Who pays the guild's fee?"

"The court does. In kind."

"In kind," he repeated. "The court pays in memories."

"The court pays in what it has."

"And what does the court get, besides its map?" He kept his voice level, clerk-flat. "A courier's soul isn't enough to interest Mount Verity. I've read the old trade terms. They want volume. They want *choir.*"

Something moved behind Odile's eyes—not guilt, nothing so honest. Calculation adjusting its weight.

"The court gets," she said, "the agent of record. Delivery, negotiation, and settlement are traditionally performed by a bonded representative, whose bond serves as surety for the transaction." She smiled again. "You know how surety works, Dominic. You've written it ten thousand times. Something of value, held against completion. If the trade concludes cleanly, the surety is released."

"And if it doesn't conclude cleanly?"

"Then the surety forfeits."

*One childhood entire.* The third line of the bill sat in his memory, printed there forever, the way everything he read was printed there forever. Surety. Collateral. He would walk into that court carrying a map and a contract, and the moment he crossed the threshold, the Regent would hold his soul in her filing system like a document awaiting signature—and Odile would have sold him the same way she'd sold his childhood, with the same mild voice and the same clean wax, and this time she wouldn't even need to erase his memory of the deal because he'd be signing it himself.

She meant to trade him. Whole. To feed the mountain's hunger and buy whatever Odile Vane bought with souls these days—leverage, longevity, the slow acquisition of being untouchable. And the genius of it, the part he almost applauded, was that she'd built the trap out of his own escape route. The trail erased. The past cleaned. All he had to do was become the payment.

He looked down at the contract and read it fully, every clause, because that was who he was, and found what he knew he'd find: clause nine, surety of record, *one memory-thief, entire.*

"Clause nine," he said.

"Necessary," Odile said. "The court insists on surety. They're traditionalists."

"They ate the last traditionalist who dealt with them."

"And yet here we all are, still dealing." She rose, which ended meetings the way a gavel ends auctions. "You leave tomorrow, with Voss as escort. Rest tonight. Eat something. You'll want your strength—Mount Verity is a long climb even before you get underground." At the door, as he turned, she added, lightly, as a gift: "For what it's worth, I remember your mother's song. It came with the inventory. I've never played it. Some stock a keeper simply holds."

He stopped. For half a heartbeat the room tilted and he was seven years old in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere in the world except inside an amber bottle on a cold shelf.

Then he filed it. Grief, category: deferred. He had a contract to steal.

"Inventory," he said, "can be misfiled."

"Yes," Odile agreed pleasantly. "It can. That's rather the point of keepers."

* * *

Sera walked him down. Neither of them spoke until the ground floor, where the salt smell of the harbor came through the loading doors and the day began making its noise outside.

"Well?" she said finally. "Congratulations on your promotion."

"You knew."

"I carry the escort orders, Dom. I don't read them." But her eyes said otherwise, the way her eyes always said otherwise; she traded in loyalty the way he traded in memory, and right now her loyalty was pointed at Odile like a drawn bow. "You should eat. And sleep. And not do anything clever between now and tomorrow's tide."

"Clever," Dominic said, "is the one thing I never do."

She laughed at that—short, real, surprised out of her—and for a moment he saw the person under the enforcer, and hated how much he noticed, because people were liabilities and noticing them was how you started keeping ledgers of your own.

He left her at the stairs and went out into the gray morning, down to the harbor wall where the water slapped the pilings and the fish-guts smell of the market covered a man's tracks. Wren would be waiting where he'd told her to wait, furious, armed, and right. The courier's daughter, with her father's soul filed under a dead man's name in a cathedral of shelves. And somewhere under that mountain, in a vault labeled in Odile's neat hand, a boy's seventh year sat in the dark, humming a song his mother had taught him, waiting to be claimed by whoever held the ledger.

Two days ago he had been invisible. Now he was clause nine.

Fine. He'd been reading other people's fine print for eleven years. Tomorrow he would deliver the map, exactly as promised. He would present himself as surety, exactly as promised. And then, quietly, in the margins, in the grammar of ledgers and filings and transfers, he would begin to change the entries—one line at a time—until the bill of sale read differently than anyone expected.

Odile had made one mistake, and it was the same mistake every buyer makes: she believed that what a man sells, he surrenders.

Dominic Quintana had never surrendered anything in his life. He only ever moved it—from one column to another, from one hand to another, from one name to another—waiting, patient as salt, for the day it came back to him.

Tomorrow they went under the mountain. Tonight, there was a forged clause to draft, and a girl to convince, and a song to memorize before he lost even the memory of wanting it back.

He turned toward the market and began to walk.
