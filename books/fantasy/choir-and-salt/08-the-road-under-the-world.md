## 8. The Road Under the World

The road began where the salt road ended, which is to say it began badly.

Dominic had expected a tunnel. What he got was a throat. The old miners' way descended through rock that widened and narrowed like breathing, its walls streaked white with ancient brine, and the air tasted of something he couldn't name—old rain, maybe, or the inside of someone else's mouth. His lantern threw their shadows long against the stone, two figures stitched together at the edges.

Wren walked ahead. She had insisted on walking ahead, and he had let her, because arguing with her was like arguing with weather.

"You're limping," she said, without turning.

"I'm favoring my good leg."

"That's what limping is."

He almost smiled. He didn't, because she'd see it, and seeing things was her whole method. The courier's daughter had a thief's eyes in a cartographer's face—she read ground the way he read ledgers, every fault line and false bottom catalogued behind those gray irises. Two days out from the city and she hadn't asked him once about the map. That worried him more than asking would have.

"Rest here," he said.

"We've been walking an hour."

"And I've been limping an hour."

She stopped. Ahead of them the road forked around a fallen pillar, and in the fork—Dominic saw it before she did, because his kind always saw it first—a shimmer. Low to the ground, like heat haze off summer cobbles, except this was winter and they were half a mile under the world.

"Don't," he said.

"I haven't done anything."

"You're about to." He caught her sleeve. "That's pooled memory. Forgotten stuff. It drains down from the surface through cracks in the rock. It looks like water. It isn't."

"How do you know?"

"Because I can feel it from here." A stranger's contentment, warm as bathwater, lapping at the edges of his mind. "Somebody's wedding day. Recent enough to still have sugar in it."

Wren stared at the shimmer. Her face did something complicated—the same expression he'd worn, once, standing outside the guild's memory vault for the first time. Hunger and horror holding hands.

"Can you drink it?" she asked quietly.

"You could. Anyone could. It's why the old miners went strange." He released her sleeve. "It's also how we find water down here, if it comes to that. Memory pools near real pools. Grief collects where people wept. Follow the sad ones downhill and you'll hit a spring eventually."

"That's the bleakest mapmaking I've ever heard."

"It's the truest."

She looked at him then, really looked, and he understood with a lurch that she'd been studying him the whole journey the way he studied everything—for weaknesses, for seams. "You talk about it like it's plumbing," she said. "Like souls and memories are just weather in the walls."

"What would you call it?"

"My father's in one of those pools somewhere." Her voice didn't crack. He respected that; it must have cost her. "His last hours, or whatever was left after the Regent took his soul. And you're describing it like drainage."

Dominic had no answer for that. He was a man who had spent thirty years making sure he had no answers for anything that mattered.

* * *

Wren watched the thief sleep and thought about pushing him into the pool.

Not the small one—that was just somebody's wedding day, sugar-sweet, harmless. The big one. They'd found it an hour past the fork: a black sheet of shimmer lying in a basin of rock like spilled ink, wide as a barn floor, still as glass. Even standing ten feet back she could feel it pulling at her, a low tidal tug behind the eyes. This one wasn't sweet. This one tasted of iron and smoke even from here.

Grief collects where people wept. Fine. Then the pool ahead was an ocean of it.

Quintana slept sitting up, back to the wall, lantern balanced on his knee like a man who'd learned never to be fully dark. His hands stayed loose and ready even asleep. She'd watched hands like those before—her father's, when he came home from guild work and couldn't stop checking the locks.

She hated that she was beginning to like the thief. Hated it the way you hate a draft in a warm room: aware of it constantly, unable to seal it out.

He had her father's map. He had walked her into the earth on the strength of a promise so vague it was barely a sentence—*I'll get him back, and yours too if it comes to that.* She had no reason to believe him. Thieves believed in nothing but exits. And yet when he'd said *the pools are how we'll know the way*, she'd believed him instantly, completely, the way she'd believed her father's hand on a chart.

Trust was just a map you hadn't checked yet.

She stood and walked toward the black pool. Just to look. Just to stand at the edge and feel the shape of the thing, the way her father taught her—read the coast before you commit the harbor.

Ten feet out, the pull strengthened. Five feet, it became a voice without words, an invitation shaped like a hand on the back of her neck. At three feet she could see images in it, faint as fish under ice—a corridor of stone, a door, a man's coat, the particular blue of her father's good coat—

She didn't decide to move. Her foot simply left the ground and came down closer, and the pool rose—not much, an inch, a ripple—but rising, reaching, and suddenly she was waist-deep in cold that wasn't cold, and it was in her ears, and it was saying *here, here, here* in her father's cadence—

Someone grabbed her collar and hauled.

She came up gasping on stone, soaked in nothing—the pool hadn't wet her, which was worse—and Quintana was above her, both fists full of her jacket, his face white as the salt streaks on the walls.

"The edge," he said, voice shaking. "You don't touch the edge. You especially don't wade into a pool of dying."

"It was his coat. I saw his—"

"If you'd gone under, you'd have lived his last hours front to back in the time it takes to drown. And drowning in them, Wren—" He stopped. Swallowed. "There's a woman in Verity who drowned in a pool of someone else's childhood. They fished her out three days later. She's still there, in a manner of speaking. Sitting by the water. Waiting to go back in."

Wren realized she was gripping his wrists hard enough to bruise. She didn't let go.

"You could have pulled me in instead," she said. "Easier. One less person to split the Regent's attention."

"I've spent my life taking things from people," Quintana said. "I'm told it's possible to do the opposite. I'm practicing."

* * *

They drank from the sad pools, as promised, and the water tasted faintly of other people's sorrow, which was somehow better than tasting of nothing. On the third day the road tilted steeper and the pools grew thick on the ground, silver and black and one memorable shade of green, and Dominic began using them as waypoints exactly as he'd described: follow grief downhill.

It was at the green pool that he made his mistake.

It sat in a hollow beside the road, shallow, bright as bottle glass, and it sang—quietly, tunelessly, a snatch of fiddle music. He knew before he knelt that it was a celebration. He told himself he was only reading it. Reading was his profession. You read a thing, you catalogued it, you moved on.

He touched two fingers to the surface.

A stranger's wedding day poured up through his skin. Not the same one from the fork—another, older, the colors sun-faded. A bride with flour on her hands because she'd been baking that morning and forgotten she was getting married until her sister dragged her to the dress. A groom who cried before she did. A village hall with a leaking roof everyone ignored. Dancing. Someone's uncle playing the fiddle badly and beautifully. A toast: *may you forget nothing*, which was the traditional blessing in the mining towns, Dominic realized, because forgetting was the local hazard, like damp or cave-in.

And underneath the joy, the thing that made it forgotten: the bride had died two winters later, and her widower had come down the miners' road and drunk himself empty at this pool, pouring the day out so he wouldn't have to carry it. The memory had pooled where he'd wept. Sugar over salt over stone.

Dominic lifted his fingers. He was crying. He wiped his face angrily.

"Whose was it?" Wren stood behind him. Of course she did.

"A wedding," he said. "Nobody's. Now."

"That's not how it works, is it? You told me it doesn't wash off."

"No. It doesn't." He flexed his fingers, feeling the faded colors settle somewhere behind his ribs, next to the duchess's grief and forty years of traded first kisses. "Now it's mine too. That's the trade. You take a drink, you keep the cup."

Wren sat down beside him at the pool's edge—safely back, he noted, a full arm's length farther than she'd stood yesterday. She'd learned.

"Do you remember any of yours?" she asked. "Before the guild."

"No."

"Does it frighten you? Walking into a mountain made of memory, when you're—"

"Hollow?" He said it before he could soften it.

"I was going to say *empty*. Hollow sounds like someone did it to you."

"Someone did." He looked at the green pool, at the faded bride dancing forever in bottle-glass. "That's what the Regent buys, Wren. Not souls, not exactly. Souls are just the vessel. She buys the part of you that says *this happened to me*. Everything else keeps going—heartbeat, appetite, the works. But the *this happened to me* goes quiet. I should know. I've sold pieces of mine and stolen everyone else's, and I've spent thirty years being the most functional hollow man you'll ever meet."

Wren was quiet a while. Then: "You cried at a stranger's wedding."

"Yes."

"That's the first true thing you've said without dressing it up as trade."

He laughed—short, rusty, surprised out of him. Above them, far up the throat of the road, something answered. Not an echo. Echoes came after. This came *during*, a low tone under the stone, sustained, patient, like a held breath the size of a mountain.

They both went still.

"That's not water," Wren whispered.

"No."

"That's not a pool."

"No." Dominic stood, and the sound came again, and this time he felt it in his teeth, in the scar tissue of his palms, in the locked door at the center of himself that he'd opened once and resealed—the humming pressed against that door like a thumb. The mountain knew what was behind it. The mountain had been singing about it all along.

"What is it?" Wren asked.

Dominic watched the lantern flame lean, though there was no wind.

"It's starting to sing," he said. "And I think—I think it knows my mother's song."
