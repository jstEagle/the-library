## 9. The Singing Stone

The road ended where the singing began.

We heard it for an hour before we saw anything—thin at first, like a kettle left on in another room, then thickening until it wasn't sound anymore so much as pressure, a hand laid flat against the breastbone. Wren stopped walking twice and pressed her palm over her heart as if to hold something in.

"Do you hear that?" she asked the second time.

"Everyone hears it," I said. "That's the point."

I had read about the mountain's throat in ledgers, of all places. The Saltwork Guild kept shipping records going back two hundred years, and buried among the manifests of bottled grief and decanted courage were the old miners' reports, written in a clerk's careful hand: *the deeper galleries sing; the men who work them come up knowing things they shouldn't.* The guild had bought those galleries cheap and sealed them cheaper. Nobody steals from a place that remembers everything you've done.

The tunnel opened without warning. One moment we were walking single-file through salt scarred with ancient pick-marks, and the next the walls fell away and we stood in a chamber that made my eyes water—not from dust but from scale. It was a throat in the anatomical sense, a vast vertical column of stone descending below us into blue-black dark and rising above us into shadow, and every surface of it was carved with lines. Writing, maybe. Or notation. Rows and rows of fine grooves spiraling up and down the walls like the growth rings of an impossible tree.

And the whole thing was singing.

Not with a mouth. Not with wind. The stone itself vibrated with it, a chord with no beginning, layered voices braided together—high, low, some almost words, some just ache. I put my hand against the wall to steady myself and the song came up through my fingers and into my teeth.

Then it came up through something else entirely.

The wall showed me a harbor. Not this harbor—the one above, but older, pre-guild, all timber and tar-smell. A woman laughing on a dock. Sunlight on wet rope. I knew, instantly and absolutely, that this memory belonged to a man dead eighty years, that he had stood exactly where I stood with his hand on this exact stone, and that the mountain had kept what he left behind the way other places keep footprints.

I snatched my hand back.

"It shows you things," I said. My voice sounded small and stupid under the choir. "Don't touch the walls."

Wren had already touched the walls. She stood with both palms flat against the stone, eyes closed, tears cutting clean tracks through the road-grime on her face. Her lips moved. Whatever the mountain was giving her, she was drinking it like a woman who'd crossed a desert.

"Wren." Nothing. "Wren."

"My mother," she said, without opening her eyes. "She used to hum this. I thought I made it up. I thought—I was five when she died, I thought I'd invented her voice out of nothing, and it turns out—" Her breath hitched. "It turns out the mountain knows her too."

* * *

We should have passed through quickly. That was the plan: the throat was the last passage before the court, and every hour we spent in range of the stone was an hour the Regent might feel us coming. But the chamber held us the way deep water holds a swimmer, gently, without effort, and when Wren sat down on a shelf of white salt rock and said, "I need to listen," I found I couldn't invent a reason to refuse her.

So we listened.

The mountain sang everything. That's the only honest way to say it. It sang a shepherd's first snowfall and a tax collector's slow corruption and a bride left at the altar who went home and laughed anyway, alone in her wedding kitchen, until the laugh turned into something else. It sang in memories the way an organ sings in pipes, each stored life a stop that could be pulled open, and the Regent—or whatever built this place, centuries before the Regent, because some of these voices were older than any ledger I'd ever touched—had arranged them into a single sustained note. A chord of kept things. Proof of purchase, played forever.

"This is how she does it," I said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "The souls she buys. They're not prisoners down there. They're instruments."

Wren looked up at me. Her face had gone very still, the stillness of someone deciding something.

"Play one," she said.

"What?"

"The map. You hid it in your own head—you told me on the road, don't look at me like that, you talk in your sleep. If this stone keeps everything and gives it back—" She pointed at the wall beside her. "Put it here. Let me see it. I've been following a piece of wax and your word for three hundred miles. I want to see where my father walked."

There were a dozen reasons to refuse. The map was the only leverage I had; showing it meant trusting her; the stone remembered everything, which meant once I set the map against it, the mountain would have a copy. Maybe the Regent would too.

But I had watched this woman nearly drown in a pool of her father's dying hours two days ago and come up swinging. And the truth, the truth I kept having to relearn around her, was that I was tired of being the only person I relied on.

I pressed the map-memory to the stone.

It took more out of me than I expected—pulling a stolen thing up from its hiding place always does, like dragging furniture up stairs—but the mountain helped. It wanted contents. It drank the map out of my hands and unrolled it across the rock face in lines of pale fire, and there it was: the court beneath Mount Verity, corridors and shelves, the cathedral-filing-system of souls, every stair and seal marked in the courier's dying hand.

Except the stone didn't stop there.

The map was inked in memory, and memory has margins, and the margins were full. Around the map's edges bloomed everything else the courier had carried—his daughter's face at four years old, gap-toothed; the taste of the tea she made him; the exact weight of the purse he'd saved to buy her soul back—and the mountain sang it all, generously, indiscriminately, into the chamber.

Wren made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

"Oh," she said. Just that. Then: "He practiced. Look—he practiced asking for me. Over and over, on the road, in taverns—there—" A scene kindled in firelight on the wall: the courier, alive, haggard, standing before a mirror in some rented room, straightening his coat, opening his mouth, closing it again. Rehearsing a bargain with an ancient thing. *My name is Alden Vael and I've come about my daughter.*

"Alden Vael," Wren whispered. "Nobody's called him that in twenty years. He was just Da."

The stone kept singing. It had all of him now, or all of what he'd carried near it, and it gave him back in pieces: the courier's fear climbing the mountain road, his hands shaking around a lantern; his fury at the guild, at Odile, at the paper trail that had priced his daughter's soul like cargo; his last night in the world above, sitting up all hours because he didn't want to waste any of the ones he had left.

And then the stone sang the part that broke her.

The audience. The court beneath—we hadn't reached it yet, but the courier had, and the mountain had recorded what he brought back. Shelves rising into darkness. The Hollow drifting between them like moths in tailored coats. And at the center, courteous and terrible, the Regent herself, listening to a exhausted man rehearse his plea one final time.

*I've come about my daughter,* Alden Vael said, and his voice cracked exactly where it had cracked in the rehearsal, and the Regent smiled the smile of a creditor who already knows the balance of your accounts.

What he offered her was not money. There was nothing in his pockets the court wanted. So he offered the only collateral he carried that she valued.

Himself.

*Passage for the girl,* the Regent said—or the memory said, the stone reproducing even the dry silk of her voice. *Her soul returns to the world above, free and clear. Yours stays here, on my shelf, and sings.*

"And he said yes." Wren was on her feet, and her voice came out flat and precise, which is how I'd learned to recognize her rage; grief made her quiet, but anger made her legible. "He said yes. He walked out of there without a soul and climbed back up the mountain and finished the run anyway, because the deal wasn't done until the map was delivered, until *someone* delivered the map—" She stopped. Turned to me.

The map was still burning on the wall between us. The courier's final act. He hadn't been fleeing with stolen goods after all, not exactly. He'd been making one last delivery—one last job, taken on with a body that was already mostly sold, because the terms required it, because the Regent's bargains always require the debtor to dig their own grave.

"He gave me the map," Wren said slowly. "In your back room. Dying. He gave it to you because—" Her jaw worked. "Because the delivery had to be completed. Because if the map never arrived, the contract voids, and I go back on the shelf. His whole sacrifice, contingent on some stranger balancing books in a salt warehouse."

"I'm sorry," I said, and discovered I meant it, which frightened me more than the mountain did.

"Don't be sorry." She turned back to the wall, to her father's face rendered in firelight and stone, and I watched something close behind her expression like a door. "Be useful."

* * *

We sat in the chamber while the song worked on us, because there was no longer any pretending we could leave untouched. The stone doesn't shout; it seeps. I caught myself humming a lullaby in a language I don't speak and had to bite it off mid-note. Wren talked more than she ever had—not to me exactly, but adjacent to me, narrating fragments as the mountain surfaced them: a birthday, a beating, a winter when her father sold his coat and pretended he'd lost it gambling, because pride was the one luxury he refused to give up.

"He knew," she said at last, quietly. "At the end. In your guildhouse, bleeding out—he knew he'd already paid for me, and he still couldn't say it. All he said was *the Regent keeps what's mine.*" She shook her head. "He thought I was still down there. He died thinking the trade hadn't gone through yet."

"It hadn't," I said. "Not fully."

She looked at me.

"The map is proof of terms, not proof of completion. Guild law—court law, same rotten root—the contract settles when the collateral changes hands on both sides. Her side took his soul." I nodded at the wall, at the recording of the court's shelves. "His side never delivered. Which means somewhere in that filing system there's a ledger entry marked open. His soul is held in escrow. Pending."

Wren stared at me. "You're telling me my father's death can be undone by paperwork."

"I'm telling you everything down there can be undone by paperwork. That's the whole horror of the place. Souls filed like invoices." I heard my own voice go flat and professional, the guild-clerk cadence, and for once I let it. "I've spent eleven years learning the grammar of theft, Wren. Bottled years, first kisses, a duchess's grief—all of it moves by entry, by signature, by stamp. If the court runs on ledgers, then the court can be robbed. Properly robbed. Line by line."

The song filled the silence after that, patient, enormous.

Wren crouched, picked up a shard of fallen salt-rock, and turned it in her fingers. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register entirely. The grief was still there—I could hear it underneath, a bass note that would never resolve—but it had been folded into something harder.

"When we started out," she said, "all I wanted was the map. Get the map, get to the court, trade fair, walk out with Da. Simple. Stupid." She looked up. "I don't want the map anymore."

"No?"

"The map got him killed. The map is *bait*—don't you see it? The Regent wants it delivered. She engineered the whole route; she let a dying courier crawl halfway across a kingdom because she needs a thief to carry it the rest of the way, and here I am, delivering you." The shard of salt-rock snapped in her grip. "So fine. Deliverance it is. But not the delivery she ordered."

"What do you want, then?"

Wren stood, and behind her the mountain sang her father's rehearsal one more time—*my name is Alden Vael and I've come about my daughter*—and she let it play, all of it, the crack in his voice and the shaking hands and the yes he gave, and she faced it with her chin up like a woman taking an oath over a body.

"I want her shelf," she said. "The one with his soul on it. I want it empty."

"That's the plan I already have."

"Your plan ends at the door, Dominic. Mine goes further." She dropped the broken rock. "I want the Regent to know, at the end, exactly whose daughter emptied her court. I want her to hear his name in her own throat."

For a long moment neither of us moved. The stone sang on, indifferent, keeping everything—including, I realized with a chill that started in my spine and spread outward, this conversation. Every word we spoke here became permanent the moment we said it. Somewhere below us, the Regent owned a copy.

Which meant she already knew we were coming.

* * *

I understood it fully an hour later, in the last stretch of tunnel before the descent into the court proper, when the character of the song changed.

The mountain had been singing memories—stored, static, archived. Now, threading through the great chord like a wire through wool, came something live. Something attentive. A new voice, thin and cold and unmistakably aimed, moving along the stone the way a searchlight moves along a wall.

It was reading us.

I felt it pass over me—weightless and total, turning my pockets out, checking my teeth. It lingered longest on Wren, on the shape of her grief, and then it withdrew upward, toward the court, unhurried, the way a host withdraws to tell the kitchen a table is coming.

The singing softened. Not quieter—warmer. Welcoming.

"She knows," Wren said. Not a question.

"She's known since the throat," I said. "Maybe since the road. The mountain's her instrument, and we've been playing it for two days." I touched the satchel where the wax-sealed map rode against my ribs, though the true map lived behind my eyes where no one could take it without taking me. "Every step we've walked down here, we've walked on her floor, under her roof, inside her song."

"So we turn back."

"We can't. Your father's soul is in escrow down there, mine is in a vault down there, and the only way out of a creditor's house"—I started down the sloping passage, toward the light that was beginning to rise ahead, gold and wrong, lamplight where no lamps should be—"is through her ledger."

The tunnel narrowed, then opened, and below us the court beneath Mount Verity spread out like a cathedral built by accountants: ten thousand shelves ascending into darkness, and between them, drifting with terrible patience, the shapes of the Hollow, tending their files.

One of them, far below, looked up.

It had been waiting for us.
