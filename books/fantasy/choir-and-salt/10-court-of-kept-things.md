## 10. Court of Kept Things

The door into the mountain was not a door.

It was a throat of black stone that had been carved—or grown—into the shape of an open mouth, and standing before it, Dominic understood why the salt-miners' road had ended where it ended. Roads go to places people want to reach. Nothing wanted to reach this.

Wren stood beside him with her father's knife in her fist and her chin up in a way he was beginning to recognize as fear wearing armor. "Say something," she said. "You've been quiet since the singing stone."

"I'm composing an apology," Dominic said. "To whoever has to explain us."

He touched the map in his mind—the real one burned into the memory he'd hidden inside himself, the wax-sealed original traded away to Odile's delegation three days ago and now somewhere behind them with Sera Voss. The map had shown him this: a seam in the mountain's flank shaped like a keyhole, and beyond it, the court. What the map hadn't shown him was how it would feel to stand at the threshold of the place where his childhood sat on a shelf like a jar of preserves.

"Ready?" Wren asked.

"No," he said, and stepped through.

* * *

The first thing was the smell: parchment, beeswax, and underneath both something older and sweeter—the smell of the memory-pools they'd drunk from on the road down, concentrated until it wasn't a smell anymore but a pressure, a hand flat against the back of the skull.

The second thing was the light. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a pale gold wash without source, and it fell on shelves.

Shelves. Ten thousand of them. A hundred thousand. They rose out of sight into a vaulted dark that might have been sky or might have been more shelves, row upon row upon row, receding in every direction like rain made vertical. Each shelf held bottles. Not glass—he saw that as they moved closer, Wren's breath going shallow beside him. The vessels were made of something like amber and something like ice, and each one held a curl of light that moved when you looked at it. A soul didn't sit still in its bottle. A soul paced.

"Cathedral," Wren whispered. "It's a cathedral."

"It's a warehouse," Dominic said, because his mouth was faster than his sense of wonder, and because naming it a warehouse kept his hands steady. But even as he said it, he knew she was right. The rows were laid out in a nave. The vaulting overhead arched like ribs. And at the far end, where an altar would be, there was a dais of white stone, and on the dais—

He stopped looking at the dais. He'd get there eventually. Thieves don't look at the prize first; thieves look at the guards.

And here were the guards.

They came between the shelves on soft feet, and they were beautiful in the way that furniture is beautiful—proportioned, polished, purposeful. Courtiers. They wore robes of grey so fine it seemed woven from fog, and their faces were smooth and courteous and utterly blank, eyes like coins left in a fountain, and when one turned toward them Dominic felt the specific vertigo of looking at a person-shaped absence.

"The Hollow," Wren said. "My father wrote about them. Courtiers who sold their names for rank. They—" She faltered. One of the Hollow had drifted close, head tilted, regarding them with polite appetite.

"They gave up the memories that made them *them*," Dominic finished quietly. "Rank for self. That's the trade." He knew the trade. He'd been making small versions of it his whole life—giving away pieces of himself until he was forgettable, until he was nothing anyone could hold onto. These creatures had simply completed the transaction.

The nearest Hollow inclined its head. When it spoke, its voice was warm and toneless, like a room after everyone has left. "Be welcome in the court beneath Mount Verity. The Regent keeps all things. You will be kept also, if it pleases her."

"And if it doesn't?" Wren asked.

The Hollow smiled. It was a perfect smile. It had been filed under *hospitality*, and it fit the face the way a ledger entry fits a column. "Then you will be kept anyway. All things are kept. That is what keeping means."

Dominic's thief's heart did something shameful then. It sang.

Because while the Hollow spoke its courteous menace, his instincts—the ones Odile had spent twenty years sharpening—were reading the room. Every vault he'd ever cracked had a grammar, and this one spoke fluently. The shelves were organized by category, he could see it already: near the entrance, shallow racks holding thin pale lights, the trivia of souls—a grudge here, a lullaby there, someone's talent for whistling, sold cheap. Deeper in, the lights grew denser, warmer, the bottles heavier in their cradles. Loves. Griefs. Whole childhoods. And at the center, past the nave, past the dais, a vault within the vault: a cage of black iron and white stone where the lights burned so bright the Hollow tending them wore smoked-glass visors.

Souls entire. Not pieces. Collateral, like the courier's. Like—

He stopped walking.

Wren bumped into him. "What? Dominic, what?"

Three shelves up, in a row marked with a brass tag he could read from ten feet away because he had spent his life reading other people's labels, sat a bottle of amber-and-ice no bigger than a fist. Inside it curled a light the color of late afternoon through kitchen windows. It was pacing. It moved the way a boy moves when he can't sit still in school, quick and restless, pressing against the walls of its container and sliding down and starting again.

The label was written in ink the color of dried blood, in a hand Dominic knew better than his own—which was easy, because he didn't know his own hand at all; he'd never seen himself write.

*One (1) childhood, male, age 0–12 years,* said the label, in Odile Vane's precise, unhurried script. *Sold in full. Payment rendered to the court beneath Mount Verity against delivery of goods specified. Condition: excellent. Provenance: guild acquisition, Harbor Quarter.*

Beneath that, smaller, in the same hand, the note that undid him:

*Buyer requested the boy remember nothing of the sale.*

Dominic reached up. His fingers were steady—that was the horror of it, they were perfectly steady—and they closed around the bottle, and it was warm, and the light inside pressed itself against his palm like a cat that knows the hand holding it.

"Dominic." Wren's voice came from very far away. "There are four of those things looking at us. Put it down. *Put it down.*"

He put it down. He arranged it exactly as it had been, label squared to the shelf edge, because a thief leaves no trace or leaves a trace that points elsewhere, and his hands did this while the rest of him screamed. Then he turned and smiled at the Hollow, and found to his mild surprise that his face produced a perfect smile too, warm and toneless, hospitality filed and fitting.

"We're expected," he said. "The Regent is expecting us. We've come about the courier's account."

The Hollow regarded him for a long moment. Somewhere in that coin-eyed blankness, something consulted something. "Yes," it said at last. "The account of Corvin Ashe. Outstanding balance: one soul, entire. And—" the smile widened by a precise millimeter, "—one payment received and held in trust these nineteen years. You are the payment, aren't you. Come to inspect the merchandise."

* * *

They walked the nave toward the dais, flanked by two Hollow apiece, and Dominic catalogued everything because cataloguing was how he stayed a person.

He noted the filing system: alphabetical within category, category within decade, decade within—of all things—*mood*. Joy shelved apart from sorrow. He noted the maintenance crews: Hollow with feathered brushes, dusting bottles that gathered no dust. He noted the security, which was the true marvel: there were no locks. Not one lock in a cathedral of stolen souls. Why would there be? Who could steal from a mountain that remembered everything? The only theft possible here would have to come from outside, from a man whose whole art was making himself absent from his own life.

He noted, too, the way some of the lights leaned toward them as they passed. Bottled souls, straining toward anything that still walked free. One of them, in a row marked *Griefs, Maternal, 1800s*, flickered in a rhythm he almost recognized—a rhythm like singing—and he made himself look away before it could teach him something he'd have to carry.

"You're reading the room," Wren murmured. Her shoulder pressed his. In the gold light her face was set and pale, and she had not once looked at her father's soul, though she must have been searching every shelf. Discipline like hers was rarer than courage.

"I'm pricing it," he said.

"And?"

"And I've robbed banks with worse manners and better locks." He kept his voice below the Hollows' hearing, if hearing was even the word for how those things perceived. "No locks. No wards I can feel. Just them, and whatever's sitting on that dais, and the fact that the whole building is the vault. You can't crack a vault that's also the country you're standing in."

"So we're thieves with nowhere to fence the goods."

"We're thieves," Dominic said, "who are currently being escorted to the buyer. Which is either the worst position in the world or the best one, depending entirely on whether the Regent likes to talk before she eats."

The dais grew as they approached, resolving from glow into structure: steps of white stone worn hollow in the centers, as if generations of feet had climbed them to surrender something. And above the steps, enthroned in a chair carved from a single block of salt, sat a figure that was mostly shadow and patience.

But Dominic barely saw the Regent. Because halfway up the steps, set apart on a pedestal of its own like the crown jewel of the whole impossible cathedral, stood a bottle larger than all the others, and inside it burned not one light but many—dozens, scores, tangled together like a skein of golden thread thrown into fire—and the tag hanging from its neck bore the court's seal and a line of text that stopped his breath:

*Choir, resident. Souls in residence: 214. Status: singing. Do not open while singing.*

Two hundred and fourteen souls, bottled together, singing. Singing the song that kept the mountain stable. Singing the song he'd heard through the stone on the road down, the song that had shown Wren her father's last act, the song that was—his skin went cold—the same song his mother used to hum over the sink, the same melody, note for note, that lived in the torn remnant of the memory he'd finally unlocked and found gutted by a professional hand.

The mountain hadn't learned that song. The mountain had bought it.

"Dominic." Wren again, urgent now, her hand hard on his arm. "Look at me. Whatever you just figured out, keep it behind your teeth. They're watching your face."

She was right. The nearest Hollow had gone very still, its coin-eyes fixed on him with an attention that felt less like curiosity than appraisal. Above them, on the salt throne, the shadow that was the Regent shifted, and when it spoke, its voice was dry and kind and old as the shelves, and it said the last thing in the world he wanted to hear:

"There you are," the Regent said. "I have been keeping your childhood safe for nineteen years, little thief. Come up and tell me—have you come to take it home?"

Behind him, faint and terrible, the choir began a new verse, and Dominic recognized the tune before the first bar finished, and his mother's voice rose unbidden in the wrecked room of his memory to sing along.
