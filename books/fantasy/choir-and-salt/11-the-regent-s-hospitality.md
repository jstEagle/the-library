## 11. The Regent's Hospitality

They came for us an hour after the Hollow filed us away in the guest cells—cells being a generous word for rooms without doors, because the Regent clearly understood that beneath Mount Verity there was nowhere to run that wasn't still inside her.

The escort arrived without sound. Three Hollow, moving down the corridor like furniture deciding to walk, and behind them a fourth carrying a folded gown of grey silk. The lead Hollow spoke in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

"The Regent will receive the thief and his companion."

Not *the guests*. Not *Wren Nakamura and Dominic Quintana*, though Wren had given her father's name freely enough on the road. *The thief.* Word had traveled ahead of us, which meant word could travel at all, which meant the Regent had ears even here, two hundred feet under the skin of the world.

I looked at Wren. She was pale but level, arms crossed, jaw set the way it had been set since the Singing Stone showed her what her father had done. Grief had burned off everything soft in her and left something with edges.

"Don't eat anything," she said.

"I hadn't planned to."

"And don't agree to anything."

"That," I said, "might be harder."

* * *

The throne room—if that was the word—was a cathedral built by someone who had heard about cathedrals secondhand and decided they lacked adequate storage.

Shelves ran from floor to a ceiling lost in darkness, hundreds of feet of them, curving up along walls that might have been natural stone once but now bore the smooth, licked look of things that had been eaten slowly. Every shelf was full. Jars, boxes, ledgers, stoppered phials glowing faintly amber, grey bottles that gave nothing back when you looked into them. And among the containers, other things: a wedding ring threaded onto a cord. A child's shoe. A tooth. Labels everywhere, tens of thousands of labels, written in hands I recognized because I'd spent ten years reading them—the Saltwork Guild's clerks, Odile's own ledger-masters. Inventory. This was where inventory went when it was sold forward.

Souls, filed like correspondence.

My thief's instincts woke up and started doing arithmetic before my fear could stop them. A vault this size needed a cataloguing system; a system had choke points; choke points were where a man like me lived. I counted sight lines. I counted the Hollow. I noted which shelves sat nearest the doors and which sat in the deep dark where nobody would hear a jar uncorked. It was obscene, standing in a cathedral of stolen people and thinking *I could rob this place*, but the instinct is older than conscience and it doesn't ask permission.

Wren made a small sound beside me. I followed her eyes up. Somewhere in those thousands of labels, somewhere, was one that read *Courier*. Her father. She was trying to find him the way you'd scan a graveyard for one stone, and I put a hand on her arm because if she started pulling jars we'd both die in the next minute and her father's soul would spend eternity filed between a miller's grudge and somebody's memory of rain.

"Later," I breathed. "There'll be a later or there won't."

At the far end of the hall, beneath an arch of black salt, stood a plain wooden chair. On it sat a woman so old that old didn't seem like the right shape for her anymore.

She was small. That surprised me. I'd imagined the Regent of Mount Verity as vast—a giantess, a mountain wearing a person—but she was barely five feet of grey-robed woman with white hair coiled at her neck and hands folded in her lap like a retired schoolmistress. It was the eyes that did it. They weren't blind and they weren't dead, but looking into them was like looking down a well where the water had been drunk. You could see exactly how deep the emptiness went.

She watched us cross the whole length of her court without rising, without speaking, and when we stopped ten feet away she smiled as if greeting grandchildren.

"There you are," she said. Her voice was dry paper, warm at the edges. "Sit. No—" as we hesitated, "—stand, then. Standing is fine. Sitting is a habit of the old." She gestured at the shelves around us with something like affection. "Forgive the clutter. When you live long enough, everything you're given becomes a keepsake, and eventually you need somewhere to keep them."

"You know why we've come," Wren said.

"I know why *he's* come." The Regent's eyes moved to me and stayed there, and I felt the look go through me like a hand going through a coat's pockets. "You, girl, are a complication he brought with him. But him—him I've been expecting for years."

"Then you have me at a disadvantage," I said.

"No. You have yourself at a disadvantage. That's rather your condition, isn't it?" She unfolded her hands. "Dominic Quintana. Ledger-keeper of the Saltwork Guild. Trained by Odile Vane herself in the family trade. Do you know what the trade actually is? Not what the guild calls it—*acquisition, appraisal, brokerage*—but what it is?"

"Memory-theft," I said.

"Theft is clumsy." Dismissal flickered across her face. "A thief breaks in and takes. What you do is finer. You walk into a person and find the thing they can least afford to lose, and you lift it so gently they never feel the absence—they only feel a smoothness where the sharp place used to be. You don't steal memories. You steal *weight*. And you have been doing it since you were young enough that no one thought to guard against a boy with kind eyes." She leaned forward slightly. "Do you know why that matters to me, Dominic Quintana?"

"Enlighten me."

"Because I am full," she said, "and empty, and both at once, and it is unbearable." The words came out flat and terrible, a confession delivered like a menu. "I ate my own past to gain what I am, and power remembers nothing. Every year the court brings me souls to sing the mountain stable, and every year I remember less about why stability matters, or what I'm ruling, or who I was before the hunger. I must be fed not just souls but *content*—lives, loves, griefs, whole childhoods poured into me so that I can remember how to be a queen instead of a stomach." Her smile returned, patient, bottomless. "And here walks in a man whose gift is taking weight out of people without breaking them. Do you understand what you are, to me?"

"An instrument," I said.

"The perfect instrument," she agreed pleasantly. "Odile knew it too. Why do you think she sold a seven-year-old boy's childhood to me twenty-three years ago, and then kept the boy close for two decades? Not sentiment. *Stock management.* She was raising her finest blade in my pantry."

The floor seemed to tilt. Twenty-three years. Chapter three of my own ledger, the entry I'd found in Odile's vault and told myself I'd misread. *One childhood, male, age seven. Sold to the court beneath Mount Verity. Payment received in full.*

"You're saying my childhood is here."

"On that shelf." She pointed, lazily, at a wall maybe forty feet up—a grey bottle with a wax seal, indistinguishable from ten thousand others except that a red thread was tied around its neck. "Third row, marked in Odile's hand. It arrived sealed. I haven't opened it. I save things, you see. Even appetite has manners."

Wren's hand found mine and gripped hard. "This is very touching," she said, in a voice that could have stripped paint, "but my father's soul is in this room, and I'm not leaving without it."

"Yes," the Regent said, turning to her with genuine sympathy, "the courier. He came to me months ago, alone, with nothing to trade but himself. He asked me to release his daughter's soul—he'd pledged it as collateral years back, did you know? A desperate man, borrowing against his own child. He was honest, at least. He said, *take me in her place.*" Her well-eyes softened. "I declined. His soul is thin. Yours, girl, is dense with anger and love and it sings beautifully. But his devotion was worth filing. He's in the second gallery, third shelf, blue label. I keep families together where I can. It's tidier."

Wren made a sound I'll never be able to describe as long as I live, and I pulled her arm through mine and held on.

"So here is my offer," the Regent said, and now her voice changed—warmer, lower, the voice of someone sliding a knife between your ribs so gently you thank them for the massage. "To you, thief. Two services, two payments. First: give me the map the courier died to deliver. Second: perform one theft for me, here, in my court. There is a memory I need lifted from a certain party—a delicate extraction, beyond my own abilities, since my touch spoils what it takes. Do this, and I will release the courier's soul into your keeping. Both of them. Father and daughter, whole and free, to walk out of my mountain together."

Wren turned to me and I watched hope hit her like a wave hitting a seawall—wanting to believe, knowing better. "Dominic. Don't—"

"And the map," I said over her, because if I let myself hear her finish I'd never negotiate, "you get. What does the theft cost *me*?"

"Nothing," the Regent said. "That's the beauty of you."

Everything in me that had survived twenty years of the guild rang like a struck bell. Nothing. Nothing is never the price. Nothing is what they tell you while they're measuring you.

"You want something," I said. "Nobody offers two souls for a map and a job."

"What I want," she said, "is a thief who is hungry again. You've grown careful, Dominic. Careful thieves leave residue. But a thief who *wants*—" her smile widened by one tooth, "—that I can use. So. One theft. The map. And in exchange, the courier goes home to his daughter."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you stay here, and the girl stays here, and eventually I take what I need the slow way. I am very old and very patient and the mountain does not leak." She spread her small hands. "But you won't refuse. I can see it. You've been dying of curiosity since you walked in—that bottle on the third row is screaming at you, isn't it? Your mother's voice. The smell of wherever you lived before the guild. Your own name, before Odile renamed you. All I ask in return is one small theft."

"One more question," I said. My mouth was dry. "The theft you want performed. From whom?"

The Regent settled back in her chair, folding her hands, and for a moment her well-eyes looked almost girlish with pleasure.

"Why," she said, "from yourself."

The hall went very quiet. Somewhere above us, the mountain hummed its single low note.

"A thief cannot open his own locks—I've tried for centuries, believe me. But you'll have help. The extraction is simple: there is a memory in your head, placed there recently, hidden in a place no thief would think to look. I want it taken out intact. Do that, and everyone walks away rich." She tilted her head. "You've already guessed, of course. You hide things beautifully. But I am older than hiding."

The map. She meant the map—the courier's map, the one I'd stolen from myself in a bleeding man's last minute of life, folded into a memory of my own making where no guild enforcer, no Regent, no god would ever search. She couldn't reach into me. But she could hire me to reach into myself, and the price of the courier's soul was that I hand her the one thing two people had died to protect.

And she'd framed it as a gift. That was the artistry. She'd wrapped the theft of the map in the language of payment, so that even my betrayal would arrive pre-forgiven, contracted, invoiced. Odile had taught me that trick. Or Odile had learned it here.

"I need time to consider," I said.

"Of course." The Regent waved a hand, gracious as spring. "Dinner will be sent to your cells. Eat nothing that tastes of salt—it's how I mark what's mine." Her eyes drifted, one last time, to the grey bottle with the red thread, forty feet up in the dark. "Do think quickly, Dominic. Appetite is a clock. And yours—" she smiled at me with all the warmth of an empty house, "—yours is already running."

The Hollow escorted us out. Wren didn't speak until the guest cells swallowed us again, and then she rounded on me, eyes blazing.

"Tell me you're not considering it."

"I'm considering everything." I sat on the stone bunk and put my head in my hands. "She's offering your father back. She's offering him *to you*. Do you understand what that's worth?"

"I understand what it costs. The map. The one thing standing between her and—" Wren stopped. Went still. "Dominic. What exactly did she mean, *from yourself*?"

I looked up at her. At the set of her jaw, the ink stain on her knuckle from her father's letters, the fury and the faith she carried for a man she thought was me. And I felt the full shape of the trap settle over us both, elegant as a ledger line: take the deal, betray the map, betray her—or refuse, and watch her father stay shelved forever while the Regent took what she wanted anyway, slower.

There was no version of this where I got to keep everything. There never is. The guild's first lesson: *every acquisition is a loss you've agreed to schedule.*

"She wants me to steal the map back out of my own head," I said quietly. "And hand it to her. That's the theft. That's the whole theft."

Wren sat down slowly beside me. For a long moment neither of us said anything, and the mountain hummed through the stone like a held breath.

"So we're agreed," she said finally, in a voice that had gone very cold and very steady. "We take whatever she gives us—and then we rob her blind."

I reached out and gripped her hand, and for one dangerous moment I forgot to be a man who trusts no one.

"One condition," I said. "When we do this—you don't let me forget why."

* * *

Dinner arrived an hour later. Bread, cheese, dried fruit, a jug of water. Nothing tasted of salt.

I didn't touch any of it.

Neither did Wren. We sat across from each other in the doorless cell while the mountain sang its long note overhead, and between us on the floor lay the map—not the physical one, which I'd burned on the road as a decoy, but the true one, folded into a memory I had built brick by brick to be unopenable: a grey morning, a harbor market, a woman laughing at a fruit stall who had Wren's exact laugh.

I had hidden the key to the court beneath Mount Verity inside a memory of her. Before I'd ever met her.

Which meant either the map was smarter than I was, or some part of me had known, all along, exactly what I'd be asked to trade.
