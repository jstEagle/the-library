## 12. Terms of Trade

The guest cells of the court beneath Mount Verity were not prisons, exactly. That was the first thing Dominic understood about them. Prisons were for people the Regent feared. These rooms—carved from pale stone that hummed faintly when you pressed your palm to it, furnished with a bed, a basin, a shelf of books no one had written in two hundred years—these were waiting rooms. The court was very old and very patient, and it had learned that most things worth keeping would stay put if you made them comfortable.

Dominic did not stay put. He paced.

The cell had no door, only an archway hung with a curtain of woven salt-crystal that glittered like frozen rain. Beyond it, the corridor glowed with the same sourceless light that filled the whole court, light that came from nowhere and cast no shadows, as if the mountain itself were remembering daylight badly. Somewhere down that corridor, Wren was in her own comfortable room, being comfortable. Somewhere above them both, three hundred feet of living stone, the Regent sat in her hall of shelves with the map in her hands and an offer on the table.

*Bring me one more theft. And I will give you the girl's soul, whole and singing.*

He could still feel the shape of the bargain, the way a thief feels the weight of a purse through a coat. It was a good offer. That was what made it obscene. Wren's father had died for her soul. Dominic could finish what the courier started with a single act of his oldest craft, and the price—the price was only a memory. One memory. Of one person. He'd stolen thousands. He'd forgotten hundreds more. What was one Wren against a whole soul set free?

*You're negotiating with yourself,* he thought. *That's how Odile started too.*

He stopped pacing. Sat on the edge of the bed, which was soft in the way of things that wanted nothing from you except stillness, and listened to the mountain hum.

* *

She came at what might have been midnight, or might have been no hour at all. He heard her before he saw her—boots on stone, quick, angry—and then the salt curtain rattled aside and Wren stood in the archway with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the expression he was beginning to think of as her working face. The face she'd worn the day she found him in the harbor market and demanded the map or revenge. She'd traded up since then. Now she wanted both.

"You've been talking to her again," Wren said.

"I've been *listening* to her again. There's a difference."

"Not to me." She came in without being asked, which was also becoming a habit, and planted herself in front of him. In the shadowless light her eyes were hard to read, gray going silver. "The Hollow won't tell me anything. They smile like doors closing. So tell me. What did she offer?"

Dominic considered lying. He was good at lying; it came bundled with the theft. But he'd spent the descent under the world learning something about this girl—that she drank strangers' memories out of puddles and wept for people she'd never met, that her grief was the honest kind, unbottled—and lies sat differently in his mouth now. Heavier.

"She'll release your father's soul," he said. "Give it to us. Whole. In exchange for one more job."

"What job?"

"A theft."

Wren waited. She was better at waiting than he was. The silence stretched until it creaked.

"The theft is a memory," Dominic said finally. "Mine."

"Whose memory?"

"*Mine.*" He heard his own voice go flat, thief-flat, hiding the vault behind the ledger. "One specific memory. She takes it, I forget it, and you walk out of here with your father. Clean trade."

"Forget what?" And then he watched her get there, watch it land on her face in stages: confusion, calculation, and then something worse than anger. "Me," she said. "It's me. She wants you to steal—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, he could see her hating it, and he loved her for hating it, which was inconvenient. "You'd forget me."

"I'd forget the deal. Not you entirely. Just—" But there was no *just*. He knew how memory worked; he'd made a profession of its architecture. You couldn't lift one thread without unraveling the weave. Take the bargain and Wren would become a stranger with a familiar face. The road under the world, the pool where he'd tasted a stranger's wedding day, the moment in the singing stone when she'd stopped crying and started planning a regicide—all of it gone, and the woman standing in front of him reduced to an unexplained ache, a name someone else would have to give him.

"That's not a trade," Wren said quietly. "That's a burial."

"It's your *father*, Wren."

"And it's your—" She stopped. Raked a hand through her hair. When she spoke again she'd wrestled her voice back down to level. "Do you know what my father would say? He spent his last coin on my soul. His actual last coin, and then his actual last breath. If you told him the price of getting it back was one memory of one annoying thief, he'd pay it himself and tip the Regent besides. But *I'm* not paying it. I didn't come down here to be bought with pieces of you."

"That's not your choice to make."

"It became my choice the moment you decided to be noble about it." She sat down beside him on the bed, not touching, close enough that he could feel the warmth off her shoulder. For a while neither of them said anything, and the mountain hummed its noteless hum, and Dominic thought about how strange it was that silence could be loud.

"Refuse," Wren said at last. "We refuse. We find another way. Your paperwork trick—"

"Every forged entry rings a bell somewhere in this mountain. We have days at best before she notices the pattern. Probably less, now that she has the map and can read the shelves herself."

"Then Sera—"

"Is coming tomorrow with Odile's delegation." He hadn't meant to say it yet. It came out anyway. "A guild convoy, full honors, arriving to formalize terms. Odile doesn't send delegations. Odile sends invoices."

Wren turned to look at him. "Then we're out of time."

"We were out of time in the harbor," Dominic said. "Everything since has been interest."

* *

They argued until arguing wore itself out, the way arguments do—not resolved, just exhausted, circling the same three positions until the words went smooth with handling. *Take the deal. Refuse the deal. Find a third door.* There was always a third door. Thieves believed that the way sailors believed in weather, and half the time they drowned anyway.

When Wren finally left—squeezing his hand once, hard, like a promise or a threat, he genuinely couldn't tell which—he lay awake listening to the court breathe. It did breathe. He'd noticed it the second night: a slow tidal rhythm in the stone, in the light, in the humming, as though the whole mountain inhaled and exhaled on some vast patient cycle. A sleeping thing digesting. Or a ledger being read, line by line, by something that never skimmed.

Sleep took him like a pickpocket takes a purse—quickly, professionally, from behind.

He dreamed of the door he never opens. In the dream it stood open, which it never had in waking life, and beyond it the mountain sang, and a woman with his mother's face stood in the throat of the stone with her mouth moving, and no sound came out, and he woke with salt on his lips and no way to know if he'd been crying or if the walls were sweating.

* *

The delegation arrived to the ringing of bells that had no bells.

Dominic heard it from the corridor, where the Hollow had collected him with their courteous, door-closing smiles—they called it *escorting*, the way an auctioneer calls it *acquiring*—and marched him toward the great hall. The sound rolled through the mountain like weather: a bright, thin pealing that came from everywhere and nowhere, and he realized it was the court itself announcing guests, stone vibrating in the register of ceremony. Mount Verity remembered every arrival it had ever received. Apparently it liked to make an occasion of it.

In the hall of shelves, the delegation was assembling beneath the Regent's dais, and the sight of it hit Dominic like cold water. Twelve guild enforcers in salt-gray livery, standing with the drilled precision of people who had crossed the world under orders. Travel chests stacked in neat rows. And at the front, in a traveling coat the color of wet slate, her white hair cropped shorter than guild fashion allowed, stood Guildmaster Odile Quintana.

His aunt. His maker. The woman who had bought his childhood and taught him to steal other people's pasts so he'd never miss having one of his own.

And beside her, hands loose at her sides, wearing her market-day smile like a mask that fit too well—Sera Voss. Their eyes met across the hall. Hers gave him nothing. That nothing was a message in itself: *I know you can read me. So I've written a blank page. Figure out what's underneath.*

The Regent received them from her throne of stacked ledgers, and her voice filled the hall without effort, dry and courteous as dust settling.

"Guildmaster. You bring me goods."

"I bring you inventory," Odile said. Her voice hadn't changed in fifteen years. It was the voice of the flashback, the one that lived in Dominic's head in gold-lettered memory: *a commodity's value is measured by what someone will surrender to keep it.* "Twelve chests. Bottled and sealed. First memories, mostly. Some loves. A few deaths—those keep well, oddly. Grief especially. Grief is the finest vintage in the cellar."

Dominic felt the floor tilt under his certainties. Twelve chests. The guild's vault held twelve chests. He knew because he kept the ledgers, because he'd catalogued every bottle in every crate, every duchess's grief and cooper's first snowfall, in his own careful hand. This wasn't a shipment.

This was the whole vault.

"The entirety of your stock," the Regent said, and even she sounded faintly surprised, in the way of an ancient thing encountering novelty. "That is not a trade, Guildmaster. That is a liquidation."

"I'm exiting the business." Odile's chin came up. "Retirement requires capital. You're offering immortality to favored clients. I'm simply negotiating the premium."

The hall went quiet enough that Dominic could hear the mountain breathing. He stood among the enforcers, invisible by lifelong habit, and did arithmetic he didn't want to do. Odile had emptied the guild. Every bottled year, every stolen kiss, every scrap of harvested childhood—including, presumably, whatever fragments remained unsold of his own seventh year. All of it poured into this hall to purchase one thing: a seat beside the throne that never aged. She hadn't come to negotiate terms of delivery.

She'd come to move in.

*You sold the guild,* he thought, with something between horror and admiration. *You sold the whole rotten church, and you're the only one who gets communion.*

He looked at Sera. Sera was looking at the chests. And Dominic had spent enough years reading faces across ledgers to catch it—the flicker, quickly buried, of a woman doing her own arithmetic and disliking the sum. Twelve chests. Her devotion purchased and installed like a bottle on a shelf. If loyalty could be sold once, it could be sold twice, and Odile had just demonstrated the exchange rate.

The Regent's hollow eyes moved across the hall and settled, briefly, warmly, horribly, on Dominic. *My thief,* the look said. *Still deciding what you cost.*

Then she turned back to Odile. "Your terms will be drawn up," she said. "The court keeps excellent records. You may inspect the cells we've prepared for your people. And Guildmaster—" the ancient mouth curved, "—do advise your enforcers that the Hollow enjoy meeting new friends. Names are such a small price for rank."

The enforcers laughed, obediently. Dominic did not. He was watching Sera Voss watch Odile, and he saw the exact moment the blank page got written on—a single line, fine as a hairline crack:

Sera Voss had just realized that in Odile's new arrangement, there was no entry anywhere for her.

* *

They were given an hour to mingle, which in the Regent's tongue meant *be catalogued*. The Hollow drifted among the delegation offering refreshments and questions in the same courteous breath. Dominic kept to the wall and let Wren find him, slipping to his side with a tray of cups she'd lifted purely for the camouflage, because she was, after all, her father's daughter.

"Tell me," she murmured.

"Odile sold everything," he said into his cup. "The entire vault. For a chair that doesn't die."

"And Sera?"

"Here. Under orders. Which orders, I don't know yet."

"Can you trust her?"

"No." He watched Sera across the hall, standing at Odile's shoulder, smiling her market-day smile at a Hollow courtier who was almost certainly pricing her name. "But I can use her. Trust is just leverage you haven't spent yet."

"You don't believe that," Wren said. "Not anymore. Not since the road."

He didn't answer, which was an answer, and she let it be, and they stood together in the humming hall like two documents filed side by side.

Across the room, Sera detached herself from Odile's shadow and strolled toward them, unhurried, all charm, stopping close enough to speak low. Up close her market-day smile had frayed at one corner.

"Cozy," she said. "The thief and the mapmaker's brat, holding a wine tasting at the end of the world."

"Sera," Dominic said.

"Guildmaster wants you at the drafting table within the hour. She's drawing up the transfer contract, and she wants her ledger-keeper's hand on it. Yours is the only signature the court's records will accept without complaint—you built the guild's books, apparently the mountain's filing system recognizes the craftsmanship." Sera's eyes flicked to Wren, then back. Something moved behind them, fast and quickly suppressed. "Also, a word of professional courtesy, free of charge: whatever deal you struck with our hostess, sign Odile's version first. Because I've seen the draft, Dominic, and in the Regent's copy there are three names on the transfer. In Odile's copy there are two."

He went cold in a slow, even wave, the way deep water goes cold. "Whose name is missing?"

"Guess." Sera glanced once more at Wren—there and gone, a needle-flash of something that might have been warning or might have been envy, and Dominic realized he genuinely could not tell whether Sera Voss was saving them or setting them up, and understood that maybe she couldn't either. Then the smile snapped back into place like a fan opening. "Within the hour," she said, and walked away, hips swinging, loyal as a purchased thing, straight back to the woman who owned her.

Wren exhaled. "Well?"

"Well," Dominic said, "now I know what everyone costs."

"And what about you?" She looked up at him, and the shadowless light made her seem lit from inside, a soul waiting on a shelf. "What's your price, Dominic? Everybody here has one. What's yours?"

He thought about the deal on the table—her father's freedom for his memory of her. He thought about Odile selling a church to buy a coffin that never filled. He thought about Sera, smiling with a crack in it, carrying orders she might already be planning to betray. Everyone had a price. The whole world under the mountain ran on prices; it was a cathedral of them, shelves groaning with things people had surrendered to keep something else.

And maybe that was the point. Maybe the only way to beat a market was to refuse to sell.

"My price," he said slowly, "is that I stop paying."

Wren snorted. "That's not a price. That's bankruptcy."

"In this place?" Dominic looked up at the endless shelves rising into the dark, thousands of bottles catching the sourceless light like a sea under frost. "Bankruptcy might be the only weapon anyone's brought through that door."

The bells that weren't bells began to ring again—summoning, insistent, ceremonial. Down the hall, the Hollow were arranging themselves around a long table where a contract lay waiting, its pages blank and patient, its ink already poured.

Odile sat at its head and beckoned to him with two fingers, the same gesture she'd used fifteen years ago to summon a boy closer to see what his childhood looked like in a bottle.

Dominic took a breath, tasted salt, and went to sign whatever he decided to sign.
