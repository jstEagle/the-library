## 13. The Thief's Grammar

The court beneath Mount Verity kept its accounts the way a miser keeps his teeth: close, constant, and with a certain relish.

Dominic had been a guest of the Hollow for three days now, and in three days he had learned what every thief eventually learns—that the difference between a fortress and a filing cabinet is only a matter of how flattered the guards are by their own importance. The Hollow were very flattered. They swept the cathedral of shelves in their nameless gray livery, dusting souls with lambswool brushes, and they believed themselves above suspicion because they had sold everything worth stealing. You cannot blackmail a man who has traded his own name for a pension. It made them serene. It also made them blind.

He sat in the guest cell they had given him—generous, by prison standards: a bed, a brazier, a desk of black stone that drank the heat out of his hands—and he copied.

That was his cover. He had asked the Regent's chamberlain, with the shy pride of a guild man shown a rival's operation, whether he might study the court's ledger system. *Professional curiosity,* he'd said. *I keep books myself.* The chamberlain had smiled with a mouth that had no opinions behind it and granted him access to the reading gallery: a balcony of iron lattice overlooking the shelves, where visiting auditors could consult the master index under supervision.

The supervision was a Hollow named Brother Vast, who had no ears worth the name. Dominic had tested him on the first day, dropping a stylus twice, coughing, letting a page fall. Brother Vast turned each time, but slowly, like something that had forgotten what surprise felt like. By the third day Dominic knew his rhythm: four circuits of the gallery an hour, each circuit eleven minutes, each pause at the western rail ninety seconds while Vast watched the singers below with whatever passed, in a face like that, for longing.

Four times an hour, ninety seconds. A thief's grammar was written in gaps.

The master index lay open before him now, chained to its stand by a chain that was mostly ceremonial. It was beautiful work; he gave the court that. Every soul on those miles of shelving had an entry: name or former name, date of deposit, provenance, condition, and—the part that made his fingers itch—a *location code*, a string of letters and numerals that told any clerk in the court exactly which shelf, which row, which bottle-shaped niche held which screaming fragment of a person.

And here was the thing. Here was the thing he'd found on the second day and hadn't told anyone, not even Wren, until he was sure.

The location codes could be amended.

There was an amendment column in the margin, ruled off in red ink, and it had entries in it—dozens, hundreds, going back centuries. Souls moved between shelves. Souls consolidated. Souls transferred to the Regent's private reserve. And each amendment bore not a seal, not a signature, but a *citation*: a reference to some prior entry that authorized the move. Ledger citing ledger citing ledger, a paper ancestry stretching back into the dark.

No lock. No ward. No blood-price. Just citation.

Souls, Dominic understood, sitting very still in the cold gallery light, could be moved by paperwork.

He had spent twenty years stealing things that lived in people's heads, and he had always assumed he was practicing the strangeest trade in the world. He was wrong. The strangest trade in the world was practiced here, and it was his oldest skill dressed in vestments: theft as bookkeeping. If you could forge a citation—if you could make entry 40,000-odd point back to some plausible authorization buried six centuries deep—you didn't need to touch a shelf, break a seal, or silence a guard. You needed only to be believed by the ledger itself.

And the ledger, as far as he could tell, believed anything written in the correct hand.

* * *

Wren brought the supper, because Wren had insisted on being useful and the court found her too small to fear. That was its own kind of camouflage. They let her carry trays through corridors Dominic couldn't cross, and she came back each evening with maps drawn in gravy on napkins, and a running census of the Hollow's habits, and a fury she kept banked down to coals so it wouldn't show on her face where the gray ones could see it.

She set the tray down, waited until Brother Vast's footsteps reached the far end of his circuit, and said, "Well?"

"Your father is on Row Nine," Dominic said. "West range. Code Vessel-Cordite-Nine-Fourteen."

Something went through her—not softening; tightening, like a rope taking a load. "You're sure."

"The index doesn't lie. It just doesn't care." He pulled the scrap of napkin-map from his sleeve and smoothed it flat. "Which brings us to the problem."

"You said there wasn't one. Last night you said we could do it with ink."

"We can." He tapped the amendment column. "But watch what happens when I show you this." He turned the master index back two hundred years, to a transfer from the reign of some Regent-or-other equally ancient, and pointed to a marginal note in faded brown ink: *Amendment logged. Regent notified by bell.*

Wren read it twice. "Notified by bell."

"There's a bell somewhere under this mountain that rings every time an entry changes. Probably it's charming when it rings for routine reshelving. Probably nobody notices." He met her eyes. "But we're not going to be routine. We're going to steal a soul out of the Regent's own collection using a forged citation, and when the bell rings, she's going to come looking, and she is going to find us."

"Then we don't get caught between the ring and the looking."

"That," said Dominic, "is exactly the plan."

He laid it out for her the way he'd once laid out jobs for Odile, back when he still believed the guild was a trade and not an appetite. One forgery, done slow and clean, in a hand copied from the court's own clerks—he'd been practicing on the backs of his supper bread for two days, flour-dusted loops, the peculiar hooked numeral seven the Hollow used. The citation would reference a consolidation order from three centuries ago, an order so old and so dull that no living memory in the court contained its terms. The dead paperwork would authorize the living theft. Your father's vessel would be carried, by ordinary procedure, by an ordinary Hollow, to the reintegration chapel—where vessels whose citations had lapsed were routinely returned to their heirs.

"Heirs," Wren repeated.

"You're his daughter. There's a blood-right clause. It's in the founding statutes, which are public, posted in the entry hall, and have apparently never been read by anyone." He allowed himself the thinnest smile. "Bureaucracy's great weakness is that it assumes nobody reads the old rules. Mine is that I read everything."

"And after?"

"After, we walk out of the chapel with your father's soul in a jar, and we are halfway up the miners' road before the Regent finishes checking the citation." His smile died. "Or the bell rings early, she checks immediately, and we're caught in a room with one door holding the one thing she'll burn the mountain to keep."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Below the gallery, faint as weather, the shelves sang.

"My father spent his soul to buy my passage," she said. "If this goes wrong, I don't come out of it without him. Whatever you decide, decide knowing that."

"Wren—"

"No. You've been deciding things around me since the market, quietly, the way you decide everything, and I let you because you're good at it. But he's mine. This one's mine." Her voice stayed level; her knuckles didn't. "I hold the pen."

Dominic considered arguing. Then he considered the last person who'd tried to decide things *for* him, decades ago, in a room smelling of lamp oil, and shut his mouth.

"The pen's yours," he said. "But we write tonight. The bell tolls for reshelving at midnight—Vast told me, proud of the punctuality. When it rings, the whole court listens to the bell. Ninety seconds where nobody anywhere is listening to anything else." He slid the forged citation across the desk, ink barely dry, the hooked sevens perfect. "Ninety seconds is plenty."

* * *

They did it in fifty.

The chapel of reintegration was a low round room off the west range, domed, windowless, lit by lamps that burned without oil—a detail Dominic filed away under *later, and don't think about it now*. At its center stood a lectern holding the chapel's working ledger, and beside it, in ranked niches, the vessels awaiting return: small glass souls-in-suspension, each glowing its owner's color. Most were the gray-green of old age, peacefully surrendered. One, ninth row, fourth niche, burned a stubborn copper-orange that Dominic recognized without wanting to.

Wren saw it and made a sound like a struck wire.

"Not yet," Dominic murmured. "Ink first."

She took the citation from inside her coat with both hands, the way you'd carry water in a drought, and opened the working ledger to the current day. The amendment column waited, ruled in red, patient as a trap. Around them the chapel hummed with the mountain's attention—not quite watching, but *listening*, the way a sleeping animal listens. Dominic felt it against his skin like the moment before a lie leaves your mouth.

"Copy the citation number exactly," he breathed. "Then the clause. Then today's date in their calendar—I wrote it on your wrist."

"I know. I memorized it. I'm not you, Quintana, I don't need notes."

"No," he said softly. "You need steadier hands than me, and you have them. Go."

Her pen touched the page.

The scratch of nib on paper sounded, in that dome, enormous. Somewhere far above them, threaded through the roots of the mountain, Dominic felt the ledger-system stir—felt the citation he'd built begin to pull weight, dead order tugging dead order, three centuries of dormant paperwork waking up one link at a time to vouch for a lie. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever stolen and it wasn't even finished. Clause. Number. Date. Wren's hand moved through the hooks and loops without one tremor, and when she finished the final numeral, the ink did something he'd never seen ink do.

It settled. Sank into the page as if the page had been waiting for it.

The copper-orange vessel in the fourth niche flickered—brightened—and rolled gently against its glass, the way a sleeping man turns toward a familiar voice.

"It knows," Wren whispered.

"It complies. Different thing." But his voice had gone rough, because he'd watched souls bought and sold and bottled for twenty years and he had never once seen one answer to its own name. "Chapel procedure says returned vessels are collected at dawn. Procedure can—" He stopped. Listened.

From everywhere and nowhere, the bell rang.

Once—a single bronze stroke, soft, almost polite—and with it the entire mountain changed key. The singing in the shelves bent upward. The lamps guttered though nothing touched them. And beneath the bell's fading note Dominic heard something worse: footsteps. Not the Hollow's shuffling circuits. Measured. Unhurried. Coming down the long west stair with the confidence of someone who owned every door she walked through and most of the things behind them.

The Regent had heard her ledger change.

"Ninety seconds," Dominic said. "We used fifty. Give me the jar and run."

Wren already had it—fourth niche open, vessel in her bare hands, and it was burning her, he could see it, the copper light biting into her palms, her father's soul pressing toward her skin like heat toward a wound. She didn't flinch. "He comes with me or I don't go."

"Both. Now."

They ran. Behind them the chapel door swung open on darkness, and out of the darkness came a voice like paper turning pages, courteous and ancient and horribly amused:

"Thief. You've amended my book."

Dominic put Wren ahead of him, put the west corridor at their backs, and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the exact moment it's too late for it to be useful, that the forged citation had been perfect—that was the problem. Nothing that clean had touched the Regent's ledger in six hundred years, and perfection was a signature all its own.

The bell rang again. Not the reshelving stroke. Something faster.

Somewhere above them, every Hollow in the court stopped sweeping at once.
