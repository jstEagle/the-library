## 14. What Odile Sold

The order came by courier, because Odile never trusted paper she hadn't watched arrive.

*Sera — retrieve the map. By any means. Bring me the man if the map resists him.*

By any means. That was Odile's way of saying *don't ask questions*, and for eleven years Sera Voss had never asked one. She'd been fifteen and starving on the harbor steps when a woman in gray found her, fed her, and told her she had a talent for being exactly where she was needed. The guild didn't buy loyalty from its enforcers. It earned it, the way you earn anything—by showing up when no one else did.

Sera believed that the way you believe your own name.

She sat in the safehouse above the cooper's yard, sharpening a knife she wouldn't need, and thought about Dominic Quintana instead of the knife. The ledger-keeper. The forgettable man. She'd shadowed him through the market two weeks ago, catalogued his tells: he touched his left cuff when he lied, he counted things under his breath when he was nervous, and when she'd smiled at him over the oyster stall, something behind his eyes had flinched like a man hearing his own name spoken in an empty house.

He was going to betray them. She was certain of it, and certainty was her whole trade.

And yet.

She kept coming back to the moment at the fishmonger's, when a cart nearly clipped him and she caught his arm without thinking. He'd looked at her hand on his sleeve with genuine confusion—not suspicion, not fear, but the bafflement of a man who couldn't remember the last time anyone had touched him on purpose. She'd felt something crack open in her chest, small and warm and inconvenient.

*Focus*, she told herself. *Find the map. Do the job.*

But first there was the matter of the vault.

---

Odile kept her private contracts in a warded strongroom beneath the guildhall, three floors down past the memory-cellar where bottled years glowed like drowned lanterns. Sera had walked those shelves a hundred times on escort duty, past jars labeled in Odile's neat clerk-hand: *a duchess's grief, vintage uncertain*. *First kiss, salt-market boy*. *Seventh year, complete*—

She stopped walking.

That last label had been there for years. She'd read it before without it meaning anything, the way you read the same sign every morning until one day you actually see it. *Seventh year, complete.* A boy's entire childhood, sealed in glass, sold to someone who wanted it badly enough to pay what Odile had charged.

Dominic Quintana had no childhood. Everyone knew that about him; he said it himself, dry as ledger-paper, whenever anyone pried. *Nothing to steal, nothing to miss.*

Sera stood in front of the shelf and felt the guildhall go very quiet around her.

*Don't*, she told herself. *You have an order. Retrieve the map. By any means.*

But the order was why she was here at all. To retrieve the map, she needed leverage, and leverage meant knowing everything Odile knew—and Odile kept her contracts here, in the strongroom, behind a door keyed to blood and rank. Sera had rank. Sera had blood. What she had never had was permission, and she stood at the threshold for a long moment, listening to her own heartbeat, before she pressed her thumb to the lock.

The door opened like it had been waiting for her.

---

Inside, the strongroom smelled of wax and cold iron. Contracts hung in rows, each one sealed with the guild's black salt-stamp, each one a life reduced to terms. Sera moved along them with a thief's economy she didn't know she had, reading fast:

*A courier's soul, collateral, court beneath Mount Verity.*
*Passage rights, Mount Verity road, paid in kind.*
*One childhood, seventh year, complete, delivered intact.*

There it was. The purchase price for the courier's debt—a boy's seventh year. And beneath it, in Odile's hand, a single notation: *Buyer satisfied. Seller: Quintana estate. Instrument: O.Q., age seven.*

Instrument. Not seller. *Instrument.*

The guild hadn't bought a childhood from a family. They'd taken it *from the child*, used it as currency, and then—this was the part that made Sera sit down slowly on a crate of sealing wax—they'd hired the child back as their ledger-keeper. Given him work. Given him purpose. Given him a place to belong, to a man they'd emptied first.

It was elegant. It was monstrous. It was exactly the kind of thing Odile would call good business.

Sera should have stopped there. She had what she came for—the shape of Odile's game, the proof that the Guildmaster intended to trade Dominic like so much inventory. But the contracts were alphabetical by seller, and between *Vance* and *Verity Shipping* there was a folder with her own name on it.

*VOSS, SERA.*

Her hands were steady. She noticed that from very far away, the way you notice weather in another country.

She opened it.

---

The contract was short. Most of them were; lives compressed well.

*Item: One loyalty, unconditional, subject permanent. Donor: Voss, Sera, age 15, destitute. Purchaser: Saltwork Guild. Consideration: food, shelter, training, purpose.*

*Subject permanent.*

Sera read the phrase four times. It didn't change. Words on old paper have a way of staying exactly what they are while the world rearranges itself around them.

*Subject permanent* meant it wasn't devotion. It wasn't gratitude. It wasn't the slow accumulation of trust between a starving girl and the woman who fed her. It was an *installation*. Somewhere in this building—probably on the third shelf of the memory-cellar, probably in Odile's neatest hand—there was a bottle containing whatever Sera Voss had actually felt toward the guild before they poured this into her. Containing the girl who had been hungry and frightened and *free*, free to feel whatever she felt, before they decided what she would feel forever.

She tried to remember being fifteen on the harbor steps. She could summon the scene perfectly: the cold, the gull-noise, the woman in gray crouching down to her eye level. But the feeling underneath it was smooth as river stone. No edges. No fear that wasn't tidy. No resentment, no doubt, no moment where she might have said *no thank you* and crawled back into the dark. Fifteen-year-olds were not smooth. She had met fifteen-year-olds. They were all edges.

Someone had sanded her down and filled the gaps with devotion.

And the terrible thing—the thing she would carry out of that room and into everything that came after—was that she could not tell which parts of her love for the guild were real. Maybe none. Maybe some. There was no seam, no scar, no place where the installed thing met the original. She had been loyal for eleven years and she could not remember choosing it, and not remembering was the whole point.

*This is what we do to people*, she thought. *This is the business.*

She thought of Dominic touching his cuff when he lied. Of course he lied constantly. He was a man built out of other people's missing pieces; lying was just breathing to him. And she thought of the way he'd looked at her hand on his sleeve—as if touch were a language he'd once known and lost. A man with no childhood, working for the people who took it, keeping their books.

They'd made each other, she realized. The guild made orphans and then employed them. Every person in that building was either stolen goods or stolen goods' caretaker, and nobody ever checked the labels.

Except now she had.

---

She took the keys.

Not all of them—she wasn't a fool—but the vault keys hung in the strongroom on a ring shaped like a fishhook, and Odile had always said the vault would outlive them all. Sera lifted the ring, weighed it in her palm, and understood that she was crossing a line that had no return crossing. Eleven years of loyalty, and it fit inside one closed fist.

Or eleven years of something *installed*, she corrected herself, and that was worse, and better, and true.

On her way out, she passed the memory-cellar. The bottles glowed their drowned-lantern glow. On the third shelf, exactly where some part of her had known it would be, sat a jar labeled in Odile's clerk-hand:

*Voss, Sera — original affect, age 15. Retain per standard practice.*

Standard practice. They kept the originals. Somewhere in the ledgers of the Saltwork Guild, the real Sera Voss—the frightened, edgy, unfinished girl—was filed like a duchess's grief.

Sera stood in front of the bottle for a long time. Her hand rose toward it and stopped. Taking it now, alone, unprepared—what then? Smash it into her own skull and hope the girl inside was someone worth being? Drink a stranger and call her yourself?

No. Not alone. Not blind.

She knew someone who read bottles the way priests read scripture. Someone who had spent his whole life handling other people's selves and could tell her, maybe, what hers looked like from outside. Someone whose childhood was sitting on a shelf in this same cellar, waiting, labeled *complete*.

The enemy of my enemy, she thought. Except he isn't my enemy. He never was. I'm the one who came armed.

She pocketed the jar.

---

She found him where she knew she would—at the harbor end of the salt-road, near the low door where a courier had died, because men like Dominic returned to their wounds. He heard her boots on the stones and turned, and she watched him do the arithmetic of escape routes and dismiss them one by one; she'd cornered too many people not to recognize the exercise.

"Sera Voss," he said. "Come to collect me?"

"Depends." She stopped ten feet away, hands visible, the fishhook ring heavy in her coat. "I'm supposed to bring you to Odile. Or the map. Preferably both."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I read her contracts tonight." She let that land. "Yours. Mine."

Something moved behind his eyes—that flinch again, the empty-house look. "Mine, I can guess."

"Can you?" Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Do you know what she charged? Do you know the buyer was satisfied?" She pulled the jar from her coat and held it up between them, the glow catching his face. "I know what mine cost. Food and shelter. Eleven years of service. I know that what I feel about the guild was *poured in*, Dominic. Subject permanent. I don't know which parts of me are left."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he crossed the distance between them—not quickly, the way you approach a spooked animal—and looked at the jar without reaching for it.

"Original affect," he read. "Age fifteen." His voice was quiet and terribly gentle, the voice of a man who had catalogued ten thousand losses and never once been allowed to grieve his own. "You want me to tell you what's in it."

"I want you to tell me if there's anyone in there worth rescuing."

"There's always someone," he said. "That's the first thing the guild teaches you to forget." He looked up at her, and for the first time since she'd known him, the forgettable man was impossible to look away from. "They kept the original. That means it can be returned. That means you're not broken, Sera—you're *filed*. There's a difference, and I've built my whole miserable life on that difference."

"The vault keys," she said, and put the fishhook ring in his hand. "Every lock below the guildhall. And the contract proving Odile sold your seventh year to the mountain—and is planning to sell the rest of you next. Deliver the map, she told me. Bring the man if the map resists him."

His fingers closed around the ring. "Why?"

"Because I spent two weeks watching you for signs of betrayal," Sera said, "and the only thing I caught you betraying was yourself. Because somebody sanded me down and I want to know what shape I was. And because—" She stopped. The honest thing was right there, ugly and bright. "Because I don't know if wanting to help you is real or installed. But I'm done letting Odile decide what's real. Either way, it's mine to find out."

Dominic looked down at the keys, then at the jar glowing in her hands, and when he spoke, his voice had the cadence of a ledger balancing.

"Then we have a problem," he said. "Because the vault isn't the only thing Odile emptied. Last night she signed away the guild's entire stock—every bottle, every soul—to the Regent under the mountain. Bought herself eternity. Which means somewhere on that road, right now, there's a delegation carrying our whole cellar to feed a hungry queen." He tucked the keys away and met her eyes. "We can still beat them there. If we leave tonight."

Above the harbor, the mountain hummed, faint as a struck glass, and Sera—who had lived her whole life in its shadow without ever hearing it—heard it clearly for the first time.
