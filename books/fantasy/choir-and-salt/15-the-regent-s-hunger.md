## 15. The Regent's Hunger

The Regent did not sleep. Sleep was a thing for creatures who trusted their own minds to still be there in the morning.

She sat instead in the high seat of the court beneath Mount Verity, in a chair carved from the mountain's first throat-stone, and she listened to the shelves. Ten thousand souls, filed and humming. They made a sound like rain on a distant roof, like a choir heard through three walls of stone. She had built the court so that every corner of it sang to her. A ruler who could not remember needed a kingdom that would not forget.

"Vessel," she said.

The Hollow who served her wine came forward without footsteps. He had sold his name two centuries ago for a seat at her table, and he had never once regretted it, because regret required a self to do the regretting, and he had none. That was what she liked about the Hollow. They were the only company that did not make her hungry.

"The western galleries," she said. "Sing them to me."

The Vessel opened his mouth, and what came out was not his voice but the court's — a low chord drawn up through the floor, through the roots of the mountain, through the shelved souls themselves. Each soul had a note. Each note carried its history: whose it had been, what it had been traded for, which ledger-line held it. The Regent closed her eyes and read the chord the way other rulers read reports from their provinces.

The western galleries were clean. The salt-flats holdings, clean. The collateral shelf where the courier's soul hung — clean.

Her hands tightened on the arms of the chair.

Because the eastern deep-stack was *off*. Not wrong, exactly. Wrong was loud; wrong announced itself. This was subtler. It was the silence of a book whose pages had been counted and recounted by someone hoping not to be caught. Somewhere in the deep-stack, an entry had been touched. An entry had been *moved*.

She had eaten forty thousand memories in her long unlife, and if there was one thing they had taught her, it was the taste of a lie told carefully.

* * *

Once — and this was the part of herself she kept in the deepest vault, behind the souls, behind the ledgers, behind even her own name, which she had been the first to sell — once she had been a girl with a voice.

Not this voice. Not the vast instrument she had become, the one that could crack stone and shelve a soul with a sustained note. A small voice. A girl's voice, thin as a reed, singing in the dark of the mine while her father worked the salt face below. The mountain had loved her voice. That was the joke at the heart of everything, the joke she could no longer laugh at because she had eaten the memory of how laughter worked: the mountain had *loved* her, and she had repaid it by learning to eat.

It began innocently, the way all hungers do. A memory is heavy. Grief is heavier. She had sung her father's death out of herself one night, swallowed it whole, and woken lighter than she had ever been, and the mountain had hummed approval through the rock, and she had thought: *this is power.* Not the voice. The eating.

So she ate her fear. Then her doubt. Then her mother's face, because grief for her mother kept trying to grow back like moss in a wet cave, and she was tired of carrying it. Then her first love, her first winter, the smell of bread, the color of the sky above the mountain's mouth — she ate the sky itself, eventually, because she could not remember blue without wanting it, and wanting was unbearable.

She rose. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. Every memory she devoured made her stronger, and every strength made the next memory easier to eat. By the time she understood the trap, she was already the Regent, already ancient, already ruling a court of the hollowed with a mind like a cathedral with nothing in it. Power without content. A crown worn by an appetite.

And then the hunger had turned, as hungers do, from memories to souls. Because a memory, eaten, fades. But a soul keeps regenerating its own past, the way a wound keeps weeping. A soul in a bottle remembers forever, and forever remembering, forever feeds her. She did not rule the court beneath the mountain. She farmed it.

She knew what she was. That was the last cruelty the eating had left her: full comprehension without the weight of anything worth comprehending. She could recite her own monstrosity like a ledger entry and feel nothing, and the feeling-nothing was the monstrousness.

Some nights — nights being merely when the lamps were dimmed, since she did not sleep — she stood among the shelves and pressed her palm against the glass of a bottled soul and tried to remember what it was like to be *inside* something rather than holding it. She could not. She had eaten that too.

This was why she needed the map. Why she needed the thief. Memory could be bought, stolen, filed — but a life, a whole unbroken life, poured into her all at once? That was not a meal. That was a resurrection. Somewhere in the world above there were people who had never sold anything, who carried their childhoods around like loose coins, and she wanted one the way the drowned want air.

* * *

"Wake the Archivist," she said.

The Vessel bowed and went, and the Regent descended from the high seat into the deep-stack, her robes whispering over stone worn smooth by two hundred years of her pacing.

The eastern deep-stack smelled of cold and old paper. Souls hung in their bottles along the walls in their thousands, each labeled in the Archivist's meticulous hand, each glowing faintly with the light of a life going nowhere. She walked the aisle slowly, letting her hunger range ahead of her like a hound.

There. Third shelf from the end. A gap in the dust.

Dust was the court's honest witness. Nothing in the deep-stack moved unless the Archivist moved it, and the Archivist did not move things without her seal. But here, on a shelf holding the minor collaterals — a fisherman's courage, a spinster's patience, small sad commodities — someone had lifted a bottle, held it, and put it back. Or hadn't put it back. She counted. The count matched the ledger. The count *matched*, and that was worse, because a thief who steals leaves a hole, but a thief who forges leaves a mirror, and mirrors are how thieves walk out of vaults wearing the vault's own face.

Ledger-entry transfer. She almost admired it. In the early years, before the court had grown its bureaucracy, she had done her own filing, and she knew the deep grammar of the place: a soul belongs wherever the ledger says it belongs. Change the words and the bottle follows, docile as a dog. Someone down here — or someone above, reading the court through borrowed eyes — had learned her grammar.

Someone was stealing from her in her own handwriting.

For a long moment she stood in the aisle, and the bottled souls flickered nervously at her stillness, the way birds go quiet before weather.

Then she began to laugh. It was a rusty sound. She had not laughed since she'd eaten the memory of her sister teaching her to skip stones, and the laugh came out wrong, too large for the aisle, rattling the bottles in their cradles. Because it was funny. It was genuinely, exquisitely funny. She had spent two centuries building a machine to keep the world's memories out of her reach except on her terms, and now some clerk-thief with ink-stained fingers was picking the machine's lock with a pen.

Let them come, she thought. Let them forge. Every forgery is a signature. Every moved soul leaves a warm spot on the shelf. You cannot steal from me quietly, little thief, because I am the quiet itself, and I have been listening to this mountain sing since before your guild had a name.

* * *

The Archivist found her there. He had been the court's record-keeper for ninety years, a Hollow of great precision, and he carried the master ledger against his chest the way other men carry infants.

"Majesty. The Vessel said—"

"I know what the Vessel said." She did not turn. "Bring me the transfer records. All of them. And wake the Wardens."

"Majesty, the Wardens are—" he hesitated, and hesitation in a Hollow was a remarkable thing, a splinter of the man he used to be working its way to the surface, "—the Wardens require feeding before they can hunt. Three souls each. From the reserve."

"Take them from the reserve," she said.

He flinched. Ninety years of service, and he still flinched, and she noted it, and filed it away in the part of her that was still capable of noting things. Even the Hollow were not entirely empty. Even the Hollow kept one small coal of what they'd been, banked under ash, and she had learned to find those coals and blow on them when she needed obedience hotter than duty alone could burn.

"You object," she said.

"No, Majesty."

"You do. Tell me why. I collect reasons the way you collect entries."

He looked at the master ledger in his arms. "They were people," he said finally. "The ones in the reserve. Before."

"Yes," she said. "And I was a person before. Look what became of me." She smiled at him, and had the pleasure — a small, real pleasure, one of the few left to her — of watching him understand that the smile was not a threat but a warning label, the way poison comes labeled. "Feed the Wardens. Unspool the galleries. If a thief is walking my court, I want the court itself to tell me where he steps."

* * *

Unspooling was the word the Archivist used, and it was accurate, and it was obscene.

A stored soul, undisturbed, dreams in its bottle. But a soul can be *poured* — stretched thin through the corridors of the court like thread through a loom — until every passage, every gallery, every stair is strung with living memory. Anything that moves through the threads touches them, and the touch reads back to her: warmth, weight, intention, name. It was how she had caught the last intruder, sixty years ago, a guild cartographer who had come down the miners' road with more bravery than sense. She had felt him coming for six days. She had let him reach the second gate just to hear him pray.

But unspooling cost. Stretched souls frayed. Poured too often, they thinned past recovery, and a thinned soul sang flat, and fed her less, and eventually was only residue. Her reserves were not infinite. Nothing was infinite anymore except her hunger, which grew by exactly what it ate.

She did it anyway. She stood at the center of the court with her arms raised and her voice pitched below hearing, and she drew the souls out of their bottles like drawing silk off a loom, and the whole mountain began to glisten with other people's lives.

She felt them immediately. Two threads, moving together, in the lower galleries near the miners' road. One burned bright and ragged — grief in it, fresh grief, the kind that made a soul taste of iron. The other was strange. The other was *quiet* in a way that made her hunger sit up like an animal scenting meat.

A hole in a person. She knew that shape. She had made that shape in herself, stitch by stitch, bite by bite. This one moved like a man who had amputated himself and learned to walk on the stumps. A thief who had stolen from his own inventory.

*Oh,* she thought, and the thought went through her like a bell through water. *Oh, you're the one.*

The courier's map. The forged entries. The boy's childhood she had bought from Odile all those years ago — sealed, unpaid interest accruing, waiting in her vault like a letter she had never dared open, because she suspected — she had always suspected — that the payment Odile had brought her was not merchandise at all. That it was kin. That somewhere in the sealed paper of that childhood was a face she had eaten the memory of ever loving.

She had bought a child's seventh year from the Saltwork Guild, and she had never opened it, because opening it might mean finding her daughter's face inside, and she had eaten her daughter, and even an appetite has one door it does not open.

Until now. Until the thief came walking down her threads with his amputated self and his dead courier and his clever pen, forcing the vault open whether she willed it or not.

"Archivist," she said, and her voice filled the mountain, and every stretched soul in every corridor shivered. "Double the watch on the vault of personal acquisitions. And bring me the Warden-captain. I want the two in the lower galleries taken alive — the grieving one for the shelves, and the quiet one brought to me *unspoiled*."

"And if they resist, Majesty?"

"Then take whatever's left," she said. "I have waited two hundred years. I can wait a week. But I find—" and here she paused, because the next thing was true and she disliked saying true things, true things had weight, and weight was the enemy, "—I find I am tired of waiting."

The Archivist bowed and withdrew. The Regent remained alone in the center of her webbed and glittering court, threaded through with ten thousand borrowed lives, holding none of her own.

Far below, through the loom of poured memory, she felt the quiet thief pause on a stair, as if he had heard something. As if he had felt her listening.

She smiled again, and this time it was not a warning label. It was the smile of a girl in a mine, singing to the dark, certain the dark was singing back.

*Come up,* she thought at him, pouring the thought down the threads like oil down a wick. *Come up and see what I kept of you.*
