## 16. Ledger of the Damned

The court's filing system had a grammar, and like all grammar it could be lied to.

Dominic sat in the alcove behind the Shelf of Unclaimed Years with a stolen stylus and three sheets of vellum that smelled faintly of the Regent's incense, and he wrote lies in a hand so perfect it frightened him. That was the trick of it—forgery wasn't about copying. It was about believing. The ledger beneath Mount Verity was not paper and wax; it was the mountain itself, a living index of every soul filed on every shelf, and the mountain believed what was written into its stone the way a drowning man believes in rope.

*Transfer: all holdings, Court of Kept Things, to bearer.*

One line. He'd spent two nights learning why one line would work. The court's entries were hierarchical—a soul shelved under a Hollow's name could be moved by that Hollow's authority, and authority flowed upward to the Regent, whose seal was not ink but hunger. You couldn't forge her seal. But you didn't have to. Every entry in the deep ledgers traced back through intermediaries, through sold names and resold names, down to the original transactions—the couriers, the guild, the desperate mothers and fathers who'd traded pieces of themselves for bread or vengeance or time. And at the root of every chain of custody was a phrase the court never used because it was obscene to them:

*Original owner.*

Wren crouched at the mouth of the alcove, counting footsteps in the dark. She'd learned the Hollow's patrol rhythm faster than Dominic had—three passes of the Long Gallery, then a silence of forty breaths while they tended the lamps, which were not lamps but bottled memories burning slow.

"Two minutes," she whispered. "Maybe less. They changed the count after the last forgery."

"Then we write fast." He dipped the stylus. The ink was Sera's contribution—guild ink, mixed with salt from the old mines above, the only substance the mountain's stone would accept from an unsealed hand. Odile had taught him that, years ago, in a lesson he'd thought was about bookkeeping. *Everything is a ledger, Dominic. Even stone. Especially stone.* He wondered if she'd known, even then, that someday he'd turn the lesson against her. Against everyone.

Sera stood watch at the gallery door, and watching her stand there did something complicated to his chest. Three days ago she'd been Odile's knife. Then she'd found her own contract in the vault—found the bottle labeled *Voss, Sera: loyalty, undiluted*—and understood that every moment of devotion she'd ever felt toward the Guildmaster had been installed like a fixture, screwed into her soul by someone else's hand. She'd come to his cell that night with the vault keys and eyes like a woman reading her own autopsy.

"I don't know which parts of me are mine," she'd said. "So I'm going to act as if all of them are, until I can tell the difference."

Now she held the door, and her purchased loyalty was burning out of her by degrees, because that was what happened when a bought thing was used against its seller—it consumed itself. He could see it. Each hour she spent defying Odile, something in her face went quieter, emptier, more honestly hers. It looked like grief. It might have been freedom.

"Entry point," Wren breathed.

Dominic wrote the last line. Not the transfer itself—that was already inscribed across all three sheets, triplicate, the way the court required—but the authorization. There was only one name in the world that opened every shelf in the Court of Kept Things without question, and it wasn't the Regent's. It was the first singer's. The mountain remembered its mother before it remembered its queen, and though the Regent had eaten her own past, the ledgers kept a ghost of it, a root-entry older than her reign, signed in a script no living hand used anymore.

He forged it anyway. Thief's arrogance. If the grammar existed, it could be lied to.

The stone beneath the stylus shivered. Somewhere far below, in the roots of Mount Verity, a bell that was not a bell began to toll—one low note, felt in the teeth rather than heard—and every shelf in the cathedral of kept things exhaled at once, a sound like ten thousand drawers opening a hair's width.

"It took," Wren whispered. Her voice cracked on it. "It actually took."

"Not yet." Dominic rolled the three sheets together. "An entry isn't a transfer until it's read aloud at the Root Ledger. We carry it down. We speak it. Then every soul in this mountain belongs to whoever holds this page."

"And then we run."

"And then we run."

They ran.

* * *

The way to the Root Ledger went down through the Shelf of Sold Children, and Dominic made himself look at none of it. He'd looked on the way up, days ago, when looking had been reconnaissance. Now each jar was a face, each label a name, and the mass transfer humming in his hands meant that in a few minutes every one of those souls would pour back into the world—to bodies long dead, mostly, or into nothing, into whatever waits for a soul with no home to return to. Freedom, maybe. Or scattering. He'd stopped pretending he knew which.

The stairs narrowed. The mountain's hum thickened until it pressed on his eardrums like water. Wren went first, quick and silent, her father's courier instincts alive in her even though the man himself was still filed somewhere below them—entry four thousand and eleven, *soul, whole, collateral*, and tonight, if the words worked, *returned*.

They reached the antechamber of the Root Ledger as the lamps dimmed for the change of watch. The chamber was small—that was the joke of it, the Regent's entire dominion resting on a room the size of a chapel—with a single desk of black salt-stone and a book upon it the size of a paving slab. The book was open. It was always open. It had been open since before the Regent had a name.

Dominic laid the first sheet on the desk and put his palm flat on the page, the way the court's own clerks did, and spoke the entry.

"Transfer: all holdings, Court of Kept Things, to bearer. By right of the first song."

The Root Ledger turned its own page.

He felt it happen—not saw, *felt*, a sensation like a great wheel taking up slack somewhere beneath the world. The hum of the mountain changed key. Above them, through a hundred fathoms of singing stone, the shelves began to rattle, and the bottled lights along the galleries flickered in a long rolling wave, like wind moving over a field.

Wren grabbed his arm. "Dominic."

"I feel it."

"No—" She pointed up. "Listen."

He listened. Under the rattling, under the wave of light, there was another sound rising now, thin and growing: voices. Thousands of them, muffled, coming awake inside their jars. Souls don't sleep, exactly, but they dream, and the transfer had touched every dream at once, and the dreams were turning toward the surface like sleepers hearing their names called.

"The Hollow will feel it too," Sera said from the doorway. Her voice was steady but her knuckles were white on the frame. "Every courtier in this mountain just felt their holdings slip out from under them. You've got minutes. Maybe one minute."

"Second sheet." He set it down, spoke the entry again—triplicate was law; the mountain honored what was confirmed—and the wheel turned further, groaning now, and somewhere overhead something glass shattered, and then something else, and the voices got louder.

That was when the bells started for real.

Not the low root-note this time. Alarm—three sharp tones cascading down through the stone, and with them came light, hard white light flooding the stairwells, and the sound of the Hollow moving. Not walking. *Pouring.* They moved like water finding a grade, robes whispering, faces smooth as eggs, and they were coming from every direction except—

Except the one Sera was standing in.

"Go," she said.

"Sera—"

"They'll funnel through the Long Gallery to reach the Root. It's the only approach wide enough for a charge." She stepped backward into the corridor and drew the vault keys from her belt, all of them, and held them up between her fingers like a hand of cards. "And I am the only person in this mountain who knows how to lock a guild door. Odile built me for exactly this. Isn't that funny? She bought a door, and the door is going to close on her."

"You don't have to—" Wren started.

"Yes I do." Sera smiled, and it was the first smile Dominic had seen on her that wasn't deployed for a purpose, and it was crooked and human and terrified. "Besides. I want to know what's left of me on the other side of this. Somebody has to stay and find out." She looked at Dominic, and for a moment the enforcer was gone entirely and there was just a woman, young, unmoored, holding keys to everything she'd been. "Read the third sheet. Don't stop reading no matter what you hear. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Liar," she said fondly. "Go."

She turned and walked into the white light, and the doors of the Long Gallery boomed shut behind her, and the locking began—a sound like a piano being dismantled, guild-locks throwing bolt after bolt, each one keyed to a dead language of commerce—and Dominic took Wren's hand and they ran for the Root.

* * *

The third sheet burned his palms. He didn't know why until later, and then he understood: the ledger was arguing with him. A mass transfer of this size wasn't an entry anymore; it was a wound, and the mountain's stone fought the stylus the way flesh fights a blade. Halfway through speaking the final confirmation, the floor tilted. The lamps guttered. From far above came a sound he would carry for the rest of his life whether he wanted to or not—Sera's voice, raised not in fear but in *recitation*, calling out guild counter-signs in a voice gone ragged, buying seconds with passwords, and under it the whispering pressure of the Hollow pressing at her door like tide against a breakwater.

"Third sheet confirmed," he gasped. "*By right of the first song, all holdings return—*"

The Root Ledger turned its last page, and the mountain screamed.

Every shelf in the Court of Kept Things let go at once. Ten thousand jars, ten thousand bottles, ten thousand sealed souls—uncorked, unlabeled, released—in a single ascending chord that was not a chord at all but a choir remembering itself. The sound came down through the stone and hit the Root chamber like weather. Wren fell to her knees. Dominic clung to the desk. And through the black salt-stone floor, up through his boots, into his bones, poured everything: a fisherman's thirty years of mornings, a duchess's grief, a child's first snow, a traitor's single perfect summer, a mother singing—

He knew that song. His body knew it before his mind refused it, throat tightening around a melody he had no memory of learning, and he had one instant to understand that his own childhood was in that flood, loose, rising, coming home to a boy who no longer had a shelf to put it on—

Then the doors blew in.

Not the Long Gallery doors. Those held—held and held, Sera's recitation fraying at the edges like rope over fire. These were the small doors, the clerks' doors, the ones nobody guarded because nobody had ever needed to. Six Hollow came through them, smooth-faced, unhurried, and behind them, filling the doorway like a shadow fills a keyhole, came the Regent.

She looked at the empty desk. At the Root Ledger, its pages turning themselves, spitting out entries in a script that was dissolving even as it wrote—*void, void, void, returned, returned, returned*. At Dominic, still touching the page, still the bearer of everything.

"Well," she said, and her voice was almost gentle. "You've made rather a mess of my library."

The Hollow moved. Wren moved faster—she got between them and Dominic with a courier's knife in her fist and her father's stubbornness in her spine, and it was magnificent and useless, six against two, and Dominic was reaching for the only weapon he had left, the ledger itself, thinking *I hold every soul in this mountain, I can trade, I can bargain, I can—*

The Long Gallery doors exploded.

The sound arrived a half-second before the debris. Through the smoke and the settling dust came Sera Voss, walking, barely walking, one hand braced on the wall, and where her purchased loyalty had been there was now a hole shaped exactly like it, and she was burning up from the inside with the effort of having spent herself all at once. Behind her, pouring through the ruined doors, came the Hollow—dozens, a tide, done with being locked out.

Sera looked at Dominic. Looked at the ledger in his hands. And smiled that crooked, human, terrified smile one more time.

"Told you," she said. "Door closes on her."

Then the Hollow took her, gently, the way the court took everything, and the last thing Dominic saw before Wren wrenched him toward the clerks' passage was Sera's face going smooth, going quiet, going *filed*—her name spoken once by the Regent in a tone of terrible courtesy, *Voss, Sera: retained for questioning*—and then they were running, down and down, the choir roaring overhead, the ledger heavy as a drowning man in his arms, and the mountain shaking itself apart around the one thing the Regent wanted more than her court:

The thief who'd emptied it.
