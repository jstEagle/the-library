## 17. The Bargain Reversed

They brought him to the Regent's hall without binding his hands.

That was the insult of it, or the compliment—Dominic hadn't decided which. Two Hollow flanked him, their faces smooth as unsent letters, and their grip on his arms was gentle in the way of people handling something already owned. The doors opened on their own. The hall beyond was the same cathedral of shelves he'd first walked through with Wren at his side and a thief's map of the place folded against his heart, but it looked different now. Emptier. He had done that. Three days of forged entries, three nights of Wren's steady hand copying his script, and whole rows of the Regent's collection stood blank—shelves stripped to the wood, labels fluttering like molted skin.

The Regent sat at the far end, beneath the great stone arch where the mountain's throat opened into her court, and she was not looking at him.

She was looking at the ledger.

It lay open on a table before her—the ledger, the one they'd built entry by forged entry, the one that now held the deeds of every soul they'd stolen back. She had it. Which meant the Hollow had caught Sera at the door, which meant the door had held long enough, which meant—

*Which meant it worked,* he told himself. *Whatever happens now, it worked.*

But his hands wouldn't stop shaking, and the Regent finally raised her eyes, and he understood that she could see the shaking and was counting it.

"Quintana," she said. His name in her mouth sounded like a figure being entered into a column. "Do you know what I admire about thieves?"

"I've heard a great many things admired in this court," Dominic said. "Most of them were lies."

"Thieves understand value." She rose. She was smaller than he remembered from the first audience—age had a way of compressing people, or perhaps she had spent more of herself since, feeding the mountain's hunger with the memories it demanded. Her eyes were the color of a candle burned to its socket. "A merchant loves his goods. A king loves his crown. But a thief—*knows* what a thing costs. You've spent three days robbing me, Dominic Quintana, and you did it with ink. That is the finest compliment anyone has ever paid my court."

"You're welcome," he said.

She smiled. It was a terrible thing, that smile—not cruel, exactly. *Hungry.* The smile of a woman who had eaten her own past and found it not enough. "The Hollow took your friend at the archive door. Sera Voss. She fought. She burned through something inside herself to do it—loyalty, I think, though it wasn't hers to begin with, was it? Your Guildmaster installs devotion in her people like a mason installs mortar. I've bought from her before." She turned, walking the length of the table, trailing one finger along the ledger's edge. "Your other friend is in a cell below. Wren. The courier's daughter. She has her father's stubbornness and none of his sense."

"Then you know why I'm here," Dominic said. "Name your price."

"Oh, I intend to." She stopped. "You've taken back two hundred souls from my shelves. Do you imagine I didn't feel it? Every forgery was a tooth pulled. I felt each one. And now I hold the ledger that proves the theft, and a thief who can forge my entries, and two souls I can spend as I please." She clasped her hands. "I could unmake you all. Slowly. I could feed your friend's father to the stone and make you watch. I could keep the girl singing in my choir until her voice gave out and her name with it."

"But you won't," Dominic said. "Because you want something more."

The smile again. "You *do* understand value."

* * *

She led him down. Not to the cells—past them, deeper, along a corridor where the walls were lined with bottles the color of dusk, and the humming of the mountain grew so loud he felt it in his teeth. Wren's cell door stood ajar as they passed. Through the gap he saw her, sitting on the floor, arms around her knees, and she looked up at him with an expression he couldn't read—fear, or fury, or the terrible arithmetic of someone watching a bargain being made over her head.

He didn't stop. If he stopped, he would turn around, and if he turned around, everyone in this mountain died for nothing.

The vault was at the corridor's end, behind a door of black salt that the Regent opened with a word she spoke in a language older than the guild, older than the court. Inside, the air was cold and still as the inside of a closed book. Shelves again—but these were different. These were not the filed souls of strangers. These were *kept* things. A wedding ring on a velvet cushion. A jar of first snow. A child's tooth on a square of silk.

And on the center shelf, alone, in a bottle of green glass sealed with wax the color of dried blood, a label in a hand he knew better than his own.

*Odile's hand. Odile's script. The neat, upright, merciless letters of the Guildmaster of the Saltwork Guild.*

The label read: *Payment received in full. One childhood, complete. Buyer: the Court Beneath Mount Verity. Seller: Odile, for the Guild. Provenance: the boy Quintana, age seven.*

Dominic's mouth went dry. He had known. He had known since the vault in the guildhall, since the ledger turned on its keeper, since the dream of the mountain and the singing and his mother's face. But knowing a thing and standing in front of it are different trades entirely.

"This," the Regent said softly, "is what you came for. Not the map. Not the girl. This." She lifted the bottle from its shelf with a reverence that made his skin crawl. "I have kept it thirty years. I could have drunk it a hundred times over—a whole childhood, unspoiled, *lived*—but I didn't. Do you know why?"

"Because you're saving it," Dominic said. "For something you'd actually be hungry for."

"For leverage." She turned the bottle in her fingers, and the light moved through it, and inside—God, inside he could see *shapes*, impressions like figures in fog: a kitchen, a woman's hands, a song. "A thief who doesn't know what he lost will do almost anything to see it. And a thief who has seen it—" her socket-bright eyes found his "—will do *anything* to keep it."

"And the price?"

"Simple." She set the bottle on the table between them. "The map. And the two souls. Sera Voss and the courier's daughter, returned to my shelves, their entries rewritten in your hand—your *true* hand, the hand that forges mine. In exchange, your childhood. Sealed, whole, yours again. You walk out of this mountain a boy of seven wearing a man's scars, and they sing in my choir until the mountain is done with them."

* * *

He thought about saying no. He thought about it honestly, the way he balanced every ledger—assets against debts, gains against losses. He could refuse, and the three of them might fight their way out, might not, probably wouldn't. He could accept, and Wren would never forgive him, and Sera—Sera, who had burned out her own installed loyalty to hold a door for him—Sera would understand, which was somehow worse.

He thought about his mother's face in the torn memory. The singing. The mountain humming under a boy's window.

And he thought about the thing Wren had said to him in the guest cells, the night before the heist, when he'd told her he didn't know who he was underneath all the stolen goods. *Then find out,* she'd said. *Even if it's ugly. Especially if it's ugly.*

"Agreed," Dominic said.

The Regent studied him. "Just like that?"

"You expected begging? Bargaining? I'm a ledger-keeper, Your Majesty. I know a price when it's quoted." He held out his hand for the map—the true map, the courier's map, which the Hollow had taken from him at the door and now a silent courtier brought forward, rolled and sealed. He took it, and the wax was warm under his thumb, and he set it on the table beside the green bottle.

"There," he said. "The court beneath Mount Verity. Every corridor, every shelf, every door. The courier's last work. Yours."

The Regent didn't touch it. "You're giving this up rather easily, for a map that cost a man his life."

"The map cost me nothing," Dominic said. "I memorized it years ago. Well—three days ago. The paper was always just paper." A lie. The paper was the paper, and the Regent likely knew it, and the look in her eyes said she knew it, and let him have the lie the way a card player lets an opponent keep a losing hand. "The souls, then. Where do you want them?"

"Rewritten by morning. Your friend the enforcer and the courier's daughter, transferred back to the general collection." She slid the green bottle across the table until it touched his fingers, and the glass was cold, and through it he could see the fog-shapes shifting, and—there, faint as breath on a window—*a woman's mouth, open, singing.*

"Take it," the Regent said. "Open it when you're alone. A memory like this should be unwrapped the way you'd unwrap a letter from someone who loved you."

He picked it up. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

* * *

He walked out of the vault alone, as she insisted, as the bargain demanded. Past the cells again. This time Wren was on her feet at the bars, and her hands wrapped the iron, and her voice came out low and cracked.

"What did you give her?"

"Paper," Dominic said. "I gave her paper."

"Dominic." His name in her mouth, and he couldn't look at her, because if he looked at her he would see what the bargain cost and he would do something stupid and noble and fatal. "What. Did you give her."

"Go back to your cell, Wren."

"*Look at me.*"

He looked at her. Her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than anything else in this whole buried court, and he made himself smile the smile he used on clients, the one that closed doors.

"Thirty years ago," he said, "a boy was sold, and everyone kept the receipts. I'm just collecting."

He walked on before she could answer. Behind him he heard her palm strike the bars, once, and then her voice, chasing him down the corridor: "You come back. You hear me? Whatever you opened in there—*you come back and you tell me what you found.*"

The green bottle was cold in his hand, and the mountain hummed, and somewhere above them the choir of kept souls sang the Regent's song, and Dominic Quintana walked to his rooms alone, carrying his childhood like a letter he was afraid to open.

He sat. He lit no candle. The light from the bottle was enough—green and dim, like the bottom of a harbor.

He broke the seal.

The wax parted with a sound like a held breath released, and the memory rose out of the glass the way smoke rises, the way a song rises, and it did not pour into his eyes or his ears but into *him*, all of him, and the room fell away, and the mountain fell away, and he was seven years old, and it was morning, and his mother was singing.

He was in the kitchen of a house he had never remembered owning, and there was flour on the table, and his mother's hands were in it, and she was singing a song about salt and about a choir, and her voice was the most beautiful thing in the world because he was seven and everything his mother did was the most beautiful thing in the world. And then—there were men at the door. Guild men. And his mother went still, flour on her hands, and she looked at them the way you look at weather you knew was coming.

And one of the men stepped aside, and behind him stood a woman Dominic had never seen in any memory, any ledger, any dream—

The Regent.

Young. Whole-eyed. Wearing a face he knew, because it was his mother's face grown older, and she was looking at her daughter across the kitchen with a hunger that had nothing left in it of love.

*Mother,* his mother said. Not *Your Majesty.* Not *Regent.* *Mother.*

And the Regent smiled her hungry smile, and said, in the old language, in the language of the vault door, in the language of the mountain's first singer:

*"I've come to collect."*

The memory tore.

Not at the edges. Not like the professional cut he'd found before, clean as a scalpel. This tear was from the *inside*—the memory devouring its own ending, the way the Regent had once devoured her own past, and Dominic came back to himself in the dark room with the empty bottle in his hands and his own voice hoarse, screaming a word he hadn't known he knew.

*Mother.*

The mountain hummed. The choir sang. And Dominic Quintana sat alone in the dark, holding the shattered seal of his childhood, understanding at last what the Regent had really sold him thirty years ago—and what she had kept.
