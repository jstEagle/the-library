## 18. The Memory of a Boy

The Regent opened him like a letter, and he fell inside.

There was no transition. One moment the vault's cold light, the smell of stone and old salt, her hollow eyes watching him with something that might have been tenderness in a creature that still had a face for it—and then he was seven years old and it was raining.

Not memory-the-way-he-remembered-memory. Not the flat, secondhand feeling of a thing told to you by someone else. This was whole. This was wet. Rain on his knuckles, rain flattening the dust in the yard behind their house at the edge of the Saltwork district, rain making the world smell like iron and green things. He could feel his own small hands. He could feel the loose tooth in his jaw, worrying at it with his tongue because his mother had promised a copper coin from the tooth-ferry if he let it come out on its own.

He was inside his own childhood, and it was *vast*.

*This is what they took,* he thought, and the thought arrived in his adult voice, floating above the boy like a gull over a harbor. *This is what I have been missing without knowing the shape of the missing.*

The boy—Dominic, little Nico, the name fit him like a coat two sizes too big—ran through the rain to the kitchen door. Inside: steam, bread, lamp-oil, and his mother.

He had spent twenty years not thinking about her face. It is a professional skill, not-thinking; Dominic had built his whole self out of it. Now there was no room for the skill. Her face filled everything.

She was singing.

Of course she was singing. He knew this the way you know your own heartbeat—not as information but as fact. She sang while she worked, always, low and wandering, a song with no beginning he'd ever caught and no end either, just verses surfacing like fish and sinking again. The mountain song, she called it once, when he asked. Songs travel, Nico. They go where they're needed.

Her voice in the memory was better than any voice he had heard since. That was the cruelty of restored memories: they came with all their original fidelity. Every bottled year in the guild's vault, every traded first kiss and duchess's grief—he had handled thousands of them, held them up to the candlelight, and never once understood what he was actually holding. A memory wasn't a record. It was a *place*. And places could be lost the way countries are lost, and you could spend your whole life standing on the border of one, looking at maps.

His mother kneaded dough and sang and pushed a strand of hair off her forehead with the back of her wrist, flour to the elbow, and seven-year-old Nico sat on the table because sitting on chairs was for guests, and watched her the way children watch the sun.

*Say something,* adult-Dominic begged the scene. *Say anything. I don't remember your voice saying words.*

As if the memory heard him—as if twenty years of compression were only now being unpacked, shelf by shelf, crate by crate—she spoke.

"Supper first," she said, "then the ferry coin," and laughed at whatever his face did, and the laugh went through him like a blade going through water, no resistance at all.

* *

The guild's agents came on a Tuesday. He knew it was Tuesday because market-day leftovers meant soup, and soup meant his mother would be gone until dark, and dark was when they came.

In the memory he was upstairs, supposed to be asleep. He was not asleep. Seven-year-olds are smugglers of wakefulness, and Nico lay in his narrow bed listening to the voices below—the front door, which they never used, opening without a knock.

Three voices he didn't know. One he did.

He crept to the top of the stairs the way he would later teach himself to creep everywhere: heel first, weight slow, breath banked. Through the banister slats he saw lamplight and legs. His mother stood with her back to the hearth, and even now, even through twenty years and a child's blurred sight, Dominic could see what the boy had seen and not understood: she was not afraid. She was *negotiating.*

"The price is the price," said a woman's voice. Smooth. Amused. A voice like a ledger with good handwriting. "You signed. Everything signs, eventually. Even songs."

"I signed for passage," his mother said. "For us. For me and my son to leave the city. You've changed the terms."

"Terms change. Debts don't." A pause, and the amusement thinned into something older. "You know what I am. You've known since you were small enough to sit on my knee. Did you think the blood made you exempt?"

Blood. The word snagged in adult-Dominic's mind like a hook in cloth. But the memory moved on, indifferent to his questions, faithful to its own sequence.

His mother was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had dropped into the register of her singing—low, level, the note under all the other notes.

"Take mine, then. Not his."

"That was never the arrangement."

"It's the arrangement now." She stepped forward into the lamplight, and the boy at the top of the stairs saw her face fully for the first time, and adult-Dominic felt the ground of the memory shift beneath him, because she was not afraid and she was not negotiating and she was not even bargaining.

She was choosing.

"You want a singer. You've wanted one since the mountain went quiet. I'm the last voice left of the old line, and we both know it." She lifted her chin. "Mine is worth more than his. Take mine, and the boy walks free, and the debt dies with me, and you never touch him. Swear it the way your kind swears. In salt. In stone."

Silence below. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down through floorboards.

"You sound like her," the smooth voice said at last, and there was something raw in it now, something that did not belong in a negotiation. "You sound exactly like—"

"Don't." His mother's voice cracked, one clean break. "Don't say it. If you say it, you'll want to keep me instead of eat me, and keeping isn't mercy either. Swear the trade or leave this house."

And the woman in the lamplight—adult-Dominic was assembling her now from fragments, the ringed hand, the gray-streaked braid, the eyes that caught the firelight and gave nothing back, *hollow*, he thought, *I know those eyes, I sat across a table from those eyes three days ago*—the Regent of the court beneath Mount Verity looked at his mother for a long, long moment, and swore.

In salt. In stone.

* *

The sale happened in spring. The eating happened the same night.

The memory did not flinch from it and neither, now, could he. He was seven, hiding in the cupboard where the winter blankets lived, because his mother had told him: *Whatever you hear tonight, Nico, whatever you see, you stay in the dark until morning, and then you forget. Forgetting is a gift. I'm giving it to you early.*

But the boy had not forgotten. That was the thing no one had known—not the guild, not Odile, not the courier whose map had led him here, not the Regent herself. The extraction had taken the memory whole, sealed it, sold it as payment, filed it in a vault beneath a mountain. But a seven-year-old's mind keeps what the professionals miss: not the scene itself but the residue of it. A smell of salt. A sound through the cupboard door like singing that went wrong. His own small hands pressed over his own small ears, and the terrible arithmetic a child does when the adults' story doesn't add up.

Now the memory played it entire, in fidelity so complete it was indistinguishable from return.

He watched, from inside the boy's body, from behind the cupboard slats, as his mother walked out into the yard with the Regent. There was no violence. That was the worst of it. There was a circle drawn in spilled salt, and the two of them stood in it, and his mother began to sing—the mountain song, the one with no beginning—and the Regent closed her eyes and *leaned in*, the way you lean toward a fire, the way a drowning man leans toward air.

And the song went into her. And his mother went with it.

Not death. Death would have been kinder and simpler and would have left a grave. What happened in that yard was a *transfer*. He watched his mother's face grow lighter, and lighter, the way a lamp grows lighter as the oil goes, and he watched the Regent's hollow eyes fill—for the first time in what her face suggested had been centuries—with color, with weather, with a daughter's scraped knees and a daughter's wedding and a daughter's betrayal, an entire lifetime pouring into a vessel built to hold lifetimes.

And when it was done his mother folded down into the grass like a dress slipping off a hook, empty, and the Regent stood alone in the salt circle with her hand over her mouth, and she was crying.

That undid him more than anything. Not the hunger. The grief. She had eaten her own daughter and wept while she did it, and the weeping had not stopped her, and that—that was the shape of the thing that ruled the court beneath Mount Verity. Not malice. Malice would be manageable. This was appetite wearing love's face, hunger that remembered being human and used the remembering as a knife to open the next throat.

The boy stayed in the cupboard until morning, as instructed. Then the forgetting came—but not the way anyone intended.

Because the guild agents returned at dawn, per the amended contract, to collect the payment's *documentation*: the sealed memory, extracted from the house's ledger-stone, from the mother's effects, from every witness surface within reach. Professional work. Odile's work, though she'd have been younger then, apprentice-handed, careful. They swept the yard, the kitchen, the stairs.

They missed the cupboard.

They missed the residue in a sleeping child—the salt-smell, the wrong-singing, the shape of a choice made in his name and without him. They sealed the full memory in wax and glass and carried it away to be sold as collateral against a debt, and the debt traveled, and the collateral traveled, and twenty years later a dying courier bled out on a guild floor pressing a map into the hands of a man who did not know why the mountain song felt like home.

*Oh,* adult-Dominic thought, in the vast lit interior of his own seventh year. *Oh, you fools. You beautiful, thorough, blind fools. You took the memory and left the wound, and I have been living in the wound ever since. That is what I am. That is what a thief is. That is what the Regent is. We are both of us people walking around inside the shape of what was taken.*

* *

The memory should have ended there. The cupboard, the morning, the agents, the seal.

It didn't.

There was more. There was one thing more, tucked at the very end like a verse surfacing late in a song with no ending, and it surfaced now as the vault around him trembled—because far above, in the real stone dark of Mount Verity's throat, the Regent had begun to unseal the wax, had begun to read her purchase, had begun to taste her granddaughter's seventh year—and the memory, feeling her attention like sun on closed eyelids, opened its last page.

His mother, three days before the sale. Kitchen. Flour. The boy on the table.

She had stopped singing mid-verse. She had looked at him—looked at him the way you look at something you are memorizing on purpose—and she had said:

"Nico. Listen to me. Someday, someone will give you back this year. It sounds strange now, but someday a door opens and everything returns, and when it does—" she had taken his face in her floury hands, and her eyes had been fierce and wet and absolutely certain— "when it does, you'll find the last verse of my song. The one I never sing in the house. It's in you already. It's been in you since before you were born. Old line, little love. The song doesn't live in singers. Singers live in the song."

She had kissed his forehead, flour and all.

"And if you ever meet her—my mother, the one who rules under the mountain—if you ever stand in front of her, don't hate her. Hate is a hunger too. Sing instead. Sing the last verse, and watch what happens to a thing that has eaten everything except forgiveness."

Above him—or around him, or through him—the vault shuddered. Wax cracking. The Regent's attention pouring deeper, drinking him, drinking the boy, drinking the flour and the rain and the cupboard-dark, and she did not yet know what she was swallowing.

Adult-Dominic stood in the last light of his childhood, twenty years late for supper, and understood at last what he was carrying.

Not stolen goods. Not collateral. Not a debt.

A weapon. The only weapon in the world that hunger cannot digest.

He opened his mouth and began, for the first time since he was seven years old, to sing.

The last verse rose in him whole—it had been waiting, coiled in the marrow, in the blood, in the old line—and the vault walls caught it and gave it back, stone remembering stone, and somewhere above him the Regent went very, very still, the way the world goes still when it hears a note it buried centuries ago.

The shelves began to shake.

And in the dark of the court, ten thousand sealed souls turned toward the sound like flowers toward a sun they had forgotten existed.
