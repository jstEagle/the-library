## 19. The Unmaking Song

### Dominic

The ledger was still warm from his hands.

He stood in the wreckage of the filing hall with the mass-transfer entry open across both arms—a book the size of a door, its pages shivering like water—and he understood, in the way a thief understands a lock at last, that he had not stolen the court's memory. He had *moved* it. Every soul on every shelf now lived in this one binding, filed under no name but their own.

And the Regent knew.

Her voice came down through the cathedral of shelves like cold air through a cracked window. Not angry. Worse than angry—*hungry*.

"Thief," she said. "You have taken my pantry."

"I've taken your prisoners," Dominic said.

"Those are the same word here."

Shelves were collapsing in the dark above them. He could hear the Hollow moving—hundreds of them, nameless courtiers with candle-flames where their faces should be, converging on the light of Wren's lantern. Sera was somewhere behind him, holding the archive door with her whole body and whatever was left of her purchased loyalty, and every second he spent standing there was a second she paid for.

But he didn't run. Because he had opened his childhood like a letter, and he had read what no one knew—not Odile, not the guild, not even the dead courier who had carried the map with his dying hands:

His mother had been the Regent's daughter.

And the Regent had eaten her.

The knowledge sat in him like a swallowed coal. It did not make him brave. It made him *precise*. Grief, for a thief, is just information about what matters.

"Wren," he said quietly. "When I tell you—open the ledger to the first page and hold it flat."

"Why?" Her voice shook. She had heard her father's last act sung out of the mountain's stone an hour ago; she had arrived at this cathedral wanting the Regent's head, and she had gotten something more dangerous instead: a reason.

"Because," Dominic said, "I'm going to sing."

---

### Wren

Her father had been tone-deaf.

She remembered that about him—one of the few things she remembered, because the guild had bought most of the rest before she could walk. He hummed anyway. Badly, constantly, in the kitchen of the house she could barely picture, and when she'd asked him once why he bothered, he'd said: *Because songs aren't for the singer, little wren. They're for whoever's listening.*

She hadn't understood it then. She understood it now, watching Dominic Quintana stand in the middle of a collapsing vault with a ledger full of souls in his arms and sing.

It was his mother's song—the one the stone under Mount Verity had shown them, the one she'd sung to a seven-year-old boy before men with guild seals came and took his childhood away in bottles. Wren didn't know the words. Nobody living knew the words. But the mountain knew them. The stone itself remembered everything, and as Dominic's cracked, untrained, absolutely unlovely voice found the first line, the mountain *answered*.

The humming they'd felt since the salt road began—that low note under everything, the sound she'd thought was the mountain's hunger—resolved into harmony.

The shelves lit up.

Not all at once. One shelf, then ten, then hundreds, each bottled soul flaring like a struck match as the song reached it. And Wren saw what happened inside the glass: the memories turned over. The souls stopped being *stored* and started being *had*. A woman's voice rose from a jar labeled only with a serial number, singing a lullaby in a language Wren had never heard. A man's laugh broke out of a flask like something escaping. Names—real names, first names, the names people are called by people who love them—began to rise off the labels and hang in the air like breath in winter.

"What's happening to them?" she shouted over the growing choir.

"They're remembering themselves." Dominic's face was wet. He kept singing. "A soul can't be owned while it knows its own name. That's the whole trick of this place. That's been the whole trick everywhere, hasn't it? The guild. The court. All of it. You keep people by keeping them from themselves."

The nearest shelf groaned, tilted, and came down in an avalanche of glass.

Wren flinched—but the bottles didn't shatter. They *opened*. Thousands of stoppers popped at once, and what came out wasn't smoke or spirit or anything she had a word for. It was *people-shaped absence*, and then it wasn't absent anymore. It was a crowd. It was centuries of the court's stock, standing up out of their containers, blinking, clutching at each other, saying their own names aloud like prayers.

An old man near Wren fell to his knees and said, "Marek. My name is Marek. I had a boat. I had—" His face crumpled. "I had a daughter."

Wren grabbed his arm and hauled him up. "Then walk, Marek. Walk toward the door. There's a woman holding it who'll die before she lets it close."

### The First of the Hollow

She had been the court's steward for longer than she had been anything. She had sold her name in the third century of the Regent's reign for a rank she could no longer remember wanting, and she had tended the shelves ever since, and she had believed—she had *needed* to believe—that what was stored there was not suffering, only order.

Now the shelves were singing.

Now the candles of her fellow courtiers were guttering, and behind each guttering flame something old and buried was clawing upward, and the First of the Hollow felt it happen inside herself: a crack, a warmth, a word rising through her like a bubble through deep water.

*Liesl.*

She dropped her candle. It went out. And where her face had been—where the smooth blankness had been for two hundred years—there was suddenly a woman's face, lined and plain and weeping.

"Liesl," she said aloud, tasting it. "My name is Liesl."

Around her, the court was waking. Hollow after Hollow stopped mid-stride, staggered, and put their hands to their faces as if checking whether they still had one. Some screamed. Some laughed. One old courtier simply sat down in the aisle among the broken glass and repeated *my name, my name, my name* like a man counting coins he'd forgotten he owned.

Liesl looked up at the dais, at the throne of black stone where the Regent was rising, and she did the thing that two hundred years of service had made unthinkable and one remembered name had made inevitable.

She pointed.

"There," Liesl said. "There she is. *That's the one who ate us.*"

### The Regent

She had eaten her daughter and it had not been enough.

This was the truth she had arranged three centuries of rule to avoid hearing, and now it was being sung at her by a boy with his mother's jawline and his mother's terrible pitch, and the court's entire memory was loose in the air around her like a flood breaking a dam.

The Regent had always fed carefully. One soul at a time, sipped slowly, the memories sorted before swallowing—the sweet ones kept, the bitter ones discarded into the stone. Discipline was the whole art of what she was. Hunger without discipline was just a grave with a crown on it.

But the song would not stop, and the memories were not coming one at a time anymore. They were coming as a *choir*. Ten thousand lives at once, every one of them insisting on itself, every one of them carrying its griefs and weddings and small kindnesses and debts, and she had no time to sort, no time to choose, no time to spit out the bitter.

She drank anyway, because the alternative was starving, and starving meant becoming the mountain again—becoming nothing again, the way she had been nothing before she'd learned that a soul eaten is a power gained.

The first hundred years hit her like weather.

She was a fisherman drowning under ice. She was a bride in a lemon courtyard. She was a child burning with fever while her mother sang—*sang, sang, everyone in this cursed place was always singing*—and the Regent staggered on her throne as the memories piled up faster than she could digest them, undigested, unsorted, *alive* inside her, each one a guest that refused to leave.

"Stop," she said.

The choir got louder.

"STOP." She came off the dais, and the black stone of the throne cracked behind her, and for the first time in three centuries the Regent of Mount Verity raised her voice in something that was not command. It was fear. "I am the mountain's first singer! I ate my own past to become what I am! I am older than your names—"

"Liesl," said the former Hollow, pointing at her. "Marek. Anouk. Tomas. Bright-Eyed Sal." The freed souls were taking up the roll call now, shouting names at her like stones. "Odile's mother, whose grief she sold twice. The courier Corvin, who died buying back his daughter. *Sera Voss, who is holding your door right now.*"

Every name landed. Every name was a memory she had swallowed and never digested, and they were all coming up at once, and the Regent felt the architecture of herself begin to fail—centuries of careful appetite collapsing into one vast, undifferentiated ache.

"You cannot digest us," said the boy, still singing, tears cutting through the dust on his face. "You never could. That's why you needed the shelves. That's why you needed the guild. You don't want to remember, Mother-of-nothing. You want to *hold* remembering without paying for it. And it doesn't work. It has never worked."

The mountain shook. Somewhere far above, a gallery of shelves let go entirely, and the crash rolled down through the court like thunder learning to speak.

---

### Dominic

He held the ledger against his chest and sang until his voice tore, and when it tore, the freed souls carried the melody for him—thousands of voices, none of them trained, all of them certain. His mother's song had come apart in his mouth after the third verse; he'd never actually learned it, only dreamed it. But it turned out the song had never been a fixed thing. It was a shape, and any voice could fill it, and the mountain filled it best of all, booming up through the floor, through their boots, through their bones.

The Regent came apart the way a knot comes apart—slowly, then all at once.

First her composure. Then her outline. She flickered like a bad candle, and in each flicker Dominic saw what she really was: not a queen, not even a monster. A first singer who had been afraid of forgetting, who had solved forgetting by eating everything, and who had therefore never held a single memory long enough to be changed by it. Three centuries old and completely unlived-in. A vault that walked.

"You're doing this on purpose," she gasped. The flickering was worse now. Her eyes—hollow-eyed, courteous, ancient—found his across the ruined hall. "You're pouring yourself into the song. Everything you carry. I can taste it. Your childhood. The guild's ledgers. *Me.*"

"Yes," Dominic said.

"It will empty you. You know what you are without your memories. Nothing. A blank page."

"I know exactly what I am without my memories." His voice was wrecked. He sang anyway. "I've been one for twenty years. I just didn't know it yet."

The Regent reached for him—one last reflex of appetite, a hand like smoke and crown—and the freed souls closed around her. Not attacking. *Remembering at her.* Marek and his boat. Liesl and her two hundred lost years. Anouk, Tomas, Bright-Eyed Sal, the bride in the lemon courtyard, the child with the fever, the drowned fisherman, the courier Corvin with his last act of love. They pressed in close and gave her everything she had ever swallowed, returned with interest, undiluted, all at once.

The Regent of Mount Verity opened her mouth to scream and sang instead.

One note. Her mother's note—no, her *daughter's* note, the note the woman she had eaten used to sing in a kitchen Dominic could almost picture—and then she came apart into the stone, into the walls, into the deep roots of the mountain, and the humming that had lived under the world since before anyone's memory stopped, and in the sudden silence Dominic heard, very faintly, a woman's voice say: *oh.*

Just that. Just recognition. Three centuries late.

Then the mountain exhaled, and the shelves came down like rain, and Dominic Quintana stood in the ruin holding a ledger containing every soul in the court, and the weight of it nearly took him off his feet.

Not the paper weight. The other kind.

Ten thousand lives, filed and waiting. His mother somewhere in the deep vault, sealed in glass by the Regent's own hand. Sera bleeding behind a door. Wren's hand finding his elbow in the dust-dark, steadying him.

"The ledger," Wren said. Her face was gray with rock dust, streaked with tears she didn't seem to know about. "What do we do with it?"

Dominic looked down at the great book in his arms—at the whole court's memory, bound and heavy as a sleeping child—and he understood that the hardest theft of his life was still ahead of him, and it was a giving-back, and it would cost him everything he had, including the self he had only just begun to find.

"We open it," he said. "Every entry. Every soul goes home."

"And yours?"

He didn't answer. Above them, through the shattered ceiling of the court, dawn-light was falling into the underworld for the first time in three hundred years—and in that light, on the top step of the ruined dais, something small and glass caught fire.

A single bottle. Unbroken. Labeled in a dead courier's careful hand.

Wren saw it at the same moment he did, and went white. "That's my father."

"No," Dominic said, turning toward it, the ledger dragging at his arms like a tide. "Look at the seal. That's guild wax. That's *Odile's* mark."

The bottle sat in the light, and inside it, faint as a fingerprint, something moved.
