## 20. Everything He Ever Stole

The court was screaming in a hundred voices, and every voice was his.

Dominic stood at the center of the collapsed nave, the master ledger open across his forearms like a wounded bird, and felt them all at once—the Hollow's names flooding back into their mouths, the shelved souls waking in their jars of glass and salt, the Regent's ten thousand stolen years churning through him like a tide that had mistaken a man for a harbor.

He had thought he understood what it meant to carry memories. He had been a thief for twenty years; he had carried duchesses' griefs and murderers' first loves and whole decades peeled off farmers who wanted to forget a bad harvest. He had thought himself a ship built for cargo.

He had not known what it was to carry a mountain.

*Let go,* said his mother's song, still ringing in the stone, still holding the court open like a hand holding a door. *Let go, let go.*

But letting go was the one thing he had never learned.

---

The Regent was on her knees.

That was the first thing he truly saw—past the roar of returning memory, past Wren dragging Sera's limp body behind a toppled shelf, past the choir of freed souls rising up through the broken floor in columns of light and salt-smell. The Regent of Mount Verity, ancient and courteous and hollow-eyed, was on her knees in the rubble of her own cathedral, and she was *hungry*.

"You brought me a feast," she said, and her voice no longer sounded like courtesy. It sounded like a well with the bottom dropped out. "Ten thousand souls' worth of remembering, all poured into one small vessel. I can *smell* it on you, little thief. Every year you took from Odile's vault. Every soul you emptied from my shelves. You're carrying my whole court inside your skin."

She began to crawl toward him. She had been beautiful once—he saw that now, saw it because her memories were in him and he could see *through* her: a woman on a mountainside four thousand years ago, singing to stone because she was lonely, eating her first memory because the silence hurt, and the power had tasted so much better than the grief.

"I could take it gently," she said. "I always took gently. That was my gift. Ask your mother."

Dominic did not flinch. He was proud of that, later, in whatever life came after this one. He did not flinch, though the words went into him like a blade finding an old scar.

"You ate her," he said.

"I ate everyone," said the Regent. "It's what rulers are. Give it here, boy. All of it. You'll feel nothing ever again, and isn't that what thieves want? Isn't emptiness your country? I'm offering you citizenship."

And God help him, some starving corner of him wanted to say yes. Twenty years of being a stranger in his own skull. Twenty years of doors. She was offering him the one thing he'd never managed to steal for himself: peace.

Then he heard Wren shout his name—not *thief*, not *Quintana*, just *Dominic*, ragged and furious—and remembered chapter by chapter why he'd come down into the dark.

"No," he said. "I know how this ends. I've read the ledger. Everything that gets eaten ends up in someone's belly, growing heavy, growing hungry. If I keep all this—" he lifted the ledger, lifted the weight behind his eyes, "—then in a hundred years I'm you. Kneeling in a hole, eating children, calling it ruling."

"Everyone becomes me," the Regent said, almost tenderly. "Given enough time."

"Not everyone," Dominic said. "Only the ones who keep."

---

Here is what a memory-thief knows that no one else does:

A memory is not a thing. A memory is a *debt*. Every recollection is a small promise made between a moment and a mind—that the moment will not be lost, that it mattered, that someone will come back for it. Hoarders break the promise. They take the moment and never return it, and the debt compounds, interest upon interest, until the hoarder is nothing but a vault with teeth.

Dominic had been breaking promises since he was seven years old.

The ledger in his hands held every transfer he'd forged in the night watches—every soul moved from shelf to freedom, every name re-entered against its owner. But there was one final entry, and it was written in the oldest grammar he knew, the thief's grammar, the grammar of giving back.

*Return to sender,* it said. *All accounts.*

Not to the shelves. The shelves were broken, the court collapsing around them, and anything filed here would be found by whatever grew in the Regent's place. No. Back to the world. Back to the living. Every year, every kiss, every grief, every name—poured out of him and into the mountain's throat, where the stone would sing them home the way it had sung everything home since the beginning.

Including his own. Including the childhood sealed in wax and Odile's handwriting. Including the mother's face. Including, if he was honest, the last twenty years themselves—the guild, the ledgers, the small competent darkness he had built his self out of.

A blank page. That was the price. That was what it cost to end a hunger without becoming one.

He looked at Wren. She was kneeling over Sera, pressing her coat against the enforcer's wound, and her face was streaked with salt-dust and tears, and she was the only person alive who knew what he was about to do.

"Dominic," she said. She'd already read it in his hands. Thieves' hands are honest; they can't help it. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. You taught me the ledger—you showed me—there's always *another entry*—"

"There isn't," he said. "This is the last one. And it's a good one." He smiled, and found, strangely, that it didn't shake. "Listen. When it's done, I won't know you. I want you to know that going in. I'll look at you like a stranger, because I'll be one. Whatever I was to you—it goes back too."

"I don't care," Wren said, standing, crossing the rubble toward him with her fists clenched. "My father gave everything to buy me one passage home, and I spent years being angry about it, and I am *not* doing that again. Do you hear me? I'll remember for both of us. That's what memory is *for*. It's not a vault. It's a *choir*. One voice carries the line until the others find it again."

Somewhere above them, the freed souls were singing. His mother's song, passed mouth to mouth through a hundred throats that hadn't sung in centuries.

"One voice carries the line," Dominic agreed.

The Regent lunged.

---

She was fast for something four thousand years old—fast the way famine is fast, fast the way fire is fast—and she got one hand around his wrist before the ledger opened fully, and her fingers were cold as a filing drawer, and her eyes were right there, inches from his, and they were so *empty*. That was the horror of her, finally seen close. Not malice. Vacancy. A woman who had eaten everything she was to become everything she ruled, and now ruled nothing but the hunger itself.

"Little thief," she whispered. "Give it back. Give it *back*."

"That's exactly what I'm doing," said Dominic Quintana, and turned the page.

---

He had expected it to feel like drowning. It felt like *spring*.

Every memory left him at once, and it was not loss, it was thaw—a river cracking loose after a hard winter, everything locked and silent suddenly moving at speed. He felt the duchess's grief leave him and go home to whoever had earned it. He felt the courier's final hours flow out through his palms like warm water—*the Regent keeps what's mine*—and settle somewhere safe. He felt ten thousand strangers' Sundays, their weddings, their betrayals, their ordinary miraculous mornings, streaming out of him in a bright flood, and the mountain drank them and sang them upward and outward, scattering them into the world like seed.

And under all of it, deepest, the Regent's own hoard—the four millennia of eaten lives—pouring out of him faster than she could catch them, and she *tried*, God forgive her, she clawed at the air trying to hold even one year of one child, and each one slipped her grasp like water through a fist.

"No," she said. "No—those are *mine*—"

"They were never yours," Dominic said. "You just kept them longest."

She came apart the way salt comes apart in rain.

There was no scream. There was a long, low note—the first note, maybe, the one she'd sung alone on the mountainside when she was still only lonely—and then the great hollow architecture of her unraveled into dust and light and the smell of the sea, which is the smell of old salt, which is the smell of everything the world remembers eventually. What was left of the Regent of Mount Verity sank into the stone floor of her own cathedral, and the stone accepted her, the way stone accepts everything, without judgment, and began, very quietly, to sing over her.

The mountain stopped humming and started *breathing*. Dominic felt the change move up through his boots and out through the shattered roof, and far away—impossibly far, or perhaps not far at all—the choir of freed souls rose to meet it.

Then the flood reached the last page.

His own memories.

He had time for one breath. In it he thought: *salt on my lips, someone's voice reading to me, a door I never opened, a door I'm finally walking through.* He thought: *I hope whoever I am next is kinder.* He thought: *Wren.*

Then he gave that back too.

---

* * *

---

Silence.

Dust drifting down through a hole in the world, catching light.

A man stood in the rubble of a fallen court, holding a closed book, and did not know where he was.

He was crying. He noticed this with mild curiosity, the way one notices weather. His face was wet. His chest ached in the specific way a chest aches after carrying something enormous that is no longer there. He looked down at his arms as if expecting to find the missing thing still in them.

Nothing. Empty hands. An empty man. A page with nothing written on it yet.

Around him, ruins breathed. Shelves taller than churches lay broken into kindling, and among the wreckage moved people—no, more than people, *luminous* people, souls in the act of becoming bodies again, or bodies in the act of becoming souls, he couldn't have said which and lacked the vocabulary to try. They were singing. He didn't know the song. It made him cry harder, and he didn't know why that was either, and he stood in the middle of the ruin weeping at a melody he'd never heard, holding a book he couldn't read.

Footsteps. Someone running toward him through the dust—a young woman, dark-haired, salt-stained, with her father's stubbornness written all over her face, though he had no way of knowing that.

She stopped in front of him. She searched his eyes the way you search a house after a flood, looking for anything that survived.

"Do you know me?" she asked.

He shook his head. "Should I?"

Something broke in her face and something else, sturdier, rebuilt it in the same instant. She wiped her cheeks with the back of her hand, leaving a smear of dust like war paint.

"No," she said. "Not yet. But you will." She took the ledger gently out of his hands—she handled it like it was sleeping, like it might wake—and tucked it under her arm. Then she took his hand instead, which was empty, which was blank, which was waiting. "My name's Wren. I'm going to tell you a story on the way home. It's about a thief. It's long, so pay attention."

Above them, through the torn-open roof of the world, the mountain sang, and the freed dead sang with it, and the man who had been Dominic Quintana tipped his head back and hummed along to a tune he did not know, tears running clean lines through the dust on his face, not knowing why—

—but knowing, in the way of the body, in the way of the blank page that still remembers the pressure of the pen:

that the song was *his*, somehow;

that he had paid for it;

and that somewhere in the wreck of the world above, a woman with a ledger full of names was already learning the first line, ready to teach it back to him one morning at a time.
