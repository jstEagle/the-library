## 21. A Blank Page

The first thing I learned about Dominic Quintana after the mountain came down was that he cried without knowing why.

He sat in the rubble of the court with his hands open on his knees, palms up, the way a man sits when he's been holding something so long his fingers have forgotten how to close. His eyes were wet. His face was empty. Not blank the way a wall is blank—blank the way a page is blank, the way a thing is blank that is waiting to be written on.

"Who are you?" he asked me.

And because I had spent eleven days learning to hate him and three hours learning not to, I said, "Wren. My father was the courier. You carried his map."

He nodded like that meant something, though it couldn't have. He nodded the way people nod at a language they used to speak.

That was the first day.

* * *

Sera Voss found us on the third.

She came down the miners' road with soot in her hair and the guild's vault keys on a ring at her belt, walking like a woman who had set down something heavy and wasn't sure her spine would hold its old shape. Her purchased loyalty had burned out of her in the court of kept things—I saw the moment it went, saw her face go from duty to terror to a kind of naked, furious freedom—and whatever the guild had installed in her, it was gone. What remained was Sera. Just Sera. She told me later it was like walking out of a house she'd always believed was her body.

She took one look at Dominic sitting in the ash with his empty hands and his wet eyes and said, "Oh, you idiot. You absolute, magnificent idiot."

"You knew him before," I said.

"Everyone knew him before. That was the problem. He was the man nobody remembered." She crouched in front of him. "Dominic. It's Sera Voss. I was sent to watch you. I ended up holding a door for you. It cost me—" she touched her temple, "—most of what I was. And I'd do it again, which tells you something about what I've become."

He looked at her with the terrible patience of the empty. "I'm sorry," he said. "I don't know you."

"No," she said. "You don't. That's rather the point of you now."

Then she stood, dusted off her knees, and looked at the ruin of the guild's old trade the way a carpenter looks at a burned house—grieving the loss and measuring the timber in the same breath.

"We'll need a ledger," she said to me.

"We have one," I said. "The one he emptied the court into. It's blank now. Every soul in it went home."

Sera smiled, and it was the first honest smile I'd ever seen on her, and it changed her whole face. "Good," she said. "I've always wanted to write in a blank book."

* * *

We rebuilt in the harbor district, in the guild's old counting house, because it was there and because neither of us could stomach the idea of the Saltwork rising again under anyone else's name. Sera tore the sign down the first morning. She had a new one painted by week's end, in plain letters, no salt, no sigil:

**THE HOUSE OF RETURN**

Underneath, smaller: *Bring us what was taken from you. We will help you take it back.*

The old guild had bought and bottled and sold. The new house did the opposite. People came at first out of curiosity, then out of desperation—a widow whose husband's last words had been sold to a moneylender; a soldier who'd traded his mother's face to pay a debt and could no longer remember why he wept at lullabies. Sera sat with each of them in the back room where a courier had once died in my father's arms, and she opened their contracts, and she taught them the thief's grammar: how a memory moves, how a ledger-entry can be forged, how to steal yourself back.

"We're not thieves," she told them. "We're thieves' children. We know the locks."

I kept the other ledger. Dominic's ledger—the one from the court, the one he'd poured everything into and then poured everything out of. It was blank, but it wasn't only blank. The pages remembered. If you held one to the light you could see the ghost of the entries, faint as breath on glass: names, songs, a duchess's grief, a boy's seventh year. The book had held the whole court's memory in its binding, and even emptied, it remembered the shape of what it carried.

So I wrote in it. Not entries. Stories.

* * *

Every morning I read to him.

We kept a room for him above the counting house, small and clean, with a window on the harbor. He didn't need keeping, exactly—he could dress himself, feed himself, find his way home from the market as long as the route was simple. What he couldn't do was answer the question *who are you?* with anything but a shrug and a small, apologetic smile.

So every morning I sat on the chair by his window with the ledger open on my knees, and I read him himself.

"Your name is Dominic Quintana," I'd begin. "You were born in the salt quarter. You had a mother who sang, and her song was—"

And I'd sing it. Badly. I have a courier's voice, not a singer's, flat and practical, built for carrying words across distances, not for making them beautiful. He never minded. He'd close his eyes and listen the way you listen to weather.

"Your mother's name was Lira. She was the daughter of a woman who lived beneath a mountain and ate her own memory to become powerful, and when that woman grew hungry, she ate Lira too. You were seven. Men came from the guild. Your childhood was the price that sealed a bargain, and you grew up without it, and you became a man who took memories from others because you could not bear the hole where yours had been."

He'd listen. He always listened. Sometimes he'd say, "That's sad," in the mild voice of a stranger hearing a stranger's story. Sometimes he'd say, "Was she a good mother?" and I'd say, "I think she must have been," and he'd nod.

And once—only once—he said, "The song. Sing it again," and when I did, his lips moved with the words before he knew they were going to, and he stopped, and looked at his own hands, and cried without knowing why.

I didn't tell him that was his mother's song living in his body, deeper than the theft, deeper than the emptying. I didn't tell him that the mountain's stone had sung everything back to him before it came down, and that some things go into the bones where no ledger can reach them. I just sang it again, and let him cry, and after a while he stopped, and asked me what was for breakfast.

* * *

The story I told him most often was the story of what he gave away.

"You stood in the court of kept things," I'd read, "with the whole mountain's memory in your hands, and the Regent was coming apart, flooded with everything she'd eaten and could not digest. And you could have kept it. You could have been her. You were a thief, and you were holding the greatest theft in the history of the world, and no one could have stopped you from keeping it.

"Instead you opened your hands."

I'd turn the page, though there was nothing on it but the ghost of writing.

"You poured it all back. Every soul in the shelves went home that night—the Hollow woke on their cathedral floor and remembered their names, some of them weeping, some of them laughing, one old courtier standing in the rubble shouting *Mirelle, my name was Mirelle* over and over like a man saved from drowning. The Regent dissolved into the stone she'd hollowed. And you stood there with nothing left, not even the memory of why you were crying.

"You gave away everything you had ever stolen. Including the only thing that was ever really yours."

He'd listen to that part more carefully than any other. I could tell because of his hands. When a story matters to him, his hands go still.

"Did it work?" he asked me once. "The giving away. Did it do what it was supposed to do?"

"You're alive," I said. "She isn't. The souls went home. Sera is free. I have my father's soul back—he's not *here*, he's gone where souls go, but he's *whole*, he's where he chose to be, and no one owns him. That's what it did."

"No," he said, gently, the way you correct a child. "I mean—did it work on *me*?"

And I didn't know how to answer that. Because the honest answer was: we'd find out. Because the honest answer was: a man is not his memories, or a man is nothing but his memories, and no one alive knows which, and Dominic Quintana had volunteered to be the experiment that settled it.

"I think," I said finally, "that we're finding out one morning at a time."

He considered that. Then he said, "Then I'd like breakfast. One morning at a time starts with breakfast."

It was the first joke I ever heard him make. Sera, in the doorway, laughed so hard she had to sit down on the stairs.

* * *

He began, slowly, to make new memories. That was the thing none of us had expected—the empty page didn't stay empty, it just started writing differently.

He learned the harbor. Not the way he'd once known it—every shadow, every escape, every pocket of the market where a thief could vanish—but the way a man knows a place he *likes*. He knew which fishmonger gave extra to the gull-bitten kids. He knew where the light came through the warehouse slats at the end of the day, in long gold bars you could stand in. He learned that I take my coffee bitter and Sera takes hers sweet enough to be a dessert, and he started making all three cups without asking, because that's a memory too, the kind that doesn't go in ledgers.

He kept a book of his own. A small one, cheap paper, string-bound. Sera bought it for him in the second month, and he carried it everywhere, and he wrote in it in a careful, unpracticed hand:

*Salt on my lips in the morning. Wren's voice, reading. The gold light in the warehouse. Sera laughs from her whole body. The song—I don't know where the song comes from, but I know all of it.*

A ledger of small kindnesses instead of sins. He didn't call it that. I did, in my head, and then one day out loud, and he looked at me with such a naked, startled pleasure that I understood he'd never thought of himself as anything but a ledger of sins, even now, even empty. Even blank.

"You're not that anymore," I told him. "You're not the ledger. You're the hand that writes it."

He thought about that for a long time. "Whose hand?" he asked.

"Yours," I said. "Whoever that turns out to be."

* * *

The choir started in the spring.

The mountain had not stopped singing entirely—Sera had warned us it might not, that a thing which remembered everything could not be expected to forget it all just because its keeper was gone. But the song had changed. It was no longer the Regent's hungry drone, the sound of a memory being digested. It was a choir now. The freed souls, the ones who'd gone home into the world, had left something of themselves in the stone, or the stone had kept something of them, and on clear evenings you could stand on the harbor wall and hear it: hundreds of voices, wordless, rising from the mountain's throat like heat off a roof.

Dominic would walk up there. He never asked why. He'd just feel it, some evenings, some pull he couldn't name, and he'd climb the old salt-miners' road—the road he'd come down once with a map in his skin and a hole where his childhood should have been—and he'd sit on the rocks below the mountain's throat, and he'd hum.

He didn't know the song. That's what he said. He didn't know the song, he'd never heard it before, he had no memories, he was a blank page.

But he hummed along. Every part. Without missing a note.

The first time Sera and I followed him up and heard it—heard this man with no past harmonizing with a choir of returned souls, his voice threading into theirs like it had always belonged there—Sera had to turn away and walk a little distance off, and I pretended not to see her wipe her face.

"He gave everything away," she said, when she came back. "Everything. And the mountain *still knows him*."

"Maybe that's the answer," I said. "To the question. Whether a man is his memories."

She looked at him, sitting in the last light with his mouth open around a melody he couldn't source, and she said, "Or maybe the question was wrong from the start. Maybe a man is what he *gives*."

* * *

Tonight I read him the whole ledger, start to finish, the way I do on the first of every month. His name. His mother's song. The courier at the salt door. The map hidden inside a stolen memory. Odile's bargain and her betrayal. The road under the world, and the singing stone, and the shelves, and the forgery, and the door Sera held. The Regent opening his childhood like a letter, and what he found inside it—his mother, the Regent's daughter, eaten by her own mother's hunger. And then the song, and the unmaking, and the open hands.

When I finished, he was quiet for a while. The harbor sounds came through the window. Somewhere below, Sera was arguing cheerfully with the night clerk about the return ledger's new filing system.

"The boy in the story," he said at last. "The one whose childhood was sold. What happened to him? After."

I closed the book. "He's sitting in that chair," I said.

He looked down at himself. Looked at his hands—still, the way they went when a story mattered. Then he looked at me, and there was something in his face that wasn't blank at all, something being written even as I watched, letter by letter, morning by morning.

"Then he did all right," Dominic Quintana said. "Considering."

Outside, far off, the mountain began to sing. And he tilted his head, listening, already halfway to the melody, already humming a song he didn't know he knew—and I reached for the ledger, because I had one more entry to write, and this one, for the first time, was not about what he'd lost.

It was about what he'd found.
