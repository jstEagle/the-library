## 22. Choir and Salt

The tide comes in twice a day, and twice a day it leaves a line of salt on the harbor stones like a signature on a letter no one sent. Dominic goes down to read it most mornings. He doesn't know why. That's the shape his life has taken: he does things without knowing why, and then he writes them down.

*Went to the water. The water was there.*

That's the first entry in the new ledger, dated almost a year ago, in handwriting that was still learning itself. His old hand had been cramped and quick, the hand of a man who wrote other people's sins down for a living—Wren told him that, showed him pages from the Saltwork Guild's books before she burned them. He doesn't remember the old hand. The new one is slower, rounder, like someone who has decided that whatever he writes might be worth reading again.

He keeps two ledgers now. The first is the guild's—the guild Sera rebuilt—and it is boring in the way only honest books can be boring. Tons of salt. Barrels of pitch. Wages paid on time. He balances it every evening by candlelight, out of what he suspects is habit older than his memory, and finds, to his mild surprise, that he is good at it. Numbers sit still. Numbers don't ask him where he was born or who sang to him when he was seven or why he sometimes stops in the middle of the market with his throat tight for no reason at all.

The second ledger is his own. It has no columns. It has entries like:

*Mrs. Adeye left bread on our step again. I don't know how she knows when we're low.*

*A boy came into the office looking for his sister's memory. Sera didn't charge him. I wrote down the look on his face when she gave the bottle back.*

*Wren laughed at supper. Not at anything. Just laughed.*

That last one appears more often than any other entry. He has stopped being embarrassed about it.

This morning the tide line is silver, and there is a gull standing on the breakwater with the aggrieved dignity of a tax collector, and Dominic stands in the cold wind with salt drying on his lips and thinks: *this*. This is a thing I have. No one gave it to me. No one sold it, bought it, bottled it, filed it, or forged an entry to move it. It's mine because I'm standing here having it, and when it's gone it will be gone, and somehow that makes it worth more than everything in the vault ever was.

He doesn't remember the vault. But he knows about it the way you know about a country you've never visited—from maps drawn by other people, from Wren's stories told late and slow, from Sera's careful corrections when the stories contradict each other. He knows he did something once. Everyone in the harbor town treats him like a man who did something once. They are kind to him in a way that has weight to it, the way you're kind to a soldier back from a war nobody talks about. Mrs. Adeye's bread. The ferryman never takes his coin. Children stop their games to watch him pass, not with fear but with something close to reverence, which unsettles him more than fear would.

"You're going up the mountain today," Wren says when he gets back to the house. She says it while mending a net, not looking up, in the tone she uses for things that are already decided.

"Am I."

"It's the ninth." She ties off a knot. "You always go on the ninth."

He checks his ledger. She's right. There's a year of nines now, each one noted: *Went up the mountain. Listened. Came home.* It is, he reflects, the closest thing he has to a liturgy, and like all good liturgy he performs it without needing to know what the words mean.

"I'll bring bread," he says.

"Bring the big loaf. Kestrel eats like a starving gull lately."

Kestrel is one of them—one of the freed souls who chose to stay near the mountain after the world shook and the court beneath it came down. There are forty-some of them living on the mountain's lower slopes now, in cottages they built themselves out of rubble and stubbornness. They don't age quite right. They eat too much or too little. Some of them cry at nothing; some laugh at less. They are people who were kept in bottles for years, decades, longer, and who woke one day inside their own bodies with everything they'd lost poured back in at once. Sera calls them the Returned. They call themselves the choir, though most of them, Kestrel included, have terrible singing voices.

The real choir isn't theirs.

* * *

The road up Mount Verity is paved with old salt-miners' stone, and halfway up, where the switchbacks begin, the air changes. Dominic notices it every time and forgets between visits: a thinness, a ringing quality, like the silence inside a struck bell. Below him the harbor shrinks to a toy. Above him the mountain's broken crown still shows the scar—a wound in the rock where, a year ago, something vast opened its throat and sang itself apart.

He passes the first cottage at the tree line. An old woman is beating a rug, and as he passes she pauses, and they nod to each other, and neither of them speaks, because there is nothing to say that hasn't been said every ninth day for a year. She was in the vault. So was he, in a way. They have that in common, along with everyone else on this mountain: none of them know exactly what they are owed, but they all know they've been paid.

Higher up, the humming starts.

It's not a sound, exactly. He's tried to catch it the way you catch ordinary sounds—by stopping, by listening hard—but it slips through attention like water through fingers. You hear it best when you're not trying. Walking helps. Breathing helps. The humming lives somewhere below thought, in the body, in the bones of the ear, and it rises and falls in long patient phrases that never quite resolve.

The stone remembers, Wren told him once. Even emptied, even broken, the mountain still sings back everything that was ever given to it. And since the day the whole court's memory came pouring out—into the air, into the soil, into the sea fog that rolls up the slopes at dusk—the mountain has had a great deal to sing about.

Dominic reaches the flat shelf of rock below the scar and sits, and unwraps the bread, and waits.

They come gradually, the way weather comes. Kestrel first, young-seeming, sharp-faced, carrying a bundle of her own. Then the old woman with the rug, rug abandoned, hands folded. Then others, a dozen, two dozen, settling onto the stone in the loose half-circle they always form, facing the scar. Nobody organizes this. Nobody leads it. The first time Dominic came up here, a year ago, three days after waking in the rubble remembering nothing—not his name, not his mother's face, not the taste of his own life—he had followed the humming like a child follows smoke, and found these people sitting in the sun with tears running down their faces, listening to the mountain sing back the dead.

He sits with them now. He breaks bread and passes it around. Kestrel takes two pieces, as predicted.

And then the choir begins.

It starts, as it always does, with the mountain—a swell in the humming, a gathering, and then voices inside it, hundreds of them, thousands, layered so deep that individual singers surface and sink like fish in dark water. Freed souls, Wren explained, souls released into the stone itself when the shelves came down, singing themselves back into the world a phrase at a time. Some of the voices are speaking languages no one alive knows. Some are children. One, very faint and very high, sounds—everyone agrees, though no one will say so aloud—like a woman singing something that might be a lullaby, if lullabies were sung to mountains instead of babies.

Dominic listens. The bread goes around. The wind moves the grass on the slopes.

And somewhere in the middle of it, the way it happens every time, he starts to hum.

He doesn't decide to. His chest simply fills and joins, finding a line under the great braided mass of sound, a low steady thread that fits so exactly into the gaps between the other voices that the whole choir seems to lean toward him. The Returned notice. They always notice. Kestrel turns and looks at him with an expression he can't read, an expression that is equal parts grief and celebration, and he hums on, not knowing the song, not knowing where he learned it, not knowing why his eyes are wet, until the phrases gather and rise and resolve at last—for the only time all day—into something like an ending.

Silence. Wind. Gulls, absurdly far below.

"Do you feel it today?" Kestrel asks quietly. It's her question every time. *Do you feel it?* Meaning: do you remember? Meaning: is there anything of you coming back?

"No," Dominic says, as he always says. And then, because a year of ledger entries has taught him that small additions matter: "But I felt something. I don't know its name yet."

Kestrel smiles, and it's a wrecked and beautiful smile, and she says, "Write it down anyway."

* * *

He walks down the mountain in the gold end of the afternoon and finds Wren waiting at the foot of the road, arms crossed, pretending she wasn't watching for him. She has gotten better at pretending many things this year. She runs the guild's eastern books now, and she is ruthless with the arithmetic, and once a month she reads him entries from a different ledger entirely—an old one, salvaged from the ruin, written in a cramped and hurried hand.

She asked him, early on, whether he wanted to know. Everything. Who he was. What he did. What it cost.

He had thought about it a long time. Then he said: tell me the parts that matter. Tell me who stayed.

So she tells him about a courier who died pressing a map into a stranger's hands. She tells him about a thief who could have saved himself a hundred times and didn't. She tells him about a choice made in the deep dark under the mountain, when he held everything in his hands—every stolen year, every bottled soul, his own whole childhood sealed like a letter—and gave it all away rather than keep one scrap of it for himself. She tells it plainly, without varnish, the way you tell someone else's story, because that's what it is: a story about a man she loved who no longer exists, whom she has decided to love anyway in his successor.

Sometimes he wonders if that's cruel of her, keeping him tethered to a ghost. Mostly he thinks it's the opposite. She is doing for him what the mountain does for the souls inside it: singing him back, a phrase at a time, so that whatever he becomes will have somewhere to stand.

Tonight they walk home along the tideline, and the salt line is fresh, and the light does something generous to the water.

"Sera wants us both at the office tomorrow," Wren says. "There's a ship in from the capital. Someone's asking about the Guildmaster's estate."

"Odile." He knows the name the way he knows the names in his ledger—flat information, no weight attached. The woman who ran the guild before Sera. The woman who, according to the story, sold everything, including him.

"She's dead," Wren says. "Has been for a year. But contracts live longer than their signers, and this one's got clauses nobody's found yet. Sera thinks—" She stops herself, which she rarely does, and looks at him sideways. "Sera thinks some of what Odile sold wasn't in the vault. Some of it went further down than the mountain. And somebody on that ship just paid harbor fees in old coin. Court coin."

The word *court* lands in him strangely. Not fear, exactly. A resonance, like a finger on a wet glass rim.

"Should I be worried?" he asks.

"You should write it down," Wren says. "You always say that. Whatever happens, write it down. So we'll know."

* * *

That night he sits at the kitchen table with the candle and the second ledger open to a clean page, and he thinks about what deserves recording. The bread on Mrs. Adeye's step. The boy and his sister's bottle. The choir, and the nameless feeling, and Kestrel's ruined smile. The way Wren waited at the foot of the road pretending not to. Salt on his lips all day, in his hair, in his food, in everything—the whole town tastes of it, the whole life.

In the end he writes:

*Ninth of the month. Went up the mountain. Sang without knowing the song. Felt something whose name I don't have yet. Came home. Home is a place I know the way I know my own handwriting: slowly, and more every day.*

He hesitates. Then adds:

*Something is coming. Old coin, old contracts. If I forget the next part of this, whoever reads this: the song on the mountain is mine. I don't know how I know that. But when I hummed today, the whole choir leaned in to listen.*

He sets down the pen. The candle gutters. Out past the harbor the mountain stands black against the stars, and he can hear—or believes he can hear, which for a man with no past amounts to the same thing—the humming rising off its slopes, patient, immense, keeping every note it was ever given.

Somewhere down in the dark water of him, something turns over.

He doesn't know why he's crying. He writes that down too.
