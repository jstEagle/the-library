## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The mountain had a way of telling you when it didn't want you there. Most people never learned to listen. Oscar Fairweather had made a career of it.

He crouched at the mouth of the Hollow Stair, where Thornfell's western face folded in on itself like a closed fist, and he listened. Wind through the scree, high and thin. Water somewhere below, moving with purpose. The old stones settling, the way they always did after dark, groaning like an old man rolling over in bed. Nothing else. Nothing wrong.

"Clear," he said, and moved.

Behind him came the soft, disciplined scramble of six smugglers who were very good at their jobs and knew it, which was both the strength and the weakness of Maren Duskwell's crew. They carried lanterns hooded down to embers and crates that clinked in a manner crates shouldn't. Choir sacramental wine, if you believed the manifest, which nobody did. Oscar hadn't asked what was actually in them. Asking questions was how guides ended up in unmarked graves, and besides, he'd guided for Duskwell long enough to know the answer would either be boring or dangerous, and either way it would be above his pay.

Three years running these paths. Long enough that his feet knew the route better than his eyes did. Long enough that the mountain felt less like a workplace and more like a bad marriage he kept coming back to because at least it was honest about wanting to kill him.

He took them down the Stair two at a time, one hand trailing the rock, counting turns out of habit. Eleven. Twelve. At thirteen the passage narrowed and the ceiling came down low enough that Brannick, the big one, had to turn sideways with his crate hugged to his chest like a fat ugly baby.

"How much farther to the crossing?" one of the new ones whispered. Kellin. Three runs in. Still whispered.

"The shrine," Oscar said. "Then the crossing."

"There's really a shrine down here? Who worships *under* a mountain?"

"Someone," Oscar said, "who wanted to be left alone."

That shut the boy up. Good. Talking in the deep passages wasted breath and drew echoes, and echoes in Thornfell had a way of traveling farther than sound had any right to go. Oscar had once heard a rockfall from three valleys away, arriving in his ears a full minute before the dust did. The mountain played loose with its own rules. You respected that or you got buried.

They came down into the shrine chamber forty minutes later, and Oscar stopped so fast that Kellin walked into his back.

The ceiling had come down.

Not all of it — not recently, either, by the look of the rubble's edges, already rounded and silted grey. But the far half of the chamber, the half where the altar should have been, where on every previous crossing he'd seen that worn basin and the shallow carved channels that had once — Ysolde-the-heretic would have known, but he wasn't thinking about her, he'd never heard of her — carried offerings down to something below. All of that lay under a fan of collapsed stone and packed earth, maybe twenty years' worth of it, maybe fifty.

And water. A new spring had found its way through the fracture, threading between the fallen slabs, pooling black and patient in the low places before spilling off toward the underground river. It had been eating the shrine alive for years. Tonight it had finished a meal.

Oscar held up a fist. The crew stilled.

"We cross along the wall," he said quietly. "Nobody touches the rubble. Nobody drinks the water."

"What's wrong with the water?" Kellin asked.

"It's been sitting in a church."

Brannick snorted. The others grinned behind their hooded lamps, and just like that the fear drained out of them, replaced by the comfortable cynicism of professionals in familiar territory. Oscar let them have it. Fear was contagious; better they caught his calm instead.

He picked his way along the eastern wall, testing each foothold, and the crew followed in his prints like ducklings. It was only at the rubble's edge — where the floodwater had cut a fresh little channel through the debris, scouring it down to older layers — that he saw the corner of the book.

It was wedged under a slab no bigger than a paving stone, tilted up by the water's work, and what showed was leather. Black leather, gone green-black with wet and age, but leather, and stitched at the spine in a pattern he recognized without knowing from where: triple cord, knotted at intervals. Hymnal binding. He knew that the way he knew the routes — somewhere below thought, in the place where the oath lived.

He should have left it. Every instinct honed by three years of guiding said leave it: unknown objects in collapsed shrines were somebody else's problem, probably a dead man's problem, possibly a curse's problem. He had crates to move and a crossing to make and a schedule that Maren enforced with a ledger and a look.

But the oath pulled.

It always pulled, near shrines. Near any old holy thing. Like a dog straining at a leash toward a house where it used to live. Oscar had sworn himself to Veyl, god of the silence between words, at nineteen, in a rite that bound his life to the god's service, and for eleven years the god had answered him — not in words, Veyl didn't do words, but in that vast attentive quiet, the feeling of being *heard*. Then, seven years ago, on no particular night, with no particular warning, the silence had changed. It had stopped listening. It had become merely empty.

He still performed the rites. Morning oblation, evening vigil, the small observances of a sworn man. He did them the way other men shaved: regularly, without thinking, without expecting the razor to mean anything.

He knelt now beside the slab. Behind him, Brannick muttered something and Kellin laughed, nervous. Oscar ignored them. He worked his fingers under the wet leather edge and pulled.

The book came free with a sucking sound and a smell like a drowned church. It was heavier than it looked, waterlogged past saving, pages fused into a solid brick in places. But the cover had protected the first gathering, and when he worked it open with a thumbnail — gently, the way you'd open a dead man's hand — the inner pages were legible. Faded brown ink in a cramped liturgical hand. Prophecy, by the look of the marginal signs. Old calendar marks in the gutter. He turned pages with increasing carelessness, because it was gibberish to him, archaic High Cant with abbreviations he couldn't parse, and he was already composing the excuse he'd give Maren for the delay—

His eye snagged on plain script. Buried in the middle of a page of dense prophecy, set apart like a sentence someone had underlined twice:

*On this night shall the Sigil-Bearer stand at the threshold, and the ink shall know him by name.*

Below it, a date.

Oscar read the date. Read it again. His mind did what minds do — reached for the easy outs. Different calendar. Scribal error. A name that merely resembled—

*Oscar Fairweather.*

His full name. His mother's spelling of it, the one he hadn't used since she died, the one not even Maren's ledgers had ever gotten right. In a book that smelled of a century's drowning, in ink that flaked brown and ancient under his thumb, in a hand that had gone to dust before his grandfather drew breath.

And beneath the name, tonight's date. Not similar. Not close. The day, the month, the year. The year.

The chamber went very quiet. The water still ran; the crew still shifted behind him, someone's lamp clicking, Brannick's breathing. But around Oscar, around the book, the silence took on a quality he hadn't felt in seven years.

Attentive.

"Fairweather." Maren's voice, from the rear. She'd come up the line to find out why her guide had stopped moving. "We're burning moonrise. What did you—"

She saw his face. Whatever she saw in it, she went still, and Maren Duskwell going still was worse than most people shouting.

"Boss," Brannick said, uncertain.

"Pick up the crates," Maren said, without taking her eyes off Oscar. "All of you. Now. Move to the crossing and wait."

Grumbling, they went. Maren didn't. She crouched opposite him across the book, and in the ember-glow of his hooded lamp her face was all planes and shadows, older than her years.

"That's a shrine book," she said. "Century old, if it's a day."

"It is."

"You can read it?"

"I can read three lines of it." He heard his own voice and was distantly proud of how level it came out. Practiced calm. The professional mask. Underneath, something cold was spreading through his chest with the slow authority of floodwater finding a cellar. "One of them is my name."

Maren said nothing for a long moment. Somewhere above them, deep in the mountain's bones, something shifted — a settling, a groan, ordinary geology. Ordinary. He made himself believe it was ordinary.

"Put it back," she said finally, quietly. "Whatever it says, put it back and walk away. Some things are cheaper unread."

"The date's tonight, Mare."

Her jaw tightened. She'd sold him out once, three years ago, to buy the Choir's tolerance — he'd forgiven her the way you forgive weather, meaning he'd survived it and never let either of them forget it — and he watched her now weigh whether this was a debt situation or a liability situation. With Maren it was always one of the two.

"Tonight," she repeated.

"Tenth of Deepfrost. This year." He turned the book so the lamp caught the page. Her eyes tracked the faded letters, and he watched the exact moment she found the name — watched the blood leave her face in real time, this woman who once haggled with an Inquisitor and won.

"That's not possible," she said. "Ink fades. Books rot. They don't wait a hundred years to be right about Tuesday."

"No," Oscar agreed. "They don't."

*The ink shall know him by name.* He touched the page again and the flaking letters left a faint brown ghost on his fingertip, like ash, like dried blood, like something that had been waiting a long time to touch him back.

Sigil-Bearer. He didn't even know what it meant. Bearer of what sigil? The wards under Thornfell — every smuggler knew the stories, the war-god buried beneath the mountain, the old seals the Choir pretended were superstition while quietly forbidding anyone to dig — was that what it meant? Him? Him, who'd never carried anything heavier than contraband and an oath to a god who'd stopped speaking?

The cold spread further. And beneath the cold, worse, quieter, the thing he'd admit to no one: a flicker of something almost like recognition. Seven years of praying into a silence that never answered, seven years of performing faith to an empty room — and now a book told him the emptiness had scheduled an appointment. There was a horrible comfort in it. Even being a pawn meant someone was playing.

He closed the book. The sound was very loud.

"We're crossing," he said, standing, and his voice held, and he hated how much effort holding cost. "Tonight, same as planned. I'll take the book."

"Oscar—"

"It has my name in it, Mare. Leaving it in a flooded hole isn't walking away. It's just leaving it armed."

She looked at him a moment longer — calculating, guilty, afraid, all three at once, because Maren had never once in her life shown him only one thing at a time — then nodded sharply and turned toward the crossing.

Oscar followed, the drowned hymnal heavy against his ribs inside his coat, and behind them the black water kept threading patiently through the bones of the shrine, carrying what was left of a century down toward the river, toward the deep places, toward whatever slept under Thornfell and kept its own count of the days.

The date, he kept thinking. Tonight.

By the time they reached the crossing, he'd already decided where he was going next. Greyhollow had a library. Greyhollow had a library with walls, and walls had things living in them, and some of those things could read.

He just didn't know yet that the mountain was already shaking.
