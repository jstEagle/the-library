## 2. Sworn to Silence

Oscar Fairweather had a ritual for the hour before dawn, and he kept it the way other men kept knives: close, honed, and out of sight.

He rose before the rest of the camp—Dree and his boys still snoring in their bedrolls under the lean-to, the mules stamping frost off their fetlocks—and climbed the switchback behind the shrine ruins until he found a shelf of granite that faced east. Thornfell's shoulder cut the sky black against the last stars. He sat, crossed his legs, laid his palms open on his knees, and began the rites.

The Rite of Opening first. Then the Litany of the Held Breath. Then, hardest, the Vigil of the Ear—the one that required him to make of himself a hollow thing, an empty room, a doorway with no door.

He had done this every morning for eleven years. He would do it every morning until he died or until Veyl spoke again, whichever came first.

*Veyl,* he prayed, in the old tongue Ysolde would have wept to hear, though he didn't know that yet, *your servant listens.*

Silence answered.

Not the silence of absence—that was a different animal, and Oscar knew it well, having grown up in it, having been raised by it in the gutted mission towns along the Ash Road. This was silence with texture. Silence like static. Like standing in an empty room where someone once shouted, pressing your ear to a wall that remembered sound the way scars remember blades. Eleven years ago, when he'd sworn himself at the broken altar in Vaelmoor, the silence had been warm. Present. A held breath from something enormous on the other side.

Now it was just cold.

He finished the Vigil anyway. He always finished. That was the whole of the oath, in the end—not *hear me*, but *I will listen*. A god's servant didn't get to decide whether the god showed up. He got up. He knelt. He made himself hollow.

He was so busy being hollow that he almost missed the light below—a lantern moving through the shrine ruins, careful, low, wrong. Nobody in Dree's crew moved like that at this hour. Nobody moved like that except somebody looking for something they didn't want found.

Oscar came down the switchback without a sound, which was its own kind of prayer, and put his back against the fallen lintel of the collapsed shrine, the book—a dead man's book, a century-old book with his own name rotting in its margins—wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, against his heart, where he could feel it like a second heartbeat that wasn't his.

The lantern stopped.

"Fairweather." Maren Duskwell's voice, from three years away and none the better for the trip. "We need to talk."

"We don't," Oscar said, and drew his knife, because professionalism was just loneliness wearing armor, and he had worn it so long he'd forgotten there was anything underneath.

But that came later. First, the dawn rite had failed, as it always did, and Oscar Fairweather sat alone on a cold rock above a sleeping camp, being professional about it.

* * *

Eleven years earlier, in the ruined mission at Vaelmoor, Oscar Fairweather had sworn an oath with blood and breath and a name.

He was seventeen and had nothing else to give. The Ash Road fever had taken the found-family of scavengers who'd half-raised him—one grave a week for a season, until the graves ran out of room and the survivors ran out of grief—and Oscar had walked into Vaelmoor's gutted chapel to die of the same fever, or of anger, whichever arrived first. The fever never came. Instead, in the dark, in the dust of a shattered altar, he had felt something attend him.

Not arrive. Attend. As if it had been there the whole time, patient as stone, listening to a boy breathe.

*What do you want?* It hadn't been words, exactly. More like the shape words leave in a room after they're spoken.

"Nothing," Oscar had said, which was a lie, and the silence had held the lie gently, the way you hold something breakable, until he took it back. "A voice," he'd said. "Somebody to talk to who isn't going to die."

And the silence had said—or shaped, or breathed—*Then swear, and I will speak, and you will hear me all your life.*

So he'd sworn. Blood on the broken altar-stone, breath in the old liturgy he somehow already knew, the way you know a song you've never heard—because the liturgy wasn't learned, it was *remembered*, some deep structure in him answering some deep structure in the god—and finally his name, spoken aloud into the hollow dark: "Oscar Fairweather, sworn to Veyl, who keeps the silence that keeps the world."

The warmth had wrapped around him like a cloak. And Veyl had spoken—not words, but presence, attention, a vast and gentle *noticing* that made the boy weep without shame for the first time since the fevers came.

For two years, Oscar Fairweather was never alone. He argued theology with a god while walking fence lines. He asked questions at campfires and felt answers settle over him like snowfall. When he guided smugglers through Thornfell's passes, Veyl went with him, a pressure at his shoulder, a warmth in the hollow places. Other sworn servants existed—he'd met two, ever, both old, both quiet—but Oscar was young enough to believe the warmth would last forever.

It ended nine years later on an ordinary Tuesday, between breakfast and the saddling of horses.

He'd felt it go the way you feel a house go quiet when the last occupant leaves—not a sound ceasing but a *presence* ceasing, the specific quality of being attended to, gone. He'd dropped his porridge and run to the shrine at Harrow's Ford and performed the Rite of Opening with shaking hands, and gotten nothing. Nothing the next day. Nothing the day after. Nine years of nothing now, and counting, and every dawn rite a knock on the door of an empty house.

The other servants had died within the year—both of them, weeks apart, peacefully, in their sleep, as if released. Oscar had gone to their funerals and stood in the back, the last sworn servant of a god that had stopped speaking, and had not known whether he was widower or orphan or simply a man who'd been left.

He told people he served Veyl out of tradition. Out of professionalism. Out of the practical advantages of a god's mark on a smuggling résumé. He dressed the loneliness in competence and nobody ever checked underneath, because nobody ever checked underneath Oscar Fairweather for anything.

That was the arrangement. That was the deal. He listened every morning; the silence stayed silent; and somewhere beneath the armor, a seventeen-year-old boy waited for a voice that had promised, and lied, or died, or both.

* * *

Back in the present, the book was the problem.

He'd tried to burn it the night before. Truly tried—dry kindling, good flame, the oilcloth unwrapped, the water-warped pages fanned open over the fire. His name had stared up at him from the margin of the page dated tonight, in ink a century old, and he had watched the flames take the corners of the book and curl them black.

The fire had guttered. Not spread. Not died—*guttered*, bent sideways as if inhaling, and then the flames had simply declined, politely, the way a door declines to open, and the center of the book had remained damp and cool and entirely unburnt, and Oscar had sat back on his heels in the cold with his hands burning with shame at nothing.

You couldn't burn prophecy. Fine. He was a professional. You worked with what the mountain gave you.

So instead he did what he always did with problems too large to kill outright: he broke them into pieces small enough to carry. Piece one: the book was real, and old, and named him. Piece two: tonight's date was real, and arriving, and named him too. Piece three: whatever the book wanted from him, it would happen in Thornfell, on smuggler routes he knew better than anyone living, which meant—if nothing else—he would be the one holding the map when it happened.

Piece four: there were perhaps six people in the borderlands who could read the script in that book, and five of them were dead, and the sixth was a goblin archivist in Greyhollow who owed him nothing and charged accordingly.

He needed the book read. Which meant he needed Greyhollow. Which meant he needed a road out of Thornfell that the Choir's patrols weren't watching, because Inquisitors had been asking his name in three towns this season, and a man whose name appeared in century-old prophecy should probably avoid the attention of people who burned books.

Which meant, gods help him, Maren Duskwell.

He unpacked the thought the way he'd unpacked the book—carefully, at arm's length, ready to drop it. Three years since she'd sold his route to the Choir's advance riders for what he'd assumed was profit. Three years since he'd spent a wet week in a Choir cell learning exactly how much of his body could be hurt without ending it, before Maren's money had bought what her loyalty wouldn't. He still had the scar where they'd tried to take the oath-mark off his wrist, cutting around the sigil in neat surgical arcs, and the sigil had simply refused to bleed away, and the interrogator had eventually laughed and said, *Well. Your god may be dead, but he's stubborn.*

Oscar had not corrected him. But he had thought about it every day since: *my god is not dead.* Because the alternative was worse.

Now he needed her. There were roads only the clans held, tolls only Duskwell coin could pay, and the date on the page was coming toward him at the speed of days.

He looked at the book, wrapped again in oilcloth. He looked at the east, where Thornfell's mass hid whatever slept beneath it. And he did the thing he'd done every morning for nine years: he knelt on cold stone and performed the Vigil of the Ear, making himself hollow, making himself a doorway with no door.

*Veyl. Your servant listens. Whatever you are doing in there—wherever you went—I'm still here. I'm listening. Say something.*

The static silence pressed back, ancient and indifferent, or attentive and unable, and Oscar Fairweather could not tell the difference, and had never been able to tell the difference, and that—more than the fear, more than the name in the book, more than the date approaching like weather—was the loneliest truth of his life:

He could not tell whether he served a god who had abandoned him or a god who was drowning.

* * *

He was still kneeling when the tremor came.

Small. Subtle. The kind of shiver men in the borderlands ignored the way sailors ignore weather—Thornfell grumbled; Thornfell always grumbled; the mountain had been settling for a thousand years. Dust sifted from the shrine's collapsed vault. Somewhere below, a mule complained.

But the silence—the static silence Oscar had lived inside for nine years—*moved.*

Not spoke. Not stirred. Moved, the way deep water moves when something vast turns over far below. For one instant the hollow space inside Oscar's vigil was full, the way a room is full when someone walks in behind you, and the pressure of attention was so old and so heavy and so *straining* that Oscar gasped and lost the rite and came back to his body on his knees on frozen granite with tears freezing on his face and no memory of crying.

Then it was gone. Static. Cold. Empty as nine years of empty.

He knelt there a long time, breathing.

Professional assessment: tremors in Thornfell were common. Attention in the silence might have been wishfulness, exhaustion, the book's proximity playing tricks on a lonely man's hollowed-out senses. The rational move—the professional move—was to saddle up, run the route, collect Dree's fee, and get to Greyhollow to have the damned book read before tonight's date could mean whatever it meant.

Personal assessment, filed separately, sealed, buried: *something heard me. Something down there heard me, and it was glad, and it was afraid, and neither of those things was Veyl.*

He stood. He brushed the frost from his knees. He went down to wake the camp and be competent at it, because that was what Oscar Fairweather did with everything that could unmake him: he folded it small, packed it deep, and carried it uphill.

Below the shrine, unaware, the world went about its morning. Dree's boys cursed the cold and the mules. Frost smoked off the river. Far to the south, in a town called Greyhollow, Inquisitors were asking a merchant pointed questions about a mercenary named Fairweather, and buying rope.

And beneath Thornfell, in the deep places where even silence feared to go, the thing that was not Veyl turned over again in its sleep, and smiled at the taste of a name:

*Oscar Fairweather.*
