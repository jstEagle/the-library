## 3. The Choir Comes Singing

The fire came first, as it always did. Before the hymns, before the banners, before the white-and-gold procession wound down from the mountain road into Greyhollow like a length of pulled thread—there was fire.

Seraphine Lark watched the pyre take the books and thought, as she had thought at every burning for eleven years, that heresy smelled sweeter than scripture. Old paper, old lies, old ink going up in a column of greasy black smoke that stained the morning sky above the border town like a bruise.

*That is not a holy thought,* she told herself, and folded it away the way she folded all such thoughts, into the small dark room behind her ribs where doubt was kept on a very short leash.

"Inquisitor." Brother Callum, her junior, stood at her shoulder with his ledger open and his voice pitched for reverence. He was twenty-four, earnest, and had never once in his life written down an impure thought, which Seraphine considered either sainthood or a failure of imagination. "The town records are ready for inspection."

"Then let us inspect them," she said.

Greyhollow was the last place light touched before the mountains swallowed it. A customs post, a garrison of forty men who hadn't been paid on time in two years, three taverns, one shrine to the Radiant Choir with a roof that leaked, and—according to the manifest Seraphine had read four times on the road—a population that swelled every spring with smugglers, prospectors, and the kind of pilgrims who went into Thornfell seeking gods better left unlit.

It was, in other words, exactly the sort of soil in which heresy put down roots.

The pyre crackled. Townsfolk watched from doorways with the flat, careful expressions of people who had learned that watching was safest done without expression at all. Somewhere behind her, the choir began the *Canticle of Unveiling*, forty voices rising in the sweet, merciless harmony that the Choir had carried across half the world, and Seraphine saw a woman by the well flinch at the sound—a small thing, a twitch of the shoulders, gone in a heartbeat.

Seraphine catalogued the flinch. She filed it beside the sweet-smelling smoke and the folded-away thought, and she led her retinue toward the town archive.

* * *

The Greyhollow archive occupied the basement of the customhouse, which meant it was damp, badly lit, and organized according to a system that could only be described as spiteful. The town clerk, a stooped man named Hedwin with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who had survived decades of inspections by being aggressively forgettable, led them down the stairs with a lantern.

"We keep everything," he said. "Trade ledgers, toll receipts, bounty postings, grievance filings. Thirty years back, all of it."

"You'll burn nothing today," Seraphine said. "We're looking for a name, not a doctrine."

Hedwin's lantern hand did not tremble, which Seraphine noted approvingly. Frightened clerks dropped lamps on archives, and archives were where truth lived, however muddled.

"A name, Inquisitor?"

She set the warrant on the table between them. The Choir's seal gleamed: a sunburst pierced by seven ascending notes, rendered in wax the color of old blood.

"Fairweather," she said. "Oscar Fairweather. Mercenary. Sworn, we believe, to the silent god."

Something moved behind Hedwin's forgettable eyes. It was there and then gone, quick as the woman's flinch at the well, but Seraphine had spent eleven years training herself to catch such movements, the way a fowler catches birds—with patience, stillness, and an eye for the flicker of wings.

"He guides caravans through Thornfell," Hedwin said carefully. "Smuggler roads. If he exists, he'd be known to the guild brokers, not to me."

"If he exists," Seraphine repeated. "You know the penalty for shielding a sworn servant of a dead god, Master Hedwin."

"I know the penalty for lying to the Choir, Inquisitor." Hedwin set the lantern down with great deliberation. "I've seen it applied. I'm an old man who keeps receipts. I don't shield anyone. There's no profit in it."

There it was again—that flicker. Not fear. Something closer to respect, or its opposite. Seraphine let it pass. She opened the ledger he offered and began to read.

* * *

Three hours later, she had a picture assembled out of toll receipts, bounty postings, and the marginalia of guild brokers, and the picture bothered her.

Oscar Fairweather had guided eleven documented caravans through Thornfell in the past two years alone. He was, per every record she could find, competent to the point of tedium: no brawls, no debts, no abandoned clients, no dead travelers except the two lost in the White Sorrow avalanche four years back, which every other guide had also lost people to that season. He paid his guild dues early. He tipped shrine-keepers. One receipt, six years old, recorded a donation to the Greyhollow orphanage in the amount of "one month's wages, anonymous, see attached note declining recognition."

None of it matched.

The dossier on Fairweather that the Inquisition maintained—the dossier Seraphine had memorized on the road—described a killer. A man marked at nineteen by the rites of Veyl, oath-bound to a god that had not spoken in a hundred years, and therefore, according to doctrine, a man whose faith had curdled into something worse than heresy: superstition without even the honesty of worship. The dossier listed suspected contacts among smuggler clans. It listed, in a later hand, an incident three years past in which Fairweather had been briefly detained by Choir agents in this very town and released for insufficient evidence—an embarrassment the dossier noted with unusual bitterness.

And at the bottom, in the current Provost's own script, a single line:

*Subject is designated Sigil-Bearer. Termination authorized on confirmation of identity. Do not detain. Do not question. Do not allow him to reach Thornfell's heart.*

Do not question.

Seraphine had read that line perhaps fifty times since receiving her orders, and it had taken her until now—standing in a basement that smelled of mildew and honest bookkeeping, holding a receipt for an anonymous donation to orphans—to hear how strange it was.

The Choir questioned everyone. Questioning was the whole architecture of the faith: the *Interrogatories of Light*, seven questions asked of every soul from emperor to ditch-digger, designed to strip away self-deception until only truth remained. Her entire order was built on the premise that nothing was beyond examination—not scripture, not conscience, not kings. That was what separated the Radiant Choir from the ash-and-scream cults they burned.

Except here was a man they were ordered not to question. Ordered to kill on sight, on confirmation, without trial, without interrogation, without the seven questions.

"Why?" she asked the empty basement.

Hedwin, pretending to reorganize a shelf across the room, did not answer. He was wise that way.

Seraphine sat down slowly on the clerk's stool and pulled the dossier toward her, and for the first time in eleven years of service, she read a document the way heretics read scripture: looking for what had been removed.

* * *

She found the excision within the hour. It was beautifully done—a page replaced so skillfully that only the stitching betrayed it, thread a shade too pale against the leather spine. Whatever had been written about the sigil itself, about why the bearer mattered, had been cut out and rewritten somewhere between Greyhollow and the Sunspire, and Seraphine knew in her bones that the rewriting had happened high up. Higher than her. Possibly higher than the Provost himself.

She should have reported it immediately. Procedure demanded it; doctrine demanded it; eleven years of her own folded-away doubts demanded it, because those doubts had always ended the same way—*the Choir is right, and I am small, and the light does not need my understanding to be light.*

Instead she sat in the mildewed dark with her thumb resting on the crooked stitch, and she thought about smoke.

Her mentor, High Cantor Malvane, had once told her—Seraphine had been newly sworn, sixteen, luminous with certainty—"Doubt is not the enemy of faith, child. Doubt is the whetstone. A blade that never doubts its edge has never been tested." Malvane had said it kindly. Malvane always said terrible things kindly.

So test the edge, Seraphine thought. That was all this was. Whetting. She would go up the mountain to the Sunspire, she would present her findings, she would ask—one question, a small one, professional in every particular—why the Church's greatest living heresy-hunter was forbidden to ask questions. And the light would answer, because the light always answered, because—

Because a woman by a well had flinched at a hymn. Because a clerk who feared nothing else had flickered at the word *shielding*. Because the sweetest-smelling fires she had ever attended had all been lit over books, and she had stopped noticing the smell around year six.

"Inquisitor?" Callum stood at the foot of the stairs, silhouetted against the ordinary daylight. "The garrison captain reports a caravan came off the mountain this morning. Smuggler goods, mostly contraband relics. And—" he consulted his slate, "—one item of interest. A recovered text from a collapsed shrine. Old. The broker's holding it for us."

Something turned over in Seraphine's chest, slow and cold, like ice shifting in a spring lake.

"Old how?" she asked.

"Century, maybe. Why?"

Because the dossier's cut page was old stitching. Because the Provost's order had been written in ink that matched documents twice her age. Because her whole life she had been taught that heresy was a fire you lit from outside, and no one had ever told her that sometimes the fire was already burning at the center, and had been for a long time.

"Bring it to me unopened," Seraphine said. She rose, tucked the dossier away, and pressed her thumb once more against the crooked stitch beneath the leather—her small private proof that the world contained seams.

"And Callum." She climbed the stairs toward the light, toward the hymns, toward the pyre that would be lit again at dusk. "Find out everything the garrison knows about a mercenary named Fairweather. Quietly."

"Are we interrogating, Inquisitor?"

Seven questions, she thought. Seven questions she was permitted to ask anyone in the world except the one man the Church wanted dead.

"No," Seraphine Lark said. "We're asking."
