## 4. Old Debts, New Roads

The thing about selling a man to the Inquisition, Oscar had learned, was that it didn't kill him. It just made everything after harder.

He came down out of Thornfell's lower passes on the third morning with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest and his oath-mark itching under his glove, and by the time Greyhollow's crooked rooftops appeared through the pine haze he had already rehearsed every version of the conversation ahead of him. In most of them he was civil. In one or two he shouted. In the best one, she apologized first.

He knew better than to bet on the best one.

Maren Duskwell ran her operation out of a tannery that hadn't tanned anything in twenty years, hard against the town wall where the watch didn't bother looking and the smell covered every other sin. Two of her people flanked the door — a Kellish bruiser with hands like ham hocks and a wiry kid with a crossbow he was holding wrong. Oscar let them see his palms open and empty.

"Tell her Fairweather's here."

The bruiser squinted at him. "She said if a man with a Veyl-mark comes asking, we're to say she's not home."

"That so."

"She also said," the bruiser continued, with the pained look of a man reciting someone else's poetry, "if you pushed the matter, I'm to tell you the back room's got a window, and you're welcome to climb through it and die on her floor instead of her step."

Oscar considered that. Three years ago it would have made him laugh. "She's feeling generous."

"She's feeling something," the bruiser said, and stood aside.

* * *

The back room of the dead tannery was dim and crowded with the wreckage of a hundred jobs: coil after coil of rope, crates stenciled with lies, a map table buried under charts of three different mountain ranges. Maren Duskwell sat at the far end of it with her boots up and a knife she was ostensibly cleaning, and she did not look up when he came through the window, which meant she'd been looking at the door for an hour.

"You're holding the knife backwards," Oscar said.

"I know what I'm doing with it."

"Three years ago you'd have—"

"Don't." She set the knife down flat, deliberate as a card player folding a hand she didn't like, and finally looked at him. She'd put a new scar through one eyebrow since he'd last seen her, silver-white, and there were more lines around her mouth, but her eyes hadn't changed at all: dark, quick, always doing arithmetic with whatever was in front of them. Right now the arithmetic kept coming out to something she didn't want to pay. "Sit down, Oscar. You look like something the mountain chewed up and gave back."

"I feel flattered by the comparison." He didn't sit. He unwrapped the oilcloth instead, laid the prophecy book on the table between them, water-stained and swollen, its leather cover gone stiff as old bark. Maren's eyes went to it and stayed there, and the arithmetic behind her face changed speed.

"What is it?"

"A hundred-year-old book with my name in it and tonight's date. I need to be out of the mountains before tonight. Out of Choir territory entirely. You owe me a road."

"I don't owe you anything." But she said it the way people say things they've rehearsed until the words wore smooth. She reached out and turned the book toward herself without touching it, the way you'd approach a trap you suspected but couldn't prove. "Fairweather. What did you find up there?"

"Trouble with my name on it. Which brings me to you, since you've got experience putting trouble and my name together."

Maren leaned back. Her boots came off the table. When she spoke again, her voice had gone flat and businesslike, which was how Oscar knew she was bleeding. "You want to rehash Greyhollow Quay."

"I want a way south through the Serrat mines, and I want it today, and I figure the woman who sold me to the Radiant Choir for enough coin to buy her first ship owes me at least that."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere out front, the bruiser dropped something heavy and swore about it.

"It wasn't the coin," Maren said finally.

"No?"

"No." She picked the knife back up. This time she was holding it right, and Oscar understood that was the point — that she wanted something in her hand while they did this. "The Choir came to me with your name and a choice. Give up the mercenary who'd been running their mountains blind for two years, or lose the whole route. Every contact. Every safe house. They'd already burned two of my crews, Oscar. I gave them you because the math said—"

"The math." He laughed, and it came out uglier than he intended. "You know what the funny part is? I'd have taken the fall for you if you'd asked. If you'd come to me and said, Oscar, the route's blown, I need a name to give them — I'd have given it. That's what partners do. Instead you drugged my wine and left a lantern burning in my window so the inquisitors could find the right door."

Something crossed her face then, fast, like a fish under ice. "You knew about the lantern."

"I've had three years to think about it. You don't leave many details out when you spend three years replaying a thing." He finally sat, across the table from her, and put his gloved hand flat over the prophecy book. "Here's the difference between us, Maren. You did the math and sold a man. I'm doing the math now, and the math says you're still the only person who can move cargo — any cargo — out of these mountains inside twelve hours. So I'm here anyway. That's all this is. Don't make it more than it is."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and he watched her decide — visibly, the way she decided everything, weighing and discarding — whether to keep fighting or start working.

"Twelve hours," she said. "Where am I taking you?"

"South. Anywhere south of the Choir's writ. Fast, quiet, no manifest, no passengers who talk."

"That's my whole trade description." She pulled a chart toward her, then stopped, her fingers resting on the edge of the prophecy book. "This cargo. Is it going to get my people killed?"

Honesty, he decided, was the only coin she'd accept from him now. "Probably not. Possibly. The Radiant Choir wants me dead, and they want it badly enough that they're asking questions in border towns."

"How badly?"

"They sent someone to burn a shrine full of heresy two valleys over. They're not sending a novice."

Maren blew out a breath. She looked at the book again, really looked, and Oscar saw her thumb move once across the water-warped cover, almost gentle. He remembered that about her — how she handled old things carefully and new people carelessly, and how nobody had ever explained to him which category he'd been in.

"One condition," she said. "You tell me what's in it. Not all of it. But if I'm running you past an inquisitorial checkpoint, I need to know if I'm smuggling a man or a war."

He thought about lying. It would have been easy; she'd earned his lies. But the date was tonight, and the road was long, and somewhere under his ribs the silence of Veyl pressed against him like weather.

"There's a god under Thornfell," he said. "Buried. Chained. The wards keeping it there run on a century of silence, and according to this book, my death tonight either seals those wards for another age or breaks them open. Depends on how I go. Believing or doubting." He shrugged, and it felt grotesque, shrugging with that sentence attached. "That's the cargo. A dead man's itinerary."

Maren didn't laugh. That was the thing about her he could never make himself hate: she never laughed at the impossible, only at the improbable. She sat very still, and then she started rolling up the chart.

"Serrat mines are watched since the Quay job," she said. "But there's a route under the old tollbridge road, down through the ashline. My cousin's boy runs goats that way. Nobody stops a goat train." She stood, and the chair scraped, and suddenly she was all motion, all business, the conversation sealed away behind her teeth like everything else. "You'll travel with the herd. You'll hate it. The goats will hate you worse."

"Maren."

"What."

"Why the lantern?"

Her hands stopped on the rolled chart. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer, and he was preparing to take his book and his doomed name back into the rain. Then she said, quietly, not turning around:

"So you'd see it coming. And so the Choir would think I'd cooperated fully. There's a difference, in their books, between a traitor and a coward, and only one of them gets hunted afterward." She turned, and her eyes were dry and furious. "I couldn't stop them taking you. I could make sure you weren't asleep for it. That's all I had. Take it or don't."

He took it. Not gracefully — nothing between them would ever be graceful — but he took it, because three years was long enough to learn that mercy arrived misshapen more often than not.

"The goat train," he said. "Fine."

"And Oscar?" She handed him the chart, and her fingers held on to it a half-second longer than the transaction required. "Whatever's in that book. Whatever the Choir thinks you're going to be. You were a good partner once. Best I ran routes with." A beat. "Don't die on my cargo. Bad for the reputation."

Outside, the rain was starting again, and the mountains above Greyhollow stood dark under it, and somewhere beneath all that stone something old was waiting on a date. Oscar folded the chart into his coat, next to the book, next to the silence.

Behind him, in the doorway of the tannery, Maren watched him go — and then, as he reached the corner, she called out, voice carrying sharp across the yard:

"Fairweather. One more thing."

He turned.

"The Choir isn't just asking questions in border towns." Her face had gone pale under the new scar. "My cousin's boy came down this morning. Says there's an inquisitor already in Greyhollow. A woman. Gray coat, silver voice. She's been at the library since dawn, asking about a book."
