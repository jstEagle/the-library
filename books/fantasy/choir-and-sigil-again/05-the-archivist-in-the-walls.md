## 5. The Archivist in the Walls

Pip had lived in Greyhollow's library longer than most of its librarians had been alive, which Pip considered a professional advantage and not, as the newest cataloguer had shrieked upon discovering a goblin eating dried plums in the map room at three in the morning, "a horrifying infestation."

The library was called the Athenaeum of Saint Vellum, which Pip found funny for reasons nobody else ever got. It was four floors of stone and dust and paper older than the town that had grown up around it like moss around a grave marker. Humans used the front doors. Pip used the gap behind the third-floor shelving where a rat had once gnawed through the plaster, because rats understood architecture better than masons did.

Pip's territory ran through the walls like veins: a network of crawlspaces and dead chimneys and one genuinely luxurious stretch of abandoned dumbwaiter shaft, lined with stolen quilts and lit by tallow stubs hoarded from the reading lamps. Every horizontal surface held books. Not stolen, exactly—*rescued*. Books the librarians had condemned to the pulping crate. Books water-damaged beyond lending but not beyond love. Books that smelled of mold and were therefore, by Pip's standards, perfect.

Which was why, when Oscar Fairweather had come to the service entrance two nights ago looking for someone who could read a dead script, Maren Duskwell—who owed Pip eleven favors and knew it—had known exactly where to go.

"He wouldn't say how he got it," Maren had said, pressing the wrapped bundle into Pip's hands in the alley behind the bindery. She'd kept her voice low and her eyes moving. That alone told Pip more than any explanation would have. Maren Duskwell was frightened of this thing.

"What is it?" Pip had asked.

"Old. Wet once. He says it's got his name in it."

Pip had looked up at her. Maren had that particular expression she wore when she was pretending not to care about something, which meant she cared about it enormously.

"And yours too," she'd added, almost as an afterthought. "Your name. In it."

Pip had carried the book home through the walls very carefully, the way one carries a sleeping animal or an unexploded shell.

* * *

Now, two days later, Pip sat cross-legged on the good quilt with the book open on a board across both knees, and understood why Maren had looked like that.

It was bad.

Not the content—well, also the content—but first the physical condition. The book had been soaked, dried badly, and soaked again sometime in its long ugly life. Whole gatherings had fused into brick. The vellum had cockled into waves. Ink had bloomed into gray ghosts of itself. Any ordinary reader would have opened it, seen gibberish, and set it down.

But Pip was not an ordinary reader. Pip was, by self-appointment and universal acclamation among a community of zero other goblin archivists, the finest conservator in the Greyhollow basin.

The script itself was the second problem, and the delicious one. It wasn't Old Imperial. It wasn't High Choiran. It wasn't even First Tongue, though it wore First Tongue like a coat over something underneath—the way a child traces letters over a hidden picture. Pip had seen this trick exactly twice before, both times in fragments the Choir had burned before proper study. A base text in a script so old it predated writing's manners, with a later hand traced over it, using the old letterforms as scaffolding for new words.

A palimpsest that thought it was clever.

"Whoever you were," Pip murmured, wetting a brush and touching it to the worst-stuck page, "you wanted to be read by someone specific and unreadable by everyone else."

The moisture relaxed the fused leaves by degrees. Pip worked with a bone folder and a breath held steady, teasing pages apart the way one teases apart frozen fingers. Hours went by. The tallow burned down and was replaced. Somewhere above, the day librarians stamped returns and shushed students and had no idea their building had a heartbeat in the walls.

By the time the light through the rat-hole shifted from gray to gold, Pip had the alphabet.

That was always the moment. Other people loved endings; Pip loved *decipherment*, the exact instant when noise became language, when the shapes stopped being shapes and started being *saying things*. It was better than plum wine. It was better than the look on a librarian's face when a "lost" codex reappeared on their desk, immaculately repaired, no note.

Then Pip read the first full line, and the pleasure curdled straight into something colder.

*In the year the mountain breathed, the Sigil-Bearer walks under Thornfell's shadow.*

Pip read it again. Then again, lips moving, because some sentences don't get absorbed until the mouth confirms them.

Prophecy. Fine. Libraries were full of prophecy—nine-tenths fraud, one-tenth fraud with footnotes. But this didn't read like the Choir's manufactured revelations, all trumpets and chosen peoples. This read like a ledger. Dry. Specific. Uncomfortably specific:

*A mercenary sworn to the silent god shall carry the mark of the ward upon his left wrist, and his name shall be Fairweather.*

Pip put the book down. Pip picked it up. Pip turned back three pages to check the water damage hadn't created the word *Fairweather* out of something innocent like *fairfields* or *farewells*.

No damage. No ambiguity. *Fairweather.*

"Okay," Pip said aloud, to the empty dumbwaiter shaft, in the reasonable tone one uses with a book that has begun making threats. "Okay. Coincidence. There are—statistically—dozens of Fairweathers. Mercenary is a common profession. Silent gods, there must be—" Pip stopped, because there weren't. There was exactly one silent god anyone still swore by, and everyone knew what happened to Veyl's faithful: the god went quiet a century ago and never came back, and the few oath-bearers who kept the rites anyway were regarded the way people regard men who talk to empty chairs.

And Oscar Fairweather had a scar on his left wrist. Pip had noticed it when he'd handed over the book—a pale ringed mark, old, deliberate. Pip noticed hands. Hands told you a person's whole trade. Pip had assumed knife work gone wrong.

Pip had assumed wrong.

* * *

Sleep was not on the schedule after that. Sleep was for people whose books stayed politely fictional.

Pip worked through the night by tallow-light, mapping the palimpsest layer by layer. The over-text—the newer tracing—was the prophecy proper: dates, names, conditions. The under-text was older and stranger, and it took Pip until well past midnight to realize the under-text wasn't a different document at all. It was the *same* document. An earlier draft. And the differences between draft and final were not scribal errors.

They were edits.

Here, in the draft: a passage about the Sigil-Bearer's death, three lines long, dense with qualifiers. In the final version, traced over it: the same passage compressed, the qualifiers stripped away, leaving only the bare mechanism—die believing, seal the god; die doubting, free it.

Someone had taken a prophecy with options in it and sanded the options off.

Pip sat very still for a long moment, brush hovering.

Editing prophecy was not unheard of. Institutions edited everything; that was half of what institutions were *for*. The Radiant Choir had a two-hundred-year track record of discovering that inconvenient verses were apocryphal, mistranslated, or regrettably flammable. If some Choir hand had gotten hold of this book and tidied it—

But no. The ink of the over-text was wrong for the Choir. Choiran scriptoria used iron-gall cut with violet salt; it bloomed purple as it aged, and every forged relic Pip had ever examined glowed faintly lavender under magnification. This ink was plain carbon, lampblack, cheap and local. Whoever had made these edits had done it quietly, privately, close to wherever this book lived—and had done it *carefully*, preserving the letterform scaffolding so the forgery would pass a casual glance.

This wasn't institutional censorship. This was personal. Someone had needed the prophecy to say only two things—seal or free, death either way—and had made it say them.

"Why?" Pip whispered. The question itched worse than any undeciphered glyph. "What did the original say?"

Pip went hunting in the under-layer.

The draft text was harder going—faded, water-eaten, the strokes thin—but decipherment had its own momentum now, the alphabet fully built, the grammar falling into place the way grammar does once you stop fighting it. Pip reconstructed the damaged passage stroke by stroke, cross-referencing against the final text where both survived, filling gaps from context the way a locksmith fills a lock: patiently, by feel.

Near dawn, the passage came clear.

*Three roads lie open to the Bearer: to die believing and be the lid; to die doubting and be the key; or—*

The next word had been scraped. Physically scraped, with a blade, down to the flesh of the vellum. But scraping never gets everything. Ink sinks into skin below the surface, and a hundred years of damp coaxes it back up like a bruise rising. Under the raking light of Pip's best candle, angled low across the page, ghost-strokes surfaced in the scarred patch.

Four letters. Then a hole where water had eaten clean through. Then two more letters.

Pip transcribed the ghosts onto scrap, stared at them, tried the obvious roots, rejected them, tried again. The first four letters fit a verb root Pip knew from exactly one other source: a fragment of pre-Choiran liturgy preserved, ironically, in a Choir polemic quoting it in order to mock it. A root meaning *to turn aside*. To decline. To say no.

The last two letters completed it into a form Pip had never seen conjugated anywhere, in any tongue, dead or living. A form that, if Pip's grammar was right—and Pip's grammar was right; Pip's grammar was the single most insufferable fact about Pip—wasn't future tense at all.

It was permissive. The tense of allowance.

*He may refuse the ink.*

Pip dropped the brush.

It rolled off the board, left a small black comet-tail across the quilt, and came to rest against the wall, and Pip did not pick it up for a full minute because Pip was busy experiencing several emotions simultaneously and needed both hands free to do it.

Because if the draft was right—if the original prophecy had contained a third road—then everything followed like dominoes. Someone had found the prophecy. Someone had realized it offered the Sigil-Bearer a way out. And someone had scraped that way out off the page and let the water finish the job, so that when the appointed bearer finally read his own name and his own date, he would see exactly two doors and a wall between them. Die right or die wrong. No exits.

Which raised the question Pip asked out loud, because the walls deserved to hear it:

"Who benefits from a man who thinks he has no choice?"

And then, immediately, the colder question behind it, arriving like a stone down a well:

"Where's the rest of the book?"

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing Pip had been circling all night without letting himself land on. Prophecies of this scale—the mountain, the buried god, the ward beneath Thornfell—did not get written once, in one copy, in one hand. Anything that mattered that much got copied. Got distributed. Got *hidden in pieces*, so that no single thief or fire or tyrant could own the whole truth. Pip had spent a decade cataloguing suppressed texts, and the pattern was iron: dangerous knowledge survives by being split.

Which meant somewhere there was a second volume. A matched pair. The other half of the ledger—the other half of the *edits*, maybe. Proof, in a second hand, that the third road had been real.

And where did one hide a book that powerful, a book about the Church's oldest secret, for a century, guaranteed safe from goblins, smugglers, and fire?

One gave it to the people whose entire institution was built on the edited version.

One gave it to the Choir.

* * *

Pip did not sleep. Pip paced instead, which in the walls meant walking the dumbwaiter shaft end to end, two hundred steps each way, tail lashing, muttering.

The mathematics of it were terrible and clean. If the Choir held the second book, the Choir had always known the prophecy could be refused. They had known for a hundred years, and they had spent those hundred years teaching the world the two-door version: believe and seal, doubt and free. Building doctrine on top of it. Building *offices* on top of it—an Inquisition with budgets and ranks and burnings—whose entire sacred function was making sure the Sigil-Bearer, whoever he turned out to be, reached his appointed date believing. Or failing that, simply reaching it. Dead either way. Doors closed, wall intact, institution eternal.

And now the bearer was a mercenary named Oscar Fairweather, currently hiding somewhere in Greyhollow with Maren Duskwell, holding half the evidence, with maybe days left before the date in the book caught up to the calendar.

Pip thought about Oscar's face when he'd handed over the bundle. Pip hadn't paid much attention at the time—hands, always hands—but memory supplied it now: a man trying to look bored and achieving instead the look of someone carrying a weight in his chest that he'd already checked twice for exits. Two doors and a wall. He'd read it. Of course he'd read it; Maren said it had his name in it, and no creature alive reads a book with their own name in it only halfway.

He thought he had no choice.

Pip stopped pacing.

Terrified exhilaration was not a comfortable combination. Terrified said: the Inquisition burns people for less than this, and I am a goblin in a wall with contraband literacy and a stolen tallow budget. Exhilaration said: *I am the only person in this valley, possibly in this country, who knows the wall has a door in it.* Both feelings agreed on exactly one point: doing nothing was not among the available actions.

There was, however, a problem, and the problem had red trim and a habit of asking questions at the service door. The Radiant Choir maintained a mission house in Greyhollow—small, but staffed. And two days ago, according to gossip piped down through the walls the way everything traveled through the walls, the mission house had received visitors from up-country. Inquisitors. Asking after travelers. Asking, the cook's cousin's boy reported, after a mercenary.

They were already here. Of course they were already here. The book had a date in it; the Choir had calendars too.

Pip sat down on the good quilt amid the wreckage of brushes and scrap transcriptions and the open book with its scraped-away mercy, and made Pip's kind of plan—not a soldier's plan, not a smuggler's plan, but an archivist's plan, which meant it began with evidence and ended with leverage.

Step one: finish the transcription. Every legible word, both layers, plus drawings of the ghost-strokes under raking light, dated and signed. If Pip died tomorrow, the proof had to survive in triplicate, hidden in three separate wall-caches that even Pip would need the map to find. Archivists think about dying tomorrow professionally.

Step two: get the transcription to Fairweather. Not the book—the book was bait now, and fragile, and besides, Pip had grown attached to it in the way one grows attached to anything one has resurrected. The transcription, with the reconstruction of the scraped passage marked plainly: *he may refuse the ink.* Let the man with two doors learn about the third one.

Step three—

Step three arrived on its own, uninvited, the way the worst and best steps do, because step three depended on a fact Pip had been holding at arm's length all night: the second book, the Choir's half, the corroborating witness. If the plan was going to be more than handing a doomed man a comforting footnote, somebody had to confirm the other volume existed, establish what it said, and ideally acquire it. Which meant infiltrating, sooner or later, an institution that burned goblin texts on principle and goblin persons, Pip suspected, on convenience.

Pip looked around the little lamplit kingdom in the walls—at the rescued books stacked like sandbags, the quilts, the plum jar, the rat-hole opening onto a world that had never once made room for Pip and never would unless Pip made the room personally.

"Well," Pip told the assembled archive, and the archive, being well-trained, said nothing, which Pip chose to take as agreement. "I always did want to read the restricted section."

Getting out of the library was easy; Pip had a dozen exits. Finding Oscar Fairweather before the Inquisition did—that was the trick of it. But Pip knew a woman who knew the man, and Pip knew a woman who owed eleven favors, and Pip knew, better than anyone alive, how to move unseen through the spaces between buildings.

Pip rolled the transcription into oilcloth, tucked it into the harness sewn between shoulder blades where no one ever looks at a goblin, pinched out the tallow, and stood at the mouth of the rat-hole, watching dawn come up gray over Greyhollow's rooftops.

Somewhere out there, a man believed he was standing in front of a wall.

Pip grinned, all teeth, and dropped down into the morning to tell him about the door.
