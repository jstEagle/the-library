## 6. What the Prophecy Actually Says

Pip worked through the night the way other people bled—steadily, and with a lot of noise about it.

I'd given them the back room of Maren's safehouse, a candle stub, and the water-swollen book I'd pulled out of the collapsed shrine under Thornfell. By the time I came down at dawn, the little goblin had spread pages across every flat surface like a fortune-teller laying out a bad hand. Ink-stained fingers. Candle wax in their hair. Three empty teacups stacked with the smug precision of someone who'd solved something.

"You look terrible," Pip said.

"Thanks. You look like you slept in a drawer."

"I did sleep in a drawer. It's called efficiency." They held up the book, open to the page that had ruined my life three nights ago. "Sit down, Fairweather. We need to talk about what this actually says."

I sat. My knees didn't want to.

* * *

Here's what I'd read in the shrine, by torchlight, alone, with dust sifting down around me like the mountain itself was leaning in to watch: my name. Tonight's date. A title in a dead language I'd half-recognized from funeral rites—*Sigil-Bearer*. And then a block of text so damaged by water and time that it might as well have been weather.

That was the version of events I'd carried for three days. Name, date, dread. I'd told myself the rest was unreadable. Unreadable was safe. Unreadable meant maybe it said nothing at all.

Pip had spent one night proving me a coward.

"The water damage is real," they said, tapping the page. "But water doesn't eat everything equally. It follows the grain of the paper. Which means—" they flipped to a sheet of their own cramped notes, columns of translated glyphs marching down the page like ants, "—you can reconstruct the missing passages from context. The liturgical formulas repeat. Veyl's rites are formulaic; your god never met a sentence it couldn't say the same way twice."

"My god doesn't say anything," I said. "Hasn't in years."

"Which," Pip said, with a small, terrible smile, "is going to matter more than you know. But first things first." They smoothed the page flat with both palms, the way you'd calm a spooked animal. "The passage. The whole passage. As best I can render it."

They read aloud. Their voice was reedy and quick, a scholar's voice, no theater in it, which somehow made it worse.

*"On the night written here, the Sigil-Bearer comes to the ward beneath Thornfell. He dies there. If he dies believing—in his god, in his oath, in the silence that holds—the seal closes upon Ashkarath forever, and the world forgets the war it buried. If he dies doubting—if his faith fails him at the last, if the silence feels like abandonment rather than sacrifice—then the sigil breaks with his heart, and Ashkarath rises, and the mountains will run red to the sea.*

*"There is no third reading. There is no escape written. The ink does not negotiate."*

The candle flame didn't move. The room didn't move. I moved—I stood up without deciding to, walked to the wall, put my hand against the plaster because stone and plaster were the only things in Greyhollow that weren't trying to kill me.

"That's not all of it," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. Flat. Professional. The voice I used when the job went sideways and panic would get people killed. "There's more. There's always more."

"There's more," Pip agreed. "But that's the spine of it. Everything else is ornament. Names of witnesses. Blessings. A truly unhinged amount of praise for whoever transcribed it, which tells you the scribes knew exactly how important this thing was." They paused. "Oscar. Look at me."

I looked at them. Four feet of goblin in a borrowed coat, candle-wax hair, eyes that had gone very still and very serious in a way that didn't fit their face.

"You die on that date," Pip said. "Or the world ends. Those are the options on the page."

"And if I just don't go?"

"Then the ward frays on its own. Slowly. The prophecy describes that too—it's in the margins, the parts nobody reads because they're boring. Decay schedules. Like a maintenance ledger." Pip laughed, a short ugly sound. "Your god built a doomsday device and wrote the service manual in the same book."

* * *

I made tea. It's what I do. When the world cracks open, I put a kettle on, because a man holding a kettle can't hold a sword, and there are moments when that's the only mercy available.

Maren drifted through the kitchen while I waited for the water, moving boxes of contraband with her usual economy, pretending she wasn't watching me. She was always watching me. That was the problem with Maren Duskwell—she watched everyone, catalogued everyone, priced everyone, and once she'd sold you, she kept the receipt.

"So it's true, then," she said, not quite a question.

"It's true."

"And the date is—"

"Tonight. Tomorrow night. Depends where midnight falls." I wrapped my hands around the kettle because it was warm. "Three days ago I was guiding a shipment of choir-candles past a toll station. Now I'm a load-bearing element in a war god's prison."

Maren leaned against the counter, arms crossed. "You're taking this remarkably well."

"I'm taking this exactly as well as I look."

"That's what worries me." She studied me the way she studied cargo manifests—for damage, for hidden weight, for anything that might shift in transit. "Fairweather. I've known you eight years. You've had arrows in you that got less reaction than this."

"Arrows are honest," I said. "An arrow says: I am an arrow, and I am in you, and now we both know where we stand. This—" I tapped the table where the book lay closed, "—this says I've been a piece on a board since before I was born, and everyone who moved me knew except me."

"Who moved you?"

"That's the part I keep circling." I poured the tea, two cups, pushed one at her because even traitors deserve tea. "Think about it. The shrine collapses the exact night I'm passing through. The book survives—water-damaged but legible, legible enough that a name jumps off the page. My name. In a cache that's been sitting under Thornfell for a century. Either the mountain has a sense of humor, or somebody wanted me to find it."

"Somebody like who?"

"Somebody like whoever wrote 'there is no third reading' and then left the book where a Sigil-Bearer would trip over it." I drank. The tea was bitter. Everything was bitter lately. "Prophecies don't just happen, Maren. Prophecies are written by people. People have reasons."

"You're saying it's fake."

"I'm saying I don't know anymore, and that might be worse." I set the cup down hard enough to chip the saucer. "Because if it's fake, the Choir is hunting me over nothing, and I'm running for nothing. And if it's real—" I stopped. Real was a country I wasn't ready to enter yet.

"If it's real," Maren said slowly, "then the Choir isn't hunting you despite the prophecy. They're hunting you *because* of it. They need you dead on schedule. Believing."

"Do I strike you as a man who dies believing?"

"You strike me as a man who hasn't decided what he believes in years." She said it gently, which from Maren was almost worse than saying it cruelly. "You pray to a god who doesn't answer. Every night. I've heard you through the wall. You kneel, you say the words, you wait in the quiet, and then you get up and go to work. That's not faith, Fairweather. That's habit wearing faith's coat."

"Thank you for the theological review."

"Don't thank me. Listen." She stepped closer. "If the prophecy's right—and I'm not saying it is—but if it's right, then whether you believe or doubt on that mountain isn't a private matter anymore. It's a weapon. Your doubt could crack the world open. Somebody out there knows that. Somebody out there has been working on your doubt for years."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. I thought about the silence. Years of it. Every rite answered with static, every prayer swallowed, every vigil ending in nothing. I'd blamed myself. Bad servant. Weak faith. Oath mispronounced somewhere along the line, some syllable dropped, some debt unpaid.

"What if the silence isn't absence?" I said. "What if it's design?"

Maren looked at me for a long moment. "Then somebody designed it," she said. "And you should find out who, before tomorrow night."

* * *

Pip surfaced again around noon, having slept two hours in the drawer and woken up furious about something in the footnotes.

"Okay, so, update," they announced, dropping onto the bench across from me and shoving their notes into the middle of the table like a dealer pushing chips forward. "Two things. One bad. One worse."

"Love those odds."

"The bad: the prophecy names the mechanism. The ward runs on Veyl's silence. Literally. The old tongue uses the same word for 'silence' and 'seal'—*vessk*—and the text plays with that pun for six straight lines, which in liturgical terms means it's load-bearing. The god's voicelessness isn't a tragedy that happened near the ward. It *is* the ward. The silence is the mortar. The sigil is the brickwork. Take away the quiet and the whole thing starts shedding stones."

I stared at my tea. "So my unanswered prayers have been structural this whole time."

"Every silent night you knelt, you were doing masonry." Pip said it without cruelty, which took skill. "Which brings us to the worse thing." They flipped a page. "The prophecy doesn't just predict your death. It *requires conditions*. Read this."

*"He must come willingly. He must come knowing. A Sigil-Bearer dragged to the ward seals nothing; a Sigil-Bearer ignorant of his name breaks nothing. Only the bearer who walks the road awake, who carries his own death like a lantern, may tip the scale either way."*

"They need me informed," I said. The vertigo arrived right on schedule, a slow tilt behind my eyes, the sense that the floor was a page and someone was writing on it. "They need me to know. To walk in with my eyes open. Because only then does my choice—believe or doubt—actually count."

"Now you're getting it." Pip's grin had no joy in it. "Congratulations, Fairweather. You're not a victim of prophecy. You're an instrument of it. And instruments have to be tuned."

"Tuned by who?"

"By whoever left the book for you to find." Pip gathered their notes, tapped them square. "Think about the sequence. The shrine collapses. The book surfaces. You read your name. You panic—which, fine, normal—and you run to the one person in Greyhollow who can read Old Veylic script. Me. A goblin archivist who's been living in library walls translating dead liturgy since before you grew your first beard scar." They spread their hands. "I'm not saying I'm part of anyone's plan. I'm saying if I *were*, this is exactly how it would look from inside."

The room tilted again. I gripped the table.

"Everyone I've met in the last week could be a hand on the wheel," I said. "You. Maren. The Choir. The mountain itself."

"Yes." Pip said it simply, no drama. "And here's the thing about being a pawn, Oscar. Pawns only stay pawns while they follow the script. The moment you do something the prophecy doesn't account for—something petty, human, unplanned—you're off the board. Not free. Off the board. Different thing. Worse, sometimes. But yours."

"What would the prophecy not account for?"

Pip smiled, and for the first time all day it reached their eyes. "I've been thinking about that. Give me until tonight."

* * *

Evening came down over Greyhollow like a lid. I stood at the safehouse window and watched the town light its lamps one by one, and I counted hours instead of sheep. Twenty-two, give or take, until the date on the page stopped being a prophecy and started being an appointment.

Ysolde hadn't arrived yet—that was tomorrow's thread, or the day after, though I didn't know that yet. The safehouse held four: me, Maren inventorying her stock with the focus of a woman avoiding conversation, Pip hunched over the book muttering in a language older than the walls, and the silence. The silence filled the rest of the space like furniture.

I knelt. Habit wearing faith's coat. I said the evening rite, the one Ysolde would later tell me the Church had gutted, the one whose middle verses I'd never known I was missing. I said it to a god who had been silent for years, who I now understood had been silent *on purpose*, whose silence was a wall between the world and a chained thing that ate wars.

*Veyl. If you can hear me—and I know now that you can't, or won't, or that hearing you is the same as the wall holding—I need one word. Not a sign. Not a vision. One word, any word, so I know whether I'm walking up that mountain as a believer or a doubter. Because apparently that's the whole game. Apparently that's what I'm for.*

The silence answered.

It always answered. That was the horror of it, the joke so old it had gone stale: the silence was the answer, had been the answer all along, and I'd spent years reading it as rejection when it was architecture. My god hadn't abandoned me. My god had been bricked into a foundation, holding up the ceiling of the world, and every unanswered prayer was the sound of a god spending its voice as mortar.

I got up. My knees ached. My hands shook, a little, and I let them, because Pip was right about one thing—a man who performs calm all the way to the altar is a man who arrives at the altar already lying.

Maren appeared beside me. She'd brought the good bottle, the one she saved for funerals and big scores, and she poured two fingers into a cup that had recently held tea.

"To the pawn," she said.

"To the pawn," I agreed, and drank.

Pip looked up from the book. "Before you two get maudlin—one more thing. The last legible line. The one after 'the ink does not negotiate.'" They read it out, slow, letting each word land:

*"So says the ink. But ink was written by hands, and hands were made by something older than writing, and what the hands remember, the ink forgets."*

We all three sat with that. Outside, Greyhollow's lamps burned steady. Somewhere north, the Choir was burning books and asking questions about me. Somewhere east, the Sunspire held whatever answers the Church had locked away. Somewhere below us, under miles of stone, something chained turned over in its sleep and dreamed of red running to the sea.

Twenty-one hours.

"Hands remember what ink forgets," Maren repeated, tasting it. "That's either hope or a threat."

"With prophecies," Pip said, closing the book, "it's usually both."

And that was when the knock came—three sharp raps, then a pause, then two more. Not Maren's code. Not mine. Someone outside the safehouse, in the dark, who knew we were here.

Pip went white. Maren went for the crossbow. I went to the door, because apparently I was still the kind of man who opened doors, prophecy or no prophecy.

Through the gap, hooded, rain-soaked, smelling of incense and exile, stood a woman I'd seen once across a burning market square—a defrocked priestess with an oath-mark on her wrist that matched mine like a key matches a lock.

"You're Fairweather," she said. "We need to talk about what the Church erased. Tonight. Before your date arrives."
