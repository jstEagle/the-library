## 7. Seraphine's Doubt

The reliquary of the Greyhollow chapter house smelled of beeswax and old smoke, and Inquisitor Second-Class Seraphine Lark had been awake in it for nineteen hours.

She told herself it was devotion. Devotion kept a cleaner shape than insomnia. Devotion had a liturgy; insomnia only had the small hours and the hiss of her own thoughts, which lately sounded less like prayer and more like argument.

The book lay open before her on a lectern of black ironwood, chained at both corners—the Choir's half of the *Canticle of Buried Names*, recovered from the smuggler's cache two days before Fairweather's people got their hands on whatever remained. She had read it four times cover to cover. On the fifth pass she stopped reading it as scripture and started reading it as a document.

That was the mistake. Or the beginning of one. She would spend years deciding which.

* * *

Seraphine had entered the Choir at nine years old, an orphan of the Ashfall plagues with a voice the cantors called "clean." Clean meant obedient. Clean meant you could hold a note for a full minute without letting anything human wobble into it. For twenty-two years she had been clean. She had burned texts in eleven provinces. She had knelt through the Rite of Severance without flinching while the Cantor's brand kissed her shoulder. She believed—she still believed, most days, in the way you believe in load-bearing walls—that the Radiant Choir was the only thing standing between the world and the thing under Thornfell.

The doctrine was simple enough to teach to children: A thousand years ago, the war-god Ashkarath came down the mountains like an avalanche given appetite, and the gods died stopping him—all but Veyl, who fled, or failed, or abandoned the field depending on which heresy you asked about. The Choir kept the wards renewed by song and vigilance. The prophecy of the Sigil-Bearer was the Choir's compass: one day a marked man would die at the ward's heart, and his death, rightly ordered, would seal the chains for another age. The Choir's sacred duty was to find that man and ensure the death happened *rightly*. Believing. Kneeling. Singing.

She had recited that last part so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone. She had never once turned it over to look at the bottom.

Until tonight.

* * *

It started with a marginalia problem. Small things. The kind of thing you only notice when exhaustion strips away the reverence and leaves you looking at ink like a scribe instead of a supplicant.

The *Canticle* existed in the old tongue, Veyl-speech, dead now for a century except among scholars the Church didn't license. Every chapter house worked from the Standard Translation, redacted under Cantor Malvane's predecessor's predecessor, three hundred years gone. Seraphine had memorized the key passage at fourteen:

*And the Bearer shall come to the stone at the appointed hour, and his death shall be the lock, and his faith the key, and the sleeper shall sleep until the stars forget his name.*

Clean. Beautiful. She'd sung settings of it.

But here in the original—and she was good enough at Veyl-speech to be dangerous, good enough that Malvane kept assigning her to text recovery, which should have told her something—the line ran longer. Rougher. There were words the translation simply didn't have room for, or hadn't been given room for.

*Veth-karai*, she read. *The Bearer may turn the page.*

Turn the page. Not *shall*. Not *must*. *May.*

She sat back. The candle guttered. Somewhere above her, the night cantors were singing Compline, and the sound came down through the floorboards like water through a ceiling crack.

She checked herself, the way you check a wound with fingers you don't quite trust. *Veth* could be a scribal flourish. Could be dialect. Could be—

Could be nothing. Except that once she saw it, she saw others.

The word rendered as *faith* in the Standard Translation was *shel-veyl* in the original—not belief, but *oath*. Not what a man felt in his heart but what he had sworn and to whom. And the word rendered as *appointed hour* was worse. It wasn't a fixed date at all. It was *the hour he chooses*. The grammar was unambiguous. First person volitional. The Bearer chooses the hour.

You couldn't ensure a chosen death. You could only wait for it, or ask for it, or—her mind supplied the word before she could stop it—*respect* it.

Seraphine put her palms flat on the lectern and breathed until the shaking stopped.

Three hundred years. If the translation had been doctored—and the alternative was that every licensed scholar of Veyl-speech for three centuries had been incompetent, which was its own kind of scandal—then someone high in the Choir had taken a prophecy about choice and sanded it into a prophecy about obedience. Had taken *may* and made it *must*. Had built a machine for manufacturing martyrdom and called it holiness.

And the Choir had killed marked men before. Four, in the archives she could access. Four Sigil-Bearers found and "rightly ordered" across the centuries, each death celebrated in the *Liber Vitae* as the renewal of the seals. She had wept at the feast of the third one when she was a girl.

Had any of them chosen? Had any of them even been asked?

* * *

"You've found it."

The voice came from the doorway, and Seraphine's whole body went to iron before her mind caught up. High Cantor Malvane stood in the reliquary arch, silver-haired, serene, dressed for travel despite the hour, as if he had been on his way somewhere and merely paused to watch her break.

She rose and bowed. "High Cantor. I was cross-referencing the—"

"I know what you were doing." He came into the room the way weather comes into a valley, unhurried, total. He was not a large man. He had never needed to be. "I've been waiting nineteen years for someone in this chapter house to be good enough to find it and honest enough to tell me. Most just find it and start lying."

Her mouth was dry. "Then it's true. The translation has been—"

"Refined," Malvane said. "Adjusted. Preserved. Choose the word that lets you keep your footing, child; they all mean the same thing underneath." He stopped at the lectern and looked down at the open page with something almost like tenderness. "Do you know how old the Choir is?"

"Thirteen hundred years."

"And do you know what happens if the ward fails?" He didn't wait. "Ashkarath stands up. That's all. He doesn't rage, at first. He just stands, and everything within forty leagues dies of proximity. Then he remembers he's angry." He turned to her, and his eyes were kind, which was the worst thing about him. "Now. Do you know what happens if the Sigil-Bearer is asked whether he'd like to die for us?"

Seraphine said nothing.

"He says no," Malvane said gently. "Every time. In three hundred years of asking, in the two generations when we still asked—they always say no. Men will give anything, Seraphine. Anything but permission. So we stopped asking, and the seals held, and the world went on turning, and children in eleven provinces grew up never hearing the name Ashkarath except as a story to frighten them toward bedtime prayers." He touched the page, one finger resting beside *veth-karai* without covering it. "Is that evil?"

"It's a lie," she heard herself say.

"It's a wall," he said. "Walls are lies we agree to live inside. You've lived inside this one your whole life. It has kept you warm." He straightened. "Here is what happens now. The bearer is in Greyhollow. Fairweather. Ex-mercenary, sworn to Veyl, carrying the smuggler's half of the Canticle. My order is simple, and I want you to hear it plainly, because I am giving you a gift: kill him. Tonight, tomorrow, this week—but before the date in the book. Kill him believing, if you can manage it. Kill him however you must. But do not talk to him first. Do not show him mercy, because mercy is just doubt wearing a kinder face, and doubt in a Sigil-Bearer frees the god. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," she said, because her body answered before her soul could object.

"Good." He moved toward the door, then paused, and did not turn around. "One more thing. I was like you once. I found the same word. I sat where you're sitting and I thought: *I will fix it. I will restore the true text and the Choir will be better for it.*" His voice stayed level, almost fond. "I brought it to my mentor, and my mentor brought it to hers, and I learned what you're learning now: the lie goes all the way up. There is no one to appeal to. There is only the wall, and the work of holding it, and the question of what you're willing to become to hold it." Now he turned, and smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Sleep, Inquisitor. We march on the safehouse at dawn. I'll want your blade there."

He left. The candles burned. Above her, Compline ended, and the silence that followed had a texture she had never noticed before—a listening quality, like the pause after a question no one wants to answer.

* * *

She did not sleep. She walked the cloister instead, boots silent on wet stone, rain coming down soft and endless off the mountains, and she took inventory of herself the way you take inventory before a long road: what do I still have, what's broken, what can be trusted to bear weight?

Her faith, examined honestly, was still there. That surprised her. It wasn't the doctrine that had cracked—it was smaller than doctrine, older than it. It was the thing that had made a plague-orphan kneel in the ash and sing to something she couldn't see. Whatever that was, it was intact. It had simply, quietly, changed address. It no longer lived in the Choir. It lived in the space between the translated line and the true one, in the gap where *may* had been murdered and buried under *must*.

Someone had to stand in that gap. Someone had to look at the wall and ask what it cost, and who paid, and whether the payment had ever actually been required—or only demanded, century after century, by men who had decided the question was too dangerous to survive being asked.

Malvane thought he was giving her a gift: the plain order, the clean task. Maybe he was. Maybe this was the test, and the ones who passed were the ones who killed Fairweather without talking to him first.

Or maybe—her mind turned the thought over like a coin with an unfamiliar face—maybe the test was the other direction. Maybe the ones who passed were the ones who asked.

*Veth-karai.* The Bearer may turn the page.

She stopped at the cloister's edge, where the rain fell past the torchlight in long silver threads, and she realized she had already made a decision, sometime in the last hour, in the place beneath decision where real choices are made. She hadn't decided to disobey. She hadn't decided to obey. She had decided something prior to both: that she would look at Oscar Fairweather with her own eyes before anyone's blade touched him. That she would ask him one question, the question the translation had spent three hundred years burying.

Whether he would choose. Whether anyone had ever let him.

Dawn was two hours off. The safehouse was in the Tanners' Quarter, past the burning-barrels, behind a door Maren Duskwell's people thought was secret. Seraphine knew the address. She had known it since yesterday, and she had not yet reported it, and she understood—with a clarity that felt like falling—that the delay itself was already treason. Small treason. Hairline, like a crack in glaze that hasn't reached the clay.

But cracks travel. Everyone in the Choir knew that. It was why they burned books.

She went back to the reliquary, took the *Canticle* from its chains—her rank permitted it, her rank excused it—and copied out the true line in her own hand, in her own cipher, on a scrap of vellum small enough to hide in her glove. Then she put on her traveling cloak, took up her blade, and went out into the rain, telling herself she was going to scout the target.

Knowing she was going to knock on the door.
