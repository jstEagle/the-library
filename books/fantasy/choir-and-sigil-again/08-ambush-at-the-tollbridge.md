## 8. Ambush at the Tollbridge

The tollbridge over the Greywater was older than the town that taxed it, older than the road, maybe older than the god Oscar had sworn himself to and never once heard speak. Its stones were black with a century of river spray, slick as oiled glass in the places the sun didn't reach, and every one of them had been counted by somebody's grandmother for somebody's coin.

Oscar knew the bridge the way he knew his own hands. He'd crossed it forty times running cargo—salt, iron, the occasional crate that rattled wrong when you lifted it—and he'd crossed it twice running from things with white robes and worse singing. He knew which plank near the third arch groaned even when nobody stepped on it. He knew the toll-keeper's hut leaned east because the flood of '61 had pushed it and nobody had bothered to push it back.

What he didn't know, standing on the north bank in the last grey light before dawn, was that he was walking into an ambush he should have smelled from a mile off.

He'd tell himself later that he *had* smelled it—that the smoke hanging over Greyhollow that morning was wrong, too sweet, too much like burning vellum and not enough like woodsmoke—but that was a lie he told after the fact, the way men do, rearranging their own stupidity into foresight so they can live with themselves. The truth was simpler: he was tired, he was alone, and he wanted out of the mountains before the Choir's riders fanned any wider through the passes. The bridge was the fastest way south. Fast is its own kind of blindness.

He paid the toll. The keeper—a woman named Brack whose chin hair he'd once braided for a dare during a long winter—counted his coins without looking up from her ledger.

"Alone this time?" she asked.

"Company's expensive."

"Company's also company." She stamped his token. "Riders came through yesterday. White cloaks. Asked after you by name."

"They always ask after me by name. It's the only interesting thing about me."

Brack looked up then, and her eyes were old and kind and worried. "They offered silver for it, Fairweather. Real silver. Not Choir scrip."

That landed differently. The Radiant Choir paid in indulgences and threats; silver meant someone else was buying, or the Choir had decided he was worth actual expenditure, which was worse. He pocketed the token, said something reassuring and empty—"I'll be across before they wake"—and stepped onto the bridge.

* * *

Forty steps out, the fog took him.

It rolled off the Greywater in a slow white tide, thick enough to swallow the far bank, and Oscar did what he always did in fog: he slowed down, dropped his pack's rattle-loose buckles against his chest, and started counting his exits. Bridge meant two exits, north and south, unless you counted the water, and the Greywater in spring melt was less an exit than a formality of dying. Cold enough to stop a heart in six minutes. Fast enough that you wouldn't feel most of them.

Twenty more steps. The third arch's groaning plank passed under his boots without a sound, which was itself a bad sign—he'd heard that plank groan in earthquakes, in gales, once under a loaded mule. Silence where there should be noise meant someone had been here ahead of him, doing work.

He stopped.

The fog around him was not empty. He couldn't have said how he knew—the way a man knows a room has been straightened, the faint wrongness of air that has been breathed and rebreathed—but he knew. His hand found the haft of his axe where it rode across his back, and he drew it slow, no scrape, the blade coming free of the oiled leather throat-wrap without a whisper.

"Veyl," he said, quietly, to the god who hadn't answered in years, "if you're still in there, now would be a time."

Nothing. Of course nothing. There was only the river talking to itself under the stones, and somewhere in the white, a sound like a hymn being hummed very softly, very badly, by someone who had learned the notes but not the meaning.

They came out of the fog on both sides at once. Four of them, maybe five—he never got a clean count—white-robed, faces wrapped to the eyes, carrying the Choir's ugly little signature weapons: short swords blessed into brittleness, the kind that chipped rather than bent, because a weapon that could break was a weapon that could be sacrificed. One of them carried a censer on a chain, swinging it in wide arcs, choking the bridge deck with incense thick enough to taste.

They weren't good fighters. That was the thing about zealots; the Choir recruited certainty, not skill, and certainty makes men brave in exactly the way that gets them killed fast. But there were enough of them, and the bridge was narrow, and Oscar had nowhere to go but backward or down.

The first one rushed him with a war cry that was mostly scripture. Oscar put the axe head into his shield and shoved, hard, and the man went sideways into his companion and both of them went down in a tangle of robes and chipped steel. The second came low, sword hunting for his knee, and Oscar gave ground—one step, two, boots finding the wet stone by feel—and let the man overextend, then hooked his ankle and dumped him onto the planks hard enough to knock the fog out of him.

For about ten seconds it was going well. He'd fought worse odds in worse places. He was backing toward the north bank, breathing, counting bodies, already composing the report he'd give Maren if he ever saw her again: *your bridge is compromised, your toll-keeper talks.*

Then the censer swung.

It wasn't aimed at him. It was aimed at the *air*, at the fog itself, and where the smoke touched the mist the mist caught—some alchemical filth in the coals, oil of ash and powdered resin, the Choir's little trick for making fog into fire. A line of flame walked across the bridge deck between Oscar and the north bank, waist-high, hungry, cutting off his retreat with a wall of heat that smelled like burnt sugar and something underneath it, older, like a temple burning.

And behind him, at the south end, he heard new boots. More of them. Of course more of them. The four in front had been the bait, the visible thing, the thing a professional would count and dismiss. The real number had been sitting in the fog the whole time, patient as stones, waiting for him to commit.

Oscar stood on a burning bridge between two fires, and for the first time in a long career of standing in bad places, he felt the specific vertigo of having run out of clever options. He could fight forward through five men on a narrow deck. He could try the water. He could stand still and burn. None of these were plans. They were just ways of choosing what killed him.

He chose forward. Not because it was best—because the water was certain and standing still was worse than either, and a man sworn to a silent god learns to move even when moving means nothing, because movement is the only prayer left to him.

He charged the flame line. Axe high, shoulder low, breath held. The heat peeled his eyebrows back and scorched the leather of his bracer and for one half-second he was inside the fire, inside the white roar of it, blind—

—and then he wasn't fighting anymore. Something hit him from the side. Hard. Not a sword, not a shield. Something heavier, moving faster than any man on that bridge, and it took him off his feet and through the smoke and out the other side of the flame line, tumbling across wet stone, and when he stopped rolling and got his bearings he was lying flat on the south end of the tollbridge with a horse breathing in his face.

Not a horse. A mare. Black, mud-caked, ears pinned, with a rider leaning far out of the saddle holding a boat hook of all things—the long iron kind harbor-men use to snag drifting crates—and the boat hook was smoking at the tip, because she'd used it to drag the burning debris off the bridge deck, clearing a lane through the Choir's fire like it was a chore she did on Tuesdays.

The rider was Maren Duskwell.

* * *

Later, when he tried to reconstruct those ninety seconds, they refused to arrange themselves into sense. There was the charge, and the fire, and then there was Maren, arriving out of the fog like weather, like a debt come due, scattering zealots with a boat hook and a warhorse and what sounded suspiciously like profanity delivered in three languages.

She rode the length of the bridge twice. The first pass she scattered the southern group before they'd finished forming, hooking one man across the chest and sending him spinning into his fellows like a bowling ball with opinions. The second pass she came back for Oscar, hauled on the reins, and pulled up close enough that he could have touched her stirrup, which he did not, because touching her stirrup would have meant admitting he needed saving, and some part of him would rather have burned.

"You're alive," she said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't quite a greeting. Her voice had the flat, clipped quality it got when she was spending anger instead of feeling it. "Congratulations. I had money on it. Small money. I don't bet big on people I've sold."

"I remember," Oscar said. Three years ago, a border checkpoint, a bag of Choir silver, and his own name in her mouth like it weighed nothing. He'd spent three years building a life on the far side of that betrayal, and every stone of it had been mortared with the memory of her voice saying his name to men in white. "You came back."

"I came back for my bridge." She gestured with the boat hook at the smoke, the scattered zealots regrouping in the fog, the toll-keeper's hut with its shutters beginning to char. "This is my crossing. My tolls. My arrangement with Brack, may her ledger stay balanced. You burning on it is bad for business. That's all this is."

"That's all this is," Oscar repeated, and almost believed her, right up until he noticed the second thing she'd done, the thing she hadn't mentioned: while she'd been riding her passes, one of her crew—a wiry boy called Finch with a crossbow and no fear response whatsoever—had been dragging the wounded zealots off the bridge deck and laying them out on the south bank, alive, disarmed, their broken swords stacked neatly beside them like evidence.

Maren Duskwell did not leave witnesses. Everyone in the trade knew that. Leaving five living Choir soldiers on a riverbank, within sight of Greyhollow, was not the act of a woman protecting her business. It was the act of a woman making a point she wanted the Choir to receive intact: *I could have killed them. I didn't. Ask yourselves why.*

Or maybe it was the act of a woman who had changed, somewhere in three years, in ways she hadn't announced.

Oscar didn't ask. Asking was how you got lied to, and he'd had his fill of her lies. He got to his feet instead—slowly, because the tumble had cost him a rib or the use of one—and looked at the battlefield she'd handed him. Fog thinning. Fires dying in the wet. Five zealots breathing on the bank, staring at the sky, and beyond them the road south, open, unguarded, running down out of the hills toward the low country and every exit he'd been praying toward since the night he read his own name in a dead man's book.

"Go," Maren said, reading him. "South road's clear for maybe an hour. Then the riders from Greyhollow catch up, and they won't be five farmers with brittle swords. They'll be Lark's people, or whatever passes for Lark's people out here, and they'll be better at this than these ones were."

"You're letting me go."

"I'm *making* you go. There's a difference, and the difference is that if you're standing here in ten minutes arguing semantics, we both get to die stupid." She turned the mare's head north, back toward the smoke and the town. "You want to thank me, don't. Thanking implies a relationship. We don't have one. What we have is a bridge, and you're standing on it."

"Maren."

She paused. Just for a moment. Long enough for him to see her jaw set, the muscle jumping under the ear the way it did when something got past her guard.

"The book," Oscar said. "The one from the shrine. You knew what was in it."

"I knew there was a book. I didn't read it. Reading's for archivists and dead men."

"It had my name in it. And a date. And the date is soon."

Something moved behind her eyes—not softness, nothing so cheap, but a recalculation, the look of a woman discovering the cargo manifest she'd been carrying had a line item she hadn't priced. She covered it fast, the way she covered everything, with speed.

"Then ride faster," she said, and kicked the mare north, and was gone into the fog before he could say anything else, which was, he admitted, the most honest conversation they'd had in three years.

* * *

He took the south road at a dead run, then a staggered trot, then the shuffling lope of a man with a cracked rib and no dignity left to spend. Behind him the tollbridge smoked itself out. Ahead the road dropped through pine shadow along the river, and the river ran south the way rivers do, indifferent, going where it goes.

Finch caught up with him at the first switchback, crossbow slung, breathing easy in the way of the young and unburdened.

"She said to give you this," the boy said, and pressed a folded scrap of oilcloth into his hand. Inside was a strip of dried meat, a pinch of willow bark for the rib, and a coin—Choir silver, minted clean, the Radiant Sun on one face and on the other a number scratched crudely with a knife.

Thirty.

A bounty. His own bounty, or a piece of it, paid out in advance to the smuggler queen who had sold him once and saved him twice and marked his price on a coin like a bookkeeper closing a column.

Oscar stood on the switchback with the coin in his palm and the sun coming up red through the pines, and he understood, finally, the shape of what Maren had done on that bridge. She hadn't saved him out of guilt. Guilt was too small a word and too private a currency. She'd saved him the way you save a thing you've already sold once and can't stop valuing—angrily, at arm's length, with the books balanced to the copper and the heart kept strictly off-ledger.

Thirty pieces. Even the Choir had a sense of humor.

He laughed, once, and it hurt his rib, and he pocketed the coin anyway, and went south.

Behind him, far behind, back up the river in the burning fog of Greyhollow, a bell began to ring—not the toll-keeper's bell, not any bell he knew. It rang in a pattern he'd only ever heard described in banned books and defrocked priests' confessions: three strokes, silence, three strokes. The Choir's call to assembly. Somewhere in that town, someone was gathering the faithful, and the someone had a name he'd been hearing whispered for a week now, getting closer with every telling.

Seraphine Lark.

He ran.
