## 9. The Defrocked Priestess

Ysolde Vane was mending a net when the god walked into her kitchen.

She didn't know it yet, of course. What she saw was a man in the doorway—big, road-worn, favoring his left side the way men do when they've taken a blow to the ribs and decided not to mention it. Mud to the knees. Blood on his collar that was mostly not his. The kind of face you forget on purpose.

But under all of that, humming against her skin like a struck bell nobody else could hear, was an oath-mark.

She put down the net. Very carefully. Her hands had learned that trick years ago—the careful stillness, the *do not spook it* stillness—back when she'd been Sister Ysolde of the Radiant Choir, and the things she noticed were dangerous enough to burn for.

"You're bleeding on my floor," she said.

"Not my blood." His voice was low and even. A mercenary's voice. Trained flat. "Mostly."

"Then bleed outside."

He didn't leave. Instead he looked around her kitchen with an expression she recognized, because she'd worn it herself once: the slow confusion of a man seeing ordinary things and failing to trust them. The hanging herbs. The copper pot gone green at the rim. The nets, because a defrocked priestess living on the coast south of Greyhollow had exactly two options—fishing or worse—and Ysolde had decided she'd done worse already, in another life, in white vestments.

"Are you Ysolde Vane?" he asked.

"Depends who's asking."

"Oscar Fairweather." He said his own name the way people say a password they don't understand. "Maren Duskwell said—if I told you the tide-priests lied—you'd hear me out."

The net-mending needle sat on the table between them. Ysolde did not reach for it. She reached instead for the thing she'd spent nine years teaching herself not to reach for: attention. She let it open like a hand.

And there it was again. Not words. Not voice. Veyl had never given anyone voice, that was the whole cursed point of him—but the silence around this man was *shaped*. It had architecture. It pooled around his left forearm the way cold pools around stone, and when she looked there with her eyes instead of her training, she saw it: a mark running from wrist to elbow, fine as script, dark as old ink.

An oath-mark. A full-life oath-mark. She hadn't seen one since the seminary, where they kept one preserved in glass—a dead mercenary's arm, severed at the elbow, displayed as a warning about swearing yourself body and soul to gods the Church didn't license.

"You'd better sit down," she heard herself say.

"I'll stand."

"Sit down, Fairweather. You're going to want to be sitting."

* * *

She gave him willow tea for the rib and salt bread because it was what she had, and she watched him eat like a man who'd been rationing himself for weeks, and she asked the question carefully, the way you ask anything that might break.

"When did you swear to Veyl?"

"Nineteen." He turned the cup in his hands. "You're supposed to tell me the Choir unmade every priest who served the Silent God. That's what Maren implied. That's what everyone implies."

"The Choir calls us *misled*," Ysolde said. "It's kinder than what they call me specifically." She pulled back her sleeve, and showed him her own forearm, where a thin white scar ran crooked from wrist to elbow—the shape of a brand, half-healed, half-carved-away. "They don't just defrock you. They take the mark. There's a rite for it. There's a hymn for it, if you can believe that. They sing while they cut."

He looked at the scar a long time. Then at her face.

"Why?" he asked. "What did you do?"

"I listened." She laughed, and it came out bitterer than she intended. Nine years, and she still couldn't get the bitterness to sit down and behave. "That's all, Fairweather. That's the whole crime. I was a novice, then a full sister, then—they were grooming me for the archives, I had a gift for the old liturgies, dead tongues, that sort of thing. And one day during the office of silence—I was twenty-three—we were all kneeling in the dark, doing our hour of holy quiet, and everyone else was praying to the Choir's radiant host, and I..."

She stopped. Set down her own cup, because her hands wanted to do something with it and none of those things were useful.

"Everyone else heard nothing," she said. "Because there is nothing to hear. That's doctrine. Veyl is dead, or sleeping, or was never anything but a story smugglers invented to explain why the mountain doesn't answer. And I knelt there in the dark with thirty sisters around me, and the silence *leaned on me*. Like something enormous standing very close in a small room. Like a listener. And I—wept. Out loud. In front of everyone. Because it felt like being answered after a lifetime of shouting into wind."

"And they cut you for it."

"They gave me three chances to recant first. Very generous." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a kind smile. "I want to be clear that I'm not a saint, Fairweather. I recanted twice."

"What changed?"

"The third time, they brought in the instrument they use for taking oath-marks. To demonstrate. On someone else. A boy, seventeen, who'd sworn himself quietly to Veyl the way boys do—not a real oath, not a life-oath, just a child's vow, whispered. And they took his mark in front of the novices as a lesson." Her voice stayed level. She'd had practice. "And I stood up in the middle of the rite and sang the opening line of Veyl's liturgy. The real one. The banned one. In front of the whole chapter house."

Silence in the kitchen. The good kind, actually—the kind she'd learned to love out here by the water, where quiet wasn't an accusation, just weather.

Fairweather set his cup down.

"Sister," he said, "I have sworn to a god who hasn't spoken to me in eleven years. I've done the rites. All of them. Every dawn, every dusk, the fasts, the vigils, the—there's a vigil where you hold your breath until you nearly pass out, did you know that? Nobody knows that. Nobody alive remembers any of this except me and a handful of dying smugglers. And I keep doing it. Into nothing." His jaw worked. "I came here because Maren said you might know why. And if the answer is *because he's dead*, I'd rather hear it from someone who'll say it plainly."

Ysolde looked at him—at the oath-mark crawling faintly along his arm, at the flat professional calm stretched over something that had been screaming quietly for a decade—and felt the oldest feeling of her life rise up in her chest, the one that had gotten her branded, the one no rite could cut out:

*This man is mine to help.*

Not because a god said so. Nothing had ever said so. That was the joke of her whole existence—that she'd been cast out for hearing an answer, and had spent nine years unable to prove whether the answer was real or madness. But whatever the silence was, it had gathered itself around this man like a tide around a post, and Ysolde Vane had exactly one skill left worth the name, and it was reading silences.

"It's not dead," she said. "I need you to understand what I mean when I say that, because I could be wrong. Nine years of exile, and everything I know comes from books I memorized before they burned them. But no, Fairweather. Dead gods don't lean."

"Lean?"

"Dead silence is empty. This—" she pointed at his arm, at the mark, at the air around him that her skin insisted on noticing, "—this is *occupied*. Something is holding that silence like a man holding his breath. And there are only two reasons a god holds its breath, so far as I ever found in the liturgy."

He waited. She made herself say it, made the words real by giving them air, knowing there was no taking them back.

"Either it's mourning. Or it's *working*."

* * *

She got the whole story out of him over the next hour, in pieces, the way men like him give things up—smuggling run, collapsed shrine, a book with his name in century-old ink, a date that was now four days past and counting. The Choir hunting him. The goblin archivist decoding. Maren Duskwell, of all people, somehow back in his debt and his company both.

"And the prophecy says my death does it," he finished. "Seals it or frees it. Depending." On how he died—believing or doubting—he didn't say, and she didn't push, though she saw the shape of the omission clearly. Some weights you only name when you're ready to be held up under them.

"A death," Ysolde repeated. She stood, because sitting still had become impossible, and began to pace her narrow kitchen, and as she paced, the old training woke in her like a limb remembering it had fingers. "They wrote you a death. Of course they wrote you a death. The Church has only ever known one ending for a story about sacrifice—it's their favorite instrument and their favorite currency. But listen to me." She stopped in front of him. "The liturgy of Veyl predates the Choir by eight hundred years. Do you know what the oldest stratum of it is? Not prayers. *Contracts.* The old tongue doesn't have a word for worship, Fairweather. Its word for prayer translates literally as *terms of agreement*."

"So?"

"So oaths are bargains. Always have been. You swore your life to Veyl—your *life*, your living, breathing, waking life—and the mark went on your arm. And now a book tells you the bargain ends in your death, and the Church agrees, and a buried war-god probably agrees, and everyone is very certain that the terms are settled." She crouched to his eye level, and took his marked forearm in both her hands, gently, the way you handle scripture. "But I have read the original contracts. And I am telling you, as the last person alive fluent in the grammar of your god: *no one has read you the closing clause.*"

Something moved behind his eyes. Hope is too clean a word for it; hope is what you feel before you've been hurt enough times to fear it. This was more cautious than hope. An audit, almost.

"You can teach me," he said. Not quite a question.

"I can teach you what the Church erased," Ysolde said. "Which is most of it. The vigils you're doing are Choir redactions—stripped, bowdlerized, safe. The real liturgy of Veyl isn't a devotion. It's a dialogue. One party speaks and one party keeps silence, and the keeping of silence is *itself the speech*. Your god hasn't abandoned you, Fairweather. You may simply have been answering him wrong for eleven years. Asking questions into a conversation that was waiting on you to say something else entirely."

For a moment he said nothing. Then, quietly, with the care of a man touching a wound to learn its depth:

"And if I've been doing the rites wrong, all this time—what have I been saying?"

Ysolde held his gaze and told him the truth, which was all she had left to give anyone.

"I don't know," she said. "We'll find out tonight."

* * *

They waited for full dark, because the old rites belonged to the dark—Veyl had never been a god of night, exactly, but of the spaces light hadn't gotten organized yet. Ysolde cleared her table, chalked the circle from memory, her hand moving through the geometry without hesitation even after nine years, and hated herself a little for how good it felt. Her body remembered the vestments. Her hands remembered the liturgy. Exile, it turned out, lived in the mind but not in the marrow.

"Kneel here. Left knee. The mark faces the center." She positioned him like a soldier, because he was one, and soldiers understood formation better than faith. "Now. The first phrase. You know it as *Veyl abides*—that's the Choir's translation. The actual words are older, and they mean something closer to *I am here to listen*. Say it as listening. Not asking. Listening."

He said it.

Nothing happened.

He said it again, and this time—whether exhaustion or desperation loosened something—he stopped performing it, stopped saying it like a mercenary reciting orders, and simply *meant* it, the naked stubborn attention of a man who had shouted into wind for eleven years and refused to stop.

Ysolde felt the silence move.

There was no other word for it. The quiet of the room acquired weight, direction, *attention*—the enormous leaning presence she had felt once at twenty-three, in the dark of the chapter house, and never again. It bent toward the kneeling man like grass bending under wind. The candle flames stood perfectly straight. Somewhere out on the water, the gulls went abruptly, totally silent.

"Keep going," she breathed.

He kept going. Phrase after phrase, the old contract-tongue rolling off him clumsy and accented and utterly sincere, and with every line the leaning grew stronger, until Ysolde's eyes were stinging and her scarred forearm ached like the brand was fresh, and she understood, with a joy so fierce it was indistinguishable from grief, that she had been right. All these years. Mad, probably, exiled certainly—but *right*.

And then the silence *listened back*.

It lasted perhaps three heartbeats. The quiet in the room gathered itself around Oscar Fairweather like a tide rising, and in that tide Ysolde felt—not heard, there were no words, there would never be words—something that her training could only render as a single vast impression, ancient and exhausted and unbearably patient:

*I hear you. I have always heard you. I could not answer.*

Then it withdrew. The candles flickered. The gulls resumed. Oscar knelt in the chalk circle shaking, one hand pressed flat to the floor, the other pressed to his oath-mark, which had gone from ink-dark to faintly luminous, like letters catching lamplight.

"What," he managed, "was that."

"That," Ysolde said, and her voice cracked clean in half, and she didn't care, "was the first half of a sentence nine hundred years long. And we are going to finish it."

She helped him up. Two exiles, in a fisherman's kitchen, at the edge of the world—both of them marked, both of them cast out, both of them suddenly and terribly necessary. Outside, far inland, toward Thornfell, the ground shivered once, softly, like something turning over in its sleep.

Oscar felt it through the floorboards and went very still.

"First lesson's tomorrow," Ysolde said, already reaching for chalk and memory and the banned books she'd rebuilt from scratch in her own hand. "Eat something. Sleep if you can."

He nodded. But he stood a long moment at the window, looking north toward the mountain, his marked arm held against his chest like something newly alive—and Ysolde, watching him, wondered what exactly they had just woken, and whether either of them was ready for the rest of the sentence.
