## 10. Lessons in Dead Tongues

Ysolde had taught novices before. She had stood in the white cloister of the Sunspire with forty children of the Choir arrayed before her, their spines straight as candlewicks, their voices trained to rise in perfect unison toward a god who never answered. Teaching them had been like teaching bells to ring themselves. They made sound. The sound was all they had.

Oscar Fairweather was nothing like her novices.

He stood in the cellar of Maren's safehouse with his boots on and his jaw set and his oath-mark bared at his throat like an accusation, and he looked at the old liturgy spread across two barrels and a plank table as though it had personally wronged him.

"This is a waste of time," he said. "I've done the rites. Every one. For eleven years."

"You've done the rites the way the Choir let them survive," Ysolde said. "Which is to say: badly, and backwards, and with all the meaning scraped out." She tapped the page. "Sit down, Sigil-Bearer. Class has started."

He sat. It was, she noted, the first thing he'd done without arguing since she'd agreed to teach him. That was something. Hope came in small denominations these days.

* * *

She began where the Choir had ended—because that was always where truth lived, in the places doctrine had amputated and left to bleed out quietly.

"The Church teaches that Veyl went silent," she said. "That the Quiet God turned its face away from the world some hundred years ago, and that faith without answer is the purest faith. They built a whole theology around abandonment. *Blessed are the unanswered.* Do you know why?"

"Because it keeps people kneeling," Oscar said.

"Because it keeps people afraid," Ysolde corrected. "Fear is easier to harvest than love. But here's what they cut out." She turned the page, to the passage she had copied out by hand three times in her life—once as a novice, once as a priestess, once the night they stripped her vestments and put her out the gate. "The liturgy doesn't begin with speech. It begins with listening. The old rites were never prayers. They were *invitations*. You don't ask Veyl for anything. You make a silence so honest that whatever listens in the dark has somewhere to sit down."

Oscar's eyes narrowed. "And if nothing sits?"

"Then you've still made an honest silence," Ysolde said. "Which is more than most people manage in a lifetime. Now. Posture."

"I know the postures."

"You know the Choir's postures. Hands folded, chin dipped, spine bent in supplication. Everything the Choir does is supplication, because supplication is control dressed in humility." She rose, moved behind him, and put two fingers between his shoulder blades. He flinched; she didn't withdraw. "Straighten. Not proud. *Open.* Chest up, like you're making room. Palms loose at your sides, not folded—folded hands are a wall. And breathe low, into your belly, slow enough that your body forgets it's braced for a blow."

"That's—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "That's how I stand when I'm on watch."

Ysolde smiled despite herself. There it was. Eleven years of dead rites, and the man had been doing the true posture all along without knowing it, alone in the dark, watching over other people's sleep. "Then you already know the first half of everything," she said. "Good. Now unlearn the second."

* * *

They worked through the morning.

The cellar was cold and smelled of lamp oil and turnips, and Pip had colonized one corner with the prophecy book and six stolen candles, muttering translations in a language that was half goblin-tongue and half pure scholarly profanity. Upstairs, Maren's footsteps crossed and recrossed the floor above them—pacing, always pacing, a woman wearing a groove into her own guilt. The whole house felt like a held breath. Ysolde chose not to think about that. She chose, instead, to think about declensions.

"The liturgy isn't a language you speak," she told him. "It's a language you *stop*. Every word in the old canon is designed to close a door in yourself. *Veth*, which the Choir translated as 'peace'—it means no such thing. It means the laying down of grievance. *Solm*, which they called 'rest'—it's the laying down of fear. Word by word, rite by rite, you put down everything you're carrying until there's nothing left in you but the shape of you. An empty room. Clean floors. A hearth swept and waiting."

"And then?" Oscar asked. His voice had changed over the hours. Less iron in it. More question.

"Then whoever the room was built for comes home. Or doesn't." She shrugged, and hated how casual she'd learned to make that shrug. Twenty years in the Sunspire had taught her to wear disappointment like a garment. "Faith, real faith, is just hospitality extended into the dark, with no guarantee of a guest."

"My god stopped talking to me eleven years ago," Oscar said flatly. "The day I swore the oath. My voice for its silence. That was the bargain. I've kept my half."

"Have you?" Ysolde asked, gently enough that he couldn't take it as attack. "Or have you been keeping it the way the Choir taught you—kneeling to a door, hammering on it, calling the hammering prayer?"

He didn't answer. But his hands, which had been folded—the wall, always the wall—came slowly apart and settled open on his knees.

Ysolde looked at those hands and felt something she hadn't permitted herself in nine years: the ache of almost-hope. This man had been sworn to her god since he was little older than a novice. His oath-mark was genuine; she'd seen it glow faintly in the dark of the tollbridge aftermath, a thin silver line at his throat like a scar left by moonlight. The mark didn't lie. Whatever had happened to Veyl, whatever bargain or sacrifice or catastrophe had emptied the god out of the world, this mercenary had kept faith with it through eleven years of static. He deserved better than the mutilated liturgy. So did the god. So, if she was honest, did she.

* * *

*

* * *

By the third day, she had him chanting.

Not singing—the Choir sang, and singing was performance, and performance was another wall—but chanting, low and metronomic, each syllable set down like a stone in a wall that wasn't a wall. *Veth. Solm. Kireth vael. Veth solm anna-kireth.* The laying down of grievance. The laying down of fear. The opening of the hand that holds.

Pip had abandoned the prophecy book entirely by the second afternoon and sat cross-legged near the barrels, ears tilted forward, taking notes in a shorthand Ysolde suspected would get them both burned if anyone ever read it. Maren had stopped pacing overhead. Twice now Ysolde had caught the smuggler queen on the cellar stairs, halfway down, saying nothing, leaving again. Listening. Whether to the lesson or the liturgy, Ysolde couldn't say.

"It's not working," Oscar said, on the fourth evening. His voice was raw from the chant. Four days of putting down grievances, and he sounded like a man who had carried a great deal of grievance and set it down only reluctantly, one finger at a time.

"It's not supposed to *work*," Ysolde said. "It's not a lever. It's not a key. It's sweeping a floor."

"I've swept the same floor for four days."

"Yes. That's what sweeping is." She let him glare. He was, she was discovering, a glarer of considerable talent. "You want results. Results are the Choir's currency. Pray this much, receive this much. Sing louder, be blessed harder. The old rites promise nothing. That's what makes them holy."

"That's what makes them useless," he shot back.

"Maybe." She gathered the pages, unhurried. "Tell me, mercenary. When you stand watch at night—the true posture, remember, the one you taught yourself—when you keep that open silence over sleeping people who'll never know you did it—does that work? Does it accomplish anything? Can you point to the result?"

His mouth opened. Closed.

"No," Ysolde said. "You do it because it's right. Because keeping watch is what love looks like when nobody's watching. The liturgy is the same act, aimed at a bigger dark. Nothing more. Nothing less." She paused at the stair. "Tomorrow night we do the full rite. The Rite of the Opened Room. Wear something you can kneel in."

"I thought kneeling was a wall."

"It is," Ysolde said. "But there's one kind of kneeling that isn't. Come find out."

* * *

The fifth night, Maren doused every lamp in the safehouse.

It had taken argument to arrange—Maren did not enjoy darkness in her own house, and Pip did not enjoy darkness anywhere, and Oscar had objected on principle to ceremony on principle. But Ysolde had won, the way teachers win: by simply continuing until resistance exhausted itself. Now the cellar held only the light of a single stub of beeswax, guttering in a dish, and the four of them arranged in its penumbra like figures on an old coin. Even Maren had stayed. She sat on the stairs with a knife in her lap, whittling nothing, pretending she wasn't attending a service.

"Last instruction," Ysolde murmured. "When you reach the end of the liturgy, you will want to do something. All people do. You will want to fill the silence—to ask, to beg, to check whether it worked. Don't. The final word of the rite is *annath*. It means 'the door stands open.' After it, you do nothing. You hold the emptiness. Whatever happens next is not yours to cause."

"And if nothing happens next?"

"Then nothing happens next," Ysolde said. "And tomorrow you sweep the floor again."

He looked at her a long moment in the dying candlelight. Something moved behind his face—not doubt exactly. Doubt would have been easier. It was closer to the look of a man standing at the edge of cold water, knowing he was going in anyway.

Then he closed his eyes, straightened his spine, opened his hands, and began.

Ysolde had heard the liturgy performed in the Sunspire's great nave by two hundred voices and the Silver Organ, and it had been, she could admit now, magnificent garbage—a parade dressed as a prayer. What Oscar did in that cellar was the other thing. The true thing. His voice was rough and untrained and it cracked twice, and none of it mattered, because every syllable was a door closing: grievance laid down, she could see it leave him, a tightening around his jaw easing like a knot worked loose. Fear laid down; his breathing dropped low into his belly, slow, the watch-stander's breath. The hand that holds, opened; his palms turned upward on his knees, loose as falling leaves.

*Veth. Solm. Kireth vael.*

The candle flame stopped flickering.

Ysolde noticed it the way you notice a missed heartbeat—not as event but as absence. The flame stood perfectly upright, a single golden teardrop, burning without movement. The draft from under the door still stirred the hem of her robe. The air still moved. Only the fire had gone still, as though listening.

*Veth solm anna-kireth,* Oscar chanted, and his voice had changed—it was still his voice, rough, human, tired, but underneath it, or through it, or behind it in a direction Ysolde had no proper word for, something else had begun to attend. Not a sound. The silence in the cellar had always been ordinary silence, cellar silence, the silence of stone and stored turnips and sleeping houses. Now it was *shaped*. It leaned. It had the quality of a room when someone enters it—the quality of being-inhabited.

Pip's pen had stopped moving. On the stairs, Maren's knife had stopped whispering against wood.

*Annath,* Oscar said. *The door stands open.*

And then he stopped, as instructed, and held the emptiness, and Ysolde watched him do the hardest thing in the entire canon: nothing. Watched him sit in the open room of himself with his hands open and his chest open and his whole scarred stubborn heart swept clean and offered, wanting nothing, demanding nothing—

—and the silence *listened back*.

There was no voice. No thunder, no whisper, no organ-note from beyond the world. What happened was smaller than any of that and infinitely stranger: the silence drew close. It gathered, the way warmth gathers around a hearth, the way a gathering gathers. Ysolde felt it pass across her skin like the attention of some vast gentle animal—one moment absent, the next moment present, regarding the man on the bare boards of a smuggler's cellar with what she could only call recognition. Eleven years of static, and here, tonight, in the sweep of one cleaned-out heart, something that had been far away took a single step nearer.

The candle flame bowed. Once. Toward Oscar, like a head inclining.

Then it was gone—or not gone, but settled, returned to ordinary silence, though an ordinary silence that was now, forever after in Ysolde's memory, a different color. The flame resumed its flickering. The draft stirred. Somewhere above, the house creaked on its beams like any house.

Oscar opened his eyes.

He didn't speak for a long time. When he did, his voice was barely more than breath. "It heard me."

"*Yes*," Ysolde said, and found her own voice had gone ragged. Nine years of exile, twenty years of unanswered hymns, and her eyes were wet in a lamplit cellar over a mercenary and a candle. She laughed—short, cracked, involuntary. "Yes. It heard you."

"What does it mean?" Pip whispered, pen forgotten, ears wide. "Theoretically. Liturgically. What does it *mean*?"

"It means," Ysolde said slowly, working it through even as she spoke it, feeling the shape of the truth the way you feel the shape of a key in the dark, "that Veyl isn't gone. Silent, yes. Distant, perhaps. But not deaf, and not dead, and not indifferent. The Choir's whole theology is a lie built on top of a tragedy. Something silenced our god. Something *did this to it*." She looked at Oscar—at the oath-mark faintly silvering his throat in the dark, brighter than she'd ever seen it. "And whatever your book says about wards and war-gods and the date of your death, Sigil-Bearer, understand what happened here tonight. You didn't pray to Veyl. You made room, and Veyl came. Which means the god can come. Which means the god can be *reached*."

Hope, she thought. Fragile as frost, thin as the light off that stub of candle. Nine years she had refused to carry it because it broke so easily and cut worse when it broke.

She picked it up anyway.

"So we do it again," Oscar said. It wasn't a question. His hands were shaking slightly; he looked down at them with the expression of a man rethinking his entire relationship with the world. "Tomorrow. We sweep the floor again."

"Tomorrow we sweep the floor again," Ysolde agreed.

On the stairs, Maren had gone very still, and her voice, when it came, was quiet and flat and wrong in a way that Ysolde would remember afterward:

"Then you'd best learn fast." She stood, and the knife vanished into her belt, and her face in the half-light was grim as a closed gate. "Because there's riders on the north road. Choir livery. A lot of them. And they're asking after us in every village between here and the pass."
