## 11. Maren's Confession

The storm came in off the pass two days after the ambush at the tollbridge, and with it came Maren Duskwell's oldest habit: counting exits.

She stood at the window of the safehouse above the tannery in Greyhollow, watching rain hammer the cobbles, and counted them out of pure reflex. The back stair, which groaned on the fourth step. The roofline to the chandler's next door, an easy jump if you were willing to break an ankle. The cellar tunnel that flooded every spring and was flooding now. Three ways out, none of them good.

Oscar Fairweather sat at the table behind her, cleaning a blade he hadn't needed to draw since the bridge. Ysolde was asleep in the loft, worn hollow from the day's liturgy lessons. Pip had gone to ground in the library again, carrying the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth like a colicky infant.

Which left Maren alone with the one man she'd sold, and no exit worth taking.

"You're going to wear a groove in those boards," Oscar said without looking up.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous pastime."

She almost smiled. Almost. That was the trouble with Fairweather — three years of silence between them and he could still find the old rhythm like it was stitched somewhere safe. She turned from the window. "We need to talk about the Choir."

"We've talked about the Choir."

"Not about why they knew where you'd be. Not about the tollbridge." She crossed her arms. "They didn't stumble onto you, Oscar. Zealots don't patrol tollbridges in the rain for their health. Someone told them where you were going."

His hands went still on the whetstone. He looked up at her then, and his eyes were the same flat grey they'd been the day she'd watched him ride out of Vell's Crossing under Choir escort, wrists bound, believing she'd been captured too.

"You," he said.

"Me." Her voice held. She made damn sure it held. "I sent word after the ambush. Before it, actually. I told them your route."

* * *

He set the blade down carefully, the way he did everything — quiet, deliberate, a man who'd sworn his voice to a silent god and so made silence into a craft. For a long moment the only sound was the rain and the tannery vats bubbling somewhere below, sour and animal.

"Say the rest," he said.

"There isn't a rest. I sold you twice. Once three years ago, once last week. Same coin both times, if you want to know — not coin at all, but you'll hear it or you won't."

"Then say it plainly, Maren. You owe me plain."

And there it was. He was right. She owed him more than plain; she owed him three years of nights she'd spent telling herself the story where she'd had no choice, and stories rot if you don't open the lid sometimes.

So she sat down across from him, put her hands flat on the table where he could see them, and gave him the truth like a debt collector gives up a ledger.

"My brother Tam is alive," she said. "Everyone thinks he died at Redford Ford with the rest of the Duskwell boys. He didn't. He was taken. The Choir took him — took all of them, but the others they killed outright, quick, because dead smugglers make better warnings than live ones. Tam they kept." Her throat worked. "They keep him in the Sunspire. Seventh level, the penitent wards. I know because I paid a clerk to tell me, and the clerk's description of the place still wakes me up some nights."

Oscar said nothing. He was good at nothing. It was a weapon when he used it.

"The Inquisitor who ran the purge — Lark's predecessor, a man named Corvane — sat me down in a room that smelled like candle grease and told me the terms. Not arrest terms. Business terms. The Choir does business, whatever their hymns say. He wanted the clans mapped. Every route, every cache, every name that moved contraband through Thornfell. And he wanted a name I wouldn't give — a name the Choir had been hunting for years." She met his eyes. "Yours. They'd been looking for the Sigil-Bearer before either of us knew there was such a thing. Corvane just didn't have the book yet. He had rumors. A mercenary sworn to Veyl who walked shrines like he was reading them."

"And you gave him my routes instead."

"I gave him everything except you. For six years I gave him nothing but you, piece by piece, whenever he tightened the screws. Your contracts. Your employers. Where you slept in winter." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You think I built the Duskwell routes alone? Half my roads are roads you cut, and I handed the map over one line at a time because every time I did, Corvane would send me a letter with Tam's handwriting on it. Just his handwriting. A few words. *Still here, Mare. Still here.*"

The rain filled the pause. She let it.

"Three years ago he asked for you by name. Not your routes. You. He said the hunt was ending, and if I delivered you, Tam would walk out of the Sunspire with a pardon and a purse. And I—" Her hands curled on the table. "I drugged your wine at Vell's Crossing. Mild stuff. Enough that you'd ride slow and sleep deep, enough that the Choir's riders could take you without a fight, because I couldn't stomach the idea of you dying in front of me. I told myself that made it kinder. It didn't. Nothing about it was kind. They had you for eleven days, Oscar. Eleven days I drank myself stupid in Vell's Crossing waiting to hear you were dead, because dead would've been cleaner than what I'd done."

"How did I get out?"

"They lost you. Or someone lost you — I never learned how, and I stopped asking, because the answer might've been worse. Corvane was furious. Said I'd cheated him. Kept Tam anyway, kept the letters coming, and raised the price. Next time, he said. There will be a next time. So when the Choir came back this season with a new face — Lark, young, hungry, asking after a mercenary named Fairweather — I told myself it wasn't the same thing. It was just a route. Just a warning. Just keeping Tam breathing another month." She finally looked away, out at the rain. "It was the same thing. It was always the same thing."

* * *

Oscar rose from the table. Maren's whole body braced — she'd seen men take that particular calm into violence often enough to know its weather — but he only walked to the window and stood where she'd been standing, counting exits that weren't there for him either.

"Eleven days," he said at last.

"Yes."

"I remember most of them. The cell was white. They sang at me. Did you know that? Hours of it. Hymns until the words stopped meaning anything, until your own name sounds foreign." He turned. "I got out because a jailer fell asleep, and because I'd spent eleven days learning the sound of boots in that corridor. I swore after that I'd never be taken slow again. And here you are telling me you're the reason I was taken at all."

"I know."

"Do you?" Something cracked in his voice, just a hairline, quickly plastered. "Because I've been building trust brick by brick since the bridge, Maren. Letting Pip decode the book with us. Letting Ysolde teach me rites I don't understand. Sleeping under the same roof as you. And the whole time you've been running messages to the people trying to kill me."

"No." She stood too, and now her voice did rise, because guilt was one thing and being misread was another. "I'm done running messages. That's what tonight is. Corvane is dead — Lark replaced him, and Lark doesn't honor his arrangements, or doesn't care to. The last letter I sent was the tollbridge, and the answer I got back wasn't Tam's handwriting. It was a printed notice. *Compliance noted.* Like I'm a warehouse." She pulled the folded paper from her coat and slapped it on the table between them. "I burned my bridges with the Choir for a man who signs his letters like a tax office. So no, Oscar. I'm not selling you anymore. There's nothing left to sell you *for*. Except—"

"Except?"

"Except the Sunspire holds two things I want. My brother. And the other half of your prophecy book — Pip says the Inquisition keeps a matched copy in their reliquary, and I believe Pip, because Pip is the first person in years who's scared of the right things." She held his gaze. "I can get us inside. I know the Sunspire's bones — the clerks' corridors, the supply roads, the faces that can be bought. I've been buying them for three years to keep one prisoner alive. I can turn that whole ledger toward getting him *out*."

Oscar picked up the printed notice. Read it. Set it down. When he spoke again his voice had gone back to that terrible evenness, and somehow that was worse than shouting.

"Why tell me now? Why not on the road, when I couldn't leave? Why not never?"

"Because we're walking into the mountain that buried Ashkarath, and I've watched what happens to crews who carry secrets into the dark. They come apart. And because—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "Because you deserve to choose whether I'm at your back. Not me deciding for you. I've decided enough for both of us."

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she catalogued every version of the next minute: him walking out, him drawing steel, him forgiving her, which would somehow be worst of all, because forgiveness was a door she hadn't earned and doors like that swing shut on people like her.

"You asked me once," Oscar said finally, "on the road to Vell's Crossing, years ago, why I stayed sworn to a god who never answers."

"I remember."

"I told you it was professionalism. It wasn't. It was because Veyl was the only thing that had never lied to me." He tapped the table, once, softly. "You lied to me for six years. But you also carried me out of the Gullet with an arrow in my lung, and you fed my crew through two bad winters, and you never once sold me cheap. Corvane offered you a fortune and a pardon and you took routes and rumors instead. You sold me in installments, Maren, small enough that you could pretend each one wasn't the whole of me. That's not nothing. That's not forgivable, either. Not yet."

Her chest hurt. She nodded anyway. "Not yet is more than I expected."

"Don't mistake me." His grey eyes didn't soften. "If we go into the Sunspire and you send one word, one signal, one glance that costs any of us — Pip, Ysolde, any of them — I will not kill you. That's the mercy. I'll just leave you wherever we are, in whatever's coming, and the mountain can have its opinion of you. Understood?"

"Understood." And the strange thing, the thing she'd carry afterward, was that the threat landed like relief. Boundaries. At least boundaries were honest.

"But," Oscar said, and now something shifted in his face, something tired and human, "you told me. When you didn't have to. When never was on the table and you chose now." He exhaled slowly. "That counts for something. I don't know how much yet. But it counts."

Maren laughed — a real one this time, wet and rough at the edges. "Fairweather, you have a gift for making absolution sound like a customs inspection."

"Somebody has to. Veyl's not talking."

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, done. Then she spread her hands on the table, and the smuggler-queen came back into her spine like a tide.

"All right. Here's what I know about the Sunspire." She pulled a stub of charcoal from her pocket and began to draw on the tabletop, quick sure lines: towers, terraces, the winding pilgrim road. "Supply gate on the north face, staffed by four guards who haven't been paid properly since spring. Clerks' stair from the archive levels — Pip's credentials could get us partway. Penitent wards on seven, which means we go through the reliquary on six to reach them, which means your second book and my brother are one floor apart. The Choir likes to keep its sins organized."

Oscar studied the map she was scratching into the wood, and slowly, inevitably, the mercenary took over from the wounded man — shoulders settling, eyes going tactical. She'd seen that transition a hundred times and it still moved her, the way competence could look like devotion.

"One floor apart," he murmured. "Book and brother."

"One job, then," Maren said. "Impossible, suicidal, and mine. You in?"

He looked at her a long moment — measuring, weighing, the scales still tipped against her and likely to stay there. Then he nodded, once.

"In. But Maren—" He caught her wrist as she turned, gentle, immovable. "When we get Tam out, you and I finish this conversation. Whatever's left of it."

"If we get him out," she said, "there won't be anything left to finish."

Above them, in the loft, floorboards creaked — Ysolde, awake, listening to more than she'd ever admit. And far below the town, beneath the rain and the tannery stink and the whole loud world, something in the roots of Thornfell shifted in its sleep, and the silence that answered it felt, for the first time in three years, less like absence.

More like attention.
