## 12. The Inquisition Closes In

The rain came down on Greyhollow like a verdict.

Seraphine Lark stood at the window of the Wayhouse of the Radiant Choir—the only building in the border town with a roof that didn't leak and windows that hadn't been patched with hide—and watched the water run silver through the gutters. Three days of rain. Three days of asking questions in taprooms and tollhouses, of pressing copper into the palms of drovers and dockhands and one very talkative ferryman who had seen, he swore on his mother's grave, four travelers heading upriver toward the old quarry road. A mercenary with an oath-mark like a brand of shadow. A woman with a smuggler's swagger and a knife she wore like jewelry. A goblin in a coat too big for him, carrying books wrapped in oilcloth. And a fourth figure, hooded, whose face no one would describe—which was itself a description, because people always described faces, unless the face belonged to someone they'd once knelt before.

Ysolde Vane.

Seraphine's teacher. Seraphine's shame. The name the Choir did not speak above a whisper, as if saying it loudly might reawaken whatever heresy had lived in her.

She turned from the window. Behind her, the wayhouse common room had been converted to the Choir's purposes: choir-banners hung over the hearth where someone else's trophies used to be, the bar repurposed as a writing desk, and Brother Cassian—her second, her shadow, the man the Cantorate had assigned to her the moment this commission left the Sunspire—was sorting confiscated texts into two piles. Burn and keep. The piles were uneven. They were always uneven lately, and the imbalance troubled her more than it should have.

"Report for the day, Inquisitor?" Cassian asked. He was a broad, placid man with a voice like warm milk and eyes that missed nothing. That was the trouble with Cassian. Everyone assumed the gentle ones weren't watching.

"The trail goes cold at the quarry fork," Seraphine said.

It did not go cold at the quarry fork. It went *up*. The ferryman had been precise about that, once the copper stopped being a promise and became a fact. Up the quarry road, toward Greyhollow itself—and then past it, toward the high passes, toward Thornfell, toward everything the Choir pretended not to fear because fear was a form of doubt and doubt was the first sin.

But she said *cold*, and she watched the word land, and she felt something shift under her ribs like a stone settling in a wall.

Cassian nodded and made a note. "The Cantor expects us at Thornfell's edge within the fortnight. If Fairweather reaches the mountain—"

"He won't."

"You sound certain."

"I am thorough." She took the report from his hands before he could write more, scanned it, corrected a date. Her own hand. Her own lie, sitting there in her own careful script, looking exactly like truth. It looked so much like truth that for a moment she believed it herself, and the believing was worse than the lying. That was the thing about treason: it began not with a knife but with a pen.

* * *

That night she could not sleep, which was unusual; she slept the way soldiers sleep, quickly and completely, a discipline she'd learned in the novitiate where Ysolde Vane taught that rest was also a prayer. *A body kept awake by worry is a body that has stopped trusting the Choir to keep watch.* She could still hear Ysolde's voice saying it, warm and unhurried, chalk dust on her fingers, the morning bell rolling over the seminary courtyard.

So instead of sleeping she read.

They had seized forty-one texts since entering Greyhollow. Most were the usual sediment of a border town: love charms mislabeled as heresy, herbalists' manuals with diagrams some junior acolyte had mistaken for summoning circles, a book of funeral songs that predated the Choir's canonization of grief-as-joy and therefore had to burn. She had signed each destruction order without hesitation. She had done it for eleven years. The burning was not cruelty; it was *editing*. The Choir held that history was a hymn, and hymns required arrangement, and arrangement required cutting verses that caused the congregation to stumble.

Tonight she pulled the oldest seizure from the stack: a fragment of liturgy recovered from a shrine collapse in Thornfell, water-damaged, its margins crawling with annotations in three dead hands. It had come in with a smuggler's cache, according to the manifest, though the manifest was written in a clerk's lazy shorthand and told her nothing useful except that the cache had passed through Duskwell hands.

Duskwell. Maren Duskwell, who had sold Oscar Fairweather to them three years ago and then somehow, impossibly, was traveling with him now. Seraphine had read the file on that betrayal twice. Files on informants were always thin—they were the thinnest documents the Choir produced—but this one had bothered her even then. There had been no payment recorded. No favor granted. Maren Duskwell had given up a man she'd run cargo with for years and received nothing the ledger could see.

Nothing the ledger could see.

Seraphine set the fragment down and pressed her fingertips against her closed eyes.

Here was the shape of the thing she was refusing to look at directly: she had found the trail in half a day. The ferryman, the quarry fork, the tanner's boy who'd seen a goblin buying lamp oil in quantities suggesting a long stay somewhere dark. She could have written the true report. By tomorrow night the Cantorate's riders would be in Greyhollow, and by the following dusk they'd have the four of them cornered in whatever cellar or library hollow they'd nested in, and it would be done. Clean. Doctrinal. The Sigil-Bearer dead before he could choose, the wards preserved, the prophecy fulfilled the way the Choir had arranged for it to be fulfilled a century ago when the first translators bent the ink.

*When the first translators bent the ink.*

She had told herself, on the road out of the Sunspire, that she would verify the translation herself. Due diligence. An Inquisitor's rigor. She had even begun the verification—comparing the Choir's canonical rendering of the Thornfell prophecy against the older glosses she'd memorized in Ysolde's classroom, back when learning dead tongues was a devotional act rather than a weapon.

The canonical text said the Sigil-Bearer's death would *seal* Ashkarath, full stop. Death in faith, sealing forever. Simple. Clean. A martyrdom the Church could schedule.

The older glosses said something else. Not in so many words—the old tongue was slippery, built on roots that meant several things at once—but the verb rendered as *seal* in the canon carried, in its earliest attestation, a secondary sense. *To seal. Or to close the door upon.* To shut something away—or to shut something *out*. As if the original authors had understood that a ward powered by a death was not a lock but a debt, and debts came due.

And beneath that, fainter, in a marginal hand so old it had nearly faded to memory: *the bearer may refuse the ink.*

She had reported none of this. She had filed her preliminary findings—*translation consistent, no discrepancies noted*—and sealed the folio with wax and the Choir's sunburst, and the seal had gone onto the document the way a lid goes onto a well, and now here she was, three hundred miles from the Sunspire, standing in the dark over her own signature.

Treason, she thought, usually announced itself. Banners. Armies. A renunciation spoken from a scaffold.

Hers had arrived as a clerical error she had chosen to make.

* * *

Brother Cassian found her at dawn in the wayhouse chapel, kneeling before the Choir's great sunburst icon—a disc of gilded light with a face that was all radiance and no features, the god rendered as pure approval. Seraphine had knelt before it ten thousand times. Tonight—this morning—she knelt the way you kneel when you are checking whether the floor still holds.

"Inquisitor." Cassian's boots stopped beside her. He did not kneel. He never knelt when they were alone; he said kneeling was for congregations, and between the two of them he preferred honesty. She had once found this refreshing. "You sent the report?"

"Sent at midnight. Fast courier."

"To the Cantor's field office at Vell's Crossing."

"Yes."

Vell's Crossing was nine miles south. The report she'd sent said the trail was cold, that Fairweather had likely doubled back east toward the lowlands, that she recommended widening the search perimeter along the river roads. Every word of it was a door she had closed with her own hand.

Cassian was quiet for a while. Rain still worked at the windows. Somewhere below, Greyhollow was waking—carts, curses, the smell of frying onions climbing the stairwell.

"I've served with you six years," Cassian said finally. "I've watched you interrogate a smuggler for fourteen hours and get the truth out of him so gently he thanked you at the end. I've watched you burn a library in Kelder's Ford and stand in the ash until your boots went gray, and you told me the ash was necessary, and I believed you." He crouched down beside her, unhurried, until his face was level with hers. His eyes were kind and absolutely awake. "So tell me why the courier rode south at midnight carrying a report you finished writing before sundown, and then sat in this chapel all night like a woman waiting for the floor to open."

Seraphine kept her gaze on the featureless golden face. Approval without eyes. Judgment without a gaze. She had loved that about the icon once—that the Choir's god saw everything and blinked at nothing, that mercy and scrutiny were the same light. Now the blankness looked less like omniscience and more like absence. A face where a face should be.

"Because I'm tired," she said.

"No," Cassian said gently. "Try again."

"Because the translation is doctored, Cassian." The words came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh—relief and pain in the same motion, and blood everywhere after. She hadn't decided to say it. It decided itself. Eleven years of discipline and it simply walked out of her mouth in a damp border-town chapel at dawn. "The canonical text of the Thornfell prophecy has been altered. Not once. For generations. The verb that means *seal* meant something else first. There's a margin note—older than the canon, maybe older than the Choir's charter—that says the bearer can refuse. Refuse the ink, refuse the death, refuse the whole architecture. And we kill anyone who gets close enough to read the original." She finally looked at him. "We're not preserving the wards. We're preserving the *editors*."

Cassian received this the way he received everything: completely, without flinching. Then he said the sentence she would remember for the rest of her life, however long the Choir allowed that to be.

"I know," he said. "I verified the glosses myself, two years ago, off a pre-canonical fragment in the deep archive. I wrote a memorandum. It reached the Third Cantor's desk and I was reassigned within the month—to you. The brightest inquisitor of my generation, they said. An honor." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "I've been your second ever since. You understand what that assignment was. I'm not your partner, Seraphine. I'm your leash. And the question I have sat with for two years is whether you feel it."

The rain filled the silence.

"And last night," he continued, "you put a false report in a courier's satchel and sent it south. Which means either you feel the leash, or you've decided to test how far it stretches." He rose, straightening his cassock with the same calm he brought to everything. "I need to know which. Because if the Cantor's riders come through Vell's Crossing and find nothing there—if they follow your false trail east and lose a week while Fairweather walks free—someone will ask me what my Inquisitor did on the rainy night in question. And I will have to decide what to say. And I have been deciding, quietly, for two years, what kind of man I intend to be."

"What will you say?" she asked.

"That the trail was cold," Cassian said. "That my Inquisitor is rigorous beyond reproach and that I checked her work myself." He paused at the chapel door, hand on the frame, not looking back. "The goblin bought lamp oil. Lamp oil, Seraphine. Nobody buys that much lamp oil for a short stay. Whatever hole they're hiding in, they plan to be in it a while, reading. If you mean to find them before the Choir does—not to kill them, we both know that now—you should start with who in this town sells oil and hates the Choir enough to forget selling it."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the corridor, and Seraphine knelt alone before the golden face that had never once, in all her years of devotion, answered her.

She thought about Ysolde Vane, defrocked for hearing holiness in a silence everyone else called empty. She thought about a margin note in a dead hand: *refuse the ink.* She thought about how a person becomes a traitor—not in fire, not in a single burning moment, but one small false line in a report, one courier riding the wrong direction, one dawn spent kneeling before a god-shaped blank and admitting, at last, that the blank had never knelt back.

Seraphine rose. She took the canonical folio from her satchel, the one with her sealed findings, the one that said *no discrepancies noted*—and she opened it, and unstoppered her ink, and beneath her earlier lie she wrote a single line in her own precise hand:

*Discrepancy confirmed. Original text permits refusal. Investigation continues. —S.L.*

She did not send it anywhere. Not yet. But she folded it flat, and hid it inside the binding of a funeral song slated for burning—the one text in forty-one seizures that no zealot would ever think to search—and she felt the strangest sensation as she did it. Not guilt. Not fear.

Relief.

Like setting down something heavy she had been told, all her life, was holy.

Outside, the rain was stopping. Over Greyhollow's crooked roofs the clouds were tearing apart, and light came through in long gray blades, and Seraphine Lark—who had been the Radiant Choir's perfect instrument for eleven years—stood at the window and let it fall across her face and thought:

*They're in the town. Under it, probably. And I have perhaps three days before Malvane stops sending riders and starts sending armies.*

Three days to find a defrocked priestess, a traitor smuggler, a goblin archivist, and a marked man the whole machinery of her faith wanted dead—

—and decide, finally, which side of the ink she stood on.

Somewhere beneath her feet, in the dark of Greyhollow's foundations, she imagined she could almost hear it: pages turning.
