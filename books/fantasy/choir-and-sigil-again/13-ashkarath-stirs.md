## 13. Ashkarath Stirs

The mountain spoke first.

Not in words—Oscar had spent eleven years learning the difference between a mountain's noise and a mountain's meaning—but in a long, grinding groan that came up through the floorboards of Maren's safehouse at Greyhollow's edge and kept going, longer than any groan had a right to. The tea in his cup shivered into rings. A jar of pickled eels walked itself half an inch along the shelf and stopped, as if embarrassed.

Pip looked up from the prophecy book, quill hovering. "That's not weather."

"No," Oscar said.

"That's not even geology. Geology is slower. That was—" Pip's ears flattened against their skull. "That was something turning over."

Ysolde set down her cup with both hands, careful as a woman handling a relic. She'd gone pale under her freckles. "Under us," she said. "That came from under us."

Oscar crossed to the window. Outside, Greyhollow went on being Greyhollow—goats in the lane, a tanner arguing with his apprentice, smoke rising straight from forty chimneys into a windless grey sky. Nobody had noticed. Nobody ever noticed the first tremor; that was the trick of them. They were tuned below the frequency of ordinary attention, a note only the guilty and the god-sworn could hear.

He could hear it still. A second groan, deeper, rolling up out of Thornfell like a cart moving through a cathedral.

Maren appeared in the doorway with a lantern she hadn't lit, holding it anyway, which told him more than anything she might have said. Three years of knowing her and he'd catalogued all her tells: the way she touched her left cufflink when she lied, the way she hummed when she was planning something she didn't want discussed, and the way she stood very still with an unlit lantern when the world frightened her.

"How bad?" she asked.

"I don't know yet."

"You always know. It's insufferable."

"Then I'll be insufferable later." He turned from the window. "Get word to your people on the mountain roads. Anyone guiding loads over Thornfell tonight pulls off and waits at the lower camps. I don't care what it costs in contracts."

"Contracts are how I eat."

"Maren."

She held his look for a moment, then nodded once and vanished into the back of the house, already muttering names—Brack, the Vetch sisters, old Tam Hollis who ran the high pass and was too stubborn to die but not too stubborn to be crushed.

Pip had pulled the prophecy book toward themselves again and was smoothing the water-warped pages with a goblin's particular tenderness, the way you'd handle a wounded bird. "It says here the ward frays before it fails," they said, not looking up. "'As a hem frays before the garment falls.' I translated that line weeks ago and thought it was poetry. I'm reconsidering."

"Everything in that book is poetry until it isn't," Ysolde said quietly.

Oscar looked at the sigil-mark on the back of his own hand—the oath-mark, the brand he'd taken at nineteen in a shrine no bigger than a coffin, swearing his life to Veyl's silence. He'd felt nothing from his god in years. The silence had been total, professional, absolute. Tonight, for the first time since the day he'd taken the oath, the silence had texture.

It wasn't empty anymore. It was occupied.

* * *

He tried not to sleep. That was the first mistake.

Maren's safehouse had one proper bed and three pallets, and by unspoken agreement Oscar took the pallet nearest the door, because that was where the man who expected trouble slept, and Oscar had been expecting trouble so long it had become a kind of faith. He lay on his back listening to the house settle around its occupants: Pip's scratchy breathing from behind the bookshelves where they'd built their nest of stolen folios, Ysolde's slow even rhythm, Maren upstairs doing whatever Maren did instead of sleeping, which was mostly inventorying grievances.

The tremors came again around midnight. Closer together now. Shorter. Like footsteps quickening.

He must have slept despite himself, because the dream began without his permission.

He was standing on the tollbridge—or a bridge like it, except the gorge beneath had no bottom, just a darkness so complete it seemed less like absence of light than presence of anti-light, a blackness with weight and appetite. The planks under his feet were warm. That was wrong. Stone and timber should be cold this deep in a mountain night.

*You feel it,* said a voice.

Oscar knew every register of silence. He'd made a marriage of it. Veyl's silence was clean, like snowfall, like the inside of a bell that has never been struck. This voice came *through* that silence the way a worm comes through an apple—from inside, wearing the fruit's skin.

*I know what you are,* Oscar said, and his dream-voice didn't shake, which surprised him. *Ashkarath.*

The darkness below the bridge moved. Not stirred—moved, deliberately, the way a man shifts in chains to get comfortable. And far, far down, something caught what little light the dream allowed: a gleam like a link of chain the size of a river barge, swinging gently, coming to rest.

*A century I have listened to that god of yours say nothing,* the voice said. It was patient. It was enormous without shouting. It had the tone of someone who has owned a thing for a very long time and expects to go on owning it. *Do you know what I heard in all that silence? Nothing. Do you know why? Because there is nothing to hear. Your Veyl did not fall silent, little oath-bearer. Your Veyl stopped existing.*

"Liar." The word came out flat and hard and true, and he clung to its trueness like a handhold on a cliff face.

*Am I lying?* Something like amusement moved through the dark. *Ask yourself, Fairweather. Eleven years. Eleven years of rites performed into a well with no bottom. You kneel, you bleed, you keep the old forms—and what comes back? What has ever come back?*

Oscar stood on the warm planks and made himself look down into the dark, because looking away was the same as agreeing.

*But I am awake,* Ashkarath said. *I am here. I am the only voice in this mountain that answers. And I am generous, Fairweather—I am famous for my generosity among dead men. Doubt him. One doubt. That is all I ask. Stand on this bridge in your sleep and say: perhaps he is gone. Say it once, mean it a little, and I will give you—*

The dark bloomed. Images rose out of it like drowned bodies finally surfacing: Maren, alive, her debts burned, her brother whole and laughing at some joke Oscar couldn't hear. Pip in a tower library of their own, respected, funded, fed, no more walls to live inside. Ysolde restored to her choir robes, singing again, her voice unbroken. Himself—himself with the mark gone from his hand, the oath dissolved like sugar in water, walking free through a world with no prophecies in it bearing his name.

*All of it,* Ashkarath said softly. *For one small word. Doubt is such a little thing, Fairweather. It weighs less than a coin. Men carry heavier doubts to market every day and never notice the cost.*

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Oscar would carry out of the dream like a splinter under the nail: the offer was good. Not the terms—the terms were rot, obviously, the oldest rot in the world—but the *diagnosis*. The voice had read him correctly. There were nights, real nights, waking nights, when he knelt at his private shrine and performed the rite and felt absolutely nothing come back, and the silence sat on him like a lid, and he thought: *dead gods don't answer, and I have given my life to a corpse.* Those thoughts existed. He'd fed them and starved them by turns for a decade. The war-god hadn't invented the doubt. It had only found it, the way a finder of mushrooms knows which log to turn.

*You're afraid,* Ashkarath observed, almost kindly. *Good. Fear is honest. Your god's silence was never honest—it was abandonment dressed as mystery. Come down to me, Sigil-Bearer. Or stay up there and wait for the Choir to do what they've promised to do. Either way, the ink gets you. Only I'm offering you a choice about whose hand holds the pen.*

Oscar woke.

* * *

He woke the way soldiers wake—all at once, hand already closing on the knife under the pallet—and for three full seconds he didn't know where the dream ended. The ceiling beams of the safehouse swam above him. His heart was going like a siege drum. And the mark on the back of his hand was burning.

Not metaphorically. He raised it into the strip of moonlight from the window and saw the sigil-lines flushed red, hot to the touch, as if the oath itself had run a fever in the night. As if something had gotten close enough to it to scorch.

"You felt it too."

Ysolde was sitting up across the room, wrapped in her blanket, watching him. Her eyes in the dark were steady and sad. Of course she'd felt it. She'd been defrocked for hearing holiness in Veyl's silence; if anyone alive would notice the silence getting crowded, it was her.

"He talked to me," Oscar said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Through the silence. Wearing it."

"What did he want?"

"Doubt." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry ugly sound. "One word. That's all. One little word and everyone lives happily ever after. He showed me—" He stopped. Started again, because Ysolde would see through the omission and he was tired of managing people tonight. "He showed me all of you. Fixed. Happy. Debts paid, voices restored, libraries of our own. All of it priced at one word I'd barely miss."

Ysolde was quiet for a while. In the next room, Pip muttered in their sleep—a string of archaic syllables, translating even unconscious—and somewhere above them Maren's floorboard creaked once, twice, her insomnia pacing its cage.

"And did you believe him?" she asked at last. "About Veyl?"

Oscar looked at his burning hand.

Here was the truth he'd tell no one else, maybe not even himself in daylight: for one moment—one suspended, vertiginous moment on the warm planks of that endless bridge—he had *wanted* to say the word. Not because he believed Veyl was dead. Because the offer had shown him a version of the future where nobody he loved had to die for a century-old sentence in a water-damaged book, and wanting that future had felt so much like believing that the two had become indistinguishable, and he'd stood there on the edge of damnation discovering that despair doesn't announce itself. It arrives wearing hope's coat.

"I believed him for exactly as long as it takes to want something badly enough," Oscar said. "Then I remembered he's a war-god chained under a mountain, and everything he offers is a hook."

Ysolde came and sat beside him on the edge of the pallet, uninvited, the way family does. She took his hand—his marked hand—and turned it over in hers, studying the fevered sigil lines like a physician reading symptoms.

"This heat means the ward is thinning," she said. "Your oath is tied to it. When the ward strains, you strain. Which means—" she looked up at him, and the moonlight caught the silver threads in her cropped hair, "—which means Ashkarath didn't send you a dream, Oscar. He sent you a progress report. He's testing the seal. And he wanted you to know it, because a frightened Sigil-Bearer doubts faster than a calm one."

"So the temptation was also a weapon."

"Everything a god does is also a weapon. That's rather the problem with gods." She pressed his hand closed, folding the burning mark inside his fist, as if she could hold the fever in. "Listen to me. Whatever he showed you—your friends happy, your debts gone—that future costs exactly one word, and the word is a lie, and lies compound. You know this. You've smuggled enough contraband past enough bribed inspectors to know how a small lie becomes a large grave."

"I know it." He did. That was the worst part—he knew it the way you know the location of every exit in a room you're trapped in.

"But?"

"But he's right about one thing." Oscar opened his fist and looked at the sigil, red as a fresh wound. "Eleven years of silence. Rites into a well with no echo. If Veyl is alive—if Veyl was ever alive—then he chose to leave me kneeling in the dark for a decade, and I don't know which is worse: a dead god or one who watches and says nothing."

Ysolde's grip tightened on his hand.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we find out which. Because there's a third option you haven't considered, mercenary, and it's the one I was cast out for believing." Her voice dropped low, urgent, lit from inside. "What if the silence isn't abandonment? What if it's *spent*? What if your god gave its voice to something—to the ward, to the seal, to the thing keeping that creature's chains tight—and what you've been praying into all these years isn't a corpse at all, but a man with his mouth full, holding shut a door?"

Oscar stared at her.

Outside, Thornfell groaned again—longer this time, deeper, and this time it didn't stop after a breath or two but rolled on and on, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat to speak. Down in the town, dogs began to bark, street by street, a wave of them. A shutter banged. Somewhere a child started crying.

And in the silence between the tremor's end and the world's response, Oscar heard it—faint, threaded through Veyl's great quiet like a hairline crack through porcelain:

A whisper. Not Ashkarath's. Something older, wearier, buried deeper.

Two words, in a dead tongue Ysolde had taught him only last week, and his blood went cold as meltwater when he understood them.

*Hold fast.*

He was on his feet before he knew he'd moved, dragging on his boots, and Ysolde was already reaching for her lamp, and from behind the bookshelf Pip's voice came, wide awake and shaking: "I heard it too. Tell me somebody else heard it too."

From the top of the stairs, Maren, lantern finally lit, her face carved sharp and pale in its glow: "Somebody had better explain why the mountain just learned to talk."

Oscar looked at the four of them—at the ragged, impossible family fate had assembled to stand between the world and the thing underneath it—and heard himself say the words that would spend the rest of their lives:

"It's time we went down and met the god who's been keeping us."
