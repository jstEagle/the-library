## 14. The Sunspire Job

Nobody said yes. Nobody said no, either. They just stood there in Maren's kitchen while the mountain finished its sentence somewhere far below the floorboards—a long, grinding hush, like a giant turning over in its sleep—and then it was Oscar who broke first, because of course it was. He'd been the one to say the mad thing out loud, and now he had to live with it.

"Down," Pip repeated from the doorway, arms crossed over their chest like a small furious parenthesis. "You want to go *down*. Toward the thing that just whispered through a dead god's silence. Into the dark. On purpose."

"I want to know what we're standing on," Oscar said. "I want to know what's waking up and why it's waking up now."

"And I want a boat," Maren said, "but I don't go around telling people about it." She set her lantern down on the table with a click that ended the conversation before it started, which was a skill she'd spent thirty years perfecting. "Sit down. All of you. If we're going to be idiots, we're going to be organized idiots."

They sat.

Maren looked at them—the mercenary who couldn't stop walking toward his own death, the priestess who'd been thrown out of heaven for listening too closely, the goblin archivist who'd been living in walls so long they flinched at open sky—and felt something twist under her ribs that she refused to name. She'd run crews before. She'd run *fleets* before. She knew what a crew looked like when it stopped being a crew and became something else, something worse, something that made you do stupid things for free.

This was starting to look like that.

"Here's what we know," she said, all business, because business was how she kept from feeling. "The Choir's got half a prophecy book locked up in the Sunspire. The Sunspire is a fortress built into the face of a mountain by people who thought mountains were a good idea. It's garrisoned, warded, lit like a festival, and the last time any of us got close to it—" she tapped her own sternum, "—it cost me three years and a brother."

Silence. Even the mountain kept quiet, which was considerate of it.

"So before anybody gets romantic," Maren went on, "let's establish what this actually costs. Not the coin. The rest of it."

* * *

It was Ysolde who spoke first, and when she did, her voice had the flat calm Maren had learned to dread on other people's faces. "My order will kill me if they take me alive. That's not rhetoric; that's policy. Defrocked choir-priestesses get a formal trial, mostly for the record, and then a formal pyre. So whatever we do, we don't get taken."

"Noted," Maren said.

"They'll also recognize me," Ysolde added. "The Sunspire trained me. I sang in its choir for eleven years. Half the people in those corridors watched me take my vows."

"That's either a problem or an asset," Pip said.

"It's both," Ysolde said. "Which is the worst kind of thing to bring on a job."

Maren nodded slowly. She'd smuggled a lot of things through a lot of checkpoints, and the one universal truth of the trade was that the best cover story was the one closest to true. "Then we use you," she said. "Not as a disguise—as bait. A defrocked priestess dragged back to the Sunspire in chains looks like a victory. Nobody interrogates a victory too hard."

Ysolde considered this with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic on her own life and finding the sum acceptable. "I'll need to look convincingly captured."

"I've done convincing before," Maren said, and didn't elaborate, and nobody asked, and that was the kind of trust she was learning not to spit at.

* * *

Pip, meanwhile, had produced a piece of chalk and was drawing on the tabletop, because Pip drew when they were frightened and everyone had learned to let them. What emerged was the Sunspire—not from memory, but from documents. "Requisition ledgers," they said, tapping as they sketched. "Supply manifests. A tithing schedule I copied off a merchant's letter two years ago because it had a pretty seal. Buildings tell you everything if you read their paperwork. The Sunspire has four gates: the Processional, for ceremony; the Merchants' Post, for goods; the Windstairs, for the garrison; and the Deep Gate, which opens onto the old mine roads."

"The mine roads," Oscar said quietly. "That connect to Thornfell."

"Everything connects to Thornfell," Pip said. "That's rather the point of a prison, isn't it? You build your jail on top of your prisoner and then you build your roads around the jail. The Choir didn't choose the Sunspire for the view."

Maren stared at the chalk lines. Of course they hadn't. She'd been running cargo past that fortress for twenty years and never once wondered why the holiest building in the borderlands sat directly above the most cursed mountain in them. You didn't wonder about things like that. Wondering was bad for business.

"So the same tunnels that lead down to the war-god lead up into their reliquary," Oscar said. There was something in his voice Maren didn't like—something eager, almost hungry, the sound of a man who'd found a door marked DANGER and was already reaching for the handle.

"No," Maren said.

Oscar looked at her.

"We are not going down through the deep roads," she said. "Not yet. We go in through the top, clean, quiet, and out again before anything under the world knows we're visiting. The deep roads are the emergency exit, not the front door. You don't open the emergency exit unless everything else has already gone wrong."

"And if everything goes wrong?" Pip asked.

"Then we'll already be running," Maren said. "That's the trick. Always be running before you need to."

* * *

It took them until well past midnight to build the shape of it, and even then the shape was less a plan than a list of ways to die, ranked by preference.

The core of it was Ysolde. She would walk into the Processional Gate in chains—or the appearance of chains, Pip swore they could forge manacles convincing enough to pass a ten-foot inspection—and be presented as a recaptured heretic, delivered by a bounty crew. Which meant the bounty crew needed credentials, which meant Pip spent an hour with ink-stained fingers and a stolen template muttering things like *"the Choir uses a double-seal for field warrants, which is idiotic, because the second seal is only checked at the gate, and the gate sergeant changes every six days, and today is day five."*

"You're enjoying this," Maren observed.

"I'm experiencing joy adjacent to terror," Pip said. "It's very productive."

The bounty crew would need faces. Maren could provide two—herself and Oscar—but a crew of two escorting a high-value heretic raised questions, so they'd need a third, and there was exactly one candidate, and he was currently asleep in the room Maren had rented him, which meant she'd have to wake him, which meant she'd have to have *that conversation.*

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem was simpler and worse.

"Say we're inside," Ysolde said. She was pacing, lamp-light catching the faded choir-mark still inked faintly on her wrist, half-scraped but not gone. "Say the gate swallows us. Where's the book?"

"The reliquary," Pip said. "Sub-level three. Same level as—" they stopped.

"As the cells," Maren finished.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Ysolde sat down slowly, like someone setting down something heavy.

"Your brother and the prophecy," she said, "are in the same building. Possibly the same corridor. The Choir doesn't waste space."

"I know," Maren said.

"That's not a coincidence," Ysolde said. "That's a leash. They keep him where keeping him matters. Every step toward the book is a step toward him, and every step toward him—"

"—is a step they can cut short," Maren said. "Yes. Thank you. I'd worked that out around the third time I woke up screaming."

She hadn't meant it that sharp. She heard it land and saw it land, in Ysolde's flinch and Pip's sudden fascination with their own chalk, and she exhaled through her nose and made herself say the next part, the part she'd been chewing on since the mountain spoke.

"But here's the thing about leashes," she said. "They only work if the dog doesn't know where the stake is. I've spent three years pretending I didn't know why I keep taking jobs near the Sunspire. Three years of telling myself it was routes and rates and coincidence." She looked around the table. "I'm done pretending. My brother is in that rock, and the book is in that rock, and I am going into that rock to get both or die explaining to him why I didn't. That's the job. Anybody who's in, is in for *that*, not for the prophecy and not for the god and not for the fate of the world. The world can queue."

* * *

It was Oscar, of course, who ruined the moment by being honest.

"Maren." His voice was quiet. "If it comes down to the book or Tam—"

"Don't." 

"If it comes down to it," he pressed, gentle and immovable, "you take your brother. We'll find another way to the book. Or we won't, and Ashkarath wakes up, and at least you'll have done the one right thing available to you."

"You don't get to spend my conscience for me," Maren said. "That's my coin."

"Then spend it yourself," Oscar said. "But decide *now*. Not in the corridor. In the corridor is where people freeze."

She wanted to throw the lantern at him. Instead, because he was right and they both knew he was right, she sat with it, jaw working, hands flat on the table like she could pin the decision down physically before it bolted.

"My brother," she said finally. "Tam first. Always Tam first. The book's a bonus." And then, quieter, almost against her will: "Though if some of you happen to be near the reliquary anyway—"

"We'll be near the reliquary anyway," Pip said.

"We'll all be near the reliquary anyway," Ysolde said.

"See?" Pip said. "Efficient."

And Maren laughed—short, cracked, surprised out of her—and the laugh did something to the room that none of the planning had managed. It made it a crew. Not four strangers bound by apocalypse, but a crew, with a job and roles and someone to blame afterward.

* * *

They worked the details until the candle drowned in its own wax.

Pip laid out the forged warrant, the route papers, the names they'd wear inside the walls—Maren as *Captain Ilset Vane*, licensed reclamation contractor, a profession that apparently existed solely to give legal cover to people exactly like Maren. Oscar would be her sword-hand. Ysolde the prize. Pip—

"Pip stays outside," Maren said.

Pip's head came up like a drawn crossbow.

"You're our way back," Maren said. "You hold the tunnel mouth at the old mine road with horses and a story for the guards, and if we're not out by the third bell after midnight, you burn the papers and ride for Greyhollow and you *don't come back for us.*"

"I'm not leaving you in—"

"You're the archivist." Maren leaned forward, and put every year of command she had into her voice. "If we die in there, the only copy of everything we've learned dies with us unless it's sitting safe outside with you. You're not the least important person on this crew, Pip. You're the *payload*. Payload waits in the wagon."

Pip glared at her for a long, trembling moment—and then, horribly, their eyes filled, and they scrubbed furiously at their face and muttered, "Fine. FINE. But I'm writing a formal complaint into the historical record. 'The crew of the Sunspire job, having been warned, ignored all warnings, per usual.'"

"Witnessed and attested," Ysolde said solemnly.

"Put it in verse," Oscar said. "It'll last longer."

* * *

Near dawn, when the others had stumbled off to grab a few hours' sleep, Maren stood alone at the window looking east, where the Sunspire would be catching first light off its gilded crown like a knife catching fire.

Three years ago she'd traded a man's location for her brother's breathing. She'd told herself a hundred stories since—that Oscar was a mercenary, he'd survive, the Choir only wanted questions answered, that a queen of the smuggler roads couldn't afford sentiment. All true. All lies. Both things at once; that was the whole art of her life.

Now she was going to walk back into the place that owned her debt, carrying the man she'd sold, to steal from the people who held her collateral.

There was a version of her, once, who'd have called that suicide. The version of her that existed now just called it *Tuesday*, and started counting supplies.

Behind her, floorboards creaked. Oscar, sleepless too, a shadow in the grey light with a cup of something hot he'd evidently made for two.

"For what it's worth," he said, handing her one, "I forgave you about a year ago. Took me that long to notice."

Maren looked at the cup, and at the mountain, and at the impossible, ragged, doomed crew sleeping in her house.

"Just don't make me regret the sequel," she said.

He smiled, and touched his cup to hers, and below them—soft as breath, patient as stone—the mountain hummed once, as if it had heard the plan too, and approved.

Far to the east, the Sunspire caught the dawn.
