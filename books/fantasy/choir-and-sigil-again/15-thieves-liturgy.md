## 15. Thieves' Liturgy

Pip had forged documents before, obviously. You didn't survive two decades living inside the walls of a Greyhollow library without learning to make a seal that would pass at three paces in bad light. But forging credentials for the Radiant Choir was a different order of blasphemy entirely, and Pip was finding that blasphemy, like good ink, required patience.

The problem was the light.

Choir writs weren't paper — they were vellum cured with consecrated oil, and under lamplight they shimmered with a faint gold thread woven into the grain, called *grace-line* by the people who made it and *that smug golden squiggle* by Pip. Every credential the Choir issued carried it, and every checkpoint between here and the Sunspire would have some bored acolyte tilting the document toward the sun to check for it.

"You can't fake grace-line," Maren said, watching over Pip's shoulder in the way that made Pip want to bite her. She smelled of pipe smoke and last night's cheap brandy, and she'd been hovering since dawn.

"Wrong," Pip said. "You can't fake it *quickly*. Give me a week and a dead choirman's vestment to strip the thread from."

"A dead choirman's—" Maren paused. "We don't have one of those."

"Not yet we don't." Pip held the stolen letter up to the window light, squinting until the golden filament caught. There. Woven diagonally through the third line of the benediction, subtle as a spider's silk. "But Ysolde has a whole trunk of old robes from her defrocking days, and I'd bet my left ear at least one of them still has the original stitching."

"That's an oddly specific wager."

"I'm a goblin of specific talents."

Maren snorted and drifted off toward the front room, where Oscar was doing something quiet and heavy-handed with a whetstone, because Oscar processed all his emotions through sharpened implements like some kind of large, sad knife-adjacent man. Pip watched her go, then turned back to the forgery spread across three borrowed tabletops: sample letters, a cracked signet ring bought off a fence in Lowmarsh, pots of ink mixed to match the Choir's distinctive violet-black, and the prophecy book itself, closed and wrapped in oilcloth because some things you didn't leave lying around even among friends.

Well. Among friends and one former betrayer. Pip was still calibrating on Maren.

The safehouse was a disused tannery loft that smelled like forty years of regret and vinegar, but it had good light, a solid table, and a door that locked, which put it three rungs above anywhere else Pip had slept this month. Below, the others moved through their morning routines — Oscar's whetstone, Maren's pacing, Ysolde's low humming as she unpacked the trunk she'd hauled halfway across Greyhollow on a handcart.

The trunk thumped open with a sound like a coffin giving up.

"Right," Ysolde announced to the room at large. "Vestments. Everyone come look terrible in holy clothing."

* * *

Pip had never actually seen a full set of Choir vestments up close. From the library walls, sure — Pip had watched processions through arrow-slits for years, cataloguing the white-and-gold pageantry the way other people catalogued birds. Up close, the thing was absurd. Layers upon layers. An alb, a stole, a chasuble embroidered with sunburst sigils dense enough to stand up on their own, a cincture with seventeen knots that apparently each meant something, and a hood designed, as far as Pip could tell, to make the wearer look like a very solemn candle.

"This is a costume," Pip said flatly, holding up the chasuble. "This is what happens when a religion gets dressed by a curtain shop."

"It is a *liturgical garment*," Ysolde said with the strained patience of a woman who had worn one for twenty years. "Every element signifies. The alb is baptismal purity. The stole is the yoke of the Radiant Word. The seventeen knots represent the seventeen canticles of the Dawnfall."

"What do the eighteen through twentieth knots signify?"

"There are only seventeen."

"Then why did I count eighteen?"

Ysolde took the cincture back and counted, lips moving, and Pip permitted themselves a small, private, extremely goblin satisfaction. It turned out there were eighteen knots; the eighteenth was a repair, added by some long-dead sacristan, and Ysolde had never noticed in twenty years of wearing the thing, which told Pip something important about the Choir's relationship with its own traditions that Pip immediately filed away for future weaponization.

"Now," Ysolde said, recovering, draping the alb over Oscar's shoulders. He stood there, six feet of mercenary swallowed in white linen, looking like he'd been arrested by the concept of holiness. "Walk."

"Walk where?"

"Toward me. Like you belong in it."

Oscar walked toward her like a man approaching a tax audit. Ysolde circled him, tugging hemlines, adjusting the cincture, muttering.

"No. You walk like you're carrying the garment rather than being carried by it. A chorister believes the robe makes him holy. You have to believe it too, just for the length of a checkpoint."

"The robe makes me look ridiculous."

"The robe makes you look *ordained*, which is worse for them. Nothing clears a checkpoint faster than rank. If you act like you outrank whoever's questioning you, half the time they'll apologize for asking." She straightened his stole with two firm tugs. "The other half of the time they'll arrest you. Hence everything Pip is doing on that table."

From the table, without looking up: "I heard my name. Am I being blamed for something?"

"Always," said Maren, from the window.

* * *

By afternoon, the tannery loft had become something Pip didn't quite have a word for.

It wasn't a heist planning session, though there was a heist being planned. It wasn't school, though Ysolde was drilling them with the merciless cheer of someone who had taught novices for two decades before the Church threw her out. It wasn't family dinner, though Maren had somehow produced bread and hard sausage from nowhere and everyone kept drifting over to cut slices and drifting back.

Whatever it was, Pip worked at the edge of it with ink-stained fingers and the growing, unfamiliar suspicion that they were happy.

"—and when the acolyte offers the salutation, you respond—"

"'Light precedes,'" Oscar recited, flat as slate.

"With feeling."

"It's a nonsense greeting."

"It's a *liturgical* greeting, and if you say 'light precedes' like you're reading a laundry list, some bright-eyed novice will notice. Again. Light—"

"*Precedes,*" Oscar said, with sudden, startling conviction, and even Pip looked up, because for a moment he hadn't sounded like Oscar at all. He'd sounded like a man who genuinely believed the sun rose for reasons.

Ysolde blinked. "...Yes. Like that. Where did that come from?"

"Twenty years of listening to priests talk before I killed things for money." He shrugged the alb straight. "It gets in your ears."

"It gets in your ears," Ysolde agreed softly, and there was something old and bruised in her voice, so Pip loudly changed the subject.

"The credentials are nearly done. I need everyone's names as they'll appear, and before anyone asks — no, we're not using our real ones, and no, Maren Duskwell cannot be 'Sister Sandpiper,' I can hear you thinking it."

"It's a good alias," Maren protested from her slice of bread.

"It's a *bird*, and you're the least sandpiper-shaped person alive. You're 'Cantor-Adjunct Vell.' Congratulations on your promotion."

"And him?" Maren nodded at Oscar.

Pip pulled the finished letter from under the weights — the best of the batch, grace-thread harvested from Ysolde's oldest stole and rewoven through fresh vellum in a pattern Pip had reverse-engineered over eleven hours and two minor nervous breakdowns — and slid it across the table. Oscar picked it up, tilted it to the light.

Gold thread caught and gleamed. The violet-black ink declared, in flawless ecclesiastical script, that **Investigator Oren Fair**, bearer of these presents, conducted business of the Inquisition under authority of the Radiant Choir, and was to be afforded every courtesy, etcetera, etcetera, blessed be the dawn.

"Fair," Oscar read. "You gave me my own name with the top shaved off."

"Best forgeries live close to the truth. If someone shouts 'Fairweather' across a courtyard, you won't flinch, because nobody's shouting it." Pip tapped the seal — wax, pressed with the cracked signet, dusted with ash from Ysolde's incense burner to get the exact matte finish the Choir used. "Also, 'Fair' is funny. I thought you could use something funny."

Oscar looked at the letter a moment longer. Something crossed his face that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood.

"Investigator Fair," he said, testing it. "All right. And who did you give yourself?"

Pip drew out their own credential with a flourish. "**Archivist Under-Canon Priddle**. I'm a church bureaucrat of no consequence who exists to carry documents and be ignored. Typecasting."

"You're beautiful," Maren said solemnly, "when you commit to a bit."

"I'm beautiful always. Ysolde?"

Ysolde hesitated. Her hands went still on the vestments.

"...Seraphine will know me," she said finally. "Whatever name I wear. She was my novice for six years. I taught her the salutation. I taught her *why* the cincture has seventeen knots." She glanced down at the repaired eighteenth, and something wry flickered through the grief. "If we cross her path, no name will save us. So call me what you like."

"Priestess Emerita Hymnbook," Pip said gently, and after a beat Ysolde laughed — short, rusty, real — and the whole room loosened a notch, like a knot slipping free.

* * *

The drills went on past dusk.

Ysolde made them process. She actually made them *process*, single file around the tannery's drying frames, Oscar leading with the censer — a battered brass thing Maren had acquired through means she declined to specify — swinging on its chains in slow, liturgical arcs.

"Slower. The censer is not a weapon."

"It's shaped like a weapon."

"Everything is shaped like a weapon to you. *Slower.* The smoke should fall behind you like a thought you've decided not to have."

"What does that even mean?"

"When you've swung a censer ten thousand times, you'll know. Slower."

Maren kept snagging on the turns. Pip kept forgetting to stop walking while reciting. Oscar, infuriatingly, was good at all of it — the walk, the salutation, the posture of unbothered institutional arrogance — and when Pip said so, he got that uncomfortable look he got whenever anyone suggested he might be excellent at something.

"Choir camps used to hire my company for escort work," he said. "Long winters. You watch."

"You *watched* and *absorbed*?" Maren said. "Like some kind of ceremonial sponge?"

"Don't tease the sponge," Pip said. "The sponge is carrying our cover story."

They broke for supper, and supper became the thing Pip would remember afterward, when remembering was the only occupation available. Maren produced more food than seemed possible from her various hiding places — actual cheese, a jar of pickled something-or-other that Pip claimed and defended with a fork — and Ysolde told stories from her Sunspire years, careful ones, funny ones: the Cantor who fell asleep during his own sermon, the sacred flame that went out twice in one winter and had to be secretly relit with a kitchen taper, the novice Seraphine at fifteen, ferociously serious, correcting her teachers' pronunciation in dead tongues.

"She would have hated this," Ysolde said, gesturing at the room — the stolen vestments heaped on a chair, the forged seals, Pip eating pickles directly from the jar. "All of it. Rules were armor to her. She needed them."

"And now she burns books in border towns," Oscar said quietly.

"Now she burns books in border towns," Ysolde agreed. The firelight caught the lines of her face. "Armor rusts same as anything. Sometimes it rusts from inside."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside, Greyhollow muttered and clattered through its evening, ordinary and unaware, and Pip sat in a circle of lamplight with four people — four, counting the space where Seraphine Lark might or might not ever stand — and felt the strange architecture of belonging assemble itself around them, beam by beam, without permission.

Pip had lived in walls their whole life. Walls were safe. You could hear everything through a wall and touch nothing. This was different. This was sitting in the middle of the room.

"So," Pip said, because feelings were for people with better immune systems, "the credentials. Real talk. They'll pass a visual inspection, they'll pass the tilt-test, they'll pass a seal-check. What they won't pass is a registry lookup. The Choir keeps records at every major chapterhouse. Investigator Oren Fair doesn't exist in them yet."

"Yet?" Oscar said.

"Yet." Pip reached under the table and brought up the last piece: a thin copper tube, sealed at both ends, stamped with the Choir's postmark — forged, but forged beautifully, if Pip said so themselves, and Pip did. "Greyhollow has a chapterhouse with a registry office. Registry offices receive dispatches. Dispatches travel in tubes like this one. And sometime tomorrow, a dispatch will arrive from the Sunspire confirming that Investigator Fair and party have been reassigned to border duties and their credentials reissued." Pip smiled, and knew from experience it was the smile that made bigger people nervous. "Because I'm going to write that dispatch tonight, and tomorrow we're going to arrange for it to arrive."

"Arrange how?" Maren asked.

"The way everything arrives everywhere, captain. Smugglers."

Maren grinned slowly. "Now you're talking my language."

"It is a terrible plan," Ysolde said. "It depends on at least four things going right."

"It depends on three things going right and one person being bored," Pip corrected. "Registry clerks are the most bored creatures in creation. Boredom is a door, and I am walking through it."

Oscar turned the forged credential over in his hands one more time, gold thread glinting, and when he spoke, it was with the particular gravity he saved for decisions already made.

"We leave for the mountain roads in two days. Storm season closes the passes after that." He looked around the lamplight, at each of them, lingering half a heartbeat on the empty chair. "Two days to learn the rest of this liturgy. Two days to become people who can walk into the Sunspire and out again."

"Investigator Fair," Pip said, raising the pickle jar in toast, "and associates."

They drank — even Ysolde, even Maren, even Oscar, whose mouth did that almost-smile thing again — and Pip went back to the table to write a lie in holy ink, and found their hands were steady, and their chest was warm, and the wall behind them was just a wall.

* * *

Pip finished the forged dispatch near midnight, when the others had banked the fire and settled into their bedrolls, and was sealing it with the last of the consecrated wax when Ysolde's voice came soft out of the dark.

"You counted the knots deliberately."

Pip didn't pretend otherwise. "I wanted to see if you'd notice."

"Why?"

"Because a teacher who knows her own tradition has been *repaired* is a teacher worth having. And because if you didn't notice, I'd know exactly how much of the Choir is ritual and how much is rot." Pip pressed the seal down, held it, lifted it clean. "For the record, most of it is ritual. The rot's all administrative."

A low laugh in the darkness. Then, quieter: "Sleep, Archivist Under-Canon Priddle. Tomorrow we steal a god's paperwork."

Pip sat alone a while longer in the lamplight, surrounded by lies done well, and told themselves the warmth in their chest was the candle.

It was a lie Pip chose to keep anyway.

And it was somewhere in that small grey hour before dawn, with the seal cooling and the storm building somewhere over Thornfell, that boots sounded on the tannery stair — quick, uneven, deliberate — and a fist struck the door three times, pause, twice, and Maren was up with a knife before anyone else had finished breathing.

"That's not one of ours," she whispered.

Through the door, muffled but unmistakable, a woman's voice said:

"Light precedes. And I need to speak with Ysolde."
