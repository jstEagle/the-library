## 16. The Ascent

The rain started an hour before dawn, and I took it as a good omen. Rain meant the Choir's patrols huddled under eaves and oilcloth, meant scent-hounds worked with their noses full of water, meant even zealots were just cold, miserable people who wanted to be somewhere else. Bad weather was the closest thing to mercy this mountain offered.

"Good omen," Maren said, reading my face the way she always had, like a ledger she'd once balanced herself. "You're smiling at a sky that's trying to drown us."

"I'm smiling because it's trying to drown them first."

She snorted and pulled her hood lower. Three days out of Greyhollow, and we'd already burned through the easy part—the river road, the ferryman Pip had bribed with forged Choir scrip, the long grey trudge through pine country where the only danger was boredom and Ysolde's singing.

We stood now at the tree line, all four of us, looking up at what the maps called the Spine and smugglers called the Staircase: a wall of black rock going up into cloud, cut here and there by gullies that might have been paths if paths were made by goats with grudges. Somewhere above, past the cloud deck, past the checkpoints, past three thousand feet of vertical ice-slicked stone, sat the Sunspire—the Choir's fortress-monastery, white as a held breath against the sky, holding Maren's brother and half a prophecy book between its teeth.

"All right," I said, and shrugged my pack higher. "From here up, we do exactly what I say, when I say it, without discussion."

"Ah," said Pip, from the vicinity of my left boot. "So, nothing like every other day of this journey."

"Pip—"

"No, no, I'm embracing it. Obedience. It's a growth opportunity." The goblin adjusted the strap of their satchel—a satchel that contained, among other crimes, two forged writs of passage, a stolen set of choir-robes in Seraphine Lark's own rank colors, and the water-warped prophecy book wrapped in oilskin like a sleeping infant. "I'm told the Choir values obedience above thought. Which explains so much about the Choir."

Ysolde laughed—a low, surprised sound, like she still hadn't gotten used to her own laugh being allowed. She'd been quiet for most of the morning, eyes on the peaks, lips moving sometimes around fragments of liturgy I was starting to recognize. Prayers, maybe. Or rehearsals. She hadn't been inside a Choir stronghold since they stripped her vestments and put her out, and I could see the old architecture of fear working behind her face.

"Hey." I waited until she looked at me. "They can't hear you coming. That's the whole point of you."

"That's not comforting, Oscar."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true." I pointed up the mountainside, to a notch in the rock that looked like nothing at all unless you'd spent eleven years learning to read terrain the way other people read scripture. "There's our road."

Maren squinted. "That's a cliff."

"That's a cliff *with* a road on it."

"Oscar Fairweather, I have known you since you were a skinny knife-boy who couldn't hold his liquor—"

"And you sold me to the Choir anyway, yes, we've covered it."

"—and that is not a road. Roads go places. That goes up."

"It goes up to the Shepherd's Cut, which crosses the Spine below the third checkpoint, which lets us walk into the Sunspire's shadow without ever touching the pilgrim road or the freight switchbacks or any place where a man with a horn can see us coming." I pulled the oilcloth off my map case—not a map, actually, never a map; the Choir burned cartographers' work when they found it, so everything I knew lived in charcoal sketches and memory—and showed her the line I'd drawn the night before by firelight. "My teacher called it the Widow's Ledger. Because it keeps accounts."

"Charming man, your teacher."

"He died on this road. So did his charm. Move."

* * *

The first hour was scree and misery. The second hour was scree, misery, and hail, small and vicious as thrown gravel, rattling off our hoods while the wind came down the gully in long shoves that tried to take our knees out from under us. I went first, calling holds back over my shoulder—left hand, crack, waist high; don't trust the gray stone, trust the dark; three steps right, then stop and wait for me—and behind me they came, the strangest cargo I'd ever carried up a mountain.

Maren climbed like she fought: fast, economical, furious at the rock for making her work for it. Pip climbed like a rumor, appearing at intervals in places that shouldn't have supported a cat, chattering the whole time because Pip believed silence was for libraries and funerals. And Ysolde—

Ysolde climbed like a penance.

I watched her at the bad traverse, the one where the ledge thinned to a hand's width and the drop went down into cloud, and I saw her freeze. Not the body—the body kept moving, trained into her by years of processions and staircases—but something behind her eyes went somewhere else, some colder cloister, and her breathing went thin and counted.

"Ysolde." I'd come back along the ledge to her, close enough to touch. "Look at me. Not down. Me."

"I'm fine."

"You're reciting the Rite of Ascent under your breath and your knuckles are white enough to see from Greyhollow. Look at me."

Her eyes came up. Rain ran off her chin.

"This ledge has held every smuggler who ever used it, and smugglers are heavier than guilt. It'll hold you. One step. Then another. That's the entire secret of mountains—they're too big to argue with, so you don't. You just take the next step and let the mountain be wrong about you."

"What's it wrong about?"

"Everything." I held out my hand. "Come on, priestess. The mountain thinks you fell years ago. Prove it a liar."

She took my hand. Her grip was stronger than her face let on. We crossed the traverse together, and when solid ground came back under our boots she didn't let go right away—just squeezed once, quick, like sealing a letter—and let go.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't mention it. Literally. If Pip hears a story with me being competent in it, I'll never hear the end of it."

"I HEARD THAT," said the mountain, in Pip's voice, from forty feet above us.

* * *

The Widow's Ledger earned its name at midafternoon, where the path crossed a chute of loose black stone the size of a chapel, funneling down from some broken crag above. Avalanche country. Even in the rain I could smell the wrongness of it—too clean, too raw, the mountain's wound still open.

I stopped the line with a raised fist and crouched, listening. Wind. Water. And underneath, faint, a ticking sort of sound, stones settling against stones, the mountain doing arithmetic.

"We cross light," I said. "One at a time, ten paces apart, no talking. If the slope starts to move, don't run downhill—run sideways, toward that spine of gray rock, and get behind it. Everyone understand?"

Three nods. Good. I sent Maren first because she'd sulk otherwise, then Pip, then Ysolde. I crossed last, as always, because the person who knows the road walks it last—that way nobody gets ahead of what they know.

Pip, naturally, couldn't stand ten paces of silence.

Halfway across the chute—halfway, dead center, the worst possible address—the goblin paused to adjust their satchel, and their boot dislodged a stone the size of a fist. I heard it go, knocking down the slope, picking up friends, and then the whole hillside cleared its throat.

"MOVE!" I roared, and the mountain answered.

It wasn't an avalanche. Avalanches are for snow. This was just several tons of black rock deciding, all at once, that gravity had been patient long enough. I saw Maren reach the spine and throw herself behind it. Saw Ysolde lunge the last few yards and make it. Saw Pip—small, fast, clever Pip—slip on wet stone and go down hard, satchel skidding away, the prophecy book tumbling free in its oilskin, and the rockslide bearing down on both of them like the mountain reaching out a hand.

I didn't think. Thinking was for afterward. I dropped down the slope in four long controlled falls, caught the oilskin bundle one-handed as it passed me—my heart doing something obscene in my chest—and got my other arm around Pip's harness and threw us both sideways, off the fall line, into the shallow lee of a boulder older than the Choir.

The slide went over us like a train. The world became noise and impact and the taste of stone dust. Something the size of my head clipped the boulder a foot above us and exploded. Then it was past, roaring on down into the clouds, and the silence it left behind rang like a struck bell.

"Oscar." Pip's voice, very small, pressed against my shoulder. "Oscar, am I dead? Is this death? It's very loud."

"You're not dead."

"The book—"

"Got it." I held up the oilskin bundle, dust-caked, intact. "You're welcome. Now get off me, you weigh like a bag of hammers."

Pip rolled clear and sat up, gray-faced, dust painting them into something ancient and spectral. They looked at me, and then at the book, and then at me again, and something moved through their expression too fast for me to name.

"You caught the book before you caught me," Pip said quietly.

"I caught both."

"You reached for the book *first.*"

I wiped grit off my face and stood, offering them a hand up. "The book's irreplaceable. You bounce."

But Pip didn't take the joke. They held my gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed, and whatever they saw—or didn't say, and I'd learn later how much Pip didn't say—settled into their face like sediment. "Right," they said finally. "Bounce. Noted. Let's never do that again."

Above us, Maren's voice came down the slope, ragged with something she'd deny to her grave: "Is everyone alive down there, or do I start dividing your possessions?"

* * *

We made the Shepherd's Cut as the light died, a slot canyon threading through the Spine itself, narrow enough in places that we walked sideways with our packs scraping both walls. Overhead, twenty feet up, a blade of sky still held the last bruised purple of evening, and against it I could see—when the wind tore the cloud open—the Sunspire.

It was worse than I remembered. White stone, impossible height, spires like needles sewing the fortress to heaven, windows glowing with that particular warm gold the Choir favored, as if holiness were something you could achieve with good lamplight. From this distance it looked serene. It looked like salvation. I supposed that was the point. Everything the Choir built was designed to look like rescue from exactly far enough away that you couldn't see the bars.

"There it is," Ysolde breathed. Twenty years since she'd seen it last, and her voice cracked on it like ice under a boot.

"Home sweet heresy," Maren muttered.

"How many checkpoints between here and the gates?" Pip asked, unrolling their forged writs across a flat stone with the reverence of a scholar and the nerves of a counterfeiter whose product was about to be tested.

"Two that matter." I traced the route with a finger. "The Gate of Song, on the pilgrim road—we avoid it entirely, come up the drainage channel on the north face instead, which the Choir doesn't watch because it floods twice a year and smells like it. Then the Cloister Door, which is a service entrance for ash-carriers and corpse-handlers. There's a checkpoint there, but it's staffed by lay brothers, not inquisitors. Bored men doing a job they resent. Those are the best guards in the world, if you speak their language."

"Which is?" Maren asked.

"Grief and overtime." I tapped the writs. "Pip's papers say we're a mortuary detail from the valley parishes, bringing up remains for consecration. Nobody wants to inspect a coffin. Inspecting a coffin means thinking about death, and men who think about death at the end of a double shift start asking themselves why they're standing in the cold guarding a door for a god who never answers."

Ysolde flinched at that—just barely, just visibly—and I cursed myself. Old habit, speaking carelessly about the silent god in front of someone who'd lost everything for loving Him wrong.

"I'm sorry," I said.

"Don't be." She pulled her hood up. "You're more right than you know. That resentment you're counting on—it's real. I felt it for twenty years before I understood what it was pointing at. The lay brothers aren't bored, Oscar. They're *hungry.* Every one of them is standing at that door waiting for a voice that never comes, and hating themselves for waiting." Her mouth twisted. "The Choir calls it faith. I call it the loneliest watch in the world."

Pip and Maren exchanged a look over her bowed head—the look of people realizing, again, that the defrocked priestess carried more intelligence in her grief than the Choir carried in its entire archive. I just watched her, and thought, not for the first time, that Veyl's silence had made more saints than the Choir's shouting ever had.

"Then we give them what they're hungry for," I said. "Respect. Tiredness. A shared complaint about the weather. You don't fight a guard, you *join* him, for ninety seconds, and then he waves you through because you've confirmed his whole worldview." I shouldered my pack. "Get some sleep. We move at moonset, climb the north face in the dark, and walk through the Cloister Door as the saddest mortuary crew the Choir has ever seen."

* * *

Moonset came at the coldest hour, the one the old guides called the godless hour, and we climbed.

The north face drainage was exactly as foul as advertised—wet black stone, a trickle of meltwater down the channel's spine, and a smell like a latrine that had ambitions. We went up it roped together, Maren and me trading the lead, Pip wedged in the middle where they couldn't fall more than one body-length at a time, Ysolde climbing with her eyes fixed on the rope ahead and her lips moving in that silent liturgy that had gotten her through the Widow's Ledger and would, I was starting to believe, get her through anything.

Twice, patrol horns sounded from the ramparts above—long notes rolling down the rock, echoing off the walls of our channel until it seemed the mountain itself was being questioned. Both times I pressed us flat into the black wet stone and waited, counting my heartbeat, listening to four people breathe as quietly as four people had ever breathed. Both times the horns moved on, chasing some rumor of the pilgrim road, some ghost of the route we weren't on.

And once—once, near the top, when the channel opened onto a ledge in the Sunspire's very shadow—I heard something else.

Not a horn. Not wind. Just... a pause. A hole in the night's noise, shaped exactly like attention. The same static-silence I'd known for years at my prayers, the silence I'd stopped calling holy and started calling empty. It settled over the ledge like frost, and every hair on my arms stood up, and for one absurd heartbeat I could have sworn the silence was *watching us climb,* the way a father watches from a doorway.

"Oscar." Ysolde's whisper, right at my ear. She'd felt it too. I could tell by her face, pale and lit with something that wasn't fear. "Did you—"

"Yeah," I said. "I did."

Neither of us said what it might mean. There wasn't time, and neither of us quite had the words.

Below us, Greyhollow was a scatter of embers. Above us, the Cloister Door glowed with lamplight, and a bored voice was already calling down the stairs: "Who's there? Detail's not due till prime—identify yourselves!"

I smoothed my face into exhausted grievance, checked that Pip's forged writs were in my outer pocket, and put my hand on the door's iron ring.

"Valley mortuary detail," I called up, in the flat accent of a man whose feet hurt. "With three coffins, brother, and none of them getting lighter. Open the door before we all find religion."

Bolts clattered. Lamplight spilled down the stairs.

And standing at the top of it, lamp raised, wearing the white-and-gold of the Inquisition's highest rank, was a woman I'd hoped never to see again outside of nightmares.

Inquisitor-General Malvane smiled down at us like sunrise.

"Well," she said. "The Choir does receive the most interesting deliveries."
