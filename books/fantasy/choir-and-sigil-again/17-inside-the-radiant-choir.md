## 17. Inside the Radiant Choir

Seraphine Lark had walked the Sunspire's reliquary corridors a thousand times, and she had never once felt watched in them. Tonight she did.

She paused beneath the arched window where the evening bells were still settling into the stone. The Choir's mountain citadel hummed its usual hymn—two hundred voices at vespers, the sound rising through flues and vents until the whole building seemed to breathe devotion. She had been raised inside that breath. It was the first sound she remembered and, she had assumed, it would be the last.

But someone had moved through this corridor who did not belong to the breath. Someone whose footsteps fell wrong—too light, too quick, spaced like a smuggler's count rather than a chorister's procession.

She turned the corner with her hand already on the censer-chain at her belt.

Four figures stood in the shadow of the reliquary arch, dressed in the vestments of minor cantors. Three of them wore the robes badly—the hems wrong, the cowls too deep, one of them with what looked suspiciously like a goblin tail pinned up under the cloth in a way no amount of pinning could disguise. The fourth wore the vestments like she'd been born in them, because she had.

"Sister Ysolde," Seraphine said.

The woman turned. Even after eleven years, even with grey threading her temples and a scar Seraphine didn't remember cutting down her jaw, Ysolde Vane's face was the same face that had taught Seraphine her first canticle. The same patient eyes that had told her, when she was nine years old and weeping over a botched harmony, that the Choir's music was not about being perfect but about being true.

"Hello, little lark," Ysolde said quietly. "You've grown into your name."

The endearment landed like a knife between ribs. Seraphine's hand tightened on the chain. Behind Ysolde, the false cantors went very still—one of them, a broad-shouldered man with a mercenary's stance and an oath-mark visible at his collar, shifted half a step forward as if to put himself between them.

"You're under interdiction," Seraphine said. "Excommunicate. Your name is struck from every litany in the Choir. If I speak it aloud in the nave, the stones themselves are supposed to reject the sound." She heard her own voice and hated how young it sounded. "Eleven years I have not spoken it."

"And yet here you are," Ysolde said, "speaking it."

* * *

The corridor was empty. Vespers would hold everyone for another quarter hour; Seraphine had counted on that herself, once, when she'd been the one sneaking through these halls as a novice, following Sister Ysolde to the sealed archives to read texts that were "for mature faiths only," which had meant: texts that might make you ask questions.

Ysolde had taught her to ask questions. That was the poison of it. That was the wound that would not close.

"Give me one reason," Seraphine said, "why I should not pull this cord." She nodded toward the alarm-rope set into an alcove ten feet away, braided silver, connected to bells that would bring forty zealots of the militant wing down this corridor in under a minute. "One reason, teacher. You used to be able to give me reasons. It was your gift."

"I could give you a reason now," Ysolde said. "It would take longer than a minute."

"Then give me the short version."

"The short version is that the book in that reliquary is a lie." Ysolde's voice stayed level, almost gentle, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "And you already know it is. Don't you, Seraphine? How long have you known?"

The question hit the exact fracture line that had been widening in her since Greyhollow. The doctored translation. The margins scraped clean and rewritten. The way High Cantor Malvane's face had gone smooth and serene when Seraphine asked—who asked?—and Malvane had answered, *the faithful don't ask*, in a tone that had sounded, for one terrible moment, less like doctrine and more like threat.

"That is not my determination to make," Seraphine recited. "Doctrine is discerned by the Conclave—"

"Stop." Ysolde took a step forward, and Seraphine's grip tightened on the chain, and Ysolde stopped, hands open at her sides, showing they were empty. Eleven years ago those hands had adjusted her cowl before her first service. "I'm not going to argue theology with you in a hallway. I'm going to ask you one question, and you're going to answer it honestly or you aren't, and either way I'll know. Are you ready?"

Seraphine said nothing.

"When you reported the location of Fairweather and his companions from Greyhollow," Ysolde said, "you wrote that they had gone east, toward the coast. You knew they'd gone north. Why?"

Behind Ysolde, the mercenary—Fairweather himself, Seraphine realized, the man whose death the prophecies demanded and whom she had been hunting since autumn—went rigid. The goblin made a small strangled sound. The smuggler woman, Duskwell, just watched Seraphine with the flat assessing look of someone counting exits.

Seraphine opened her mouth to deny it. The denial was right there. It was doctrinally perfect, procedurally sound: *a clerical error, Inquisitor Lark misreported coordinates under pressure.* She had even prepared it. She had written it out in her journal three nights ago and burned the page.

"I misread the intelligence," she said.

"No," Ysolde said softly. "You didn't."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, the vespers hymn climbed toward its apex, two hundred voices ascending on a chord of radiant certainty, and Seraphine stood in the cold corridor below it feeling like a diver who had gone too deep and could no longer remember which way the surface was.

"Why?" she asked, and her voice cracked on the word. "Why does it matter so much to you? Why did you let them cast you out rather than recant? Eleven years, Ysolde. Eleven years in the mud and the border towns, teaching dead languages to heretics, and for what? What did you see that I haven't seen?"

"I saw what happens to people who ask questions here," Ysolde said. "I saw a novice—a girl of fifteen, brighter than you, kinder than you—taken into the Conclave chambers for asking why the Rite of Severance requires a death. She came out different. Quiet. Smiling at the correct times. And six months later she volunteered for the Penitent Guard, and the Guard marches into Thornfell, Seraphine. Into the deep places. And none of them come back, and nobody asks why, because asking why got that girl smiling again."

Seraphine's mouth was dry. "That's not—"

"It's not doctrine," Ysolde agreed. "It never was. Doctrine is what they tell you after. The question comes first, always, and the question tonight is simple: the prophecy says the Sigil-Bearer dies on a named date. Your half of the book has been edited to remove the possibility of refusal. Edited by whom? For how many centuries? And what does an institution gain by ensuring that every generation, some poor soul dies on schedule believing it's holy?"

"She's lying," Fairweather said suddenly. His voice was rough, strained. Everyone looked at him. "Ysolde—don't. Not like this. Whatever she decides, make her decide it clean. Don't stack the deck."

Something in Seraphine's chest twisted. She had imagined this man a hundred ways—as fanatic, as fool, as weapon—and in every imagining he had been smaller than this. This man was telling his own teacher not to manipulate the inquisitor sent to kill him. Because he wanted her choice to be real.

"You're him," she said. "Fairweather. The Sigil-Bearer."

"Oscar," he said. "If we're doing names."

* * *

They ended up in the novices' scriptorium, which was empty at this hour and, more importantly, had a door that locked from the inside. Seraphine locked it. She told herself it was to prevent escape. She knew perfectly well it was to buy time.

Duskwell had picked the lock on the reliquary case before anyone finished arguing—of course she had, Seraphine thought numbly, three years of intelligence reports on Maren Duskwell and not one of them mentioned she could defeat a Choir warded case in forty seconds—and now the second prophecy book lay open on the scriptorium table between all of them, next to the copy Seraphine carried in her satchel. Her copy. Her half. The text she had sworn her life to.

Pip—the goblin, the archivist, the creature the Choir classified as *vermin, literate*—bent over both books, long fingers tracing parallel lines. "Here," Pip said. "Same passage. Same hand, same century, same ink batch—I can smell it. Listen." And the goblin read both versions aloud.

Her copy: *"And the Sigil-Bearer shall die upon the appointed day, and his death shall be as the Choir wills."*

The other book: *"And the Sigil-Bearer shall die upon the appointed day, unless the Sigil-Bearer refuses the ink."*

Seven words. Seven words scraped off a page and replaced with four others, and the difference between them was everything.

"Do you understand what this means?" Pip said, looking up at Seraphine with fierce, luminous eyes. "Someone in your Conclave, centuries ago, decided what the prophecy should say and then made it say it. Every martyr. Every Sigil-Bearer before this one. Every 'appointed day.' They weren't fulfilling fate. They were enforcing an edit."

"That's impossible," Seraphine said, but her voice had gone thin. "Alteration of a primary text is—" She stopped. She was going to say *heresy*. She was going to reach for the word the way she reached for all the words that ended thoughts instead of starting them.

"Is exactly what happened," Ysolde said. "I found the scrape marks twenty-two years ago, Seraphine. I brought it to my mentor, and my mentor brought it to hers, and eleven days later I was standing in front of a Conclave tribunal hearing my own students testify against me." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing Seraphine had ever seen. "You were one of the witnesses, little lark. You were twelve. You testified that Sister Ysolde had taught you to doubt. Do you remember?"

Seraphine remembered. She remembered the candlelit chamber, the terrifying kindness of the tribunal, and how they had praised her afterward for her honesty. *A true faith survives questioning,* they had told her. *You have proven yourself.*

They had proven something else entirely. They had proven she could be made to burn the person who taught her to think, and thank them for the match.

"I have to report this," she whispered. It wasn't a decision; it was a reflex, the last convulsion of the person she had been an hour ago. "This is—you're asking me to look at evidence of corruption at the highest level of the Conclave and simply—"

"We're asking you," Oscar said, "to ask who benefits. That's all. Same question Ysolde taught you to ask when you were nine. Who benefits from the edit?"

And there it was. The answer assembled itself in her mind with terrible, orderly clarity, each piece clicking home like a verse resolving to its tonic: An institution that controls the interpretation of prophecy controls the deaths that fulfill it. An institution that manufactures martyrs on schedule manufactures legitimacy on schedule. The Radiant Choir had not been guarding the ward beneath Thornfell for a thousand years. It had been farming it.

"If you report this," Duskwell said, speaking for the first time, her voice like a blade being drawn slowly, "Malvane will kill all of us and bury the books, and you'll get a commendation. Maybe a bishopric. That's the honest math, inquisitor."

"I know the math," Seraphine snapped. Then, quieter: "I know the math."

She looked down at the two books side by side. Her whole life, she had believed the Choir kept the light. That was the literal meaning of the name: radiance against dark, song against silence. She had hunted heretics in that belief. She had burned texts in that belief—including, she now understood, probably copies of the very truth sitting in front of her.

"How many?" she asked Ysolde. "How many Sigil-Bearers died believing?"

"We'll never know," Ysolde said. "The records are kept by the people who did the editing."

Seraphine closed her eyes. When she opened them, she found she had made the decision somewhere in the dark behind her eyelids, the way you find a bruise you don't remember earning.

"The reliquary vault also holds the Severance Blade," she said. All of them went still. "The instrument of the old rite. If Malvane intends to force the prophecy—if Fairweather won't die on schedule—she'll need it. It's warded, it's guarded, and only a ranking inquisitor can sign it out of the vault." She pulled her seal from around her neck and set it on the table between the books, the radiant sigil catching the lamplight. "My credentials will get you to it."

"Seraphine." Ysolde's voice was very careful. "Do you understand what you're doing?"

"I'm asking a question," Seraphine said. "You taught me that the question comes first." She looked around the table—at the defrocked priestess who had never once stopped being her teacher, at the smuggler, at the goblin, at the man fated to die whose death she had chased across three provinces. "Get out of the spire before matins. My seal opens the reliquary vault, the west postern, and the armory gate. After matins, I report the breach myself, and I describe four intruders I never saw clearly."

Pip slid the second prophecy book across the table. "Take it," the goblin said. "You'll need the original if you're going to convince anyone. Or—" Pip hesitated, and something flickered across the small sharp face, too fast to read, gone before Seraphine could name it. "Or maybe you won't need convincing at all."

They gathered their stolen vestments and their stolen truths and moved toward the door. At it, Ysolde paused, and touched Seraphine's cheek the way she had when Seraphine was nine.

"You were the best of them," Ysolde said. "That's why it hurts."

Then they were gone, down the corridor, four shadows dissolving into the spire's vast sleeping dark, and Seraphine Lark stood alone in the scriptorium holding her own seal in her fist and listening to the vespers hymn fade into silence.

She had committed treason. She understood that with perfect calm, the way you understand weather.

What she did not yet understand—what would keep her staring at the ceiling until the bells rang matins—was that the goblin had lied. There was something else in that book, something Pip had seen and folded shut without reading aloud, a passage concerning the appointed day itself.

And tomorrow, when Malvane summoned her at dawn and asked, smiling, why the reliquary vault seal had been signed out overnight, Seraphine would discover that the trap she had sprung for her superiors had teeth pointing inward too.
