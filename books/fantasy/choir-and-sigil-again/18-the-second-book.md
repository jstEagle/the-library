## 18. The Second Book

The reliquary of the Radiant Choir smelled of candlewax and old money.

That was the thing nobody told you about holiness, Oscar thought, pressing himself flat against the cold marble of a pillar while two acolytes shuffled past with a censer: it smelled like a counting house. Incense laid over the top of it, sure, but underneath—the tallow, the gilt, the dust of centuries of donations—holiness here had the same undertone as any smuggler's strongroom. Wealth, guarded. Wealth, defended.

He'd guarded enough strongrooms to know the difference between a lock that wanted to be picked and a lock that wanted you dead. The reliquary door had been the second kind. Pip had spent forty minutes on it, tongue between their teeth, muttering in three dead languages, and when it finally gave, the little goblin had looked almost offended.

*"Cheap,"* they'd whispered. *"The Sunspire's front door is a masterpiece and the reliquary is a merchant's safe with a halo painted on it. They never thought anyone would come down here. Why would they? Everything in here is theirs."*

That was the arrogance of institutions. They built fortresses against enemies and left the treasure room to a padlock, because who would dare?

Oscar would. Oscar had. And now he was inside the belly of the thing that had hunted him for three years, one floor below the offices of Inquisitors, with a stolen choir robe itching at his neck and his oath-mark hidden beneath a borrowed stole.

The acolytes passed. The censer smoke drifted away, sweet and cloying. Oscar counted to thirty and moved.

* * *

The reliquary was a long vaulted hall, three tiers of iron-bound shelves rising into shadow, every niche holding something the Choir had decided belonged to God. Relics of saints. Bones in filigree. Banners from wars Oscar only knew as footnotes. A crown that had once sat on a king the Choir had crowned and later unmade. Gold everywhere, and almost none of it what he was here for.

Pip had the map memorized. Of course they did. They'd spent six years in the Greyhollow library walls reading everything the Choir ever printed, including the internal catalogues that no outsider was supposed to know existed. The catalogue had listed the reliquary's holdings down to the last thimble, and one entry had made Pip go very still and very quiet, which for Pip was louder than screaming.

*Manuscript, prophetic, paired volume. Access: High Cantor only.*

"Third tier," Pip breathed, materializing at Oscar's elbow out of the shadow between shelves. They were wearing an acolyte's robe three sizes too big, sleeves rolled six times, hood pulled low over their ears. The ears were the problem. The ears were always the problem. "Third tier, east wall, behind the reliquary of Saint Vessia. She's the one with the jar of tears. Don't touch the jar, it's allegedly still wet and allegedly cursed, and I don't believe in either of those things but I'm not paid enough to find out."

"We're not paid at all."

"Which is why I'm not paid enough. Move."

They moved together down the east aisle, Ysolde a step behind them, her own borrowed vestments fitting her too well—ten years in the Choir before they cast her out, and the robe remembered her shape. She kept her eyes forward, her hands folded, her breathing in the slow four-count rhythm of choir meditation. If anyone looked at her, they'd see a senior priestess about her devotions. It was the most dangerous disguise in the building and the best one, and Oscar had hated agreeing to it.

The reliquary of Saint Vessia sat in a niche of its own, a silver urn the size of a wine jug on a velvet cushion, flanked by two candles that had apparently been burning since before Oscar was born. Behind it, the shelf back was plain iron.

Pip ran their fingers along the seam, found the catch—a small brass stud, worn smooth—and pressed.

The shelf back swung inward on a whisper of hinges. Behind it: a shallow cabinet, and inside the cabinet, on a stand of black oak, a book.

It was the twin of the one in Oscar's pack. Same dimensions, same cracked leather, same water stain along the spine—no, not a water stain. He leaned closer. A burn. The Greyhollow copy had been water-damaged; this one had been burned and salvaged, its edges charred to a crisp brown that crumbled when Pip's breath touched it.

"Matched pair," Pip whispered. Their voice had gone strange, reverent and furious at once. "I was right. I hate being right, it never leads anywhere good. Look at the binding."

Oscar looked. On the cover, embossed in the same faded silver as his own copy, was the sigil of Veyl—the circle with the vertical silence-cut through its heart—and beneath it, in the old liturgical script Ysolde had spent weeks teaching him to read, a title.

*The Two Deaths of the Sigil-Bearer.*

His copy, the smuggler's cache copy, had been titled *The Book of the Sigil*. Different name. Same hand—he could see it now, the same cramped, careful lettering, the same ink gone brown with age. Someone had written this book twice. Or written it once and copied it, and the copies had been separated on purpose, and one half had spent a century in a collapsed shrine under a mountain while the other half sat behind a saint's tears in the most guarded building in the Sunspire.

"Why hide it *here*?" Oscar murmured. "Why not destroy it?"

"Because you don't destroy your own weapon," Ysolde said quietly. She had come up beside him, and her eyes on the book were old grief and older anger. "You keep it where you can control it. Oscar. Open it."

He opened it.

* * *

The pages were vellum, soft as skin. The first third matched his copy exactly—he checked, pulling his own water-warped book from its oilcloth wrapping and setting them side by side on the shelf, the burned twin and the drowned twin, and the text ran parallel line for line. The prophecy of the Sigil-Bearer. The date. *His* date. The description of the ward beneath Thornfell, the war-god bound beneath it, the death that would decide everything.

Then, two-thirds through, the copies diverged.

In his copy, the passage he'd read a hundred times in the last weeks, the passage that had been read to him by Pip and translated by Ysolde and repeated in his own head every night until it wore a groove in him, went like this:

*And the Sigil-Bearer shall come to the ward in the hour of its fraying, and his death shall be the hinge. If he dies believing, the seal is made whole and the Sleeper sleeps until the sun burns out. If he dies doubting, the seal is broken and the Sleeper wakes to a world unguarded. There is no third road. The ink is written; the ink shall be read.*

He knew it the way you know a scar.

In the Choir's copy, the same passage ran to the same words—*there is no third road*—and then kept going. There was more. A full page more, in the same hand, the same brown ink, that simply did not exist in his copy. Not damaged. Not lost. His copy ended mid-sentence at the bottom of the page and the next page was blank, and he had always assumed water and time had eaten the rest.

The rest was here.

*Yet hear the older word, which the first scribes feared and the later scribes cut: the ink serves the bearer, not the bearer the ink. The Sigil-Bearer was never commanded to die. He was *asked*. And an ask may be refused. The bearer may refuse the ink—may set down the book, unbind the oath, and walk away whole, though the ward shall then fray at its own slow pace, and the Sleeper shall wake not in his hour but in some later, unchosen one. This is the third road. It leads nowhere kind. But it is a road, and it is his.*

Oscar did not move for a long time.

The candles burned. Somewhere above them, muffled through floors of stone, the Choir was singing evening offices, hundreds of voices rising in harmonies engineered to sound like the voice of God. The sound came down through the marble like water through a ceiling, and Oscar stood in a reliquary vault holding a century-old book that had just told him he had never been trapped at all.

"Read it again," Pip said softly. "I know you're going to. So do it properly."

He read it again. The words didn't change. *He was asked. An ask may be refused. He may refuse the ink.*

"Ysolde." His voice came out wrong, scraped. "The copy I found. The Greyhollow copy. It just—ends. Water damage, I thought."

"Water damage." Ysolde's jaw was tight. She reached past him and turned the Choir's copy back a page, then another, checking. Her fingers stopped on a passage near the front, and she went very still. "Look here. The genealogy of the ward. In your copy, this line is garbled—remember? I told you the vellum had rotted, that a phrase was illegible. *'And the god Veyl gave its voice unto the stone, and the stone—'* something, something, illegible."

"I remember."

"It's not illegible." She tapped the Choir's copy with one finger. "Here it says: *'and the stone sang back, and the god fell silent willingly.'* Willingly. The same hand. The same ink. Your copy wasn't rotted, Oscar. It was *cut*. Someone went through your book with a blade and a damp cloth and lifted the ink out of the vellum, page by page, every passage that said the bearer had a choice and the god had a will."

Pip made a sound low in their throat. "The cache copy. The smuggler's copy. Who put it in the shrine?"

"No one knows. It was there when the mountain came down on it." Oscar's mind was moving fast now, ugly and clear. "But someone put it *somewhere* first. Someone decided where it would be found, or didn't care, and someone else—" He stopped. Started again. "The Choir has had this book for a century. They've had the full text. They've always had the full text."

"And they let us believe—" Pip began.

"No." Ysolde's voice cracked like a whip, quiet as it was. "They didn't *let* us. They built it. Think. A prophecy with two endings—believing or doubting—both of which require the bearer's death, both of which can be forced. Kill him while he has faith, the seal holds and the Choir takes the credit and the power of being the church that saved the world. Kill him in despair, the god wakes, and the Choir takes the credit for warning everyone and the power of being the only shield against the end. Either way, the Sigil-Bearer dies, either way the Choir stands on the mountain of it, and the one ending they could never control—the one where he simply *refuses* and walks away—was cut out of every copy they didn't hold."

"The one ending," Oscar said slowly, "where nobody gets to be a martyr. Where nobody gets to be a savior. Where the whole machine just—doesn't get its sacrifice."

"Where the Choir doesn't get its sacrifice," Ysolde said. "Gods, I sat in their choirs for ten years. I sang the liturgy of the Watchful Seal. *Blessed is the death that holds the stone.* We sang it every feast day. I sang it at funerals. I sang it over—" She stopped. Her hand had gone to her own throat, where a defrocking scar sat under the stolen vestments. "They don't want the seal, Oscar. They want the *death*. The seal is the story they tell. The death is the thing itself. A church that can demand your life in the name of the world's survival can demand anything, and who refuses? Who dares? You'd have to be a monster to put your one life above the sleeping god."

"And if the choice was never death at all," Oscar said, "then every martyr they've ever made—"

"Was murdered," Ysolde finished. "By a translation."

The evening song above them swelled into some triumphant verse, and the sound of it came down into the reliquary, and for the first time in his life Oscar heard it for what it was. Not holy. Just loud. A hundred voices singing over the small, quiet truth locked in a cabinet behind a saint's tears.

* * *

They worked fast after that. Fear does that; it sharpens the hands.

Pip copied the restored passages—every divergent line, every cut verse—onto three separate sheets in three separate scripts, in case any of them didn't walk out. Ysolde cross-checked the liturgical grammar twice, because a doctored prophecy was a weapon and she refused to pick up a weapon she hadn't tested. And Oscar stood over the two books, side by side, and felt the shape of his last weeks rearrange themselves around him like furniture in a dark room he'd been navigating by memory.

The ambush at the tollbridge. The zealots hadn't tried to capture him. They'd tried to kill him outright, in the open, with no rite and no witness—sloppy, desperate, *unofficial*. He'd wondered about that for weeks. Now he knew. Someone in the Choir had known the prophecy's hinge was coming and hadn't wanted to risk the careful version. Kill the bearer doubting, kill the bearer believing, kill him in a ditch, it made no difference to the men with swords—only to the men with books. And the men with books had spent a century making sure the world never learned there was a third option, because a third option meant the swords might one day be pointed at *them*.

"Oscar." Pip's voice, very small. "There's one more thing. Last page. I don't—maybe you should sit down."

"I don't sit down in enemy houses."

"Fine. Then grip the shelf."

He gripped the shelf. Pip turned to the final leaf of the Choir's copy, and there, in the same brown ink but a different hand—shakier, later, pressed hard enough to bite the vellum—was a single line of annotation, and beneath it a date.

*The bearer's name is written. The bearer is born. God forgive us for what we must do when he comes of age.*

The date was twenty-six years old.

The year of Oscar's birth.

The reliquary tilted. Or he did. Ysolde's hand landed on his shoulder, steadying, warm through the stolen cloth, and he focused on that—on the pressure of it, on the real weight of a real person—until the floor agreed to stay where it was.

"They've known," he said. "Since before I could walk. Someone wrote that line the year I was born and then they—" He laughed, and it came out with no humor in it at all. "They *waited*. They let me grow up. They let me swear my oath, take my contracts, live my whole stupid life, and the whole time some clerk in this building had my name in a drawer."

"Names get written in drawers all the time," Ysolde said. "What matters is what they could do about it. They couldn't touch you before the hour of fraying—the prophecy binds them too, Oscar. The ward can only be sealed or broken at the fraying, by the bearer, at the bearer's death. Kill you early and you're just a dead mercenary and the ink goes looking for another name. They had to wait for the mountain to start shaking. They had to wait for *tonight*."

"Then they're out of time," Oscar said. "And so are we."

He closed the Choir's copy, wrapped it in the oilcloth beside its drowned twin, and slung both books inside his robe. Two halves of one lie, reunited. Somewhere above them the evening song ended, and in the silence that followed he heard the thing he'd been dreading: footsteps. Many. Moving with purpose. And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, a sound he knew from three years of being hunted—the flat, ringing tone of a Choir alarm bell being rung, not in ceremony, but in earnest.

Pip's ears went flat. "That's not for us. Can't be for us. Nobody knows we're—"

The footsteps stopped. A voice, distorted by stone and distance, shouted something down a stairwell, and a second voice shouted back, and the word that came through was one Oscar knew in four languages.

*Prisoner.*

Ysolde went white. "Maren's brother. He's held on the penitent's floor, that's three levels up, that's directly on our way out—"

"I know," Oscar said.

The alarm bell rang again, closer now, and the reliquary's candles guttered in a draft from a door opening somewhere far above, and Oscar Fairweather—Sigil-Bearer, oath-sworn, twice-prophecied, and for one shining hour the freest man in the Sunspire—turned toward the stairs.

"Change of plan," he said. "We're not leaving without him."
