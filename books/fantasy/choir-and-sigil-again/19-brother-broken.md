## 19. Brother, Broken

Maren Duskwell had climbed a lot of stairs in her life. Stairs in burning warehouses, stairs in collapsing mines, stairs she'd been thrown down by people who regretted it afterward. She had never climbed stairs like these—white stone, each step carved with a verse of the Choir's liturgy, so that every footfall ground her boot sole against scripture.

*Blasphemy as transportation,* she thought. *That's the Sunspire for you.*

Behind her, Oscar moved quiet as spilled shadow. Ysolde kept pace at his shoulder, whispering the ritual courtesies of the penitent floors under her breath—the pass-phrases, the posture of deference, the way you were supposed to keep your eyes lowered and your hands folded like a woman waiting to be forgiven something enormous. Pip brought up the rear with Seraphine's stolen credentials clutched to their chest like a talisman, goblin eyes enormous in the candlelight.

Three years. Three years since Maren had sold Oscar Fairweather to these people, and here she was climbing back into their throat to pull her brother out.

The universe had a sense of humor. It was just a bad one.

"Left at the landing," Ysolde breathed. "The penitents' cells face inward, toward the light shaft. They want them to be able to see the sun."

"Why?" Pip whispered.

"Because they can't reach it," Ysolde said, and didn't explain further.

Maren's hands had gone cold somewhere around the second floor and hadn't warmed up. She flexed them anyway, working feeling into her fingers, because in about ninety seconds she was going to need every one of them. The plan—if you could call three sentences whispered in a reliquary a plan—was simple. Get in, get Tam, get out before the alarm that was already ringing found its way down three levels to where they stood.

The alarm was ringing louder now. Somewhere above them, zealots were discovering an empty reliquary, a broken display case, and a prophecy book missing from the most secure shelf in the Sunspire. Which meant the clock they were racing wasn't a clock anymore. It was a fuse.

*Second cell from the end,* the map in her head said. She'd bribed a laundress two years ago—a laundress who washed the penitents' shifts, who'd mentioned a smuggler's brother kept on the third floor, who talked in his sleep about tide-tables and cargo manifests. Tam. Her Tam, who'd been twenty-two when she left him holding the ledgers in Greyhollow while she went to negotiate with the Choir, and who had been twenty-two when the negotiation turned out to be an ambush aimed at her, and who had been taken instead because he was there and she wasn't.

Twenty-five now. If he was still—

She stopped the thought the way you stop a boat from shipping water: both hands, hard, right now.

"Cell block," Ysolde murmured. "Two guards. Don't look at them. Walk like you belong."

They walked like they belonged. It should have been absurd—Oscar Fairweather in Choir grays two sizes too big across the shoulders, a defrocked priestess leading a smuggler-queen and a goblin archivist down a corridor of white stone—but the Sunspire was built on the assumption that no one would dare walk wrongly through its halls, and so nobody looked twice. Arrogance is a kind of blindness. Maren had built a career on it.

The guards at the cell block door straightened. Ysolde lifted her chin exactly so, said a phrase in Old Radiant that Maren didn't understand but that made both guards' eyes go soft and obedient, and then Oscar's hand came out of nowhere—one motion, fast and clean—and laid them both gently on the floor like a mother putting down sleeping children.

"Chained," he said. Not a question.

"They always are," Ysolde said, already kneeling with a lockpick she'd produced from somewhere in her vestments. "Keys are on the duty belt. And we have maybe four minutes."

Maren took the keys herself.

* * *

The cells faced inward, just as Ysolde had said. A long gallery open to a central shaft of pale mountain light, doors of iron-barred oak, each one marked with a slate: name, crime, days of penitence. Maren walked the row reading slates, and the names blurred past her—heretics, debtors, a woman imprisoned for singing a hymn in the wrong key—until:

**TAMMAS DUSKWELL — OBSTRUCTION OF HOLY INQUIRY — DAY 1,094**

A thousand days. She did the math without wanting to. Nearly three years. He'd been inside this mountain almost exactly as long as she'd been carrying the weight of what she'd done to put him there.

The key fit the lock. Of course it did; everything tonight had gone too smoothly, and some old smuggler's superstition in her gut said *smooth is the sound a trap makes*, but her hands were already turning the iron.

"Tam?"

The figure on the cot didn't move.

He was sitting up, or something like sitting—propped against the wall with his legs folded wrong, staring at the light shaft with the flat patience of a man who has learned that looking at things doesn't change them. He'd been broad once. She could see the ghost of it in his shoulders, in the frame of him. Now he was mostly knuckles and collarbone, and his hair had gone gray at the temples, and he was twenty-five years old.

Maren had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road north. In most versions, he shouted at her. In some, he cried. In one particularly honest version, he hit her, and she deserved it, and she let him.

In none of them did he look at her the way he was looking at her now, which was barely at all—his eyes sliding over her face like she was weather, like she was a draft from a door left open somewhere far away.

"Tammie," she said, and her voice cracked on the old childhood name, and she hated herself for the crack because cracking was a luxury, cracking cost seconds. "It's me. It's Mare. We're leaving."

His mouth moved. No sound came out.

Then, after a long moment, in a voice like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in a thousand days: "They ask the same question."

Maren's stomach dropped through the floor.

"What question?" Ysolde asked softly, from the doorway, because Maren couldn't speak.

Tam's eyes focused—not on Maren, but on the middle distance, on some invisible inquisitor only he could see. "Where is your sister. Where does she keep her caches. Who else knows the passes." His head ticked slightly to one side, a motion that had clearly been beaten into rhythm. "I say I don't know. I don't know anything. I'm just the brother." The tick again. "Then they ask again."

"How often do they ask?" Oscar's voice, low, from behind Maren. Gentle, the way you're gentle near a frightened animal.

Tam considered. "Days used to be between. Then weeks. Now—" the tick, the tick— "they come every day. Since spring. Since the tremors started." Something flickered behind the deadness, a spark of the boy who'd once argued tide-tables with grown harbormasters at fourteen. "Something woke up under Thornfell. They're scared. Scared men ask harder."

Maren crouched in front of him. Up close she could see what the dimness had hidden: the fine white scars on his forearms, the burn mark on his collarbone shaped like a stylized flame—the Choir's sigil, pressed hot. Interrogation marks. A thousand days' worth.

"Tammie," she said. "Look at me."

Slowly, with visible effort, he dragged his gaze to her face. For a heartbeat—just a heartbeat—something lived in his eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or maybe just the animal knowledge of a familiar voice.

"You came," he said. Wondering. Like a child. Like a man who had stopped expecting anyone to come a very long time ago.

"Yeah," Maren said. "Course I did. Now stand up, little brother, because we've got about three minutes and a whole lot of stairs."

He tried. That was the worst part—he tried immediately, no argument, no suspicion, hauling himself upright on legs that shook like a foal's, and Maren got her shoulder under his arm and took his weight and felt how light he was, how *light*, and something in her chest tore along a seam she'd been pretending wasn't there for three years.

"We've got him," she called back. "We're moving."

* * *

They made it nine steps out of the cell block.

Maren would count them later, in the bad hours. Nine steps, Tam leaning on her, Pip darting ahead to check the stairwell, Oscar bringing up the rear with the loose-limbed readiness of a man who expected the world to lunge at him and intended to be disappointed. Nine steps of white stone and scripture underfoot, the light shaft pouring gold down the center of the gallery, and everything almost quiet.

Then a bell began to ring—not the measured alarm of discipline, but the frantic clanging of someone hammering a rope with both fists, and a voice overhead, amplified by the Sunspire's acoustics into something godlike and vast:

*"THE RELIQUARY IS BREACHED. THE HERETICS ARE IN THE SPIRE. SEAL EVERY FLOOR. IN THE RADIANT NAME, SEAL EVERY FLOOR."*

Iron boomed somewhere below them. Then above. Doors dropping, portcullises falling, the great building swallowing itself.

"Stairs," Oscar said calmly. "Now."

They ran. Or rather, everyone else ran, and Maren half-carried her brother, which is a different thing entirely, a slow-motion sprint where every second costs double. Tam's legs worked, technically. They worked the way a rusted winch works. Behind them she heard Ysolde swearing in Old Radiant, using holy language for entirely unholy purposes, and Pip chattering route calculations—"the service shafts don't have gates, the service shafts were built for masons, nobody seals the masons' shafts—"

"The masons' shafts," Oscar said, "are behind us. We came up the pilgrim stairs."

"Then we go *down*," Pip said, wild-eyed, "through the laundry, through the cooling vaults, there's a drainage culvert from the original construction, it's in the foundation plans, I read them last winter for—"

"For fun," Maren gasped. "You read fortress blueprints for fun."

"I contain multitudes!"

A door ahead burst open. Two zealots in white-and-gold poured through with ceremonial blades—beautiful things, useless things, made for altar work and repurposed in panic—and Oscar met them in the space of a single breath. Maren saw maybe half of it: a turn, an elbow, a wrist seized and folded until the blade rang on the floor, and both men folded down the stairs ahead of him, groaning but breathing, because Oscar Fairweather had never once in all the time she'd known him killed anyone who could be left alive.

"Sorry," he told them sincerely, and stepped over their bodies.

"That's *twice*," Maren panted, "that I've seen you apologize to people you just beat unconscious."

"It's polite."

"It's *insane*."

"It's both," said Ysolde, shoving past them both.

They plunged down through the building's guts—laundry halls full of steam and startled novices who took one look and hid, cooling vaults where the Sunspire stored its ice, corridors narrowing from cathedral grandeur to working-stone utility. Pip led now, small and quick, reading the walls like text, and behind them the clanging bells multiplied, and boots thundered on floors they'd already left, and once a crossbow bolt buried itself in the wall a hand's width from Tam's head, and Tam didn't even flinch, and Maren flinched for the both of them so hard she nearly dropped him.

Then the culvert. A black mouth in the foundation stone, breathing cold air, running water somewhere below.

Pip produced a lantern from their pack—of course they had a lantern, of course they had rope and chalk and a backup set of picks—and lit it, and the light showed a channel wide enough for a stooped man, sloping down into the mountain's roots.

"That leads to the cliff face?" Oscar asked.

"It leads *down*," Pip said. "Everything down here leads down. That's sort of the problem with mountains." A beat. "But it comes out above the tree line on the north side. Eventually. Probably. The blueprint was torn."

"'Probably,'" Maren repeated. "'Torn.' Wonderful. Into the probably-torn hole, everyone."

Tam made a sound. Small, rusty, unmistakable.

Everyone turned. He was looking at the dark culvert, and then at the light behind them, and his face—for the first time since the cell—was doing something complicated.

"I know that dark," he said. His voice had a crack in it, a live wire under the deadness. "Under Thornfell. They—when they wanted to soften me, early on, they put me in a dark cell over the deep places. And the deep places *talked*." His hand closed on Maren's sleeve, gripping hard enough to hurt, gripping like drowning. "Not out loud. Inside. Something down there is *awake*, Mare, and it knows your name, it knew all our names, it said yours first—"

The bells above them crashed on. Boots. Shouting. The Choir closing floor by floor.

And below, in the culvert's cold breath, Maren heard—or thought she heard, or dreamed she heard—something vast turning over in its sleep, and a whisper threaded through the silence like a needle through cloth:

*...fairweather...*

Oscar went very still.

"Down," he said quietly. "It wants us down anyway. Let's not keep it waiting."

Maren pulled her brother into the dark.
