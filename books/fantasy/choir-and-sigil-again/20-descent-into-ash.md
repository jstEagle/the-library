## 20. Descent into Ash

The alarm bells followed us down three levels of the Sunspire before the mountain swallowed their sound.

We ran the way you run when stopping means dying—Maren half-carrying her brother, whose feet kept forgetting they were feet; Pip clutched against my back like a burr, arms locked around my neck, the stolen prophecy book jammed inside my coat where it beat against my ribs like a second heart. Seraphine brought up the rear, white robe exchanged for smuggler's grey, her inquisitor's seal left somewhere behind us in a reliquary that was, by now, surrounded by a great many angry men with fire-pikes.

"Left," I said, and we went left, because I'd spent two days memorizing the Sunspire's service ways and because left was away from the shouting.

"Left is a wall," Maren panted.

"Left is a wall with a door in it. Old smuggler's trick. The Choir builds walls; the clans build doors." I found the seam by touch, cold iron set flush into stone, and hauled. It opened on darkness and a wind that smelled of deep places—wet rock, old mineral, something older underneath all that. Something sulfurous.

Pip's grip tightened on my neck. "Oscar."

"I smell it."

"What *is* it?"

"Honestly? I was hoping sulfur."

Behind us, above us, the alarm bells finally died entirely, replaced by something worse: silence, and then a sound I had no name for. A low groan, structural and vast, as if the Sunspire itself had shifted its weight.

Seraphine caught up to me at the doorframe, her breath ragged. "The tunnels under the Sunspire," she said, "go down."

"They go through," I corrected. "There's a difference."

"They go *through* what?"

I didn't answer that. I lit the first lamp instead, and the flame bent sideways, drawn toward the dark like it wanted to leave us.

* * *

The first hour was smuggler country: rough-cut galleries, rope anchors rusted but holding, the kind of passages Maren's people had used since before the Choir owned the peaks. Maren knew them. That was the only reason we made time—she'd go ahead, fingers on the stone, reading the rock the way Pip read dead languages, and call back, "Clear," or "Collapse, right side, duck," or once, just once, "Don't touch that, it bites."

Her brother Tam walked between us. He'd been carried for the first hour and then, somewhere around the third gallery, had insisted on walking, and there was something in the way he put one foot after the other—deliberate, careful, like a man relearning an argument he'd lost—that made it impossible to refuse him. He hadn't spoken. Not since we cut his bonds. His eyes tracked everything: the lamps, our faces, the ceiling, always the ceiling.

"He'll come back to himself," Maren said quietly, when we paused at a fork to drink from her waterskin. She wasn't looking at him when she said it. She was looking at me, and asking.

"The mind's a tough thing," I said. "Tougher than the body. It bends before it breaks."

"You'd know."

"I'd know."

She passed me the waterskin. Three years ago she'd have poisoned it rather than share it, or so I'd told myself every night for a year after she sold me out. Now she was reading stone for me in the dark under a mountain, and neither of us had said anything about it, and I was starting to understand that this was how forgiveness actually worked—not a conversation, not a scene, just a waterskin passed hand to hand in a place where either of us could have let the other die and didn't.

Pip caught up beside me, short legs churning. The goblin had the book out now, walking and reading by lamp-glow, which was a good way to break your skull open on a low lintel and which I told them so.

"The original line," Pip said, ignoring me, which was also how forgiveness worked with Pip. "'He may refuse the ink.' I keep going over it. Refusal isn't in the grammar of any other prophecy I've ever catalogued. Doctrinal prophecy is closed—sealed futures, binary outcomes. This is *open*. Someone wrote a door into it on purpose."

"Veyl," I said.

"Or whoever translated it honestly, before the Choir got hold of it." Pip ducked under the lintel I'd warned about, barely. "A god who writes exits into its own prison-prophecy. That's not doom, Oscar. That's a god leaving a note under the door."

I thought about the silence I'd carried for eleven years. The oath-mark on my forearm, cool as riverstone even now, even here. *Leaving a note under the door.* It sounded like something my god would do. It sounded like everything my god had never been allowed to say.

Then the floor tilted.

Not much. A degree, maybe two, felt more than seen—the lamp flames swung together, and loose scree whispered across the gallery floor, all of it sliding the same direction. Down. Deeper.

Tam stopped walking. He looked at the sliding stones, and then at me, and for the first time since the Sunspire he spoke.

"It knows you're coming," he said.

* * *

We made camp in a gallery where the air still moved, because Ysolde had taught me that much—dead air means dead ends and worse things. Seraphine built the fire small, almost apologetic, the way someone who'd spent years burning books built fires: carefully, and with a certain amount of guilt.

Tam slept, finally, curled against Maren with his back to the flame. In sleep his face was younger and worse—you could see the shape of what the Sunspire's questioners had made of him, the architecture of it. Maren watched him breathe with an expression I'd only ever seen her wear over ledger books, tallying something she couldn't afford.

"So," she said, without preamble. "These tunnels."

"Mm."

"They're not going out, are they. Through the mountain." She looked at me. "You checked the maps. Pip checked the maps. We're going *down*."

I fed a stick to the fire. "The smuggler roads exit on the north face. There's a collapsed gallery between us and them that wasn't collapsed on any map I've seen. Pip thinks—"

"Pip thinks the mountain collapsed it on purpose." Maren rubbed her eyes. "Say it plain, Oscar. You've never once said anything plain in your life."

"All right. Plainly: every route out of here bends toward Thornfell's roots. Every single one. I've mapped it four times tonight. It's not geology. Geology doesn't steer." I met her eyes. "Something down there wants us walking toward it, and we don't have the food or the light to argue."

"And Ashkarath is down there."

"Ashkarath is down there."

She nodded slowly, absorbing it, and then did the thing that Maren Duskwell did instead of despairing: she started counting. "Four lamps. Two days of food if we starve ration. One defrocked priestess—" she nodded at Seraphine, who raised two fingers in a lazy salute— "one apostate inquisitor, one archivist, one broken brother, one mercenary with a god-shaped hole in his arm. And beneath us, a war-god with a century of patience and a grudge."

"When you put it that way it sounds bad."

"When you put it any way it sounds bad." She lay back, pulling her cloak over Tam's legs. "Wake me for my watch."

* * *

It was during Seraphine's watch that the singing started.

I came awake all at once, hand on knife, and found her sitting very still by the banked fire, head tilted, listening. Pip was awake too, hunched over the book, and Ysolde—Ysolde stood at the gallery's far edge, facing the dark, her lips moving.

"Do you hear it?" Seraphine asked me. Her voice was strange. Stripped.

"Hear what?"

And then I did. Not sound, exactly. It came up through my boots, through the oath-mark, through whatever Veyl had made of me: a pressure, rhythmic, patient. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Like a hymn sung so low it became geology.

"It's not singing," Ysolde said, without turning around. "It's counting. It's been counting for a hundred years. And we just crossed a threshold it was counting toward." She turned, and her face in the firelight was calm in a way that frightened me more than panic would have. "This is the old processional way, Oscar. Pilgrims used it. Before the Choir, before the sealing—when Ashkarath's worship was lawful, they came down these same galleries to make offerings at the ward. The mountain remembers the road. It's leading us the way it led them."

"That's not comforting."

"I didn't say it was comforting. I said it was a road." She crouched, took chalk from her sleeve, and drew a small mark on the stone—a circle, quartered, with a gap in one quarter. "But roads can be walked with intent. If we must go down—and we must—then we go down as pilgrims, not as meat. There's a difference. Meat gets eaten. Pilgrims get answered."

Pip looked up from the book. "What's the difference, ritually speaking?"

"Consent," Ysolde said simply. "Ashkarath has power over the unwilling. Fear is a door. But a pilgrim walks toward the thing willingly, and willingness closes doors." She looked at me, and her eyes were steady. "You taught the Choir that once, before they burned the lesson. You of all people should remember, Seraphine."

Seraphine's jaw worked. "I burned the lesson," she said. "Personally. Three times."

"Well," said Ysolde, "you're here to unburn it. That's more than most manage."

* * *

The next day—we called it day; the lamps said nothing either way—the tunnels changed.

The smuggler's rough work gave way to something ancient. Walls of fitted black stone, seamless, veined with lines of pale crystal that caught our lamplight and held it a moment too long before letting go. The processional way, wide enough for ten abreast, sloping always down. And along the walls, at intervals, niches—empty now, worn smooth, each one the size of a kneeling person.

"Offering niches," Seraphine murmured. She ran her fingers along one and pulled them back glistening, as though the stone sweated. "Choir doctrine says the old cults made blood offerings here. Live sacrifices, walked down this road and never walked back up."

"Choir doctrine says a lot of things," Ysolde said.

"Some of them are true."

"All the more reason to walk like pilgrims."

Tam stopped at every niche. He examined each one carefully, thoroughly, the way a man checks the locks on a door. Then at the fiftieth or sixtieth he turned to Maren and said, in a voice like dust, "They weren't sacrifices."

Everyone stopped.

"They were volunteers," Tam said. His eyes were clear, terribly clear, the clarity of a man whose mind has been taken apart and reassembled by experts and therefore sees the mechanism of all minds. "That's what they don't want anyone to know. I heard it in the Sunspire, in the questions they asked me—they weren't really asking about you, sister. They were asking about the ward. The offerings weren't dragged down here screaming. They walked. Singing. Because the ward doesn't feed on death." His voice dropped. "It feeds on *given* things. Given deaths. Given lives. Given oaths. That's why it's failing. Nobody gives anymore. Everybody hoards."

The tunnel was very quiet. The crystal veins pulsed faintly, in rhythm, like something breathing in its sleep.

Maren took her brother's hand. "Tam."

"It asked me, in the Sunspire," he said. "Every day it asked me. Whether I would give. And I never answered, and that's why they couldn't use me, and that's why I'm alive." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen. "Refusal is a kind of answer too. Your book is right, little archivist. You can refuse the ink. But nobody's ever refused it *all the way down.*"

He started walking again, and we followed, because there was nowhere else to go, and because none of us could think of a single word to say.

* * *

On what Pip insisted was the third day, the processional way ended.

It ended at a doorway—not carved, grown, the black stone flowing into an arch that framed pure darkness, and set into the arch was a sigil. I knew it the way you know your own name. It was my oath-mark, writ large, a hundred feet high, the quartered circle with the gap—but the gap was wider than Ysolde's chalk drawing had shown it. Wider than it should be. Cracks ran out from it like frost on glass, and through the cracks came warmth. Not heat, exactly. Warmth, the warmth of a living thing, enormous and slow and pressed close against the other side.

Pip made a small sound. Seraphine swore in High Liturgical, which apparently she'd known all along. Maren put her hand on her knife, which was useless, and which she clearly needed to do.

Ysolde looked up at the sigil for a long moment, and then at me, and her expression was the one I imagined she wore when she'd first heard Veyl's silence as holy: recognition, and grief, and a strange fierce joy.

"It's fraying faster," she said. "When we started down it was cracked. Now it's torn. Whatever the Choir did in the Sunspire—whatever Malvane is doing up there right now—it's accelerating. Oscar." She caught my arm. "How long do we have? Honestly."

I reached out and laid my palm flat against the arch, against my own mark scaled up beyond all sense, and the oath-mark on my forearm answered—pulled, faintly, like a compass needle finding north, or a dog hearing a whistle no one else can hear. And through my hand, for just a moment, I felt the shape of what waited below: vast, chained, patient, *awake*, and dreaming of my name.

"Less than we need," I said. "More than none."

Below us, the stairs went down into the warm dark, and the dark breathed, and I understood that we had stopped fleeing somewhere in the last three days without noticing. Fleeing implies somewhere to flee *to*. There was no out anymore, no north face, no smuggler road, no horizon. There was only down, and what waited down, and the five of us and our small lights, walking toward it on a road built for exactly this.

"We rest here," I said. "Full lamps, full meal, everyone sleeps. Tomorrow we meet a god."

Maren snorted softly, checking Tam's bandages. "And what do we bring a war-god? Bread and salt?"

I looked at the sigil, at the widening gap, at the cracks spreading through a seal that a silent god had bled a century of voice to hold. I thought about given things. Given deaths. Given lives. Given oaths.

"No," I said. "I think we bring it the one thing it's ever actually wanted."

"Which is?"

I didn't answer. I didn't know yet—not fully, not in words. But the mark on my arm was warm, and the dark below was warm, and somewhere in the space between the two, an idea was forming with the slow inevitability of water finding its level.

Above us, far above, muffled through a mile of stone, the mountain groaned again—longer this time, and lower, and at the end of it, unmistakable, came a sound none of us will ever forget: a single note, deep and vast, like the first syllable of a word a hundred years long finally being spoken.

Pip looked at me, eyes huge in the lamplight.

"It's starting to say your name," they whispered.
