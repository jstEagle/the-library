## 21. The Chained God's Court

I have been dreaming for a hundred years, and my dreams are the only country I still rule.

They think I sleep beneath Thornfell the way a sword sleeps in its sheath—quiet, patient, waiting for a hand. They are wrong. There is no hand coming. There is no army at the gate, no priest chanting the old words of my rising. There is only stone, and the weight of a mountain laid over me like a grave-cloth, and the ward above me humming its one long note of silence, and me, beneath all of it, dreaming.

But dreams are doors. Dreams have always been doors. Even chained, even buried, even reduced to a whisper in the marrow of the world, I am a god, and a god's hunger does not atrophy. It sharpens.

Four of them are coming down toward me. I felt them the moment they entered my mountain—the smuggler woman with her debts like knives in her boots, the priestess with her unfinished song, the little archivist with a stolen truth folded up inside her chest like a letter never sent, and the man. The Sigil-Bearer. The ink that walks.

They flee the Sunspire, and they think they are fleeing *away* from something. Fools. Every tunnel in Thornfell bends toward me the way all rivers bend toward the sea. They are not running from the Choir. They are running to me. They simply haven't been told yet.

So I open the doors of my court, and I invite them in.

Not with fire. Fire is for gods with armies. I invite them with the oldest magic there is, the one that needs no ward and breaks no oath:

I show them what they want.

* * *

The smuggler first. Maren Duskwell, who walks like a woman who has already calculated the price of her own death and found it acceptable.

I give her a harbor.

Not Greyhollow's frozen quay with its customs men and its Choir informants—a true harbor, southern, sun-warm, masts thick as a forest, and every ship flying her colors. In the dream she stands on her own deck, and her brother Tam stands at the rail beside her, whole, unbroken, laughing at some joke only the two of them share, the way they laughed before the Choir took him, before the Sunspire, before she sold a friend to buy his safety and learned that the price never stops being paid.

"You could have this," I tell her, in a voice I shape from her father's, dead twenty years, dredged up from the sediment of her memory like a coin from a wreck. "One word from you. One small surrender. Wake the mountain, and no Choir inquisition will ever hold anything over you again, because there will be no Choir. No Sunspire. No one left to bargain with."

Maren looks at the harbor. She looks a long time. I have broken harder souls with softer dreams.

Then she says, in her father's voice I am wearing, in a voice that is entirely her own: "Tam wouldn't laugh like that. Not after what it cost."

And she turns her back on the sun.

Ah. But I am patient. I have been patient for a century. I fold the harbor away like a chart and turn to the next.

* * *

The priestess. Ysolde, defrocked, disgraced, exiled for the crime of listening to a silence and calling it holy.

She is the easiest and the hardest. Easiest, because her want is enormous—she has spent fifteen years starving at a table set for a god. Hardest, because her want is not for herself, and wants that are not for oneself do not bend. They only break.

So I do not offer her a god's voice. Not at first. I offer her the thing beneath the want.

I give her the choir loft of the Sunspire, the morning of her ordination, fifteen years ago, before the trial, before the stripping of the vestments, before the road. She stands in the dream in white and gold, and the great organ breathes beneath her, and the cantors rise to sing, and I let her stand there in the moment before everything, the moment she has revisited every night since like a tongue returning to a broken tooth.

"You can have it back," I say, and this time I use no borrowed voice. I use the voice of Veyl. I have worn that silence like a mask for a hundred years; I know its shape better than its owner does, now. "Not the Choir's counterfeit. The true liturgy. The old tongue. The rites the Church burned. Come to me, and I will teach you everything they erased. You will be the first priest of the waking world in a century. Every exile, every outcast, every one they cast out for hearing what you heard—they will come to *you*."

Her hands, in the dream, are trembling. I have her. I am certain I have her.

"Everything they erased," she repeats.

"Everything."

She is quiet a long moment. Then: "And what will it cost the man with the oath-mark?"

There it is. The flaw in the offering, the crack I cannot seal, because she will not look at a gift without asking who pays for it. It is the priestess's disease. It is also, I note with something like respect, the reason she was worth defrocking.

"Costs are for merchants," I say.

"Then you're not a god," Ysolde says, "you're a merchant with better vestments," and the choir loft dissolves around her like sugar in rain, and she walks out of my dream with her chin up and her song still unfinished, and I add her name to the small list of those who have refused me and felt the mountain tremble with my displeasure.

Two down. Two remain.

* * *

The mercenary I save for last, because the mercenary is the prize. The ink that walks. The hinge on which the ward swings.

But first—the little one.

The goblin. Pip.

I almost do not bother. Archivists are dust-eaters, footnote-fondlers, creatures of marginalia. What could a goblin archivist want that I could give? Gold? I have no gold. Freedom? I have none to spare.

Then I look closer, the way I have looked into ten thousand hearts from the bottom of the world, and I see it, and I nearly laugh hard enough to crack the ward myself.

The goblin wants to *matter*.

Not fame. Not glory. Something older and hungrier than either. Pip has spent a life in the walls of other people's institutions—cataloguing other people's books, footnoting other people's greatness, knowing things and never being asked. And now Pip carries the greatest secret in the world, has carried it since a water-damaged book gave up its grammar in a Greyhollow library, and has told no one.

Yes. I know. I have been listening to the walls of their camp for six days. I know what the little archivist read in the second book and did not say. I know that the prophecy's cruelest line was softened in translation, that the word the Choir struck out and the word my own cultists mistranscribed was *may*—*the Sigil-Bearer may refuse the ink*—and I know that Pip read it, understood it, and folded it away like a letter never sent.

I do not know why. Curiosity, in a god, is indistinguishable from appetite. I intend to find out. And so I build the dream precisely, carefully, the way a siege-engineer builds a breach.

I give Pip the library.

Not Greyhollow's cramped archive with its damp and its rats and its head librarian who calls goblins "it." The Library. The one from the oldest stories, the one every archivist secretly believes in the way sailors believe in the far shore—the library where every book that was ever burned is shelved, where every censored line stands restored, where the complete works of the dead wait in perfect order, and where, on a brass plaque above the reading room door, in letters of gold:

*Pip Underbough, First Archivist.*

A chair with Pip's name on it. Assistants who ask Pip's opinion. A lifetime—no, an *afterlife*—of mattering. Of being the one who knew. Of the footnote becoming the text.

"Everything you ever found," I whisper in the voice of Pip's first teacher, the old archivist who died believing goblins couldn't be trained and was wrong, "every truth you ever carried alone—here, it counts. Here, it's worth something. Here, *you're* worth something. You only have to want it. You only have to stop fighting me. Let the mountain wake. A new age needs a new library, and every age's library is built on the ashes of the last."

Pip stands in the great reading room, small and green and trembling, and I watch the want move through them like a tide.

And here is what I did not anticipate—here is the flaw in my siege-engine, the stone I mis-cut:

Pip looks at the brass plaque. And Pip's face does something I have not seen in a century of dreaming. It does not soften with longing. It *hardens*.

"That's not my name," Pip says.

I pause. "It is the name you were given."

"It's the name I was *left*." Pip's voice is small and does not waver. "Underbough. The family that found me in a drain and fed me until I was useful and then lost me in a filing error and never came looking. I kept the name because names are records, and I don't falsify records." Pip looks up at the golden letters, and something in that look is more dangerous than any sword the mercenary carries. "But that's not who I am. I don't know who I am yet. I was going to figure it out *after*."

"After what?"

"After I tell them." And Pip's eyes—black, bright, terrified—come open in the waking world, and the library burns away like fog, and the little archivist sits bolt upright on the cold tunnel floor of my mountain, three miles of stone above my prison, and begins, very quietly, to cry.

I have broken kings in this court. I have unmade saints. I have turned generals' dreams into slaughterhouses and lovers' dreams into graves. And a goblin archivist has just refused me over a *name*.

I should be furious. I find, instead, that I am—interested. It has been so long since anything interested me.

* * *

And then there is the fourth. The ink that walks. Oscar Fairweather, sworn to the silent god, marked by an oath he no longer understands, carrying a death sentence written a century before his birth.

I have been saving him. I have been *savoring* him, the way a starving man saves the last food. Because Oscar Fairweather is not like the others. The others have wants I can hold up a mirror to. His want is a wound, and wounds are doors that never close all the way.

He dreams of the day he took the oath.

I give it to him whole: the shrine of Veyl in the border hills, the incense, the kneeling, the old priest's thumb pressing the sigil into the skin over his heart. He was nineteen. His mother was three years dead. His father's debts had sold him twice before he sold himself. And the silence of Veyl came down over him like snowfall, and he called it peace, because he had never had any, and did not know the difference.

"Here is what I offer," I say, and for him I wear no borrowed voice at all. I wear my own—the voice of the war-god, the voice that once made mountains kneel, the voice that has not spoken aloud in a hundred years and has grown strange and beautiful and terrible in the dark. "Not power. You've seen power offered and refused it in others' dreams; you'd refuse it in your own. I offer you *truth*."

The shrine in the dream goes dark. The incense smoke hangs motionless.

"Your god did not abandon you, Oscar Fairweather. Your god did not fall silent because it was distant, or dead, or never there at all. Your god is *here*. Beneath your feet. Chained beside me, drained into the ward above me, its voice spent a century ago buying the silence you have been praying into ever since." I let the truth out slowly, the way a torturer turns the screw a quarter-turn at a time. "You have been praying to a god that cannot hear you. You have sworn your life to a silence. And when you die—believing or doubting, it does not matter which—the ward fails, and everything your god spent itself to buy is spent for nothing. Unless."

"Unless," he says. In the dream his voice is flat. Mercenary-flat. I know that flatness. It is the sound of a man listening very hard while showing nothing.

"Unless you wake me. I can break this chain. I can tear the ward open from below. And I can give your god back its voice—not the drained, dying whisper in the stone, but *me*. I am a god of war, little Sigil-Bearer, but I was a god of oaths before I was a god of war. Every vow ever sworn in fire was sworn to me first. Your oath-mark—I can make it sing. I can make your silence into a shout. You will never be alone in your own head again. You will never kneel to nothing again. You will *matter*, and your god will live again, and the only cost is a mountain and the people who are about to die in the pass above you, who will die anyway, who are already dead, everyone is already dead, you know this, you have buried enough of them—"

He is quiet for a long time.

And then Oscar Fairweather, the ink that walks, the man I have spent a century waiting for, looks at me in the ruins of his own remembered shrine and says the one thing I did not prepare for.

"You're scared."

The dream goes very still.

"You're offering me everything," he says slowly, "to everyone, all at once, and you're doing it *fast*, before I can think, because you're scared. A god doesn't beg, Ashkarath. Whatever's down here with you—" and I feel him understand it, feel the realization move through him like cold water, "—it's not just the ward. You're chained to something too. And you need me to open the door before it gets here."

I strike the dream down. I strike it down the way a man slams a door, and in the tunnels above me, Oscar Fairweather wakes with his hand pressed flat over his oath-mark, heart hammering, staring into the dark.

* * *

They are all awake now. All four, in the deep ways beneath Thornfell, sitting up in the black with the tremors rolling through the stone like a slow heartbeat, each of them certain the others are still asleep, each of them hiding the shape of what they saw.

I can feel them from below. I can feel the smuggler's hand on her knife, and the priestess's lips moving soundlessly through the liturgy, and the mercenary's silence, which has always been his native tongue.

And I can feel the goblin.

Pip is not hiding. Pip is sitting with their back against the tunnel wall, knees drawn up, and I can feel the thought turning over and over in that small fierce skull like a coin being flipped: *tell them, don't tell them, tell them, don't tell them.* I can feel the library dream still clinging to them like smoke. I can feel the brass plaque with the golden letters, and the terrible arithmetic of a life spent in the margins, and the fear—not of me, never of me, I am only a god—but the fear that the truth in their chest is the only thing that has ever made them worth a chair at anyone's table, and that to give it up is to become a footnote again.

They nearly had it, in my court. They nearly knelt. I saw their knees begin to bend.

And now, above me, in the dark, I feel them stand.

I feel them cross the tunnel—three steps, four—and I feel them stop at the edge of the dying fire where the others sleep, and I feel the words rise in them like water rising in a flooded shaft.

I do not know what they will say. For the first time in a hundred years, I do not know.

And beneath the mountain, in my court of chains and dreams, the war-god Ashkarath leans forward on his buried throne to listen.
