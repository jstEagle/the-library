## 22. What Pip Carries

I have rehearsed this confession four hundred and eleven times.

I know because I count things when I'm frightened. Steps. Words. Seconds between lightning and thunder. And for the last three days, walking through the deep dark of Thornfell with a war-god's dream pressing up through the stone like heat through a floor, I have counted every version of the speech I'm about to give, and none of them survive contact with their actual faces.

Version one hundred and six: lead with the technical details, bury the betrayal in the footnotes. *The grammar of the second clause is ambiguous, technically, in the original Low Vellish, and—* No. Ysolde would stop me at the first subordinate clause and Maren would stop me at the first lie and Oscar would stop me at nothing, which is worse, because he would just look at me with those tired grey eyes and wait, and Oscar's waiting is a thing that could crack a vault.

Version two hundred and thirty-three: blame the translation. *The scribe's hand is genuinely terrible, the descenders on the letter thorn look like—* No. I am an archivist. The whole point of me is that I do not lie about texts. It's the one law I have. It's the only thing I have.

Version four hundred and eleven, the one I'm walking with now, is the shortest one, and the truest one, and the one that makes my hands shake so badly I have to shove them in my pockets:

*I knew. Since Greyhollow. I knew, and I said nothing.*

* * *

Here is what happened in the library walls, the night I decoded the book, and I am going to tell it to you the way I will have to tell it to them, in order, no footnotes, no hiding in the grammar.

Oscar brought me a water-ruined prophecy book that smelled like a drowned church, and I was so happy I could have died. Do you understand? A first-edition Vellish liturgical codex, pre-Choir, pre-censorship, the kind of thing that exists in maybe six copies in the whole world, and it was in my hands, in my walls, in my library, and nobody was going to take it away from me. I worked on it for nine hours without eating. I reconstructed the water-blurred passages by cross-referencing the marginalia against the Canticle of Chains, which is banned, which is why I have the only annotated copy, because I stole it, because that's what archivists in occupied countries do, we steal the truth and sit on it like dragons.

And at about the fourth hour, I found the line.

*The Sigil-Bearer may refuse the ink.*

Five words. Five small words in a dead language, sitting right there in the middle of the passage about believing and doubting, sealing and shattering, the great binary that the whole world was going to break itself against. The original text doesn't present a binary. It presents a binary *and then an exit*. A door marked in the oldest, plainest Vellish there is, the linguistic equivalent of a hand-painted sign on a tavern door: REFUSE THE INK. It's not even hidden. It's not in cipher. It's just there, and the Choir had spent a century sanding it out of every copy they could reach, and I found the unsanded one, and I read it, and I understood exactly what it meant.

It meant Oscar Fairweather did not have to die. Not believing, not doubting. He could refuse. There was a third path, written down, a hundred years old, and I was holding it.

And I said nothing.

I want to be very precise about why, because precision is the only apology I know how to make. It wasn't fear of the Choir. I've been afraid of the Choir my whole life; fear of the Choir is like fear of weather, it doesn't change what you do, it just changes what you wear. It wasn't even fear of what Oscar would do with the knowledge. I've watched the man walk into a tollbridge ambush like he was picking up a delivery.

It was this: for two days, I had been the most important creature in Greyhollow. Oscar needed me. Ysolde needed me. Maren, who once called me *the vermin in the walls* to my face and then apologized, needed me. I was the only person alive who could read that book, and I could feel, for the first time in my entire small green life, the shape of mattering. It fit me like stolen vestments. It fit me *beautifully*.

And if I told them about the third path—if I told them the prophecy had a door in it—then the crisis would end. The quest would end. The *needing* would end. And Pip would go back to being the goblin in the walls, useful when there was a lock or a language, furniture the rest of the time. I told myself a hundred stories about it. *The timing's wrong, they're in danger, it would destabilize the group's morale, the translation needs verification.* I am an archivist. I know a forged document when I forge one, even when the document is my own conscience.

So I sat on the door out of the burning building, and I let them keep walking toward the fire, because the fire made me warm.

* * *

The fire, as of last night, is a literal finger of a buried god pushing up through the stone of the world, so I think my metaphor has aged badly, which is appropriate.

Here's the thing about Ashkarath's court, the dream-country he showed us in the tunnels: he doesn't tempt you with strangers. He tempts you with *you*. He showed Maren her brother, whole and laughing, on a beach with no Sunspire in it. He showed Oscar a version of himself that had never sworn the oath, some fat and happy ferryman on some quiet river. He showed Ysolde the Choir's great hall, and her old seat in it, and her name in the cantor rolls, unstruck.

And he showed me a library.

Not a grand one. My library. The walls of Greyhollow, the gap behind the shelving in the north stacks, my nest of stolen books and candle stubs, except in the dream it went on forever. Every banned text in the world was in it. Every burned book, restored. Every censored line, unsanded. The complete, uncensored memory of the whole world, and a little brass plaque on the first shelf that said *ARCHIVIST: PIP*, and I stood in that dream with my hands shaking the same way they're shaking now, and I understood, with the total clarity the dream-god gives you so you'll take the bait, that I would only ever be given that library by someone who wanted something. That everything I had ever been offered by the powerful had a price tag folded inside it, and the price was always the same: *be small, be useful, be quiet.*

I walked out of the dream. I'm the only one who walked out clean, and I want that noted, because it's the last clean thing I have, and I'm about to spend it.

* * *

They're awake. All three of them, sitting up around the coals, and Oscar is looking at me the way I knew he would, patient as a closed door, and I realize the speech I rehearsed is gone. All four hundred and eleven versions. There's just me and the truth and the dark, and honestly? The dark I'm used to. I've lived in it my whole life. It's the truth that's the stranger here.

"Say it," Maren says. Not cruel. Just Maren, who pays her debts and expects other people to.

So I do. I say all of it, standing, because I can't sit, in the flat archivist's voice I use for inventory, which is the voice I use when I'm bleeding: the line in the codex, the night I found it, the hours I spent not telling them, the reasons, all the reasons, the small ones and the shameful ones and the one at the bottom that's just *I wanted to matter*. I tell them about the dream library and the plaque with my name on it and how I walked out, and I tell them the worst sentence, the one I've been carrying since Greyhollow like a stone in my cheek:

"I had the door out of this. I had it for weeks. And I kept it shut because I was afraid that if the story ended, you'd all stop needing me, and needing you—" my voice cracks, and I hate it, archivists don't crack, "—needing you was the first time in my life I wasn't lonely. So I chose the story over the truth. That's what I carry. That's what Pip carries."

The silence afterward is the real kind, Veyl's kind, and I stand in it and wait to be evicted.

Ysolde moves first. She's been dying by inches since the tunnels—whatever Ashkarath's court took out of her, it's still bleeding—but she reaches over and takes my hand in both of hers, priestess's hands, ink-stained, and says, "You walked out of the dream. Do you know how rare that is? I didn't. Maren didn't. The strongest man I ever trained didn't. You looked at everything you ever wanted and you *put it down*." Her voice goes rough. "That's not nothing, little one. That's the whole liturgy. Renunciation is the only prayer the old god ever required."

"That's not the point," I say, because it isn't. "The point is I lied by omission for weeks and it nearly got us all—"

"Yes," Oscar says.

I stop. He's still sitting by the coals, and the light does that thing it does to his face, half-lit, half-not, like a man permanently on a threshold.

"Yes, it did," he says. "It nearly got us killed, and I'd like to know why you thought we couldn't survive the truth. Because here's what I've learned about this group, Pip, in about the hardest way there is: we don't break. Maren sold me to the Choir and we're here. Ysolde lost her god and her church and she's here. Seraphine burned down her entire life in one night and she's *here*. You sat on the most important five words in the world for weeks." He shrugs, and it's so Oscar, so tired and so gentle. "You're in good company. You're in the only company I've got."

"Forgiveness isn't owed," Maren says flatly. "But I'll tell you what I told Oscar, and what he never quite believed: betrayal with a reason is still betrayal, but it's not the same animal as betrayal for profit." She pokes the coals with a knife, not looking at me. "You did it because you were scared of being alone again. I did it because they had Tam. Neither of us did it because we liked it." A pause. "You ever sit on something that big again, goblin, and I'll sell *you* to the Choir myself. Understood?"

"Understood," I say, and my eyes are doing something embarrassing, and Maren pretends very hard to be interested in the coals, which is how Maren says *it's all right*.

"Also," Ysolde adds, "for the record, your translation work is exquisite. The refusal clause reading is philologically sound. I checked it against your marginalia while you were sleeping and I've been trying to work out how to tell you I already knew you knew."

I stare at her. "You—"

"I'm a defrocked priestess who spent fifteen years reading banned texts in a cellar. You think I can't smell an unsanded codex?" She smiles, and it's the first real smile since the tunnels. "I was waiting for you to be ready. That's not a criticism, Pip. That's a lesson I learned too late in my own life, and I'm glad one of us learned it early: the truth keeps. It keeps better than anything. It was always going to keep until you could carry it."

Oscar gets up then. Walks over. He doesn't do speeches, Oscar; he does weight. He puts his hand on my head, between the ears, the way you'd steady a small thing in a high wind, and he says, "Third path. Refuse the ink. Say it again, all of it, because if I'm going to refuse a prophecy, I want to refuse the *accurate* prophecy."

And that's it. That's the whole trial, the whole sentencing, the whole pardon. We're a ragged, criminal, heretical little family, and we don't do absolution, we do *continuation*: the crime is named, the debt is marked in the ledger, and then you get handed a task, because in this family the punishment for betrayal is that you have to keep going together.

* * *

Later, when the others sleep, I take out the codex by candle stub and turn to the page, and I read the five words aloud in the old tongue, quietly, the way you'd read over a grave.

*The Sigil-Bearer may refuse the ink.*

And underneath it, in the margin, in a hand a century dead, some forgotten scribe has written a note so small I needed a lens to find it, and I never showed it to anyone, because it frightened me more than the main text did. It says:

*But the ink may refuse to be refused. Ask what the ink is made of.*

I look at Oscar, sleeping with his oath-marked hand curled open like a man who has already let go of something. I look at the sigil, faint and patient under his skin, drinking his silence the way the ward drinks Veyl's.

I am an archivist. It is the one law I have that I do not lie about texts.

So tomorrow, first light, I am going to show them the marginal note. All of it. No footnotes.

Because I think the ink might be made of him.
