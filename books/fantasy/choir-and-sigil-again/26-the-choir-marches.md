## 26. The Choir Marches

High Cantor Malvane had not slept in nine days, and did not need to.

This was the first thing the soldiers noticed about her, when they dared notice anything at all: that she rode at the head of four thousand faithful through the freezing passes of Thornfell with her eyes bright and her spine straight as a hymn staff, as though exhaustion were a sin she had simply declined to commit. The second thing they noticed was that she sang. Not constantly—Malvane was too disciplined for that—but at intervals, at dawn and dusk and at every mile-marker shrine along the old pilgrim road, she would lift her voice in a single sustained note of the Radiant Liturgy, and four thousand throats would answer her, and the sound would roll down the mountainsides like a landslide made of light.

She was sixty-one years old. She had been High Cantor for nineteen years, and before that she had been an Inquisitor for twenty-two, and before that she had been a foundling girl in the Sunspire's creche who had once asked why the Choir's oldest hymns were all written in a key no human throat could comfortably hold. She had never stopped asking questions. She had only learned, over the decades, to ask them of the right people—the ones with answers already prepared—and to stop asking them of herself.

The answers were prepared. That was the whole beauty of doctrine. You could set your whole weight upon it and it would not shift.

"Report," she said, without turning in the saddle.

Cantor-Adjunct Ferro brought his mule alongside hers. He was a broad, sweating man with a voice like a rockslide and a faith like a hammer—useful qualities both, though Malvane privately ranked them in reverse order. "Scouts came back an hour past, Eminence. The pass at Greyhollow is held by smuggler clans. Maybe six hundred fighters, maybe more. They've torn up the road and they're dug in behind the tollbridge."

"And Fairweather?"

"No confirmed sighting. But the heretic Lark's last true report placed him in Greyhollow, and nothing since has contradicted it." Ferro hesitated. "There is... the matter of Lark herself."

"Yes." Malvane let the word sit in the cold air between them. "There is."

Seraphine Lark had been her finest student in thirty years of teaching. Sharp, devout, merciless in exactly the way mercy sometimes required. And then, somewhere in the mountains, something had gone quietly wrong with her—a hairline fracture running through the crystal—and now her reports arrived late, or vague, or suspiciously precise in ways that felt like misdirection. Malvane had read the last three twice each. The third time, she had read them aloud to her inner circle in a flat voice, letting them hear what she heard: a woman choosing her words the way a thief chooses footsteps.

"She is compromised," Ferro said. It was not a question, but he wanted it confirmed. Confirmation was a kind of warmth.

"She is *incomplete*," Malvane said. "Which is worse. A heretic you can burn. An incomplete believer you must first finish breaking." She adjusted her grip on the reins. "When we take Greyhollow, find her. If she kneels, she will be given work suited to her condition. If she runs—" Malvane smiled, and it was a gentle smile, the kind she used on children and the dying. "Then she will have answered her own question, won't she?"

* * *

They made camp that night in the shadow of a ruined waystation, and Malvane walked among the fires as she always did, because an army was not a weapon but a congregation, and a congregation needed its shepherd visible.

The soldiers loved her. This was not vanity; it was engineering. She knew their names—not all four thousand, but enough, always enough, the names passed down through the cantor hierarchy like water through channels until it reached her. She knew that Brother Aldous in the Third Phalanx had a daughter with a cleft lip the Choir's surgeons would mend if he served with distinction. She knew that Sister Veyra had joined after the fever took her village and the Choir's granaries had fed the survivors. She knew their fears, too, because fear was the raw ore of faith and she had spent a lifetime refining it.

"High Cantor." A young spearman, barely twenty, stood and knocked his fist to his breastplate so quickly he nearly dropped his supper. "Eminence. Is it true? What they're saying—that there's a god under the mountain, a real one, one that isn't ours—"

"It is true," Malvane said, and watched his face go pale, and continued gently, "and it changes nothing. Sit, child. Eat."

He sat. The men around him leaned in. She let them.

"The Choir has never claimed there are no other voices in the world," she said. "We claim something far more important: that only one voice is *worth* following. Ashkarath offers strength. Do you know what strength costs, unbought by devotion? It costs everything you are. The war-god does not save his faithful. He spends them." She looked around the fire, meeting eyes. "The Radiant Choir asks less than everything. We ask only your song. And in exchange—" she spread her hands, taking in the fires, the banners, the four thousand sleeping faithful, the whole vast machine of light rolling toward the pass— "we give you each other. Forever. There is no god under any mountain that can make that offer, because there is no god under any mountain that *has* anyone."

The young spearman nodded slowly. Around the fire, others nodded. Malvane saw the doubt go out of them like frost burning off a morning field, and she felt the familiar satisfaction, warm and clean, of a mechanism working as designed.

She did not think of it as lying. That was the trick of it, the discipline of it, the thing she had mastered while Seraphine Lark was still learning her scales. Every word she had spoken was true. Ashkarath *did* spend his faithful. The Choir *did* feed the hungry and mend the broken. The lie, if you insisted on calling it that, lived only in the arrangement—in what was placed next to what, in the silence where certain other truths might otherwise have stood. Truth was clay. Doctrine was the kiln. Malvane had fired a thousand vessels in that kiln and not one of them had ever cracked in her hands.

Only in Seraphine's. And that, she admitted to herself, walking back toward her tent through the blue dark, was the single flaw in thirty years of flawless work. Somewhere, in the making of that brilliant girl, a seam had been left unfired.

It would be corrected. Everything was correctable. That was the final comfort of institution: you did not need to be right, only persistent, and persistence was merely time wearing a uniform.

* * *

In her tent, by lamplight, Malvane opened the case she allowed no one else to touch.

Inside, wrapped in choir-silk, lay the Second Book—the Choir's half of the matched prophecy, recovered from the reliquary's breach two weeks ago by a courier who had arrived half-dead and raving about thieves in stolen vestments. Malvane had read the courier's account four times. Thieves in stolen vestments, inside the reliquary itself, with help from within. Help from within. She had signed the order for the reliquary wardens' executions herself, all eleven of them, and had felt nothing she would call regret. Grief, perhaps, at the waste. The difference mattered.

Now she turned the ancient pages by lamplight, moving with practiced care to the passage that had governed her life's final great work.

*And the Sigil-Bearer shall stand at the ward's heart on the appointed day, and his death shall be the lock, and his belief the key, and the Sleeper shall sleep until the world's last hour.*

Simple. Beautiful. A hundred generations of Cantors had built the Choir's power upon those lines, and upon the corollary that followed like a shadow follows a body: *the Choir alone knows the appointed day, and the Choir alone may pronounce the rite.* Whoever controlled the death controlled the seal. Whoever controlled the seal controlled the world's fear, and fear, properly tithed, became obedience, and obedience became the Choir, forever.

Except.

Malvane's finger rested on the margin, where some long-dead hand had written a notation in faded brown ink. She had read this notation ten thousand times across forty years, and for forty years she had read it the same way, the way she had been taught to read it, the way all Cantors were taught:

*A marginal gloss. Apocryphal. Possibly a later forgery. To be noted and disregarded.*

*The bearer may refuse the ink.*

Four words. She had dismissed them for four decades because dismissing them was her office, her duty, the load-bearing wall of everything. If the bearer could refuse—if choice could interpose itself between prophecy and fulfillment—then the Choir was not the keeper of the world's salvation but merely one more voice arguing in the dark, and Malvane had built her soul on the certainty that the Choir was not arguing. The Choir was *right*. Rightness did not argue. Rightness marched.

But Fairweather had the First Book now. And Fairweather had Ysolde Vane, the defrocked priestess who had spent fifteen years in the archives Malvane herself had ordered sealed. And Fairweather had, by every report, refused to die on schedule—which meant he had either read the marginal gloss or guessed at it, and either way, the load-bearing wall was under strain.

Malvane closed the book and sat for a long moment with her hands folded on its cover, listening to the wind worry the tent ropes.

Here was the truth she kept even from herself most days, brought out now into the lamplight like a coin counted in private: it did not matter whether the gloss was genuine. It did not matter whether Fairweather could refuse the ink, or whether refusal would shatter the ward and loose Ashkarath, or whether some third path existed that no prophet had thought to write down. None of it mattered, because the arithmetic was unchanged. If the Sigil-Bearer died believing, the ward sealed and the Choir's dominion was sanctified by prophecy itself. If he died doubting, the ward broke—but it would break *because the heretics had corrupted him*, and the Choir would inherit a terrified world begging for structure, which was the same inheritance arriving by a different door. Either way, the Choir endured. Either way, the song went on.

The only outcome Malvane could not permit was the one where he did not die at all.

"He will die believing," she said aloud, to the tent, to the book, to the dead hand that had written the gloss. Her voice was calm and carried no anger, because anger was fuel burned inefficiently. "Whatever he believes when he reaches us, he will die believing in the Choir. I will see to the believing myself."

It was, she reflected, almost a kindness she planned for him. Most people died without meaning. He would die as the hinge of history, sanctified, necessary, sung about for a thousand years. That he would die *unwillingly*, that his refusal would be taken from him at the last moment and repurposed—that was not cruelty. That was editing. The world was a text, and Malvane was simply the red pen that history had handed her, and she had never once hesitated to strike a line.

* * *

Dawn came grey and hard over Thornfell, and the army struck camp with the efficiency of a body waking.

From the ridge above Greyhollow Pass, Malvane reined in and looked down at the town for the first time: smoke rising from a thousand chimneys, the tollbridge barricaded, the smuggler clans' ragged banners snapping in the wind—and beyond it all, the mass of Thornfell itself, white-capped and silent, hiding beneath its stone the sleeping thing that gave this whole campaign its urgency.

Beside her, Ferro waited for the order. Four thousand faithful waited for the order. Somewhere below, in that stubborn little town, a mercenary with a dead god's mark on his skin was waiting too, whether he knew it or not.

"High Cantor?" Ferro said.

Malvane drew breath, and began to sing.

It was the opening verse of the Marching Liturgy, the one every soldier in the host had known since childhood, and her voice caught it up and lifted it high and threw it down the mountainside, and the army took it from her the way a river takes rain—four thousand voices joining hers, wave upon wave, the sound crashing against the cliffs and rolling ahead of them down into the pass like a tide made of certainty.

Below, she knew, the defenders would hear it coming. Let them hear. Fear was the first hymn; the Choir merely harmonized with it.

Malvane sang, serene and certain, and the Choir marched.
