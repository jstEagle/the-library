## 27. Last Quiet Night

**MAREN**

Maren Duskwell had cooked for crews of forty in mountain camps where the wind came through the canvas like a knife through wet paper, and she had never once been nervous about it. Tonight her hands shook cutting onions.

Not because of the army. Armies she understood. You could price an army, route an army, bleed an army into a pass and let the stones do half your work. She'd been doing arithmetic like that since she was fourteen, when her mother first put her on a mule train through the Thornfell switchbacks and told her that fear was just inventory you hadn't counted yet.

No. Her hands shook because of who was coming to dinner.

Six plates. She'd only ever set five before — herself, Oscar, Pip, Ysolde, and whatever ghost of Tam she kept at the edge of the fire like a chair left out in the rain. Tomorrow the Choir's vanguard would come down Greyhollow Pass in their white-and-gold, ten thousand strong if Malvane's scouts could be believed, and every one of them carrying more conviction than armor. But tonight there would be six places at the fire, because Seraphine Lark had crossed over. Because Tam was out of the Sunspire, alive, barely, and sleeping in the back room of the Wayhouse with his eyes open and his hands curled like a man still being questioned.

Maren had spent three years not saying things to Oscar Fairweather. *I'm sorry* sat between them like a stone in a boot. He'd taken it back once, in the tunnels under the mountain, after the ward — not with words, never with words, Oscar didn't spend words — but by handing her the rope first when they climbed, by letting her lead them down the smuggler roads no map recorded, by trusting her with his life the way he hadn't trusted her since the night she sold it.

Tonight she was going to say it out loud anyway. Not because he needed to hear it. Because tomorrow might be the last night anyone got to say anything, and Maren Duskwell did not leave debts unpaid. That was practically scripture among the clans: settle your ledgers before the pass takes you, because the pass doesn't keep books.

She made the stew rich. Too rich, honestly, for men and women who might be dead by noon — smoked goat, the good barley, the dried cherries Pip had been hoarding like a dragon since Greyhollow, a splash of the plum brandy Maren had been saving for a victory that might never come. Let tomorrow's soldiers fight on hardtack. Tonight her family ate like kings, because tonight they were still alive, and alive people deserved butter.

Tam came to the table without being asked. That was new. For two days he'd flinched from doors, from raised voices, from the sound of anyone humming — the interrogators in the Sunspire had sung hymns while they worked him over, and now any melody sounded to him like a threat. He sat with his back to the wall and ate slowly, mechanically, like a man refilling a stove rather than enjoying a meal. But he came. And when Maren ladled his bowl full without him asking, he looked up at her with something that wasn't quite trust yet but was at least its foundation stone.

"You don't have to stay for tomorrow," she told him quietly, when the others were arguing about arrowhead counts. "There's a clan road west. I can give you a mule and enough coin to reach Saltmarsh."

Tam set down his spoon. His hands weren't steady. His voice, when it came, was worse than unsteady — it was flat, careful, the voice of a man reciting himself back into existence.

"You sold me to buy Oscar time," he said. "You spent me. Fine. That's what family is sometimes — spending each other so somebody lives." He swallowed. "But nobody spends me twice. I'm staying."

Maren wanted to cry. She didn't. Crying was for after; the clans had rules about that too. She just refilled his bowl and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer, and Tam understood it, because he was hers, and Duskwells always understood silence better than speech.

**PIP**

Pip had never owned anything worth mending until this year.

Goblin archivists didn't accumulate. They curated. Everything Pip possessed fit into a satchel that doubled as a pillow, and everything in it had been stolen, salvaged, or traded for with marginalia — a page of Veyl's old liturgy here, a footnote on Ashkarath's imprisonment there, knowledge passed hand to hand like contraband because that's what it was. Knowledge was the only currency that couldn't be confiscated, only killed, and Pip had watched the Choir kill a lot of it.

So tonight, while the stew bubbled and the others argued tactics, Pip sat apart with their kit spread across a blanket like a surgeon laying out instruments, and they mended.

The bowstring first. Pip was the worst archivist and the best shot in the company, a combination that had saved all their lives at least four times, and a frayed string was not a thing you carried into a battle against ten thousand zealots. They waxed it twice, tested it against their thumb, tied the spare loops. Then the fletching — eleven arrows refeathered with goose quills Ysolde had brought back from some defrocked priestess errand months ago, feathers from birds that had flown over the Sunspire itself, which Pip found either meaningless or deeply meaningful depending on how much brandy was in them, and there was brandy in them tonight.

Then the book.

The prophecy book lay open on the blanket, water-stained, century-old, its spine held together with fish glue and stubbornness. Pip had decoded it in a library wall in Greyhollow what felt like a lifetime ago — had felt the terrified exhilaration of realizing it was one of a matched pair, had learned what it actually said, had known since then that the ink could be refused and had said nothing for weeks because saying something meant admitting they mattered less than the words they translated. They'd confessed it eventually, in the dark under the mountain, trembling, honest, forgiven. The family had absorbed it without breaking. That was the night Pip stopped being a hanger-on and started being kin.

Now they repaired the binding. Carefully. Stitching the spine with waxed linen thread, pressing the pages flat under river stones, smoothing the warped vellum with warm hands and warmer breath. It was slow work, devotional work, work that required exactly the kind of patience Pip usually refused to practice, and they found themselves doing it anyway, because tomorrow someone might try to burn this book, and a burned book with a broken spine burns faster.

Halfway through, their needle slipped and stuck, and Pip swore — goblin oaths were profane, inventive, and anatomically specific — and then sat very still, looking at the page beneath their hands.

Oscar's name. Tonight's date, a year ago now. The line underneath, the doctored line, the original line recovered from the Choir's reliquary: *the Sigil-Bearer may refuse the ink.*

Pip touched the words. Just touched them, fingertips on old vellum, the way you'd touch a scar to prove it was real.

"We're going to need you tomorrow," they said aloud, to the book, to the room, to whoever listened — and something in the walls of the Wayhouse listened, Pip was almost sure of it, some residue of Veyl's silence pooled in old timber like water in a cellar. "Not the prophecy. You. The refusal. The part they tried to cut out. We're going to hold that line the way you held the rest."

And then, because sentiment was rationed like everything else, they went back to stitching.

Later, near midnight, Pip did the accounts. This was also ritual. Every debt in the company, entered in a ledger no bigger than a prayer book: Maren owed Oscar an apology, paid in full as of this evening, witnessed. Oscar owed Maren nothing, having overpaid years ago in blood. Ysolde owed Pip eleven pages of liturgy transcription, to be delivered "when the world stops ending," a due date Pip had entered with grim humor. Seraphine owed everyone an apology for a decade of burning, and had begun paying it in intelligence reports, maps, and the names of every Choir officer too stupid to be trusted with command. Tam owed Maren his life, and had refused to let it be a debt, insisting instead it was a gift, which Pip had ruled admissible on the grounds that gifts were debts voluntarily defaulted.

The ledger balanced. Pip closed it, tucked it inside the prophecy book, and slept in shifts like everyone else, one ear tuned for horn calls on the pass road.

They dreamed of shelves. Of course they dreamed of shelves. Somewhere in the dream, a voice that was not quite a voice read aloud from a book Pip had never catalogued, and the reading went: *"What is written may be unwritten. What is sworn may be offered. What is silent may speak."* Pip woke with the words lodged behind their teeth like a seed, and decided not to tell anyone yet. Some seeds you plant yourself.

**SERAPHINE**

Seraphine Lark had not slept properly in nine days, and tonight she did not intend to try.

Sleep, for an Inquisitor, had always been a tactical vulnerability dressed up as a human necessity. She'd trained herself out of it during her novitiate — four hours, kneeling, a stone floor, the Radiant Choir's evening canticle playing in her ears until the melody wore grooves in her skull. Doubt crept in through dreams; doctrine held the line while you were awake. Everyone in the Sunspire knew this. It was why the zealous slept badly and the cynical slept well, and Seraphine had spent twenty years being zealous.

She was neither, now. She was something the Choir had no word for, though Malvane had found one fast enough: *heretic.* Officially declared, formally excommunicated, her name read out in the Sunspire's great hall with the same ceremony they used for burning texts. There had been a moment — she'd admitted this to Ysolde on the climb down, both of them roped together on ice-slick stone — when hearing her own name pronounced damned had felt like freedom wearing a prison uniform. Still did.

Tonight she sat outside the Wayhouse with her inquisitor's seal in her palm, turning it over and over. White gold, sunburst sigil, heavy enough to use as a weapon, which she had done, more times than she could count, and heavier still with what it had cost other people. She'd promised Ysolde she'd burn it. She hadn't yet. She kept almost doing it and then stopping, the way you stop at the edge of a decision you know is right and can't quite step off.

Ysolde found her there, past midnight, wrapped in a smuggler's wool cloak that looked absurd on a woman who'd worn vestments worth more than this whole building.

"You said you'd burn it," Ysolde said gently.

"I said I would. I keep—" Seraphine closed her fist around the seal. "It's the last thing I have from before. Burning it feels like pretending I was never her. The woman who burned libraries. Who signed orders. Who put questions to prisoners in rooms with drains in the floor."

Ysolde sat down beside her on the cold bench, shoulder to shoulder, the way she'd once sat with novices in the cloister garden, back when the garden still existed and so did the cloister.

"Then don't burn it," she said. "Bury it. Give it to the ground the way you'd bury a body. With words. With grief. Let it be dead instead of destroyed — there's a difference, and the difference is that dead things can be mourned, and mourned things stop ruling you."

Seraphine turned that over for a long time. The seal turned in her fingers. The stars turned overhead, indifferent, the same stars she'd navigated by on heresy hunts across three provinces.

"I put forty-one people to the question," she said finally. "I know the number because I kept the ledger myself. Doctrine required records. I told myself the records were accountability. They were receipts." Her voice went thin. "Some of them sang, at the end. The ones who heard Veyl's silence. They sang into it, and it didn't answer, and they sang anyway, and I thought that was madness. I reported it as madness. Resistant subjects, delusional, hearing absence and calling it god."

"And now?"

"Now I think they were the only ones in those rooms who were sane." She laughed, a small dry terrible laugh with no joy in it. "Do you know what Malvane said when I asked her, one time, one time only, whether the Choir had ever verified the translation of the Ashkarath prophecy? She said verification was pride. That faith did not audit itself. I wrote it down in my report as an anomaly. I should have written it as a confession."

Ysolde reached over and took the seal from her hand. Not stealing — asking, in the old gesture, palm open, palm open, the liturgical sign for *I receive what you cannot carry.*

"Tomorrow," Ysolde said, "you'll sing the true liturgy against Malvane, if it comes to that. You'll need hands free of iron. So let me carry this tonight, and tomorrow we'll bury it together, above the treeline, where the ground is soft even in winter. And if we live, we'll plant something on it. If we don't — well. The ground keeps what it's given."

Seraphine let her take it. The moment her palm emptied, it felt lighter and emptier both, the way a wound feels when the splinter finally comes out — relieved and raw and strange.

She didn't sleep. She sat up instead and wrote. Not a report — she was done with reports — but a letter, long and unsent, addressed to the forty-one. Names she remembered, descriptions where names failed her, the room numbers, the dates. An accounting no court would ever hear, entered into a book that would go into the ground with the seal if it had to. *I cannot return what I took,* she wrote at the end. *I can only stand between you and the ones who taught me to take it. It is not enough. It is what I have.*

Dawn was two hours off when she finished. She folded the letter into her coat, over her heart, where the seal used to hang, and went inside to check the watch rotation, because old habits of competence die harder than old habits of cruelty, and she was glad of it tonight.

**YSOLDE**

Ysolde had taught liturgy for nineteen years before the Choir cast her out, and she knew every technique for teaching under pressure: repetition, rhythm, call and response, sleep-learning whispered into students' ears through chapel walls. None of it applied tonight. Tonight she wasn't teaching technique. She was teaching inheritance — how to carry a thing forward when the carrier might not survive the delivery.

She gathered them in the common room after the meal, all five of them plus Tam sitting passive at the edge like a man watching weather he didn't understand, and she taught them the Rite of the Opened Silence. All of it. Not the fragments she'd fed Oscar over the training weeks — the whole architecture, beginning to end, every verse, every turn, every place in the structure where a singer could fail and what the failure would sound like.

"The Choir cut this rite in half four hundred years ago," she told them. "Kept the opening. Buried the close. The opening summons. The close releases. A summons without a release is a hook — it catches a god's attention and never lets it go, and that's what the silence around Thornfell has been for a century. Not peace. A hook. Veyl caught, holding the ward shut with its own silenced throat."

Oscar sat closest, cross-legged, oath-mark bare on his forearm where he'd rolled his sleeves for the occasion, which he never did, which meant something.

"Sing it back," Ysolde said.

He did. Badly, at first — Oscar Fairweather had many virtues and none of them were musical, a fact he'd confessed early in their lessons with the flat honesty of a man listing his own inventory. But he'd gotten better, and tonight, tired, frightened, fed, surrounded, he sang the opening verses clean, and then, unprompted, he sang the close. The release. The part the Church erased.

The room changed.

Not dramatically. Nothing toppled, nothing glowed. But the air thickened slightly, the way it does before snow, and the fire's crackle dropped half a tone, and Pip — sensitive as a tuning fork, goblins often were — looked up sharply from their mending and said, "Something's listening."

"It's always listening," Ysolde said. "That was never the question. The question is whether it can answer." She looked at Oscar, and her voice did the thing it did when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed alone. "Tomorrow, when you offer your oath — not your death, your *oath*, living, ongoing, a ward that walks — you'll need the close. All of it. Sung by more than one throat, because a single voice can be cut down mid-verse. I've seen it happen. I've *been* it happening."

She didn't say what she meant. Everyone heard it anyway.

"Teach Tam," she said instead, briskly redirecting, because grief was a luxury scheduled for later. "He has the best ear of any of you. Broken men often do — interrogation teaches you to hear what's under words. Tam, can you hold a line?"

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Then he hummed a note, low and uncertain, and then another, and then, astonishingly, a third, and the three notes braided with Ysolde's sustained drone and held, wavering, true.

"There," Ysolde said softly. "There's your second voice. The Choir gave him that, in their way. Everything they break, something else can use."

She taught until the candles guttered. Seraphine learned the counter-harmony, the part designed to answer Malvane's corrupted rite note for note, inversion for inversion — Ysolde had reconstructed it from memory and margin notes and one surviving fragment sewn into the hem of a stole she'd stolen from the Sunspire herself, decades ago, a theft she'd never explained until this week. Maren learned the percussion: not drums, never drums, but the stamped rhythm, boot-heels on plank flooring, the heartbeat-line that carried singers who lost their place. Pip learned the words in the dead tongue, all of them, because Pip learned everything, and recited them back flawlessly in a language older than the Choir, older than the wards, possibly older than the mountains, pronouncing consonants that had no business fitting in a goblin mouth and fitting them perfectly.

At the end, past two in the morning, Ysolde dismissed them to sleep. They went. All but Oscar.

He stayed, and rolled his sleeve back down over the oath-mark, and stood by the fire, and said nothing for a while, which with Oscar was a form of conversation.

"You'll sing it with me tomorrow," he said finally. Not a question.

"If I'm standing," Ysolde said. "And if I'm not, you'll sing my verses too. I've written them out. Your copy's under your plate. Don't read it now. Read it at dawn, when your voice is rawest and you need the words most."

"Why under the plate?"

"Because food and liturgy come from the same place," Ysolde said. "Everything that keeps us alive does."

Oscar almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth he'd produced in her presence since the day they met, and she treasured it the way you treasure a coin you suspect is the last of its minting.

She banked the fire and went to bed and did not sleep either. She lay listening to the house — Tam breathing in uneven rhythms in the back room, Pip's needle finally stilled, Seraphine's pen scratching somewhere above, Maren walking the perimeter out of habit, Oscar on the porch doing whatever Oscar did instead of resting, which was existing very intensely at a low wattage — and she thought about the unfinished song. Hers. The one she'd been singing since the day they defrocked her, the liturgy of the silenced god, nineteen years of composition interrupted by exile and resumed in barns and wayhouses and one memorable flooded quarry. Tomorrow she might finish it or be cut off in the middle again.

Either way, she thought, it would have been sung. That was the whole of the discipline, really. Sing it while you have breath. The silence will keep it.

**OSCAR**

Oscar Fairweather had faced death often enough that it had stopped being dramatic. Ambushes, betrayals, avalanches, one memorable encounter with a wight in a flooded mine that he still didn't discuss. He'd developed a procedure. Check your gear. Check your exits. Eat something. Sleep if possible. Say nothing you wouldn't want carved on your marker.

Tonight the procedure failed at every step.

His gear was checked — blade honed, bowstrung, boots waterproofed with tallow Maren rendered herself, the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against rain and flame alike. His exits were mapped; he'd walked the pass at dusk, alone, counting chokepoints, noting where the scree could be loosed, marking the secondary trail only he and Maren knew. He'd eaten. Sleep was not possible. And the things he wanted to say were precisely the things he'd never permitted himself to say, because Oscar Fairweather's entire adult life had been built on the principle that a man sworn to silence should probably extend the courtesy to his own feelings.

But tonight the silence around him wasn't empty anymore. He'd felt it change, these last weeks — felt it listen, felt it lean, felt it gather like a tide deciding where to land. Whatever Veyl was doing in that long mute century, it was doing it *toward* something. Toward him, maybe. Toward tomorrow. And a man standing in front of a listening silence finds, eventually, that his own silence starts to feel less like discipline and more like cowardice.

So he broke it. Quietly. In pieces. The way you'd pay out a rope.

To Maren, on the porch, past midnight, both of them too old for this and too committed to quit: "Three years ago you sold me and I hated you for it, and I was right to, and I forgave you anyway somewhere in the tunnels without telling you, and tonight you apologized out loud and I didn't answer because answering felt like grading it, and it isn't. It's just — received. Fully. Ledger closed." He paused. "You're the reason any of us are alive. Not the prophecy. You. Your roads, your clans, your brother's stubbornness, your refusal to leave me on that tollbridge. When this is done, I'm buying you a ship."

"A ship," Maren repeated, suspicious.

"A small one. Ugly. Fast. Yours outright. No cargo obligations to me ever."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," she said, "and I've been married twice."

To Pip, finding them asleep over the ledger at dawn's edge, cheek on the prophecy book like a child on a schoolbook: he covered them with a blanket and did not wake them, and stood there longer than a practical man would have, because Pip had given him the single most important sentence of his life — *the Sigil-Bearer may refuse the ink* — and had nearly destroyed themselves carrying it alone, and had confessed it shaking in the dark, and the family had held. Oscar had no words adequate to that. He had a blanket. It would do.

To Seraphine, briefly, in the yard, where she was practicing the counter-liturgy under her breath with the fierce concentration of a woman cramming for an exam in a subject she used to teach: "Whatever you buried today," he said, "it stays buried. Whatever you sing tomorrow — sing it like the forty-one are listening. They might be."

Seraphine looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse and then realized was simply *grief, freshly acknowledged by a stranger,* and nodded once, and went back to her scales.

To Ysolde he said nothing, because she'd already arranged everything, including his silence, including his verses, including the exact moment tomorrow when he'd need her voice and the contingency for its absence. Some debts you repay by simply doing what the creditor planned. He'd follow her script to the letter. It was the least chaotic thing he'd ever agreed to, and therefore the hardest.

And to himself — to the silence, to Veyl, to whatever waited under the stone listening to a mercenary talk to an empty porch at three in the morning — he said the thing he'd been carrying since the collapsed shrine, since the date that shouldn't exist, since the first night he'd read his own name in century-old ink and felt the cold dread move in beneath his practiced calm like a tenant with no lease:

"I know what you gave up. I didn't know until she showed me. A hundred years with no voice, holding a door shut with your throat." He stopped talking and just spoke, which is a distinction only people who've spent their lives silent understand. "I'm not going to die for you. I'm not going to doubt you, either, which is what the war-god wants, and what the Choir wants, and what the ink wants, all three conspiring toward the same grave. I'm going to do the thing nobody wrote. I'm going to stand in the door *with* you. Alive. Loud, if it comes to that. A ward that walks. A silence that answers."

The wind moved through the pass. It didn't answer. But it didn't leave, either, and Oscar Fairweather, connoisseur of silences, could tell the difference.

He went inside as the sky went grey. Checked Tam's door was barred. Checked Pip's blanket. Checked the stew pot Maren had left soaking, because tomorrow someone would need to eat before dying and it might as well be easy. Sat on his bunk with Ysolde's pages under his plate, unread, per instructions, and let himself feel the thing he'd been refusing all night:

He did not want to lose any of them.

Not brave, stoic, professional. Just *want*, naked and enormous, the want of a man who'd spent thirty years traveling light discovering he'd accumulated a family the way a pack accumulates weight — gradually, then all at once, then irreversibly.

He was still sitting with it when Maren's lookout whistled from the ridge. Two notes. Pause. Two notes.

Contact. Far off. The pass road, white shapes moving in the grey, banners that caught the first light like blades.

Dawn, and the Choir, arriving together, exactly on schedule.

Oscar stood, buckled his blade, and woke the house.
