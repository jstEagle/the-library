## 28. The Battle of Greyhollow Pass

Maren Duskwell had fought in eleven battles before this one, and she had hated every one of them for honest reasons: fear, greed, the simple animal desire to keep her own skin stitched shut. This was the first she'd ever fought out of love, and it turned out love made a worse general than either.

She stood on the ridge above Greyhollow Pass in borrowed armor that didn't fit—plate scavenged from a dead caravan guard, cut down at the shoulders with a smuggler's shears—and watched the Radiant Choir come up the valley road like a wound opening across the snow.

They sang as they marched. That was the worst part. Ten thousand voices in unison, the great hymn of the Radiant Choir rolling up the pass and bouncing off the cliffs until the whole valley thrummed with it, and Maren felt it in her back teeth, in the plates against her ribs, in the old scar on her palm where she'd once sworn an oath to a god who never answered. The Choir's song wasn't music. It was a claim. It said: *this valley is ours, these stones are ours, you are ours.* Every note a lien filed against her soul.

"Gods," said Brannick beside her, one of her own captains, a man who'd run powder through three wars and never flinched at anything but his mother-in-law. "You can feel that in your fillings."

"You'll feel worse things before noon," Maren told him. "Eat something."

The clans were strung along the ridgeline in a ragged crescent two miles long—the Thornfell smugglers, the Kettle Street runners, the ash-road carters, the river families who poled contraband up the Greywater under tarpaulins of legitimate fish. Four hundred fighters, maybe four-fifty if you counted the ones who'd shown up drunk and would be useless by midmorning. Against them: the vanguard of High Cantor Malvane's army. Pikes. Sunsteel. Battle-choirs in white and gold whose whole doctrine of war was to sing louder than the men they were killing.

Four hundred against ten thousand. Any sane commander would have surrendered the pass and taken her people into the deep tunnels to hide.

Maren Duskwell had not become queen of the Thornfell smugglers by being sane.

"We hold the pass until dark," she said, for the hundredth time, to anyone who'd listen. "One day. One day and the Fairweather boy does whatever he's going to do down there in the mountain, and none of this matters anymore. We're not fighting to win. We're fighting to *last*."

Brannick spat. "Lasting's harder than winning."

"I know," she said. "I've been doing it my whole life."

* * *

The debt ledger lived in her head, and it was long.

She'd spent three years keeping accounts the way smugglers do—not coin, but favors and betrayals, each entry written in someone else's blood. Oscar Fairweather, sold to the Choir for the price of her brother's breathing. Pip, nearly left behind in a library fire because the extraction window was tight and goblins don't vote. Ysolde, defrocked and hunted, given a bedroll and half rations when Maren could have afforded whole ones. Seraphine Lark, an inquisitor fed at Maren's own table, trusted exactly as far as a knife thrown across a room.

Every name on that ledger had been paid or pardoned by now, except one. The biggest one. And the man she owed it to was a mile underground right now, walking toward a chained god with his name torn out of a prophecy book, betting everything that Maren and four hundred thieves could buy him the time he needed.

So no, she wasn't fighting out of love alone. She was fighting like a woman settling accounts, and she intended to overpay.

The first horns sounded at dawn—not the Choir's horns, which were silver and terrible, but the cracked goat-horn of Old Tam Wickerby, the Kettle Street runner who'd appointed himself the clans' signalman on the grounds that nobody else could be bothered. Three short notes. *Contact.*

Maren put the spyglass to her eye and watched the Choir's vanguard halt at the mouth of the pass, where the road narrowed between scree slopes. They halted in perfect order, which told her what she already knew: Malvane hadn't sent zealots ahead. She'd sent professionals. Rank upon rank of sunsteel pikes, the morning light catching them so the whole column glittered like a frozen waterfall, and behind them the battle-choirs on their wheeled platforms, robed singers with throats like bellows, and behind *them*, at the far end of the valley where the spyglass couldn't reach, the white banner of the High Cantor herself.

"They're going to sing them forward," Brannick said. "That's their way. Soften the line with hymn-magic, then walk through."

"Then we answer," Maren said.

She'd argued about this with Ysolde for two nights, before the priestess went underground with Oscar. *You can't fight a choir with noise,* Ysolde had said. *Noise is just failed silence. You have to give them something to listen to instead.* And then she'd taught them, in the cold hours before the descent, a smuggler's liturgy—a working built from the old rites of Veyl, stripped down, profaned, made practical. Not a hymn. A hush. A silence shaped like a wall, laid across the pass like a tarpaulin over cargo.

Four hundred voices couldn't out-sing ten thousand. But four hundred people who'd spent their lives moving quietly through mountains, who knew every trick of stillness and shadow, could make a silence that would swallow a hymn whole.

"Now," Maren said, and gave the signal—a lantern shuttered twice, green glass.

The clans went silent. Not quiet. *Silent.* No coughing, no armor-clatter, no prayer, no last words. Two miles of ridgeline holding its breath, and into that held breath the smuggler-liturgy went out from forty trained throats stationed along the crescent—the hush-work, the shaped absence, Veyl's own gift turned to war.

She felt it happen. She couldn't have described how; you didn't hear a silence, you heard the world lean into it. The Choir's hymn rolled up the valley, struck the invisible wall above the pass, and—frayed. Thinned. Broke apart like smoke against glass. The vanguard's front ranks faltered, visibly, the pike-drummers losing their beat, the singers on their platforms clutching their throats as their harmonies came apart in their mouths.

For nine full seconds, the mightiest army in the Radiant Choir's history stood in its own valley and heard nothing.

Maren had learned to measure time in heartbeats during interrogations. Nine seconds was enough for a man to decide to run. It was enough for four hundred smugglers to loose the first volley.

* * *

What followed was not a battle so much as a negotiation conducted in blood, and Maren was the only party at the table who'd read the contract beforehand.

The plan was hers and it was ugly, because ugly plans survive contact. Let them come up the narrow road. Bleed them at the chokepoint with rockfall traps—three weeks of quarry work, six tons of stone rigged on greased timbers above the road. Fall back in stages, trading ground for time, always keeping the Choir's mass funneled where numbers meant nothing. And underneath it all, the real weapon: the tunnels. Every smuggler in Thornfell knew the mountain's secret veins, and a third of the clans weren't on the ridge at all. They were *underneath*, in the dark, waiting to open holes in the Choir's rear and vanish again before the sunsteel could turn around.

Hit them like weather, not like an army. Come from everywhere, stay nowhere, cost them a hundred men for every five of yours, and do it until sundown.

It worked. For four hours it worked beautifully, and Maren paid for every beautiful hour.

The rockfall came down at midmorning and buried the vanguard's second company alive—she heard the sound from the ridge, a grinding roar like the mountain clearing its throat, and then the shrieking, and then nothing, and she made herself look away because looking was how commanders became monsters. Sixty of the Choir died in that one breath. Maybe more. Men who'd marched under a banner promising light, crushed in the dark by stones dropped by people they'd called vermin.

She felt the ledger tilt in her head. Every kill bought time for Oscar, yes—but every kill was also a name she'd carry now, a family in some Choir province lighting a candle for a son who'd come to the mountains to burn heretics and found the mountain instead.

"Doesn't get easier," Brannick said quietly, beside her. He'd seen her face.

"It's not supposed to," she said. "The day it gets easier, hand me your knife and I'll save everyone the trouble."

* * *

By noon the pass was a butcher's diagram and both sides had stopped pretending otherwise.

The Choir adapted fast—professionals always do. They stopped funneling into the road and started climbing, companies of pikemen swarming the scree slopes in wedge formations while the battle-choirs below sustained a hymn that burned the hush-wall thin. Maren watched her eastern line start to bend, the river families giving ground yard by yard, and she did the arithmetic she'd sworn not to do: at this rate, the ridge fell by midafternoon, and midafternoon was too early, and the only reserve she had left was herself.

So she went down into it.

Not heroically. She'd like to report that she led a charge with a war-cry and streaming banner, but the truth was she climbed down the goat track behind the eastern line because it was faster than the stairs, arrived winded and furious, and started shouting orders in the flat smuggler drawl that carried further than any horn. *Fall back to the cairns. Not past the cairns. Tam, get your runners off the skyline, you beautiful idiot. Second company, hold the switchback until I blow the charge, then RUN, don't walk, run like your mothers are watching.*

She fought, too, because there came a point in every battle where the commander's sword mattered more than the commander's map, and that point arrived when a wedge of sunsteel pikemen crested the switchback ten feet from her position and the river families' line simply dissolved around her like sugar in rain.

Maren Duskwell fought the way she smuggled: close, mean, and with total commitment to the ugliest available option. No flourish. A boarding axe taken off a dead carter, hooked into shield-rims and hauled down, the spike driven where the armor didn't meet. She took a pike butt across the jaw that rang her head like a chapel bell and kept her feet through pure spite. She killed a Choir sergeant who looked barely older than her brother and felt the ledger groan under the weight of it.

And through all of it, under the fear and the fury and the ringing skull, ran the thought that had carried her since the night Oscar Fairweather stood in her kitchen with a torn page in his hands and forgave her without asking her to deserve it:

*Pay it back. Pay it all back. Every debt, today, with interest.*

She fought like that. Everyone said so afterward. Like a woman paying back every debt at once—which was true, though none of them guessed the currency was herself.

* * *

The tunnel attacks turned the afternoon.

At the hour when the light goes long and gold, the mountain opened beneath the Choir. Old Tam's people blew a smuggled cache of blackpowder under the supply train and the valley floor swallowed thirty feet of road, wagons, and a battle-platform with its singers still on it. From the smoke came the Kettle Street runners with axes and no intention of staying, and the Choir's disciplined center broke into a mob for eleven minutes—eleven minutes in which the clans did more damage than they had in the previous six hours combined.

Maren watched it from the ridge, jaw swollen, ear bleeding, and allowed herself one moment of fierce, unholy joy. Just one. Then the cost arrived, as costs do.

Because the Choir adapted again. A colonel with a ruined face rallied two companies on the western slope and did the thing Maren had feared most: he stopped attacking the lines and started hunting the tunnel mouths. Sealing them. Collapsing them. With people still inside.

She saw it happen in fragments through the smoke—the western vents, the ones her cousin Dell's crew had used, choked with stone and sunfire. Saw the runners who'd gone in after the raid trying to come home and finding the door closed. Heard, carried on the wind across the battlefield, the sound of pickaxes from the wrong side, digging outward, and then, after a long time, not hearing anything at all.

Dell was under there. Dell, who'd taught her to read current marks on the Greywater when she was nine. Dell, who'd covered her retreat from the Sunspire job three years ago and never once mentioned it.

She sent a rescue crew. She sent two. The mountain kept its dead, and the digging stopped near dusk, and Maren stood on the ridge with the battle still raging around her and let herself feel it for exactly as long as it took to say his name once, silently, and add him to the ledger on the other side of the page—the debts owed *to* her, which were suddenly, horribly, growing again.

Standing together had a price. She'd always known it the way you know a thing in theory. Now she knew it the other way, the way you know fire.

* * *

Sundown came at last, violet and freezing, and with it the thing she'd been buying all day: darkness.

The Choir did not press the attack in the dark. Armies of light rarely do—it's doctrine, and doctrine is a cage even for the righteous. Their horns sounded recall, their lines contracted around their campfires in the lower valley, ten thousand points of gold light arranged with terrible beauty, and above them the ridgeline belonged to the survivors of the clans.

Maren walked the line as the stars came out. Counted. Recounted. Did the arithmetic she'd been refusing all day.

Four hundred and twelve had stood on this ridge at dawn. Two hundred and sixty answered the evening muster. Some of the missing would crawl in overnight—smugglers were good at crawling in—but she knew the shape of the number, and the number was ruin. Half her people. Dell and his crew. Old Tam, who'd blown the last cache himself and hadn't come back off the skyline. The Wickerby horn hung silent on its peg, and nobody had the heart to move it.

Brannick found her at the eastern cairns, bandaging her jaw where the pike butt had landed. "We held," he said. "Dark to dark. Nobody's held the Choir like that in a century, they'll write songs—"

"Don't." Her voice came out through gritted teeth. "Songs are for winners. We're not winners. We're *still here*, which is different, and only different until sunrise."

He looked at her, and then at the valley of golden fires below, and understood. The Choir had lost a thousand men today and could lose a thousand more tomorrow and the day after, and Malvane knew it, and would spend them gladly, because that was what institutions did with people. The clans had lost half of themselves and had no tomorrow-after-tomorrow. One more day. Maybe not even that.

"So we buy the night," Maren said finally. "All of it. Every hour. Because down there in the dark, under our feet, a man with a torn-out name is standing in front of a god's prison making a choice no book ever wrote for him. And every hour we're still singing up here—still *here*, still loud, still stubborn—is an hour he gets to choose freely."

She paused. Looked east, toward the deep peaks, where the mountain hummed faintly under the starlight—an actual hum now, she could feel it through her boots, a vibration like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. Something was happening down there. Something enormous. The ward, maybe. The god. The silence itself, waking after a hundred years.

"And if he fails," Brannick asked, "if the mountain opens—what then?"

"Then we die badly and it won't matter," Maren said honestly. "But if he succeeds—if he finds the third path, the one that isn't in any prophecy—then tonight is the most expensive night anybody ever bought, and we will have paid for it together, and I will have paid my debt to Oscar Fairweather in full, with interest, in the only currency that counts."

She sat down on the cairn stone among her dead and living, poured a cup of the harsh plum brandy the river families brewed, drank half, and poured the rest onto the stones for the ones who'd crawled in yet.

The mountain hummed beneath her. Louder, now. Closer. As if something vast, far below, had begun—very slowly, very quietly—to remember its own name.

And then, near midnight, the humming stopped.

All at once. Completely. The way a held breath stops.

Maren felt the silence fall over the pass like snowfall, felt it settle into her bones, into the stones, into the golden campfires of the enemy below—and in that immense, sudden, impossible quiet, she heard a single voice rising from the depths of Thornfell. Not Ashkarath's war-shriek. Not the Choir's hymn.

Something new. Something that sounded, impossibly, like every silenced prayer in a hundred years, sung all at once, in harmony.

Oscar's voice, or something wearing it.

The battle for the pass was over. What was happening under the mountain had only just begun—and Maren Duskwell, half-deaf, half-broken, wholly unpaid, started running toward the sound.
