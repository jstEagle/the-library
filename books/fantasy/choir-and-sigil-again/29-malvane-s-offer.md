## 29. Malvane's Offer

The silence was still singing when Oscar came up out of the deep.

He didn't remember climbing. His hands were bloody—some of it his, most of it stone dust and older things—and the tunnels behind him had gone dark in a way that felt less like night and more like a held breath. Above him, through a fissure in the mountain's shoulder, the sky burned with the colors of battle: firelight, signal-flares, the pale gold radiance of Choir ward-lanterns strung along Greyhollow Pass like a festival nobody had asked for.

The voice—that impossible harmony of every silenced prayer—was fading now. Not dying. Settling. The way a bell settles after it has been struck, still humming in the metal long after your ears insist it has stopped.

*That was me,* he thought. *Some of that was me.*

His legs carried him toward the fissure because they had nothing better to do. His mind kept snagging on the same image: the ward beneath the world, vast as a cathedral turned inside out, its sigil-lines blazing with borrowed light. Veyl's voice pouring through him like water through a cracked vessel. And Ysolde—

Ysolde had been behind him. He was almost sure of that. She had been chanting the liturgy of assumption, her voice threading his oath into something the old stone could hold, and then the tremors had come again, worse, and there had been falling rock and screaming and Maren dragging Pip one direction while Seraphine shouted words Oscar hadn't understood, and after that everything was motion and noise and the specific, animal simplicity of *run.*

She'd been behind him. He was almost sure.

He climbed out of the fissure into snow and smoke and found the pass transformed. The smuggler clans' positions were shattered, burning, abandoned—but so were the Choir's forward lines. Bodies lay in drifts of their own making. The great hymn-engines the Choir had hauled over the mountains stood toppled like drunk giants, their bronze throats split, leaking light instead of sound.

And standing alone amid the wreckage, at the exact center of the pass where no sensible commander would stand, was High Cantor Malvane.

Waiting for him.

Of course she was. Oscar had spent twenty years reading terrain, reading men, reading the small honest tells that preceded violence—and everything about this scene read like an invitation written in a hand he recognized from the prophecy book. *On this date the Sigil-Bearer comes to the place of bones.* He'd assumed that meant the ward. Maybe it meant here. Maybe the ink had always been more patient than he gave it credit for.

He checked his knives out of habit. Both still there. His bow had been lost somewhere in the tunnels, along with, possibly, his sanity. His oath-mark—the pale scar on his palm where Veyl's silence lived—throbbed with a warmth it had never had before. The god's new voice hummed under his skin like a second pulse.

Malvane watched him approach without moving. Up close, away from her pulpit and her processions, she was smaller than her legend. A spare woman in white-and-gold vestments gone gray with ash, her face lined like a page much copied. Her eyes were the unsettling part: not cruel, not mad. *Certain*. Certainty, Oscar had learned, was the most dangerous expression a human face could wear.

"Oscar Fairweather," she said. Her voice was beautiful. That was the horror of the Choir—they made even menace sound like music. "I wondered which of you would come up first."

"You could have just sent an invitation." His voice came out rougher than intended. Smoke did that. So did grief you hadn't finished counting yet.

"I did. You're looking at it." She gestured gently at the ruined pass, the dead, the burning. As if it were all a table set for two. "You've had quite a night. I imagine you have questions. I imagine everyone does, after touching what you touched down there." Her gaze flicked to his marked palm, and something moved behind her certainty—hunger, maybe, or envy. "May I ask how it felt? To be a god's mouthpiece?"

"It felt," Oscar said, "like being used. Which is a thing you'd know about, being what you are."

"I am the Choir's servant. There is no higher calling."

"There's plenty higher. I've seen some of it tonight."

Something flickered across her face—irritation, quickly mastered. "You're angry. Good. Anger is honest. Let me be honest with you, Fairweather, because you've earned honesty if anyone has." She clasped her hands before her, a preacher settling into scripture. "You know the prophecy. You've read both halves—I know you have; my people recovered enough of our reliquary's wreckage to know what was taken. Believe, and seal Ashkarath forever with your death. Doubt, and free it. Refuse, and let the ward fray until the war-god claws free on its own schedule." She tilted her head. "Three doors. All of them terrible. I'm told you've been hunting a fourth path since the day you learned your name. I'm here to tell you it doesn't exist."

"I've heard that sermon before."

"From priests who didn't understand what they were reading. From a defrocked woman who taught you half-truths out of spite. From a goblin who thinks footnotes are salvation." Malvane stepped closer, boots crunching on frozen ash. "I am not those people. I have spent forty years studying the wards, the rites, the theology of silence. And I am going to offer you the only real choice left in the world. Not a fourth door. A key."

Snow began to fall again, fine and slow, catching in her ash-gray vestments. Somewhere down the pass, wounded men were screaming. Neither of them looked.

"Absolution," Malvane said.

The word dropped between them like a coin into a well. Oscar waited for the splash.

"You are a mercenary, Fairweather. Twenty years of contracts, of blood sold by the pound. You have killed men whose names you never learned, for employers whose causes you never believed in. You swore yourself to Veyl not out of devotion—I've read the records of your initiation; the Choir keeps files on every sworn silent—you swore because a dying man bought your life with an oath and you were too young and too tired to refuse." Her voice softened, and the softness was the trap's silkiest thread. "None of that condemns you. The Choir does not condemn. We *cleanse*. Every sin you carry—every face behind your eyelids at three in the morning, every grave you dug and every grave you fled—we will burn it away. Full absolution. Not just yours. Your mother's debts. Your father's name. The whole ledger of Oscar Fairweather, balanced and closed, in the Radiant Choir's golden book."

"And the price?"

"You die tonight." She said it the way a physician says *the bone will need setting*—clinical, kind, final. "You die believing. Fully, truly believing—in the Choir, in the seal, in the necessity of your death. The ward takes a believer's death and locks Ashkarath beneath the world for another thousand years. The mountain holds. Thornfell's villages keep their children. The war-god starves in the dark forever. And you—" she smiled, and it was almost gentle, "—you go into the golden light with your sins washed white, celebrated by ten thousand voices, remembered as the martyr who saved the world. Saint Fairweather of the Pass. They'll build shrines. Children will take your name at confirmation. Is that not worth one clean death?"

Oscar looked at her for a long moment.

Then he laughed. It surprised him as much as it clearly surprised her—a short, ugly bark of laughter with blood-taste in it, the laugh of a man who has just seen the shape of a trap so perfectly constructed that admiration and revulsion arrive in the same breath.

"What?" Malvane's certainty developed its first hairline crack. "What is funny?"

"You are." He shook his head. "Do you know what you just offered me? You offered me the one thing the prophecy doesn't require. It says I die *believing*. It doesn't say believing in what. So you've built me a martyrdom with all the trimmings—absolution, sainthood, a golden book, children named after me—and you've dressed your trap up as mercy." He spread his arms. "High Cantor. I grew up among smugglers. I have been conned by professionals on three continents. This is the finest con I've ever seen. Truly. But I can see the stitching."

"The stitching," she repeated coldly.

"Believing." He tapped his temple. "You need me to believe. Genuinely. Because the ward reads the soul, not the mouth. And here's your problem, Malvane: I have spent tonight with an actual god's actual voice in my chest. I know what belief feels like now. It isn't a transaction. It isn't absolution-for-services-rendered. What you're offering is a *purchase*, and the moment I accept it, the moment I die thinking 'this buys my sins off,' I'm not a believer. I'm a customer. And customers don't seal wards." He smiled back at her, and enjoyed, briefly and viciously, watching the crack widen. "You haven't built a martyrdom. You've built a machine that manufactures doubt and calls it faith. If I take your deal, I die doubting. And the war-god wakes up wearing my failure as a door-key."

For the first time, Malvane went very still.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. Then the certainty reassembled itself, smooth as poured wax, but Oscar had seen it—he'd seen the instant where the High Cantor of the Radiant Choir realized her perfect script had a hole in it, and that the mercenary she'd dismissed as a blunt instrument had found it before she finished her opening speech.

"You misunderstand," she said.

"No," said a third voice, from the rocks above the fissure. "He really doesn't."

Seraphine Lark climbed down over the scree with the unhurried precision of a woman who had decided that fear was a luxury she could no longer afford. Her inquisitor's whites were scorched, her left arm bound in a sling of torn choir-cloth, and her seal—the radiant sunburst that had defined her entire adult life—was conspicuously absent from her collar.

Malvane's head turned slowly. The look she gave her former protégée contained no surprise at all, which somehow made it worse.

"Sister Lark. Or should I say Sister *Nothing*, now. I received the report from your last position report. Deliberately falsified. Do you know what the penalty is?"

"Treason," Seraphine said. "Unmaking. I've read the statutes. I helped draft the amendment in my second year."

"You were the finest student I ever trained."

"I know." Seraphine reached the valley floor and positioned herself—not between Oscar and Malvane, Oscar noticed, but beside him. Shoulder to shoulder, the way soldiers stand. "That was the problem. You trained me too well. You taught me to check sources. So I did."

"Your teacher is dead," Malvane said flatly. "Ysolde Vane died a heretic's death years ago. Whatever she told you—"

"Is alive." Seraphine's voice cracked, just once, and she let it. "Or was, an hour ago, under the mountain. Singing the true liturgy. The one your predecessors cut out of the canon four hundred years ago because it proved the wards were never meant to run on deaths at all." She drew a breath. "You knew. The Choir has always known. The original rite takes a living oath, freely given. Every Sigil-Bearer the Choir has murdered across five centuries died because killing them was easier than surrendering power."

Something happened to Malvane's face then. Not a collapse—Oscar had expected anger, denial, the snarling mask of exposed villainy. Instead there was only a profound, exhausted sadness, the sadness of a woman holding a weight she had carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body.

"Yes," Malvane said quietly. "We knew."

The confession landed in the snow between them and neither of the others moved.

"Do you imagine I enjoy this?" the High Cantor continued, and her beautiful voice finally showed its rust. "Five hundred years ago, the Choir inherited a failing ward and a terrified world. Killing each Sigil-Bearer bought us another generation. Another generation of order, of light, of children who sleep unafraid. Yes, we doctored the books. Yes, we buried the living-rite, because a living warden is a *person*, and persons can be argued with, corrupted, killed by ordinary means. A martyr is cleaner. A martyr is a lock that cannot pick itself." She looked at Oscar, and her eyes—for one naked moment—were simply tired. "I have signed the orders for six Sigil-Bearers before you, Fairweather. Six. I told myself each time: the alternative is Ashkarath. I will tell it to myself again tonight, if I must. But you deserve to hear it plainly first, since you've earned plainness. I am not offering you salvation because I believe you'll take it. I'm offering it because doctrine requires the offer. And then I will kill you, believing or otherwise, and pray the mathematics favor us."

"There it is," Oscar said softly.

"You don't seem frightened."

"I spent a year being frightened of this conversation. Turns out it's just a woman with a job, same as me." He flexed his marked hand, feeling Veyl's hum rise in it like heat. "Here's my counter-offer, High Cantor. The one you didn't put in your script. I don't die believing in the Choir. I don't die doubting. I don't die at all—not tonight, not for your mathematics. Down in that ward, Ysolde started teaching me the rite your church amputated. Living oath. Freely given. A warden who can't be picked, argued with, or murdered, because murder leaves the ward standing." He took a step forward, and the snow around his boots seemed to fall slower, as if the air itself had begun listening. "Veyl went silent a century ago because your god-machine needed fuel and Veyl chose to become it. Tonight I'm making the same choice awake. Not a corpse in your lock. A living hinge. And when the ward drinks my oath instead of my death, your whole rotten arithmetic dies with it."

Malvane studied him for a long, long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders like a shroud being fitted.

"The rite is unfinished," she said at last. "Your priestess was interrupted. An incomplete assumption doesn't bind the ward—it *tears* it. You would wake the god faster than any doubt."

The bottom dropped out of Oscar's stomach.

Because she was right. He'd felt it down there—the liturgy breaking off mid-line, the ward seizing around the unfinished phrase like a joint locking wrong. Ysolde had fallen silent somewhere in the dark behind him and he had run, and the rite hung incomplete in the stone, a held note that could resolve into salvation or catastrophe depending on what happened next.

Which meant Malvane didn't need to kill him believing. She needed only to kill him *soon*—before the rite could be completed, before anyone could finish the line Ysolde had left hanging. A dead warden with an unfinished oath might tear the ward open wide enough for anything.

"That's the real offer, then," Oscar said. His mouth had gone dry. "Not absolution. A deadline."

"Everything is a deadline," Malvane said, and drew her cantor's blade—a slender thing of white gold, humming with stored hymn-light. Behind her, at last, the survivors of her honor guard rose from concealment among the rocks: two dozen zealots, blades drawn, faces serene. "I'm sorry, Fairweather. Genuinely. In another life I think you'd have made a magnificent Choirman."

"In another life," Oscar said, raising his knives, "you'd have made a decent smuggler."

Seraphine's hand found a weapon. The zealots began to sing—the marching cadence, the one that stripped fear from their hearts and replaced it with obedience—and the pass filled with terrible harmony.

And through the snow, up the trail behind them, Oscar heard running footsteps, and Maren Duskwell's battle-shout, and beneath it all, rising from the deep places of the world, the unfinished line of Ysolde's liturgy still hanging in the stone like a sword waiting for someone brave enough—or foolish enough—to grasp the hilt.

* * *

They came at him singing, and Oscar Fairweather, who had spent his whole life listening to silence, finally understood what the silence had been training him for.

The first zealot's blade met his knife in a shower of hymn-light and the impact rang through his marked palm like a bell being struck, and Veyl answered. Not words. Not yet. But the silence around him *leaned*—the snow slowed, the song flattened, the zealots' perfect cadence stumbling as though the air itself had thickened into wool. Oscar moved through the dragged-down tempo of the fight like a man walking through a crowd that couldn't quite see him, turning blades, spending breath, buying seconds.

Seconds were the whole currency now. He grasped it with total clarity even as the third zealot nearly opened his throat and Seraphine's counter-song tore two more out of the fight: the rite below was unfinished, and unfinished it was a bomb, and finished it was the only future in which anyone in this pass survived past dawn. Which meant he had to get back down. Through two dozen singers, past a High Cantor with a white-gold blade and nothing left to lose, carrying a liturgy only half of which he'd memorized, to complete a line whose ending Ysolde had died—

Had she died?

He didn't know. That was the thing clawing at him under the rhythm of combat, worse than any blade: he did not know, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like swallowed ice.

Malvane cut through Seraphine's defense with the bored economy of a master and came for him, and her blade was fast, faster than anything he'd faced in twenty years of contracts, and Oscar did the only thing left that the prophecy had never predicted.

He stopped fighting.

He opened his marked hand to the falling snow and poured everything into it—the oath, the fear, the memory of a smuggler's boy kneeling in a dying man's blood, the sound of every silenced prayer, the unfinished line of a dead-or-dying woman's song—and said, aloud, in the old tongue Ysolde had taught him:

*"Veyl. Take the rest."*

The mountain inhaled.

Every flame in the pass bent sideways. Every hymn choked off mid-syllable. Malvane's white-gold blade stopped a finger's width from his heart, caught—not parried, not blocked, simply *stopped*, as if the world between edge and flesh had decided to be somewhere else.

And in the ringing quiet, faint but unmistakable, rising from the deeps where an unfinished rite hung waiting, Oscar heard the next line of the liturgy.

Sung in a voice he knew.

Weak. Fractured. But continuing.

Ysolde was alive, and she was finishing it alone, and she did not have enough breath left to reach the end.

Oscar ran for the fissure, and the whole Choir of the Radiant, singing or silent, could not make their feet move fast enough to catch him before the mountain swallowed him whole.
