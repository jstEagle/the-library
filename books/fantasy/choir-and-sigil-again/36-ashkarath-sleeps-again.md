## 36. Ashkarath Sleeps Again

The silence came down like weather.

Oscar felt it before he understood it—a pressure, gentle and enormous, settling over the deep places of the world the way snow settles on a roof: without violence, without hurry, with the patience of something that intends to stay until spring. Except there would be no spring for Ashkarath. There would be centuries of this. Millennia. The war-god's rage, which had been a furnace roar in Oscar's skull for three days and nights, dimmed to embers, then to ash, then to nothing at all.

He stood at the heart of the ward with his hand still pressed flat against the living stone, and he listened to the quiet come down.

It was not Veyl's old silence. He knew that silence the way a man knows a dead wife's voice—intimately, painfully, by its absence. That had been a held breath, a god biting its own tongue for a hundred years so that its body could be a wall. This was different. This was *sung*. Every silenced prayer Oscar had ever poured into the dark, every oath whispered by every mercenary who'd ever sworn to Veyl and heard nothing back, all of it was in the ward now, moving through the stone like blood through a body, and his own oath was the newest thread in the weave, still bright, still warm, still *his*.

*You are the wall now,* something said—not in words, not exactly, but in the way a bell is rung by being struck. *And the wall does not end.*

"Yeah," Oscar said aloud, because his voice still worked, because he needed to hear something human in the dark. "I got that part."

His legs folded under him.

He didn't faint, exactly. It was more like the mountain caught him—lowered him down with a tenderness that should have been terrifying coming from a thing made of chained god-stone, but wasn't, because it was *his* tenderness too now, poured out of him an hour ago, still circulating. He sat in the dust at the center of the sigil with his back against the ward's keystone and laughed, once, a cracked and disbelieving sound that echoed strangely, harmonized strangely, came back to him with a faint choral overtone that had absolutely not been in his voice yesterday.

"Okay," he told the empty chamber. "That's going to take some getting used to."

* * *

Seraphine found him there.

She came down the last stretch of tunnel with her inquisitor's coat burned off her shoulders somewhere above—she wore only the grey undertunic now, and without the white-and-gold she looked smaller, younger, like a woman who had walked out of one life and not yet decided what the next one was. Her boots crunched on fallen stone. She stopped ten feet away and just looked at him, the way you look at a man standing in a house that should have collapsed.

"You're alive," she said.

"Reportedly."

"The whole pass shook. Malvane—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Malvane is gone. Not dead. *Gone.* The ward took her mid-rite and there wasn't even ash. I keep waiting to feel something about that and it hasn't arrived yet."

"It will," Oscar said. "Or it won't. Both are allowed. You're not Choir anymore, Seraphine. Nobody's grading your grief."

Something moved across her face—too fast to name, too raw to hide. She sat down against the opposite curve of the keystone, an arm's length from him, close enough that the ward's hum passed through both of them at once. Up close, Oscar could see what the last day had cost her: the burn scar along her jaw from the zealot's censer, the tremor in her hands she kept trying to still.

"I spent my whole life," she said quietly, "being taught that the divine speaks in light. Trumpets. Certainty." She huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "And then the truest holy thing I ever witnessed was a smuggler telling a god *no*."

"He needed to hear it," Oscar said. "Nobody else was going to say it to his face."

"And now you're—" She gestured at him, at all of him, at the faint luminescence that lived under his skin when the ward pulsed, which it was doing now, slow as breathing. "What does it feel like? Being half a hymn?"

Oscar considered the question honestly, because Seraphine had earned honesty, and because he genuinely didn't know the answer yet.

"Like this." He held up his hand. In the ward's light, the lines of his palm were faintly visible through the flesh—not gory, not wrong, just *legible*, like a page held up to a candle. "Like I'm being read. Constantly. Gently. Like something very large is keeping track of me and finds me... interesting." He flexed his fingers; the glow followed, obedient, warm. "My oath used to be a rope around my neck. Now it's more like—" he searched, "—a root system. I'm planted. I don't know if I can leave the mountain."

The words landed in the chamber and neither of them picked them up right away. Because they both understood what they meant. Maren. Pip. The road. Greyhollow. Everything.

"We'll find out," Seraphine said finally. "Together. One league at a time."

* * *

They climbed out of the deep together, and the mountain helped them.

That was the strangest part, Oscar thought—the help. For three days Thornfell had been trying to kill them: collapsing tunnels, shifting stairs, the whole architecture of a prison straining against its prisoner. Now the passages steadied under their feet. Rubble fields offered handholds. When Oscar hesitated at a fork, a warmth in his chest leaned left, and left was always the way out. The ward sang through him and he sang back, wordlessly, the way you hum along to a song you've known since childhood without ever learning the lyrics.

Halfway up, they found Pip sitting on a broken lintel with their knees pulled to their chest, waiting.

The goblin's ears were singed. Their satchel was scorched. Their eyes were red-rimmed and ferociously dry, the way eyes get when someone has cried everything they have and decided never again. They looked at Oscar approaching out of the dark, glowing faintly, alive, and their face did several complicated things in rapid succession.

"You're supposed to be dead," Pip said. "Statistically. Prophetically. By every model I ran."

"Sorry to ruin the data."

"Don't apologize, it makes the models worse." Pip slid off the lintel, marched up to him, and punched him in the thigh with all the force of a wet sock. Then they grabbed two fistfuls of his coat and pressed their forehead against his sternum and stayed there, shaking slightly, saying nothing, which from Pip was a eulogy, a homecoming, and a reconciliation all at once.

Oscar put a hand on the small hot head. "Hey, archivist."

"I knew," Pip said into his coat, muffled. "About the refusal line. Since chapter five. Since the library walls. And I didn't say anything because if the prophecy was fixed then I was the one who could read it, and if it wasn't fixed then I was just—" their voice cracked, furious at itself, "—just a goblin in a wall."

"You were the one who read it anyway," Oscar said. "Fixed or not. That's the job. That was always the job." He waited until Pip's grip loosened. "We're not doing ledgers tonight, Pip. Nobody owes anybody."

Pip wiped their face on his coat, which ruined it further, and stepped back wearing the expression of someone aggressively reassembling themselves. "Maren's above ground holding the pass with the clans. Your smuggler queen fought a High Cantor's honor guard with a boat hook. She's going to want proof of life. Also she stole my good quill and I want it noted that I noticed."

"Noted," Oscar said gravely.

They climbed on. The mountain hummed. Somewhere below them, under miles of stone, a war-god slept beneath a blanket woven from every prayer anyone had ever whispered into silence, and the newest thread in that blanket was a mercenary named Fairweather, walking toward daylight with a defrocked priestess and a goblin archivist, glowing faintly, alive against all models.

* * *

Daylight hit him like a blow.

Not the sun itself—it was evening, grey-gold, the long light of a day ending—but the *openness*. Sky without a ceiling. Wind without a direction chosen for him. Oscar stood at the mouth of the tunnel where the pass opened onto the battlefield aftermath and simply breathed for a while, because his body had apparently decided that breathing deserved his full attention.

Below, Greyhollow Pass looked like a war had happened there and then thought better of it. Fires burned low and orderly. Smuggler clans moved among the wounded. The Choir's army was a scattered constellation of white-and-gold, kneeling, disarmed, leaderless—some weeping, some staring at nothing, some already quietly removing their insignia as though the metal had begun to weigh differently. On a ridge above it all, a field altar had been built from a broken supply wagon, and Ysolde lay on it under a cloak, and people kept stopping there, one or two at a time, the way travelers stop at a shrine.

Oscar's chest did something complicated. The ward felt it—the grief moved through him and out into the stone and came back softened, shared, carried a little by something larger than himself—and he understood, with a tired wonder, that this was part of the arrangement too. He would never again feel anything alone.

"She died singing," Seraphine said beside him. "I checked with four witnesses. Different verses, same song. The liturgy of the unspoken name." Her voice was steady in the way of things recently shattered and carefully glued. "The Choir will call her a heretic forever. Everyone who was actually here will know better."

"Then everyone who was here has work to do," Oscar said.

Maren found them before they'd taken ten steps into the camp.

She came down the slope fast, boat hook still in hand, blood on her that was mostly not hers, and she did not slow down and she did not embrace him—Maren Duskwell did not embrace, that was doctrine older than any scripture—but she stopped a foot away, looked him up and down once, twice, cataloguing damage with a smuggler's eye, and then reached out and gripped the back of his neck, hard, the way you hold a brother, the way you hold something you sold once and bought back at a price you'll be paying the rest of your life.

"You absolute bastard," she said thickly. "You were supposed to die believing or doubting. Clean. Legible. Instead you invent a fourth option and make the rest of us look unimaginative."

"Somebody had to. The book was badly written."

Her laugh broke in the middle. She covered it by shoving a waterskin at his chest. Behind her, limping, bandaged, wrapped in a smuggler's cloak three sizes too big, came a thin man with Maren's eyes and a stranger's hollowed face—her brother, alive, blinking at the sky like a man remembering it existed.

"This is Tam," Maren said. "Tam, this is the idiot who made all of this possible by refusing to follow instructions. Tam, say thank you to the idiot."

"Thank you," Tam said, in a voice like a rusted hinge, and meant it with his whole ruined self, and Oscar found he couldn't speak at all for a moment, so he nodded, and the ward carried even that—carried it gently, added it to the song, filed it under *reasons*.

* * *

Night came down on Greyhollow Pass, and for the first time in a century, the silence over Thornfell was a kind one.

Oscar sat apart from the fires—not far, just apart, on a shelf of rock where he could see everything: Maren arguing cheerfully with clan heads about salvage rights, Pip already surrounded by three awed junior archivists reconstructing the prophecy books from memory, Seraphine feeding Tam soup with the patient competence of someone who had decided that tending the broken was the only rite left worth performing. Above them all, on its wagon-altar, Ysolde's cloak stirred in the wind like a held note.

He turned his hand over. The glow was fainter now, settled, domestic—a coal rather than a flare. Through his palm he could feel the ward's pulse, slow and vast, and threaded inside it, unmistakable, a voice that was not a voice:

*Rest, Sigil-Bearer.*

"That's not my name anymore," Oscar murmured to the dark. "Try again."

A pause. The mountain considered. Then, warm as banked coals, amused as an old friend:

*Fairweather.*

"Yeah," Oscar said, and closed his eyes, and let the song hold him up. "That'll do."

Far below, Ashkarath turned over in chains that were now also a choir, and slept.

And far above, at the treeline, where the first stars were coming out over a world that no longer had a prophecy with his name in it, Oscar Fairweather began—slowly, wondering, half-mortal, half-hymn—to plan how a man walks out of a mountain he might be bound to, and what he becomes if he can't.

The answer, he suspected, was going to start with a road.
