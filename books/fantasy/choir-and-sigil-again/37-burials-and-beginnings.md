## 37. Burials and Beginnings

**Maren**

The ground above the treeline was too hard to dig, so they built a cairn instead.

Maren had carried stones up the mountain all morning, her arms burning, and she kept thinking: *she would have hated this.* Ysolde would have hated the cold, the wind that came off the peaks like a blade looking for work, the way the light up here was too honest, too white, forgiving nothing. Ysolde liked candlelight. Ysolde liked warm rooms and bad wine and arguing about theology until three in the morning. Ysolde liked being *alive*, loudly, inconveniently, in the face of everyone who told her she couldn't be.

And now here was Maren, stacking rocks over her like a woman building a wall against the sea.

They buried her with her song unfinished. That was what Oscar said, standing at the cairn's foot with his hands folded and his oath-mark bare in the mountain sun. He didn't say it as a metaphor. He said it as a fact, the way you'd say *the bridge is out* or *the road floods in spring*. Somewhere between the first note of her final liturgy and the blow that killed her, there was a gap—a half-line of music hanging in the air of the world, unresolved, waiting for a voice that wasn't coming back.

"We finish it," Pip said.

Everyone looked at the goblin. They were small and gray-faced with grief, wrapped in three layers of stolen vestments against the cold, and their voice cracked on the second word but they didn't stop.

"That's how it works. Liturgies aren't— they're not performances. They're not finished when the singer stops. They're finished when the *prayer* stops, and prayers don't stop just because—" Pip's jaw worked. "Because someone puts them down mid-verse. The verse is still there. Somebody has to carry it."

"Then somebody carries it," Maren said.

It came out harder than she meant it to, and softer than she'd planned, both at once, which was apparently just how her voice worked now. She hadn't slept. None of them had. Her brother was asleep in Greyhollow below them, in a real bed, under a real roof, breathing—and she still woke every hour to check, because some part of her refused to believe it until she'd seen it ten thousand times.

Tam was alive. Tam was broken in ways that might never mend, but he was alive, and every breath he took was a debt Maren owed to four people standing on a frozen mountainside, and one person who couldn't collect anymore.

Oscar started the verse. His voice was rough—he wasn't a singer, never had been, had sworn his voice to a god who wouldn't answer—but he knew the shape of it from Ysolde's lessons, and he knew enough to start low and leave room. Seraphine came in next, and that was its own kind of miracle: an inquisitor's trained voice, the voice that had condemned a hundred heretics, threading itself into a defrocked priestess's unfinished prayer because it was the only true thing left to do with it. Then Pip, thin and wavering and absolutely refusing to quit. Then Maren herself, who sang like she smuggled—off-key, committed, trusting the cargo more than the route.

The verse went around twice. On the third pass, something happened that none of them could explain afterward without sounding mad.

The mountain hummed.

Not an earthquake. Not Ashkarath's tremors—those had gone quiet since the ward renewed, since Oscar walked out of the deep changed. This was different. This was the sound of a very large silence deciding, briefly, to be a sound. It moved through the stone under their boots and up through the cairn and into their teeth, and it took the shape of the melody they were singing, held it for one long moment like a hand closing gently around a bird—

And let it go.

Pip sat down hard on the frozen ground. Seraphine made a small noise she would deny forever. And Oscar stood very still with his eyes closed and tears running freely down his face, and when he opened his eyes he said, in a voice like a man reading good news off a page:

"She heard it."

Nobody argued. Nobody could have.

* * *

**Maren**

Afterward, on the walk down, Maren fell into step beside Seraphine, because there was a thing she'd been carrying since the pass and it was getting heavy.

"You saved my life," Maren said. "At the tollbridge, and again in the reliquary corridor, and again when Malvane—" She stopped. Restarted. "I'm bad at this."

"I've noticed."

"I sold this man to your people once. Sold him for less than I'm ashamed to say out loud, though I'll say it anyway because apparently that's who I am now. And then I spent three years telling myself I did what anyone would do, and then I met people who did the opposite of what anyone would do until it killed one of them." She kicked a stone off the path, watched it bounce away down the slope. "So I'm trying to figure out the arithmetic. What I owe. Who I owe it to. Whether owing is even the right word or if that's just another way of keeping score."

Seraphine walked in silence for a dozen steps. Up close, without the vestments, she looked younger. Tired in the bones rather than the posture.

"When I burned my seal," she said finally, "the fire was green for a moment. Choir fire isn't green. I've decided not to investigate why."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's a neighborly observation." A ghost of a smile. "As for arithmetic—I burned my seal because the numbers stopped working, not because I solved them. Some debts don't resolve, Duskwell. They just get carried. You carry yours. I'll carry mine. Between us we'll manage the freight."

Ahead of them, Oscar was carrying Pip piggyback, because the goblin had twisted an ankle on the descent and refused all offers of help except the ones involving being carried like cargo, and Maren watched her mercenary—her Sigil-Bearer, her friend, her impossible, stubborn, living friend—laugh at something Pip whispered in his ear, and thought: *freight. Fine. I can carry freight.*

Her brother was waiting at the bottom of the trail.

He was sitting on the inn's back step in the afternoon light, wrapped in a blanket, watching the street with the flat careful attention of a man relearning that streets could be safe. When he saw her he didn't stand. He didn't smile, quite. But he lifted one hand—the scarred one—and made a small gesture, the old one, the one they'd invented as children on the docks of Saltmere: *counted, accounted, safe home.*

Maren crossed the yard at a dead run and nearly knocked him off the step.

He was thinner. He was grayer. His hands shook when they closed around her back. But he smelled like her childhood and he said her name into her hair, just once, just "*Mare,*" like a man setting down something enormous, and Maren Duskwell—who had stared down inquisitors and warlords and the entire Radiant Choir without blinking—burst into tears like a drain unstoppered and didn't stop for a long time.

"I looked for you," he whispered later, when they were inside, when she'd forced broth into him and he'd managed half. "Every day. I looked for you."

"I know." Her throat was wrecked. "I know, Tam. I'm sorry it took me so long."

"The god," he said. His eyes went distant, the way they did sometimes, snagging on things she couldn't see. "In the deep place. When everything was loud. There was a quiet that held me together. Did you know? There was a quiet that held me together, and it sounded like—"

He trailed off. He didn't have words for it. Maren thought she might.

"Yeah," she said. "I think it sounds like family."

* * *

**Seraphine**

She burned the rest of it at dusk, in the inn's yard, alone.

Not the seal—that was ash already, scattered somewhere along the pass road. But an inquisitor carried more than a seal. She carried her commission papers, signed in High Cantor's ink. Her warrant, which authorized her to do things she would spend the rest of her life understanding she had done. Her copy of the Choir's half of the prophecy, annotated in her mentor's hand, the doctored translation marked up with centuries of confident error.

And a letter. Unsent. Written in her own hand three nights before Greyhollow Pass, addressed to the Conclave, reporting everything she'd learned and requesting guidance.

Requesting permission, really. That was the shame of it, looking at it now. Even at the end—even after Ysolde, even after the truth—some part of her had still wanted the Choir to tell her what to believe.

She fed it all to the fire one page at a time.

The commission went first. It burned quickly, eagerly, the way institutional paper does, as if it had been waiting. The warrant fought a little longer, its wax seal resisting, and she held it in the flames until the Radiant sigil curled and blackened and came apart like a living thing dying badly.

The letter she almost kept. Her thumb lingered on the fold.

Then she remembered Ysolde's voice across thirty years: *Question everything, little wren, especially the answers you're given in your own handwriting.*

She dropped it in.

When the last of it was ash, she sat by the coals in the gathering dark and felt—not lighter, exactly. Lighter implied weight removed. This was more like an amputation: the loss of something she'd grown around, something structural, and the strange new balance of a body learning its altered shape.

Footsteps behind her. Oscar, of course. He moved quietly, but he always let you hear the last few steps, a courtesy she'd noticed and never mentioned.

"It's done?" he asked.

"It's done."

"And?"

Seraphine considered the question honestly, because he'd earned honesty and because lying to him had become genuinely difficult.

"I spent twenty years believing that certainty was holiness," she said. "That doubt was rot. That the worst thing a soul could do was stand at a crossroads and choose wrong, so better to be given a road and walk it blind." She stirred the coals. "Ysolde taught me the opposite in a single afternoon, thirty years ago, and I called it heresy and reported her for it. Do you know what she said when they defrocked her? Nothing. She *sang*. In front of the whole tribunal. An unfinished liturgy, the same one we finished today, up on that freezing mountain. I've been carrying the ending of that song for thirty years without knowing it."

Oscar sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them mentioned it.

"What happens now?" he asked. "For you. The Choir won't forget Seraphine Lark."

"The Choir can wait in line behind my conscience." She watched sparks rise. "There are others like me, Fairweather. Inquisitors with hairline fractures. Priests who've heard the silence and been told it's damnation. Cells of the faithful who were taught the old rites and forbidden to sing them. Someone has to find them before Malvane's successors do—or before the next prophecy finds *them* and gives them two roads and no map." She glanced sideways at him. "You didn't think I burned all of it, did you?"

From inside her coat she drew a slim bundle—not Choir paper, plain grey stock—and unwrapped it. Pages. Notes. Names, some of them. The true translation of the prophecy in her own careful hand, alongside the doctored version, discrepancies marked in red.

"A record," she said. "Of what was done, and how, and by whom. And of what happened instead. If the story ends with us, it ends as gossip. If it ends written down—" she tapped the pages, "—it ends as evidence."

"Pip will want to catalog it."

"Pip has already offered. Twice. With diagrams."

And despite everything—despite the cairn on the mountain, despite the grave-grief sitting in all their chests like swallowed river stones—Seraphine laughed. Short and rusty and real, and Oscar laughed with her, and above the inn the first stars came out over Thornfell, over the sleeping war-god, over the wards that now hummed faint and constant like a struck bell fading so slowly it might ring for a hundred years.

* * *

**Pip**

Pip stayed up all night with the books, because grief had always processed better for them as work.

The restored prophecy book lay open on the inn's common table, its water-damaged pages flattened and pressed and, where possible, read. Beside it, the Choir's half, rescued from the reliquary. Beside *that*, Seraphine's notes, and beside *that*, a pot of tea going slowly cold, and beside *that*—growing by the hour—a new manuscript. Blank pages. Fresh ink. Pip's own hand.

*Choir and Sigil Again,* they'd titled it, because goblin archivists believed in accurate titles even when the titles were embarrassing.

The original book ended, as prophecies did, at the moment of crisis. Everything after—the refusal, the oath, the god remembering its name, Malvane coming apart in the ward-light like smoke in a strong wind—all of that existed only in memory. Four memories, now. Five, counting Tam's fragments. And memory was the least archival substance in the world.

So Pip wrote.

They wrote until the candles guttered, and then they lit more candles, and somewhere past midnight the door opened and Oscar came down the stairs, drawn no doubt by the scratch of the quill, and stood in the doorway in his shirt with his arms crossed, looking at the wreckage of paper.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"Different jobs." He came over, looked down at the fresh pages. His expression did something complicated when he saw the title. "You're writing us down."

"I'm writing *it* down. Us is incidental." Pip dipped the quill. "Though incidentally, yes."

"Why?"

Pip considered several answers—the archivist's answer, the historian's answer, the goblin's answer—and discarded them all in favor of the true one.

"Because somebody lied in a book a century ago and it nearly ended the world," they said. "Books matter, Oscar. Ink matters. Whoever holds the pen decides what people believe is possible, and for a hundred years the pen said you die on a certain date, full stop, no alternatives, and nobody questioned it because it was *written*." They looked up at him—at the oath-mark on his forearm, faintly luminous in the candlelight these days, the sigil that lived in his skin now like a scar that had decided to be a star. "So I'm holding the pen for a while. And the first line of my book says you lived. And someday, when some frightened kid in some library wall reads it, they're going to learn that prophecies can be refused. That's worth losing sleep over."

Oscar was quiet a long moment.

"Read me what you have," he said finally, and dragged a chair over, and stayed while Pip read aloud the account of a smuggling run and a collapsed shrine and a date in century-old ink, and it was strange, hearing it as a story, stranger still knowing how much of it was still unwritten.

Near dawn, Pip added the last line of the night's work, describing the cairn above the treeline and the mountain's brief, vast hum:

*The verse remains unfinished. We carry it.*

Then they blew out the candles and let themselves grieve properly, at last, in the dark, in the safety of a room full of friends' footsteps overhead and a book that told the truth.

* * *

**Oscar**

Spring came early to Thornfell that year, or maybe the mountain just decided to be kind.

By the time the passes cleared, word had begun to move along the roads the way word does—smuggler-fast, rumor-shaped, growing strange in transit. A god in the mountains that answered silence. Wards that hummed. A mercenary who'd torn his name out of fate and lived. Pilgrims started appearing in Greyhollow: first a trickle, then a stream. Skeptics and zealots and the merely curious, outcasts with nowhere else, scholars with dangerous questions, a deaf stonemason who claimed—through signs—that the humming was clearer to her than any hymn.

Maren organized them the way she organized cargo runs, with manifests and schedules and a fierce refusal to call any of it a congregation. "It's a *client list*," she insisted, while assigning bunks and rationing bread. "Clients pay. Congregations beg."

Tam, mending by slow degrees, took to sitting in the inn's doorway and greeting arrivals, and there was something in his damaged gentleness that made even the hardest pilgrim set down their weapons at the threshold. Seraphine drilled the willing in the old liturgies Ysolde had saved from the fire, correcting doctrine with the relish of a woman settling a thirty-year score. Pip gave lectures in the library, officially unauthorized, attended by everyone including the librarians.

And Oscar guided.

That was the part that surprised him most—how naturally it came back, how the mountains that had been a hiding place became a teaching ground. He led pilgrims up the smuggler roads no map recorded, and the mountain hummed beneath their feet the whole way, and every traveler who heard it came down changed in some small permanent way.

On the morning the chapter of his old life finally closed—a courier arrived with the last of the Choir's withdrawal orders, Malvane's faction dissolved, the Sunspire garrison recalled—Oscar climbed alone to the cairn above the treeline.

He brought wine. Bad wine. Ysolde's favorite.

"Took your advice," he told the stones. "All of it. Eventually. Slowly. In my defense, you never explained anything in order either."

The wind came off the peak, and the cairn hummed faintly under his palm, and he could have sworn—would swear it later, under oath, in his own book—that the silence around him shaped itself, for exactly one heartbeat, into amusement.

Below, Greyhollow smoked gently in the valley, full of voices. Above, the snowfields gleamed. Somewhere between them, the road waited, and it was his road, unwritten, wide open.

Oscar Fairweather poured the wine out on the stones, shouldered his pack, and started down toward the sound of his family arguing about breakfast.
