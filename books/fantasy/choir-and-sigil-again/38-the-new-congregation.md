## 38. The New Congregation

Pip had catalogued eleven thousand four hundred and six books in the Greyhollow library before anyone thought to catalogue Pip.

That was the thing about archives. You learned early that existence and record were two different animals. A book could sit on a shelf for three hundred years, breathing dust, utterly real, and still be *nothing* until some clerk with a quill wrote it down. Pip had spent most of their life as one of those books—present, useful, unrecorded. The humans of Greyhollow had known there was *a* goblin in the walls the way you know there's a mouse. Something that rustled. Something you set out scraps for, if you were kind, or traps for, if you weren't.

Now there was a ledger.

Pip kept it on the front desk of the ruined chapel, because the chapel had better light than the library and because the irony pleased them in a way they couldn't quite explain to anyone without sounding deranged. It was a plain thing, leather gone soft at the corners, ruled in Pip's own careful hand: *Register of the Quiet Congregation.* Underneath, smaller: *no singing required.*

There were forty-one names in it.

Three months ago there had been zero.

* * *

The first pilgrim had arrived nine days after they'd buried Ysolde above the treeline—a drover woman with a burned face and a voice like gravel, who stood in the chapel doorway holding her hat in both hands and said, "I heard the mountain went quiet. Proper quiet. Like it was listening." She'd looked at Pip for a long moment, clearly deciding whether a goblin archivist could be trusted with whatever she carried next, and then said: "My daughter stopped speaking when she was six. Doctors, priests, nothing. Last week she started humming. She says someone answered her."

Pip had written her name down—Brenna Ost, of the low pastures—and felt something crack open in their chest that they would spend the rest of the season trying to name.

Because here was what Pip knew, and what nobody else seemed to have fully grasped yet, not even Oscar, who was too busy being half-mortal and half-hymn to think like an archivist: Veyl hadn't just come back. Veyl had come back *audible*. A god that had spent a century as silence was now answering silence, and there were more silent people in the world than anyone had ever bothered to count. The mute. The grief-struck. The ones the Choir had sung over so loudly for so long that they'd forgotten their own voices were allowed to exist.

They were all coming to Greyhollow.

* * *

"You've got another one," Maren said, dropping onto the chapel steps beside Pip with the particular gracelessness of a woman whose body still expected to be fighting a war. Her brother Tam sat three steps down, whittling something that was either a bird or a knife; since the Sunspire he did everything within arm's reach of somebody. Pip had stopped commenting on it. Some wounds you mended by proximity.

"Two," Pip said. "A stonemason from Cragfall who claims his dead wife speaks through the gaps between hammer strikes, and a Choir deserter who won't give a name but cries whenever anyone hums."

"Charming congregation."

"We're not picky. That's rather the point." Pip turned the ledger toward her. "Forty-one. Eleven this week alone. At current rates of increase—" they did the arithmetic out of habit, lips moving, pencil scratching— "we'll need a bigger building by midwinter."

Maren looked at the numbers, then at the chapel—the cracked bell tower, the roof they'd patched with smuggler's canvas, the pews Seraphine had spent a full week sanding splinters out of because, as she put it, heretics deserved comfortable seating too—and laughed. It was still strange, hearing Maren laugh. Three months ago it had been a weapon. Now it was mostly just a sound.

"The smuggler queen of the Thornfell roads," she said, "reduced to running ferry service for pilgrims."

"You could charge them."

"I *do* charge them. Fair rates. Word gets around I'm soft and every bandit from here to the coast starts testing my tolls." She nudged Pip's shoulder with hers. "Besides. Somebody has to keep the roads open. Can't have a congregation if they all die in a ravine on the way."

Pip wrote that down too, because it sounded like scripture and Pip was, professionally speaking, a collector of scripture now. *Somebody has to keep the roads open.* Filed under: things Maren Duskwell said while pretending not to have changed.

* * *

The trouble with belonging, Pip had discovered, was that it multiplied your responsibilities exponentially.

When you belonged to no one, you owed no one. When you belonged to four people—one mercenary, one smuggler, one defrocked priestess-shaped hole in the world, one inquisitor-turned-heretic—you owed them everything, and it was manageable, arithmetic you could hold in your head. But forty-one names? Forty-one was a *collection*. And collections wanted curating.

Take the matter of the skeptics. Because they came too—scholars mostly, and one magnificently rude philosopher from the coastal universities who arrived with a satchel of instruments and the stated intention of proving the whole affair was seismic activity plus mass hysteria. His name was Aldous Wrenn. He measured the chapel floor for vibrations for six days. On the seventh day he sat down in a pew during evening quiet, intending, he announced, to demonstrate that nothing would happen.

Something happened. Pip never learned exactly what—Wrenn refused to describe it, went pale as paper, and asked, in a voice stripped of all its rudeness, whether there happened to be room in the ledger for one more.

There was. There always was. That was the rule Pip had made and would defend with teeth: *the ledger does not close.*

But skeptics required patience, and pilgrims required feeding, and deserters required forgiveness they often hadn't earned yet, and all of it required coordination, which meant Pip—who had once managed an archive of eleven thousand volumes entirely alone and unseen—was now, horrifyingly, an *administrator*. They had committees. Actual committees. Brenna Ost ran the kitchens. The stonemason, Gorrick, had taken over repairs and could not be persuaded to stop improving things. Even Tam had a job, of sorts: he sat by the door and whittled, and somehow his presence made frightened people feel safe, which Pip suspected was the entire reason Maren had suggested it.

And above it all, threaded through it, the silence.

Evening quiet had become the congregation's central rite, though rite was too grand a word for it. They simply gathered when the light went amber, and sat, and said nothing. No hymns—Ysolde's liturgy existed only in fragments now, and Seraphine was still translating, slowly, weeping at intervals she tried to hide. No sermon. Just forty-odd people being silent together in a cracked chapel under a mountain that hummed like a struck bell held just below hearing.

And sometimes—Pip had documented fourteen instances, cross-referenced, witnessed twice each—sometimes the silence *answered*. Not words. Never words. More like... acknowledgment. The feeling of being read. Of a vast attention passing over the room the way weather passes over a field, bending every stalk the same direction.

Pip had started keeping a second ledger for those instances. *Record of Responses.* Fourteen entries. In the margin of the first, in handwriting they didn't remember using, small and precise:

*She heard us. All of us. Every prayer we swallowed.*

Pip was fairly sure that was Ysolde's hand. They were fairly sure they were not imagining it. They had decided, as an archivist, that this fell under provenance questions best resolved later.

* * *

Seraphine found them at dusk in the library stacks, reshelving. Old habits. The chapel was the congregation's heart, but the library was still Pip's spine, and some nights the weight of forty-one souls sent them scurrying back to the shelves where problems could be alphabetized.

"You missed evening quiet," Seraphine said.

"Pip attended morning quiet. Pip is entitled to alternate."

"You're hiding."

Pip's ears flattened. "Pip is *archiving*."

Seraphine leaned against the shelf opposite, arms crossed, and regarded them with the expression she'd once used on heretics—which these days meant she'd identified a lie and was prepared to wait it out with terrible patience. She'd gotten good at waiting. Defection taught you that, apparently. The Choir's fastest sword had become the slowest person Pip knew.

"It's the anniversary soon," she said finally.

Pip's pencil stopped.

"Oscar's date," Seraphine went on, gently, because she was gentle now in a way that had cost her everything and she wore the cost like vestments. "The one the prophecy named. One year. People are going to come. More than usual. They'll want to mark it."

"People want a lot of things." Pip shoved a volume into its slot harder than necessary. "People want miracles and relics and a saint with a nice tragic story. What they'll get is a tired man who guides caravans and burns the porridge."

"Yes," Seraphine said. "Isn't it wonderful?"

And that was the thing about Seraphine—she could say something like that, with total sincerity, and Pip would feel the truth of it land somewhere under the ribs. A tired man who burned the porridge. A god that answered in silences. A ledger that did not close. None of it matched the old prophecies, and that was precisely the point, the whole point, the point Ysolde had died mid-liturgy defending: the ink was not the authority. It never had been.

"He doesn't like being looked at," Pip said.

"No. So we'll stand around him instead." Seraphine pushed off the shelf. "Also—Malvane's seal turned up in a market in Cael Sarrow. Someone's selling pieces of her regalia. Which means someone got close enough to strip her body, which means the Choir knows exactly where she died and possibly why. I've written to my old contacts. We should talk tomorrow. All of us."

She left Pip alone with the shelves and the lamplight and the slow cold drip of a new worry joining the collection.

* * *

Pip found Oscar at midnight, because of course they did. Some debts you tracked down.

He was in the stable loft over the chapel's old tithe barn, awake, as predicted, mending a harness by lamplight with the frowning competence he brought to everything—ropes, wounds, lost people. He glanced up when Pip's head crested the ladder.

"Can't sleep either?"

"Pip sleeps fine. Pip is working." Pip hauled themself up and sat on a hay bale that smelled of summer despite the season. "Pip has been reviewing the ledgers."

"That sounds ominous."

"It is *archival*." Pip produced the register and set it between them. "Forty-one names. Do you know what that number means?"

"A middling-sized ambush?"

"It means," Pip said, with the gravity the moment demanded, "that you are no longer a man with three friends. You are a man with a *congregation*. And congregations have needs, Oscar Fairweather. They need doctrine and disputes settled and roofs fixed and—" they tapped the page— "they need their founder to show up on the anniversary of his own prophesied death and let them look at him, because hope runs on specifics, and you are the most specific hope any of them will ever meet."

Oscar looked at the ledger for a long time. Lamplight moved on his face. When he spoke, his voice had the roughness it got when he talked about Veyl, or Ysolde, or anything else that had gone through fire and come out shaped different.

"They shouldn't build all that on me. I'm a waystation, Pip. Not a destination. The god's the—the god. I just carry the mail."

"Yes, well." Pip pulled the ledger back and hugged it to their chest. "Pip has read every prophecy in three languages, and Pip can tell you that gods historically do a very poor job of burning porridge. People need someone *reachable*. Congratulations. You're reachable."

Oscar laughed—quietly, the way he did everything now, as if loudness might wake something that needed to keep sleeping. Then he reached over and turned the ledger back toward Pip, to the last written page, and tapped one line with a scarred finger.

Under the forty-first name, in Pip's own hand, there was a blank line. And beneath the blank line, also in Pip's hand, though Pip had no memory of writing it, in that same small precise script that might have been Ysolde's:

*The ledger does not close. Neither do we.*

"Who wrote that?" Oscar asked.

"Pip doesn't know."

"Do you believe it?"

Pip considered lying, considered deflecting, considered the eleven thousand four hundred and six books and the forty-one names and the fourteen recorded responses and the whole improbable architecture of a life that had begun in the walls of a building nobody cared about.

"Pip believes," they said, "that records outlive their keepers. And that whoever wrote that line intended to be here for the next entry."

Outside, far off in the dark, the mountain hummed. Just below hearing. Just enough.

Oscar blew out the lamp. "Then we'd better get some sleep. Tomorrow we plan the anniversary. And Seraphine wants to talk about Malvane's seal."

"Yes," Pip said, already halfway down the ladder, already composing the agenda in their head—*item one, doctrine of porridge; item two, the seal; item three, the blank line*—and paused at the bottom, looking back up at the dark loft where the Sigil-Bearer lay down like an ordinary man among ordinary hay.

"One more thing," Pip called softly. "Name forty-two arrived today. Didn't leave one. Just a child, maybe ten years old, who walked in during evening quiet and sat down and hasn't spoken since. Brenna's feeding her. She keeps touching the ledger."

A long pause from the loft. Then Oscar's voice, tired and warm and entirely unprophecied:

"Leave the space next to her open, Pip."

It already was. It always was.

Pip walked back across the yard under the stars, past the chapel with its patched roof and its cracked bell that nobody would ever ring again, and stopped at the door, and listened—to the mountain, to the silence inside the silence, to the faint sound of forty-one sleeping breaths and one waking child.

Somewhere in the dark, very faintly, the silence hummed back.

And on the desk inside, in the ledger that did not close, the blank line waited—patient as stone, patient as snowfall, patient as a god learning, one whispered prayer at a time, how to speak.

Tomorrow, the anniversary. Tomorrow, the seal. Tomorrow, whatever came down out of the mountains next.

Tonight, the congregation slept, and the ledger stayed open, and that—Pip thought, locking the door behind them—was the whole of the miracle, and it was enough.
