## 39. Fairweather's Road

The commission came to me the way most good things in my life had come: through Maren Duskwell sliding a folded paper across a tavern table and saying, "You owe me."

I unfolded it. Read it. Read it again.

"They want you," Maren said, "specifically. By name. There's a list of names on that paper and yours is at the top, underlined twice, and I'd take it as an insult if I didn't know you'd say yes anyway."

"Twelve pilgrims," I said. "Greyhollow to the Thornfell shrines and back. Three weeks." I set the paper down. "That's not a smuggling run. That's a Sunday walk with extra kneeling."

"That's exactly what it is." Maren leaned back, arms crossed, wearing the particular grin she reserved for moments when she was about to make my life difficult out of love. "And that's the point, Fairweather. You've spent twenty years leading people through those mountains who were running from something. Here's twelve souls walking *toward* something, and every one of them asked for the man who walked out of the deep with a god in his chest."

"I don't have a god in my chest."

"You have half of one. Don't be modest."

She had me there. It had been eight months since the ward. Eight months since Veyl had poured itself into my offered oath like water finding a crack, since I'd come up out of Ashkarath's prison alive but rewritten—half mortal, half hymn, Ysolde would have said, if Ysolde had lived to say it. Eight months of learning what it meant to carry silence that wasn't empty.

"Fine," I said.

Maren's grin widened. "Knew it."

"Maren. If this goes badly—"

"It won't."

"If it does, I'm telling your brother you made me do it."

"Tomas is running the Sunspire end of the business now. He'd laugh." She stood, drained her cup, and paused beside me just long enough to press two fingers briefly against my shoulder—the closest thing to affection she allowed in public. "Bring them home, Oscar. All twelve. The mountains are different now. Let them see why."

* * *

They gathered at Greyhollow's north gate on a morning so clear the peaks looked cut from glass.

Pip had insisted on coming to see us off, which meant climbing onto the gatehouse wall and addressing the pilgrims as if launching a fleet. Pip was dressed—against all my objections—in archivist's robes with a new sigil stitched on the breast: a closed mouth over an open ear. The mark of Veyl's congregation, drawn by Pip's own hand, argued over for six weeks, and finally adopted because Seraphine pointed out that a faith founded by a mercenary, a goblin, a defrocked priestess, and a smuggler queen could hardly be precious about its iconography.

"Pilgrims!" Pip announced. "You have chosen to walk the road of Oscar Fairweather, Sigil-Bearer, Refuser of Ink, Voice-of-the-Quiet—" 

"Pip."

"—who has asked me to stop listing his titles because he says they itch. Rules of the road! One: nobody wanders off the trail, not even for the pretty blue flowers, which are poisonous. Two: complaints about the cold go to me, and I will ignore them. Three—" and here Pip's voice softened, and the goblin looked down at me with eyes that had read a century of prophecy and helped rewrite its ending, "—listen while you walk. The mountains are speaking now. He'll show you how to hear."

Twelve faces turned to me. A farrier's widow. Two brothers from the coast who'd lost their fishing grounds to the tremors of the old war-god's stirring. A young Choir acolyte who'd walked away from her chapter house after Malvane's unmaking, carrying nothing but doubt and a change of socks. A stonecutter. An innkeeper. Five others whose reasons I'd learn on the trail, the way you always do—one fire at a time, one watch at a time.

"Three weeks," I told them. "We walk at the pace of whoever struggles most. We eat when the light says eat and camp where the stone says camp. And nobody carries more than they can carry." I shouldered my own pack. "Including guilt. Leave that at the gate. The mountain doesn't take bribes."

The acolyte—her name was Betha, and her hands hadn't stopped shaking since I'd met her—almost smiled.

We walked.

* * *

The mountains had changed. I don't mean the rock had moved, though in places it had, great slumped shoulders of Thornfell where the old ward had flared and settled. I mean the *feel* of them. For twenty years these passes had been mine the way a knife is mine—I knew their edges, their weight, the ways they could cut. They had been work. They had been silence, the godless kind, the kind that just means alone.

Now there was something underneath the wind.

I felt it first at the old tollbridge, rebuilt since the ambush—wider now, with a shrine-house at either end where travelers had started leaving offerings of small carved mouths, closed-lipped, the congregation's humble version of devotion. I stood on the planks and let my awareness sink down through the timber, into the gorge, into the bones of the world, the way I'd learned to do.

*Here,* said the quiet. Not words. Veyl didn't use words yet, not often, not cleanly. But there was a texture to the silence now, a grain, like the difference between a room that's empty and a room where someone is sitting very still, waiting to hear what you'll say.

"You're awake," I murmured.

The silence did not answer, because answering is not what silences do. But the wind came down the pass warm instead of cold, and the farrier's widow, walking behind me, stopped dead in the middle of the bridge and said, "Oh," in the voice of someone who has just realized the stories are true.

By the second week, three of the twelve could feel it too.

* * *

We made camp below the collapsed shrine where I'd found the prophecy book, because I wanted to see it, and because the pilgrims deserved to see where it began—not the grand places, the Sunspire, the ward beneath the world, but a smuggler's cache full of wet paper and a man too tired to know he was reading his own death date.

The shrine had been partially restored. Pilgrim hands, mostly—rough mortar, mismatched stones, an altar that was really just a flat boulder with a carved mouth on it and a hundred little wax stubbs melted around its base. Someone had swept out the rubble. Someone had left fresh-cut firewood under an oilcloth.

Betha stood looking at the place where the bookshelf had been, the gap in the wall I still knew the shape of better than my own hand.

"This is where you found it?" she asked. "Your name? Your date?"

"Under a loose flagstone. Water-damaged. Smelled like rat droppings and century-old ink." I crouched, touched the stone out of habit. "I thought my whole life had been written before I lived it. I thought the best I could do was die well instead of badly."

"What changed?"

I thought about lying, or giving the tidy answer, the sermon answer: *the truth set me free.* But these were pilgrims, and pilgrims deserve honesty or nothing.

"A goblin who couldn't stand being irrelevant," I said. "A woman who took a blade meant for me and died singing. A smuggler who chose her brother over her reputation and then chose us too. A priestess who heard holy music in nothing at all. And an inquisitor who committed treason one misreported location at a time until treason was just called doing right." I stood. "The book gave me two doors. My friends built a third one out of spite and stubbornness. That's what changed."

The widow—her name was Corla—spoke without turning from the altar. "My husband died in the pass two winters ago. Rockfall, during the tremors. I've hated these mountains since." Her jaw worked. "I keep waiting to feel angry standing here. I only feel... listened to."

"That's him," I said quietly. "That's Veyl. It spent a hundred years holding shut a door with its own voice, so it never got to hear anyone. Now it listens. Everything you've ever prayed into the quiet—it kept all of it."

Corla wept then, without sound, which seemed fitting, and the other pilgrims pretended not to notice in the kind way, and I stood in the ruined shrine where fate had found my name and felt—for the first time in my life, I think—that I was standing inside my own story rather than someone else's.

* * *

On the nineteenth day we climbed above the treeline, to the shelf of bare granite where we had buried Ysolde.

Her cairn still stood. Seraphine had warded it with Choir-work before she left—she'd burned her inquisitor's seal in Greyhollow's square, then walked south to rebuild the Choir from the inside, God help her, and before she went she'd laid a working on the stones that no weather, no animal, no zealot's spite could trouble. The cairn was neat as the day we finished it. Someone had left flowers anyway. Mountain asters, gone to seed, their stems tucked carefully between rocks.

I let the pilgrims rest and walked the last stretch alone.

"Ysolde," I said. The wind took the name politely and gave nothing back, which was right. She'd never been one for ghosts. "Still teaching me, if you're wondering. Half the time I open my mouth to lead a rite and hear your corrections before I get the first syllable out. *Slower, Oscar. Silence is not a pause, it's a presence.* You were insufferable." I sat on the cold stone beside her cairn. "You were right, though. About everything. About the liturgy, about the god, about me. You told me once that an oath isn't a chain. It's a duet. I didn't understand it then."

Below me the pass opened up gold and green, the new road Maren's clans had cut toward Greyhollow, the smoke of honest chimneys, tiny figures moving on ordinary errands. Above me Thornfell rose, quiet, sealed, sleeping under a ward renewed not by a dead god's last breath but by a living man's stubborn yes.

Veyl stirred in me—or I stirred in Veyl; after eight months the distinction had worn thin—and the silence around the cairn filled, just slightly, with something like a held note. The same note Ysolde had been singing when she died. I hadn't imagined it. I'd checked a hundred times. Every time I came here, the mountain hummed it back to me, unfinished, patient, waiting for me to pick up the next line whenever I was ready.

I wasn't ready yet. Maybe next year. Maybe on some anniversary, some ordinary Tuesday, forty years from now if I was lucky. Veyl could wait. Waiting was the first thing it had ever taught me.

"I brought them," I said. "Twelve. Taking all twelve home. You'd have liked the acolyte. She hears it already—really hears it, the way you did, before anyone taught you. I'm going to send her to Pip when we get back. Try to talk her out of the robes. You know how that goes."

Wind. Stone. The long unfinished note.

"Yeah," I said. "Love you too."

* * *

We came down out of the high country on the twenty-third day, all twelve, sunburned and thinner and carrying less than they'd brought, because guilt doesn't survive altitude. Betha walked beside me on the last stretch of road, and when Greyhollow's rooftops appeared through the trees she stopped and stared at the town as if seeing it for the first time.

"They'll want to know what it's like," she said. "The listening. How do I explain it?"

"Don't," I said. "Tell them what it isn't. It isn't answers. It isn't thunder. It's just the sense that when you pray, somebody stayed in the room." I shrugged. "After that, everyone works it out for themselves. That's the whole theology, near as I can tell. Pip keeps trying to write it down and it keeps coming out too short."

Betha laughed—a real laugh, the first I'd heard from her—and something in my chest that had been clenched since Ysolde fell loosened another degree. Healing, I'd learned, wasn't an event. It was erosion. Slow, patient, in the right direction.

At the gate, Pip was waiting with the entire congregation behind them—maybe sixty souls now, outcasts and skeptics and ex-zealots and one extremely smug smuggler queen—and they cheered us in like we'd conquered something, which I suppose, in a very small way, we had.

Maren found me at the gate. Looked me over. Counted heads.

"All twelve," she said.

"All twelve."

"And?"

I looked back up at the pass, where the evening light was going long and violet over Thornfell, and the mountain hummed its low unfinished note, and somewhere beneath it a war-god slept and a silent god listened and a road wound on that no prophecy had ever named.

"And I'm thinking about the spring routes," I said. "There's a village past the eastern shrines that's never had anyone come teach them the rites. And the high valley folk—they've been praying into nothing for generations. Somebody should tell them it stopped being nothing."

Maren studied me, and then she laughed, loud and delighted and entirely unrepentant. "Oscar Fairweather. Twenty years a smuggler, one year a martyr, and now you want a *parish*."

"Not a parish. A road." I smiled. "Just a road. With me on it."

That night the congregation ate together in the library yard, under Pip's windows, and the prayers went up wordless into the dark, and everything I'd ever sworn to heard every single one.

It was enough. I remember thinking, watching Betha show Corla how to shape the opening rite, that whatever came next—whatever the books said or didn't say—

It was more than enough.

Then Pip dropped out of the library window, landed in front of me, and held up a letter bearing the broken seal of the Radiant Choir, southern chapter, and said, "Oscar. Seraphine's found something in the reliquary vaults. Something the old Cantors hid even from themselves. She says—we need to come south."

The mountain, very softly, agreed.
