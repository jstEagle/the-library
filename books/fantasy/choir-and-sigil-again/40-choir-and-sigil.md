## 40. Choir and Sigil

A year is not so long a thing, once you've watched a god remember its own name.

Oscar Fairweather woke before dawn on the anniversary of his death and lay still for a while, listening to the mountain hum.

That was the only word for it. Thornfell had always been a quiet place — the kind of quiet that pressed on your ears like deep water, the silence of a thing holding its breath. Now the silence had texture. It had warmth in it, the way a room has warmth after someone has just left it. Some mornings he could almost pick out words in the hum, the way you can almost pick out faces in a fire. He'd stopped trying months ago. Veyl would speak when Veyl had something to say, and mostly what the god said was nothing at all, said generously.

He dressed in the dark, took up his pack by feel, and let himself out of the house without waking anyone.

The house was Maren's, technically — a squat stone thing above Greyhollow's lower quarter with a crooked chimney and a view of the pass, bought with smuggler money that had gone, as she put it, "semi-legitimate." Pip had claimed the attic because it was full of old beams and older dust and connected, via a gap in the stonework no adult human could have negotiated, directly to the town library's archive wall. Tobin slept in the ground-floor room because he still didn't like stairs, or doors he couldn't see around, or being far from an exit. He was getting better. That was the word they all used around him, gently, like setting down something breakable. *Better.*

Oscar took the switchback trail upward, past the last of the houses, past the terraced fields the pass-clans had planted in the spring, past the shrine — new-built, cedar and river-stone, no idol inside because Veyl did not want one; the congregation had asked and the silence had answered *no* so warmly that half of them wept — and up beyond the treeline, where the air went thin and clean and honest.

Ysolde's grave stood on the shoulder of the mountain, above the mist, facing east.

It was a cairn, not a headstone, because Ysolde had been a choir-priestess once and had told them, in one of her dry, sideways moods near the end of that last terrible autumn, that her people sang their dead into stone. "Stack it high enough," she'd said, "and the wind makes music of it. That's the whole liturgy, really. Everything else is commentary." So they had stacked it high, and the wind did make music of it, a low hollow flute-note that came and went, and if you were very quiet — and Oscar was always very quiet, it was his whole profession and then his whole life — you could hear the note and the mountain-hum agreeing with each other, two silences harmonizing.

He sat down beside the cairn and got out his flask and poured a measure of the good whisky onto the stones, because she'd been insufferable about the good whisky and would have risen from the grave to mock him for drinking anything else.

"Morning," he said.

The wind took the note again.

"Big day," he said. "You knew that. You'd have made a joke about it. Something awful. Something about punctuality." He drank. Below him, Greyhollow was waking — lamplight going out window by window as hearth-light replaced it, smoke standing up straight from thirty chimneys in the windless hour. A year ago this had been a border town you passed through on your way somewhere worse. Now there were pilgrims' tents on the common, and a bell in the library tower that Pip had requisitioned through channels Pip refused to describe, rung three times a day not to call anyone to worship but simply, as Pip put it, "to remind the silence we're here."

One year ago tonight, the prophecy said, Oscar Fairweather died.

He turned his hand over and looked at the sigil.

It lived under the skin of his palm now, the way a scar lives there — except scars don't glow faintly when the weather changes, and scars don't itch when someone within a mile tells a lie, which made card games with Pip a permanent injustice. The mark of Veyl, redrawn. Not the oath-mark he'd carried since he was nineteen years old and desperate enough to swear his life to a silent god for the privilege of hearing nothing back. This was the same mark remade from the inside, the way a wall becomes a door. When Ashkarath's first finger had breached the stone, when the ward had been fraying like rope over an edge, when every written path ended in ruin — sealing death, freeing doubt, slow fraying — Oscar had done the only unwritten thing left. He had offered the god not his ending but his continuing. *Take my oath instead of my death. Let me be the ward that walks.*

He hadn't known if it would work. That was the point. Every path in both books was written; refusal meant stepping off the page entirely, into blank vellum, into whatever came next. It had turned out that what came next was a hundred years of silenced prayers, all at once, pouring through a single mortal man like flood through a culvert. He didn't talk about those minutes much. There weren't words for most of it, which he supposed was fitting. But he remembered the sound. Gods, he remembered the sound — every prayer Veyl had swallowed for a century coming home, midwives and murderers and children and kings, all of them saying *please* in a hundred tongues, and the silence that had held them like cupped hands finally opening.

*He lived,* Seraphine had said afterward, sitting in the ash of the pass with her inquisitor's seal in pieces at her feet, looking at him like a woman rereading a book she thought she'd finished. *That was never one of the options.*

*That,* Oscar had said, *was rather the idea.*

He finished the whisky and stood, and did what he had come up the mountain to do, though he would have told you — had told Maren, who hadn't believed him — that it was just a walk.

He touched two fingers to the cairn, the way the clans do, and started down.

* * *

Pip was waiting for him at the library steps, which meant Pip had been waiting for some hours, because goblins hear human boots on gravel from a long way off and pretend otherwise for the pleasure of saying *I knew you'd come* with maximum smugness.

"You're late," Pip said.

"I'm early."

"You're late for yesterday. I finished the restoration in the night and I've been dying." Pip fell into step beside him, short legs working double-time, satchel of pens bumping their hip. Pip had grown into the title of Archivist of the Silent Congregation the way a vine grows into a trellis — completely, and with visible satisfaction. There were now four apprentices living in the library walls, all goblins, all ferociously territorial about the shelving. "The binding took. Aldous's glue, the good batch, not the one that smells like regret. And I collated it against the Choir's copy — Seraphine's copy, the real one, not the doctored liturgical text — and Oscar, it's *whole.* For the first time in four hundred years, the prophecy exists complete and correct in one binding, and every doctored line is marked, and the original line is *there*, on the last leaf, exactly where they cut it out of every copy they burned their way through." Pip stopped on the steps, and the smugness drained out of their face, leaving something younger underneath. "I checked it nine times."

"And?"

"And it says what you already know it says." Pip's ears dipped. "'And should the Sigil-Bearer refuse the ink — refusing both the believing death and the doubting one — then the ward shall fail, unless a living oath be offered in its stead.' That's it. That's the line they cut. One sentence. They amputated one sentence and built four centuries of doctrine on the stump."

"They were protecting the world," Oscar said. It wasn't forgiveness, exactly. It was just accurate. "Or they thought they were."

"Everyone in this story was protecting something." Pip shrugged, and the shrug dislodged the smugness, which crawled back on. "Anyway. It's downstairs. It's yours. Well — it's ours, technically, it's a congregational document now, but tonight it's yours, because tonight's the—" Pip stopped. Looked at him. Goblins are hard to read for humans, mostly, but Oscar had spent a year learning, and what was on Pip's face was the same expression they'd worn in the walls of the old library, the day they'd decoded the first book: terrified exhilaration, aged a year into something steadier. "Tonight's the date."

"Tonight's the date."

"The date you were supposed to die."

"Mm."

"And instead you're going to write in the book."

"That's the plan."

"It's a terrible plan. You know that. Archivally speaking. You can't write in a restored codex, the ink will—"

"Pip."

"—bleed, the binding's barely cured, I'll have to supervise the humidity—"

"*Pip.*"

Pip shut their mouth. Folded their arms. Unfolded them, because folded arms were a human gesture and Pip had decided years ago not to perform humanity for anyone's comfort.

"Yes," Pip said quietly. "All right. Yes. Come on, then. Before I talk myself out of letting you touch it."

* * *

The reading room of Greyhollow's library had been a smuggler's counting-house once, and before that, if the foundations were anything to go by, a shrine to somebody even older than Veyl. Oscar liked that about it. The town kept its history in layers, like sediment, like the strata of a grave mound; nothing here was ever demolished, only buried and built upon. The room held two long tables, lamps with green glass shades, and a smell of paper and tallow and mountain cold that had soaked into the stone so thoroughly it would outlast everyone currently alive.

The book lay open on the center table under a brass weight shaped like a dove. Two books, rather, bound as one — the smuggler's cache volume and the Sunspire reliquary volume, water-damaged and fire-scarred respectively, married along their spines with new thread and old care. Prophecy of the Sigil-Bearer, said the title page, in a hand a century dead. Oscar stood over it for a while without touching anything.

Maren was leaning in the doorway. She must have come in behind them; he hadn't heard her, which never stopped being remarkable, given everything. She had her arms crossed and her jaw set at the angle that meant she'd been crying earlier and had handled it.

"Tobin's lighting the lamps on the common," she said. "There'll be singing later. Actual singing, apparently. Ysolde's people do it every year now — well. They started this year. Everyone decided anniversaries were a tradition immediately, without the awkward century of doing it first." She came forward and looked down at the book. Her thumb moved over her fingers, the way it did. Three years of guilt, one year of penance in the pass, fighting like a woman paying back every debt at once — she'd settled most of them. The last one she owed to herself. "Seraphine's coming up from the shrine. Said she wouldn't miss it."

"She doesn't have to—"

"She wants to. Don't make it strange, Oscar." Maren looked at the empty final leaf. "So. You're really doing it. Writing in it."

"You think I shouldn't."

"I think," Maren said, "that a year ago I watched you tear your name out of that thing with your bare hands, on fire, underground, with a god's finger coming through the floor of the world, and it worked, and I've been frightened of you ever since. In the good way." She grinned, quick and crooked, the old smuggler's grin with the new softness under it. "Write small. Pip will kill us all if the ink bleeds."

They came, in the end, the way the family came — without announcement, without ceremony, drawn up the mountain roads by whatever instinct binds people who have buried one of their own together. Seraphine arrived in ordinary clothes, which still startled him; she wore grey wool like any traveler, and her hair was shorter, and her hands were a scholar's hands again instead of a hunter's. She stood at the table's corner and read the restored line — *unless a living oath be offered in its stead* — and closed her eyes briefly, and said nothing, and stayed. Tobin came in with lamp-oil on his sleeves and took the chair nearest the door and angled it, as always, toward the exit, and nobody minded, because the room had arranged itself around his needs so smoothly that it took an outsider to notice it was deliberate. Pip hovered. Pip produced ink — iron-gall, properly aged, "the only acceptable choice, don't argue" — and a nib ground fine, and supervised the humidity with a small dish of water and an expression of immense suffering.

Outside, the sun went down behind Thornfell, and the mountain hummed, and the anniversary of Oscar Fairweather's death began.

"What will you write?" Seraphine asked.

Oscar thought about it.

He thought about the boy he'd been at nineteen, kneeling in a shrine with an oath in his mouth and nothing answering, buying silence at the price of his whole life and calling it faith. He thought about the mercenary he'd been, guiding killers through passes, professionalism worn like armor over loneliness. He thought about a collapsed shrine and a water-swollen book and his own name in century-old ink, and the vertigo of discovering you are a pawn, and the longer, stranger vertigo of discovering the board could be flipped.

He thought about Ysolde, who heard holiness in silence and died mid-liturgy with her song unfinished — and whose song, he now knew, had been finished by everyone else. That was the thing about choirs. The composer never needs to live to the end. The song carries.

He thought about a war-god sleeping under snowfall, dreaming uneasy dreams that were growing gentler year by year, because the ward around it now was not a dead god's sacrifice but a living man's promise, renewed daily, the way promises are. Some nights Ashkarath's dreams reached him, faint, the way distant thunder reaches you through a wall. Lately the dreams had been only landscapes. Empty battlefields going green. Even hatred sleeps better when it's warm.

He thought about the pilgrims on the common, outcasts and skeptics and broken soldiers and one reformed Inquisitor, gathering around a god whose entire theology was *listen.* He thought about Tobin laughing last week — actually laughing, sudden and startled, at one of Pip's jokes, and how Maren had gone out to the yard right after and stood there a long time with her face in her hands, and they had all pretended not to see, because that too was liturgy.

"He lived," Oscar said, "and it was enough."

He dipped the nib. He wrote it beneath the ancient lines, in plain script, no flourishes — he'd been a sign-painter's apprentice before he'd been a soldier, and he still believed a thing plainly said beats a thing beautifully said, on doors and on gravestones and in prophecy alike:

*Here ends the Prophecy of the Sigil-Bearer. On this date the named death did not occur. The bearer chose his companions over his destiny, his oath over his ending, and the unwritten road over the written ones. He lived, and it was enough.*

He set down the pen. The ink dried dark and ordinary and permanent.

Pip leaned over it for a long moment, checking letterforms, checking bleed, finding none, and then sat down abruptly on the bench as if their knees had been quietly removed. "It's done," Pip said. "It's — that's a primary source now. Of its own refutation. Do you understand what you've—no, of course you don't, you're a mercenary." But Pip was smiling, wet-eyed, and patted the page once with two fingers, the way you'd pat a sleeping animal.

Seraphine read it through twice, silently, lips moving. Then she said, in the voice of a woman performing her last act of doctrine — carefully, correctly, one final time: "The Church would call this blasphemy. Writing yourself into scripture." A pause. The corner of her mouth moved. "I've burned my seal. I'm no longer in a position to object."

Maren laughed, and Tobin laughed with her — a small laugh, quickly spent, but real — and outside, faintly, the singing started on the common, a dozen voices, then more, no melody Ysolde had taught but one that fit the shape she'd left in the world anyway. The mountain hum rose to meet it.

Oscar stood at the table with his family and listened.

And in the silence between the verses — the true silence, the generous one, the cupped-hand silence he had sworn his life to and been answered by at last — he heard, unmistakably, for the second time in his life and the first time in a year, Veyl speak.

Not thunder. Not command. Just two words, in a voice like every silenced prayer at once, warm as a hearth seen through a window from the cold:

***Well written.***

Oscar Fairweather smiled, closed the book, and blew out the lamp — because the room didn't need it anymore, and because somewhere below, his people were singing, and because he had a guide commission at first light: forty pilgrims, the long road, the high passes, mountains that hummed.

The dead god slept. The living ward walked home.
