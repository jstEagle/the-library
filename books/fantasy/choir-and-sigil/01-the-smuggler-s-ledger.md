## 1. The Smuggler's Ledger

The mountain told Marguerite it was going to snow three days before the sky did.

It said so in the small ways she had learned to read: a spring above the treeline running cloudy with ground-silt, a marmot colony gone silent on the south scree, the wind swinging around to the north and holding there like a man deciding whether to say something cruel. She had logged all of it in her head, in the same place she kept the ledger of roads — which passes were open, which were watched, which had simply stopped existing since the last time anyone needed them.

Tonight the ledger said: *Cairn Hollow. The low road under the Widow's Teeth. Two hours ahead of the storm, if the storm keeps its word.*

She crouched at the mouth of the pass with her hood back, letting the cold bite her ears awake, and counted her cargo by feel. Six crates of unlicensed iron, stamped with a forge-mark from a town that didn't officially exist. Three bolts of grey silk that would fetch more per yard than the iron weighed. One sealed letter, wax black, no name on the outside, which she had agreed not to read for double her usual fee and had not agreed to carry at all until she saw whose seal it was.

Twelve years of this. Twelve years since she had walked out of the order's chapterhouse with nothing but her sword, her oath-name, and a silence she kept like a wound she was afraid to look at.

*Don't think about it,* she told herself, as she told herself every night. *You're a smuggler now. Smugglers don't have pasts. They have ledgers.*

"Move up," she called softly, and the mule train shifted in the dark behind her, six animals and two hired men who knew better than to ask questions. Tam, the older one, had been with her four years. He was good with the mules and bad with silence, which made them a fair match.

"You feel that?" he asked, for the third time since dusk.

"I feel everything. That's the job."

"No, I mean—" He stopped, listening. "It's too quiet. Even for up here."

Marguerite listened. He was right, and she hated that he was right. Thornfell was never quiet; it muttered and groaned and ticked as the frost worked into stone, as scree settled, as wind found its way through gaps it had spent ten thousand years widening. A mountain was an instrument always being played badly. But tonight the instrument had stopped, and the silence had a texture to it, a held-breath quality, like the pause before a choir takes its first note.

She put the thought away where she put all thoughts that didn't earn coin.

* * *

The Widow's Teeth were two granite spires leaning toward each other over a slot of road barely wide enough for the mules, and beneath them ran the low road — a smuggler's invention, half tunnel, half overhang, carved by nobody and maintained by everybody who couldn't afford the king's highway. Marguerite knew every handhold of it blindfolded. She knew which section of roof had fallen in the year of the red summer and been shored up with ship's timber. She knew the exact stone, third from the left at the second bend, that rang hollow when struck because the mountain had a cavity behind it like a rotten tooth.

She also knew, though she had never told anyone, that the low road had not been carved by smugglers at all. The tool-marks were wrong. Too fine. Too old. And every few hundred yards there were symbols cut into the rock at shoulder height, worn nearly smooth, that she could still read if she let herself — and never let herself.

Oath-script. The writing of the swordspeakers, used to bind wards into living stone.

Someone had warded this road a very long time ago, and the wards were still working, and Marguerite walked beneath them twelve times a year pretending they were just decoration.

"Underhill." Tam's voice, tight. "The mules won't—"

They wouldn't. The lead animal had planted itself at the entrance to the slot, ears flat, doing the stubborn arithmetic mules do when their instincts and their training disagree. Marguerite went forward, put her hand on its neck, and felt it trembling.

"It's the storm coming," she said. "They smell it."

"They smell *something*."

She looked down the slot. In the dark between the Teeth, the air had a faint grey shimmer, like heat off a summer road, except it was winter and the shimmer was cold. She had seen that once before, years ago, high on the Devourer's Stair, and had told herself then it was ice-fog catching starlight.

She was running out of years in which telling herself things worked.

"Blindfolds on the leaders," she said. "We go through quick and quiet."

* * *

They came out the far side into a bowl of hanging valley, and that was where the sky broke.

Not clouds breaking — she knew every way clouds broke, had a whole taxonomy of them in her head, cumulus and knife-clouds and the woolly banners that meant wind at the pass. This was different. The snow did not fall so much as *arrive*, in sheets, in walls, as if somewhere above the peaks a door had opened onto a country made entirely of winter and the door was being held wide. The temperature dropped so fast her breath cracked audibly in the air. The stars went out not behind cloud but behind whiteness, erased top-down, like a page being wiped clean.

Tam swore. The younger man, Pell, started reciting a prayer to Saint Aldric, patron of lost travelers, which Marguerite considered a poor choice of saint given the circumstances.

"Waystation," she said. "There's one two miles east. Old chapel, dry roof, hearth that draws. We make it or we don't."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we're a story someone tells later." She pulled her hood forward. "Keep moving."

But even as she said it, some part of her — the part she kept locked below the ledger, below the roads, below the careful competent surface of Hazel Joubert's most reliable narrator — that part had gone very still, the way a deer goes still, the way she herself had once gone still in a chapterhouse courtyard at nineteen while an old woman traced a sigil in the frost and said words Marguerite had sworn never to speak again:

*When the winter comes without a season, and the snow falls upward, remember your name.*

The snow was falling upward. She could see it now, in the flashes between gusts — flakes rising past her face, climbing, as though the sky were drinking the mountain.

Her hand found her sword hilt without her permission. The steel hummed, faintly, a note she hadn't heard in twelve years.

"Marguerite," she said aloud, to the storm, to herself, testing the sound of it. Not the oath-name. Just the name. The safe one.

The mountain did not answer.

But far below, down through the rock and the roots of the world, something else did — a single clear tone, like a bell struck once under a lake, and in the whiteout ahead of her, where the road should have been, the air split open along a seam of light.

A man fell out of it.

He fell the way people fall in dreams — wrong, unhurried, limbs loose, snow parting around him as if reluctant to touch him — and landed in a drift twenty feet ahead of her mules, and lay still.

Marguerite stood in the roaring white with her hand on her humming sword and her heart going like a war drum, and made the first decision of the rest of her life.

She hated it already.

"Tam," she said. "Hold the train."

And she walked toward the hole in the world.
