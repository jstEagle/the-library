## 2. A Man Out of the Snow

The storm had a wrongness to it that Marguerite could taste before she could name.

She'd weathered forty winters in the Thornfells, or close enough — she'd stopped counting somewhere around the year she stopped counting anything — and she knew every flavor the mountain could throw at her. The dry hiss of wind-scoured snow. The wet, heavy fall that meant a warm front climbing the western face. The needle-sleet that came off the high ice and sang against stone like a struck wire.

This was none of those. This snow fell *sideways and upward*, flakes rising past her lantern like ash from a fire burning below the world, and it was warm. Each flake melted on her glove with a faint sting, like a spark that hadn't finished dying.

"Bad," she told the mule. The mule, whose name was Debt because she'd bought him on credit and never forgiven either of them, laid his ears back in agreement.

She was three hours out of the Hollowmere waystation, deep in the Kettle Pass, where the road narrowed to a shelf of rock with a drop on her left so sheer the wind came up it sounding like breath drawn through teeth. The shipment was small this run — sealed tins of emberleaf tea, a bolt of grey silk that was probably not just silk, and a letter she hadn't read, sewn into Debt's saddle blanket, addressed to someone in Lowmark who paid very well for not being asked questions. Ordinary work. Honest work, if you didn't look too closely at the word *honest*.

She should have turned back at the treeline. She knew that. But the pass closed behind her by nightfall regardless, and the waystation ahead had walls and a hearth, and Marguerite Underhill had spent eleven years learning that the only direction worth moving was forward, because behind her there was nothing but an order that wanted her dead and a name she no longer said aloud.

So she went forward, into snow that fell the wrong way, and told herself the crawling feeling between her shoulder blades was just the cold finding the gap under her collar.

It wasn't the cold. She'd known what cold felt like since before she could walk. This was older than cold. This was the mountain holding its breath.

* * *

The rift opened forty yards ahead of her, without warning, without sound — which was itself the loudest thing about it. A seam simply appeared in the falling white, a vertical line of air that wasn't air, glowing a pale bruise-violet, and along its whole height the blizzard bent away from it as though the snow refused to touch whatever was behind that light.

Marguerite froze. Debt did not; he screamed — an actual scream, a sound she'd never heard a mule make — and hauled sideways, nearly dragging her off the shelf. She got a fist in his halter and held, boots skidding on ice, heart slamming, and watched the seam widen like a wound deciding to open.

A man fell out of it.

Not stepped. *Fell* — the way things fall when they've been dropped rather than arrived. He hit the shelf of rock hard, rolled, and lay still, and the rift snapped shut above him with a sound like a page tearing, and then there was only the blizzard again, falling its wrong warm way, and a body in the snow.

Every instinct Marguerite owned, all eleven years of them, said the same thing: *walk around him.* Whatever came through a tear in the world was not her problem. Her problem was a mule, a shipment, and a warm room two hours north. Strangers who fell out of the sky were the kind of thing that got smugglers killed, got oath-breakers found, got names spoken aloud in rooms where names were hunted.

She walked toward him.

She hated herself for it even as she did it — hated the softness, the stupid reflex of it, the girl she'd been before the order taught her better. But her feet were already moving, and later she would tell herself it was practical: a corpse on the pass drew wolves, wolves drew hunters, hunters asked questions. Practical. That was the story she'd go with.

He was alive. Barely. Young-ish — thirty, maybe, though the snow had already bleached the color out of him so thoroughly he looked carved from something pale. His clothes were wrong. Not foreign-wrong, not Lowmark wool or Vellish linen, but *wrong*-wrong: dark trousers of some fabric that shed snow like oil, a coat with a zipper — she didn't know the word yet, but her fingers would find the little teeth of it later and worry at them like a puzzle — and shoes made of some soft grey stuff with rubber soles, useless on ice, absurd on a mountain. He looked like a man dressed for walking to a shop.

There was blood in his hair. One hand was burned, the skin reddened in a perfect ring around each finger, as though he'd been holding something that had decided to leave.

"Hey." She slapped his cheek, lightly. Then less lightly. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

His eyes opened. They were brown, unfocused, and full of an absolute, unshakable conviction that he was somewhere else.

"'M going," he slurred, "to be late."

"You're going to be dead." She got her shoulder under his arm. He weighed more than he should have, or the altitude was thinner than usual, or both. "Up. Come on. Up, or the snow keeps you."

He stood, mostly. He was tall — taller than her by a head, loose-limbed in the way of men who'd never had to fight for their footing — and he leaned on her with the full trusting weight of the half-conscious, which she resented immediately and completely.

"My name's Elias," he said, into her hood, as she dragged him along the shelf. "Elias Vane. I was just — I live on Corben Street, I was walking home, it's *April*, why is it—"

"It's the last week of Frostwane," she said. "Deep winter. And you're in the Thornfell mountains, in a country called—"

She stopped. Because saying the country's name aloud, in this place, in this season, with the snow falling upward — some old instinct, older than her oath, older than her exile, put a hand over her mouth from the inside.

"—never mind," she said. "Walk."

Debt followed, trembling but obedient, and the three of them moved through the blizzard while the man on her shoulder kept murmuring about spring. About a street called Corben. About how he'd been carrying groceries, he thought, or letters — he couldn't find them in his pockets, and kept patting himself with the slow bewildered diligence of a drunk looking for keys, until finally his hand found something inside his coat and closed around it and he went quiet.

The waystation appeared out of the white an hour later, exactly when she'd calculated it would: a low stone chapel, abandoned by its congregation a century ago, adopted by smugglers ever since. Its door still swung. Its hearth still drew. Somebody — her, two seasons back — had left split wood stacked under the eave.

She got Elias Vane onto the bench beside the fire, stripped his ridiculous coat off him, wrapped him in her spare blanket, and set water heating. He shivered in great rolling waves, teeth chattering so hard she could barely understand him, and she worked with the brisk impersonal efficiency of someone dressing a wound they wished wasn't there.

Then she saw the parchment.

It had fallen from his coat when she pulled it off — a scrap, torn raggedly, no bigger than her palm. Old paper, foxed and brittle, the edges browned as though it had spent decades folded in a book. There was writing on it in faded iron-gall ink, a careful antique hand, and she picked it up meaning only to identify it, meaning only to know what she'd saved and whether it was worth anything—

—and read her own name.

*Marguerite Underhill.*

Not similar. Not a coincidence of common syllables. Her name, spelled correctly, in full, in handwriting that had been old before she was born.

The kettle began to sing. Somewhere outside, the blizzard went on falling its wrong way, and the mountain held its breath, and Marguerite Underhill stood in a ruined chapel holding a hundred-year-old piece of paper with her name on it, feeling eleven years of careful, competent solitude come apart at one seam like the seam the man had fallen through.

"What is this?" she heard herself ask. Her voice came out flat and cold and steady, which was how she knew she was frightened.

Elias Vane blinked at her from the blanket, firelight moving on his face. He was coming back to himself in pieces — she could see it happening, the confusion reorganizing into questions.

"That?" He frowned. "I don't — it was in my coat. It's been in my coat for—" He stopped. Started again, slower, testing each word like thin ice. "I found it in a book. A week ago. At an estate sale. An old poetry book, leather, falling apart. That was tucked inside. I don't know why I kept it. I don't know why I had it *today*. I was walking home, and the air went strange, and there was a light, and—"

"Read it to me," she said. "All of it. Exactly."

He took the scrap carefully, wincing with his burned hand, and held it near the fire, and read:

"*On the last night of Frostwane, in the year the long winter comes, a door opens in the Kettle Pass, and a man falls through who does not belong to any winter. And the Underhill woman shall find him, and the Underhill woman shall save him, though she hates herself for the saving. And what follows, follows.*"

Silence. The fire popped. Snow ticked warm against the window, melting where it landed.

"That's tonight," Elias said slowly. "That's — the date. That's today." He looked up at her, and she watched the rational man in him reach for the reasonable explanations and find none within arm's reach. "And you're—"

"Don't say it." She crossed the room in three strides and took the scrap out of his hand. "Don't say my name. You don't know what hearing it costs, in certain company, in certain places. You especially don't say it." She turned the paper over. Blank on the back. Old. Real. She knew old paper; she carried enough of it. "Where is the book?"

"I don't know. The estate sale was — home. Wherever home is from here." He laughed, a short unhappy sound with no humor in it. "This isn't happening. I want to be clear that I understand it's happening, because I'm sitting in it, but I also want to register formally that it can't be happening. People don't fall through — through *what*, exactly? What was that thing?"

"A rift." The word came out of her before she could stop it, in the same voice she'd used for oaths, years ago, in another life. "A door between here and somewhere else. They're supposed to be closed. All of them. They've been closed for a hundred years."

"How do you know that?"

Because she'd been there when the last one shut. Because she'd been made to help shut it. Because she belonged to an order whose entire sacred purpose had been sealing those doors, until the order decided the purpose was inconvenient and buried it, and buried the covenant with it, and buried her along with both.

"Trade secret," she said.

He stared at her. He was shivering less now, color coming back into his face, and up close his eyes were sharper than the falling-through-the-snow version of him had suggested. A clever man. A man who noticed things. She filed that away as a liability.

"You knew my name was on this," he said. "Before you read it. I watched your face."

"I knew it was trouble. Trouble has a look."

"And you saved me anyway." He said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "You could have walked past. Don't think I didn't notice you deciding not to. You stood there for a good ten seconds weighing it."

"Four," she said. "And it was about the wolves."

"Right." He almost smiled. It was a tired, cold, lost-man smile, and it did something unfair to her chest, and she turned away from it to tend the fire. "Well. Whoever wrote that thing a century ago, they got one thing wrong."

"One thing?"

"They said you'd hate yourself for saving me." He pulled the blanket tighter. "You don't seem the type to waste time on self-hatred. Seems inefficient."

She fed a split log to the flames and watched it catch, and said nothing, because the truth was she'd been hating herself since the moment her feet started moving toward him, and she was doing it right now, and she suspected she would keep doing it for exactly as long as knowing him remained cheaper than the alternative.

Outside, the warm snow kept falling upward-past-her-window, wrong as a heartbeat heard from the wrong side. And beneath the chapel floor, so faint she might have imagined it — might have, if she hadn't spent her childhood learning to feel it through her boots — the mountain hummed. One long, low note, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

Like something waking up.

Elias heard it too. She saw it land in his face, saw him go still, listening.

"What is that?"

Marguerite looked at the scrap of parchment in her hand, at her own name in a dead woman's careful ink, and felt the next several days of her life rearrange themselves around her like furniture in the dark.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we're going to find out."
