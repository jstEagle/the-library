## 3. What the Canticle Says

Elias Vane had always believed that the universe was legible.

Not kind — he'd never confused the two — but legible. Water ran downhill. Debt compounded. Grief faded at a predictable rate, like paint on a south-facing wall. He had built his whole life on the assumption that if you paid attention, kept accounts, and asked reasonable questions of reasonable people, the world would eventually give you a reasonable answer.

The mountain had answered him with a hole in the air.

Now he sat in a waystation chapel that smelled of cold wax and mouse droppings, wrapped in two blankets that smelled of smoke and something older, watching the woman who had dragged him out of the blizzard pretend very hard that he didn't exist.

She had introduced herself an hour ago, in the tone of someone doing him a discourtesy she intended never to repeat. *Marguerite Underhill.* Then she'd gone to the far end of the chapel to dry her gear by the fire, arranged her sword within arm's reach — he wasn't supposed to notice that, but he did — and stopped speaking entirely, as if conversation were a currency she refused to spend on strangers who fell out of the sky.

Elias looked down at his own hands. They had stopped shaking about twenty minutes ago, which he took as evidence that he was not, in fact, dead or dreaming. Dead men's hands didn't warm by fires. Dreaming men didn't feel blanket wool itching at their collarbones.

Which left the unbearable possibility that all of this was real. That he had been walking home from his sister's house on an evening in late April, mud underfoot and birdsong in the hedges, and the world had simply opened like a mouth.

*I was walking home in spring,* he had told her, and watched her face do something complicated and closed. *It's April. It was April.*

*Not here,* she'd said, and said nothing else.

He pulled the parchment scrap from his breast pocket again. He'd been checking it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The paper was wrong for anything he owned — heavy, fibrous, yellowed at the edges like it had spent decades near heat. The ink was brown, iron-gall gone rusty with age.

Three words, in a hand that made his stomach turn every time he looked at it, because the handwriting was his own.

*Ask Underhill.*

That was all. Ask Underhill. As if he had written himself a note before falling through the sky, as if some version of him had known where he was going and had left instructions the way you'd leave a note on the kitchen table: *milk needs collecting, ask Underhill.*

Except the ink was a century old. Marguerite had held it over the fire's light, angled it, done something with a small brass loupe she carried, and her expression when she handed it back had been the closest thing to kindness he'd seen from her — which is to say, it was pity, briefly unhidden.

"Where did you get this?" he asked her now, across the chapel, because he had asked it four times already and she had ignored him four times, and five felt like a number with more authority.

"You should sleep."

"It's my handwriting."

"I know." She fed a split of pine to the fire without looking at it. Her hands were extraordinary — scarred, precise, moving with the indifference of long habit. "That's what I've been trying not to think about since you walked out of a hole in the air holding it."

* * *

Sleep declined to come. Eventually Elias gave up on the blankets and did what he had spent his life doing when the world refused to make sense: he went looking for records.

The waystation chapel was a low stone building half-buried against the mountainside, the kind of place travelers used and priests forgot. One room, one hearth, a rough timber altar with a cloth so sun-bleached its original colors were anyone's guess. Along the back wall stood a shelf, and on the shelf sat the leavings of a hundred years of sheltering travelers: chipped mugs, a boot, a coil of rope, three tallow stubs fused into one, and books.

Most of the books were hymnals gone green with damp. But at the back, wedged upright behind a loose board as if someone had wanted it found only by people who went looking, was a volume bound in grey leather soft with age, its title stamped in letters that might once have been silver.

*CANTICLE OF ASH.*

Elias told himself he picked it up out of boredom. He was aware, even as he did it, that this was a lie. He picked it up because the name meant nothing to him and he needed it to mean something, because if there was anywhere in this impossible country an explanation of holes in the air and century-old notes in his own handwriting, it would be in the one book somebody had bothered to hide instead of abandon.

He carried it to the hearth, angled it toward the light, and opened it.

The first pages were liturgy. Call-and-response verse, the kind he associated with harvest festivals and funerals, addressed to something called *the Voice in Winter* — which struck him as an odd thing to sing to, but mountains bred odd religions, he supposed. He skimmed. His eye caught on phrases and slid off them: *the white road, the toll of breath, the door between the choir and the congregation.* Old poetry. Faded faith. Nothing to crack a man's worldview.

Then, a third of the way in, the voice of the book changed.

The earlier verses rhymed, roughly, in the manner of things composed to be sung. These lines didn't. They were set down differently too — the same even hand throughout, but here pressed harder, faster, the letters leaning forward as though the writer couldn't wait to be finished and equally couldn't stop.

*In the hundredth year of the silence,* the page began,

*a man will come out of season.
He will fall as snow falls — unwilling, unweighed,
out of a sky that has no business opening,
on the night the stars stand crooked over Thornfell.*

Elias read it twice. Then a third time, moving his lips, because some part of his mind had decided that if he could just pronounce it correctly the words would resolve into sense.

*On the night the stars stand crooked over Thornfell.*

Marguerite had said something like that, dragging him out of the drifts, half to herself, her breath smoking: *the sky's wrong tonight.* He hadn't understood. He still didn't understand. But he remembered the exact angle of her voice when she said it, and the exact angle of the stars above the pass when he'd looked up, moments later, and felt his understanding of *up* quietly come apart.

He turned the page.

*His name is not written here,* said the Canticle of Ash, *because names are doors, and we have sealed enough doors.*

But then, lower on the page, smaller, as if added later by a different pressure of pen:

*He carries a message he does not know he carries.
He will bring it to the Underhill woman.*

Elias sat very still.

The fire popped. Wind pressed at the shutters the way a large animal tests a fence. At the far end of the room Marguerite had finally slept, or pretended to, one arm flung across her eyes, her breathing deliberately even in the way of people who wanted to be believed asleep.

*The Underhill woman.*

He looked over at her. Marguerite Underhill, who had appeared out of a blizzard at the precise coordinates of his arrival like a rescue staged in advance, carrying a sword old enough to predate the nation he'd thought he lived in and a hatred she wore like armor plate.

The book knew her name. A book bound before his grandparents were born, hidden in a wall of a chapel that smelled of mice, knew her name, and knew his coming, and had put the two together the way you'd note that rain arrives with cloud.

His rational worldview — that well-built, well-lit house he had lived in all his life — developed its first crack. Not a dramatic one. Just a thin line of cold air where there shouldn't be cold air, a draft through the floorboards of everything he believed.

He kept reading, because a man with a draft in his house either fixes it or freezes, and he had never yet frozen.

*She keeps the roads no map remembers,* the Canticle said. *She speaks the speech that is not speech, and the mountain answers her, though she has sworn not to call it.*

*Do not fear her coldness. Coldness is what survives.*

*She is lonelier than she knows, and more necessary than she will ever admit, and when the hour comes she will choose rightly, though it break her.*

Elias closed the book.

His hands were shaking again. He noticed this with clinical detachment, the way you notice weather from indoors. The fire had burned down to a red heart; the shadows in the chapel had deepened and grown patient. He became aware that he had been reading aloud — not loudly, but aloud — the way people do when they cannot trust their eyes, and that the last line had left his mouth in the sleeping silence of the room:

*"Though it break her."*

"It lies," said Marguerite.

He nearly came out of his skin. She was standing at the edge of the firelight, and he had heard nothing — nothing — not the blankets shifting, not a footfall, nothing. She moved the way certain animals move, all intention and no announcement.

"The book," she said. "Whatever you think it just told you. It lies."

"Does it?" His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Because it knows your name."

"It knows everyone's name. That's what's wrong with it." She crossed to the fire and stood looking down into it, and up close he could see what exhaustion had been hiding: she wasn't angry. She was afraid. It restructured her whole face, the fear, took ten years off it and made it younger and much worse to look at. "There are copies," she said, flatly, reluctantly, each word extracted like a tooth. "My order kept them. Kept it. Read it to novices as scripture. Every swordspeaker for a hundred years has been able to recite the verses about the man out of season."

"And?"

"And none of them mention that he arrives carrying paper in my name. None of them mention—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The copies differ. Small things. Details. As if whoever wrote it couldn't keep the story straight, or kept changing it. Prophecy that revises itself isn't prophecy. It's bait."

"So you burned yours."

Her head came around sharply. "How do you—"

"You smell of smoke," Elias said, "but you haven't burned wood tonight except pine, and pine doesn't smell like that. That's vellum smoke. Book smoke. You burned something before I woke." He shrugged, feeling absurdly like a man confessing to cheating at cards. "I'm an accountant. Or I was, twelve hours and one universe ago. I notice ledgers that don't balance."

For a long moment she stared at him, and something passed over her face that he would spend a long time afterward trying to name. Recognition, maybe. The unpleasant experience of being seen accurately by a stranger.

"Yes," she said. "I burned mine. Twenty years ago, the day I stopped being what they wanted me to be." She held out her hand. "Give me that one."

"No," said Elias.

The word hung between them, enormous. He was surprised at himself; she looked genuinely startled, as if the mountain had spoken.

"That book says I fell out of the sky on a specific night," he said, "in front of a specific person, whose name it got right, along with the fact that I don't know what message I'm carrying. I have a note in my own handwriting that's older than my grandfather telling me to find you. I am standing in the wrong season, in the wrong world, and the only object in this entire country that addresses any of it is in my lap." He looked down at the grey cover, at the title stamped in dead silver. "You can burn yours. You've earned your reasons. But I memorized contracts for a living, Ms. Underhill, and if you take this one from me I'll simply have most of it in my head anyway, and you'll have made an enemy of the only person who walked out of the sky specifically to be on your side."

"I didn't say you were on my side."

"The book did. That's rather the point."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. It wasn't a smile. It was the ghost of where a smile had once been executed, a memorial plaque. She looked at him for the space of three slow breaths.

"Keep it, then," she said. "Memorize it. It won't matter."

"Why not?"

"Because the Hollow Choir burns what it can't control," Marguerite said, "and they're already on the mountain. I saw their lanterns in the pass before the storm closed it. They'll have smelled the same smoke I made, twenty years ago, and they'll want to know what else caught." She turned back toward her bedroll, then paused, and without looking at him she said, in a voice stripped of everything but fact:

"They don't burn the Canticle, Mr. Vane. They burn the people it talks about. Sleep lightly."

* * *

He did not sleep lightly. He did not really sleep at all.

Somewhere past the deepest hour of the night, Elias sat wrapped in his blankets with the Canticle open on his knees, feeding its verses into memory the way he'd once fed column figures into his head — because memory was the one vault no one had ever picked, and because Marguerite was right about one thing: the book would not survive the week. Books that predicted people got burned along with them. He could feel that truth approaching the way you feel weather change.

*She will choose rightly, though it break her.*

He copied the lines into himself, patiently, hating how much he wanted them to be false and how much he wanted them to be true. Because if the book was right about the date, and the rift, and the woman — then it might be right about the rest. About the message he didn't know he carried. About the hour coming. About a choice.

And underneath all of it, quiet as sediment, lay the question he could not stop turning over, the crack in the floorboards widening by fractions:

If a book a hundred years old described the moment of his arrival down to the crookedness of the stars — then had he fallen through the sky, or had he been *sent*? And by whom? And why had whatever wrote those hard-leaning, hurried lines sounded, to his ear, less like a prophet foreseeing the future

and more like someone leaving instructions?

Outside, the wind changed direction. Beneath the wind, so faint he might have invented it, came another sound — many voices, distant, moving up the mountain in unison.

Singing.

Marguerite's eyes snapped open across the dark room, and she was already reaching for her sword.
