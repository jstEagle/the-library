## 4. The Hollow Choir Sings

The chapel had one door and Marguerite hated every inch of it.

She'd chosen the waystation for its walls, not its exits — dry timber, a hearth that still drew, shutters that had held against forty years of Thornfell weather. But walls were a promise you made to the world, and the world had a way of calling in that promise with interest. One door, two shuttered windows, and a snowpack so deep against the north wall you could climb it like a stair. She'd noted that last part when they'd arrived, and noted it again now, lying awake on the chapel floor with her back against the cold stone and her sword across her knees.

Elias slept by the fire like a man who had never once been hunted. Flat on his back, hands folded on his chest, breathing in the slow even rhythm of someone whose worst problem that morning had been weather. He'd tried to give her the only blanket. She'd refused. He'd looked hurt. She'd decided not to care, and had cared all night anyway, which was its own kind of insult.

The Canticle's pages were still a gray scatter of ash in the hearth. She'd burned her copy at dawn — or what passed for dawn under the blizzard, a dim thickening of the dark — and Elias had watched her do it with an expression she couldn't read. Not horror. Something worse. Understanding.

"You burned yours," he'd said. "So I memorized mine."

"That's not how books work."

"That's exactly how books work," he'd said, and tapped his temple. "Paper burns. This doesn't."

She'd wanted to hit him. She'd wanted, worse, to ask him to recite it. She'd done neither, and now the fire had burned down to a red eye in the grate and the wind had changed, and Marguerite lay in the dark listening to the change and feeling it in her teeth the way you feel a note too low to hear.

Wind through Thornfell came around from the west nine nights in ten. It came from the north when a front was turning. It came from the east — down the mountain, cold and clean and wrong — when something was coming down the mountain with it.

She'd heard that east wind twice before in her life. Once the night her order died. Once the night she'd run.

She sat up.

* * *

She woke him with a boot to the sole, gentle by her standards. "Up. Quiet."

Elias came awake the way he seemed to do everything — completely and without grace, jolting upright, one hand patting the floor for a pack that wasn't there. "What — is it the storm?"

"The storm's the least of it." She was already moving, snuffing the lamp, dragging the last of the fire's heat into embers with the poker. "Get your coat. Get behind the altar. If I say run, you go out the north window and down the snowpack and you don't stop until the trees, and then you keep not stopping."

"Run where? Marguerite, we're in a blizzard on a mountain—"

"Then you'll run in circles and die warm, which is more than they'll give you." She pulled the shutters' inner bar free and set it aside, soundless. Through the gap between shutter and frame she could see nothing but white on white, the world erased. But she could *listen*.

The east wind carried voices.

Not many. Four, maybe five, moving with the unhurried spacing of people who knew where they were going and believed God was holding the door. They were singing. That was the thing that put ice in her spine where the cold couldn't reach. Nobody sang on a mountain in a blizzard. Nobody sang *in unison*, in a dead language she'd spent eleven years not thinking about, in a hymn that rose and fell like breathing.

The Hollow Choir sang when they hunted. She'd read the accounts, back when there were accounts to read, back when the order's remnants still passed warnings along the smugglers' lines like a disease. *They come in white. They sing so you can hear them coming, because they believe fear is the first verse of confession. They will offer you a chance to kneel. Take it or don't, but know that kneeling has never once saved anyone.*

She'd thought the accounts were exaggerated. Zealots always got exaggerated. It was how zealots recruited.

The singing stopped.

In its place: silence, and under the silence, boots in snow, and under the boots, a voice — clear, male, unhurried, pitched to carry through timber. "Travelers within. We know you're there. The mountain told us."

Elias whispered, "How—"

"Shut up." She was counting. Four at the door. One circling — she could hear him, or thought she could, a fifth rhythm out of phase with the wind, moving toward the north wall. Toward the snowpack. Toward the window she'd just told Elias to die through.

She revised the plan.

"The man who speaks," the voice continued, "is Brother Anselm of the Hollow Choir. We are not bandits. We are not thieves. We are the mercy of the realms, and we have come a long way through the cold to offer it." A pause, and when the voice resumed it was almost gentle. "The man who fell through the snow. We know what he is. We know what he carries. Give him to us, woman, and you can walk away. This is the only night that offer stands."

Marguerite looked at Elias. In the ember-light his face had gone the color of the ash in the hearth. "What do they think I am?" he breathed.

"A door," she said. "They think you're a door that has to be nailed shut." She drew a slow breath. "They're not lying about the offer. That's the worst part. They'll let me walk. They want *you*."

"Then give me to them."

The words landed like a dropped stone. She stared at him. He stared back, and she saw he meant it — saw the arithmetic behind it, plain and terrible. *One man I've known for a day, against whatever this is. Even trade. Better than even.*

"No," she said.

"Marguerite—"

"I said no." It came out of her harder than she'd meant, hard enough that he flinched, and she realized she couldn't explain it, not to him and not to herself, except that she had spent eleven years walking away from everything — the order, the oath, the names of the dead — and she was so tired. So tired of walking away. "Get behind the altar. Now."

The north wall creaked.

She'd known it would. She was already moving when the shutter burst inward in a bloom of snow and a figure came through it in white robes gone gray with weather, a long knife low in one hand, and Marguerite did the thing she had sworn for eleven years not to do.

She drew her sword.

It came out of the oilcloth by the hearth with a sound she felt in her sternum — not the scrape of steel, but something older, a note, a single held note like a finger lifted from a wet glass rim. The zealot heard it too. She saw him hear it, saw his head turn, saw the knife pause mid-stroke, and in that half-second of attention she put her shoulder into him and took him down through the remains of the shutter and out into the snow.

The cold hit her like a wall of hands. The blizzard was a living thing, horizontal white, a world with no floor and no sky, and somewhere in it the other four stopped singing and started running.

She came up out of the snowdrift with her back to the chapel wall and the sword between her and the white. The fallen zealot scrambled away from her, crabwise, and she saw why a moment later.

The blade was glowing.

Not bright. Not burning. It held a light like moonlight through ice, a pale blue-white that had no business existing in a storm at night, and it was *singing* — or she was, or the mountain was, a resonance climbing up through her boots and her bones and out through her arm into the steel, and the words were in her mouth before she knew she'd chosen them, before she knew she'd been carrying them for eleven years like a stone behind her breastbone:

"*Thornfell, I am thy voice. Answer.*"

The mountain answered.

She felt it the way you feel lightning decide. The snowpack on the north wall — the one she'd been measuring as a stair for an enemy — let go all at once, a white avalanche shrugging down over the window and the breach and the man who'd come through it, burying him to the chest. The wind itself seemed to bend, curving around the chapel, and in the sudden pocket of stillness she could hear everything: four sets of boots converging, the buried man's panicked breathing, Elias shouting her name through the ruined shutter, and beneath it all, deep beneath, a sound like a vast slow bell being struck once, far under the stone.

The first zealot came around the corner at a dead run, knife raised, faith in his eyes.

She didn't want to kill him. That surprised her — she'd assumed she'd have to want it, that the sword would make her into something, and instead she was only herself, terrified and furious and more awake than she had been in a decade. So she didn't kill him. She spoke the second word.

"*Turn.*"

It wasn't a word. It was a key. The mountain's voice came up through her and out of the blade and the ground under the running man *tilted* — not much, a hand's width, a treacherous shift of frozen scree beneath the snow — and his footing went and his lunge became a fall and her pommel came down on the back of his skull, once, precise, and he was asleep in the drift.

Two down. Three running.

Her heart was going like a hammer and her breath was smoking and the sword sang in her grip like something starved finally fed, and she understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than the Choir did, that she had missed this. That some sealed room in her had been waiting eleven years for exactly this and had decorated it and kept the lights on.

*That's the trap*, said a voice that sounded like her old swordmaster. *The power was always the trap. It's how they got the order in the end. Not defeated. Seduced.*

The remaining three stopped at the edge of the swordlight, in a rough crescent, and did not attack. The one in the middle — older, gray at the temples, the voice that had offered mercy through the door — raised both empty hands.

"Sister," he called. "That word. *Turn.* Only the swordspeakers spoke it. The order is dead." His eyes, when they found hers, were not fanatical. They were worse. They were *sad*. "Who are you, that the mountain remembers you?"

"Nobody," Marguerite said. "A smuggler. Walk away."

"The man inside is named in the Canticle of Ash. He is the hinge of a broken covenant, and if he reaches the heartstone, the winter comes down on both worlds and never lifts. We have read the same prophecies you burned, sister. We have spent our lives preparing for tonight." He took one step closer, snow to his knees, hands still open. "You felt the mountain answer you. You felt how it *wanted* to. Ask yourself who taught it that hunger. Ask yourself what it will demand when it's fed." Another step. "Kill us if you must. But the man goes back through the rift, or the world goes under the ice. Those are the only mercies left."

Behind her, through the broken shutter, Elias's voice, ragged: "Marguerite. The bell. Do you hear it? It's getting *louder*."

She heard it. Under the storm, under the stone, the vast slow bell was ringing again — and again — and with each strike the swordlight in her hand pulsed brighter, and with each pulse the words rose hotter in her throat, the next oath-word, the killing one, *Fall*, the word that had brought down the pass at Serel's Ford and buried a hundred of the order's enemies and half the order's honor, the word rising in her like water finding a crack—

And she understood, all at once, what the gray-templed man was doing.

He wasn't attacking. He was *feeding* it. Standing in the snow with his hands open, offering himself, offering his brothers, letting her power climb and climb because every word she spoke made the mountain hungrier and every hunger made her easier to aim. He didn't need to beat her. He needed her to speak *Fall* with Elias standing behind a wall of timber and old mortar.

"Sorrel sent you," she said.

Something moved behind his sadness. "You know the Prelate's name?"

"I know everything," Marguerite said, which was a lie, and then, because the sword was singing and the bell was ringing and she was done with eleven years of lies: "I know *nothing*. That's the problem." She backed toward the shutter, blade up, the light washing the storm white. "Here's my mercy, brother. I'm not going to kill you. And I'm not giving him to you. And when I'm gone, you can dig your brothers out of the snow and think very hard about a god that buries its own faithful."

"Sister—"

"*Turn.*"

The ground tilted. Not at the speaker — she was past that, past aiming at men, past the whole filthy economy of it. She aimed it at the mountain itself, at the cornice above the chapel's eastern face, and the cornice let go with a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and a white wall came down between her and the three of them, and Marguerite turned and dove through the broken shutter into the dark.

* * *

Inside, the chapel was chaos and ember-light. Elias had the altar shoved aside — when had he gotten that strong, or had fear done it — and was staring at the back wall, at the old stone beneath the plaster where the bell-sound was coming from, and the stone was *glowing*, faint veins of blue-white light spreading through it like frost across a window.

"Marguerite, what is—"

"Later. Move." She grabbed her pack, threw him his. "The Choir will dig out in minutes. There's a back way — old pilgrims' stair behind the altar, goes down into the deep passes. It's why I picked this place."

"You said there was one door."

"I lied." She was already hauling at the altar's last foot of weight. "I lie a lot. It's a whole character."

The stone behind the altar split along a seam that hadn't been visible a moment ago — the bell-light had found it, or made it — and cold air breathed out, ancient and mineral, smelling of deep places. A stair, cut and forgotten, descending into black.

Elias looked at the stair. Then at her. Then at the sword, still singing faintly in her fist, its light dying now, dimming like a held breath released.

"What you did out there," he said. "The words. The mountain *moved*."

"I know."

"That's in the Canticle. The swordspeaker's voice. *And the stone shall know her and rise at her word.*" He said it flat, recitation-flat, and she watched him realize what he was reciting and about whom. "Marguerite. It's not just your name in the book. It's what you *are*."

"Later," she said, and meant it this time, because the bell under the mountain had struck again, and this time she felt the strike in her teeth and her sword-hand and the scar over her ribs, and this time she understood what it was ringing *for*. Not a warning. A summons. Something down in the deep passes had felt her speak, had felt the old words move through the stone, and it was calling the voice that had spoken them.

Calling her home, or calling her to account. With the Hollow Choir, those had always been the same thing.

She looked at the stair going down into the dark, at the man the prophecies had written before he existed, at the sword that knew her better than she knew herself.

"Down," she said. "Both of us. Now. And Elias — whatever you heard that bell ringing for—"

"You first," he said, and stepped past her into the dark, and Marguerite, who had spent eleven years walking away from everything, followed the sound of the mountain calling her name.
