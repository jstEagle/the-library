## 5. Roads Only She Knows

**Marguerite**

The waystation burned behind them, orange light stuttering through the pines like a signal neither of them wanted to read. Marguerite didn't look back. Looking back was a habit for people with something to return to.

"Where are we going?" Elias Vane stumbled at her heel, his borrowed coat flapping around him. He still moved like a man who hadn't accepted the ground under him was real.

"Away."

"That's not a direction."

"It's the best one tonight." She cut off the trail at a lightning-splintered fir and dropped into a gully no wider than her shoulders, following a streambed that existed on no map drawn in the last hundred years. The stone here was blue-gray and slick, veined with quartz that caught the storm-light and threw it back in thin silver threads. Thornfell's deep roads. Her roads.

He followed. That surprised her more than the rift had. Most men argued first, then fell, then blamed her. This one just kept walking, breath ragged, asking questions with his silence instead of his mouth.

She hated that she noticed.

"The Choir will sweep the low passes first," she said, mostly to hear whether her voice still worked. "They'll assume we're running downhill, toward towns, toward people. So we go up."

"Up is where the winter's coming from."

"Up is where I know every rock by its first name." She glanced back at him. In the dark he looked hollowed out, cheekbones sharp with exhaustion, but his eyes were working — cataloguing, weighing, refusing to panic. "You want to survive the night, you walk where I walk. You want to ask questions, save them for somewhere warm."

"One question now." He held up a hand, fingers stiff with cold. "In the chapel. When you drew your sword and said that word — the mountain *moved* for you. What was it?"

She kept walking. "A word."

"It wasn't just a word."

"No," she agreed, and left it there.

The gully climbed, bent west through a seam of broken shale, and opened into a hanging valley she'd found eleven years ago, half-starved, fleeing the ruin of her order. A lean-to of stacked stone stood against the cliff face, roofed with old slate, invisible from twenty feet in any direction. She'd built it with hands that hadn't stopped shaking for a week. She'd never brought anyone to it.

She stood aside and let Elias duck in first, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the altitude.

* * *

**Elias**

There were three lines of chalk on the lean-to wall, written in a hand too disciplined to be careless, and Elias read them twice by the light of the small fire Marguerite allowed before deciding it couldn't be seen from the valley floor.

The first line was a tally — dozens of strokes, grouped in fives. Deliveries, he guessed. Years of them.

The second was a name: **UNDERHILL**, carved deeper than the rest, as if someone had needed to prove to themselves the name would hold its shape.

The third was a single oath-word, the same one he'd heard her speak in the chapel when the mountain had answered her like an old dog answering a whistle. She'd scratched it out. The gouge was so violent it had split the stone.

"You memorized the Canticle," she said, without turning from the fire. It wasn't a question either.

"Some of it. The parts about me." He settled across from her, knees drawn up. "Which feels insane to say out loud."

"Everything about you is insane. You fell out of the sky in spring clothing."

"Through a rift. On the ninth of Hoarfrost." He recited it because it was the only thing holding him upright — the words were load-bearing now. "*'On the ninth of Hoarfrost, when the white wind walks, a door shall open in the air above the high pass, and through it a stranger, and the stranger shall carry the covenant in his mouth.'*" He watched her face. Nothing moved in it, but nothing relaxed in it either. "And then there's the next part."

"Say it or don't."

"*'And the Underhill woman shall know the roads that have no names, and lead him to the heart of Thornfell, and the mountain shall remember what men made it forget.'*" He paused. "That's not all of it. There's a verse after. I haven't gotten to it yet. It's long."

Her jaw tightened, once, like a rope taking weight. "The Canticle is a book of ash and guesses. There were forty prophets writing prophecy in the last century and thirty-nine of them are laughed at in taverns."

"And the fortieth got your name right."

"The Underhills were common in these mountains once." She fed a stick to the fire. "Lots of people knew our roads."

"Nobody knows these roads. You said so yourself. Nobody living."

The fire popped. Wind combed the valley, and somewhere above them the long winter groaned — a sound he was learning fast, the sound of cold settling into stone like a tenant who intended to stay.

"I offer you a trade," Elias said. "Everything I memorized — every verse, every date, every name in the Canticle. It's yours. In exchange, you teach me enough of these mountains that I don't get us both killed."

"Why?"

"Because you can't outrun the Choir alone forever, and I can't cross Thornfell alone for a day. Because whatever that book is, we're both written into it, which makes us—" he searched for a word that wasn't *allies*, wasn't *friends*, wasn't anything she could throw back at him, "—*entangled*. And entangled people share information."

For a long moment she studied him the way she'd studied the storm — measuring it, pricing it, looking for the lie inside it.

"Entangled," she repeated, tasting the word like something she suspected of being spoiled.

"It's honest."

"It's ugly."

"So am I, before breakfast." He held out his hand across the fire. "Do we have a trade?"

She didn't take his hand. But she nodded once, sharp as a blade going back into its sheath, and in the language of Marguerite Underhill, he was beginning to understand, that counted.

* * *

**Marguerite**

They moved at grey dawn, up and out of the hanging valley onto a ridge she called the Shepherd's Spine — not its real name; it had no real name anymore, or its real name was one of the things the world had swallowed. From the ridge she could see the waystation's smoke still rising, thin and wrong, and beyond it the low country: farmsteads, a mill, the hamlet of Coldbrant where she'd traded contraband for boots two winters running.

Coldbrant was flying a white banner from its meetinghouse pole.

She stopped so abruptly that Elias walked into her.

"What?"

"Look." She pointed with her chin. "White banner on the meetinghouse. They only fly that when the village is declaring itself against something. Bandits. Tax collectors." She hadn't seen it flown against her before. "Us."

"They know we're coming?"

"They don't need to know us. The Choir's been in those villages for weeks, telling them what the winter means." Her stomach turned as she said it, because she could hear the sermon already, could write it herself, having grown up downwind of that kind of certainty. "They're saying the early snows are the fae's doing. That the veil between worlds is thinning and letting the cold pour through, and that anyone who helps a stranger might be carrying fae-taint. That travelers on hidden roads are servants of the enemy."

"But some of that's true," Elias said slowly. "Isn't it? The veil *is* thinning. The rift—"

"The truth makes the best mortar for a lie. You pack the lies in between and the whole wall holds." She started moving again, faster now, angrier. "Those are good people down there. Hard people. People who owe me nothing and helped me anyway, because mountain folk help travelers, that's the whole religion up here. And the Choir has spent a season teaching them to be afraid of exactly the virtue that keeps them alive through winters like this one."

Elias was quiet for fifty paces. Then: "In the Canticle there's a verse about that. About the villages." His voice had changed — flatter, careful, a man handling something that might still be hot. "*'The shepherds shall bar their folds against the lost, believing the wolf comes wearing a traveler's face.'*"

"Stop."

"I'm just telling you—"

"You're telling me the book agrees with me. I heard." She didn't slow down. "I don't need scripture to confirm my own eyes, Vane. That's what the Choir has. I'd like to keep at least one thing they don't."

He fell silent, and she felt the silence between them change shape — not hostile, exactly. Wounded on both sides. She almost apologized. She filed the impulse away with all the others she didn't have time for.

* * *

**Elias**

By midday they came down out of the high ground toward a place Marguerite called the Larder — a cave system where she cached supplies along her routes. It sat behind a waterfall frozen into a curtain of blue glass, and Elias decided he would never stop being astonished by the sheer *practicality* of her secret world. Every cache marked with knife-cuts in stone. Every route doubled with a fallback. A geography built entirely out of distrust, worn smooth by years of use.

She went pale at the second cache.

The supplies were untouched. The seal on the oilcloth was hers, unbroken. But nailed to the support timber was a sheet of cheap print-paper, the kind the Choir ran off their presses in the valley towns, and the ink was fresh enough to smell.

Elias picked it up before she could stop him.

*"TO THE GOOD PEOPLE OF THORNFELL,"* it began, in type so bold it seemed to shout. *"THE WINTER COMES EARLY BECAUSE THE DOORS HAVE BEEN LEFT OPEN. THE FAE STEAL YOUR CHILDREN'S NAMES AND GIVE YOU NOTHING BUT COLD. AND NOW THEY SEND THEIR AGENTS AMONG YOU — A WOMAN WHO SPEAKS WITH A FORKED TONGUE AND CARRIES A BLADE THAT ANSWERS TO HELL, AND A STRANGER NOT OF THIS WORLD, WHOM SHE SHelters IN DEFIANCE OF GOD AND MOUNTAIN BOTH."*

The S in *shelters* had been dropped by a tired typesetter. Otherwise it was polished. Otherwise it was *specific*.

"They know," Elias said. His mouth had gone dry. "They know about both of us. They printed this before we even reached the waystation."

Marguerite took the paper from him. Read it once, all of it, top to bottom — he watched her do it, thorough as an auditor. Then she folded it with great care, as if it might go off, and held the corner to the little flame she used for heating tea.

"There's a price on us," she said, watching it burn. "There always is, eventually. Look at the bottom."

He looked. Beneath the screed, a figure: **FORTY CROWNS**, and beneath that, smaller: *"INFORMATION CONCERNING EITHER PARTY, TEN."*

"Forty crowns buys a lot of silence in a bad winter," she said. "Ten buys a little. And the Choir knows this winter is going to be very, very bad. They're not just hunting us. They're making sure every hungry farmer between here and the heartstone is their eyes."

"Then we avoid the farmers."

"We've been *resupplying* off the farmers for two days. My routes run cache to cache, but caches empty." She kicked snow over the burning paper, and for a moment Elias saw her do the arithmetic he'd seen her doing since the ridge — counting miles against food, distance against pursuit, and coming up short in a column she didn't name. "We'll be fine," she said, in the tone of a woman informing a room.

"We won't," Elias said quietly. "But keep saying it. It helps."

She gave him a look that should have opened a wound, and then — unexpectedly — the corner of her mouth twitched, one heartbeat, and was gone. "Canticle-man. Any verses about starving in a cave?"

"Several. They're mostly metaphors."

"Lucky us."

* * *

**Marguerite**

They left the Larder by a route she had never shown another living soul: a chimney of stacked boulders behind the frozen falls, up through a crack in the mountain's shoulder, out onto a shelf of wind-scoured granite that ran east along the spine for six miles before dropping into the next valley. From up here the whole world was white and blue and brutally clean, and the long winter lay visible to the north — not weather but a *wall*, a standing horizon of cloud and falling snow, patient as a tide.

Elias stood beside her looking at it, and she heard him breathe in through his teeth.

"How long?" he asked.

"If it isn't stopped? Weeks. Maybe less." She turned her face from it. "My order thought it would take a century to arrive. We were wrong about a lot of things."

"You've never told me what happened to your order."

"You've never told me what you did back home. Before you were a prophecy."

"Taxes," he said. "I assessed taxes for a merchant guild. I walked roads too, just... flat ones, with signs on them."

She laughed before she could stop it — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering it could swing. He looked pleased with himself, damn him, and she looked north instead, at the wall of winter, and made her decision the way she made all of them: quickly, and alone, and then informed the other party.

"There's a village ahead. Ashenmere, it was called. Three days' walk, one day if the ice bridge holds." She kept her voice level. "It's the last place I can buy provisions without going back through Choir country. After that we climb into the heart of Thornfell, and nobody buys or sells anything up there. It's us and the mountain and whatever the Canticle says waits at the top."

"And if the village has white banners too?"

"Then we steal what we need and apologize to their ghosts." She shouldered her pack. "One more thing, Vane. If it goes wrong in there — if they corner us, if it comes to blades — you don't fight. Whatever nonsense is in your head about helping. You run the route I show you and you don't stop until the ridge. Forty crowns says they want me alive or dead doesn't matter. Ten crowns says they only need you pointed at."

"Why does that matter? Ten crowns is ten crowns."

"Because it means they fear what you might become more than what I already am." She started walking, calling the last of it back over her shoulder. "And people who fear what you might become will pay any price to make sure you never do. Keep up."

Behind them, far to the north, the long winter shifted its weight, and the first flakes of the evening's snow began to come down — fine as ash, and falling, even here, even this far south, in shapes that almost looked like letters.

Elias caught one on his sleeve and read it, and went very still, and said nothing, and walked faster.
