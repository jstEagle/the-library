## 6. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The snow had come early to the high valley, and it came wrong.

Elias noticed it before he understood it. Three days out from the waystation chapel, following Marguerite down a track that was barely a suggestion in the rock, he'd been cataloguing everything strange about this world the way a drowning man catalogues waves. The moons — there were two of them, one pale and one rust-colored, and Marguerite navigated by their argument more than by either alone. The trees, black-barked and patient. The way she said *left* and meant something other than left.

But the snow was wrong in a way that needed no translation.

It fell without wind. It hung in the air like ash over a fire that had already gone out, drifting in no direction at all, as if the sky had simply stopped holding it up. And it made no sound underfoot. He'd walked behind Marguerite for two hours through six inches of it and heard nothing but breath — hers measured, his ragged — and the creak of her sword's scabbard against her hip.

"Is it always quiet like this?" he asked.

She didn't turn. "Snow crunches."

"It's not crunching."

"I know." She stopped at the ridgeline and looked down into the valley, and Elias came up beside her, grateful for the halt, and then immediately wished he weren't.

There was a village below them. Maybe forty houses, stone-footed and timbered, smoke rising from chimneys in thin gray threads that rose perfectly straight, as if drawn with a rule. A church or shrine at the center with a bell tower. Fields half-buried, fences, a mill on a frozen stream. Everything a village should be.

And not one light burning. Not one dog barking. Not one sound except the snow falling its soundless fall.

"That's Ashforden," Marguerite said. "Or it was." She frowned, and Elias watched her frown deepen into something he hadn't seen on her face before — not calculation, not the flat vigilance she wore like armor, but confusion. Genuine, unarmored confusion. "That's Ashforden," she said again, slower, as if testing whether the words still worked. "I've run that valley eleven times. There's a woman there — Marta, keeps the waystation stores, owes me for — "

She stopped.

"You owe me," she corrected herself quietly. "No. *They* owe me. For goods." Her hand went to her temple. "I don't remember what goods."

"Marguerite."

"There were goats." She said it like a man grasping at a rope end. "Marta kept goats. Or I kept goats. Someone kept goats." She shook herself, hard, like a bird shaking off water, and when she looked at him her eyes were clear again and furious with themselves. "We're going down. Stay close. Don't drink anything you don't see poured."

"That's not — Marguerite, what just happened?"

"The snow got in my head for a second." She started down the slope. "It won't happen again."

But it did. Twice more before they reached the outskirts — once when she miscounted the fence posts along the lane, counting aloud and losing her place at four; once when she stood in the middle of the road and called him by another name entirely, a name that meant nothing to him, and then stared at him with blank courtesy, like an innkeeper greeting a stranger, until whatever she'd lost rolled back in like a tide and she swore at herself in a language he didn't know.

* * *

The first person they saw was a man sweeping snow off his own doorstep.

He swept with long, even strokes, unhurried, content. He swept the snow, and the snow fell behind him, and he turned and swept it again, and his face while he did it was the face of a man doing exactly what he had always wanted to do. Elias had never seen such peace on anyone. It was the peace of a pond with nothing living in it.

"Hello," Elias said.

The man looked up and smiled. It was a good smile, warm, arranged well in his face. "Hello," he said pleasantly. "Welcome."

"Thank you. We're just passing through — we need shelter for the night, and food if you have it."

"Of course," the man said warmly. "Whatever you need."

"What's this place called?"

The smile stayed exactly where it was. Nothing moved in it, and yet somehow it became empty, the way a room becomes empty when someone takes the furniture out one piece at a time while you blink.

"What's it called," the man repeated, tasting the question like a word in a foreign language. "Called." He looked past Elias, down the street, at the shuttered houses and the straight gray smoke. "It's called — " His brow furrowed, faintly, mildly, the way a man might frown at weather. Then the frown smoothed away and the smile came back. "It doesn't matter. Welcome. Whatever you need."

Marguerite's hand had gone to her sword hilt. She moved past the man without touching him, and Elias followed, and neither of them spoke until they reached the square by the bell tower.

"They're not sick," Marguerite said. "Sickness looks different."

"He didn't know his own village's name."

"No." She was scanning the windows. Faces appeared in some of them — a woman with flour on her hands, an old man, a child — all of them watching with mild pleasant interest, none of them opening a door, none of them calling out. "Elias. Look at the doorposts."

He looked. Beside every door in the square, carved into the wood, there were marks. He thought at first they were decorative — spirals, knots, interlaced patterns — and then he looked closer and saw they were letters, worn shallow by decades of hands, and that many of the doors had the same letters repeated, carved over and over, deeper each time, as if someone had been trying to drive the word into the wood itself.

One doorpost had a fresh carving, splinters still pale around the grooves: three letters, gouged deep and desperate.

*ASH—*

The last letter unfinished.

"Marta," Marguerite breathed. "This is Marta's house." She pushed the door open. It wasn't locked. Nothing here would occur to anyone as needing to be locked.

Inside was warm. Bread sat part-eaten on the table, going stale beside a cup of milk with a skin on it. Goats' milk, Elias realized, from the smell of the animal somewhere behind the house. A life interrupted mid-motion — and then tidied, gently, by hands that no longer knew why any of it mattered.

A woman sat in the corner chair, wrapped in a shawl, smiling at them.

"Marta," Marguerite said.

"What a lovely visit," the woman said.

"Marta, it's me. It's Marguerite. You know me. I come through twice a year, I bring you — " She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I bring you things. What do I bring you, Marta?"

"Oh, you shouldn't trouble yourself," Marta said kindly, and patted the arm of her chair. "Sit. Have some bread. It's a good winter. It's all a good winter now."

"Marta." Marguerite crossed the room and knelt, took the woman's hands. They were strong hands, flour-dusted, a carver's callus on the right palm matching the marks on the doorpost. "Your name. Say your name."

"Why, it's — " Marta smiled her lovely empty smile. The pause stretched. Somewhere outside, the snow went on falling without sound. "It's — what a lovely visit," she finished, radiant, serene.

Elias felt the floor tilt under his understanding. He'd been framing this wrong since the ridge — thinking in terms of sickness, enchantment, a spell laid over the valley. But this wasn't a veil dropped over the village. This was subtraction. Something had reached into forty houses and taken, precisely, the load-bearing pieces: names, debts, reasons. And left everything else standing, painted, smiling.

And the doorposts — the carvings weren't decoration. They were people fighting. People who felt the thing taking them and had scratched their own names into wood because paper burns and memory could be eaten but a gouge in an oak stays. Some of them had won that fight long enough to carve. None of them had won it entirely.

"Margarita," he said suddenly.

Marguerite's head snapped toward him. "What?"

"Nothing. Sorry. Your name just — " He'd been reaching for it in his own head, the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found the shelf bare. "Say your name. Right now. Out loud."

"Marguerite Underhill." It came fast and clean, reflexive — and then her face changed. "Underhill," she repeated. "Underhill is — " Her grip on Marta's hands loosened. Her eyes drifted to the window, to the straight gray smoke, and her expression began smoothing. Not into fear. Into peace. Into the terrible pond-calm on the sweeper's face. "It doesn't matter," she said softly. "None of it matters. It's a good winter. It's all a good winter now — "

"*Marguerite.*"

He grabbed her shoulders and shook her, hard enough that her teeth clicked, and for a moment she looked at him with that same mild pleasant interest, and Elias understood with a cold, total clarity what it would mean if it stayed — that the woman who knew every road in these mountains would become a smiling stranger kneading bread in a corner, that the only person who could get him to the heartstone would stand in this kitchen forever saying *what a lovely visit* —

Then her eyes focused. Focused, and flooded with horror.

"The doorpost," she rasped. "The sword. Give me — "

He already had it — her hand had been groping for the hilt and he'd shoved it into her grip. She dragged the blade half out of the scabbard, twisted her wrist, and scored a single letter into the inside of her own left forearm. Blood welled bright against the white room. She stared at it, breathing hard.

"An oath-name?" Elias asked quietly.

"A letter of one." She let the blade slide home. "The order's practice. We cut our oath-names into our arms so the flesh remembered what the mouth might forget." Her laugh was short and awful. "I thought it was vanity. Ritual. Old men loving old habits." She looked at Marta, still smiling in her chair, and at the bread going stale on the table, and Elias watched shame move through her like weather. "They knew. A century ago, they knew what breaking it would do, and they trained for the forgetting instead of stopping it. They built a lifeboat and let everyone else drown."

* * *

They did not stay in Marta's house. Marguerite wouldn't. They took the shrine by the bell tower — stone walls, one door, sightlines down every lane — and Marguerite paid a smiling man three coins for firewood that he accepted with warm incomprehension, and they barred the door and built the fire up and sat on either side of it while the soundless snow buried the village a little deeper.

Elias couldn't stop working the problem. It was how he was built; wherever he'd been walking when the rift took him, he'd been a man of ledgers and explanations, and explanations were the only rope he had left.

"It's selective," he said. "That's what frightens me. If it were brute force, if it scrambled everything, fine — damage. But look at what it takes. Names. Debts. Obligations. The *relational* memories. Everyone in that village can still sweep a step, bake bread, feed a goat. It leaves the skills and eats the bonds." He stared into the fire. "Do you understand what that is? That's not a blizzard. That's the covenant failing."

"Explain it to me like I'm stupid. I'm too tired to be proud."

"A covenant isn't a contract between two kings. It's the connective tissue. It's the promise-layer underneath everything — the reason a name means something, the reason a debt binds, the reason a village is a *village* and not forty people near each other. Your order broke the promise between the realms, and the promise-layer is fraying everywhere at once. Winter is just the part we can see. The forgetting is the part that gets you before you freeze." He looked up. "The Canticle says the long winter swallows both worlds. I don't think swallow means cold anymore. I think it means *blank*."

Marguerite was quiet for a long time. Across the square, through the shrine's narrow window, he could see the sweeper still working, patient and happy, erasing nothing, accomplishing nothing, in the falling snow.

"My oath-name," she said finally. "You want to know it."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to. Everyone does, eventually, and then they use it." She turned her wounded forearm toward the firelight, studying the raw letter. "Vess will want it too, if the fae are what the Canticle says. So hear it once, in a stone room, while I still own it: I am Marguerite Underhill, sworn of Thornfell, oath-name **Ashgrave**." She said it flatly, like a soldier reciting. "Named for the graves we made of our promises. My teachers thought it was humility." She pulled her sleeve down. "I think they knew what was coming, and they named us for it."

"Ashforden," Elias said slowly. "The village name. *Ash.*"

"Don't."

"Marta was carving ASH. Your arm says — "

"*Don't.*" But her voice cracked on it, and he saw her do the same arithmetic he'd just done: that the thing eating the valley had gotten far enough into her to reach for her first letter, that the snow on the ridge had known where to dig. That she was not a traveler passing through this sickness. She was related to it.

She banked the fire with more force than the coals required.

"We leave before dawn," she said. "North fork of the river, then the high road under the shoulder of the mountain. Five days to the heartstone basin if the weather holds, seven if it doesn't." She lay down with her back to him, sword in arm's reach, the way she slept. "Sleep, Elias."

He didn't. He sat by the fire with the Canticle verses he'd memorized turning over in his mind, and somewhere past middle-night he noticed the silence had changed quality, and he rose and looked out the window and felt his stomach drop.

The villagers were gathered in the square. All of them. Forty-some souls standing in the soundless snowfall in their nightclothes, faces tilted up, smiling gently at the sky. And they were humming. One low note, held together, unvarying, and it was only after a full minute of listening that Elias understood why it raised the hair on his arms:

It was almost a song. Almost a hymn. It had the shape of music with all the meaning scooped out of it — a choir singing the memory of a hymn, a hollow where devotion used to live.

*A Hollow Choir,* some cold voice in him observed, *would sound exactly like this.*

He woke Marguerite. She came to the window, looked out, and went very still. Then very pale.

"That's not them," she whispered. "Elias. That note. Listen to the pitch." She was already pulling her boots on, hands steady with practiced terror. "That's a swordspeech pitch. Low register. Someone is *singing the mountain*, and the mountain forgot the words, and the village is echoing back the shape of the hole." She cinched her swordbelt. "Something is using this valley's emptiness as an instrument."

"Choir?"

"Worse. Choir rides and kills. This is patient. This is — " She stopped. Through the window, at the far end of the square, a figure had appeared among the smiling villagers. Tall, robed in white that drank the lamplight, and where its face should have been there was only smoothness, and it was conducting them — one hand rising, falling, patient as snowfall — and the villagers' hum bent obediently after it, up, up, toward a note that made Elias's fillings ache and the window glass sing —

Marguerite drew her sword, wrenched open the shrine door, and spoke her first word of power since the waystation.

The mountain answered.
