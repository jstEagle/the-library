## 7. Oath-Bound

They ran through the dark hours, and the mountain ran with them.

Marguerite led by feel more than sight — a hand on wet stone, a breath of cold air from a side-vent meaning *drop below*, a silence in the wind that meant *stop*. Elias followed without complaint, which she was beginning to suspect was his most dangerous quality. Men who complained you could read. Men who simply watched, and remembered, and waited — those were the ones who got you killed.

Behind them, faint as a bell rung underwater, the Choir's hymn still drifted up the passes.

*They sing when they hunt,* she thought. *Like hounds.*

The snow fell thicker now, wrong snow, falling in dead vertical lines like threads off a loom. In the old days — before her days, in the order's last century — they'd have called it *seam-fall*. Snow that came from neither sky nor cloud but from the tear itself, sifting down where the worlds rubbed against each other.

She'd called it nothing. She'd never been taught the word. She'd had to learn it from a book she burned.

*Stop thinking about the book.*

A shelf of rock opened to their left, half-hidden behind an old avalanche tongue, and Marguerite took it without slowing. The waystation chapel was three hours behind them now. The Choir's dead lay scattered among its pews. Her shoulder ached where a zealot's blade had kissed it, and every step sent a thin wire of pain down her sword arm, and none of it mattered, because what mattered was this: she had spoken the oath-word. *Vael.* One syllable, one word, and the mountain had answered her like a dog hearing its master's whistle.

And Elias had seen.

He hadn't said anything yet. That was worse than questions. He walked behind her in the dark with the Canticle's verses folded up inside him like knives in a coat, and he waited.

The pass narrowed into a hollow beneath an overhang — dry, defensible, one approach. A smuggler's camp she'd used a dozen winters. She dropped her pack, scraped together what deadwood the mountain offered, and built a small fire because small fires were invisible and because her hands were shaking and she needed something to do with them besides shake.

Elias sat across the flames. He didn't ask.

She lasted forty minutes.

"You want to know," she said, "so ask."

"I want *you* to know," he said. "I already know the shape of it. I've read the ending." He turned his palms toward the fire. "I don't know the middle. I don't know how a woman ends up carrying a covenant alone."

Marguerite looked at the flame. And because the oath-word was still humming in her blood, and because shame kept is a heavier burden than shame told, she began at the middle.

* * *

Her grandmother's grandmother had been a swordspeaker of the Thornfell Order, back when there were thirty of them and a chapterhouse with warm stones and bells.

That much Marguerite knew from childhood stories, the ones told in kitchens, softened. The true version she learned at fifteen, kneeling on cold flagstone in the under-croft of a house that no longer appeared on any map, with High Sword Cassian's hand heavy on her head and nine elders watching from the dark.

"Say it after me," Cassian had said. "And understand, child, once said, it cannot be unsaid."

She'd been proud. That was the worst part, looking back. She'd stood there at fifteen, orphaned, hungry for belonging, and felt *chosen*.

The words were not the road-oaths every swordspeaker took — *I will not rob the traveler, I will not lie to the mountain* — the little vows that made them welcome everywhere and trusted by everyone. Those were surface oaths. Pretty glass over deep water.

The under-croft oath went deeper.

*I am the seal upon the door. What was joined let no man sunder; what was sundered let no man join; and while I stand between, the winter shall knock and shall not enter.*

Nine elders. One child. She hadn't understood then why it took nine to bind one girl. She understood later, reading the order's hidden ledger by stolen lamplight: because binding was what cowards did instead of keeping.

Here was what the kitchens never told:

A hundred years ago, the covenant between the human realm and the winter-fae had begun to fray — not from malice but from neglect. The fae asked small things. A song sung back. A name remembered. A debt acknowledged. And the order, generation by generation, found the asking inconvenient. Fae took children across the veil sometimes — not eaten, *kept*, raised in the long winter halls, and mothers wept, and mothers wrote letters, and the order grew tired of weeping mothers. So they stopped answering. Then stopped listening. Then, in the great hard winter of the order's ninety-first year, when the fae came to the chapterhouse gates demanding the covenant be honored or formally broken, High Sword Aldous Vey made a decision.

He broke it quietly.

No declaration. No war. Just a ritual in the under-croft, performed at night, that severed the formal bond while leaving the *structure* of the covenant standing — like pulling out a building's spine and propping the walls with poles. The poles were people. Bound keepers, living seals, each one anchored to a fragment of the old bargain so the realms wouldn't simply fly apart at once.

It bought the world time. It bought the order peace. The fae could not reach them to demand redress; the seal held the door shut from the inside.

And then, over the following decades, the bound keepers died. Naturally, mostly. Accidentally, some. Each death weakened a fragment. And the order — this was the part that had made fifteen-year-old Marguerite sick in the under-croft latrine until dawn — the order *did not replace them*. Because replacement required admitting the covenant still existed, required teaching new swordspeakers the deep oaths, required acknowledging that the comfortable lie had a cost.

By the time Marguerite knelt on the flagstones, she was the first keeper bound in sixty years. Bound not to maintain the covenant but to *hold the door shut while it rotted*. Not a bridge-keeper. A plug.

"They didn't bind me to keep the bargain," she said to Elias, and heard her own voice come out flat as slate. "They bound me to keep the *secret*. To be the last knot in a rope everyone else had already cut. And then—" 

The fire popped.

"And then the Hollow Choir happened. You've seen them. Zealots, yes. But they started as ours. Swordspeakers who read the same ledger I did and drew the other conclusion. They decided the covenant wasn't worth saving — that the realms should be sealed *permanently*, rifts closed forever, fae shut out to starve in their winter rather than risk another stolen child, another eaten village. And a sealed realm needs no seal-keeper. Needs no witnesses. So they hunted us. Every keeper. Every elder who objected. Every archivist who copied the Canticle." She fed a stick to the fire. "I was twenty-two. I buried my teacher under a cairn in the Grey Pass and I stopped speaking the oath aloud, because speaking it was how they tracked us. The mountain answers the voice, and voices carry."

"And you ran," Elias said gently. "You became a smuggler."

"I became useful," she corrected. "There's a difference. Smugglers get left alone. Keepers get hunted. A woman moving contraband through Thornfell is a business expense. A woman carrying the last seal of the covenant is an apocalypse waiting for its date." She looked up at him. "You want the middle? That's the middle. A hundred years of cowardice, paid down one betrayal at a time, until the whole bill landed on me. And I *ran from that too*. Twenty years I've known what I am and I've carried crates of brandy past it like it wasn't there."

* * *

Elias was quiet a long moment. The firelight moved on his face, and Marguerite realized with a strange lurch that she was studying his face for judgment and finding none, and that this unsettled her more than judgment would have.

"You said the mountain answers your voice," he said finally. "Tonight it answered *loudly*. Why now?"

Because the rift opened. Because you fell through it holding a scrap of parchment with my name on it. Because tonight is the night in the book I burned.

"The seal is failing," she said instead. "The fragments are nearly all dead. What's left of the covenant isn't holding the door anymore — it's just *me*, standing in the gap, and even a woman can only stand in a gap for so long." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "The long winter isn't coming because the fae are angry. It's coming because the door is rotting open and everything on the other side spills through. The cold. The forgetting. That village—"

"That village," Elias echoed, and his voice cracked slightly. "People smiling with nothing behind their eyes. That's not weather, is it. That's the covenant dying. Memory was part of the bargain — the fae *held* things for us, didn't they? Names. Songs. The stuff villages are made of."

"Yes." Her throat tightened. "We gave them remembrance; they gave us rest. That was one clause of many. When the bargain dies, everything it carried falls out. Including—" She stopped.

"Including your oath-name," Elias said quietly.

She hadn't told him about the morning two weeks ago when she'd woken in a waystation loft and, for four full seconds, not known her own name. Four seconds standing in a stranger's body, in a stranger's life, reaching for *Marguerite* and finding smooth blank stone. She'd told herself it was exhaustion. She'd told herself a lot of things for twenty years.

"How do you know that?" she whispered.

"Vess," he said. "The fae who found us — before you caught up to me, in the white hollow. She sang a fragment. There was a name in it that made my teeth ache. She said—" He frowned, reaching. "*'The seal forgets itself last, and when the seal forgets, the door is done.'*" His eyes lifted to hers across the fire. "Marguerite. How long?"

"Four seconds," she lied. It had been eleven. She'd counted.

He didn't call the lie. He just looked at her with that terrible patience, and the hymn of the Choir seemed very far away and very patient too.

* * *

She slept in snatches and dreamed in the under-croft. Nine shadows, one child, a hand on her head like a lid on a pot. In the dream she always tried to say *no* and always woke with the oath already in her mouth, tasting of iron and candle smoke.

When she woke properly, the fire was low and Elias was awake, feeding it sticks, and something in his posture had changed — the careful neutrality gone, replaced by a kind of settled readiness, like a man who has finished counting his money and knows exactly what he has.

"I've been doing sums," he said, without preamble.

"It's barely dawn."

"I couldn't sleep. Sums help." He poked the fire. "Here's mine. The Canticle says the covenant can be reforged at the heartstone. Reforging requires a keeper — a living seal willing to be bound again, truly bound this time, not as a plug but as a bridge. That's the middle of the prophecy. It doesn't say who pays what. Prophecies never do; they're written by optimists." He looked up. "But here's the sum that matters. Twenty years ago you decided running was survival. Tonight you spoke the oath-word and the mountain answered, and you didn't look afraid afterward. You looked *awake*. First time since I met you."

"I looked terrified."

"You looked both," he agreed. "Same thing, out here." He rose, shouldering his pack. "So here's what I think. You've spent twenty years being ashamed of a choice a fifteen-year-old girl wasn't given. Shame is a fine fuel but it burns dirty — it'll carry you through a blizzard, but it won't carry you through whatever's waiting at that heartstone. So burn something else." He held out his hand to pull her up. "Come finish the job your order started and abandoned. Not for them. For the village that forgot its name. For Vess, singing in the snow. For the four seconds."

*Eleven,* she almost said. *It was eleven seconds.*

Instead she took his hand and stood, and the shame didn't vanish — shame like hers didn't vanish, it calcified, it became bone — but around the bone of it something new set, hard and clean, like ice forming over black water. Not forgiveness. Resolve.

"The heartstone's five days north," she said. "Through roads the Choir doesn't have and can't buy, because the maps died with the order." She pulled her sword loose in its scabbard and rolled her shoulder against the ache. "Five days, and they're singing behind us, and the winter's falling ahead of us, and if we're going to outrun both we leave now and don't stop till dusk."

Elias nodded. Then, at the mouth of the hollow, he paused and looked back down the pass — south, the way they'd come.

"Marguerite," he said. "How long have those lights been following us?"

She followed his gaze. Far below, threaded along the river valley like embers spilled from a brazier, lights were climbing. Dozens. Too steady for torches, too numerous for a hunting party.

Too organized for anything but a procession.

The Choir wasn't chasing them anymore.

The Choir was *leading* them — letting them run, herding them north along the only open road, toward the heartstone, toward whatever Sorrel meant to do when she arrived first. And unless Marguerite missed her guess, the hymn drifting up from the valley had changed key in the night.

It was singing the road-oaths now. *Welcome to the traveler. Truth to the mountain.*

In her order's oldest voices.

"They're using our oaths," she breathed. "They're singing *my* oaths to blind the mountain against us." She drew a slow breath, and the oath-word rose in her throat like a struck bell, ready or not.
