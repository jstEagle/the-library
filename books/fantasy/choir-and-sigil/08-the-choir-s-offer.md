## 8. The Choir's Offer

They took him at dawn, or what passed for dawn under a sky the color of old pewter. Elias never even heard them come.

One moment he was standing watch over Marguerite's sleeping form in the lee of a granite shelf, counting her breaths the way he'd learned to count anything when his mind wouldn't stop — the way he used to count ceiling tiles in the university library, the way he counted the seconds between lightning and thunder. The next, a hand closed over his mouth from behind, smelling of tallow and pine resin, and a voice said, very quietly, "The Underhill woman sleeps. Let's keep it that way, friend."

He fought. He'd like to think he fought well. In truth he got one elbow into someone's ribs and was then folded to the ground by four sets of hands with the practiced, almost gentle efficiency of men who had done this a hundred times and took no pride in it. A hood went over his head. Someone bound his wrists with cord that bit. And Marguerite — Marguerite slept on, or seemed to, and the last thing he heard before the hood muffled the world was a voice saying, "Leave her. The Prelate wants the man alone."

*The Prelate wants the man alone.*

He turned those words over for the whole of the march, which lasted hours, his boots finding switchback stone by feel and faith. They wanted *him*. Not the swordspeaker. Not the oath-bound remnant of a betrayed order. The man who had fallen through a hole in the weather nine days ago, half-frozen, carrying a scrap of parchment with a dead woman's — no, a living woman's — name on it. The Choir had been hunting them both across half of Thornfell, burning waystations, turning villages, putting the fear of the sealed world into every hearth from here to the lowlands. And when they finally got hands on one of them, they left the other sleeping in the snow.

That meant they didn't need Marguerite dead. Not yet. It meant they needed something Elias had.

He catalogued what that could be as he walked. His knowledge of the Canticle, which was considerable now — he'd memorized every verse of the copy he'd found in the chapel at Hollowmere, every line of that terrible book that had described his arrival the way a farmer describes a harvest, date and name and all. His memory of the world on the other side of the rift, which the Choir seemed to consider either valuable or dangerous. Or, simplest and worst of all: the routes. Marguerite's routes. The map of passes and hidden stairs and frozen river fords that lived in her head and, increasingly, in his.

The hood came off in a chapel.

That was the first surprise. He'd expected a fortress, a cave, a camp of black tents — the Choir had been built up in his mind as something out of Marguerite's bitter descriptions, all iron and ash. Instead they'd stopped at a small mountain chapel, whitewashed, humble, its bell tower capped with snow. Inside, the pews had been pushed aside to make room for bedrolls, and the air smelled of woodsmoke and baking bread. A woman was stirring a pot of porridge over the fire. A boy of perhaps ten was mending a boot by the window. Someone had hung their stockings to dry above the hearth, and they steamed gently, ordinary as anything.

The zealots who had carried him here set him in a chair — an actual chair, drawn up before the fire, as though he were an expected guest — and withdrew to the doorway. The porridge woman glanced at him with mild, incurious eyes and went back to stirring.

"You'll want to eat," said a voice behind him. "Interrogations go better on a full stomach. Both directions."

He turned. The woman who had spoken stood by the cold altar, and she was not what he expected either. He had imagined Prelate Sorrel from Marguerite's hatred of her: a gaunt fanatic, a bonfire of a person, eyes full of the world's ending. The woman by the altar was fiftyish, broad-shouldered, with grey shot through brown hair cut short and practical, and the kind of face that made you think of head teachers and harbor masters — someone whose authority came from competence rather than fire. She wore the Choir's grey, but without the hood, and she moved like someone who had spent a lifetime in mountains. Her hands, he noticed, were a swordspeaker's hands: scarred across the knuckles, calloused in the specific pattern of a grip that had held a blade for decades.

She remembered him noticing. He saw her see it.

"Elias Vane," she said. "Of the city of Corvale, in the country that lies on the other side of the sky. Lecturer, I'm told. Cartographer by hobby. Orphan by circumstance." She came and sat across from him, uninvited, and poured two cups of something dark and steaming from a pot by the fire. "I've had nine days to learn about you. The Choir is thorough. It's the one virtue we have left."

"You have my name wrong," Elias said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "It's Vane with an e at the end. Everyone gets it wrong."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile had once lived and been paved over. "Vane with an e. I'll remember." She pushed a cup toward him. "Drink. It's chicory and pine. It's awful. You'll be grateful for it anyway."

He drank. It was awful. He was grateful.

"You're not going to torture me," he said.

"No."

"You're going to be *kind* to me."

"Yes." She said it without flinching, without irony, and somehow that was worse than any threat. "Kindness is not a trick, Mr. Vane. It's only a trick if the kind thing and the true thing point in different directions. In my experience they almost never do. People just find it convenient to believe they do, because it lets them refuse kindness without examining it." She wrapped her hands around her own cup. "You've spent nine days with Marguerite Underhill. She's told you we're monsters. I'd like the chance to tell you what we actually are, and then I'd like to offer you something, and then — this is the important part — I'd like to let you choose. Freely. With all the information. Can you bear that?"

"You could just not have tied me to the chair."

She looked down, and to his astonishment, unbound his wrists herself, her scarred fingers quick and gentle. "There. Now you're a guest who could leave, and hasn't. That's a better position than a prisoner, and a worse one than a friend, and I suspect it's roughly where you already were." She sat back. "Ask me anything. I'll answer truly. You have my oath on it."

"What's your oath worth? You're the one who burned Hollowmere."

"We didn't burn Hollowmere." She said it flatly, without heat. "The waystation chapel there caught fire during a skirmish with the last swordspeaker's partisans — yes, she had partisans, she's never told you that, has she, because it complicates the story of the innocent smuggler — and the fire spread. Two Choir brothers died getting the village records out. I carried a child myself. Ask your questions, Mr. Vane. All of them. I have nowhere to be until you've eaten."

And so he asked. He asked for an hour, maybe two, and she answered, and the terrible thing — the thing he would lie awake turning over for weeks afterward — was that the answers held together. Where Marguerite's account of the Choir had been a wall of black hatred, Sorrel's account of the swordspeakers had edges and shadows and reasons. The order had broken the covenant a century ago, yes — Sorrel didn't deny it, didn't soften it. "They were afraid," she said. "Fear is the most honest material there is. Everything built from it stands, and everything built from it is ugly." The Choir had not begun as zealots; it had begun as survivors, swordspeakers who had watched the broken bargain start to rot the world and drawn the only conclusion that seemed available: seal the wound before it kills the patient.

"The fae are dying," she said. "You've seen the villages. The forgetting. That's not the long winter, Mr. Vane. That's the *cause* of it. The covenant was a wall between two houses, and when the wall came down, both houses started bleeding into each other. Their magic eats our memory. Our weight eats their magic. Every year the bleed gets worse. Every year more villages like the one you walked through — you did walk through it, I know you did, the village that forgot its own name — every year more of them go quiet and smiling and empty." She leaned forward. "We are not trying to punish anyone. We are trying to stop a hemorrhage. And the only way to stop it is to close every rift, permanently, and let each world keep what's left of it."

"And the fae?"

She was quiet for a moment. The porridge woman had gone; the boy had gone; the chapel held only the two of them and the fire.

"The fae," she said, "are already dead. They just haven't finished. Their realm is coming apart — you've seen the sky through the rifts, the way it's the wrong color. Sealing the rifts doesn't kill them faster. It lets them die in their own house instead of bleeding into ours. I have grieved them, Mr. Vane. I have grieved them for forty years. Grief is not a reason to let the infection spread."

"You were a swordspeaker."

It wasn't a question, and she didn't treat it as one. She turned her hands over, palms up, showing him the scars. "Sister Sorrel of the Order of the Bound Blade. Sworn at fifteen. I spoke the oath-words before Marguerite Underhill was born. I held a blade at the last covenant-moot, the night the order broke the bargain, and I was one of only three who argued against it." Her voice didn't waver. "I lost the argument. I watched my brothers and sisters do the thing we had all sworn never to do, because the alternative was watching the world end, or so they believed. And then I watched them spend the next hundred years pretending it had been necessary, and then righteous, and then holy." She closed her hands. "I left the order. I did not leave the problem. The Choir is what I built instead — people who remember, and remember *correctly*, and have the courage to pay the bill."

"What bill?"

"That's the offer," she said. "But first — the information. All of it, as promised."

She told him about the rifts. Not the way Marguerite had — Marguerite spoke of rifts as a smuggler speaks of passes, as doors, as opportunities — but the way a physician speaks of wounds. There were eleven major rifts left in Thornfell. The Choir had sealed seven. The remaining four were held open by something, and that something, Sorrel said, was the heartstone — the old anchor-stone of the covenant, buried deep in the mountain's root, still humming the bargain's last note into the dark.

"As long as the heartstone sings, the rifts can reopen. As long as the rifts can reopen, the bleed continues. We are going to the heartstone to silence it. To close the last four rifts and end the exchange between the worlds forever." She looked at him steadily. "And you, Mr. Vane, are the only person alive on this side of the sky who knows what's on the other side. You are the only witness. The Choir has spent forty years arguing about whether the other realm even *exists*, or whether the rifts open onto nothing, onto the void. You are proof. You are the proof that makes our sacrifice meaningful — or the proof that makes it murder. I need to know which. And I need you to tell me honestly, because you're the only one who can."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved across the bell tower, and the bell gave a single low note, muffled by snow, like a word spoken through a wall.

"And if I tell you it's murder?" Elias asked.

"Then I'll grieve, and do it anyway, and let the history of the Choir judge me. I've made my peace with being judged by people who never had to choose." She set down her cup. "But that's not the offer. Here is the offer."

She rose and went to the altar, and from behind the stone — he watched her do it, watched her lift a slab that must have weighed as much as she did as though it were a hymnal — she brought out a folded thing of grey cloth, and unwrapped it, and held up a shard of glass. No. Not glass. He knew what it was before she said it, the way you know your own name in a dream. It was a piece of rift-edge, a fragment of the boundary between worlds, and it shimmered with that wrong-colored light he had seen on the night he fell through the blizzard, and it hummed, very faintly, against his teeth.

"A controlled rift," Sorrel said. "The last one we will ever open. Small. Stable. It opens onto a hillside above Corvale — I've had our best mapmakers confirm the far side against your descriptions. Spring on the other side of it, Mr. Vane. Actual spring. Green things. A sky the right color." She held it out. "You walk through it tonight, alone, and you go home. You live. You grow old in a world that still remembers its own name. And the price is one thing, and it's a small thing, and it's this: you tell me Marguerite Underhill's route to the heartstone. Not the heartstone itself — we'll find it with or without her. Just her road. The passes she trusts. The ways only she knows. So that when we meet her there, we can do it without a battle, without blood, without adding her name to the bill."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you stay here, as our guest, comfortable and free within the chapel walls, until the long winter closes the passes — and it will, within the month, and after that no one goes anywhere, and the world on both sides of the sky goes on forgetting itself until there's no one left to forget. And she dies in the deep roads, alone, because you weren't there to sing the second voice when she needed it. That's not a threat. That's arithmetic." She set the shard on the table between them, and it lay there glowing like a piece of trapped evening. "Take your time. Eat. Sleep. The rift holds until moonrise."

* * *

He did not take the shard. He also did not sleep.

He sat in the pew where they'd left him — they'd left him, unbound, with a bowl of porridge and a blanket and the shard glowing on the table like a promise — and he turned the offer over and over, the way you turn a stone in your hand looking for the crack. And the horror of it, the thing that made his stomach clench, was not that the offer was cruel. It was that it was *reasonable*. That was Sorrel's weapon, he realized. Not iron, not fire. Reasonableness. She had built her whole terrible church out of it, beam by beam, and every beam bore weight.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd left him with, and he could not make it lie flat: if the Choir was right — if the bleed between worlds was killing both of them, if the villages were the symptom and the heartstone the wound — then sealing the rifts was mercy. And if sealing the rifts was mercy, then the only thing standing between the mercy and its completion was one woman in the deep roads with a sword and an oath she didn't speak aloud, and the only thing standing between that woman and the Choir's mercy was *him*, and the price of standing there was everything — his home, his spring, his green world, his life.

And if the Choir was wrong?

He thought of the village that had forgotten its name. He thought of the way the people there had smiled — not the smile of the contented but the smile of the emptied, the way a house smiles when the family has moved out. And he thought, too, of what Marguerite had said by the fire two nights ago, in the voice she used for the things that cost her most: *They don't want to save the world. They want to be the ones who saved it. There's a difference, and the difference is blood.*

But Sorrel had carried a child out of a fire. Sorrel had unbound his wrists. Sorrel had grieved the fae for forty years and still meant to bury them, and grief like that doesn't come from monsters. It comes from people who have looked at an unbearable thing for a very long time and finally found a way to bear it, and God help you both, because now they're going to make you bear it too.

Moonrise came. The shard still glowed on the table.

And Elias Vane, lecturer, cartographer, orphan, sat in a whitewashed chapel in the mountains of Thornfell and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the choice in front of him was not between betrayal and loyalty. It was between two griefs. One grief had a face — a grey-haired woman with a swordspeaker's hands and forty years of sorrow, offering him spring. The other grief had a face too, and it was asleep somewhere on a granite shelf in the snow, breathing slow, trusting him, and it would never, ever offer him anything at all.

He stood up. The zealot by the door straightened. Somewhere above, the wind found the bell again, and this time the note rang clearer, and under it — so faint he might have imagined it, so faint he chose not to have imagined it — he heard something else, far down in the mountain's root: a second note, answering, like a struck bell heard through a hundred feet of stone.

The heartstone, still singing.

Still waiting.

He looked at Sorrel, who had come to stand in the doorway with the shard in her hands, and whose eyes — he saw it now, finally, the thing Marguerite's hatred had kept him from seeing — whose eyes were not cold at all, but full to the brim, held back by nothing but will.

"You remember everything," he said. "The covenant. The order. What the world was like before."

"Every day of it," Sorrel said. "That's what I am. I'm what's left when everyone else gets to forget."

"Then you know what the covenant cost the first time." He took a breath. "And you know it's not done costing."

Her hands tightened on the shard, and outside, far down the pass, a horn sounded — one long note, then two short, the Choir's own signal, and running feet in the snow, and a voice shouting words that turned the warm chapel cold as a grave:

*"The Underhill woman's at the gate — she's come for him—"*

Sorrel closed her eyes, just for a moment, like a woman hearing a bill come due.

"So am I," said Elias, and moved.
