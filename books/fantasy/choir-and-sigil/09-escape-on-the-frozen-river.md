## 9. Escape on the Frozen River

They had held him for two days in a chapel with no roof, and I spent both of them learning how still a body could be while it screamed.

I crouched on the ridge above Hollowmere's frozen river, snow to my chin in the drifts, and watched the Choir's fires burn orange through the trees below. Six guards on the ice road. Two at the boathouse where they kept him. A sentry on the far bank. Prelate Sorrel herself had come and gone twice, her white cloak moving between the tents like a sliver of moon that had learned to walk.

I told myself I was only scouting. That was the lie I'd carried up from the valley: that I would look, and count, and then make the sensible choice, which was to keep walking north alone toward the heartstone and let Elias Vane become a story someone else finished.

The oath-word sat behind my teeth like a coal.

*Vess had sung it to me three nights ago, half-buried in a fae lament — the true name of my sword, the first syllable of the binding. I hadn't spoken it aloud yet. I wasn't sure what it would do. I wasn't sure what I would do.*

But I knew what Sorrel's people did to men who wouldn't give them a road. I'd seen the waystation. I'd seen the village that forgot its name. And somewhere under all my good sense, in the place where the swordspeakers had once carved their oaths into me before they carved me out of themselves, there was a simple, stupid fact: he had chosen to stay. In the river cave, shaking, feverish, offered a hundred chances to turn back, he had stayed.

You don't leave that on the ice.

I counted again. Six guards, two at the boathouse, one sentry. The river ran black and glassy under its skin of new ice, wide as a road here, frozen hard enough that the Choir used it as one — sled tracks stitched down its length, lanterns hung on poles every fifty yards so their patrols wouldn't wander into the dark water country downstream. That was the flaw in their arrangement. They needed light to feel safe on the ice, and light meant they couldn't see past it.

Beyond the last lantern pole, the river bent south into the Gorge of Whispers, where the current still ran fast beneath the freeze and the ice sang all night — groaning, cracking, shifting plates of it grinding against each other like millstones. No sane person took a sled down the Gorge at night.

Which is why it was going to be me.

* * *

I went down through the trees on the far side, away from the sentry, moving the way you move when the snow is deep and your life depends on not making noise: weight on the edges, slow, patient, hating every second of it. The cold had teeth tonight. Not ordinary cold — this was the long winter's cold, the kind that got inside your thoughts and rearranged them, made you think about lying down, just for a moment, just here in the soft snow. I'd seen what that led to. A shepherd, three valleys back, sitting upright in a drift with frost in his beard and a smile on his face.

I bit the inside of my cheek until I tasted copper and kept moving.

The boathouse leaned over the ice on rotting pilings. Through its gaps I could see lamplight, and a shape sitting on the floor with his back against the wall — Elias, wrists bound in front of him with gray cord that shimmered wrong, choir-wax, the kind that ate at skin. He was talking to one of the guards. Actually *talking*, low and even, and I felt something twist in my chest that I refused to name, because even trussed up in a freezing shed waiting for whatever came next, the man was negotiating.

Then the guard laughed and walked away, and Elias's shoulders dropped, and I saw how young his face looked when he thought no one was watching. How tired.

Right, I thought. Fine. This is happening.

I waited for the patrol to pass. Counted their lantern swinging away along the ice road. Then I crossed open ice to the pilings, flat on my belly like a smuggler, because that was the one thing I had never stopped being, and slipped under the boathouse into the smell of old fish and tar.

There was a gap in the floorboards above the boat cradle. I put my mouth to it.

"Elias."

He jerked so hard he cracked his head on the wall. Then, in a whisper pitched to carry exactly as far as me and not one inch farther: "Marguerite? Or is this a test? Because Sorrel said—"

"It's me. Shut up and listen." I drew my knife and started working at the boards. "Patrol comes back around in four minutes. There's a hole in the ice thirty feet off your left shoulder, where the current runs shallow under the crust. You're going to go through the floor, drop onto the ice, roll left, and be quiet about it."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the plan we have. Do you have a better one?"

A pause. "No. But I want it noted that I said it was terrible."

"Noted." The board came free. I reached up through the gap and cut the choir-wax cord off his wrists, and heard him hiss — the stuff had burned grooves into him, raw and weeping. "Can you run?"

"I can do an enthusiastic shuffle."

"Good enough."

The patrol lantern swung back into view between the trees. I pulled my arm down, pressed myself against the piling, and listened to boots scrape overhead. Four minutes. I gave them six anyway, because the ice was going to be loud enough soon, and then I said, "Now," and pushed the second board loose, and Elias came down through the floor in a graceless tangle of limbs and landed half on me, and we lay together in the dark under the boathouse breathing each other's air like a pair of drowned things given one more minute.

"You came," he whispered.

"Don't make it strange."

"I'm just observing the data."

"*Move.*"

* * *

We ran.

Not far — forty yards across the open ice, low and fast, toward the bend and the dark beyond the last lantern. Behind us the boathouse guard chose that exact moment to look outside, and a shout went up, thin and furious, and lantern-light bloomed all along the riverbank like the mountain opening a red eye.

The ice held us. The first hundred yards it held us, and then we passed the last lantern pole and entered the Gorge of Whispers, and the ice began to talk.

It talked in groans first — long, low, tectonic complaints rolling up through our boot-soles. Then cracks, sharp as whips, running ahead of us in jagged silver lines that glowed faintly blue in the dark, spiderwebbing out from the banks where the current gnawed. Elias stumbled; I hauled him up by the collar without breaking stride.

"The ice is singing," he gasped. "Why is the ice singing?"

"Because the river never froze all the way through. It never does. Keep running."

Behind us: horns. The Choir didn't bother with stealth now. Sled runners shrieked onto the ice road, dogs baying, torchlight strobing off the gorge walls. A crossbow bolt skipped past us and shattered against the ice ten feet ahead, and I heard Elias swear with real feeling.

"They'll catch us on the flats!" he shouted.

"No, they won't." I pointed ahead, where the gorge narrowed and the river dropped into a series of low rapids frozen mid-curl, a staircase of froth turned to stone. "There. The shelf ice. It looks solid but it's layered — hollow underneath, like a honeycomb. Their sleds can't follow. We can, if we're light and lucky."

"And if we're not?"

"Then we find out what the river's been saying all night."

We came off the main ice and onto the shelf, and it flexed under us — a horrible elastic give, like walking on the hide of something enormous and sleeping. Cracks radiated from each footfall. Behind us the lead sled hit the edge of the shelf at speed and punched straight through into the hollow layer with a sound like a cathedral falling down, and the screaming of dogs and men was very brief and then very wet, and after that the other sleds stopped coming.

That should have been the end of it. But the Choir had archers on the banks, and arrows came hissing down through the dark, and one of them opened a line of fire across my shoulder, and then the shelf ice — already abused, already singing — decided it had been asked to hold enough.

It broke like a mirror struck by a fist.

I don't remember falling. I remember the cold — the true cold, the under-ice cold, the cold that isn't weather but opinion — closing around me like a hand, and the roar of the current, and Elias's hand locking around my wrist as the river tried to take me under the shelf. His grip was nothing. He was half-starved and frostbitten and lighter than I was. The water should have peeled him off me like paper.

He didn't let go.

I found a rock with my free hand — a boulder shoulder-up through the broken ice — and we hung there in the black flood with the whole gorge trying to swallow us, and I looked up at this man I'd known for eleven days, this stranger who fell out of the sky with my name in his pocket, and I thought: *he is going to die holding onto me, and it will have meant nothing, and I will have killed him.*

"Let go!" I screamed.

"No!"

"You can't hold me!"

"Yes, I can!" His face was white as the ice, blood freezing on his lip where he'd hit the rock, and he was grinning, actually *grinning*, wild and terrified and certain. "Marguerite Underhill — I read a book! A hundred years ago somebody wrote my name and yours in the same sentence, and I have been falling since the moment I stepped through that rift, and I am *done* letting go of things! You want to drown? Drown *with me* or climb!"

And that — that absurd, bookish, impossible refusal — did something to me that no oath ever had.

I climbed.

Hand over hand, boots finding purchase in the frozen spray, hauling us both up the boulder while the river tore at us and the arrows kept falling, and when we crested onto the far bank and collapsed in the snow, chest heaving, steaming like livestock, the shouting behind us was fading — the Choir regrouping, licking wounds, losing us in the dark and the noise of the water. I rolled onto my back. Stars. Actual stars, torn clean by wind, wheeling over the gorge.

Elias flopped beside me. For a long moment neither of us spoke.

"You know," he managed finally, "in case it matters — I choose you. Formally. On the record." He raised one shaking, ruined hand as if taking an oath. "Whatever Sorrel offered, whatever the book says I'm supposed to do — I pick the side with you on it. Even if the side loses. Especially if the side loses."

I stared at the stars and felt something crack in my chest that had nothing to do with the cold.

"Don't say things like that," I said quietly. "People who say things like that end up being reasons."

"Good," he said. "Be my reason."

* * *

We didn't stop until dawn, and when we finally did — holed up in a smuggler's cache I'd stocked years ago, a stone hollow behind a waterfall frozen into a blue curtain — I looked back the way we'd come, and my stomach turned over.

The winter had moved.

Not snowfall. Not weather. *Movement.* The treeline behind us, the valley we'd fled through — it was buried. White to the ridgetops, smooth and total, as though a great pale tide had come up the gorge in the night and swallowed everything we'd passed: the boathouse, the Choir's camp, the flats where the sled had gone through, all of it gone under a snowpack so deep and seamless it looked poured rather than fallen. And it was still advancing. Slowly. Inexorably. Like a flood that had all the time in the world.

Downriver, where Hollowmere should have been visible as smoke and rooftops, there was nothing. Just white.

"The villages," Elias said hoarsely. He'd seen it too. He was standing at the mouth of the cache, and his breath came out in ragged flags. "The people. Marguerite — the people were *behind* us."

"I know."

"We have to outrun it."

"No." I turned back to the fire, to my sword across my knees, to the oath-word burning behind my teeth where it had been since the river. "We can't outrun a season. We go *through* it. To the heartstone. Before this—" I nodded at the wall of white pressing down the valley, silent and total, patient as a debt. "Before that finishes arriving everywhere."

Elias came and sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and for a while we just watched the winter eat the world behind us.

"Marguerite," he said at last. "When we get there. Whatever the covenant asks—"

"Don't." My voice came out harder than I intended. "Don't make promises about prices you haven't seen."

"I'm not promising to pay them," he said. "I'm promising I'll be standing next to you when they name them."

Outside, the frozen waterfall ticked and groaned, settling deeper into itself. And far upstream, faint but unmistakable, riding under the wind like a note held too long — singing. Fae voices, many of them, mourning something that hadn't died yet.

Vess had told me once that the mourners sang loudest when death was closest.

I wrapped my hands around my sword hilt, and for the first time in seven years, I answered the song with the first word of my oath, spoken aloud, barely a breath:

"*Ash*."

The mountain, very softly, sang back.
