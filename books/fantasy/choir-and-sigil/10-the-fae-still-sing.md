## 10. The Fae Still Sing

**Marguerite**

The singing started at dusk on the fourth day past the frozen river.

Marguerite heard it before she understood it — a thread of sound woven through the wind so finely that she almost took it for wind itself. She stopped on the switchback with her hand raised and Elias walked into her pack before catching himself.

"What?" he whispered.

"Listen."

He listened. She watched his face do the thing it did when the world broke another of its rules: the small tightening around the eyes, the breath held like a man waiting for a floor to hold. "That's — voices. That's people."

"It isn't people." She said it flatly, because saying it any other way would have meant admitting what it was, and she had spent eleven years refusing to say that word aloud. *Fae.* The word sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed whole and learned to live around. "It's old, though. Older than people, maybe."

The song came again, and this time there was no mistaking it. Not one voice but many, layered and interlocking, rising out of the high col ahead of them where the pines gave up and only black stone stood against the sky. The melody had no beginning she could find; it simply was, the way a mountain is, and it moved in patterns too large to follow. Fragments of it snagged in her ear like burrs — a phrase of seven notes, repeated; then silence; then something lower, slower, like a name being said over and over by someone who has forgotten why it matters.

Elias had gone pale. "I know it," he said. "Marguerite, I *know* it. It's from the Canticle — the lament section, the part Sorrel's people never sing. I memorized it in the chapel and I couldn't make sense of it, it wasn't written to be read, it was written to be—"

"Sung," Marguerite said.

Her sword had begun, very faintly, to hum.

She felt it through the scabbard first, a vibration against her hip like a struck tuning fork pressed to bone, and when she drew three inches of blade into the blue evening light the steel itself was singing sympathy — harmonizing, a half-step behind the distant voices, like a child trying to rejoin a song it once knew perfectly.

She slammed it home again. Her hands were not steady.

"That's not possible," she said.

"You keep saying that," Elias said quietly. "And the mountain keeps disagreeing."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted, worse, to turn around and walk back down into the low passes where the world obeyed the rules she'd built her exile on: cold steel, warm bread, roads that went where they went and meant nothing by it. Eleven years she had kept this sword oiled and silent and never once let herself wonder why an order full of smiths had given their last oath-bound daughter a blade that weighed more than iron should, that drank candlelight instead of reflecting it, that no whetstone in three kingdoms could put an edge on because it apparently had never lost one.

Eleven years of not asking questions was a kind of house. She had furnished it carefully.

The singing rose, and the stone under her boots thrummed, and the house fell down.

"We go toward it," she heard herself say.

Elias looked at her. "Are you sure?"

"No," said Marguerite Underhill, and started climbing.

* * *

**Elias**

They found the singers in a bowl of stone beneath a glacier's broken snout, and Elias Vane — who had crossed between worlds four days ago and had not yet finished screaming about it internally — discovered that he still had room left inside himself to be undone completely.

There were perhaps thirty of them. He could not have said at first glance whether they were beautiful or terrible, and he suspected the distinction didn't exist where they came from. They were tall the way winter is tall — long lines, pale surfaces, edges that caught the dying light and gave nothing back. Their skin held colors like ice holds sediment: threads of blue and green and one grey like old pewter. They wore no clothes he could name and needed none; frost grew off their shoulders in delicate fern-patterns, and where they stood the snow lay undisturbed, as if they touched the world only when they chose to.

They were kneeling. All of them, in a ring around a flat slab of stone furred with ancient lichen, and they were singing down at it the way you sing at a grave.

"They're mourning," Elias breathed.

"Don't move," Marguerite said. Her voice had gone strange — stripped down to wire and load-bearing structure. She had her hood back and her sword half-drawn and she was staring at the fae with an expression he had never seen on her: not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone finding a face from a dream standing in a crowd.

One of them turned its head.

The motion was wrong — smooth in a way nothing alive moves, the head rotating without the shoulders following, like a weathervane finding the wind. Its eyes were the color of deep ice over deep water, and they fixed on Elias first, and he felt the look pass through him like cold through a coat, cataloguing him, dismissing him. A stranger. An accident. Then the gaze slid to Marguerite and stopped.

The singing faltered.

Thirty voices stumbled at once, the way a congregation stumbles when the doors bang open at the funeral, and every pale face in that ring turned toward the woman standing at the rim of the bowl with a drawn sword and snow in her dark hair.

Then they began, all together, to weep.

Elias had never heard anything weep like that. There were no tears; there was only the song collapsing inward, the great interlocking melody folding down into a single keening note that went through the stone and through his teeth and settled behind his sternum like a swallowed coal. Snow shivered off the boulders. Marguerite swayed.

"Put it away," said a voice, and the voice came from everywhere and nowhere and finally from directly in front of them, though Elias had not seen the speaker cross the distance.

It stood closer than anything had a right to stand without being noticed — taller than him by half a head, its surface threaded with that pewter grey, its eyes holding a grief so old it had polished itself smooth, like a stair worn hollow by centuries of feet.

"The sword," the fae said. Its voice was the voice of thaw — drip and crack and the groan of a lake remembering it is water. "Sheathe it. You are hurting them."

"Hurting—" Marguerite's knuckles were white on the grip. "Who are you? What is this place? What do you want with us?"

"I am called Vess." The fae inclined its head with a courtesy so precise it felt ceremonial. "And this place is a wound. As for what I want—" those glacial eyes moved to the sword, to the humming steel, and something in Vess's composure cracked, just once, the way river ice cracks: suddenly, deeply, with a sound like a shot. "I want you to understand what you carry. Because I knew that blade when it was being forged, little keeper, and the hands that made it are ash now, and every time you draw it unsheathed and unbound, it sings a question to my people that we have spent one hundred years unable to answer."

The word landed like a dropped stone. *Keeper.*

"Sheathe it," Vess said again, gentler. "Please. We have grieved long enough to recognize the shape of another grief, and yours has been walking around armed for eleven years, and it is time somebody told you why."

Marguerite looked at Elias. He realized she was asking permission — she, who navigated blizzards and ambushes and betrayal by pure unbending will, was looking at him across three feet of frozen air as if he might have some say in what happened next.

He nodded. Once.

She drove the sword home into its sheath, and the moment the steel went dark the fae ring exhaled — a sound like wind leaving a forest — and the keening note resolved, at last, into something that was not quite peace but was at least quiet.

Vess gestured toward the lichen-covered slab. "Sit, both of you. Winter is coming faster than your legs can carry you from it, and the story is long. But I will tell you the true version, which no book holds."

* * *

**Marguerite**

They sat on the slab, which was warm — warm, here above the tree line in a killing frost, warm like a living thing — and Vess crouched before them with the patience of something that measured time in glaciers.

"A covenant is not a treaty," the fae said. "Your scholars think in treaties. Lines on vellum. Treaties can be broken and survived. A covenant is a *joint*. Two realms, bound at one point, moving together — and when the joint is torn out, neither limb works rightly again." It looked at Marguerite, and she fought the urge to look away. "A hundred years ago, your order of swordspeakers came to Thornfell heartstone to renew the joint, as every generation of them had done since the first winter. And they found the price had risen. The covenant asked more of them than their grandmothers had paid. And they were afraid."

"We don't—" Marguerite started.

"*They* were afraid," Vess agreed smoothly. "Fourteen swordspeakers stood in this basin. Thirteen voted to seal the heartstone from the outside and abandon the renewal — to lock the door rather than pay the toll. And one refused."

The wind moved through the bowl. The mourners had resumed their song, soft now, a background like breathing.

"One refused," Vess said, "and was bound anyway — bound as the seal itself, the living knot that would hold the torn joint closed until someone came to reforge it properly. Bound with a blade made for the purpose, quenched in the waters of the heartstone, carrying the covenant's own signature in its steel. And then the thirteen fled south, scattered their order, burned their records, and let the world forget — because a seal works best when it does not know it is a seal. A seal that knows will strain against its binding. So they left her ignorant. Left her to be born, and grow, and take up the blade, and wander the mountains for years without ever knowing why the steel hummed when the wind sang."

The cold in Marguerite's stomach had nothing to do with altitude.

"My mother carried this sword," she said. Her voice sounded very far away. "Her mother before her. Nobody ever said—"

"They couldn't. The binding eats knowledge of itself, radiating outward. It ate the order's records first, then the villages' memories, and now—" Vess spread one long grey-threaded hand toward the north, where the horizon had gone the white of scar tissue. "Now it eats everything. Your Hollow Choir thinks sealing the rifts forever will save the human realm. But the sealing is already happening, uncontrolled, from the inside. Every village that forgets its name, every child born not knowing songs their mothers sang — that is the seal doing its work badly. Another generation and there will be no minds left rich enough to miss what was taken. Your zealots will get their sealed world, little keeper. It will simply be empty, and smiling, and hollow as a bell with no tongue."

Elias made a small sound. Marguerite glanced at him; his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic he hates. "The Canticle," he said. "The lament section. It's not prophecy, is it. It's a record. Someone wrote down what the thirteenth— the one who refused—"

"The one who refused wrote nothing," Vess said. "*We* remember. Memory is our country's whole territory, and so we are the ones who bleed." The fae's composure slipped fully then, and Marguerite saw underneath it, and what was underneath was so tired and so old and so sad that her throat closed. "Do you know what it is, to be a creature made entirely of memory, watching memory die? We sing the covenant's fragments because fragments are all that is left unbroken. We kneel at this stone because this stone remembers being a joint between worlds, and joints are holy things, and ours was torn out and the wound blamed on *us* — on the winter-fae, monsters of the high passes, stealers of children. Lies your order planted to bury its own cowardice."

Marguerite's hand had found her sword hilt without her deciding anything. Not drawing it. Just holding it, the way you hold the hand of someone at a deathbed.

"Why tell me this?" she said. "Why now? If the seal doesn't work best ignorant — if ignorance is what's killing everything — then what am I supposed to *do*, Vess? Walk up to the heartstone and apologize?"

"You are supposed to reforge the covenant," Vess said simply. "As it was always going to come to. The blade will open the binding. The stone will take the oath. The realms will knit." It paused, and in the pause Marguerite felt the whole bowl of stone lean in — thirty mourners going silent at once, the glacier holding its breath. "But hear me, and hear me clearly, because I will not soften it twice. A covenant reforged demands a keeper. Living. Bound. The old price, paid new. Whoever stands at the heartstone and speaks the binding gives themselves to the joint — their years, their strength, and their memory. All of it. Everything they were, poured into the stone, so the stone can hold what minds alone cannot."

The words went into Marguerite slowly, like cold into a boot seam.

"All of it," she repeated.

"Every face. Every road. Every name." Vess's eyes were pitiless with compassion. "You would wake at the heartstone knowing how to speak and not why you came. Knowing your own name and not whose voice taught it to you. The covenant keeps the *keeper* alive — that much mercy survives even a broken age. But the self you are tonight, the woman who climbed these passes and saved this man from the snow and learned to trust again against every instinct she owns — that woman goes into the stone, and does not come out."

Silence. Even the mourners' song had stopped.

Beside her, Elias had gone absolutely still, and when she turned her head she found him already looking at her — and the look on his face was so raw, so nakedly horrified, that she understood before he spoke that he had done the same arithmetic she had, and reached the same sum:

Everything she was. Everything they had only just, in the frozen dark, begun to become.

"There has to be another way," Elias said hoarsely.

"There is always another way," Vess said, rising, frost ferns blooming and dying along its shoulders. "It is simply worse. Come — rest tonight within our ring, if you dare, and tomorrow I will show you the last road to the heartstone. And along the way, keeper, I will show you what your thirteen sisters became." The fae's gaze drifted south, toward the lowlands, and its voice dropped to something almost like pity. "Because one of them leads the men hunting you. And Prelate Sorrel remembers *everything* — and it has driven her mad."

Far below, at the edge of hearing, a hymn was rising through the passes.

It was getting louder.
