## 11. Half of a Bargain

The fae did not sleep, and so neither did the fire. It burned without wood, fed on something Vess called *courtesy*, and threw light that moved like slow water across the walls of the hollow where we'd taken shelter — a cavern beneath a shelf of granite, its ceiling hung with frost that caught the flame and scattered it into constellations. Somewhere beyond the entrance, the mourners were still singing. They had not stopped since dusk. I was beginning to understand they never would.

Vess sat across the fire from me with her knees drawn up and her chin resting on them, and in this light she looked less like the creature who had knelt over Marguerite's sword an hour ago as if it were a coffin. She looked almost young. Almost human. Her skin had the blue-white of candle-flame and her eyes were the grey of ice under strain, but her hands — her hands were folded neatly in her lap like a woman waiting for a train.

Marguerite was asleep. Or pretending to be. She lay with her back to us, her bedroll pulled up to her shoulder, her sword within reach of her hand even now. Even asleep — especially asleep — she kept one fist closed around the scabbard like a child holding a rope over deep water.

"She will want to hear this," Vess said quietly. "But she is not ready to hear it. So it will be you."

"Me?"

"You are the one who reads." Vess nodded toward my coat, where the Canticle's pages rode against my chest. "You are the one who keeps words. That is why I will tell you first. Words survive better in a keeper than in a sleeper."

I should have woken Marguerite then. I've thought about that moment more times than I can count — the small, ordinary cowardice of wanting ten more minutes before the world changed again. I told myself it was mercy. It was not. It was curiosity, plain and ugly, and it has a price I'm still paying.

"Tell me," I said.

* * *

Vess unfolded from her seated position the way smoke unfolds, and came to sit beside me, close enough that the cold radiating off her skin made the fire lean away.

"A covenant is not a treaty," she said. "Understand that first. Your people make treaties — pieces of vellum, signatures, clauses that can be found and argued and broken by lawyers. A covenant is not made of paper. A covenant is made of *flesh*."

"Flesh," I repeated.

"The old bargain between the human realm and ours was written into two living things. One was a voice — a fae who would carry the song of the covenant, teach it to each generation, keep the words alive in memory when memory failed. That was me. I was young when the covenant was whole. I sang it every solstice at the heartstone. You cannot imagine what that was like, Elias Vane. To hold an entire peace inside your throat. Every note a border. Every verse a promise kept."

Her voice had gone somewhere else while she spoke — further away and older, the way a room gets when someone describes it after it's burned down.

"And the other half?" I asked, though some part of me already knew, the way you know the last line of a song from its first.

"The other half was the seal." Vess looked past me, at the sleeping shape by the fire. "A voice can sing a promise. But a promise must also be *kept somewhere*. Held. The old makers decided that no vault, no stone, no book could be trusted — books burn, stones crack, vaults are opened by thieves. So they wrote the covenant's binding terms into a living human being. A swordspeaker, forged and oath-bound not merely to guard the covenant but to *be* its lock. Her body the hasp. Her memory the keyhole. Her oath-word the tumblers."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the mourners' song rose and fell like breathing.

"They made a person into a contract," I said slowly.

"They made a person into a *doorframe*," Vess said. "The door itself was the heartstone. But a door is nothing without the frame that holds it true. When your order broke the covenant a century ago—"

"They didn't break it," I said. "That's what Marguerite said. She said they betrayed it, but the betrayal was—"

"Listen to me carefully." For the first time, something moved behind Vess's calm — a crack of grief through ice. "Your swordspeakers did not simply walk away from the bargain, the way men walk away from bad debts. They could not. The covenant could not be broken from outside; it was too well made. So they did something worse. They went to the seal herself — the woman who was the living lock — and they unmade her binding. They cut the oath out of her. Do you understand? They performed a rite upon their own sister, their own weapon, their own wall, and they *hollowed* her. They took the covenant's terms out of her flesh and buried them in the mountain, and they sealed her memories of the working so thoroughly that she believed she was merely the last survivor of a disgraced order. A smuggler. A ghost. A woman running from shame that was never hers."

The firelight bent. I realized my hands were shaking and made them stop.

"But the covenant was still in her," I said. "Even buried. Even hidden."

"It was *sleeping* in her," Vess said. "In her blood, in her bones, in the shape of her oath. And here is the thing your kind does not understand about such workings, Elias Vane: they do not die. They wait. A covenant made of flesh waits in the flesh until the hour of its need. And the Canticle—" she nodded again at my chest, and I felt the pages there like a coal, "—the Canticle is not a prophecy in your sense of the word. It is not prediction. It is *memory ahead of its time*. The makers knew the covenant might be broken. They knew the long winter might come. And so they wrote down the hour of its mending, and the name of the road-keeper who would carry the seal back to the stone."

"The Underhill woman," I whispered.

"The Underhill woman," Vess agreed. "Not a woman who happened to survive. Not a woman who stumbled into prophecy. A woman *made for it* — made a century before her birth, when the makers wrote her name into the ash and set her walking toward this night the way you set a clock. She was never running from her past, Elias. She was walking toward it. Every road she ever took, every pass she learned, every year she survived alone in these mountains — all of it was the covenant keeping itself intact inside her, preparing its own delivery."

I thought about the parchment in my pocket. My name on one side, hers on the other, folded together like hands.

"She doesn't know," I said.

"No."

"Why not? Why hide it from her? If she's the—the seal, if everything depends on her—"

"Because a lock that knows it is a lock can be picked," Vess said simply. "Because a woman who knows she was made to be sacrificed will spend her whole life running from the altar. Because the makers were wise and cruel in equal measure, and they understood that the sealing must be *chosen freely or it will not hold*. So they let her live blind. They let her believe she was free. And now the hour has come, and the choice stands before her, and she must make it without knowing she was carved for it — or knowing, and choosing anyway. That is the only version that binds."

"What choice?" My voice came out wrong. "What does the seal have to do? What happens at the heartstone?"

Vess looked at me for a long moment. The fire leaned further from her cold.

"That is not mine to tell," she said. "And it is not yours to hear yet. Ask me instead the question you are actually carrying."

I didn't want to ask it. I asked it anyway.

"If she's the seal. If she was made for this, forged for it, written into a book a hundred years ago — then what am I? Was I written too? Am I just... another clause in the contract? Another part that was manufactured?"

For the first time since I'd fallen through the rift, the fae smiled — and it was a sad smile, an old smile, the smile of someone watching a mortal ask exactly the question mortals always ask.

"You," she said, "are the only unplanned thing in this story. The Canticle names you because the makers saw you coming the way water finds a crack. But you were not made. You fell. You chose. Every kindness you have done since the snow took you, you chose. In a story written a century ago, Elias Vane, you are the one word that nobody wrote." She rose, unfolding upward, and her shadow climbed the frost-hung walls like a second, taller Vess. "That is worth more than you know. Written things can be erased. Chosen things—"

She stopped. Her head turned toward the bedroll with the sudden, terrible stillness of a deer.

* * *

Marguerite was sitting up.

I don't know how long she'd been awake. Long enough. Her face in the firelight was very pale and very still, the way a lake goes still before it freezes, and her eyes were fixed on nothing — on some middle distance where a century-old sentence was still landing.

"How much," she said. Her voice was flat and careful, each word laid down like a stone on a grave. "How much did I hear."

Vess said nothing. Which was answer enough.

"The Underhill woman," Marguerite said. Still that terrible calm. "Made for it. Carved for it. Set walking toward it like a *clock*." She laughed once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it at all. "I thought I was hiding from the order that abandoned me. All those years, every pass, every frozen night, every mile I put between me and Thornfell — I thought I was running *away*." Her hand found the scabbard. Her knuckles whitened around it. "I was being *delivered*."

"Marguerite." I started to stand.

"Don't." The word cracked her calm right down the middle, and through the crack I saw what was underneath, and it was worse than anger. It was grief. It was the grief of a woman discovering that the one thing she'd believed about herself — *they took my life from me, it wasn't fair, but at least it was mine* — was a lie stitched a century before she was born. "Don't you dare comfort me. Don't either of you dare. I earned every mile of those roads. I learned them with frostbite and hunger and three winters alone. Nobody *made* me—"

"Nobody made you learn them," Vess said gently. "The covenant showed you the doors. You walked through."

"The covenant *used* me."

"Yes," Vess said. "As it used me. As it used the makers themselves, who gave their lives to write it. A covenant of flesh spends everyone who touches it. That is what makes it a covenant and not a lie." She paused. "And that is why the choice must be yours. Freely given. The makers could compel your feet, Underhill. They could not compel your *yes*."

"My yes to what?" Marguerite was on her feet now, and the sword came with her, though I don't think she knew she'd drawn it — the blade catching firelight, her stance perfect and terrible. "What happens at the heartstone, Vess? What's the price? Say it. Say it aloud, because I have spent my whole life as other people's secret and I will not walk one more mile as one."

Vess looked at her for a long moment. Then at me. Something passed across her face that I would later recognize as pity, and I understood, in that moment, why she hadn't wanted Marguerite to wake.

"Not yet," Vess said. "The stone will tell you at the stone. That much, even the makers could not force — the price must be heard from the thing that asks it."

Marguerite's arm dropped. The sword point sank until it rested against the stone floor, and the sound it made — a thin, bright ringing, like a struck bell far away — echoed up through the cavern and out into the dark, where the mourners' song caught it and carried it, weaving the note into their lament as if it had always belonged there.

She stood there breathing. I watched the fury drain out of her posture and leave something else behind, something I recognized because I'd seen it in my own mirror the day I found the Canticle: the vertigo of a person discovering the ground was never ground.

"I need air," she said.

She sheathed the sword, took her cloak, and walked out into the snow and the singing, and neither Vess nor I moved to follow her, because some doors a person has to walk through alone.

* * *

We sat in silence for a while. The fire burned its courtesy-light. Vess refolded herself by the flames and stared into them as if they were a page.

"You knew she'd hear it eventually," I said finally.

"I hoped she wouldn't. Hoped is not the word. Fae do not hope; we *foresee*, which is crueler." Vess's fingers traced a pattern in the frost beside her, absent, automatic — and I realized with a small cold shock that the pattern was notation. Musical notation. She was transcribing the mourners' song from memory, or from hearing, the way I'd once transcribed inventory. A voice keeping words. "But the Canticle gives us four days to reach the heartstone, and she cannot walk there believing she is only a survivor. A survivor can turn aside. A seal..." She shook her head. "A seal does not get to turn aside. Not and take both worlds with her."

"There has to be another way."

"There is always another way," Vess said. "It is just never a way that saves what you wanted saved." She looked up at me, and her grey eyes caught the firelight and held it, unmoving. "Ask me your real question, Elias Vane. The one you swallowed."

I swallowed it a long moment. Then: "What does the seal cost? You know. You've known since you saw her sword."

"Yes."

"Tell me. If she won't hear it from you, let me carry it. Let me be the one who knows and walks beside her anyway. Someone in this story should know the price and stay."

Vess studied me for a long time. Outside, the singing swelled and thinned, swelled and thinned, tide against a shore made of ice.

"When the covenant is reforged," she said at last, very quietly, "the seal must hold it. Not guard it. *Hold* it — from inside. The terms must live in her again, fully, the way they lived before the order hollowed her out. And a covenant of that size, Elias — two realms, one door, an entire winter bound — a covenant that size leaves no room in the vessel for anything that is not the covenant itself." Her voice didn't waver. That was the worst part. It was gentle and level and absolutely certain. "She will keep the worlds. Everything else that makes her Marguerite — her roads, her grudges, her years alone, her—" a pause, delicate as frost forming, "—her recent attachments — will go into the stone. The seal remembers the bargain. Nothing else."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, the frost on the ceiling shed a single crystal that fell like slow snow and melted on my sleeve.

"She loses everything," I said.

"She gains everything else," Vess said. "Every dawn in both realms. Every child never stolen. Every village that remembers its own name. That is the trade the makers designed. It seemed merciful, a century ago, to whoever pays it is not asked to watch what they lose."

I sat with that. I sat with the image of her — this woman who had dragged me out of a blizzard while hating herself for it, who spoke oath-words like weapons, who had confessed her shame to me by firelight and let me see, for one unguarded hour, the person underneath the swordspeaker — and I tried to fit that person into a lock, emptied and shining and remembering nothing but her function.

"She'll say yes," I said. My voice sounded far away. "That's the worst part, isn't it? She'll say yes. Because that's who she is. She'll decide the worlds are worth more than she is, and she'll say yes, and she'll thank us for letting her."

"Yes," Vess said. "That is exactly who she is. The makers built true."

I looked at the cave mouth, where the dark was full of snow and song, and somewhere out there was a woman staring at the same sky I'd fallen through, learning that she'd been a doorframe all her life.

"Then she shouldn't have to do it alone," I said.

Vess went very still.

"Careful, Elias Vane."

"I mean it. Whatever the ritual is, whatever the circle needs — she holds the covenant, fine. But she shouldn't hold it *alone*. There has to be room in the working for—"

"There is not," Vess said, sharper than she'd spoken all night. And then, softer, with something that in a human face I'd have called fear: "And even if there were — do not go looking for a place to stand beside her in that circle until you understand what standing there costs. The Canticle named you for the falling and the carrying. It did not name you for the stone. Be grateful. Gratitude is the only sane response to being left out of a sacrifice."

From outside, faint through the wind, came the sound of footsteps crunching back toward the cave mouth. Steady. Deliberate. The walk of a woman who had gone out shattered and was returning assembled — differently assembled, badly, with visible seams, but assembled nonetheless.

"She's coming back," I said.

"Yes." Vess rose, and the fire dimmed in courtesy. "And you will not speak of this conversation. Neither of you will speak of it. Let her walk to the stone carrying hope and dread in equal weights. The truth will meet her there soon enough. It always does."

Marguerite filled the cave mouth, snow in her hair, her face raw with cold and something older than cold. She looked at Vess. She looked at me. And I watched her decide — visibly, deliberately, in real time — not to ask what else we'd said after she left.

"We leave at first light," she said. "Four days to the heartstone. The Choir will travel faster, so we won't take the roads." She pulled her bedroll flat and lay down with her back to us, one fist closing around the scabbard, the same as before — except that nothing was the same, and we all three knew it now, the way you know a load-bearing wall has cracked. "Get some sleep, Vane. You look like a man memorizing his own eulogy."

She was asleep within minutes. Real sleep, or the exhaustion that imitates it.

I lay awake beside the dying fire, her sword within her reach and the Canticle against my chest, and I thought about locks and vessels and the one unwritten word in a century-old book. Four days to the heartstone. Four days to a price she would pay smiling.

Unless, I thought, into the dark, unless someone found a way to stand beside her.

Out in the night, the mourners changed verses — and for the first time, clear and cold through the storm, I heard them sing a name.

Not hers.

Mine.
