## 12. I Am Not a Door

Marguerite walked away from the fire at a pace that was almost running and told herself it was a patrol.

That was the trick of the deep roads. You could call anything a patrol if you were moving with purpose. Checking the backtrail. Testing the snowpack. Listening for the Choir. She had spent eleven years turning flight into craft, cowardice into competence, and she was very good at it.

Behind her, the fire cracked. Elias did not follow. That was worse. If he'd followed she could have shouted at him, and shouting was easy, and easy things kept you upright.

She climbed the switchback above the waystation hollow until the wind found her and peeled the anger off in layers. Beneath it, as always, was the other thing. The thing Vess had said with those black eyes full of pity.

*You were not born to the seal, swordspeaker. You were made for it.*

*Made.* Like a hinge. Like a lock. Like a door.

She stopped where the trail narrowed against the cliff face and put her hand on the rock because her legs had opinions of their own. The stone was cold through her glove. Somewhere below, the fae mourners were still singing — that thin, fragmentary music that threaded through the passes like water under ice. Fragments of the covenant. Fragments of what she was supposed to give up.

Her memories. All of them. Every road she knew, every oath she'd spoken, every scar and its story — poured into the heartstone like wine into a grave. Including Elias. *Especially* Elias, said some treacherous part of her that had noticed things over the past weeks and filed them away without permission: the way he blew on his tea before it needed blowing on; the way he said her name like he was testing whether it would hold his weight.

No. She wasn't doing it. Let the winter fall. Let both worlds freeze solid and be done with the whole rotten bargain. A thousand years of snow was a cleaner death than becoming an empty thing that walked around wearing her sword-hand.

She turned back down the trail.

Elias was waiting at the edge of the firelight, arms crossed against the cold, breath smoking. He didn't say anything when she came into the light. He just looked at her with that terrible patient face of his, the face of a man who had memorized a prophecy rather than burn it.

"I'm going," she said. "There's a deep road off the Sorrowmere shelf. Old smuggler's run. It stays below the tree line, there's a hot spring, game trails, enough to last out the winter. Two people could live."

"Live," he said.

"Survive. Yes. That's what I'm offering. That's the sane option, in case you've lost track of which one that is."

"You're offering me a hole to hide in while the world ends."

"I'm offering us *winter*, Elias. Real winter. Snow you can dig through. The long winter hasn't finished falling yet — we have time, we could—"

"We don't." He said it quietly, and that quiet cut worse than shouting. "You saw the village. You saw what it looks like when the forgetting gets there first."

The village. God, the village. The smiling woman who'd offered them bread and couldn't say why she baked it. The children playing a game whose rules changed every minute because no one remembered them longer than a breath. And Marguerite standing in the square, reaching for her oath-name — *Ashvane*, the name they'd burned into her at nineteen — and finding static where it should have been, a blank spot like a tooth knocked out.

She hadn't told him about that. He'd figured it out anyway. He always figured things out anyway.

"That was one village," she said. "Deep roads are different. The old wards—"

"The old wards are failing. You know it. You felt it before I ever fell through your sky." He stepped closer, and the fire threw his shadow long across the snow between them. "And even if they weren't — even if we tucked ourselves into the warmest hollow in Thornfell and ate well all winter — what exactly survives down there, Marguerite? Walk me through it. Spring comes, the thaw opens the pass, and who walks out?"

"*I* walk out."

"Do you?" His voice stayed gentle, which made it unforgivable. "Because I watched you stand in that square for ten seconds trying to remember your own name. Ten seconds. What's it going to be in March? In June? You'll come up out of the ground blinking at the sun and you won't know why you're carrying a sword, and you'll meet someone — maybe me, maybe a stranger — and you'll smile at them the way that woman smiled, empty and kind, and you'll offer them bread you can't explain."

"Don't." Her hand had gone to her sword hilt without asking her. She took it away. "Don't you dare use them against me."

"I'm using *you* against you. There's a difference." He crouched and fed a branch to the fire, unhurried, and she realized with a lurch that he'd learned that from her — the way she fed fires, low and steady, smokeless. He'd been watching her the way she'd been watching him. They were both cataloguing each other. That was what made this unbearable. "Survival isn't the same as living. You taught me that, actually. First week. You said the mountain doesn't care if you survive — it cares whether you know who's walking on it."

"That was about not getting lost, not a philosophy."

"It's both. Everything you say is both. That's what I—" He stopped. Started again. "The village isn't dead, Marguerite. Everyone's alive. Fed. Warm. Smiling. It's the most peaceful place I've ever seen, and I would rather die in a ditch than spend one night there. Because nothing is home. The forgetting doesn't kill you. It just takes the person and leaves the body walking around, comfortable, harmless. That's not survival. That's the long version of dying."

"So what's your answer, then? Reforge the covenant. Pour myself into a rock. Become the seal, become the door, become whatever the Canticle says I was manufactured to be — and lose every memory I have, including yours. That's *living* to you?" She laughed, and it came out ugly. "At least the village keeps its body. I'd be giving up everything. I'd look at you afterward and see a stranger."

"Yes," he said.

The plainness of it stopped her cold.

"Yes," he said again. "You'd look at me and see a stranger. And I'd know you. That's the part you keep skipping. Someone remembers. I remember. Every road, every oath-word, every stupid joke you told on the frozen river when the ice was cracking under us and you were more scared than you've ever been — I carry it. The covenant doesn't erase you, Marguerite. It just makes me the keeper of you instead of the mountain being the keeper of both of us."

"And what happens to *you*?" Her voice cracked on it. "You step into a circle built a century ago for one swordspeaker and one fae voice. Nobody wrote you into it. Vess doesn't know what you'd pay. The Canticle doesn't either — you checked, you told me, there's no verse for the man out of the snow after the binding. You'd be spending yourself on a coin nobody's counted."

"Then we're even," he said, "because you've been spending yourself on a coin everyone counted before you were born, and you never got to agree to any of it."

The wind came down the switchback then, hard, and the mourners' song rose with it — clearer for a moment, close. Marguerite heard a phrase in it she almost recognized. A line of the covenant in the old tongue, the one Sorrel's order had forbidden, the one she'd spoken once at nineteen with a blade across her palms:

*I am not the wall between the worlds. I am the door that chooses.*

She had thought, for eleven years, that the order had broken the covenant out of fear. Now, hearing the words again in a dead language sung by grieving creatures of snow, she understood something worse. The swordspeakers hadn't misunderstood the bargain. They'd understood it perfectly. A door that chooses. A seal with a will inside it. And they couldn't abide that — couldn't abide a human being holding the fate of two worlds and being allowed to *want* things — so they'd forged her, bound her, and buried her in the mountains like a knife in a drawer.

"They never meant for me to choose," she said. "That's the whole point of me. I'm the part of the covenant they could control. The fae sing their half; I just... hold. Stand in the doorway and hold until I'm worn smooth." She looked at her hands, gloved in leather gone soft at the knuckles. "Vess said *made*. Made for it. Do you understand what that does to a person? Eleven years I thought I survived the betrayal. Turns out I was *built* to absorb it. My whole life is load-bearing."

Elias was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped its patience entirely, and what was underneath was rawer and better.

"Good," he said.

"—What?"

"Good. Be furious about it. You should be furious. It's monstrous, what they did to you. But Marguerite — listen to me — the answer to *they made me a door without my consent* is not *therefore I will stop being a person too*. That's letting them win twice. First they make you a tool, and then you finish the job by making yourself nothing." He stood, and came around the fire, slow, the way you approach a wounded animal, and she hated that she let him. "You're not choosing between being a door and being free. You're choosing between a self you surrender on purpose and a self that leaks out of you in a smiling village while the snow comes down forever."

"There has to be another way."

"If there were, I'd take it. You know I would. I've been looking since the day I read my own arrival date in a book older than my grandmother." He stopped in front of her. Close enough that she could see the frost in his eyelashes, the exhaustion under his eyes, the stubbornness that she'd once mistaken for naivety and now recognized as the strongest material in either world. "But if the choice is real — if it truly comes down to it — then here's mine. I'm not hiding in a hole with half of you. I'd rather have three months of the whole Marguerite Underhill, furious and terrified and magnificent on a frozen river, than forty years of a stranger who smiles at me over bread."

Something in her chest broke along an old seam.

She turned away so he wouldn't see it land. "You don't get to decide how I grieve myself."

"No. But I get to refuse to help." He said it simply. "If you go down to the deep road, I'm not coming. I'll climb for the heartstone alone, and I'll fail, probably, because I don't know the roads and the mountain hates strangers, and the Choir will find me, and that will be that. But I won't be the reason you chose to forget yourself. I won't make it comfortable. If you want to disappear, you'll have to do it knowing somebody was standing right here asking you not to."

"That's blackmail."

"That's love," he said, too fast, and then went white around the mouth, because neither of them had said it yet, and now the word was lying there in the snow between them like something dropped from a high place.

Marguerite stared at him. The fire popped. Somewhere far below, the mourners' song turned over into a new fragment, and she heard her own name in it — not Ashvane, not Underhill. Something older. Something that sounded, impossibly, like a question rather than a sentence.

*The door that chooses,* the song said. *Chooses.*

Not a wall. Not a lock. Not a tool the order could bury and a prophecy could spend. A door. Doors open from either side. Doors can stay shut. Doors are only doors because someone decides.

"I hate you," she said, and her voice was wrecked and wet and completely unconvincing.

"I know," Elias said. "You mentioned. On the river. Twice."

She laughed — one broken note, surprised out of her — and wiped her face with the back of her glove, rough, businesslike, the way you handle a task that mustn't be dwelt upon. Then she looked east, toward the heartstone road, where the sky had begun to do the thing it had been doing for days: thickening, whitening, the long winter stacking itself behind the peaks like a wave deciding.

"We leave at first light," she said. "We climb the Sorrowmere shelf, and if the shelf ice is down we go through the Hollow Stair, and if the Hollow Stair is warded we cut the ward, and if the Choir is waiting at the top—" She rolled her shoulders, and felt the oath-words shift in her chest like coals settling. "Then the Choir learns why they should have killed me when they had the chance."

Elias nodded. He started gathering the gear, and then paused, and said, carefully, not looking at her:

"For what it's worth — I didn't mean to say it. The word. But I'm not taking it back."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment, this impossible man who had fallen out of another world into her storm with her name in his pocket, and she found that she couldn't take it back either, and couldn't say it forward, and that the space between those two facts was somehow the truest place she'd ever stood.

"Eat," she said. "Sleep. We have a hard climb."

He smiled, and obeyed.

She took first watch, because she always did, and sat with her sword across her knees facing the eastern dark — and it was there, in the smallest hours, that she heard it. Not the mourners' song. Something answering it from the far side of the mountain. Many voices, disciplined and cold, rising in a hymn she knew down to the marrow, because she had sung it at nineteen with blood on her palms.

The Hollow Choir was singing. And they were singing the counter-ritual.

They weren't following them anymore.

They were already at the heartstone.
