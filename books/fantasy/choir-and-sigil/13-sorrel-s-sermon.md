## 13. Sorrel's Sermon

The chapel at Greywatch had no roof worth the name, so Prelate Sorrel preached to the sky.

She liked it that way. A roof made a congregation comfortable, and comfort was the enemy of understanding. The forty choristers kneeling in the snow before her had come from villages whose names were already softening in their mouths — Thornfell folk, border folk, people who had spent their whole lives within earshot of the mountain and its hungers — and they needed to hear the truth spoken plainly, with the wind coming through the broken rafters, so they could not pretend it was a story told indoors.

"The winter is not coming," she said. "The winter has been falling for a hundred years, one grain at a time, and you have been calling it weather."

Snow gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it away. She was sixty-one years old and she had not been warm since the night she took her vows, and this was not a hardship. It was a discipline. Cold kept the mind honest.

"You have heard the old stories," she went on. "The fair ones in the high passes who trade in riddles. Who take a child from a cradle and leave a changeling in its place. Who borrow a memory the way a neighbor borrows a knife, and return it duller than it went." She let the silence stretch until the only sound was wind and the small wet breathing of forty people afraid of the answer. "You have heard those stories called superstition. I am here to tell you they are history. And I am here to tell you why they stopped."

*Because we sealed the doors,* she did not say. *Because my sisters and I broke the covenant and nailed the realms apart, and every year since, the seal has thinned.*

What she said was: "They stopped because your grandmothers' grandmothers fought. Because ordinary people — farmers, smiths, mothers — stood at the passes with iron in their hands and refused. And the fair ones grew hungry, and the hunger grew patient, and now the patience is ending." She raised one hand, and the choristers leaned toward it like plants toward light. "The long winter you feel in your bones is not punishment. It is a door left open in a house where the fire is dying. We are going to close it. Permanently. And when it closes, the cold stops, the forgetting stops, and your children grow up in a world with one sky instead of two."

A woman near the front began to weep quietly. Sorrel marked her face. Grief was raw material. It could be shaped into anything, but it was most useful shaped into resolve.

* * *

Afterward, in the vestry — a stone room with one window, one brazier, and a table bearing the only objects Sorrel had carried for thirty years — she allowed herself three minutes of stillness before the work resumed.

On the table lay a sword.

It was not a fine weapon anymore. The blade had gone grey as old pewter, and the words once etched along its fuller had been deliberately effaced, hammered flat by her own hand on the night of her swearing-out, so that the metal held no speech and could hold none again. But she remembered what the words had said. That was the cruelty of her particular gift, or her particular curse: Sorrel forgot nothing.

That was what the Choir's rite had bought her, all those years ago, when she knelt in a cellar beneath the old chapterhouse and let them cut the oath out of her voice. Every swordspeaker who survived that night lost something different. One sister lost her sense of direction and wandered the lowlands until she died in a ditch. Another lost her own face — looked into mirrors and saw a stranger, wept, smashed every glass in three counties. Sorrel lost nothing except the ability to speak the words aloud.

Her memory stayed whole. All of it. The covenant, the binding, the heartstone, the names of the fae who had sung at her swearing-in with voices like water over stones. She remembered Vess. She remembered the taste of the bargain in the air, sweet as frost on the tongue.

And she remembered why they had done it.

* * *

The girl had been called Merrin Dace, and she had lived in a village called Aldery, and Sorrel had been twenty-six and newly sworn when the summons came.

The mother would not stop screaming. That was the thing Sorrel remembered first, always first, before the snow and the tracks and the small bare footprints that ended at a ring of mushrooms pressed flat into the heath. The mother screaming like an animal caught in a trap, and the father standing very still, holding a bundle wrapped in his own coat, and inside the bundle a baby that was almost right. Almost. Its eyes were the wrong color and it never blinked enough and when it cried — and it did cry, endlessly, hungrily — the sound came out slightly behind its open mouth, like a song played by a musician half a beat late.

"It took my daughter," the father said, in a voice with no feeling left in it at all. "Three nights ago. This is what it left."

Sorrel had knelt beside the changeling and looked into its borrowed face, and the thing had looked back with an intelligence that was ancient and amused and utterly without malice, the way a cat watches a moth, and it had smiled at her. And she had understood, in that moment, with the clarity that would organize the rest of her life, that there was no negotiation to be had. No bargain she could strike, no oath she could speak that would bring Merrin Dace home. The fair ones did not steal out of need. They stole because the crossing was easy and the taking was beautiful to them, the way mortals pick flowers.

Merrin Dace was never found. Sorrel searched for eleven days. On the twelfth she found the child's shoe, placed neatly on a stone, laced.

The order's elders had told her to let it go. *The covenant holds both realms in balance,* they said. *Balance includes loss.* And Sorrel had bowed and obeyed, because she was young and sworn, and she had gone home and lain awake every night for a year listening for mothers screaming.

Then came the winter of the eaten village. Hallowmere, it was called, though by the time Sorrel reached it the name was already going. She walked its streets and found people smiling. They were warm, fed, unhurt. They greeted her cheerfully and could not tell her where they were. Not wouldn't — couldn't. The word *Hallowmere* slid off their minds like rain off wax. Their children's names were gone. Their dead were gone. A woman showed Sorrel her garden and wept without knowing why, because her hands remembered tending a grave that her mind could no longer find.

The fae had not done it out of malice either. That was the worst part. Memory was simply what leaked across a thin place, the way heat leaks through a bad wall. The covenant had been fraying even then, worn thin by a century of small crossings, and the leak was widening, and the elders' answer was to patch it, and re-patch it, and bind another swordspeaker to the seal, and another, spending human lives like candles against a draft.

Sorrel had sat in the ruined chapel at Hallowmere with a woman who could not remember her own wedding, and she had done the arithmetic that would end her old life.

The covenant cost everything, forever, and bought nothing but the continuation of itself. It was a wall that required a body bricked into its foundation every generation, and beyond the wall the thieves went on stealing, and the leaks went on leaking, and the smiling hollow people went on smiling. There was no version of the bargain where everyone was safe. There was only the question of who paid, and how often, and whether the payment ever ended.

Sealing the realms entirely ended it.

Yes, the fae would die. Slowly, over generations, as their realm starved without the crossing of mortal memory and mortal warmth — and Sorrel would be lying if she claimed that price weighed nothing, because she remembered Vess's voice, and she grieved it even now. But grief for the beautiful and distant was a luxury purchased with the lives of the near and plain. Merrin Dace had been near. The woman in Hallowmere had been near. The fae were a poem, and poems did not bleed.

*One world, one sky, one fire that stays lit.* That was the sermon. She had preached it for thirty years, and every year the seal thinned and every year more of her choristers arrived with frostbite in July and gaps in their childhoods, and every year the sermon got easier to believe, which was how Sorrel knew it was true. Truth, she had learned, does not get harder to carry. Only lies do.

* * *

The knock came at dusk. Brother Anselm, snow to his knees, breath ragged.

"Word from the high pass, Prelate. The trapper at Cold Saddle — the one we turned last month. He sent his boy down through the storm." Anselm thrust out a scrap of paper, grease-stained, the handwriting drunken with cold. "Two travelers. A man not of these parts, come out of nowhere in the blizzard, walking with the Underhill woman."

Sorrel read the note twice. Outside, the wind rose a register, and somewhere far up the mountain, faint as a memory, something sang.

"Underhill," she said.

"You knew her?"

"I knew her order." Sorrel set the note down on the table, next to the mute sword. Marguerite Underhill. The last of them, the elders had said, the one they'd bound too late and trained too little and abandoned entirely when the order collapsed — and yet the mountain had answered her at the waystation chapel three nights ago. Anselm's scouts reported the ground shaking, the snow standing up off the drifts like startled birds. An oath-word, spoken after thirty years of silence. Sorrel had felt it through the soles of her boots from six miles away, and she had known exactly what it was, because she had once been able to make the mountain do the same.

So the seal had chosen its moment to wake its keeper. Or the keeper had stumbled into her purpose, which amounted to the same thing. Either way, the Underhill woman was climbing toward the heartstone with a stranger from beyond the rift, and if they reached it before the Choir's counter-rite was laid, everything Sorrel had built — every village turned, every pass watched, every chorister sworn — would come apart in a single night of singing.

"Prelate?" Anselm said. "Your orders?"

She looked at the sword. Hammered-flat words. A mercy she had performed on herself, once, and regretted in the way one regrets all self-mutilations, distantly, on sleepless nights.

If she caught the Underhill woman, she would offer her the same choice the Choir offered everyone: kneel, be unmade gently, live out a warm and simple life in a world with one sky. If the woman refused —

Then Sorrel would do what she had done at Hallowmere. What she had done at every thin place for thirty years. The arithmetic did not change just because the number on one side of it had a name and a sword.

"Break camp tonight," she said. "We take the Choir road to the basin. Forty singers, full rite-gear, and the iron bells." She pulled her hood up. Snow had begun to fall again, thick and silent, the long winter's edge advancing down the slopes like a tide remembering the shore. "And Anselm — send word ahead to the trappers on the heartstone road. Anyone sheltering them feeds them. I want the mountain closed to those two like a fist."

Anselm hesitated. He was young, and kind, and he had joined the Choir because his sister had been taken, and kindness in subordinates was useful right up until it wavered. "And if the villagers won't turn them away? The Underhill woman knows roads the rest of us don't. Half the high country loves her for the smuggling."

"Then remind them what love cost them." Sorrel stepped out into the dark, where the choristers were already striking tents, and raised her voice so it carried the way a sermon carries, the way a blade carries. "Tell them the fair ones are singing again in the deep passes. Tell them it's true. Tell them the only question left is who gets to close the door — us, or the woman carrying the key."

Far above, through the streaming snow, the heartstone's peak glowed faintly, like a bell struck once and still ringing.

Sorrel began to climb.
