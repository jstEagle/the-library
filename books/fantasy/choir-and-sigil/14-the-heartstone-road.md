## 14. The Heartstone Road

We climbed out of the vale at first light, and the winter was waiting for us like a debt collector.

Not snow, not yet. The long winter's edge moved ahead of us up the mountain as a stillness — a hush that fell over the pines one by one, a frost that crept across the scree in a slow silver tide. Behind it the world went gray and quiet and terribly neat, like a room nobody lived in anymore. I had spent my life reading Thornfell's weather, and I had never seen anything move with such patience.

"It's faster than yesterday," Elias said.

"Everything is." I checked the straps on my pack, then his, longer than I needed to, because touching something solid helped. "The heartstone sits in a basin below the high shoulder, two days' climb if the roads are kind."

"And if they're not?"

"Then it's two days anyway. Just longer ones." I started walking. "Keep your hands warm. The cold here isn't weather. It's an argument, and it wants you to agree with it."

He fell into step behind me, and for a while there was only the crunch of our boots and the thin whine of wind through the pass. My sword rode my hip where it had ridden for twelve years, and Vess's grief over it was still fresh in my mind — the way she had touched the blade like you touch a scar on someone else's face. *Forged for the binding,* she'd sung. *Made to hold what must be held.* I hadn't slept much. Every time I closed my eyes I saw the heartstone basin ahead of me, waiting, patient as the winter itself.

I had made my peace with it. Mostly. In the dark hours I made it again, from scratch, the way you re-tie a knot that keeps slipping. Then morning came and I tied it once more and we walked.

* * *

The first illusion caught us before noon, on the shelf of road above the treeline.

I smelled woodsmoke first. That was how I knew it wasn't real — nothing burned up here, nothing should have been burning — but knowing didn't loosen its grip. Around a bend of granite stood a hall I knew down to its foundations: the chapterhouse of the Order of the Oathbound Sword, slate roof furred with rime, the great door standing open, firelight spilling gold onto the snow.

It had burned eleven years ago. I had watched it burn. I had set the torch myself, after, to keep the Choir from raising anything in the ashes.

And in the doorway stood Serah Dane, my sword-mother, gray-streaked braid over one shoulder, arms crossed the way she always crossed them when I came in late from drill.

"Marguerite," she said. "You're late. You've been late for years, girl. Come inside. The stew's hot and the floor's swept and nobody here will ask you for anything ever again."

My feet moved. I'll not pretend otherwise. Three steps before I caught myself, boots squeaking on snow that was suddenly, impossibly, warm.

"Marguerite." Elias's hand on my shoulder. Not pulling. Just weight. Just *here*.

"She's dead," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "I know she's dead. I carried her out myself and—"

"I know." He didn't say *it isn't real*, which would have been easy and useless. He said, "What did she smell like? When you were small?"

I blinked. "What?"

"You heard me."

I looked at the woman in the doorway, perfect down to the chip in her front tooth, and I thought about being seven years old and falling asleep against her side while she mended gear, and how she smelled of tallow and cedar and cold iron.

"Cedar," I said. "And iron."

The woman in the doorway smiled. It was a kind smile. It was also slightly wrong, the way a portrait is wrong — everything correct, nothing breathing.

"That's how you know," Elias said quietly. "The mountain read your memory. But memories don't smell right when someone else wears them. They're too tidy. Too complete." He nodded past her, up the road. "Come on."

Serah Dane watched us go. She didn't follow; illusions that beg rarely chase. But as we rounded the bend I heard her voice, pitched exactly to carry:

"You could rest, Marguerite. You've earned rest. Nobody would blame you."

"No," I said, to the mountain, to the dead, to myself. "But nobody would remember me either. And I've decided I mind about that now."

The hall was gone when I looked back. Only scree, and the gray tide of frost, closer than it had been.

* * *

Elias got his turn at dusk.

We'd made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang, small fire, backs to stone. He'd gone quiet an hour earlier, in that particular way he went quiet when he was thinking hard enough to hurt himself, and I was banking the coals when he said, "There's a bakery."

"What?"

"Down the slope." He pointed, and there it was: a narrow street I had never seen and somehow recognized instantly, because I had been listening to him talk about it for weeks. Gas lamps just flickering on. Awnings. A shopfront with bread in the window and a woman behind the counter who had his mother's hands.

"There's a bakery," he said again, "and my street, and my flat above the chemist's, three flights up, and my kettle, and—" His breath hitched. "Marguerite, it's *Tuesday*. I can hear traffic. There's a Tuesday happening down there right now."

I took his hand. His fingers were freezing. Mine weren't much better.

"The mountain read you too."

"It read me and it built the whole thing. The kettle. My books. My bed." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Do you know how long it's been since I slept in a real bed? Do you know what I wouldn't give for one ordinary, boring, terrible Tuesday?"

"Yes," I said. "That's why we're going to sit here and watch it very carefully until it goes away."

"How do you know it will go away?"

"Because mine did. Because it's bait, and bait doesn't need to last." I shifted so I could see both him and the street. "Tell me something true instead. Something from here. Something it couldn't have gotten out of your head."

He thought. The lamps down the slope flickered — or maybe the whole street did, a stutter like a film catching.

"Vess," he said slowly. "Vess sang a verse about you. Before you knew any of this. 'The seal walks willingly or not at all.'" His grip tightened on my hand. "You walked. You're walking right now, on a busted ankle and half a night's sleep, toward a thing that will take every memory you have, because two worlds are worth more than your one life. That's true. That happened. That's mine."

The street guttered. The woman with his mother's hands turned her face, and where her features should have been there was smooth blankness, and then the whole scene folded up like paper and the slope was just black rock under starlight.

Elias sat very still for a long moment. Then he said, hoarse: "I wanted it. You understand that. Right up until it vanished, some part of me was already inside, kettle on, shoes off."

"I know. Mine too."

"That doesn't frighten you?"

"It terrifies me," I said. "But wanting comfort isn't treason, Elias. Stopping for it would be. So we don't stop."

* * *

We climbed through the night, because stopping felt like losing.

The frost tide gained on us in the small hours, and somewhere near dawn it reached us — the edge of the long winter rolling over the road like a slow gray wave, and where it passed, the world changed texture. Snow stopped being soft. Sound stopped carrying. My breath hung in the air and didn't disperse, just drifted after me in little ghost-ribbons that refused to fade.

And the illusions got stronger.

They stopped bothering with full scenes. Now it was fragments — a smell of cedar around a bend of rock, the sound of a kettle whistling in the wind, a snatch of a song Serah used to hum while sharpening blades. Small comforts, scattered along the road like coins. Each one cost me a moment of stride, a hitch of grief, a clench of jaw. None of them were meant to stop us. They were meant to *slow* us, and the winter behind them never slowed at all.

"They're not testing whether we're strong," Elias panted beside me, somewhere in the white hours before sunrise. His lips were going blue. We were sharing one scarf at this point, taking turns. "They're measuring."

"Measuring what?"

"How much we each weigh. What it costs to pull us forward versus let us sink." He stumbled, caught himself on my arm. "The Canticle says the mountain tests all pilgrims. I thought that was poetry."

"Everything in that book is poetry," I said. "None of it is only poetry."

* * *

Near midmorning we came to the last test, and this one had teeth.

The road narrowed onto a ridge with drops on either side — a knife-edge of granite with cloud boiling below — and standing on it, twenty paces ahead, was me.

Or close enough. A Marguerite in dry clothes with color in her cheeks and no shadows under her eyes, sword slung loose and easy, standing beside a version of Elias who was laughing at something. They looked *rested*. They looked like two people who had solved their problems.

"See?" my double called out, friendly as anything. "This is what it looks like on the other side. We found another way. We turned back, we wintered in the deep roads, we survived, and look — happy. Whole. Nobody forgot anything. Come meet yourself."

Beside me, the real Elias made a small wounded sound.

"Don't," I whispered.

"I'm not—"

"You are. So am I. Look away from them and walk."

We walked the ridge with our doubles watching, and it was the hardest hundred paces of my life, harder than the duel with Sorrel, harder than the oath I spoke over Serah's body while the chapterhouse smoked behind me. Because the lie wasn't that those people existed. The lie was that they *could*. That there was a version of this where everyone kept everything and the world kept turning and nobody had to pay.

There wasn't. There was only the road, and the price, and the choice to keep walking anyway.

Halfway across, my double stopped smiling.

"You'll forget him," it said, and the voice was mine, and that was the cruelest part. "You've known him one season. One. You'll stand at that stone and give up the only person who ever chose you without needing you to be a weapon, and you won't even grieve him properly, because grief needs memory, and you won't have any. Is that worth two worlds, Marguerite? Is *he* worth it?"

I stopped.

Elias stopped beside me. And then — this is the part I'll hold onto however long I last, the part I want stitched into whatever the heartstone leaves of me — he stepped forward, into the wind, between me and my own face, and said:

"She doesn't have to answer that. Because it's not her bargain alone anymore. Whatever that stone takes, it takes from both of us. That was decided in a river and a fae hollow and about forty arguments, and you weren't there for any of them." He turned, took my hand, laced our frozen fingers together. "Walk, Marguerite."

We walked.

Behind us my double said something else, but the wind took it, and I chose to let the wind have it forever.

* * *

We came over the shoulder of the mountain as the sun set, and there it was.

The basin opened below us like a cupped hand — a vast bowl of stone ringed by pillars of ice, and at its center, the heartstone: a single mass of pale crystal taller than a man, humming. I felt it through my boot-soles before I heard it, a note too low for ears, a struck bell that never finished ringing. The air above it shimmered. Rift-light, faint and gold, threading through the cracks in the world like veins through marble.

And filling the far side of the basin, ranked and robed and singing, stood the Hollow Choir.

Hundreds of them. Torches in the blue dusk. Their hymn rolled across the basin in waves, and where each wave broke, the rift-light dimmed a little more.

At their head, on a spur of rock, stood a figure in white and ash-gray, arms raised, conducting with slow sweeps of her hand. Even at this distance I knew the stance. I'd drilled beside it for ten years.

Sorrel had beaten us to the stone.

Elias sank down behind the ridgeline beside me, blood on his lip where the cold had split it, and looked at me with something worse than fear in his face — recognition. He'd memorized the Canticle. He knew what came next as well as I did.

"Marguerite," he said. "How much time do we have?"

I counted the hymn's cadence, the way the light thinned with each verse, and did the arithmetic, and hated the answer.

"Until dawn," I said. "Maybe less. And they've already started."
