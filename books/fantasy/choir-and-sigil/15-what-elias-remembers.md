## 15. What Elias Remembers

The mountain gave him a door.

That was the first wrong thing. There were no doors on the heartstone road — only wind-scoured stone and the blue-white glare of the long winter pressing down from every side. But there it stood at the bend of the pass, half-buried in snowdrift: a green door with a brass knocker shaped like a heron, paint peeling along the bottom edge where the rain always got in.

Elias knew that door. He had painted it himself, six years ago, on a Sunday in April, because the landlord said he could if he left the hinges alone.

"Marguerite," he started to say, and turned —

— and the pass was gone. The wind was gone. He was standing on a cobbled street in low golden light, and a woman with a market basket nodded to him as she passed, and somewhere down the lane a dog was barking at nothing, and the air smelled like bread and warm dust and spring.

His knees nearly went.

*It's not real.* Marguerite's voice in his memory, flat and certain, from three nights ago when they'd argued about whether to trust the fae road at all. *The mountain doesn't lie exactly. It just shows you what you want until you walk off a cliff wanting it.*

He looked down. His boots were his boots — the ones he'd worn the day he fell through the snow, scuffed at the toe, laces fraying. Not the furs she'd traded half their salt for. His hands were bare. No sword at his hip; he'd never carried a sword in his life.

The woman with the basket had reached the corner. She turned back and called, "You'll be late again, Mr. Vane," in a voice he almost recognized, and laughed, and walked on.

*Don't follow her,* he told himself. *Don't go through the door.*

He followed her. He hated himself for it even as his feet moved, but he followed her, because the alternative was standing alone in an invented street knowing it was invented, and he had been cold for so long, and here the air was warm.

* * *

The day unfolded the way days used to.

He went through the green door and up the stairs and there was his flat, small and shabby and perfect, the kettle with the chipped lid, the stack of survey maps on the table marked with his own cramped annotations, the window that stuck unless you lifted it just so. Outside, the city went about its business. A tram bell rang. Children shouted in a yard below. Someone was playing a piano badly two floors down, the same eight bars over and over, patient and terrible.

He made tea. His hands knew how. That was the worst part, he thought, watching the water darken — not that any of this existed, but that *he* existed inside it, seamless, a man who had never seen a rift open in a blizzard, never heard swordspeech crack the sky, never watched a village full of smiling people who couldn't remember their own names.

There was a letter on the table. He didn't remember it being there. It bore the seal of the Royal Survey Office, and inside was a single line in his supervisor's handwriting:

*Vane — the Thornfell triangulation is still wrong. Come back and finish it.*

He read it four times. In his old life — his real life, the one before the snow — he had spent two years trying to fix a survey of the Thornfell range that refused to come out right. Every instrument, every method, every clear day's observation, and the mountains would not hold still on paper. He had written in his final report, half as a joke, that the range seemed to be *elsewhere than where it agreed to be.*

He had thought it was a joke. He had fallen through a hole in the world into those same mountains, and now the letter sat on his table asking him to come back and finish the work, and Elias understood that the mountain was not showing him a memory. It was showing him an argument.

"No," he said aloud, to the kitchen, to the kettle, to whatever patient intelligence wore his city like a coat. "I know what you're doing."

The piano downstairs played its eight bars and began again.

* * *

But it was a whole day, and a man can only refuse a day for so long.

He went to the office because the letter expected him to and because refusing felt, absurdly, like rudeness. Colleagues greeted him by name. Someone had left a pastry on his desk, the kind he liked, and he ate it without deciding to. He corrected a map. He argued amiably about drainage with a man whose name he suddenly could not recall, though the man clearly knew his, and the man's face was pleasant and slightly blurred at the edges, like a photograph left in the sun.

That was the tell. Everyone here was slightly blurred. The city had sound and smell and warmth, but no one had eyes that held him. When he looked too long at a face, it grew patient rather than offended, the way scenery is patient.

Still. Still, when the workday ended and the light went amber and long across the rooftops, he walked home through streets he loved, and he let himself love them, deliberately, the way you drink something good before a fast. He bought bread. He climbed the stairs. He opened the stuck window just so, and the evening came in smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

And there, on the table where the letter had been, lay a scrap of parchment.

He knew it before he touched it. Of course he did. It was the scrap — the one he'd clutched half-frozen in the drift while Marguerite stood over him with a drawn sword and murder in her posture, the one bearing a single line of old script: *the Underhill woman.* The parchment that had made her spare him. The parchment that had started everything.

Except here it was different. Here the line had changed. In the same old hand, in ink that looked centuries dry, it read:

*and the Vane man chooses.*

"That's cheating," Elias said quietly. His voice shook. "You're supposed to show me what I lost. Not what I'm losing."

The window rattled in its frame. Downstairs, the piano stopped mid-bar.

He sat down on his own chair at his own table in his own life and held the parchment and did not cry, because some stubborn clerk-part of him insisted that men who cried at furniture had already lost. But his chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with wounds.

Because here was the thing he had been refusing to say, even to himself, even in the dark of the waystation chapel with Marguerite asleep an arm's length away: he had woken up this morning — if it was morning; time moved strangely under the long winter — and for three full seconds he had not known where he was. And in those three seconds, waking to stone and furs and the sound of Marguerite banking the fire, his first feeling had been *wrong place, wrong life, get back.*

Three seconds. Then memory arrived, and with it the strange settling weight of the last weeks — the roads, the oath-words, the fae song threading through the passes like smoke, her hand hauling him up a scree slope while the winter tide crawled after them. Three seconds of homesickness, and then a lifetime of something else.

But the mountain had found those three seconds. The mountain had built a city around them.

* * *

Evening came, and with it the dream's masterstroke.

She knocked at the green door. Not Marguerite — someone else, someone from before, a woman named Liss whom he had almost married in another year, or perhaps only almost-almost; the memory had gone soft with disuse. She stood on the landing with snow melting in her hair, which was impossible, because it wasn't snowing, and she smiled at him with a smile he had once crossed a city to see.

"You look like a man deciding something," she said.

"I am."

"It's warm in there." She tilted her head toward the flat, toward the lamplight, toward the whole invented world humming gently around them like a struck glass. "Whatever's out there — it's cold, isn't it? I can feel it on you. You're carrying cold like a coat you can't take off."

"Yes," Elias said.

"And you could put it down." She said it kindly. That was the cruelty of it — the kindness. "Stay. Finish your survey. Marry me or don't, but stay. Nobody out there will blame you. Nobody out there will even know."

And that was true, and that was the trap's whole architecture, and he saw it all at once with terrible clarity: not a prison, an exit. The mountain wasn't tempting him with a lie. It was offering him a mercy, and mercies are harder to refuse than threats, because you have to become the kind of person who refuses kindness.

He thought of the village under early snow. The smiling people. The baker who had offered them bread with vacant delight and could not say what grain went into it or who taught him to bake. Memory eaten down to the warm surface of things. Comfort with the self scooped out of it, the way a wasp leaves a husk.

If he stayed, he would be happy. He would be happy the way they were happy — a shape filled with warmth and nothing underneath, a man-shaped room with the fire lit and no one home.

"Liss," he said, using the name like a handhold, "you're not her. You're very well made, and I'm going to remember this evening for the rest of my life, whichever life that turns out to be. But you're not her, and this isn't mine, and there's a woman on a mountain who burned her own book rather than be ruled by it, and she's waiting for me, and she doesn't wait graciously."

The woman's smile flickered — not hurt, but *recalibrated*, the way a machine recalibrates — and for one instant Elias saw through the skin of the world: grey stone, blue ice, the pass, the wind. Marguerite's silhouette against the snow, arms crossed, waiting, exactly as he'd described.

Then the street closed over again, warm and golden, and Liss said, gently, "Then you'd better wake up, Elias Vane. Before you forget which one is the dream."

* * *

He woke weeping.

Not loudly. That was the humiliating part — no sobs, no drama, just tears running steadily down his face into the frozen fur collar while his body lay against the rock wall of the pass and his mind reassembled itself around the fact of cold. Real cold. Cold with no bakery under it.

Marguerite was crouched a few feet away, feeding the tiny fire they allowed themselves, and she had clearly been awake the whole time. She didn't ask if he was all right. She'd learned that about him early; he respected her for it now the way he respected load-bearing walls.

"How long?" His voice came out wrecked.

"Half a night." She fed a stick to the flames. "I pulled you back twice. You kept walking toward the cliff edge in your sleep, talking to somebody." A pause. "A woman?"

"A whole city." He sat up. Everything ached. The wound in his side from the river ice sent its usual dull complaint up through the bandages. "Bread, trams, a bad pianist, my old desk. The mountain built it out of three seconds of homesickness I didn't even know I was keeping."

"The illusions on this stretch hit everyone." Her voice was carefully neutral. "Mine showed me my order. All of them. Alive. It took me an hour to walk past them, and I've had years of practice hating them." She glanced at him sidelong. "Yours took half a night. What did it offer you?"

"Everything." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, rough, unashamed; there was no point being ashamed in front of Marguerite, who treated shame like a navigational error. "My life. My actual life, reconstructed down to the chipped kettle. And then it offered me the choice to keep it. Warm rooms, warm people, no winter, no war, no covenant. Just — go back. Be the man I was."

"And?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. He looked at his hands in the firelight — the same hands, scarred now where they hadn't been, steady now in ways they'd never needed to be. He tried to find the man who owned a flat with a green door and a stuck window, the man who complained about drainage and almost-married and measured mountains that wouldn't hold still on paper.

The man was there. He could describe him perfectly. But describing him felt like reading a report about a stranger. Somewhere between the blizzard and the frozen river and the night Vess had sung over his sword-hand, the seams had been cut and resewn, and the garment fit differently, and there was no unpicking it.

"I understood something in there," Elias said slowly. "Something I think I've been avoiding since the chapel. Even if we win. Even if we reforge the covenant and the rifts open and I can walk home through the snow into April—" His throat tightened. "I can't go back. Not because it's forbidden. Because the man who fell through doesn't exist anymore. He stayed behind on the far side of the rift the way a reflection stays in a window when you walk past. Whoever comes out the other end will have my name and my memories up to a point, and he'll be a stranger to me, and I'd be a stranger to him. Going home isn't a door anymore, Marguerite. It's a grave I'd be visiting."

The fire cracked. Above them, the long winter pressed down, vast and white and patient, and somewhere far below in the deep passes the fae were singing their broken covenant to the stone.

Marguerite said nothing for a long moment. Then, in the tone she reserved for things that mattered — the tone she usually used for oaths — she said, "Good."

He stared at her. "*Good?*"

"You finally want to be here." She poked the fire hard enough to send sparks climbing. "Do you know how exhausting it's been, dragging a man uphill who keeps looking over his shoulder for a door? Now you're looking forward. It's about time." But her voice caught slightly on the last word, and she covered it by standing, and brushing snow from her cloak, and pointing east along the pass with her sword arm.

Because the horizon had changed.

All night, while he dreamed his city, the winter's edge had been moving — they both could see it now, a front of absolute white rolling up the far valleys, not weather but *erasure*, swallowing ridgelines the way the village had swallowed names. And against that whiteness, small and black and deliberate, a line of figures was ascending the glacier toward the heartstone basin. Dozens of them. Walking in step. Singing.

The Hollow Choir had stopped hunting them.

They had simply decided to arrive first.

Marguerite watched them a moment, and Elias watched her watch them, and he saw her do the thing she did — fear going in, resolve coming out, the transformation taking less than a breath.

"They'll start the counter-ritual by tomorrow's dusk," she said. "We climb tonight."
