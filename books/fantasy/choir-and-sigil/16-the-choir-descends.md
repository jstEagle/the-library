## 16. The Choir Descends

We heard them before we saw them.

Not voices — the Hollow Choir didn't sing the way people sing. They *droned*, a hundred throats locked into a single note that rolled down the basin walls like water finding a crack in stone. It came through my boots before it came through my ears, and the heartstone answered it, and I felt both of those things in my teeth.

"They're already there," Elias said.

"I know." I lay flat on the ridge above the basin, snow to my chin, and watched the lights below. Dozens of them — lantern-glass on iron staves, arranged in a ring around the heartstone's shelf. The Choir had reached it first. Of course they had. Sorrel knew these mountains too; she'd been a swordspeaker once, before the order burned, before the world forgot her name along with everything else. She'd had a century to learn what I'd had ten years to learn, and she hadn't spent hers running contraband through frozen passes to keep her hands busy.

The heartstone sat at the bottom of the basin like a bell sunk in a lake. That was always how it had looked to me — not a rock but an instrument, dark and veined with something that wasn't quite silver, humming faintly even in summer when nothing else in Thornfell made a sound. Tonight it hummed louder than I'd ever heard it, and the drone from the Choir was pressing against that hum like a thumb over a string.

"What are they doing?" Elias asked.

"Building the seal." My breath fogged and froze on the wool of my collar. "See the ring? Those aren't lanterns. They're anchors. Each one is a rift-point — every place the two worlds used to touch, mapped and pinned down. When they finish the hymn, the stone will take the pattern and close all of them at once. Forever."

"And Vess?"

I didn't answer right away, because I was looking at the eastern rim, where the snow had been disturbed in long dragging furrows, and where something pale moved between the rocks.

"There," I said.

* * *

Vess came down the slope the way a wounded bird comes down — in lurches, catching herself on things that shouldn't have held her weight, leaving smears that glowed faintly blue against the stone. Her left arm hung wrong. Her light was guttering. When she reached us she collapsed into the snow beside my head and I saw that the wound across her shoulder wasn't bleeding blood; it was bleeding *darkness*, little threads of night seeping out of her and dissolving in the wind.

"Hymn-iron," she said, before I could ask. "They've learned to forge it. Sorrel remembered." She laughed, and it came out as frost. "She remembers everything. That's her whole terrible gift. She's the only one who kept it all, so she thinks she's the only one fit to decide."

"Can you fight?" I asked.

"No." She said it without shame, which somehow made it worse. "But we can sing. Listen — the others are coming."

And I listened, and beneath the Choir's drone I heard it: a second sound, rising out of the deep passes, threading up through the mountain itself. Not words. Not music, exactly. More like the memory of music — the way a room remembers laughter after everyone has gone. The fae mourners were coming down from the high places, dozens of them, maybe hundreds, drawn by the same thing that had drawn me: the heartstone crying out like a struck bell that no one would let stop ringing.

"They'll be slaughtered," I said.

"They will not come to fight." Vess pressed her good hand flat against the snow, and I felt the mountain shiver under my chest. "They will come to stand between the hymn and the stone. That is all any of us can do now, Underhill woman. Stand between. What happens after—" She looked at me then, and her eyes were old winter, patient and enormous. "What happens after belongs to you."

I hated her for saying it. I hated her because it was true.

Elias put his hand on my shoulder. He didn't say anything. He'd stopped trying to talk me toward the stone three days ago, somewhere on the frozen river, when he'd finally understood that the argument wasn't about courage or duty but about whether I could bear to be the thing I'd been made to be. He just stayed. That was his whole argument now: *he stayed.*

"Then we go down," I said. "Before they finish."

* * *

The basin floor was a bowl of packed snow lit from within. That was the wrongness of it — the light came up out of the ground, out of the heartstone's veins, pulsing slow as a heartbeat, and against that glow the Hollow Choir stood in their perfect circle like figures cut from black paper. Grey robes. Iron staves. Faces tilted up toward nothing, mouths open, pouring out the single sustained note that had been building since sundown.

Sorrel stood at the ring's northern point, where the oldest anchor was planted. Even at this distance I recognized her — tall, spare, white-haired, moving with the economical grace of a woman whose body remembered swordwork the way mine did. She held no weapon. She held a book, open against her forearm, and she read from it as she walked the circle, and wherever she walked, the anchors flared.

"She's writing it into the stone," Elias breathed beside me. "The Canticle backwards."

"The Canticle forwards," I corrected grimly. "That's the worst part. The prophecy and the seal are the same text. She isn't fighting fate. She's claiming it."

Below us, at the basin's southern mouth, the first of the mourners arrived.

They came out of the dark like a tide of candleflame — small lights, pale figures, some shaped almost human and some shaped like nothing that had ever walked under the sun. They did not run. They did not brandish anything. They walked into the light of the Choir's lantern-ring and began to sing, and their song went up against the hymn like water against fire.

The effect was immediate and awful. Where the two sounds met, the air itself seemed to bruise. Snow lifted off the ground in spirals. The heartstone's pulse stuttered, quickened, fought. One of the anchors cracked with a sound like ice giving way and went dark, and Sorrel turned her head toward it without breaking her note, and two robed figures detached from the circle to reforge it with hammer and chant.

"They can't hold," Elias said. "Marguerite, they can't hold for long—"

"I know."

I drew my sword.

The sound it made coming free of the scabbard was different tonight. It had been different since the river, different since Vess laid her hand on the blade and wept over it, different since I'd spoken the first oath-word in ten years and the mountain had answered me with its whole buried voice. The steel came out singing a low harmonic that matched the heartstone's pulse exactly, because that was what it had been forged to do. That was what *I* had been forged to do. A living seal. A sword-speaker whose voice and blade were the lock and key of a bargain older than either realm.

Every eye in the Choir found me at once. A hundred faces turned from the sky to the ridge line, and the hymn wavered — just a hair — and Sorrel closed her book.

"There she is," Sorrel called, and her voice carried the way voices carry in cold air, crisp and clear and almost kind. "The last sword. Come to watch, child? Or come to kneel?"

"Neither," I said, and stepped off the ridge, and let the slope carry me down.

* * *

We hit the basin floor running. Elias kept pace — he'd gotten better on the mountain, harder in the lungs and lighter in the feet, though he still ran like a man rather than whatever I'd become. Behind us the mourners' song swelled, and ahead of us the Choir's hymn bent to meet us, and between the two sounds the heartstone rang like the inside of a cathedral bell.

The first rank of zealots came apart from the circle to intercept. I gave them the flat of my blade and an oath-word — *stand* — and the word went through the snow like a shockwave, and six grown men and women sat down hard in the drifts as if the mountain had told them to. Which, I supposed, it had. Power like this should have frightened me more than it did. What frightened me was how easy it was. Ten years of silence and the old authority came back like a language never forgotten.

"Left!" Elias shouted, and I turned left, and his warning saved me from the hymn-iron net that whistled out of the dark. It hit the snow where I'd been and the flakes around it died — actually died, going grey and brittle, the warmth sucked out of the ground in a handspan. Fae-forged. Sorrel's century of memory put to work.

"Sorrel!" I bellowed it across the basin. "You want me? Here I am!"

The hymn stopped.

The silence was worse than the sound. In it I could hear everything — the mourners singing alone now, hundreds of fragile voices; the heartstone's pulse, quick and scared; Elias breathing beside me; and my own name, spoken in a voice I hadn't heard since the order fell.

"Marguerite Underhill." Sorrel walked out of her circle toward me, unhurried, book tucked under her arm like a shepherd's crook. Up close she looked tired. That was the horror of her — not cruelty but exhaustion, a woman carrying a hundred years of remembering and calling the weight holiness. "Do you know what I remember about you? You weren't even born when I last stood where you're standing. But I remember your making. I was there. We bound you into that blade before you could walk, and the order called it holy, and then the order died, and you were left holding a covenant no one else even knew existed." She shook her head slowly. "They wasted you, child. I won't. Give me the stone's consent and I will make sure no one is ever *made* for anything again."

"You'd seal the fae behind a dying door," I said. "Vess told me what your mercy costs. Their realms starve. Their children fade. You'd bury them alive and call it peace."

"I'd call it an ending," Sorrel said gently, "and endings are the only honest mercy left. Come. Stand aside. You've been standing between worlds your whole life — isn't it time someone let you rest?"

And gods help me, some part of me wanted to. That was her real weapon, the one she'd nearly killed me with on the ice — not iron, not numbers, but the truth-shaped temptation of surrender. Rest. Oblivion. No more being the hinge, the seal, the last of anything.

Then the mourners' song broke through my doubt like dawn through fog — not louder, just *clearer* — and in it I heard Vess's voice, thin and wounded and utterly unbroken, holding a line of melody the way a hand holds a rope over a drop.

No. Nobody was resting tonight.

"No," I said, and raised the sword, and spoke the second oath-word, the one I'd sworn never to use against human beings: "*Yield.*"

The word hit the basin like the mountain exhaling. Every anchor in the Choir's ring flickered. Zealots staggered back a full step. And the heartstone's hum climbed — up through my boots, my bones, my teeth — into a note so pure and furious that snow leapt off the ground all around us, and for one suspended moment the whole basin hung in ringing silence, every soul in it waiting to see which song the mountain would choose.

Sorrel smiled, sad and steady, and opened her book again.

"It doesn't choose, child. It listens. So we'll give it something worth hearing." She lifted her voice, and the Choir breathed in as one body, and the hymn rose again — deeper now, darker, aimed.

At me.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to charge. I remember the space between heartbeats, the book in Sorrel's hands, the ring of anchors blazing brighter with every bar of the hymn, and the knowledge landing in me like a stone dropped down a well: *we are out of time.* If the ring completed, if the seal took hold, then Vess and every mourner on this mountain would spend forever on the wrong side of a locked door, fading in the dark while the human world forgot they'd ever sung.

"Elias!" I shouted, and he was already moving — toward the ring's weakest point, the cracked anchor in the southeast, hammer-swinging zealots around it. He couldn't break it. I knew he couldn't. But he could buy seconds, and seconds were all I needed to reach Sorrel and end this the old way, steel to steel, oath-word against sermon—

The first arrow took Vess through the chest.

She was thirty yards behind me, singing. I heard her stop. The whole mourner-host faltered, a thousand candles guttering at once, and in that gap the Choir's hymn surged forward like floodwater through a broken dam, and the anchors blazed, and the heartstone screamed — actually screamed, a note of pure grief that dropped half the zealots to their knees and drove the rest of us to ours.

I turned. Vess lay in the snow with the shaft standing in her, her light pouring out around the wound like water from a split jug, and her eyes found mine across the chaos, and she smiled.

"Sing louder," she whispered. I don't know how I heard it. The mountain carried it to me. "Underhill woman. Finish it."

The heartstone's pulse went wild beneath my feet. The mourners gathered around their fallen sister and their song changed — no longer a counter-melody but a summons, a naming, and every note of it landed on me like a hand on my shoulder.

The last sword. The living seal. Standing between.

Sorrel was walking toward me through the blaze of her own ritual, book open, face calm, certain as winter.

I got up.

I went to meet her.
