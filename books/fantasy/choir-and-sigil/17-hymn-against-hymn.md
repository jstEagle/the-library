## 17. Hymn Against Hymn

**Marguerite**

The heartstone sang before she saw it.

She had heard it once before — a hundred years ago, or in a dream that felt like a hundred years ago — a note like a bell struck under water, rising through the soles of her boots and up through her ribs until it lived behind her teeth. Now the basin opened below them, a bowl of white stone ringed with pillars of ice, and at its center the heartstone stood taller than a man: black glass shot through with veins of light, humming, waiting.

And around it stood the Hollow Choir.

Two hundred of them, maybe more, robed in grey-white, arranged in concentric rings on the frozen basin floor. Their voices rose in unison — not words, not yet, just sound, a sustained chord that pressed against the fae song coming down from the ridgelines like two hands pushing against the same door. Where their hymn touched the snow, the snow stopped falling. It hung in the air, undecided.

"They started without us," Elias said beside her. His voice was rough. The wound in his side had stopped bleeding an hour ago, which she had thought was mercy; now she understood it was worse than bleeding. The Choir's counter-ritual had marked him when he broke their circle, and the mark did not close.

"Then we're late to our own funeral," Marguerite said. She drew her sword.

The blade came out of its scabbard with a sound like a word being remembered. Along the ridge above them, the fae mourners answered — Vess's voice first, thin and clear as a needle, then dozens joining, then hundreds, a song that had no beginning because it had never been allowed to end. The covenant's fragments, sung against the silence.

Sorrel turned.

Even across the basin, even through the blur of falling snow, Marguerite felt the Prelate's attention land on her like a hand on her shoulder. Sorrel raised one arm, and the hymn changed — resolved into language, into the old liturgy of sealing, into *close the door, close the door, close the door* — and the rings of zealots began to move.

"Stay behind me," Marguerite said.

"No," Elias said. "Behind you is where I break things."

* * *

**Elias**

He had read about battles. He had even, in another life, sat in a warm seminar room and analyzed accounts of them, tracing supply lines and casualty figures with a dry pen. Nothing in any account had prepared him for the sound.

The Choir did not scream. That was the horror of it. They advanced singing, ranks closing over their fallen without breaking stride, their hymn swallowing the space where pain should have been. And the fae met them with grief instead of fury — mourners who wept as they fought, whose song bent the snow into spears and the cold into walls, and who died making sounds like lullabies.

Elias ran down the slope with Marguerite ahead of him, her sword speaking.

That was the only word for it. Each time she swung, she spoke — short syllables in a language he didn't know but his bones recognized — and each oath-word did what steel alone could not. One word split the ice beneath six Choir singers and dropped them into black water. Another wrapped her blade in light that cut through a hymn-shield like it was paper. The mountain answered her. He could feel it answering him too, faintly, secondhand, the way you feel heat from a fire you aren't touching.

A singer broke from the ranks toward him — young, barely older than a student, eyes wide and terrified inside the grey hood. The boy raised a ritual knife and began a sealing-word.

Elias hit him with his shoulder and they went down together into the snow. The knife skittered away. The boy's mouth kept moving, shaping the syllables, and Elias pressed his palm flat over it and held him there, feeling the words die against his skin like moths.

"I'm sorry," Elias said, and meant it, and moved on, because the circle was ahead.

The Choir's ritual ring stood at the basin's center, fifty paces from the heartstone: a circle of white salt and black iron, anchored by twelve standing staves, each stave crowned with a shard of mirror-glass. Inside it, thirty singers knelt, holding the counter-ritual open — the wound in the world that would let Sorrel seal every rift forever. The hymn poured out of them in a column of visible sound, bending toward the stone.

And between Elias and the circle stood Prelate Sorrel, and she was not looking at him.

She was looking at Marguerite.

* * *

**Marguerite**

They met in the center of the battle, where the fae song and the Choir hymn ground against each other and the air itself shuddered.

Sorrel fought like someone who remembered everything. Her blade was swordspeech-forged — Marguerite knew the make, knew the order's marks along the fuller, knew this sword had hung in the armory at Cael Harrow while she was still a girl sweeping its floors. Every strike carried an oath-word behind it, and the words were old ones, correct ones, pronounced with a precision Marguerite had lost somewhere in ten years of smuggling and hiding.

*Yield,* Sorrel signed between strikes — the order's battle-cant, hands moving fast. *You are outnumbered, out-sung, and out-practiced.*

*I've been out-practiced before,* Marguerite signed back, and drove her forward.

Steel rang. Snow sprayed. Around them the battle churned — a mourner went down screaming a name no one alive remembered, a Choir singer fell silent mid-hymn and his neighbors closed the gap without looking. Marguerite gave ground, took ground, gave it again. Sorrel was stronger. Sorrel was fresher. Sorrel had spent a century keeping her edge sharp while Marguerite had spent hers trying to forget she'd ever owned one.

But that wasn't how Sorrel won. Marguerite realized it three exchanges too late.

"You were eleven," Sorrel said aloud, conversational, circling. Her voice carried impossibly well, threaded through with cant-signs that made it skip across the noise. "When they bound you. Do you remember the binding? Of course you don't. We took that from you deliberately. A seal works best when it doesn't know it's a seal."

"Liar."

"Am I?" Sorrel's blade flicked out and opened a line of red along Marguerite's forearm — shallow, deliberate, a teacher's correction. "Ask yourself, Underhill. Why you? A hundred swordspeakers survived the breaking. Why bind the youngest, the least trained, the one nobody would miss? Because the elders chose you at random?" She smiled, and it was a kind smile, which was the cruelest thing about it. "Or because you were *made* for it? Grown in the order's crèches. Bred to the covenant like a hound to a line. Did you ever wonder why you can't remember your mother?"

The doubt went in like a knife. Marguerite felt it slide past her guard — not toward her body, toward the thing underneath, the question she had carried since the waystation chapel, since Vess's testimony, since the firelight confession she still couldn't believe she'd made. *I am not a door.* She had screamed it at Elias on the mountain road. She had almost believed it.

Almost.

Her guard dropped half an inch. Sorrel's blade came through the gap and Marguerite caught it on her crossguard by reflex alone, arms shaking, boots sliding on bloodied ice. Sorrel leaned in, all her weight behind the press, her face inches away, gentle as a confessor.

"You want to survive," Sorrel murmured. "That's all you've ever wanted. Ten years of running is wanting to survive. So survive. Walk away. Let us seal the doors. The fae will fade slowly, gently, over centuries — no children stolen, no memories eaten, no villages forgetting their names. You could grow old, Marguerite. You could have a life. All you have to do is stop believing you matter."

And the terrible thing — the thing Marguerite would carry into whatever came next — was that for one full breath, she wavered. The hymn was loud. The snow was soft. Survival was a warm room with her name on the door.

Then she heard Elias.

Not his voice — his footsteps, running, past her, beyond her, toward the circle, toward the thing he had already decided to do while she stood here losing an argument with her own ghost. He hadn't waited. He hadn't argued. He had simply gone, the way he'd gone into the rift, the way he'd broken their circle once already, the way he did everything: badly, bravely, immediately.

"He doesn't hesitate," Marguerite said, and the words came out in swordspeech, in oath-tongue, and the mountain heard them.

She twisted off Sorrel's blade and struck — not at the Prelate's body but at her certainty, one clean oath-word, *remember*, and the word hit Sorrel like a wave hitting a seawall. The older woman staggered. For a heartbeat her composure cracked and Marguerite saw what was underneath: not conviction. Terror. A woman who remembered everything the world forgot, and had built a church over the remembering so she wouldn't have to live inside it.

"You remember the covenant," Marguerite said. "You remember what it was. And you'd kill the world to avoid paying your share of it."

"I paid my share!" Sorrel's voice broke, actually broke, and the hymn around them stuttered. "A century! I have held the seal alone for a *century*, child, while you slept safe in your ignorance—"

"That wasn't payment," Marguerite said. "That was hoarding."

She struck again, and this time Sorrel caught the blow, and they locked together, blade to blade, breath to breath — and neither of them saw what Elias was doing.

* * *

**Elias**

Twelve staves. Twelve shards of mirror-glass. Thirty kneeling singers holding the circle open with their voices.

He counted them the way he used to count footnotes — methodically, desperately — and understood, with the cold clarity of a man past the point of good options, that he could not fight through thirty singers. He could not out-sing a hymn. He could not out-pray a prayer.

But he had broken their circle once before, in a waystation cellar, with nothing but his blood and his stubbornness. And he knew now what the Choir's magic actually was: it was an argument. A sustained, unanimous claim that the door between worlds should be shut. And arguments, he knew better than anyone alive, had premises. And premises could be contradicted.

He walked into the circle.

The hymn hit him like a physical wall — pressure, heat, a chorus of voices insisting with total sincerity that he did not exist, that the rift was closed, that he had never fallen through any snow. He kept walking. His boots left prints in the salt. Somewhere behind him Marguerite shouted his name, and the hymn ate the sound.

He reached the center. The nearest stave stood before him, its mirror-shard showing him a reflection that wasn't quite his — a man unchanged, unwounded, standing in a spring field somewhere far from any mountain. *Come home,* the reflection suggested. *None of this ever happened.*

"No," Elias said. "This happened. I'm choosing it."

He pulled the shard free.

The feedback was immediate and total. The hymn lurched, the circle's geometry shrieked, and something in the structure of the counter-ritual reached for him the way a severed wire reaches for ground. It found his blood. Of course it found his blood — he was the contradiction, the man out of season, the living proof of an open door, and the ritual wanted him erased.

The wound in his side tore open.

Not bled — *tore*, a seam widening, and through it he felt the cold of the long winter pouring in and out of him both directions at once, and he understood distantly that this was the cost, this was the terrible cost, and he did it anyway. He ripped the second shard free. Then the third. The kneeling singers' voices cracked and scattered. The circle's light guttered like a lamp in wind.

Four shards. Five. His hands were shaking so hard he could barely grip the glass, and the glass bit him, and his blood ran down the white salt and the salt drank it greedily, and everywhere his blood touched, the circle *failed*. Six. Seven. The hymn collapsed into dissonance, into panic, into individual human voices crying out — and for the first time the Hollow Choir sounded like what it was: frightened people in robes, holding hands around a hole in the world.

Eight. Nine. Ten.

On the eleventh stave his legs gave out. He went down among the salt and the iron and the shattered mirrors, and the last shard stood above him, intact, and the circle held itself together around that single anchor like a lung holding its final breath.

"ELIAS!"

Marguerite. Running. Sorrel forgotten, the duel abandoned, the whole battle narrowing to a woman sprinting across a killing floor toward him.

He smiled. He got his hand around the last shard.

"For the record," he said, to her, to the mountain, to whatever was writing him into its book, "I was walking home."

He pulled.

* * *

**Marguerite**

The circle died with a sound like a bell breaking.

The shockwave took the Choir off their feet. It took the fae mourners off theirs. It took Marguerite off hers, twenty feet from the circle's edge, and flung her down into the snow, and when she got her knees under herself and looked up, the basin had changed.

The hymn was gone. In its place: silence, vast and ringing, and through the silence the heartstone's own note rose unopposed for the first time in a hundred years — deep, patient, unmistakably *alive*. The veins of light in the black glass brightened. The hanging snow, undecided for hours, began to fall again — downward, gently, like snow and not like a verdict.

Around the basin, the Hollow Choir knelt in the wreckage of their ritual, and no one sang. Some of them wept. Some of them simply stared at their empty hands.

At the circle's center, Elias lay on his back in the ruined salt, and the wound in his side was open, and it was not closing, and the edges of it glowed faintly with the same light as the heartstone's veins, as if the winter had signed its work.

Marguerite reached him first. She didn't remember crossing the distance.

"Hey." She pressed her hands to the wound and the light pushed back against her palms, cool and utterly indifferent. "Hey. Look at me. You absolute idiot. You beautiful idiot."

"Did it work?" His voice was thin. His eyes found hers anyway, and they were clear, and they were amused, which was somehow the worst part. "The circle. Is it—"

"It's dead. You killed it. You killed it with *footnotes*." She was laughing or crying or both, and her hands were full of light that wouldn't let her stop the bleeding. Behind her, she heard Sorrel rise slowly to her feet, heard the Prelate's breath catch as she looked at the broken circle, at the brightening stone, at the fae song swelling tentatively along the ridges — heard the exact moment a century of certainty failed to reassemble itself.

But that was a problem for the next breath. This breath belonged to the man in the snow.

"Marguerite." Elias caught her wrist. His grip was still strong. She held onto that. "The ritual's open now, isn't it. The covenant. It'll come for its keeper."

"Don't talk."

"It'll ask you to forget me." His thumb moved against her pulse, once, steady. "I need you to promise me something first. Before it asks."

The heartstone's note deepened. The light in the wound pulsed. And Marguerite felt it begin — the pull, ancient and patient, the bargain waking beneath the mountain and turning toward the one thing in the world forged to answer it.

"What promise?" she whispered.

His eyes held hers, and he smiled like a man about to commit one final act of magnificent stupidity.

"Whatever it takes from you," Elias said, "don't let it take *you*."
