## 18. The Price Named Aloud

The heartstone sang.

Not a sound she heard with her ears — it came up through her boots, through the bones of her shins, through the marrow that had once been taught to listen. The basin around it was a bowl of white stone the size of a village square, ringed with pillars that had been whole when the swordspeakers last stood here and were now broken at heights like snapped teeth. Snow fell upward in places. In others it simply stopped mid-air, hanging like a held breath.

And across the basin, the Hollow Choir was singing.

Two hundred voices, maybe more, arranged in crescents around the counter-ritual's circle — a ring of ash-white chalk and something darker that steamed where it touched the stone. Their hymn climbed in slow, patient intervals, each note laying another brick in a wall. Marguerite could feel the wall going up inside her own chest, a pressure like drowning from the inside out. Sealing. Closing. *No more stolen children,* Sorrel had promised her people. *No more eaten memories.* A mercy, sung in two hundred parts.

Vess lay against a broken pillar twenty feet away, one wing bent at an angle wings should not bend, frost blooming across skin that was supposed to be winter itself. The fae mourners had come down from the high passes in their dozens — gaunt, luminous, ragged as old flags — and they had begun to sing against the Choir, fragments of the covenant threaded through grief. Where the two songs met above the heartstone, the air tore into small bright wounds.

Elias was bleeding.

He'd broken the ritual circle ten minutes ago — driven his knife through the chalk line and the darker thing beneath it, and the Choir's working had screamed and stuttered and taken its price out of him along the forearm he'd used. The wound would not close. She'd bound it twice; the bandages soaked through both times, red spreading like ink through water. He'd gone grey-lipped and stubborn and refused to sit down.

"Marguerite." His voice was rough. "It's open. Whatever has to happen — it can happen now."

She looked at the heartstone.

It rose from the center of the basin like a bell the size of a chapel, veined with light that pulsed in time with no heart she knew. And now that the Choir's circle was broken, now that the way stood clear, she could hear what it had been trying to tell her since they first set foot on this road. Not words. Worse than words. A shape. A requirement. Something with her name already written on it, the way the Canticle had written her name a century before she was born.

*The seal must be living.*

She had known. Gods help her, some part of her had known since Vess pressed her brow against the flat of Marguerite's sword and wept over it — since the word *keeper* fell into the firelight and Elias went quiet in a way men only go quiet when they understand something terrible. She had known, and she had spent six days not saying it, because things said aloud stop being deniable.

The heartstone pulsed again, and this time it found her oath-name — the one she hadn't spoken in years, the one the village had almost eaten out of her — and spoke it back to her in a voice like ice settling on a lake.

The bill had come due.

* * *

"Say it," Elias said. "Whatever you're hearing. Say it out loud."

Because he'd learned her by now. Six days of frozen roads and shared fires and one night of confession by a dying flame, and he had learned that Marguerite Underhill went silent exactly when the truth got too heavy to lift alone.

"The covenant needs a keeper." Her voice came out flat. Smuggler-flat, ledger-flat, the voice she used for prices. "Not a ritual. Not a sacrifice. A keeper. Someone bound to the stone who holds the terms in themselves — who *is* the contract, alive, so it can't be broken the way it was broken before. Because paper burns." She looked at her own hands. "People don't."

"You."

"Me. I was made for it. You know that too."

"That's what Sorrel told you to make you run—"

"Sorrel never told me anything I didn't already suspect." She turned to face him fully. His blood was dripping off his fingertips, slow, patient, marking the snow in a dotted line. "The order forged me as the seal, Elias. Bound me before I could walk. Then they broke the covenant anyway and ran, and left me standing there like a lock on a door nobody lived behind anymore. That's my whole history. That's the shame I confessed to you by the fire. So yes. Me. It was always going to be me. The only question anyone ever got to answer was whether I'd show up."

"And what does showing up cost?"

There it was. He asked it gently, which made it worse.

"I hold the realms together through the winter. Through every winter after, until the covenant is rewritten or the world ends, whichever comes first." She made herself keep looking at him. "And a keeper can't hold what she doesn't remember. The binding takes memory. All of it that isn't needed for the holding. My order. My roads. My—" Her throat closed. She forced it open. "You."

The wind moved through the basin. Somewhere above them the fae song bent around a note of mourning so pure it hurt.

"No," Elias said.

"Elias—"

"No. There's another way. There's always another way, we just haven't—"

"There isn't." She said it softly, and that was the cruelest thing she could have done, because softness gave it weight. "I've spent three days listening to this stone while you slept. It doesn't bargain. It's not cruel — it's just *exact*. That's what broke everything last time. The swordspeakers wanted the covenant's protection without the covenant's price, so they cut the price out and called it reform, and the whole bargain came apart like wet rope." She smiled, and hated how thin it felt on her face. "I'm not going to be the second fool who tries to cheat an honest bill."

"So you'll pay it by erasing yourself? By walking into that stone and coming out a door instead of a woman?" His voice cracked on the last word. Blood dripped. "You told me — on the river, you told me you weren't a door. You screamed it."

"I'm not. That's the point." She stepped closer. "A door gets used. A keeper *chooses*. Every day, forever, the choosing is mine — the stone just holds the terms. But the terms require a mind clean enough to hold them. No grudges. No griefs. Nothing the long winter could get its teeth into." She reached out and took his unwounded hand, and his fingers closed around hers like he was holding onto a ledge. "Nothing that loves anything it might lose."

"Then it can't have you."

"It already had me before I ever met you." She squeezed his hand. "You just made the price visible. That's all you did, Elias. You walked out of a blizzard carrying my name on a scrap of parchment, and you made the bill legible. I'd spent thirty years not reading it."

* * *

Across the basin, the hymn faltered.

Marguerite felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the Choir's cadence, a hesitation rippling outward from the crescent's center like a crack through ice. Prelate Sorrel stood at the hymn's heart, arms raised, silver-haired, her voice the anchor-note that two hundred others hung from. And her voice had stopped.

Sorrel was looking at the heartstone. At Marguerite. At the space between them where the covenant's demand hung visible now to anyone with the ears to hear it — and Sorrel had been a swordspeaker once, before the order burned its books and buried its oaths. Sorrel remembered everything the world forgot.

Marguerite watched the understanding land on the older woman's face like a landslide.

She watched Sorrel lower her arms.

"Hold the hymn," the prelate said, and her voice carried without effort, worn smooth by decades of command. "Hold it. Do not advance the working." Then she walked out of the crescent, alone, across the ash-chalked stone, toward the heartstone and the two people standing beside it.

The Choir held. Two hundred voices sustained their chord, uneasy, wavering, but held. The fae mourners sang on, quieter now, watching.

Sorrel stopped ten paces away. Up close, without the hymn behind her, she looked less like a pillar of the faith and more like what she was: a woman in her sixties with frost-scars on her knuckles and the posture of someone who had once carried a sword the way Marguerite carried hers.

"You heard it," Marguerite said.

"I wrote it down forty years ago, girl. In a book the order burned." Sorrel's eyes went to the heartstone, and something in her face — something old and exhausted and terribly human — flickered there. "The covenant demands its keeper. Living seal. Memory forfeit. Yes. I heard it the first time I stood in this basin, and I ran, and I built a church on top of my running so I'd never have to look at it again."

"Then you know sealing the rifts kills the fae."

"I know sealing the rifts stops the eating." For a moment the zealot came back into her voice, iron under wool. "Do you know what I've seen, Underhill? Villages that forgot their names. Mothers who smiled at children they couldn't recognize. The covenant didn't just break — it *leaked*, for a hundred years, and everything it leaked out of the world was memory. I have knelt beside people who no longer knew they were people. Don't speak to me of costs. I have paid them in other people's faces."

"And your answer was to wall the wound shut and let half of creation die slowly behind the wall."

"My answer was *mercy*—" Sorrel caught herself. Breathed. When she spoke again it was quieter, and that was worse. "My answer was the only one I could live with. Seal the realms. End the leaking. Let the fae fade in their own country instead of hollowing ours. Grieve once, cleanly, instead of bleeding forever." Her gaze shifted to Elias, to the blood spotting the snow beneath him, and then back. "But that was when I believed the seal could be chalk and hymn. A working. Something done *to* the world by the faithful."

She looked at the heartstone a long moment.

"It wants a person," Sorrel said. "It always wanted a person. We knew that a century ago, and we forged one, and then we couldn't bear it, so we broke everything rather than let her be used." Her voice dropped. "And now it asks again, and I find that I—" She stopped. Started again. "I have preached sealing as salvation for thirty years. I believed it. I still believe the leaking must end. But I did not become a prelate to feed one woman into a stone and call it peace."

"Then stand aside," Marguerite said.

"No." Sorrel's jaw tightened. "Stand aside yourself. There is another term. There was always another term — the Canticle names it, the old liturgies name it, and I burned those pages myself so I would never have to say it aloud." Her eyes went to Elias. "The covenant was two keepers. A voice and a seal. The fae voice is dying on that pillar, but the seal need not stand alone. A second may enter the circle and share the burden — anchor the keeper's memories against his own, so that what is surrendered is the past and not the self. It is forbidden. It was forbidden because the last time anyone considered it, the order feared two people bound to one stone would love each other more than the world."

Silence. Even the hymns seemed to lean in.

"But it can be done," Sorrel said. "And if it is done, the leaking ends *and* the fae keep their country *and* no one is erased. Everything I wanted. Everything you want. Paid for properly, split between two." She spread her hands, and for the first time since the waystation, since the sermon, since the frozen river — she looked tired. Simply, enormously tired. "So I am telling you the forbidden term, girl, because I have spent my whole life confusing cruelty with honesty, and I will not do it at the end. Tell him what it costs him. Or I will."

Marguerite turned to Elias.

He was already looking at the heartstone. Already pale, already bleeding, already wearing the expression she'd learned on the frozen river — the one that meant his mind had run ahead of his mouth and was waiting for the rest of him to catch up.

"Don't," she said.

"Tell me anyway."

"If a second enters the circle, the covenant takes from him too. Not memories — *anchor-weight*. Everything he is gets poured into holding what I surrender. His past. His home. The man he was before the blizzard." Her voice frayed. "You'd lose your spring, Elias. Your life. The whole world you fell out of. Permanently. You'd be bound here, to this stone, to this winter, to *me*, until the covenant is rewritten or the world ends. That's the cost. That's the honest bill, read aloud, as required."

The wind moved. Above them, Vess lifted her head from the broken pillar, one eye finding them, and began — very softly, very badly, through cracked lips — to sing a fragment none of the mourners had sung yet. A new verse. With room in it for two.

Elias looked at the stone. At her. At his own blood on the snow.

"Okay," he said.

"*No.*"

"Marguerite—"

"You don't get to decide that in a minute! You're wounded, you're half-frozen, you haven't even—"

"I decided on the river." He stepped forward, and his hand found hers again, steady despite everything. "When I chose your side with the ice breaking under us. Everything since then has just been logistics." He managed something that was almost a smile. "You told me once you weren't a door. Fine. Then don't be a lock either. Be a keeper — with a partner. That's not cheating the bill. That's splitting it."

Behind him, Prelate Sorrel closed her eyes. When she opened them, she raised her voice to the basin, to the Choir, to the mountain itself:

"Hold the hymn no longer. Sing it *down*."

And two hundred voices faltered, wavered — and obeyed.

The counter-ritual's chord collapsed into silence, and into that silence the heartstone rang once, vast and clear, like a struck bell finally allowed to finish speaking. The light in its veins turned toward them. Toward her. The covenant, patient as winter, waited to hear its terms accepted.

Marguerite drew a breath that shook all the way down.

"Elias," she said. "If we do this — when we do this — I won't remember you. Do you understand? Whatever you anchor, whatever survives — *I* wake up not knowing your name. Not knowing why my chest hurts when I look at you. Not knowing anything except that a stranger is holding my hand and crying."

"Then I'll introduce myself," he said. "I've had practice. I'm good at falling into strange countries and starting over."

She laughed — one broken, disbelieving note — and it was almost a sob, and it was the truest sound she'd made in thirty years.

She took his hand. She took her sword from her back and drove it point-down into the snow before the heartstone, the way the old liturgy demanded, the way Vess had wept to see it done. The blade hummed against the stone's ringing until the two sounds became one.

"Together, then," Marguerite said. "On three."

She began to speak the binding.
