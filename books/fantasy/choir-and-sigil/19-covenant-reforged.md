## 19. Covenant Reforged

The circle was already drawn when I understood there was no version of this where she walked out whole.

Marguerite stood at the edge of the heartstone basin with her sword planted point-down in the snow, both hands on the pommel the way a pilgrim grips a staff, and she was reciting words I had only heard fragments of — in the Canticle, in Vess's mourning-song, in the half-burned pages of her own copy that she'd fed to a chapel fire a lifetime ago, which was nine days ago, which was forever ago. The words came out of her in the old swordspeech, the language the mountain answered when she spoke her first oath-word against the Choir, and the stone beneath our feet hummed like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

The binding required three things. A keeper willing. A name spoken into the stone. And every memory the keeper carried, surrendered one by one as the covenant drank them down and used them as mortar.

I knew all three requirements because Vess had told us. Vess had told us in the fae's way, which is to say sadly, thoroughly, and without any useful suggestion for how to get around it. *The seal must be living*, they had sung in the hollow under the frost-killed pines. *The seal must be remembering, so that the stone knows what it holds. The seal must forget, so that the stone holds it instead.*

Marguerite had gone very quiet when Vess finished. Then she'd said, "Well. At least it isn't my sword arm," in the voice she used when she was bleeding somewhere we couldn't see.

Now the Choir's counter-ritual lay broken around the basin — Sorrel's circle shattered by Elias Vane, who was apparently the kind of man who broke ritual circles with his bare hands and a stolen ceremonial dagger and only thought about the cost afterward. The cost was a wound in my side that had stopped pretending it intended to close. Blood came out of me slow and steady, dark on the snow, and every breath had a wet sound in it that I tried not to listen to.

Sorrel stood at the basin's far rim with her hymn dead in her throat. She had seen the true price. That was the strange thing about her — Prelate Sorrel, who remembered everything the world forgot, who had been a swordspeaker before she was a zealot — when she understood what the covenant actually demanded, something in her face had come apart. Not defeat. Recognition. She had spent forty years arguing that sealing the realms was mercy, and here was the mercy's invoice, and even she hadn't read the fine print until tonight.

But none of that mattered right now, because Marguerite Underhill was speaking her own name into the heartstone, and the stone was drinking.

I watched it happen. You could see it happening. She spoke *Underhill* and then she faltered on the word after it — a word about her mother, about a kitchen somewhere, about bread — and the word went out of her face like a candle going out in one room of a large house. She kept speaking. Her jaw set the way it did on the frozen river when she told me to run and I didn't. Memory by memory, the winter took them, and she fed them to it on purpose, because two realms were starving and she had been made — forged, built, shaped before her own birth — to be the meal.

"No," I said. Out loud. To no one who could help.

Vess was down at the basin's edge among the fae mourners, wounded, one wing bent at an angle that hurt to look at, singing the counter-hymn that held the Choir's sealing-verse at bay while Marguerite worked. The mourners' song filled the basin like water fills a cup — grief, but grief with a spine in it. It wasn't enough. Nothing was enough. The covenant wanted what it wanted, and it had been waiting a hundred years, and patience is the cruelest thing about winters.

Marguerite reached the memories of the order. I saw her hit them — the betrayal, the oath-breakers, the night they bound her as the seal and walked away — and she hesitated there, and for one moment I thought, hoped, feared, that the pain of those memories would make her refuse. That she'd spit out the binding and choose herself.

She didn't hesitate because it hurt. She hesitated the way you hesitate before setting down something heavy you've carried so long your shoulders don't know their own shape.

Then it was gone, and she swayed on her feet, and her eyes when she lifted them were still hers but the country behind them was emptying, province by province.

And here is the thing I need you to understand, whoever ends up reading this or remembering this or being this: I did not decide. Deciding implies an alternative was weighed. There was no alternative. There was a woman dissolving herself into a stone so that spring could continue to exist as a concept, and there was me, and between us there was exactly one forbidden act, and I had known it was forbidden since the hour Vess told us, which meant I had been carrying it in my pocket for two days like a coin I was ashamed of wanting to spend.

*Two keepers*, the oldest verse said. *A fae voice and a human blade.* It never said one keeper alone. It never said the seal must stand unaccompanied. The Choir had read it as one keeper because one was cheaper and lonelier and easier to abandon, and everyone who had ever handled the Canticle had preferred the version where someone else suffered singly.

I stepped over the broken line of the Choir's circle.

The moment my boot crossed it, everything got loud. Not sound-loud. True-loud. The heartstone's hum climbed into my teeth and my wound flared white and Vess's song broke off mid-note with a sound like a harp string snapping, and every fae mourner in the basin turned toward me with an expression I will take to my grave: horror and hope wearing the same face.

"Elias." Marguerite's voice. Frayed, already thinning at the edges, the way cloth thins before it tears. "Elias, what are you doing. Get out. Get *out*, it takes everything—"

"I know what it takes." My legs carried me across the snow, which was easier than it should have been, as if the basin had decided to let me in, as if the stone had been waiting a century for someone stupid enough and fond enough to try. "That's why I'm walking."

"You'll lose—" she started, and stopped, because we both knew the inventory. Her memories. My memories. The pass where we met, the rift, the blizzard. The waystation chapel where she burned her copy of the Canticle and I memorized mine. The village with no name. The river ice groaning under our boots while the winter chased us like a tide. Firelight on the night she confessed what the order had done, and how her voice cracked on the word *abandoned*, and how I had said, without thinking, *not anymore*, and how she hadn't answered, and how neither of us had slept much after that.

All of it. Both of us. The whole short history of us, poured into a rock.

"Yes," I said. "Probably."

"It doesn't split the burden, Elias, it *adds* it—"

"Vess," I called, and my voice cracked too, and I didn't care anymore about what cracks showed. "The verse. Say the verse."

Vess rose, bad wing and all, and their voice came out of them in the old tongue and then again in ours, and the mourners took it up, and the basin rang:

*What is given alone is lost alone.*
*What is given together is held together.*
*A seal with two hands does not drop what it carries.*

"That's apocryphal!" Sorrel shouted from the rim, and her voice broke in the middle of the word, and I have thought about that since — that the woman who remembered everything didn't know this verse, because nobody had ever been brave enough or foolish enough to make it true before. "It's never been done—"

"Great," I said. "First time for everything."

Marguerite caught my arm. Her grip was still strong; whatever else the stone had taken, it hadn't gotten to her sword-arm yet, and I laughed — actually laughed — at that, at her, at the absurd order of her priorities, and something in her face wavered between fury and the other thing, the thing we had been not saying since the river.

"The binding needs a keeper who remembers," she said, low, urgent, the smuggler's voice, the negotiating voice, the voice that talked contraband past checkpoints in three duchies. "If you give yours too, who holds ours? Who are we after? We'll be two strangers standing in a bowl."

"Strangers can introduce themselves," I said. "You taught me the roads, remember. You start with what's nearest. You work outward." I put my hand over hers on my arm. "And the stone remembers. That's the whole point, Marguerite. That's the bargain. We don't lose it. We just stop being the ones who carry it. Someone else keeps it safe for us."

"Someone else is a rock."

"A very reliable rock."

Her eyes were wet and furious and I loved her so much in that moment that it seemed obvious, suddenly, geometrically obvious, that this was what the feeling was *for*. Not for poems. For this. For standing in the mouth of a price and paying it together so that neither of us paid alone.

"If you do this," she whispered, "I won't know you."

"You'll know me again," I said. "You're good at learning things fast. Roads, languages, people. Best smuggler in Thornfell. Everyone says so."

"Don't," she said, and her voice finally broke all the way through. "Don't make me laugh while I'm dying, Elias."

"You're not dying. Neither of us is dying. We're just"—and I searched for it, and found it, and it was true, it was the truest sentence I have ever spoken and I include everything in this account—"we're just putting our lives somewhere safer than our heads."

The winter chose that moment to press down. Above the basin the long winter hung like a held breath, the storm-front that had been chasing us for days finally arrived in full, snow falling so thick the fae's lights dimmed inside it, and the heartstone's hum climbed to a pitch I felt in my back teeth, impatient, a hundred years hungry. Somewhere beyond the storm the rifts were closing one by one, and both worlds were running out of the memory of each other, and a village full of smiling hollow people stood as evidence of what happened to those the forgetting reached first.

There was no more time for the negotiation. There was only the choice.

"Say it with me," Marguerite said.

It wasn't surrender. I want that recorded clearly, wherever this ends up recorded. It was command. She seized my hand hard enough to grind the small bones, she pulled me to the stone's edge beside her, and she began the binding again from the top — the old words, the swordspeech rolling out of her with the weight of every oath she'd ever kept and every one that had been kept against her — and when she reached the place where a single keeper speaks alone, she left a space.

A gap in the liturgy. Room for one more voice.

I stepped into it.

The words weren't mine and then they were. They came up out of some part of me that had been waiting since a man walked home through a spring evening and fell out of the world — and maybe that's what the rift was, maybe it was never an accident, maybe the covenant reaches across centuries to collect what it's owed, but I felt the syllables fit my mouth like they'd been measured for it, and beside me Marguerite made a small sound, shock or relief or recognition, and we spoke together.

The stone took my hand first.

Not the flesh — the flesh stayed, cold and aching and mine. It took what the hand had held. The scrap of parchment with her name on it, gone. The blizzard, gone, though I stood in snow. The chapel, the fire, the ash of her book rising past my face. Each memory left not like tearing but like tide going out — smooth, total, taking the shape of the shore with it.

I held on to one thing with everything I had, deliberately, viciously, the way she'd taught me to hold a rope on ice: *her*. Not the memories of her. The fact of her. The way you keep your footing not by staring at your boots but by fixing on a point ahead. I fixed on the woman beside me whose hand was crushing mine, and I let the tide take everything else, and I felt it take hers too — I felt her lose the roads, lose the order, lose the betrayal, lose me, felt her grip shift on my fingers from *I know you* to *who are you* and I did not let go, that was the whole of my religion in that moment, I did not let go—

The covenant closed around us like a bell being struck.

Sound went through the basin, through the mountain, through the ground under both worlds. Every fae mourner sang at once and the song wasn't grief anymore; it was the Canticle, the real one, the whole one, verses no one alive had heard because they hadn't existed until the stone had two hands to hold them. The Choir's broken circle guttered out entirely. Sorrel dropped to her knees at the rim with her hands pressed flat against the snow, and whether she was praying or listening I couldn't tell, and it didn't matter, because above us—

Above us, the long winter stopped falling.

Not melted. Not defeated. *Stopped* — the way a clock stops, mid-tick, the storm hanging in the air like a decision postponed. Snowflakes hung suspended around the basin, thousands of them, motionless, catching the fae light like a ceiling of stars. And through the gaps in that held sky I could see it: far to the north, along the horizon, a seam of color that was not white. That was not gray.

That was dawn coming up over a world that still had mornings in it.

The heartstone's hum settled from alarm to something lower, steadier — a heartbeat, or two heartbeats very close together. The warmth of it came up through my boots, through my ruined side, and I realized distantly that I was still standing, that the wound had stopped its slow arithmetic, that the covenant apparently considered me load-bearing now and had no intention of letting its investment bleed out.

Beside me, Marguerite let go of my hand.

She looked at our fingers, separated, as if she'd never seen the mechanism of letting go before. She looked at my face. Hers was open in a way I had never seen it — stripped, new, a locked door standing wide in an emptied house. She knew her name; I could see her mouthing it, testing it, finding it still hers. Everything else had gone out with the tide.

"Marguerite," I said. My voice sounded like it had traveled a long way to get there. "My name is Elias."

She studied me for a long moment. Snow hung unmoving around us. The mourners' song softened into something almost private. And then — slowly, cautiously, the exact expression she used to wear crossing ice she didn't trust — she reached out and took my hand again.

"Elias," she repeated. Her grip tightened, testing. Whatever she found in it, in me, in the fact that a stranger was holding onto her like she was the fixed point ahead — something in her face decided.

"All right," said the last of the oath-bound swordspeakers, keeper of the covenant, half of a bargain, standing in a stopped winter with a man she had never met. "Start talking. We've got a long walk out of these mountains, and I don't know about you—"

She frowned, pressing her free hand lightly against the heartstone, listening to it hum.

"—but I feel like I've forgotten something important."

* * *

Behind us, unnoticed by either of us, Prelate Sorrel rose to her feet, and looked at the two of us standing hand in hand inside a covenant that should have eaten one soul and found two instead — and the faith of forty years came apart in her eyes, quietly, like snow giving up its hold on a branch. She opened her mouth to say something. No sermon came.

Far below the basin, in the deep roads where the mountain keeps its oldest secrets, the first thaw-drop of the year struck stone, and echoed upward, and the fae began, very softly, to sing a verse with two names in it.
