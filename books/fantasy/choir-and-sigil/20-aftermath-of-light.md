## 20. Aftermath of Light

Her name is Marguerite.

She knows this the way she knows the mountain knows her — not as a fact she retrieved but as a fact that arrived, whole, unbidden, like a bell struck somewhere behind her ribs. *Marguerite.* She says it once, or thinks it once, and the stone beneath her hums the syllables back.

She is lying on her back on warm rock, which is wrong, because everything should be cold. The heartstone basin glows faintly gold at the edges of her vision, and snow hangs in the air without falling — suspended, every flake stopped mid-descent like an audience holding its breath.

Someone is holding her hand.

She looks at the hand first because looking at the face is too much work. The hand is human, chapped, burned across the palm in a long searing line that has scabbed white. His fingers are laced through hers with a familiarity that offends her. Not grabbing. Holding. The way you hold something you have held before, many times, in worse places than this.

"Your name," she says. Her voice is a ruin. "What's your name?"

The man's face does something complicated. Grief and relief fighting for the same ground.

"Elias," he says. "Elias Vane."

"Elias Vane." She files it away. It fits nothing in her. It is a key for a door she cannot find. "I'm sorry. I don't—"

"I know." He says it fast, before she can apologize properly, before the apology can grow roots. "I know. That's all right. That was the bargain."

*Was it?* She wants to ask what bargain, whose bargain, why her hand is so cold and his so warm and why the warmth feels like something returning rather than something new. But her mouth is full of ash-flavored quiet, and the basin is singing now — a low chord under the rock, the sound of two enormous things settling against each other — and she finds she is too tired to be afraid.

*Marguerite,* says the stone.

*Here,* she answers, though she doesn't say it aloud.

* * *

Time behaves strangely after that. It comes back to her in pieces, and she keeps them like contraband.

Piece one: the sky above the basin is clear. Not night-clear. Clear the way a fever breaks. The long winter — and she knows there was a long winter, she remembers the word *winter* carrying weight like a drawn blade — has simply stopped, mid-fall, the way a man stops walking when he hears his name called from a window.

Piece two: fae everywhere at the rim of the basin, tall and pale and still as birches, their song reduced to a single sustained note that she realizes has been going on since before she woke. Mourning turned to something else. Or mourning continuing, just more quietly.

Piece three: the woman in grey kneeling twenty feet away with her hands open on her knees, palms up, empty. Sorrel. Prelate Sorrel. The name arrives attached to images she doesn't trust: a sword, a sermon, a circle of chalk and blood that is now only chalk and blood. Sorrel is crying. It is the least frightening thing Marguerite has ever seen and it frightens her anyway.

"You're awake," Sorrel says. Her voice is scraped hollow. "Good. Then witness me."

"I don't know you," Marguerite says.

"No." Sorrel smiles, and it's terrible. "You do. You just paid for the privilege of forgetting it. I remember everything, Underhill. Everything the world gave up. Do you understand what I've been carrying? Every stolen child. Every eaten name. A century of the bill, kept in my head alone because someone had to." Her hands close slowly, fists now. "I thought mercy had to be expensive. That's the whole doctrine. Mercy priced beyond reach, so no one spends it cheap."

Marguerite looks at Elias's burned hand holding hers.

"It wasn't cheap," she says.

"No," Sorrel agrees softly. "But it cost two people what I demanded of a world. Two people." She laughs, and the laugh cracks down the middle. "I built a church on the arithmetic of forever, and you've undone it with — what? A shared burden. A man who stepped into the circle when every text I possess says he couldn't."

"He's stubborn," Marguerite offers, because the man in question is watching her with an expression she can't read and it makes her want to be flippant.

"I am," Elias says quietly.

Sorrel bows her head. When she speaks again, it is not to either of them. It is to the stone, or the fae, or the hundred years behind her. "I surrender," she says. "Seal me if you must. Put me where I can't teach children to be afraid of songs."

The fae at the rim shift. One steps forward — Vess, and the name comes to Marguerite with an ache attached, like a bruise discovered by touch — and studies Sorrel for a long moment.

"The covenant doesn't punish," Vess says. "It never punished. It asked. That's what your order could never forgive it for."

Then Vess turns to Marguerite, and grief moves through the fae's face like weather.

* * *

The memories come up through the ice slowly. That is the only way she can describe it. They are things rising from under a frozen lake — visible first as shadows, then shapes, then breaking the surface all at once with a soundless splash.

The first is small: a waystation chapel, woodsmoke, a man reading aloud in a voice that keeps catching on words. She doesn't see his face. She sees her own hands sharpening a sword by the fire while he reads, and she remembers — no, she *learns* — that she was pretending not to listen and failing.

"What are you smiling at?" Elias asks.

"Nothing. Someone was reading to me once. In a chapel."

"Was he any good?"

"He did all the voices badly."

Elias laughs, and the laugh hits some submerged thing and sends ripples through her — a whole corridor of feeling opening with no rooms attached. She grips his hand harder.

More rises. A frozen river at night, ice groaning like a dying animal, a man's weight pulling her down and her refusing, absolutely refusing, to let go. A village where everyone smiled wrong. A fae pressing fingers to her sword-blade and weeping. Firelight and confession: *I was made for this, made, like a hinge is made for a door* — and another voice, furious and gentle at once: *You are not a door.*

"I said that," Elias says. He is watching her face, reading the memories as they surface, seeing his own past arrive in her eyes twice-removed. "That night. You wanted to quit and survive the winter in the deep roads, and I told you survival without selfhood is just the village's fate with extra steps."

"That sounds like you," she says, and doesn't know why her throat closes when she says it.

A shadow-shape under the ice: him falling out of the snow. Him insisting it was spring. A scrap of parchment with her name on it. She reaches for the memory of the parchment and it dissolves just before she can read it, and she understands with cold clarity that some things went into the stone entirely and are not coming back. There are holes in her with smooth edges. Whatever filled them, she chose to give it up. She holds onto that: *she chose.* Even if she can't remember choosing, the shape of the choice remains, like a footprint in snow.

"Tell me things," she says. "About us. About — whatever we were."

"Why?"

"Because your telling me might call the memory up. And if it doesn't—" She swallows. "If it doesn't, then at least I'll have the story. Stories are almost as good."

"They're better," Elias says. "Stories you get to keep."

He tells her about the blizzard. About her nearly leaving him. He tells it honestly — "you had your sword half-drawn, you looked at me like I was cargo that talked" — and each detail lands on the water of her and sinks and sometimes, sometimes, something rises to meet it. A road only she knew. The Choir singing against the fae. Hymn against hymn, and his blood on the chalk, and her screaming an oath-word she can no longer pronounce.

When he gets to the heartstone — to the binding, to him stepping into the circle — he stops.

"You don't have to tell that part," she says.

"I think I do." His burned hand trembles in hers. "You spoke the binding and the stone started taking you — your memories, top layer first. Recent things. Me. And I—" He breathes. "I did the forbidden thing. There's a reason it's forbidden, Marguerite. Sharing the burden means the covenant takes both of us. Not equally. But really. I'm bound to the stone now too. Part of what I am lives here."

"Part of you lives here," she repeats.

"So if I seem distracted sometimes, that's why. And if the mountain seems to like you more than mountains usually like people—" His mouth twists. "That's also partly why."

She looks around the basin with new eyes. The gold glow. The warmth in the rock. The snow hanging in the air like a congregation that has forgotten how to leave. The place feels, suddenly, inhabited — not haunted, nothing so sinister — inhabited the way a house is inhabited by people who love it.

"We live here now," she says. "Some of us."

"Some of us," he agrees.

* * *

Dawn comes, or something like dawn. The light enters the basin at a slant and turns the suspended snowfall into drifting embers of white, and Marguerite sits up for the first time and discovers her body works, mostly, if she doesn't ask it for poetry.

Vess brings them food that tastes like frost and honey both. The fae crouches beside her and studies her face with the patience of trees.

"You don't remember me," Vess says.

"There's an ache where you should be," Marguerite says. "Does that count?"

Vess considers this. "In the old days, that counted for a great deal." The fae touches her wrist, lightly, and sings three notes — low, descending, resolving upward at the last instant — and the ache blooms into a fragment: Vess's fingers on her blade. Vess saying *this sword was forged from the first promise.* Grief, shared, in a deep pass with the winter closing in.

"Oh," Marguerite breathes. "You knew my sword."

"I grieved over your sword," Vess corrects. "You were always more difficult to grieve over. Too busy walking." The fae's silver eyes flick to Elias, then back. "There will be things to rebuild. The covenant is reforged but thin — a new thread, not the old rope. The fae will need to learn to trust a human realm that sealed its doors on us for a hundred years. The humans will need to learn that winter has a keeper again. And you—" a pause, delicate as frost forming, "—will need to decide who you are when you are not the seal."

"I'm not the seal anymore?"

"You are the keeper who survived her own keeping. That is a different thing. Older stories exist about it. Very few of them end well." Vess rises, unhurried. "But none of them had him."

And the fae walks away toward the other mourners, leaving Marguerite with that, and with Elias's hand finding hers again without either of them deciding it should.

Across the basin, Sorrel stands. The Hollow Choir — what's left of them, maybe thirty figures in grey — watches their prelate the way people watch a bridge they aren't sure will hold. Sorrel removes the iron-grey circlet she wears and sets it on the heartstone, where it lies like a dead thing.

"The Choir disperses," she says. Her voice carries without effort; some habits are load-bearing. "Those who wish may stay and learn what the covenant actually asks. Those who don't — go, and sing nothing against anyone." She turns, and her eyes find Marguerite's, and for a moment the two of them are just women who loved the same order and lost it differently. "Underhill. When your memories come back — and they will come back, the stone gives back what it took from the willing, slowly, over years — find me. There are things I remember that you'll want. Things about the night you were made."

*Made.* The word drops through her like a stone through lake ice.

"Sorrel," Elias starts, sharp, but Marguerite squeezes his hand.

"I'll find you," she says.

Sorrel nods once and walks down the southern trail, and the Choir parts around her like water, and Marguerite discovers she is shaking and cannot say why, except that somewhere under the ice of her, a shadow the size of her whole life has begun to rise, and she can see its outline now, and it has her face.

* * *

Evening. The second evening, or the third — she loses track, and decides losing track is allowed.

They sit at the basin's edge with their backs against the heartstone, which hums faintly against her spine like a cat too large to comprehend. Below, the passes are opening. Real meltwater runs in the gullies. Somewhere down-mountain, villages are waking to a winter that ended, to names returning like birds.

"Tell me the story of us again," she says. "From the beginning."

"Again? I've told it four times."

"And I've heard it four times, and each time something new floats up. Third telling, I remembered the river. Fourth telling, I dreamed the chapel." She tips her head back against the stone. "You're not just telling me a story, Elias. You're fishing."

He laughs. Snow-bright light catches his face, and she studies it — really studies it, the way she'd study a lock before picking it — and something surfaces: his hands wrapping hers around a tin cup in a chapel, steam rising, his voice doing a character badly.

"There," she says, sitting up. "I just got something. You make terrible voices."

"Noted."

"And you burn easily. Your hand—"

"Yours, actually. You grabbed it when I went to touch the circle. Pulled me back." He flexes the scabbed palm. "It didn't take. I touched it anyway, later, on purpose."

"On purpose," she repeats, and the shape of that — a man walking into a fire knowing it's a fire — rises whole and gleaming and breaks over her, and she has to close her eyes against it. "Why would you do that."

"Because you were in there," he says simply. "And the texts said no one could share the burden, and I decided the texts hadn't met you."

Silence. The stone hums. Far above, a fae voice begins a new melody — not the mourning song, something with an ascending figure, something unfinished, a verse being written in real time.

"Elias," Marguerite says carefully. "When my memories come back. All of them. Over the years, like the stone wants. Will I become the person I was? The one who knew you?"

He is quiet a long time.

"I don't know," he admits. "Maybe. Is that bad?"

"I don't know either." She watches the suspended snow finally begin to fall again — slow, ordinary, harmless snow, coming down like the world remembering how. "It's just — I like who I'm becoming right now. Meeting you the way I'm meeting you. Finding out about us like clues." She hesitates, then commits, the way she'd commit to a dangerous pass in bad weather. "What if, when the old memories return... we introduce ourselves again anyway? Start over on purpose? Keep both?"

Elias turns to look at her fully, and whatever is in his face, she thinks she might one day earn all of it.

"You want to court me twice," he says.

"I want to choose you twice," she corrects. "Once already happened, apparently, and I'm sure it was very dramatic and involved a lot of running. But the second one I get to do sober. On a warm rock. With the whole mountain singing." She shrugs. "Seems like better terms."

Above them, the fae's unfinished verse climbs higher, and Marguerite realizes with a start that she can hear words in it now — and that one of the words, sung soft and clear against the dusk, is her name, and the word beside it is his.

"Listen," she says.

And far below, at the mouth of the basin, a travel-worn figure appears on the switchback trail with a pack on her shoulders and a familiar stride — Sorrel, come back early, moving fast, her face in the fading light the color of someone carrying news that will not keep until morning.
