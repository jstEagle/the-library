## 21. Learning Him Again

### Marguerite

The thaw came all at once, the way the mountain did everything.

One morning the snow on the high pass was blue-white and silent; by noon it was weeping, every slope streaming, the roads I had known my whole life dissolving into new rivers that ran where no rivers had run before. Thornfell was rewriting itself. I stood at the mouth of the Hollow Pass with my boots in six inches of slush and thought: *good.* Let it forget me a little. I'd had enough of being remembered by things.

Elias crouched at the treeline, poking a stick into the meltwater like a man testing whether the world would hold his weight. His wound had closed to a pale seam along his ribs — Vess's singing had done what no needle could — but he still moved carefully, like someone carrying water across ice. He looked up and caught me watching him.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm assessing."

"Assessing what?"

"Whether you'll make it down the pass without falling into a melt-swallow and drowning in forty feet of freezing water."

"And?"

I turned back to the road. "Fifty-fifty."

He grinned. That was the thing about him I kept having to learn again — not his face, which I knew from the heartstone's aftermath the way you know a room after a fire, but the *small* things. The grin that arrived sideways first. The way he counted under his breath when he was nervous, a habit he claimed not to have. The fact that he hated the cold and complained about it constantly and had walked into a blizzard for me anyway, according to everyone except himself.

He didn't remember me. Not truly. The covenant had taken my memories of him, and his memories of me had gone with them — that was the bargain's symmetry, its terrible bookkeeping. What remained was evidence. Stories told by fae. A scrap of parchment with my name on it, which he kept folded in his breast pocket like a relic. And whatever the two of us built now, day by day, out of thaw-water and trail rations and conversation.

"You know," he said, catching up, "Vess told me something last night. While you were sleeping."

"Vess tells you everything. Vess likes you better than me."

"Vess is *afraid* of you. It's different." He splashed alongside me, breath fogging. "She said that when the covenant took your memories, it didn't take them. It *moved* them. Into me. That they're still there — mine now, the way a house is still there after it changes hands."

I stopped walking.

The meltwater roared somewhere below us, patient as a debt.

"She said," Elias went on, quieter, "that sometimes I dream about a road through the pass in winter, and a woman who walks it like she was carved out of it. And that when I wake up, I miss her. Even though—" he gestured vaguely at me, at all of this, "—even though she's right here eating the last of the dried plums."

"I did not eat the last of the dried plums."

"You absolutely did. See? Evidence." He held out his hand. "Come on, Underhill. Fifty-fifty odds are better than most of what we've survived."

I took it. His hand was warm, which still surprised me every time, though I couldn't have said why it should. Some knowledge lived deeper than memory, in the marrow, in the sword-calluses. My body knew him even when my head was a locked room.

We walked down into the green.

### Elias

She talked in her sleep.

Not much — a word or two a night, dropped like coins. The first week it was oath-words, the old swordspeech, syllables that made the campfire stand up straight and the horses (we had no horses) shift nervously. Then, as the passes opened and the air went soft, the words changed.

"Left at the split stone," she murmured one night, curled against her pack under an overhang dripping with melt. "Trust the ice past the third bend. Don't look at the light."

I lay awake a long time after that, listening to the river and understanding what Vess had meant. These weren't dreams. They were *directions.* Her knowledge of Thornfell — the thing she'd offered me on the frozen river when trust was still a coin neither of us wanted to spend first — was seeping into me through sleep like water through limestone. I was becoming, drop by drop, a map of her.

I didn't tell her for three days. When I finally did, over breakfast, she went very still.

"So I'm haunting you," she said.

"You're *teaching* me."

"There's a difference?"

"There's a big difference." I poked the fire. "A ghost wants something. A teacher gives you what you need and lets go." I hesitated. "Mostly. Mostly lets go."

She looked at me for a long moment with those grey eyes that were starting to feel like home even though I could produce no evidence for why. Then she said, "Tell me the one about the light."

"The light?"

"In the pass. Don't look at the light. Tell me the dream."

So I closed my eyes and gave it back to her: a corridor of ice, blue-green, lit from within by something that wasn't fire and wasn't sun, and a woman ahead of me who never turned around because turning around was how it caught you. She listened with her whole body, arms wrapped around her knees, and when I finished she nodded slowly.

"That's the Glasswalk," she said. "It's real. It's under the north ridge. I've been through it twice." She paused. "I don't remember going through it twice. But my feet remember, and apparently so do yours now."

"Is that frightening to you? That I'm full of your life?"

She considered it honestly, the way she considered everything — like a blade being weighed. "A month ago it would have terrified me. A month ago I was a smuggler with a fake name and a sword I never drew, and my whole self fit in a pack." She looked up at the ridgeline, where the snow was coming off the peaks in long white banners. "Now I'm half of a covenant and the other half is a man who fell out of the sky, and honestly?" A ghost of a smile. "The company's better."

That night she talked in her sleep again. This time it wasn't directions.

"Elias," she said, clear as a bell. Just my name. Once. Like checking that a door still opened.

I didn't sleep much after that either. But it was a better kind of not-sleeping.

### Marguerite

The village at the foot of the Thornfell road had remembered itself.

We came down out of the high country on the ninth day and found the nameless place named again: Coldharbour, said the sign at the bridge, freshly painted, the paint still tacky. People in the lanes stopped and greeted each other by names they were trying out loud like new shoes. An old woman sat by the well teaching children a song that had no words yet, just a rising melody, and when she saw us she stopped and stared at Elias with her mouth open.

"You're him," she breathed. "The man from the Canticle. We don't— we only got our memories back yesterday, and you're already in them. Both of you. You're in the song we forgot."

"We're in a lot of songs lately," Elias said uncomfortably.

The old woman beckoned us close. Up close her eyes were wet and bright. "The fae came through the night the winter broke. First time in a hundred years anyone's seen them walk in the open. They sang in the square until dawn, and what they sang—" she pressed her hand flat over her heart, "—what they sang had *names* in it. Not just theirs. Ours. All of ours. Every person this valley ever lost to the forgetting, sung back into the world. And at the end, two names more."

"Let me guess," I said dryly. "Underhill and Vane."

"Marguerite and Elias," she corrected gently. "They sang your given names. Like family does."

We stayed one night in Coldharbour, our first roof since the heartstone. The innkeeper refused payment; Elias left coins under his plate anyway, and I let him, and understood that this too was evidence — some old habit of decency from wherever he'd come from, surviving the fall between worlds.

In the evening I found him in the inn yard practicing with my sword.

Not fighting. Practicing. Running the forms slowly, badly, with the total concentration of a man learning letters. He'd seen me do them at dawn — I still woke before the birds, some things the covenant hadn't touched — and now he was copying them, and getting them wrong, and correcting, and getting them wrong again.

"They're oath-forms," I said from the gate.

He lowered the blade, embarrassed. "Sorry. Should I ask first?"

"It's not mine to give anymore. That's rather the point." I came into the yard. "But if you're going to do them, do them right. Third form starts from the shoulder, not the wrist. You're writing, not cutting."

"Show me."

So I stood behind him in the lamplight and moved his arm through the arc, hand over hand, and felt him learn it — the small resistance, then the surrender, then the motion becoming his own. Somewhere in the middle of the fourth form he stopped needing my hands, and I stepped back and watched him carry it alone, clumsy and earnest, and something in my chest did a thing I had no vocabulary for.

"The oaths I used to speak," I said, "were written by dead men a century ago. Binding words. Sealing words. Words that made me into a lock." I watched the blade trace its arc. "Maybe it's time somebody wrote new ones. Worse ones. Better ones."

"Worse?"

"Messier. Human." I shrugged. "Oaths that leave room for the person swearing them to change their mind and grow."

He finished the form and turned to face me, breathing hard, grinning that sideways grin. "First clause of the new oath: no more sacrificing each other without discussing it beforehand."

"That's two clauses."

"Then we're off to a thorough start."

### Elias

On the twelfth day out of the heartstone, the fae came to sing to us.

They came at dusk, in the meadow below the last ridge — Vess first, walking out of the birch shadows with her frost-pale hair loose and her bare feet leaving no prints in the new grass, and behind her a dozen others, mourners no longer. Their grief-song had changed key sometime in the weeks since the covenant reforged. What they carried now sounded, to my untrained ear, almost like planting music.

Vess stopped before Marguerite and bowed — low, formal, wings furled tight.

"Keeper of the seal," she said.

"Don't call me that," Marguerite said, without heat. "I'm retired."

"Keeper of the *new* thing, then. Whatever it becomes." Vess straightened. Her black eyes went to me, and softened in the way that frost softens into water. "And you. Anchor of the circle. The one who did the forbidden thing."

"I prefer 'Elias.'"

"The Canticle has grown," Vess said, ignoring me, which seemed to be her way. "A hundred years it slept with one verse unfinished. Now it writes nightly. The singers take turns holding the book while the ink appears." She glanced at her companions, and something passed among them like wind through wheat. "Would you hear tonight's verse? It concerns you both. It finished itself an hour ago, at sunset, and the singers have been arguing about whether to bring it to you."

"Why argue?" Marguerite asked.

"Because it is a *naming,* keeper. And namings bind."

The meadow went quiet. Even the stream seemed to lean in.

Marguerite looked at me. I looked at her. Between us lay everything the last months had built — the river escape, the firelight confessions, the argument in the deep roads where she'd called herself a door and I'd called her a liar, the moment at the heartstone when I'd stepped into the circle knowing it might unmake me and doing it anyway because the alternative was her alone in the dark forever. None of it was memory, not exactly. It was newer than memory. It was *ours.*

"What does it say?" she asked Vess.

Vess lifted her voice, and her people lifted theirs beneath it, and the verse rose over the meadow in the old tongue first and then, deliberately, kindly, in ours:

*When the ash-canticle is ended*
*and the long white page is turned,*
*two shall walk the thawed passes*
*where once a single walker burned.*

*No seal, no door, no bargain-bound —*
*but free, and freely met:*
*the Underhill who chose her road,*
*the Vane who chose her yet.*

*What was forged shall be rewritten.*
*What was owed shall be forgiven.*
*Two names enter the singing,*
*and neither leaves it.*

The last note hung in the dusk like a struck bell fading. The fae lowered their eyes, waiting.

Marguerite was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "'Neither leaves it.' Is that a threat or a promise?"

"Yes," said Vess.

Marguerite laughed — actually laughed, short and startled, a sound I suspected the old order had rarely heard from her. She looked at me. "Well, Vane. We've been written into scripture. Again. Any objections this time?"

I thought about the man who'd fallen through the rift, clutching a scrap of parchment, wanting nothing but to get home to a spring that no longer existed. He'd have objected violently. He'd have demanded a refund from the universe.

I couldn't find him anywhere in myself anymore. Good riddance, mostly.

"No objections," I said. "One amendment."

Vess raised an eyebrow. Marguerite crossed her arms, but her eyes were shining.

"Add a line," I said. "*And they wrote their own verses after.* Because if we've learned anything, it's that nobody gets to finish our sentence. Not prophecy, not prelacy, not even the Canticle."

Silence. Then Vess smiled — the first true smile I'd ever seen on a face built for grief — and turned to her singers, and they began to sing the amendment into the book before the words had finished leaving my mouth, the ink taking them down greedily, as if it had been waiting all century for permission.

Marguerite took my hand in the failing light. Her grip was sword-hard and, underneath that, careful. Tender. Still learning itself.

"Come on," she said, nodding at the darkening road that unwound from the mountains toward the valleys, toward the lowlands, toward everywhere. "Long walk ahead. Lots of verses to write."

And we went down together, out of Thornfell, into the green and open world —

where, three days later, at a crossroads inn hung with early blossoms, a rider in grey waited beside a horse, wearing a prelate's broken collar like a scar, and rose from the bench when she saw us, and said:

"Marguerite Underhill. I need to confess something, and I only have until sundown to say it."
