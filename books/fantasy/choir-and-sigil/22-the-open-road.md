## 22. The Open Road

The shipment was wool and iron nails and one crate of books that Marguerite had promised not to ask about.

She asked anyway, because the man who'd hired her in Lowmark had the shifty eyes of a man whose books were worth more than his house, and because asking was her job. But that was the old job. The new job — three seasons old now — was simpler: carry what needed carrying, ask only enough questions to keep everyone alive, and split the fee with the tall man walking beside her who kept insisting he was bad at this and then proving himself wrong.

"Left at the cairn," Elias said.

"Right at the cairn," Marguerite said.

They both stopped. The cairn stood in front of them, gray stones stacked by hands long dead, furred with the first rime of autumn. Above it the pass of Sorrow's Elbow bent away into cloud, and the wind coming down it carried that sound she was still not used to, would maybe never be used to — a low thread of tone under the wind's noise, like someone humming in the next room. Like someone humming *her*.

"The mountain says left," Elias said.

"The mountain doesn't know the way to Thornmouth."

"The mountain *is* the way to Thornmouth." He crouched, touched two fingers to the cairn's top stone, the way he'd learned to do without embarrassment sometime over the summer. His eyes went distant, listening. "It says the eastern path is washed out below the shoulder. Rockfall from the thaw floods. Left is longer but it holds."

She looked at him for a long moment. Snow was starting to come down behind him — fat, unhurried flakes, the honest kind, the kind that fell in autumn and melted and meant nothing except that winter was coming the way winter was supposed to come. Not the long winter. Not the tide of white that had eaten villages and names whole. Just weather.

"You've been talking to rocks," she said.

"I've been listening to rocks. There's a difference." He straightened up, brushing snow off his knees. "Also the rock says you're stubborn."

"The rock and I have that in common."

He grinned. A year ago — no. Less than a year. Ten months since a rift had torn open in a blizzard above the Widow's Stair and dropped a half-frozen stranger at her feet carrying her own name on a scrap of parchment. Ten months since she'd nearly walked away and left him to die, and hated herself for the wanting, and saved him anyway. She remembered all of it now, or most of it, or enough of it — the memories still surfaced some mornings like things rising through lake ice, arriving without warning, a detail here, a whole afternoon there. The first time she'd seen him laugh. The night on the frozen river when he'd chosen her side out loud, in words, while the ice split behind them. The heartstone. The binding. The blankness where he should have been, and then — slowly, slowly — the man himself, reintroduced day by day, story by story, until the evidence outweighed the absence.

She'd written it all down. That was the strange mercy of it: the covenant had taken her memories, but Elias had anchored hers inside his own, and everything he held had come back to her across the spring like water finding its level. She knew her own life again. She just had to take it secondhand sometimes, from a man who told it better than she'd lived it.

"So we go left," she said.

"We go left."

They went left.

* * *

The mule's name was Turnip, and Turnip had opinions about the eastern fork that turned out to match the mountain's, which Marguerite chose not to dwell on. She led him by the halter through the switchbacks while Elias brought up the rear with the cart, and the snow thickened around them the way storms did in Thornfell — suddenly, totally, a white wall with your own breath for company.

In the old days — before, during, all of it — a storm like this would have been the enemy. She'd spent years reading weather like an adversary's face, every flake a lie the sky was telling her. She'd crossed passes in whiteouts with nothing but an oath in her throat and roads in her head that she'd sworn never to speak aloud, and she'd been alone in the way that only oath-bound people are alone, which is to say completely, even in company, even in love if it ever came to that, which it hadn't.

Now the storm came down and the mountain sang through it, and she stopped on the ledge above the river gorge and let it sing to her.

It wasn't a voice, exactly. It was more like the memory of a choir — the fae mourners' song, the fragments Vess and her people had sung against the Hollow Choir's hymn while the world came apart around the heartstone. After the covenant reforged, the song hadn't stopped. It had changed key. It ran through the stone of the passes now the way water runs through limestone, invisible and everywhere, and when the wind moved right, you could hear it. Most people heard nothing. The mountain folk said the peaks had gone quiet-blessed, whatever that meant. But Marguerite Underhill heard it clear as a struck bell, and she knew why.

She was half of the bargain. She always had been. That part hadn't changed when the memories came back; only her relationship to it had. Once, learning she'd been forged as the living seal of an ancient covenant — made, not born, shaped by dead swordspeakers into a lock for a door between worlds — had shattered her. She'd tried to run from it, deep into the roads only she knew, and Elias had refused to follow, and had argued her back onto the heartstone road with the truest thing anyone ever said to her: that survival without selfhood was the village's fate, not freedom.

But that argument had been won a year ago. What remained was quieter. What remained was this: standing in a snowstorm on a ledge above a gorge, hearing her name in the mountain's hum, and feeling not claimed but *accompanied*.

"They're singing your verse," Elias said beside her. He'd come up quietly, the way he did, cart abandoned below with Turnip. "The new one."

"I know."

"Do you want to hear it?"

"It's my verse. I wrote it."

"You wrote the words." He pulled something from inside his coat — oilcloth-wrapped, flat, familiar. Her chest did the thing it always did when she saw it. "Vess sent it ahead. Said the fae finished setting it to music and wanted you to have the fair copy before winter closed the high passes."

The Canticle of Ash.

The book had been a century old when Elias found it in a waystation chapel, its prophecies naming tonight's date, her name, the broken bargain. Marguerite had burned her copy within hours of finding his — a reflex, then, terror dressed up as practicality. The Choir had hunted every page of it. And now here it was, rebound in gray leather, the old prophecy verses intact and the new ones added in ink that shimmered faintly, the way fae-worked things did, and near the end, on a page that had been blank for a hundred years:

*Two names walk the open road. One remembers. One is remembered. The mountain keeps what they give it.*

Below that, in a different hand — smaller, sharper, unmistakably hers, though she didn't remember writing it, which meant it had been taken from her letters, or her spoken words, or somewhere else she'd given herself away without knowing:

*"I am not a door. I am the woman who opened it."*

Marguerite read it twice. Then a third time. The snow gathered on the oilcloth and she brushed it away with her thumb, careful, the way you touch a wound that has finally healed.

"When did I say that?" she asked.

"Last spring. To Sorrel." Elias's voice went gentle. "At the heartstone, after. When she asked what you thought you'd become."

Prelate Sorrel. Former swordspeaker, keeper of every memory the world forgot, sincere as a blade and twice as clean. She had believed sealing the realms apart forever was mercy — no more stolen children, no more eaten villages, even if the price was the slow death of every fae in the deep places. She had fought Marguerite with doubts instead of steel, and very nearly won. And then she had watched a human man step into a binding circle to share a burden that was never his, and had seen mercy cost less than her seal, and had knelt down in the ash and snow and surrendered.

She kept a chapel in Lowmark now. She taught children their letters. Sometimes she sent word north asking whether the fae needed anything, and the answer was always the same — *ask them yourself, Prelate; the road is open* — and someday, Marguerite suspected, Sorrel would actually make the journey, and it would be terrible and necessary and good.

"She cried, you know," Elias said. "After. When you were sleeping. She thought no one saw."

"I know. You told me."

"I tell you everything twice. It's how the repair works."

Marguerite laughed, and the mountain laughed with her — the hum in the stone bending briefly upward, brightening, the way it did when she was happy, which was a thing she had stopped questioning around midsummer. The peaks knew her name. Not the oath-name the order had burned into her, not the seal-name carved in her bones before she could walk. Her actual name. Marguerite. The one her mother gave her, back when she'd had a mother and a kitchen and a life before any of it. The mountain had learned it from the fae, who had learned it from Vess, who had grieved over her sword and then sung it into the covenant like planting a seed.

Somewhere below, Turnip brayed, offended at being left.

"Come on," she said. "We're losing light, and I want the waystation chapel tonight. There's something I have to do there."

Elias raised an eyebrow. "Something?"

"You'll see."

* * *

The waystation chapel looked exactly as it had the night Elias found the Canticle in it — low-beamed, candle-scarred, smelling of tallow and cold stone and old woodsmoke. The same bench by the hearth where he'd sat with a book that described him before he existed. The same narrow window where the blizzard had screamed. Outside, the real storm was doing its honest work against the shutters, and inside, the fire caught on the first strike, because the mountain liked her, and Marguerite set the Canticle of Ash on the table between them and sat looking at it for a long time.

"One year ago," Elias said, "you burned your copy in this room."

"I burned my copy in this room." She turned the book over in her hands. Gray leather, fae-ink, a century of prophecy and one season of new verse. "And he memorized his. Every line. Because burning felt like lying and memorizing felt like—" She paused.

"Fighting," Elias said.

"Fighting." She smiled at him sideways. "You said that. Standing right there, dripping on the floor, telling me the difference between destroying a thing and refusing to be ruled by it. I thought you were insufferable."

"You definitely thought I was insufferable."

"And then the Choir came through the door, and I spoke my first oath-word in six years, and the mountain answered, and nothing was ever simple again." She opened the Canticle to the end. The new verses. The blank pages after them — three of them, four, waiting. Fae-ink dried fast and permanent; Vess had explained the properties twice and Marguerite had understood roughly none of it, except that once written in this book, a line became part of the song, part of the mountain's memory, part of whatever the covenant was now becoming. Prophecies had started the trouble. Maybe prophecies could finish it differently.

She took out the pen she'd bought in Lowmark. The ink was ordinary — iron-gall, the cheap kind, the kind smugglers used for ledgers. That felt right. This was going in as a ledger entry. Goods received, goods carried, accounts balanced.

"What are you going to write?" Elias asked.

"I don't know yet."

"You don't know *yet*?" He leaned forward, mock-scandalized. "You've had the book since dawn. You made us camp early. You've been composing faces at it all evening."

"I've been composing *silences* at it. There's a difference." But he was right, and they both knew he was right, and the reason she didn't know was the reason that mattered: everything she'd ever been written as — swordspeaker, seal, smuggler, survivor, the Underhill woman, the living lock — had been authored by someone else. An order that made her and abandoned her. A book that named her a century before she drew breath. A covenant that called her a door and a bargain and half of itself. For thirty years she had been a character in other people's texts, and she had spent the last year learning, painfully, joyfully, what it was to be the author instead.

So the line had to be hers. All hers. Not assigned. Chosen.

She dipped the pen. Thought of the ledger entries of her old life — contraband, routes, fees. Thought of the oath she no longer spoke aloud, because oaths spoken under duress deserved to die. Thought of a village that forgot its name, and a prelate who remembered too much, and a fae mourner who sang grief until it turned into music. Thought of a man out of the snow who was currently watching her with the particular patience of someone who intended to wait however long it took, forever if necessary, and had proven it once already in a circle of light at the bottom of the world.

She wrote:

*I was made to be a seal, so I chose to be a road. Roads go both ways. Whoever reads this: the pass is open, the mountain sings, and you are allowed to become what you choose. Come find us. We'll leave the lantern lit.*

She sat back. Read it. Read it again. The ink soaked into the old paper and something in the walls of the chapel — in the stone beneath the chapel, in the bones of the mountain itself — shifted, the hum rising a half-tone, warm as a hearth. Somewhere far below, in the deep green dark where the fae kept their courts, she'd have sworn she heard Vess laugh.

"Well?" Elias said.

"It's done." She capped the ink. Her hand was steady. "That's the last line. No more prophecy after this. Everything past this page is just — life."

"Terrifying," he agreed solemnly.

"Completely unmanageable."

"We'll cope."

Outside, the storm thickened toward full dark, and the two of them banked the fire and fed Turnip and hung the wet wool by the hearth, and did all the small domestic arithmetic of travelers settling in — and it was, Marguerite thought, the mirror of another night in this same room, a year and a lifetime ago. A stranger then. A partner now. A blizzard then, falling like a verdict. A blizzard now, falling like weather, which is all a storm should ever be.

She lay awake later than she meant to, listening to the wind work the shutters and the mountain hum beneath it, and near midnight she heard it clearly — the new verse, the one with two names in it, threading up through the stone like spring water. *One remembers. One is remembered.* Except that wasn't quite true anymore, and she suspected the fae knew it, and suspected that was why they kept adjusting the music. They both remembered. They both had been remembered. That was the whole point of what they'd built in the wreckage of the old bargain: nobody carried it alone.

Beside her, Elias murmured something in his sleep — a route marker, probably, or her name, or both; the two had gotten thoroughly tangled in him over the summer, so that he sometimes navigated by calling her name at forks in the road and the mountain, shamelessly, answered.

Morning would bring the pass to Thornmouth, the delivery, the fee, the split. Winter would come on schedule and be only winter. Spring would open the high trails again, and there was talk — Vess's talk, persistent as meltwater — of a gathering at the heartstone at first thaw, fae and human both, the first of its kind in a century, to sing the Canticle start to finish with all its new verses. There would be arguments. There would be Sorrel, kneeling in the back row, learning to listen. There would be work.

Marguerite Underhill, last of the oath-bound swordspeakers — no. Strike that. First of the road-bound, self-written, two-named, lantern-lit. She closed her eyes, and the mountain sang her to sleep, and the storm fell softly on a world with its doors open.

Far to the south, though — though she didn't know it yet, wouldn't know it for another week, until a rider caught them on the flats below Thornmouth with mud to the waist and a letter sealed in wax she recognized — far to the south, in a country neither of them had ever seen, a rift had opened in broad daylight over a market square.

And the first thing to step through it was carrying a book.
