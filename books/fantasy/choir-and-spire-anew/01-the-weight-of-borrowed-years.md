## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The smuggler was dying in the way that mountain men sometimes did: quietly, stubbornly, and with a great deal of complaint about the quality of the company keeping him.

"You're no physicker," he said. His voice had gone thin as winter ice, but his eyes were still sharp, tracking Tamsin's hands as they worked. "Physickers don't smell like burnt rosemary."

"That's my tea," Tamsin said. "You're welcome to share, if you can manage not to spill it on yourself."

"Rosemary's for remembering." He coughed, wet and long, and his whole frame shook under the patched wool blanket. "Old wives' tale."

"Old wives know things," Tamsin said, and kept their face pleasant, and did not mention that they had chewed three sprigs of it before walking up from the valley, because what they were about to do deserved preparation, the way a burglar deserves a dark night.

His name was Odo Ferrin, though everyone on the Thornfell roads called him Weasel, which Tamsin had always thought unkind — weasels were excellent at their work, and so was Odo, or had been, until the rockfall on the Kettle Stair had crushed his legs and left him here in this shepherd's hut with nothing ahead of him but cold and slow ruin. Tamsin had found him by following the crows. That was the trick nobody told you about the mountains: the crows knew where everything was. Carrion, dropped cargo, dying men with full heads.

"Sit closer," Odo said.

"I'm fine here."

"Sit *closer*, Brightwater. I've got something for you and you'll want both hands free."

Tamsin set down the bowl of broth they hadn't expected him to notice, let alone comment on, and moved their stool nearer the cot. Outside, the wind came off the high passes with its usual conversation — a low moan, a rattle of loose slate, the endless complaint of stone against stone. Inside, the fire popped. Odo's breath rattled in time with it, two rhythms falling out of step and back in again.

"You've heard how I go," Odo said. "Everyone does, eventually. The trade takes us all one way or another. Falls, blades, the Spire's grey cloaks. Me, I get a rock. Forty years of dodging worse and a rock finishes it." He laughed, which turned into another cough, which turned into something that frightened them both, judging by the way his hand found Tamsin's wrist and gripped it. "I don't want to carry it all into the ground. That's waste. I hate waste."

"What are you offering?"

"The passes." His grip tightened. "All of them. Not the ones on the maps — the real ones. The ones my grandfather showed me and his grandfather showed him. The Hollow Under Whistler's Tooth. The stair behind the frozen fall where you have to count your steps because there's no light at all. The ford through the Serpent's Gullet that only runs safe when the moon is wrong." His eyes were fever-bright now. "Thirty years I carried those routes in my skull like coins in a boot. And now the boot's going in the fire either way."

Tamsin sat very still.

This was the trade, and had been since they were nineteen years old and desperate enough to learn it from a woman in Lowmarsh who charged a terrible price and never explained herself. Memory could be lifted. Not copied — *lifted*, taken out whole, like a splinter drawn clean. The donor forgot. The thief remembered, forever after, as if it had happened to them. There were rules, most of them unwritten, all of them important: never take more than offered; never take love; never take anything you aren't prepared to carry for the rest of your life, because memories didn't weigh anything and weighed everything, all at once.

And never, ever take from someone who wasn't giving.

Tamsin had watched thieves break that last rule. They always ended the same way: hollow-eyed, muttering in other people's childhoods, forgetting their own names because they'd filled their heads with so many borrowed lives there was no room left for their own. The mind was a house, Tamsin's teacher used to say. Fill it with strangers' furniture and soon you can't find your own bed.

"All right," Tamsin said. "Terms first, as always."

Odo grinned, and for a moment Tamsin could see the young smuggler he'd been, the one who'd run salt and star-iron over the Kettle Stair while wardens slept ten feet away. "Always terms with you."

"Always terms. You give me the passes freely. All of them, clear and whole. In exchange—" Tamsin nodded at the broth, the fire, the blanket. "I stay until it's done. Nobody dies alone in one of these huts if I can help it. That's worth more than coin, and you know it, because you've been trying to give me your routes for an hour and pretending it's commerce."

Odo's grin faded into something softer and more tired. "You're a strange one, Brightwater."

"So people tell me."

"They say you've got no one. No partner, no crew, no family. They say you sleep in a different hut every month and never leave a toothbrush behind."

"They say a lot of things about me. Some of them are even true."

"And do they say why?"

"They usually decide it's because I'm cold," Tamsin said lightly, "and then they stop asking, because who wants to trade with someone cold? It's very good for business."

Odo studied them with those fading sharp eyes, and Tamsin had the uncomfortable sensation of being appraised by a professional — the way they themselves appraised a mark, a route, a lock. Then he laughed, weakly, and patted the edge of the cot.

"Give us your hands, then. Both of them. This will be like the old days — I used to move goods with the best of them, and the best always said the same thing: hold it like you mean to keep it."

* * *

It was not, strictly speaking, like moving goods.

Tamsin laid both palms against Odo's temples, closed their eyes, and went in.

Every mind opened differently. Some were locked doors; some were open windows rattling in a storm. Odo's was a warehouse — of course it was, thought the part of Tamsin that never stopped narrating even in the deep places of the work — a great dim warehouse stacked to the rafters with thirty years of crated knowledge, and the crates were labeled in Odo's own blunt mental hand: *ROUTES. FENCES. DEBTS. THAT WOMAN IN LOWMARSH (DO NOT OPEN).*

Tamsin stood in the dark among the stacks and waited, because the rule was that the giver led.

Here. A crate coming toward them, pushed along the aisles by Odo's fading will, thumping to rest at their feet. Tamsin put their hands on the lid and felt the weight of it — oh, the *weight* of it, thirty years of mountain paths worn into one man's bones. They braced, breathed out, and lifted.

It came into them like water into dry ground.

The Hollow Under Whistler's Tooth: a crack in the cliff face barely wider than a loaded pack, opening after forty body-lengths into a gallery where smugglers had scratched tally-marks for every safe crossing since before the Spire was built. The stair behind the frozen fall: ninety-seven steps, and on the eighty-third you must turn left with your hand on the rope or walk straight off the ledge into blackness. The Serpent's Gullet ford, runnable only when the moon hung wrong and the meltwater dropped — and the particular lean of the second stone from the far bank, the one that rolled, the one that had broken Odo's cousin's ankle in '41.

On and on. Passes within passes. Ways through Thornfell that no living map recorded, because the maps were drawn by the king's surveyors and the surveyors never once, in a hundred years, followed the right man home.

When Tamsin opened their eyes, they were crying, which was annoying, and also standard. You always wept a little when something that large came into you. It wasn't grief exactly. It was more like standing in a cathedral and feeling small — except the cathedral was inside your own head, built out of another man's life.

"Well?" Odo whispered.

"They're in. All of it. Clean as a whistle." Tamsin wiped their face with their sleeve and managed a smile. "You counted your steps, all these years. Ninety-seven."

"Ninety-eight if the ice is thick." Odo's voice had gone very quiet, and his gaze had drifted to the ceiling, where the smoke curled above the fire. "Funny. It's still there. I thought it would feel like losing a tooth. But it's just... quiet. Like a room after the furniture's gone."

"Do you regret it?"

"No." He said it firmly, and then less firmly: "Ask me again in an hour, if I'm still here to be asked."

Tamsin stayed, as promised. They fed him broth spoonful by spoonful, propped him up when the coughing came, and talked when talking helped and kept silent when it didn't. Somewhere past midnight Odo began to talk about his mother, and Tamsin listened to every word and filed none of it away, because that wasn't the trade and never would be. Some things you heard and let pass through you like weather.

Near dawn, Odo gripped their wrist again.

"Brightwater."

"I'm here."

"You'll use them well, won't you. The routes. You won't sell them to the Spire just to be rid of a rival."

"On my life."

"That's what worries me." But he smiled, and the smile stayed on his face even as the rest of him went somewhere else entirely, sometime in the grey hour before sunrise, with the fire burned down to embers and the wind outside finally, briefly, still.

Tamsin sat with him a while anyway. It seemed rude to leave too quickly, even from an empty room.

* * *

They buried him under the cairn behind the hut, where two other cairns already stood — shepherds, probably, or other smugglers; on Thornfell the distinction had always been mostly one of paperwork. Tamsin stacked the stones properly, said the few words they knew, and left a sprig of rosemary on top, because old wives know things.

Then they shouldered their pack and walked down into the valley town of Dunmarrow, where the fences traded and the ale was bad and nobody asked anybody questions, which was the whole charm of the place.

Three days later, in the back room of Maret Pool's map shop — ostensibly a seller of charts to pilgrims, actually the busiest clearing-house for stolen knowledge on the western slopes — Tamsin named their price.

Maret Pool was a small round woman with ink-stained fingers and the moral flexibility of an eel, and she heard Tamsin out, and then she put down her pen and looked at them over her spectacles for a long moment.

"The Hollow Under Whistler's Tooth," she repeated.

"And the rest. Thirty years of Odo Ferrin's roads. Every crossing between the Spire's jurisdiction and the lowlands that isn't on any chart the king's men own."

"And Odo?"

"Dead. Buried Tuesday. His terms, freely given, witnessed by me and nobody else, because there was nobody else."

Maret whistled low. "There are buyers south of the border who'd pay in gold for that. Real gold, not the tin-and-promises kind. There are buyers in the *Spire* who'd pay more."

"We don't sell to the Spire."

"We being the royal we of your personal ethics, yes, I know, you've told me, it's very tiresome." Maret pulled a ledger toward herself and began to write. "I can move it in pieces. Routes to the caravans, one at a time, so the price holds. You'll eat for a year on this, Brightwater. Two years, if you're careful, and you're always careful, it's your single most irritating quality."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment." She finished writing and looked up, and her expression did the thing it occasionally did — softened, just slightly, around the eyes, like ice starting to sweat. "You know what I always wonder about you."

"Don't."

"Why you don't keep any of it. The good stuff. You lift a man's whole life of knowing and then you sell it off piece by piece until it's gone. You could *keep*. You could know everything anyone ever gave you, forever, a library in your skull. Instead you live like a courier. Everything passes through your hands and nothing stays."

Tamsin turned their cup on the table, considering the dregs.

Because it was a fair question, and they had a fair answer, and the answer was this: kept things grew heavy. Kept things accumulated. If you held on to every gift, every borrowed view from every borrowed life, you woke up one morning unable to remember which memories were yours, and then you were one of the hollow-eyed ones, muttering in strangers' childhoods. That was the practical reason.

The true reason was quieter. Everything Tamsin owned, they owned lightly, so that it could be left behind. Huts, towns, names, people — all of it rented, all of it temporary, all of it arranged so that when the day came — and it would come, it always came, the purge or the blade or the rockfall — there would be nothing to grieve and nowhere that knew them well enough to hurt.

It was a good system. Tamsin had run it for fifteen years without a single failure.

"It keeps the pack light," they said, and smiled, and Maret snorted and counted out the first payment, and neither of them believed it for a moment.

That night, at the inn at the crook of the river, Tamsin ate stew and drank small beer and let the new routes settle in their mind like sediment in a jar. They caught themselves tracing the Hollow Under Whistler's Tooth on the tabletop with a fingertip, forty body-lengths of remembered darkness, and realized they were smiling, and stopped.

An old man across the common room was teaching his granddaughter to play knucklebones, and cheating, badly, on purpose, and the girl shrieked with laughter every time, and Tamsin watched them for perhaps a quarter hour longer than a person with no hunger for connection reasonably would, and then paid their bill and went upstairs and did not think about it.

Alone, they checked the window latch, banked the candle, and reviewed the inventory of their life: one pack, one knife, one coat, coin enough for the season, and a head full of mountains that belonged to a dead man now, kept safe by a living one. Unattached. Unencumbered. Free as smoke.

The storm came up from the west around midnight — a proper one, thunder rolling down the passes like barrels in a stairwell, rain flinging itself against the shutters in fistfuls. Tamsin lay awake listening to it, professionally appreciative. Rain washed tracks. Rain emptied roads. Rain was a thief's best colleague, asking no share.

Which is why, when the knocking started, Tamsin was awake to hear it, and lay there frowning, because it was past two in the morning and no one honest knocked at that hour, and no one dishonest knocked at all — the dishonest ones simply came in.

Small knocks. Fists, but small ones. Patient, too: three soft strikes, a pause long enough to count to ten, three more.

Tamsin took the knife from under the mattress, crossed the cold floorboards, and opened the door onto rain and lightning and the empty street —

— and then looked down, because the knocking had come from below, and there on the doorstep, plastered gray with mud from the high trails where no road ran, stood a child. Six years old, perhaps seven. Barefoot. Shivering so hard the shivering looked like it might shake them apart. And looking up at Tamsin with eyes that caught the lightning-flash and held it a half-second too long, the way amber holds an insect.

The child opened their mouth and spoke, and the words meant nothing — a language of hard vowels and strange music, older than any kingdom, older than any chart in Maret Pool's shop — but the meaning arrived anyway, the way meanings sometimes did, directly and without permission:

*I knew you'd have a light.*

"Oh no," said Tamsin Brightwater, who had spent fifteen years making sure of it. "No, absolutely not."

But they were already stepping aside to let the child in.
