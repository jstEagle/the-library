## 2. A Knock at the Wrong Hour

The storm came up the Thornfell passes like a debt collector—no warning, no courtesy, and every intention of taking everything you had.

Tamsin Brightwater heard it coming an hour before it arrived, the way you hear a wagon with a bad axle two switchbacks down: a low grumble in the bones of the house, a rattle in the shutters they'd been meaning to fix since the thaw. They took the opportunity to do what any sensible person does before a storm. They banked the fire, latched the strongbox under the floorboards, ate the last of the smoked trout so it wouldn't go to waste, and settled into the chair by the window with a cup of barley tea and the deep, professional satisfaction of a person whose work was done.

Their work, this season, had gone well. *Well* being a relative term that meant nobody had died, nobody had noticed, and Tamsin had eaten meat four days out of seven. Three weeks ago they'd lifted a map out of the head of a dying smuggler named Corvin Hale—a cousin, as it happened, of Brannoc Hale, though the family resemblance ran to stubbornness rather than looks—and Corvin's hidden passes through the high Thornfells were now folded up safe in Tamsin's memory like letters from a lover who would never write again. There were eleven routes in there. Eleven ways over mountains that the king's own surveyors swore were impassable. Tamsin could walk them blindfolded now, in snow, in the dark, humming.

That was the trade. That was the gift, if you wanted to dress it up in church words, which Tamsin did not. Touch a brow, open the little door behind a person's eyes, and take what's inside. Memories don't weigh anything going out. The taker feels them whole—the smell of Corvin's mother's bread, the ache of his first broken bone, the particular blue of a lake he'd loved at fourteen—but the person left behind only feels the hole, smooth-edged and clean, like a tooth pulled under a good anesthetic. Most people never missed what Tamsin took. Maps. Routes. Faces of dead informants. The combination to a lock somebody no longer needed to open.

It was honest work, Tamsin told themselves, in the way that lying to yourself in a warm voice is still lying, but at least it keeps the tea hot.

They were on their second cup when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind of knocks a fist makes when the fist belongs to someone who has never knocked on a door in their life and is working it out from description.

Tamsin went very still, which is the first thing thieves learn and the last thing they forget. Nobody knocked at Tamsin's door. That was not an accident; that was architecture. The cottage sat half a mile off the smuggler road, tucked into a fold of the mountain where the pines grew thick enough to swallow smoke, and the three people who knew it existed knew better than to announce themselves. Brannoc whistled. Clients scratched twice and waited. Inquisitors, as far as anyone knew, kicked doors in with the full weight of divine law behind them, and made considerably more noise than three polite taps.

The knocks came again. Softer. Almost apologetic.

"Go away," Tamsin said to the door, because saying it out loud made it feel more like a decision and less like cowardice.

The wind answered instead, a long shriek down the chimney that put out half the fire, and through the gap in the shutters Tamsin saw the lightning go white over the ridge—and in that white flash, a shape at the edge of the yard. Small. Standing. Not moving, which was somehow worse than moving, because nothing stood still in weather like this unless it had forgotten how.

Tamsin told themselves several reasonable things. They told themselves it was a trick, that the Spire had gotten cleverer, that a child-shaped lure was exactly the sort of thing you'd design if you wanted a memory-thief to open her own door. They told themselves it wasn't their problem, that charity was a luxury for people with spare souls. They told themselves all of this while already pulling on their boots, because apparently the part of Tamsin that made decisions had stopped consulting the part that talked.

The cold hit like a slap from a stranger. Rain came sideways, hard enough to sting, and the lightning showed the yard in stutters: the woodpile, the leaning fence, and there—yes—a child. Mud-caked past recognition, hair plastered flat, wearing what might once have been a fine coat and now looked like something dredged from a riverbed. Bare feet. *Bare feet*, in this, on stone, and yet standing straight as a candle.

"Inside," Tamsin shouted over the wind, and the child turned its head—precisely, unhurriedly, like a weathervane finding north—and looked at them.

Tamsin had looked into a lot of eyes. It came with the trade; you learned to read people the way a cutpurse reads pockets, quick and shallow and without permission. These eyes were dark and enormous and utterly calm, and looking into them felt like looking down one of Corvin's hidden passes: a long way further than it should be, and something moving at the bottom.

The child walked toward them. Did not run. Walked, each step placed like a coin on a merchant's scale, and when it reached the doorstep it stopped, tilted its head back, and spoke.

The sound was clear despite the storm—clear the way a bell is clear—and it was language. Tamsin's ear, trained on a dozen border tongues and three kinds of thieves' cant, reached for it and found nothing to hold. The vowels were old. Not *rustic*-old, not shepherd's-dialect-old, but old the way bedrock is old. Each word landed with a weight that seemed out of proportion to a child's mouth, and the cadence rose and fell like something recited, or remembered aloud.

"I don't understand you," Tamsin said, uselessly, in common speech, and then, because the night was insane anyway, added in Low Cant, then in the river-trade pidgin, then in the formal court Vellish they'd stolen off a diplomat's mind two winters back and never had occasion to use.

At the last one, the child's face changed. Not understanding—recognition. The way you look at a song your grandmother used to hum. It said something else in the old tongue, softer, and held out one mud-gray hand, palm up, in a gesture that crossed every language Tamsin knew: *help me.*

"Oh, no," Tamsin said, to the storm, to the child, to whatever god handled emergencies involving barefoot children who spoke in bedrock vowels. "No, absolutely not. I am not a good person. I want to be extremely clear about that. People who come to my door end up lighter by one memory and none the wiser, and you cannot possibly afford—"

Lightning cracked the sky open directly overhead, close enough that the thunder arrived inside the same heartbeat, and the child swayed, and its knees buckled, and Tamsin caught it without deciding to.

It weighed almost nothing. It was burning hot—fever-hot, wrong-hot, heat pouring off it like a banked forge—and beneath the mud its skin had gone gray at the edges. Whatever it had walked through to get here, it had walked through too much of it.

And Tamsin, holding a dying stranger on their own doorstep, did the thing that twenty years of trade had worn into their hands deeper than any scar.

They touched the child's brow.

* * *

Later—much later, when the fire was rebuilt and the child slept and Tamsin sat across the room drinking tea that had gone cold twice without noticing—they would try to explain it to themselves, and fail, and try again.

Taking a memory was supposed to feel like lifting a purse. A small resistance, a give, a weight settling into you. You chose the drawer. You chose the shape. You were the thief; the mind was the house; you left by the same window you came in.

There was no choosing here.

The moment skin met skin, the door behind the child's eyes didn't open—it *came off its hinges*, and the house behind it turned out to have no walls at all, and Tamsin fell in.

Fire. That was the first thing, and the last thing, and the thing underneath everything: fire, but not wildfire, not the friendly orange chaos of hearth and torch. This was fire with *grammar*. Fire that moved in patterns, that rose in spirals around towers of pale stone, that hung in the air in written lines like script made of light. A city built in a bowl of mountains—white spires, terraced gardens, bridges like drawn bows—and above it, wheeling on wings wide as sails, shapes that Tamsin's mind refused for three full seconds to name, because naming them meant admitting the world was a different shape than advertised.

Dragons. Dragons over a city called—

*Vael Auren*, said a voice that was not a voice, that was memory speaking itself in the old tongue while some deeper layer translated, the way you can understand a dream without knowing how. *Vael Auren, the sanctuary, the choir of the spires, the peace that is kept—*

Peace. Kept. By them. By fire given willingly, bound into law, woven between kingdoms like mortar between stones, and Tamsin saw it from above, from a balcony, from a height where the whole world lay spread out like a map that breathed—and the balcony belonged to someone small, someone whose hand fit inside a larger hand, someone who was called—

*Heir.*

The word went through Tamsin like a spear of ice.

Then the images began to burn. That was the difference, Tamsin realized dimly, from very far away, drowning: this was not theft. Theft was cool water. This was a furnace with the door blown open, memories arriving not as pictures but as *temperature*, grief and glory and terror all at white heat, centuries of it compressed into seconds, and somewhere in the middle of it Tamsin understood—with the calm clarity of a person watching their own cart go over a cliff—that they were not robbing this child.

This child's memories were robbing *them*.

They tore their hand away. Actually tore it, flesh peeling off fevered skin with a sound like ripping cloth, and staggered backward into the doorframe, and the connection snapped, and the world reassembled itself around the cottage: rain, firelight, the smell of wet wool and woodsmoke, their own ragged breathing.

The child lay on the doorstep where it had fallen. Still breathing. Still burning.

Tamsin stood in the doorway with their heart slamming and their mind ringing like a struck bell, and did inventory, because inventory was the rope they climbed down when everything else failed. Hands: shaking. Legs: functional. Memory: intact, mostly, though the edges of tonight already felt polished smooth, like a stone the flood had gotten to. And underneath everything, coiled where the stolen things usually went, a handful of fragments that had come back with them uninvited: a spiral of golden fire. A white tower reflected in still water. A voice, warm and enormous, saying *Heir* the way you say *beloved*.

"Well," Tamsin said aloud, to the empty doorway, in a voice that came out about an octave higher than usual. "That's new."

They got the child inside. There was never really a question; there was only the interval during which Tamsin pretended there was. They hauled the small body to the hearth, stripped off the ruined coat, rubbed warmth into gray limbs, propped the head on a folded blanket. Up close, cleaned of mud, the child resolved into specifics: maybe eight years old, maybe ten, features from nowhere Tamsin could place—not Vellish, not Thornborn, not lowland stock. A face like the city in the vision: familiar in a way that suggested you'd seen it in a dream and forgotten on waking.

The child's eyes opened halfway. Found Tamsin's face. And smiled—a small, exhausted, courteous smile, the smile of a guest apologizing for the hour—and murmured something in the old tongue, two soft syllables, and slept.

Tamsin sat back on their heels and stared.

Here was the trouble, and Tamsin saw it with perfect professional clarity, the way you see the exact flaw in a lock right after your fingers are caught in it. Every mind Tamsin had ever touched had been a house. Rooms, drawers, locks, windows. Some houses were palaces and some were hovels, but they were all houses, and houses could be entered quietly and left cleanly. That was the whole trade. That was the whole *world*, as far as Tamsin had ever needed it to be.

This was not a house. This was a well with no bottom, and something at the bottom of it had looked back up.

And the fragments sitting in Tamsin's memory like coals raked from a fire—those hadn't been taken. Tamsin knew taking. Taking had a texture, a weight, a clean seam where the theft had been made. These had *followed* them home. Clung. Burned. As if the child's mind had handed them over deliberately, the way you thrust a bundle at a stranger and run: *take this, keep it, I can't carry it anymore.*

"Heir," Tamsin said quietly, testing the word in the empty room. It sounded absurd. It sounded like a story grandmothers told, like the ballads sung in lowland taverns about the Dragon Wars, the old peace, the fire that had kept kings honest until it burned out and the wars came. Everyone knew those stories. Everyone also knew they were stories, the way everyone knows the mountains are impassable.

The rain hammered the roof. The child breathed, slow and even now, the fever breaking by degrees, and in the firelight their mud-streaked face looked, for a moment, unbearably young.

Tamsin should wake at dawn, walk the child to the shrine road at Grayford, leave them with the alms-box and the kindly sister who asked no questions, and be home by noon with the whole affair folded up and buried. That was the plan. That was obviously the plan. Tamsin Brightwater did not keep strays; keeping things was how you got caught, and getting caught was how you ended up on the Spire's long lists, and Tamsin intended to die old, rich, and entirely unremarkable, in that order.

But the child shifted in sleep and said something else—dreaming now, deep down, in that bedrock tongue—and the two syllables had the shape of a name, and the tone had the shape of a question, and Tamsin, who had spent twenty years taking other people's most precious things and telling themselves it cost nothing, sat by the fire all night with a stranger's hand curled loosely around their finger, and did not sleep at all.

Near morning, the fever broke. The child woke, looked around the strange room with those bottomless dark eyes, and then looked at Tamsin—looked, Tamsin thought, *through* them, checking something—and nodded once, gravely, as if a decision had been confirmed.

Then, in a voice rusty from disuse, the child said a single word of common speech. One word, carefully shaped, chosen from somewhere:

"Hungry."

Tamsin laughed. It startled them both—the sound bouncing off the rafters of a house that had heard no laughter in years—and they got up, and put the kettle on, and tried very hard not to think about the white towers, or the wings against the storm-light, or the fact that somewhere out in the rain-soaked dark, someone or something had driven a child over impassable mountains to this particular door.

They were ladling porridge when the thought arrived, fully formed and unwelcome, the way the worst thoughts always do:

*If I could find that child's memories again—if I could learn to hold them without burning—there isn't a lock in the world I couldn't open. There isn't a price I couldn't name.*

Tamsin set down the ladle, appalled at themselves, and lied to the ceiling in a warm voice: *never, absolutely not, perish the thought.*

Outside, far down the valley, thin as a needle through the rain, a bell began to ring in the market town of Grayford. Not the dawn bell. Not any bell Tamsin knew.

Three strokes. A pause. Three more.

The Spire's summons, the smugglers called it, when they bothered to call it anything at all. It meant the grey cloaks were asking questions.

The child watched Tamsin's face go still, and tightened small muddy fingers around the spoon, and said—in the old tongue, softly, with the calm of someone reporting weather—"They are coming."
