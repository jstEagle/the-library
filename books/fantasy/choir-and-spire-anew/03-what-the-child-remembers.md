## 3. What the Child Remembers

The child slept in Tamsin's bed, and Tamsin sat on the floor beside it with a candle burning low and a thief's hands folded in their lap like a penitent's.

They had not meant to look. That was the thing they kept telling themselves, over and over, the way a person repeats a lie until it develops the texture of truth. *I didn't mean to look.* But when they had touched the child's brow—just to check for fever, just that, nothing more—the memories had come pouring into them like water through a broken dam, and now here they were at midnight, sitting on their own floorboards, unable to stop turning the fragments over. Like coins. Like stolen silver.

Tamsin Brightwater had been a memory-thief for eleven years. They knew every flavor a mind could hold. Grief tasted of iron and cold tea. Joy was honey cut with something sharp, almost citrus. Fear was brine and smoke, and after eleven years Tamsin could sort a stranger's memories by taste alone, the way a spice merchant sorts his wares blindfolded. They had lifted the faces of dead mothers from sons who could no longer bear to carry them. They had taken first kisses from soldiers who'd rather forget the war that came after. They had once, memorably, stolen an entire wedding from a bride who discovered mid-ceremony that her groom had already been married twice, and sold it back to her sister, who thought it was the funniest thing she'd ever owned.

None of it—not one stolen thing in eleven years—had prepared them for what lived inside this mud-caked, silent child.

* * *

It started, as these things did, with a door.

Not a real door. A memory-door, the kind Tamsin's mind built automatically whenever borrowed recollections arrived, so that everything had a shape and a place and could be sorted into the great cluttered house of things Tamsin knew but hadn't lived. Most people's doors were modest affairs. Brannoc Hale's worst memory came through a cellar hatch, all damp stone and darkness. The smuggler's passes had arrived through a crooked stable gate, and smelled of hay and horse sweat even now, years later, whenever Tamsin went looking for them.

The child's memory came through a door of white stone taller than any building in Thornfell, carved all over with wings, and it was standing open, and behind it there was fire.

Tamsin had seen fire before. Everyone had. They'd watched a granary burn in Lowmarket as a boy, standing in a crowd that made the particular low sound crowds make when fire is beautiful and no one has died yet. They knew fire the way everyone knows it: warm, hungry, orange, ordinary.

This was not that fire.

This fire was *gold*. Not orange-gold, not sunset-gold, but the gold of something alive, the way blood is red because it is moving. It poured down the sides of towers—towers, Tamsin realized, that made the Spire itself look like a chimney stack—and where it touched, stone did not blacken. It *bloomed*. Walls flowered with light. Roofs caught and burned and the burning looked, impossibly, like celebration.

And above the city, wheeling on wings wide enough to shadow whole streets, were dragons.

Tamsin had heard of dragons the way they'd heard of the old kings: as decoration. As weathervanes and tavern signs and the embroidered beasts on inquisitors' banners. Dead things. Story things. Things that belonged to grandmothers' tales and to the margins of old maps marked *here be monsters*, which every cartographer knew really meant *here be nothing, we got bored*.

But the dragons in the child's memory were not decorations. They were *busy*. One swept low over a harbor crowded with ships whose sails bore sigils Tamsin didn't recognize—sigils from kingdoms that no longer existed, kingdoms that had been old before the Spire was young. Another perched on a tower's crown, folding its wings with the fussy precision of a merchant closing shop, and the tower beneath it glowed gently, the way a hearthstone holds warmth long after the fire dies down.

The dragons weren't burning the city.

The dragons were *keeping* it.

Tamsin understood this the way you understand things in dreams—suddenly, completely, without any of the steps in between. The fire pouring down the towers wasn't destruction. It was maintenance. It was the city's heating and its lighting and its law, all of it, flowing from creatures the size of cathedrals who moved through the sky with the unhurried confidence of things that have never once needed to explain themselves.

A voice spoke somewhere in the memory. Deep, resonant, the kind of voice that comes from a chest bigger than a wagon. It said a word Tamsin didn't know—a long word, syllables rolling like stones down a hill—and the word settled into Tamsin's mind and refused to be translated. But the meaning came along anyway, the way meaning does, riding underneath the sound:

*Heir.*

The voice was calling the child *Heir*.

Tamsin sat very still on the floorboards. The candle guttered. In the bed, the child breathed slow and even, mouth slightly open, one hand curled around the carved wooden dragon Tamsin had found clutched in their fist the night of the storm—a crude little thing, whittled by some mountain craftsman, worth perhaps two coppers, and held the way other children hold blankets.

"Heir," Tamsin whispered to the empty room, testing it. "Heir to *what*?"

The memory answered by opening another door.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing memories: you never get the whole thing. You get edges. Corners. Fragments, the way you get fragments when a plate shatters—one piece showing half a face, another showing a pattern of flowers, and your mind doing the rest, filling in the gaps with guesswork and calling the result knowledge.

Tamsin had always been good at the guesswork. Eleven years of practice. They could take three shards of a man's childhood and reconstruct his entire village street, right down to which shutter hung crooked. It was the part of the trade they were actually proud of, though pride was a dangerous thing to carry in their line of work, so mostly they called it *competence* and left it at that.

But the child's memories defeated the guesswork entirely, because the shards were too big.

There was a fragment of a hall so vast that Tamsin's mind couldn't find its ceiling—it simply tapered upward into golden haze, and the haze was full of drifting motes that might have been dust or might have been embers or might, Tamsin suspected with a lurch, have been *names*, thousands upon thousands of names, hanging in the air of that hall like snow that had decided to fall upward instead of down.

There was a fragment of hands—small hands, a child's hands, the same small hands currently curled around a two-copper dragon—pressing themselves flat against warm stone while something enormous breathed nearby. Each breath was a wind. Each exhale carried a smell Tamsin had no name for: hot metal and rain and something sweet, like the air over a baker's roof, if bakers worked with lightning.

There was a fragment of a garden growing on a rooftop, actual soil, hundreds of feet up, where an old woman with a face like crumpled linen taught a class of children to press seeds into the earth while dragons wheeled overhead and their shadows passed over the little green rows like slow clouds.

And threaded through all of it, everywhere, in every fragment, was the word. The untranslatable word. Sometimes spoken, sometimes sung, sometimes written in letters of gold fire across stone that bloomed wherever the letters touched. And always, always, attached to the child. Pointed at the child like a finger. *Heir. Heir. Heir.*

Tamsin got up quietly, went to the shelf above the hearth, and took down the bottle of plum brandy they saved for occasions that required forgetting. They poured two fingers and drank them standing up, and then, because the occasion clearly demanded it, poured two more.

"Right," they told the room. "Let's be sensible about this."

Sensible, in Tamsin's experience, meant making lists. They found charcoal and the back of a bill of sale and sat down at the table, and the child slept on, undisturbed, and Tamsin wrote:

*Things I know about the child:*

*Mute. Or near enough. Speaks a language no one uses—no one LIVING uses, said the fence, said everyone, and everyone was wrong, because someone used it, someone used it for centuries, whole cities full of someones, and where did they GO?*

*Has memories of a city that makes the Spire look like a candle stub.*

*Those memories are OLD. Older than anything. The sigils are wrong. The kingdoms are wrong. The roads in the background of one fragment lead to places that aren't on any map I've ever stolen, and I've stolen maps from the Spire's own cartography hall.*

*Something calls this child HEIR.*

Tamsin stared at the list for a long time. Then, because honesty was a habit they usually managed to avoid, they added a fifth line:

*I am in trouble.*

* * *

The trouble, specifically, was this: Tamsin Brightwater had spent eleven years building a life out of smallness. Small thefts. Small trades. Memories that fit in a pocket, weighed almost nothing, and could be handed over a market stall with a smile and a handshake. A dying man's last sight of his father's orchard: four silver. A woman's memory of her own wedding dance, sold to her widowed sister: six. This was the trade, this was the scale, and the scale was the point. Small things attracted small attention. Nobody sent inquisitors after a wedding dance.

But the child sleeping in Tamsin's bed was carrying around memories of a lost city the way other children carried around snails and interesting rocks. Casually. In both hands. Offering them to anyone who touched their brow.

And Tamsin had touched the brow. Tamsin had *looked*.

Which meant that somewhere in the deep cluttered house of Tamsin's mind, behind a door of white stone carved all over with wings, there now lived a fragment of Vael Auren.

That was the name. It surfaced sometime past the second brandy, rising out of the fragments like a fish rising to feed. *Vael Auren.* The city had a name, and the name had come away on Tamsin's hands like grease from a stolen pie, and now Tamsin knew it, and knowing it felt less like possession and more like being possessed.

Because here was the other thing nobody tells you about memory-thieving: stolen memories don't sit still. They root. They send out feelers into the rest of your mind and start connecting themselves to things. Tamsin had once stolen a sailor's memory of a storm and spent the following month unable to hear rain on the roof without tasting salt. The Vael Auren fragments were doing worse than that. All day—as Tamsin chopped onions, as they haggled over eggs, as they mended a tear in their good coat—they could feel the fragments threading themselves in. The smell of hot metal and rain kept arriving uninvited. The drifting names in the golden hall kept brushing against Tamsin's own memories of the Thornfell markets, comparing, cataloguing, finding Tamsin's whole world *small*.

That was the part that frightened them most, if they were honest, and Tamsin was honest approximately once a decade whether they needed to be or not. Not the dragons. Not the fire. Not even the word *Heir*, though every instinct they had was screaming that *Heir* meant *wanted*, and wanted-by-whom was a question with inquisitors on one end of the answer.

No. What frightened Tamsin was that the fragments made their own life look like a footnote.

Eleven years of careful, competent, small-scale survival, and here was a child who had *lived*—or been part of something, or remembered being part of something—that dwarfed it. A city of flowering towers. Dragons keeping the peace the way Tamsin kept their coin purse: close, constant, and with teeth. And the child remembered it the way Tamsin remembered their mother's kitchen, which is to say: not as history. As home.

"Who are you?" Tamsin asked the sleeping child softly. "What happened to you? Where did the city go?"

The child didn't answer. Children rarely answer questions asked while they sleep, and mute ones answer them never, and Tamsin hadn't actually expected anything. But as they leaned over the bed—just to pull the blanket up, just that, nothing more, they were developing quite a collection of *just thats*—the child stirred, and frowned, and in their sleep said something.

A single word. Long, rolling, stone-down-a-hill syllables.

The word.

Tamsin went cold from the boots up. Because they recognized it now—they'd know it anywhere, it had been living rent-free in their skull since midnight—and the child had said it in their sleep, unprompted, to no one, the way you say a name you're dreaming of.

Except the word wasn't a name. Tamsin was almost sure it wasn't a name. It was a *title*, or a *role*, or something else entirely, something with no common-tongue equivalent, and the child had said it about themselves, asleep, and the sound of it in the dark little room was nothing like a child's babble. It sounded like a bell being struck. It sounded like a key turning in a lock older than locks.

Tamsin sat back down on the floor. Drank the rest of the brandy straight from the bottle, which they despised doing, because it was the act of a man without discipline, and discipline was the only thing separating Tamsin Brightwater from the drunks and fools they'd climbed over to get where they were.

Where they were. Which was: on the floor of their own house, drunk on plum brandy, guarding a sleeping child who dreamed in a dead language about a living fire, holding memories in their head that no fence in Thornfell would touch and no inquisitor would let them keep.

"Small things," Tamsin muttered to the bottle. "I steal small things. That's the whole trick. That's the entire career."

The child sighed in their sleep and turned over, and the carved dragon tumbled from their hand onto the mattress, and lay there in the candlelight with its crude wings and its two-copper face, looking, Tamsin thought, suddenly and absurdly valuable. Like a key someone had disguised as a toy.

They should take it to the fence. That was the sensible move. Show Merrick the carving, describe the memories without demonstrating them, feel out what the trade might bear. Merrick knew everyone. Merrick would know what *Vael Auren* meant, if it meant anything, if it wasn't just a dream-name, a nonsense-word, a child's invention. And if it *was* something—if there were buyers, collectors, scholars, anyone who paid for the past—then maybe this could still be handled at Tamsin's scale. Small trades. Pocket-sized pieces. A fragment here, a fragment there, sold carefully to people who asked no questions.

Tamsin looked at the sleeping child and felt the plan curdle in their chest before it fully formed. Because selling fragments meant letting buyers touch the brow. It meant strangers reaching into that small sleeping head and helping themselves to whatever they found. It meant treating the child exactly the way Tamsin had treated every dying smuggler and grieving soldier and betrayed bride for eleven years: as inventory.

And the child had come to their door in a storm. Had stood there in the mud and the rain, small and shaking, and knocked. Of all the houses on all the mountain roads, the child had knocked on the memory-thief's door, and Tamsin had taken them in for the oldest and least defensible of reasons: because the alternative was leaving them in the rain.

"You're going to be trouble," Tamsin informed the child, who slept on, unbothered, breathing slow, dreaming in gold. "You understand that. Whatever you are—and I don't know what you are, and I'm starting to suspect *you* don't entirely know what you are—you are going to be more trouble than I have ever been in, and I have been in trouble with the Spire, the smugglers, and a toll-warden named Doss who still owes me money."

The candle had nearly burned out. Dawn was a grey promise at the window's edge. Tamsin finished the bottle, regretted it, wrote one last line on the back of the bill of sale—

*Do NOT touch the child's brow again. (You will touch the child's brow again.)*

—folded it up, and tucked it under the inkpot where they kept all their resolutions.

Then they sat on the floor until sunrise, watching the child sleep, listening to the wind come down off Thornfell, and trying very hard not to think about the fact that somewhere out there, in the wide world beyond the mountains, there were people who knew the word *Vael Auren*. People who had reasons to know it. People who might be looking for a mud-caked child who spoke it in their sleep.

The morning market bell rang across the valley, clear and stupid and ordinary, and Tamsin got up, put the kettle on, and began assembling the lie they'd tell the neighbors: a cousin's orphan, passing through, staying the week. Nothing unusual. Nothing memorable.

They were halfway through frying eggs when the child woke, looked at Tamsin with those dark serious eyes, and pointed—at the window, at the mountains beyond it, at the high passes where the snow still lingered—and made a sound. Not the dead-tongue word. Something else. A short, urgent noise, half hum and half cry, accompanied by a gesture Tamsin's eleven years of reading strangers understood instantly, because it was the same gesture every desperate traveler made when they were trying to say *that way, please, I have to go that way.*

The child wanted the mountains. The deep ones. The high ones, past the last farms, where the passes narrowed and the old roads gave out.

Tamsin turned the eggs, and thought about the fragment of the vast hall with its drifting golden names, and about how the child had pressed their small hands against warm stone while something enormous breathed.

"Well," they said to the eggs, conversationally. "Isn't that interesting."

And then, because the universe had a sense of humor and Tamsin had long ago stopped pretending otherwise, there came a knock at the front door. Firm. Official. The knock of someone accustomed to having doors opened for them.

Through the morning fog, drifting up from the market town below, Tamsin could hear voices. Many voices. And one of them, clipped and cold as a blade laid flat against stone, was asking the neighbors a question that carried all the way up the hill:

"—a vessel of the old tongue. Short. Dark-haired. Answers to nothing. Have you seen such a child?"

Tamsin turned off the stove.

"Well," they said again, to nobody, to the eggs, to the carved dragon lying on the pillow like a sleeping key. "*Trouble.*"

They picked up the child's small hand, and the child gripped their fingers without hesitation, without fear, as if this had been arranged long ago by powers neither of them had consulted—and Tamsin reached for the back door, and the pack by the chimney, and the eleven years of careful smallness they could feel beginning, right at that moment, to end.
