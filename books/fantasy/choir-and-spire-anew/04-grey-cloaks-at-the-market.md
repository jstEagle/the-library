## 4. Grey Cloaks at the Market

The trouble with small towns, Tamsin had always maintained, was that everybody knew your business. The advantage of small towns was that everybody knew *everybody's* business, which meant that when a stranger came asking questions, the whole of Marrowgate lit up like a lamp with gossip before the stranger had finished their first cup of cider.

Tamsin had counted on that. They had not counted on how fast the light could turn.

They came down off the hill two days after the storm, Wren bundled in an oversized coat with the hood up and a story prepared—nephew, come to stay, shy, don't ask questions—and the market square was already wearing grey.

Not weather-grey. *Cloak*-grey. Six of them, maybe seven, moving through the stalls in pairs with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been charged full price for anything. Spire grey. Tamsin knew it from a distance the way a rabbit knows a hawk-shaped shadow: something old and animal in the spine said *down, be still, be nothing.*

"Stay by me," Tamsin murmured. "And if anyone asks, you're my sister's boy, and she's dead, and I'm sad about it, so don't make me do the face."

Wren looked up at them. In the grey morning light, under the hood, those dark eyes were doing the thing they did sometimes—not blank exactly, but *elsewhere*, like windows in a house where all the furniture had been covered in sheets. Then the child nodded once, solemn as a magistrate, and took hold of the hem of Tamsin's coat with two fingers, the way you'd hold a rope over a long drop.

*Well,* thought Tamsin. *That's either very sweet or very ominous.*

The market smelled of wet wool and frying onions and the particular sourness of fear that Tamsin had learned to read the way other people read weather. It was coming off the stallholders in waves. Old Marta at the cheese stand wasn't haggling. That alone told Tamsin everything. Marta would haggle with a fever, with a funeral, with the actual hand of death on her shoulder. Marta not haggling meant Marta was scared, and Marta scared meant everyone was.

"—a vessel of the old tongue," one of the grey cloaks was saying, not loudly, in the carrying way of someone who wants to be overheard. He stood at the center of the square like a nail driven into wood. Younger than Tamsin expected. Clean-shaven. A voice like a well—deep, and you couldn't see the bottom. "It speaks no language of the kingdoms. It may travel with a thief. Have you seen such a child?"

*A vessel.* Tamsin turned that word over while pretending to examine a basket of apples with tremendous scholarly interest. Not a child. Not a refugee. A *vessel*. As if the thing inside mattered and the outside was just packaging.

Beside them, Wren went very still. Not frightened-still. Still the way water goes still before it freezes.

"It answers to nothing," the inquisitor continued. "But it understands more than it says. The Spire offers gratitude for information. And for those who have—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table, "—*complications* in their history, the Spire offers something better than gratitude."

Amnesty.

Tamsin didn't look around. Didn't need to. They could feel the word move through the crowd like oil through cloth, touching every person in Marrowgate who had ever smuggled a keg past a toll, ever forged a stamp, ever sold what shouldn't be sold. Which was, in Marrowgate, approximately everyone, including the priest, including possibly the priest's dog.

Including, Tamsin thought with a slow cold settling in the stomach, *Dulcet.*

* *

Dulcet Amberly kept her shop behind the chandler's, down an alley that smelled of tallow and secrets, and she was the closest thing Tamsin Brightwater had to a friend, which is a sentence that should tell you most of what you need to know about Tamsin Brightwater's life.

She bought stolen memories the way other merchants bought wool: by weight, by quality, by season. She'd taught Tamsin the trade's finer points years ago—how to spot a memory that had already been copied, how to price grief versus joy (grief held its value; joy spoiled), how to smile at customers you'd happily see drowned. She called Tamsin "*little magpie*" and cheated them only slightly, and Tamsin had always found this, in their line of work, to be the definition of love.

The bell over her door gave its cracked little cough as Tamsin slipped inside, leaving Wren outside in the alley with strict instructions and a honey-cake as a bribe.

Dulcet looked up from her ledger. She was sixty-some, sharp-faced, dyed-red hair pinned like a fortification. Her eyes went to Tamsin, then past them, to the door, then back.

"You're pale, magpie," she said.

"Grey cloaks in the square."

"Mm." She dipped her pen. "Since yesterday. Asking their questions. Offering their amnesty." The pen scratched. Scratched. Stopped. "You know, they described the child *very* precisely. Height, hair, the shape of the face." Scratch. "Almost as though someone had told them."

The shop went very quiet. Somewhere out in the alley, Wren hummed three notes of something in the old tongue, soft and tuneless, and stopped themselves.

"Dulcet." Tamsin kept their voice level, pleasant, the voice for closing deals. "Tell me you didn't."

"Didn't *what?*" She set the pen down and folded her hands, and there it was—the look Tamsin had seen on a hundred faces across a hundred bargaining tables, the look of a person arranging their conscience into a shape they could live in. "I've got a daughter in Lowmark with debts, magpie. Debts with teeth. The Spire man says one name from me and her ledger burns." She shrugged, and the shrug was almost apologetic, and that was somehow worse than if it hadn't been. "You'd have done the same. You *have* done the same. You made your living doing the same to people who trusted you."

"That's different."

"How?"

And there was the trap, laid neat as anything, because Tamsin opened their mouth to answer and found—nothing. No difference that would survive being spoken aloud. They stole from people who trusted them. It was the whole architecture of their life. They'd simply never been on the inside of the theft before, never been the memory lifted out of a pocket, never felt the strange cold absence where something used to be.

"They'll take the child to the Spire," Tamsin said finally. "You know what happens to things the Spire takes. There's no daughter-shaped debt worth that."

"There's no debt I wouldn't pay for my daughter," Dulcet said simply. "I'm sorry, little magpie. Truly. Take the back way out. You've got until dark—that's when they come back through. I told them the child passed north with a tinker." She picked her pen back up. "Consider it interest on eleven years of friendship."

"*Friendship,*" Tamsin repeated, tasting the word like a coin bitten for gold.

"What else would you call it?" Dulcet asked, genuinely curious, and Tamsin had no answer for that either, and left without one.

Outside, Wren was sitting on an overturned crate, methodically eating the honey-cake, watching the alley mouth with the patience of a cat at a mousehole. When Tamsin appeared, the child stood, brushed crumbs from the enormous coat, and pointed—pointed with total certainty—at the far end of the alley, away from the market, toward the goat-track that climbed behind the tannery.

"No," Tamsin said. "We can't go that way. That's the high passes. Nobody crosses Thornfell in early thaw. There are routes up there nobody's used in twenty years, and half of them are landslides now, and the other half are—"

Wren pointed again. Same direction. Then, slowly, the child raised one small hand and drew a shape in the air: a curve, a switchback, another curve. Three turns of a path. Then tapped their own chest twice.

Tamsin stared.

Because they *knew* that path. Knew it intimately, had walked it in borrowed memory a dozen times—a dying smuggler's last recollection of the Shepherd's Stair, the hidden route above the treeline, three switchbacks and a shelf of stone. It was the single most valuable thing in Tamsin's head, and this child had just drawn it in the air like a sketch on fogged glass.

"You've never been here," Tamsin said slowly. "You've never been *anywhere* here. So how do you—"

Wren shrugged. It was a small, terrible shrug, the shrug of someone for whom impossible things had stopped being interesting.

The bell of the chandler's shop next door coughed. Voices in the alley mouth—two men, low-voiced, working their way along the backs of the shops, checking doors. Grey, at the edge of vision.

Tamsin made a decision the way they made all their decisions: too fast, with too much feeling, and dressed up afterward as calculation.

"All right," they breathed, taking Wren's hand. "Up the goat-track. Stay behind me, step where I step, and if I say *sheep*, you drop and don't move till I say *market*. Understood?"

Wren nodded. Then, at the mouth of the track, glancing back once at the town—the roofs, the smoke, the grey cloaks moving between them like crows walking a fence—the child squeezed Tamsin's fingers. Hard. Deliberately. Twice.

It wasn't a word. It wasn't any language Tamsin knew. But it didn't need translating. Some things are older than language, and *don't leave me* is one of them.

Something moved in Tamsin's chest, unwelcome and warm, like a coal rolling loose in a grate.

*Don't,* they told themselves sternly, climbing. *Don't you dare get attached. Attachment is just inventory you can't sell.*

They climbed anyway, hand in hand with a child who knew mountains they'd never seen, and tried very hard not to notice that they were already lying to themselves, and doing it badly.

* *

They made the treeline by dusk, and the first switchback by full dark, and camped in the lee of a boulder with a view of Marrowgate's lights scattered below like coins dropped on black cloth.

Tamsin built a small fire—small enough to cup a hand over—and shared out dried plums and the heel of a loaf. Wren ate like a bird, quick precise bites, eyes always moving. Watching the pass below. Watching the dark.

"They'll send someone up after us," Tamsin said, mostly to the fire. "Inquisitors don't climb, but they hire people who do. Smugglers. Guides." They chewed. Swallowed. "There's a man I might have to talk to tomorrow. Brannoc Hale. He owes me a favor so large it has its own gravity. If anyone can get us over the Shepherd's Stair ahead of whatever's following, it's him."

Wren looked at them across the fire. Then the child did something new: reached out, tapped Tamsin's chest—right over the heart, matter-of-fact, the way you'd tap a barrel to hear if it was full—and then pointed down the mountain, toward the town, toward the grey cloaks, and shook their head slowly.

"No," Tamsin translated. "Don't trust them. Don't trust anyone down there."

Wren nodded.

"I *know*," Tamsin said, stung. "I invented not trusting people. I wrote the book. There's a chapter on fences." They poked the fire harder than it deserved. "Dulcet was different. Eleven years. She taught me pricing. She named me—well, she named my *name*, the one I use. You don't give somebody their name and then sell them."

Wren tilted their head. And then, with the gentle mercilessness of children everywhere, the child pointed at Tamsin, then mimed—clearly, elaborately—lifting something invisible from their own open palm, examining it, and handing it over to an imaginary stranger. Then pointed at Dulcet's shop below. Then raised an eyebrow.

"That's not—" Tamsin started. Stopped. Started again. "That is *entirely* beside the point."

Wren smiled. It was the first real smile Tamsin had seen from them—crooked, one-sided, suddenly about eight years old instead of ancient—and it landed somewhere under Tamsin's ribs and stayed there, glowing, inconvenient, like a coin too hot to hold and too pretty to drop.

Below them, far down the black shoulder of the mountain, a light appeared on the goat-track. Small. Moving. Climbing.

Then a second one, flanking wide through the trees.

Tamsin put out the fire with one practiced motion and pulled Wren back against the boulder, into shadow, and watched the lights pick their way upward with the unhurried confidence of hunters who knew their prey had a child's legs and a thief's head start and not much else.

"Sheep," Tamsin whispered, and felt the small body go still against theirs.

The lights climbed. And climbed. And stopped—perhaps a mile below, perhaps less—where the trail forked, and one of them swung in a slow deliberate arc, sweeping the slope, the boulders, the dark. Searching. Patient as winter.

Tamsin held the child and did arithmetic in the dark. One route over the Stair, known only to a dead man's memory and, impossibly, to the small warm weight breathing against their side. One ally down in the valley who owed them everything. Two hunters on the trail tonight, and more behind them, and behind *those*, the Spire itself, patient as stone.

"Tomorrow," Tamsin breathed into Wren's hair, "we go see a man about a debt."

Far below, the second light went out.

The first one began to climb again.
