## 5. The Smuggler's Debt

Brannoc Hale lived at the bottom of a goat track that was less a road than an apology for one, in a stone house that leaned east like a drunk listening for bells. Tamsin had known him eleven years, and in all that time the house had leaned a little further every winter, and Brannoc had never once propped it up.

"He's stubborn," people said.

"It's not the house," Tamsin would reply. "It's company. Everything else he owns has already fallen down."

They climbed the track with Wren on their back, because the child had gone quiet and heavy-footed since the market, and Tamsin had learned in four days of flight that silence in Wren was not calm. It was weather gathering. The grey cloaks were still in Millbrook—Tamsin had watched three of them turn over a tinker's cart at dawn with the patient thoroughness of men looking for teeth in a fish—and after Millbrook there was nowhere left to go but up, and nobody left to ask except the one man Tamsin owed and who owed them back.

The debt between them was old and strange and neither of them spoke of it directly, which was how debts stayed healthy in Thornfell.

Wren stirred as they reached the gate. The child's fingers tightened in Tamsin's collar—a question without words.

"Friend," Tamsin said. "Of a sort." Then, honestly, because lying to Wren had begun to feel like kicking a deerhound: "Of exactly a sort."

* * *

Brannoc answered the door with a crossbow and lowered it with theatrical disappointment.

"Brightwater. I heard you were dead."

"You hear everyone's dead. It's how you keep from writing to them."

"I don't write. Writing's evidence." He stepped aside to let them in, then saw Wren slide down from Tamsin's hip, and stopped moving entirely. Brannoc Hale was a big man gone soft at the edges, built like a barrel that had decided years ago to stop being particular about its contents, and it took a great deal to stop him mid-stride. A mute child caked in mountain mud did it. "Whose is that?"

"Nobody's," said Tamsin. "That's rather the whole of it."

They put Wren by the fire, where the child sat with the grave stillness of a small magistrate and began, methodically, to eat the bread and honey Brannoc's housekeeper had set out. Tamsin drew Brannoc into the lean-to where he cured hams, partly for privacy and partly because Brannoc only ever told the truth in rooms that smelled strongly of something.

"Inquisitors in Millbrook," Tamsin said. "Grey cloaks. Asking after a vessel of the old tongue."

Brannoc's hands kept working the brine brush, but slowly, the way a man's hands move when his head has left them. "Spire doesn't come up the Thornfells for smugglers. We're beneath them. We've always been beneath them, bless it."

"They're not hunting me."

"No?"

"They're hunting the child."

The brush stopped. Brannoc looked through the slats of the lean-to at Wren's small silhouette by the fire, and Tamsin watched him do arithmetic. That was the thing about Brannoc that eleven years had never softened: he was kind the way a riverbank is kind, until there was money in the water, and then you found out exactly where the stones were.

"How much," Brannoc said.

"Brann—"

"No, let's have it plain, because you came here plain, and I'll answer plain. There's a price on that child. Don't tell me there isn't; the Spire doesn't send three cloaks and a tithe-man up a goat track for nothing, and my cousin in the lowlands writes me things when he's drunk. How much, and who's paying?"

"I don't know what the buyers would give. I haven't asked."

"But you know there are buyers."

Tamsin said nothing, which in the ham-smelling dark of Brannoc Hale's lean-to counted as a yes, and both of them knew the grammar of it perfectly well.

Brannoc hung the brush on its nail with exaggerated care. "You want passage. High pass, before the snows close it, past the toll at Ash Bridge without your face being remembered. I can do that. You want provisions, a name that isn't yours, and two days' head start on any cloak that asks. I can do all that." He turned around. His face in the lamplight was doing something complicated. "And what are you offering?"

"Whatever you ask short of the child."

"That's the trouble with you, Brightwater. You always come to the table having already decided which coin isn't in play. It makes negotiations dull."

"The child is not a coin."

"Everything is a coin. That's the world we live in, and you more than anyone—" He caught himself, jaw working. "Eleven years ago I stood on Gullstone Bridge with a rope around the business of it and you talked me down, and then you went into my head and cut out the worst hour of my life and carried it away like a man carrying off a diseased tooth, and I woke up the next morning not knowing why I wanted to live, only that I did. And I have been grateful to you every day since. Every single day. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I'm beginning to fear it."

"I'm telling you the debt is real. And I'm telling you that a debt is still a debt, and there are men in the lowlands—serious men, with serious purses—who will pay enough for one small mute child to buy this whole valley twice and burn it once for practice. And I am standing here trying to find the version of myself that doesn't say the number out loud." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I hate that he's in here somewhere. He's always been in here somewhere. You didn't cut him out, Tam. You only cut out the reason he'd won."

The lamp guttered. Out beyond the slats, Wren had fallen asleep by the fire, chin on chest, one hand still curled around the crust of bread like a promise.

"Say your number," Tamsin said quietly.

"The child. Delivered. To the buyers in Corvasser, low road, within the fortnight. In exchange: the high pass, the false name, the head start, and my hand between you and any smuggler who comes collecting for what you pulled on the Vell Road last spring." He said it all in one breath, like a man jumping off something. "That's the number. And I'll tell you now so we're honest with each other, which we have never once been: if you say no, I'll help you anyway, and I'll hate myself in the morning either way, and I don't know which morning is worse."

* * *

They ate late—goat, root vegetables, a hard yellow cheese that fought back. Wren woke for it and ate with the same solemn concentration, watching Brannoc the whole time the way children watch large animals at fairs.

Brannoc, to his credit or his ruin, could not stop looking back.

"So they don't speak," he said. "At all?"

"Not in any tongue I know. And I know a great many tongues I shouldn't."

"What does the old tongue sound like?"

Tamsin hesitated. Wren had not made a sound since the door—not a cry, not a laugh, not the small ordinary noises children leak constantly like badly stoppered jars. But now the child looked up from the cheese, looked at Brannoc, looked at Tamsin, and seemed to arrive at some decision, and opened their mouth.

What came out was low and resonant and structured like weather. Three syllables, maybe five, with a rise in the middle that landed somewhere behind Tamsin's sternum. It meant nothing to Tamsin's ear, and yet the room felt briefly larger, the fire briefly older, as though the language itself carried furniture too heavy for the present age.

When it was done, Wren looked at them expectantly. Waiting for understanding. Receiving none. The small shoulders settled again.

"That," said Brannoc, very carefully, "is worth money I cannot imagine."

"Don't."

"I'm not—Tam, I'm saying it plainly because we agreed to be plain. Whatever that was, there are collectors who'd empty a mine for a recording of it, and scholars who'd sell their colleges, and the Spire, which wants no one else to ever hear it at all. That child is a vault with legs. You're walking the high passes with a vault with legs and three grey cloaks behind you and you're asking me for bread." He shook his head slowly. "I used to think you were the coldest trader on the mountain. Turns out you just hadn't met anything you couldn't price."

"And you have?"

Brannoc looked at Wren. Wren, with the terrible directness of the very young, looked back.

"No," he admitted. "Haven't. Which frightens me more than the Spire does."

* * *

Tamsin slept badly on Brannoc's floor, which was standard; sleep and Tamsin had maintained a distant professional relationship for years. What was not standard was the direction their thoughts kept running.

They lay in the dark listening to the house settle and thought about transactions. That was the whole architecture of their life, and they had built it deliberately, beam by beam: memory taken, memory given, coin moved, contact brief, doors closed gently and forever. Nobody could betray you if nobody belonged to you. It was a good design. It had kept them alive through two purges, a famine, and the Vell Road affair, and it had one flaw, and the flaw was asleep by the hearth breathing in small even measures.

Because here was the sickness, and Tamsin turned it over in the dark like a stone with something under it: when Brannoc had named his price—the child, delivered—they had not been surprised. Not even a little. Disappointed, yes, in the way you're disappointed when rain finds the one hole in the roof you hadn't mended. But underneath the disappointment had run a cold current of recognition: *of course.* Of course the kindest man Tamsin knew would price a child. Everyone priced everything. Tamsin had taught half the mountain to do it. Every friendship they owned had been purchased at some counter, and they had never once, until this week, stood on the other side of a trade wanting desperately to be cheated.

That was the new feeling, then. Wanting to be cheated. Wanting someone, anyone, to look at the deal on the table and throw the scales out the window.

They must have slept eventually, because they woke to grey light and Brannoc shaking their shoulder, and his face was wrong.

"Riders on the track," he said. "Two. Cloaks under the oilskins. They stopped at my cousin's place first—I had word by pigeon an hour ago." He swallowed. "Tam, they're not asking after you."

The cold went through Tamsin like well-water. By the hearth, Wren was already awake, already standing, already watching the door with an expression no child should own: not fear exactly, but readiness, the look of someone who has been hunted before and remembers how it goes.

"There's a cellar door opens into the goat track below the house," Brannoc said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, and Tamsin understood, with a lurch of something dangerously close to grief, that whatever the man had decided in the night, he had decided it against himself. "High pass. I'll meet you at the split rock with provisions and a lie for the cloaks. Two days. If I'm not there, don't wait."

"Brannoc—"

"Go, you impossible thief. Before I remember what I am."

Tamsin took Wren's hand. Small, mud-grimed, gripping back hard. They went through the cellar into the thin screaming wind of the track, and behind them the knocking started at Brannoc Hale's front door, polite and grey and unhurried, and Tamsin ran for the high pass with the one thing in their arms that no one in their whole transactional world had ever taught them how to carry.

Something they refused, at any price, to sell.
