## 6. Flight Through Thornfell

The first thing Tamsin stole from a man's mind, twenty years ago, was the memory of his mother's face. Not on purpose—they'd been clumsy then, desperate, and grief had been easier to carry than hunger. The second thing they stole was a map. Not paper; maps could be confiscated, burned, torn from your coat by grey-cloaked hands. A map in the head went wherever you went, and no inquisitor had yet learned to search a skull.

Tamsin's skull currently held three hundred and eleven routes through the Thornfell mountains, and they were discovering, with mounting professional indignation, that not one of them was good enough.

"Left," Wren said—or rather, made the sound that meant *left*, two syllables like stones dropped in a well, and pointed up and to the east where the pass narrowed into a chute of grey scree.

"That's not a route," Tamsin said. "That's a cliff with ambitions."

Wren regarded them with the flat, patient look of a child who has been asked an idiotic question by an adult for the last time. Then they climbed. Four feet of hand-over-hand granite, boots finding purchase where Tamsin's eyes found none, and stood at the top looking down as if to say *well?*

Tamsin looked at the sky—two hours, maybe three, before the Spire's riders worked out that the fugitives hadn't taken the low road—and looked at the child, and hated what they were about to do.

"All right," they muttered. "But if we die, I'm haunting you specifically."

* * *

They climbed for the rest of the morning, and it was the strangest morning of Tamsin's thieving life, which was saying something, given the year they'd sold a bishop his own baptism back at triple rate.

Because here was the thing: the route *worked*. The impossible chute gave onto a ledge, the ledge gave onto a goat track, the goat track threaded between two leaning slabs of stone like a needle through cloth. And it wasn't just any route. Tamsin knew it—knew it the way you know a word on the tip of your tongue. Somewhere in the crowded attics of their stolen memories there was an echo of this path, a dead smuggler's recollection of a way over the mountains so old it predated the smugglers themselves, filed under *useless* because half its landmarks were ruins or rivers that had changed course.

Except Wren knew every landmark.

At the fork where the track split around a boulder split by lightning, Tamsin hesitated, digging through borrowed years. *Left,* said a dead man's memory, *unless the white stone's fallen, then right.*

Wren touched the boulder—touched it gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal—and shook their head, and pointed straight up the seam between the slabs.

"There's nothing up there."

Wren said four words in the dead tongue. Tamsin understood none of them and somehow all of them: *the old road goes this way.* It was maddening, that feeling. Like hearing a song through a wall.

"The old road," Tamsin repeated. "Of course. Why would anyone keep roads where people can find them." But they climbed, and the seam opened into a stair cut into the living rock, each step worn smooth by feet that had been dead for a thousand years, and Tamsin stood on the first of them feeling very small and very recently humbled.

"You," they told Wren, "are a walking felony I haven't figured out how to charge anyone for."

Wren smiled. It was the first time. It rearranged the whole muddy, exhausted, solemn little face, and Tamsin felt something shift in their chest that they firmly decided was indigestion.

* * *

They ate cold oats and dried plums above the cloud line, sitting in the doorway of a ruin that had been a watchpost once, when there was something up here worth watching. The wind did most of the talking. Wren traced a finger through the frost on the stone—curved letters, older than any alphabet Tamsin could fence—and Tamsin watched without appearing to watch, which was their one true talent.

*Vael,* the child wrote, and stopped, and looked at the clouds instead.

"Don't," Tamsin said, surprising themself. "Don't tell me. Every time someone tells me something important lately, men in grey cloaks show up within the week. I'm superstitious."

Wren wiped the frost letters away with their sleeve. Then, after a moment, they wrote something else: a small crooked shape. A bird, roughly. They pointed at it, then at Tamsin.

"I'm not a bird."

Wren pointed at the bird again, then mimed—with more drama than Tamsin felt the situation warranted—flight, swooping, thievery. A snatch of bread from an invisible windowsill.

"A thief-bird."

A nod, gravely delivered.

"Well," Tamsin said. "I've been called worse by better-dressed people." They chewed a plum stone sour and thought about the fence who'd sold them out, about Brannoc's face going carefully blank across his ledger, about every hand in Thornfell that had ever clasped theirs and kept a knife count in the other. "Birds fly off, though. That's the whole trouble with birds."

Wren frowned at this, unconvinced, and wrote one more shape in the frost before it melted: a nest. The bird sat in it. Tamsin looked at that for longer than was dignified, then got up briskly to check the descent path and blamed the wind for their eyes.

* * *

The second day nearly killed them both, and it was entirely the fault of Tamsin's own stolen goods.

There was a route—the Hollow Way—a smuggler named Corvess had sold them eight years ago, dying of a gut wound and charging full price regardless, the absolute vulture. It ran along the shoulder of Mount Cael, hidden in the shadow of a rock ridge, invisible from below. Perfect for avoiding the toll-wardens and the Spire's patrol roads. Tamsin had used it twice, profitably.

Halfway along, Wren dug in their heels so hard they nearly went over the edge.

Tamsin turned. The child stood pressed against the inner wall of the path, arms wrapped around themself, breathing in short sharp pulls, staring down the mountain—not at the drop, Tamsin realized, but at something below the drop. A fold of the valley where the trees grew wrong, dark and dense, hiding whatever lay among them.

"What? What is it?"

Wren's mouth worked. No words came in the common tongue; the dead tongue came instead, low and urgent, and for once Tamsin caught the shape of it plainly, the way you catch the meaning of a scream even through a shut door: *not that way. They died there.*

"Who died?"

Wren just looked at them, and Tamsin—who had spent their life reading faces for a living, who could find a lie in a blink and a motive in a shrug—looked back, and saw nothing readable at all. Only old, old grief, worn smooth like the stair-steps, carried so long it had become part of the carrying.

"We can't go around," Tamsin said gently. "Cael's shoulder drops to a cliff on the far side. We'd be seen from the valley road."

Wren closed their eyes. Then they walked to the edge of the path, past Tamsin, and pointed down the Hollow Way itself—at a spot perhaps forty paces on, where the path widened against the ridge wall—and made a gesture Tamsin needed a moment to parse. Hands pressed together, then flattened, then drawn apart.

*A door. There is a door.*

"There is absolutely not a—"

But Tamsin's stolen memories were already riffling, unbidden. Corvess. Corvess's dying sale. *Mind the wide place by the twin-horned ridge—old work behind the screen of stone—never had the nerve to see what it sealed and don't you neither.* Tamsin had assumed the man meant a cache. Smugglers always assumed caches.

They reached the wide place. Behind a curtain of lichen-covered slab, exactly where Wren pointed, a seam in the rock. Cut, not cracked. And set into it, green with a thousand years of weather, a handle shaped like a folded wing.

Tamsin stood before it for a long time, wind pulling at their hood.

"How do you know these things?" they asked quietly. "What are you?"

Wren took their sleeve. Not the handle—their sleeve. And shook their head slowly: *don't open it. But go this way anyway.* Because the door's passage, whatever it was, evidently ran beneath the valley fold where the trees grew wrong—under the place where *they died*, whoever they were, whose deaths the child still grieved on a mountainside a kingdom and an age away from home.

Tamsin wanted to argue. Tamsin was, professionally speaking, exquisitely good at arguing. Instead they heard themself say, "All right. Your mountains. I'm just renting space in them."

And they went the long way round Cael's shoulder, exposed for twenty miserable minutes to the valley road, hearts in throats, unseen—and Tamsin did not open the winged door, though their fingers itched toward it the whole way past, because some locks you could feel through the stone, and that one had been locked from the inside by people who meant it.

That night, camped in a hollow beneath a leaning pine, Wren fell asleep almost mid-motion, the way children do, one hand still curled around the strap of Tamsin's pack. Tamsin sat watching the valley lights below and thinking about doors.

Every mind they'd ever entered had been a house with the windows open. Greed left doors open. Fear did. Grief did. Even Brannoc, careful Brannoc, had gaps you could walk through if you knew the knock. But Wren's mind—that first terrible flood of it, fire and wings and a voice crying *Heir*—hadn't been an open house at all. It had been a country. Borders, walls, a language, a war. And Tamsin had come over the wall like the thief they were and been very nearly burned for it.

You couldn't steal from a country. You could only be welcomed into it, or repelled.

The child slept on. Tamsin pulled the blanket higher over the small shoulders, telling themselves it was practical—cold killed fugitives, and this particular fugitive was, apparently, the only guide through Thornfell who remembered why the passes were built—and knew perfectly well they were lying, and let themselves lie.

* * *

On the fourth day the mountains played their oldest trick: snow in the high pass, out of season, out of mercy. It came sideways and it came mean, and it erased the trail ahead and behind alike, and somewhere in the white roar Tamsin lost the thread of the dead smuggler's route entirely—couldn't remember whether the cairn field bent north or south of the frozen tarn, and the difference, per Corvess's cheerful dying notes, was a crevasse field.

They were arguing about it—Tamsin gesturing south with the authority of three hundred and eleven stolen routes, Wren pointing north with the authority of being right—when the child simply took hold of Tamsin's hand and led.

Tamsin let them. That was the remarkable thing, they'd think later. Not the snow, not the crevasse they passed close enough to hear wind sighing out of its blue throat. That a thief whose hands had picked every kind of lock in six kingdoms let a mud-streaked orphan take their hand and lead them blind through a blizzard, and the worst part—the genuinely humiliating part—was that it felt easy.

Wren stopped twice in the whiteout, head cocked, listening to something under the wind. Once they changed direction for no reason Tamsin could see. The third time, they stopped and didn't move at all, and pointed down at their feet.

Beneath the snow: carved stone. A threshold. And faintly, through the storm, a sound Tamsin first took for wind in a chimney and then understood was wind in a *flue*—a made thing, a vent, air moving through old works below.

"Underground," Tamsin shouted over the storm. "There's a way under. Is that what you're hearing?"

Wren nodded, already brushing snow from a ring of stone that had been a hatch-lid when kingdoms were young. It rose on counterweights that still worked, which frightened Tamsin more than anything else that day—someone had maintained these things, once, with love.

They descended into a corridor lit by seams of the same dormant gold light Tamsin had seen flicker under Wren's skin at odd moments, running in channels along the walls like veins in marble. Warm air rose from somewhere deep. The storm shut above them like a lid.

Wren walked the corridor without hesitation, left at the first branching, right at the second, and Tamsin followed, cataloguing intersections with a thief's eye and understanding, with a growing vertigo, that the tunnels didn't follow the mountains. The mountains followed the tunnels. Everything Tamsin had ever known about Thornfell—every smuggler's brag, every stolen map, every hard-won secret they'd traded to stay alive—was a footnote scribbled in the margin of this.

At a junction stood a pillar, and on the pillar a relief: figures carved in procession, tall ones with wings folded, and beside them—walking alongside, not serving, *alongside*—smaller figures with quick hands and satchels. One of the small figures was lifting something shining from a dragon's hoard while the dragon looked on with what the carving insisted, against all sense, was amusement.

Tamsin stared at it a long moment.

"Huh," they said. "Thieves and dragons." Something old and warm moved in their chest, uninvited. "Well. Everyone needs a profession."

Wren had stopped farther down the corridor. They were standing very still, one hand raised toward a sealed archway ahead, and when they turned to Tamsin their face was wet.

They pointed through the arch—at darkness, at distance, at a depth the light-channels didn't reach—and said one word. Tamsin didn't know the tongue, but they knew the word now; they'd heard it in the flood of the child's memories, in fire, in a voice calling through burning spires.

*Vael Auren.*

Then Wren pointed back the way they had come, up through the mountain, toward the storm and the world, and made a different sign—hands flat, pressing downward. *Not yet. Deeper first.*

And from somewhere far below—farther than any cellar, deeper than any mine Tamsin had ever fenced the location of—came a sound.

Not the wind. Not water.

A slow, vast intake of breath, as if something at the bottom of the world had slept a very long time and was, just possibly, beginning to dream of waking.

Behind them, faint and rising through the stone, came another sound entirely: iron-shod dogs baying on the high pass, and voices in the whiteout, and the certain knowledge that whatever else was true, Inquisitor Veyra had not lost the trail after all.
