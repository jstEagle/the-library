## 7. Inquisitor Veyra

The Spire kept its interrogation rooms warm, and Veyra had never understood why.

She had asked once, years ago, when she was still new enough to ask questions. The old chamberlain had shrugged and said warmth loosened tongues the way heat loosened jar lids. Veyra had tested the theory across a hundred interrogations since, and concluded that the chamberlain had been wrong, and that the truth was simpler and crueller: the Spire wanted its guests comfortable, because comfortable men confessed faster, and because the Spire liked to be able to say, afterward, that no one had been mistreated. The warmth was not kindness. It was paperwork.

She stood now at the narrow window of the warm room in the market-town garrison, watching rain stitch silver lines down the glass, and listened to Brannoc Hale decide, for the eleventh time, whether to lie to her.

He was doing it loudly. That was the thing about smugglers — they lied the way other men prayed, with their whole bodies, with conviction and flourish and the occasional appeal to a higher power. Brannoc had been at it for three hours. He had been a wool merchant, then a pilgrim, then a man who had never in his life heard of the Thornfell passes, then a man who had heard of them but considered the subject boring, and now he was a man with a bad hip who would very much like to sit down, and could this perhaps be continued tomorrow?

"You may sit," Veyra said, without turning around. "There has never been a rule against sitting, Master Hale. I find it strange that you keep asking."

"Old habit," Brannoc said. "From people who did mind."

She turned. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard that had given up any ambition of grooming and hands that were careful even at rest — the hands of a man who had carried things he did not want dropped, for a very long time. He sat in the warm room's single comfortable chair with the wary ease of someone who knew the chair was a prop.

"Let us try a different shape of question," Veyra said. She took the plain wooden stool across from him, because she had learned long ago that the geometry of an interrogation mattered more than its content. Face to face, no table. A conversation between two people, not a procedure performed on a subject. "You are not in trouble. I want you to hold on to that, because everything I say next will sound like trouble."

Brannoc's eyebrows went up a fraction. "That's a new one."

"Three days ago, a child was seen on the smuggler roads above Thornfell. Mud-caked, alone, mute in any tongue the kingdoms use. Two days ago, a memory-thief named Tamsin Brightwater — a name I suspect you know — was seen in the market town of Lowmark with a child of that description." She let that sit. "Yesterday, my agents searched Brightwater's cottage and found it empty. Cold hearth. Bed unslept. A bowl on the table with porridge dried in it, and — this detail interests me — a second bowl, smaller, chipped at the rim, set across the table as though someone had been expected."

"You can't arrest a man for a chipped bowl," Brannoc said.

"No," Veyra agreed. "But I can arrest him for what I'm about to charge him with, which is conspiracy to conceal a person of Spire interest, and the chipped bowl is going to look very poetic at the tribunal." She paused. "That was a joke, Master Hale. You are permitted to react."

"I'm deciding whether to," Brannoc said, but the corner of his mouth had moved, and Veyra noted it, and noted that he had noted that she noted it. Good. A man who could still be made to smile was a man who still wanted something from the world, and a man who wanted something could be spoken with.

She folded her hands. "Tell me about Tamsin Brightwater."

"Why would I?"

"Because I am going to tell you about them first, and you will want to correct me. Everyone does. It is the strongest instinct there is — stronger than fear, stronger than loyalty. Someone describes a person you know wrongly, and you cannot bear it." She smiled, small and genuine, because it was true and because truth, worn openly, was the only weapon she had never once regretted carrying. "Tamsin Brightwater is a thief of memories. They work the Thornfell passes, they trade in recollections the way other traders deal in salt and tin, and they are, by every account I have gathered, not cruel. They stole a memory back for you once. Shall I say which one, or would you rather I didn't?"

The warmth in the room seemed to thicken. Brannoc had gone very still, in the way of large men who had learned that stillness was their only speed.

"You don't know that," he said quietly.

"His sister," Veyra said. "The year of the purges. He watched her taken and could not live with the watching, so he found a thief who could take the watching out of him. Except it wasn't Tamsin who did it — Tamsin was the one who *gave* it back. Took the memory out of the thief who'd bought it and returned it to you, unpaid, unasked." She tilted her head. "Did I get it right?"

Brannoc stared at her. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, a garrison soldier laughed at something, the sound muffled and ordinary, and Veyra thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that the world went on making ordinary sounds while it came apart.

"You've been busy," Brannoc said at last.

"I have been thorough. It is the same thing with more paperwork." She leaned forward. "Now. The child."

"I don't know anything about any child."

"You knew Brightwater was a memory-thief before I said it. You knew the sister story was true. You are a man who knows things, Master Hale, and I am a woman who collects them, and between us we could save a great deal of time." She let her voice soften — not falsely; she had found that softness was only false if the person using it was false, and she was not. She meant every word. That was the whole horror of her, if you asked her soldiers when they thought she couldn't hear. She meant every word. "A child who speaks the old tongue is not a curiosity. It is not a rumor to be chased for a bounty. It is the most dangerous thing to walk out of the Thornfells in three hundred years, and there are people — good people, in the Spire, people I respect — who believe that danger means we must hold it gently and study it. I am going to tell you why they are wrong, and then you are going to decide what kind of man you are, and I am going to respect your decision even if I arrest you for it. Does that sound fair?"

"That sounds like a speech."

"It is a speech. I have been giving it for twenty years. I've gotten rather good." She rose and went to the window, because the next part was easier without his eyes on her, and she was honest enough with herself to admit that. "You know what the Spire teaches about Vael Auren."

"Sunken city. Old magic. Don't go swimming."

"The Spire teaches it as a story with the teeth filed off. A sanctuary of the old kingdoms, drowned in some vague catastrophe, home to the dragon-fire that 'once kept the peace.'" She said the last words in a dry recitation-voice, a schoolmistress quoting a dull text. "Here is what the story leaves out. I was born in what was left of it. Not the drowned part — the burned part. The shore-town that grew up in the city's shadow, where the refugees settled after the fire went out, fishing in the water over the roofs of a place we were told never to speak of." She watched the rain instead of him. "My mother sold eels. My father had been a scribe, before. He had seen the last of the fire, the year it guttered. He said it was like watching a hearth go out in a house full of children."

Brannoc said nothing. He was, Veyra noted, a good listener, which she had not expected, and which she distrusted slightly, and which she liked him for anyway. People were allowed to be several things at once. That was rather the point of everything.

"When I was nine," she said, "a dredging crew brought up a stone from the shallows. A door-lintel, carved with the old script, and my father — who could still read it, who had never been able to stop reading it — went down to look, because of course he did, because that is what people do with the past, they go and look." Her voice stayed level. She had practiced this. Not the words — the levelness. "What came up with the lintel was a seam of the old fire. Dormant. A thread of it, no bigger than a hair. It woke when it touched air and it woke *hungry*, and it took the dredging crew, and the crowd, and the street, and my mother's eel-stall, and my mother, and it would have taken me if my father had not put me in a water-barrel and held the lid down while the street burned around us. Forty-one people. I can name thirty-nine of them. I have never been able to find the last two names, and it bothers me more than you would think."

The rain filled the silence. Brannoc's chair creaked as he shifted.

"The Spire's scholars will tell you that was an accident," Veyra said, turning back to him. "A dormant thread, a freak awakening. And they are *right*, in the way that a knife falling is an accident. But here is what they will not say, because saying it would be inconvenient: the fire is not gone. It sleeps. And it is not sleeping the way a hearth sleeps, banked and patient. It is sleeping the way a wound sleeps, and something is walking through the Thornfells right now that can wake it — something the old script has a word for, a word my father taught me before he died, and I will not say it aloud in this room, but you have heard its shape already, because everyone has. *Heir.*" She let the word land and watched Brannoc's face do what faces did — the flicker of recognition, the swift arithmetic of a man deciding how much he had already given away. "A child of the old tongue. Seen on the dragon roads. In the company of a thief. Heading, by every sign, for the drowned city. Tell me, Master Hale. If you had a lamp and I told you a house full of children was full of spilled oil, would you carry the lamp inside to study it?"

"No," Brannoc said. "I'd put the lamp down."

"Yes," Veyra said, and something in her chest ached with how much she wanted him to have said it. "That is all I am. I am the person who puts the lamp down. And the Spire — the Spire I love, the Spire I have served since I was sixteen — wants to form a committee about the oil."

* * *

They gave her an hour alone with him after that, which was against procedure, and she had signed the form saying so, which was also against procedure, and she had long ago concluded that procedure was a fine servant and a terrible master.

Brannoc had not talked. She had known he wouldn't, in the end — she had watched the decision settle over him like weather, somewhere around the part of her story where she named the thirty-nine. He was loyal. Worse, he was loyal to someone who did not deserve it, or who deserved it in a way Veyra could not yet see, and the difference between those two things was going to matter enormously in the next few days.

She sat across from him now with two cups of tea, because the warm room had a kettle and because she had found that offering a man tea after failing to break him confused him more than any threat.

"You're going to let me go," Brannoc said. It was not a question.

"I'm going to do something more interesting," Veyra said. "I'm going to tell you the truth, and then I'm going to let you go, and then I'm going to follow you."

He blinked. "That's the worst plan I've ever heard."

"It is not a plan. It is a wager. Plans are for things you can control." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "Here is the truth. I do not want the child dead. I have never wanted the child dead. I want the fire to stay asleep, and I have spent twenty years reading everything the Spire has on Vael Auren, and I will tell you the thing I have never said aloud in the Spire, because the Spire would call it heresy." She looked at him steadily. "The fire cannot be destroyed. We have tried. There are records — sealed, old, written by people who were there — of three attempts to unmake it, and each one ended the way my street ended. The fire does not die when you attack it. It *feeds*. The only thing that has ever made it sleep is a willing hand laying it down — and the old script has a phrase for that too, and the phrase translates, roughly, as *a price freely paid*."

Brannoc had stopped drinking his tea.

"So when I say the Heir must not reach the Ember Throne," Veyra went on, "I am not saying it because I want a child's blood. I am saying it because a child on that throne is a child being *offered* the whole sky, and no child — no *person* — refuses that. You would not. I would not. I have sat with that knowledge for twenty years and I will tell you honestly, Master Hale, there are nights I am not certain *I* would refuse it, and I am the most stubborn woman in three kingdoms." She smiled, and it was a tired smile, and she let him see that it was tired. "The only mercy is for the fire to gutter out with no one to feed it. And the only way that happens is if the Heir never gets there — or gets there and chooses to walk away, which has the same odds as the rain falling upward."

"And if I just walk out of here and warn them?"

"Then I will follow you, and I will probably lose you in the passes, because I am told Brightwater knows routes that no living map holds." She set down her cup. "But you should know that my agents are already on the high road, and that I let Brightwater pass the toll at Ash Bridge this morning."

Brannoc went very still. "You *let* them?"

"I watched them buy their way past a corrupt warden with a false memory — a beautiful one, by the report. The warden is at this moment the proud veteran of a war he never fought, beloved by his men, decorated twice." Something moved through her face that was not quite a smile. "And I let them go, because a thief who sells beautiful lies at bridges is not the danger. The danger is what the thief is carrying, and whether they know it. So yes, Master Hale. I let them pass. Into the high passes, toward the drowned road, with my agents a half-day behind and closing." She stood. "You may warn them. Honestly, I hope you do. A warned thief makes better decisions than a surprised one, and I would very much like everyone involved to make better decisions, because I am so very tired of funerals."

She unlocked the door herself.

"One last thing," she said, as he passed her — big and slow, moving like a man who was already composing the argument he'd make on the mountain. "The child. When you see them. Look at their hands. My father wrote that the Heir's hands remember the fire even when the mind forgets — that the child would hold them palm-up, without knowing why, the way you hold them to a hearth." Her voice caught, only slightly, only where she allowed it. "My mother held her hands that way, the last time I saw her. Standing in the burning street. I was in the barrel. I saw it through the crack in the lid."

Brannoc stopped in the doorway. For a long moment he didn't turn around.

"You could have lied to me," he said finally. "About all of it. The street. The names. Would've been easier."

"Yes," Veyra said. "But then you'd have been defending a lie, and I'd have been chasing you for the wrong reasons, and we'd both have wasted a perfectly good storm."

He went. She watched from the window as he crossed the garrison yard, hunched into the rain, and turned — not toward the lowland road, but up. Toward the passes. Toward the high cold places where a thief and a child were climbing toward a drowned city and a throne of embers.

Veyra pressed her palm flat against the cold glass, and held it there, the way you hold it to a hearth.

"Run well," she said quietly, to no one, to the rain, to a child she had never met and already mourned. "And when they offer you the sky —"

She did not finish it. She had never once, in twenty years, managed to finish it.

She rang for the soldiers, gave the order to move out, and went to find her boots.
