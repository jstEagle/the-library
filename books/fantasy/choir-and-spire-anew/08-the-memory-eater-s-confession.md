## 8. The Memory Eater's Confession

The rockslide came at dusk, with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

Tamsin heard it first as a shiver underfoot — a long, grumbling tremor that set pebbles dancing on the trail — and then the whole face of the cliff above them let go. They ran. Tamsin grabbed Wren by the collar and hauled the child toward the dark mouth of a cave they'd been eyeing for shelter anyway, and behind them the mountainside came down in a roar of white thunder, dust and gravel chasing them into the dark like an angry dog.

Then it was over. The dust settled. The trail was gone.

Wren coughed, wiped their face with both hands, and looked out through the curtain of grit still drifting past the cave mouth. Where the path had been there was now a steep fan of broken stone, fifty feet high and twice as wide, already being sleeted on.

Tamsin surveyed it the way a merchant surveys a shipwreck: professionally, and with a sinking heart.

"Well," they said. "That's one way to close a road."

Wren signed something rapid and urgent — *the passes, we'll lose the days* — and then, remembering Tamsin's hopeless grasp of hand-speech, said it again aloud in the dead tongue, which helped not at all but had the pleasant side effect of sounding like a small bird arguing with a larger bird.

"Yes, thank you, I caught every word," Tamsin said. "We go around. There's a ledge route below the scree — I have it from a man named Osk who fell off half of it, so I know precisely which parts not to trust."

They found the ledge route in the morning. It took them two full days of cold, careful walking, Wren going ahead like a goat with opinions, Tamsin following with the particular dignity of someone whose boots were not designed for this. On the third evening, with the light failing and snow beginning to fall in fat, indifferent flakes, they found a cave.

It was a good one, as caves went — dry-floored, deep enough to stand in, with a chimney crack near the back that drew smoke away instead of filling the space with it. Tamsin got a fire going from damp wood and stubbornness, and they ate smoked river trout and hard bread, and Wren fell asleep sitting up, mid-chew, the way children do when they've been walking for three days and will not admit it.

Tamsin eased them down onto the bedroll, tucked the cloak around them, and sat back against the wall with the fire painting shadows on the stone.

And that was when the thought arrived. It arrived the way such thoughts do — uninvited, wearing its best clothes, smiling.

*You could take one.*

The child slept six feet away. Sleeping minds were the easiest kind; Tamsin had built a whole trade on that fact. One touch, one gentle pull, and Tamsin would know what Wren knew — where the drowned road began, what waited inside Vael Auren, whether the fire the Spire feared so much was treasure or plague. Every smuggler from here to the lowlands would pay a fortune for even a scrap of it. Enough coin to buy a house with an actual roof. Enough to never lift another stranger's memories again, never feel another life drain through their fingers into someone else's purse.

Tamsin looked at the sleeping child — the mud-crusted boots, the carved dragon clutched loosely in one small fist, the face that in sleep looked less like an heir of anything and more like exactly what it was: a tired kid in a cold cave.

Tamsin put more wood on the fire and did not move from their spot.

"Absolutely not," they told themselves, quietly. "We talked about this."

The fire crackled. Somewhere far above, the mountain settled with a groan.

It was, Tamsin reflected, a strange thing to be trusted by someone. Wren trusted them. Completely, stupidly, without any evidence whatsoever — the child had known them for perhaps three weeks, during two of which Tamsin had been lying about their intentions, and yet Wren followed them up a mountain without hesitation. Slept beside them. Let them take watch alone.

It was trust Tamsin hadn't earned. And it sat in the chest like a swallowed coal, warm and uncomfortable.

Because there was a thing Tamsin had never told anyone. Not Brannoc, though Brannoc knew pieces. Not any fence or buyer or bedfellow across twenty years of the trade. It was the thing at the bottom of the well of them, the reason a person becomes a memory-thief in the first place, and Tamsin had kept it sealed for so long that opening it felt less like honesty and more like surgery.

But the child was asleep, and the fire was warm, and the mountain had closed the road behind them, and there is something about being trapped that makes truth feel less like a door left open and more like water finding its level.

"Wren," Tamsin said softly, to the sleeping shape. "I'm going to tell you something you won't remember hearing. Which makes it the only honest conversation I've ever had."

* * *

"I had a sister."

The words came out rusty. Tamsin cleared their throat and tried again, speaking low, pitched just above the fire's murmur.

"Her name was Liss. Lissandra, if she was in trouble, which was most of the time, because she was the sort of person who was always in trouble and always entirely delighted about it. Two years younger than me. Small. Loud. She could whistle through her fingers loud enough to stop a market, and she did, constantly, for no reason anyone ever discovered. She once convinced an entire village that the miller's donkey could read. Don't ask me how. Nobody ever found out how. The donkey went along with it, which I've always thought says something about donkeys."

The fire popped. Snow hissed faintly at the cave mouth. Wren slept on, breath slow and even, and Tamsin went on, because now that the seal was broken the words came faster, crowding each other like people escaping a burning building.

"We grew up in Corrow Vale. Little town, terraced up the hillside, famous for nothing except apples and a shrine nobody visited. Our father was a cooper. Decent man. Our mother died young, which is common enough in the vale that nobody made much ceremony of it. And when I was eleven and Liss was nine, the Purge came through."

Tamsin's hands had gone still in their lap. They noticed this and deliberately flexed the fingers, one at a time, like a card sharp counting a deck.

"You'll know the word 'purge' from the Spire's own histories, if you ever read them, which I hope you never do, because they're written by the winners and the winners in this case were men in grey cloaks with clean boots. This was before your time — before mine, nearly. The Spire called it the Reclamation of Unlicensed Thought. Everyone else called it what it was: they came for anyone whose mind touched the old magic. Hedge-witches. Charm-sellers. People who dreamed too loudly, honestly, that was nearly the criterion — there was a woman taken from our street because she claimed her dreams came true, and the tragedy is that after they took her, everyone swore they started coming true."

"Liss touched the old magic."

Tamsin said it flatly, to the fire, because saying it any other way was impossible.

"She didn't know she was doing it. She was nine. She'd found — I still don't know where — a way of holding a memory outside her head. You'd tell her a secret and she'd hold it in a pebble, I swear it, a little grey pebble from the stream, and later you'd ask her and she'd give the pebble a squeeze and whisper your secret back word for word. We thought it was a trick. A clever girl's game. I taught her things to keep in pebbles. My own secrets. That was the arrangement: she kept mine safe, I kept hers. It was the closest thing I had, at eleven, to understanding love."

"The grey cloaks came in autumn. They had a device — a lens, brass and glass, ugly thing — and they walked the streets holding it up, and it showed them whose heads glowed. Most people didn't glow at all. Our father didn't. I didn't." Tamsin paused. "Liss lit up like a festival lantern."

"They took her on a Tuesday. I remember it was Tuesday because market day, and the stalls were still up, and I watched them walk her down the middle of the street between two soldiers while the apple-sellers pretended very hard to be arranging fruit. She wasn't crying. She was looking for me in the crowd. And I was —"

Here Tamsin stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the fire and the wind worrying at the rocks outside.

"I was hiding behind a rain barrel," they said finally. "Eleven years old, hiding behind a barrel, watching my sister look for me. And she found me. Right at the end, just before the corner — she saw me. Over the soldier's shoulder. Our eyes met and I will see that look until I die, whatever else they take from me. And then she did the last clever thing she ever did. She squeezed the pebble in her pocket. Her memory-pebble. The one holding my secrets. And whatever she pushed into it — I felt it land in my head like a thrown stone. All of it. Every secret I'd ever given her, plus everything else she was — her voice, her laugh, the whistling, the donkey, all of it — packed into one grey stone that hit my mind and stuck there."

"The grey cloaks took her. And when they questioned her — I learned this later, from a guard who sold me the memory of it years afterward, which is its own horror, isn't it — they asked her what she'd done with her gift, who she'd given her secrets to. And she said no one. She'd emptied herself into the pebble. As far as her mind could show them, she was just a girl. An empty-headed girl who couldn't even remember her brother's name, because she'd given that away too."

"They sent her north. To the Spire. I never saw her again. I don't know if she lived. I choose to believe she did, loudly, and whistled at them through her fingers the entire way."

Tamsin's voice had gone rough. They took a breath, and it shook slightly, and they were glad — glad, actually — that the only witness was asleep.

"Now. Here is the part I have never told anyone. The pebble stayed in my head for four years. I carried it everywhere. I couldn't open it — it wasn't meant for me, it was meant to be given back, and I didn't know how to give it anywhere except to her. So I just... carried my sister around inside my skull like a letter with no address. Every night I'd lie awake and feel her there, all that noise and light, and I'd think: someday. Someday I'll find her. Or find out. And I'll give it back."

"And then, when I was fifteen, I met a thief. A real one — a mind-thief, one of the old road-walkers, before the trade got dirty. He saw what I was carrying. He said, boy, that stone is killing you. It was, too — I wasn't eating, I wasn't sleeping, I was all pebble. He said he could teach me to take it out. Put it somewhere safe. A keepsake-box, a charm, anything. Just out of my head so I could live."

"He lied. Well — no. He didn't lie, exactly. He took it out. But taking a memory out of a mind leaves a hole, and holes don't stay empty, they pull things in. And what rushed into the hole where my sister had been was — nothing. A Liss-shaped nothing. I remembered that I'd had a sister the way you remember a room you've been told you lived in. I knew the facts. I could not feel a single one of them."

Tamsin laughed — a short, awful sound with no humor in it at all.

"And do you know what I discovered? It didn't hurt anymore. That was the discovery. Four years of carrying her, and one afternoon of emptiness, and suddenly I could eat, sleep, walk past a market stall without flinching at whistling. The nothing was comfortable. The nothing fit me like good boots. And I was fifteen and hollow and I thought — this is what everyone should get to feel like. This is the product. And I apprenticed myself to the man who'd emptied me, and I became very, very good at putting nothing into other people's heads, and charging for it."

"So when people say memory-thieves are monsters who steal what's precious — they're wrong, mostly. Mostly what we steal is grief. Pain. Shame. Things people beg you to take. The theft is almost a mercy. The monster part comes later, when you realize you've gotten so good at removing what hurts that you've forgotten what anything means when it stays. I can't tell you my favorite food without checking. I mean it literally — I taste things, and some part of me goes, *do I like this? Have I liked this before?* Twenty years of trading away my own bad days until my good ones lost their context. You can sell a sunset, Wren. Did you know that? Someone paid me once for the memory of a sunset, and I took it, and now sunsets are just — weather."

The fire burned low. Tamsin fed it the last of the wood and watched the flames climb, and the confession slowed, and softened, and found its ending the way a river finds the sea.

"But here's the thing I figured out too late, and I'm telling it to a child who can't hear it, which is the only way I seem capable of telling anything." Tamsin leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the wet in eyes that would deny having any. "She gave me that pebble to keep her safe. Not to free me. To *keep her*. And I spent twenty years turning the keeping into a business, and the business into a wall, and the wall into a life, and I called it freedom. Being unattached. Light as ash. And it was just the hole, wearing my name."

"So. New rule. Starting tonight, in a freezing cave, addressed to someone who will never know I said it." Tamsin reached up and pressed two fingers briefly to their own temple — an old habit, the thief's salute, usually aimed at someone else's head. "From now on, if a memory matters, it stays. Even if it hurts. *Especially* if it hurts. Whatever happens at the end of this road — Vael Auren, the fire, the throne, all of it — I am done dealing in the irreplaceable. No more taking what can't be given."

A pause.

"Which means," Tamsin added wryly, wiping their face with a sleeve and hoping the darkness covered it, "that I'm apparently going to have to start *giving* things away. Freely. Like some kind of amateur. Gods help me."

They banked the fire, checked the cave mouth, wrapped their own cloak tight, and lay down on the cold stone with the wind singing thinly overhead.

And because they were Tamsin Brightwater, thief of twenty years, keeper of ten thousand stolen yesterdays, they did one last thing before sleep took them. They reached into the quiet place where they kept their own hoard — the small vault of memories no buyer would ever see — and they took out the oldest item there. Not the pebble; the pebble was long gone, dissolved into that terrible merciful nothing. But there was one shard that had survived the theft, one splinter too small for the thief's craft to catch, lodged so deep it had become part of the wall of them:

A sound. A whistle, through fingers, sharp and bright, cutting across a market day. No face attached. No name. Just the sound, and the feeling that had come with it — that somewhere in a crowd, someone was looking for you, and had found you, and was glad.

Tamsin held it a moment. Then, carefully, deliberately — for the first time in twenty years — they gave it away. Not sold. Not traded. Given: set loose into the dark of the cave, offered up to whoever needed finding, to a sister gone north, to a child asleep six feet away, to the mountain, to the night.

"*Found you,*" Tamsin whispered, to no one, to everyone. "*Glad.*"

Then they slept, dreamless and light, lighter than they had been in years, and did not hear — because they were asleep, and because the storm had risen — the soft crunch of boots on scree outside the cave mouth, and the low voices of men who moved like wolves, and the single word passed back down the slope in the Spire's clipped cant:

"*Found.*"
