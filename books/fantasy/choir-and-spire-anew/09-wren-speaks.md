## 9. Wren Speaks

The house smelled of every season at once.

Wren noticed that first, before the crooked shutters, before the bundles of drying plants hanging from every beam like sleeping bats, before the old woman in the doorway with dirt on her hands and suspicion on her face. The house smelled of summer and autumn and something green and early, all pressed together, and underneath it all—faint as a coin at the bottom of a well—it smelled of ash.

Wren's stomach turned over.

*Not here,* they told themselves, the way they had learned to tell themselves, in the flat wordless language of a body too small for its own memories. *Not burning. Drying. Different.*

Tamsin was talking to the old woman in the low, quick voice Tamsin used when lying, which was most of Tamsin's talking, though Wren had begun to hear the seams in it—the little hitch where truth got folded under falsehood like a sheet tucked too hastily at the corners.

"—my sister's child," Tamsin was saying. "Hoarse from the storm. Barely talks at all. You know how children are."

The old woman looked at Wren for a long moment. She had white hair braided against her skull and eyes the color of strong tea, and she did not believe one word of what she was hearing, which was obvious even to someone who understood none of it. But she stepped back from her doorway anyway and let the storm-wind push them all inside.

"Name's Mother Sorrel," she said. "And you can drop the sister story. Nobody drags a sick child over the Widow's Stair in this weather unless something worse is behind them." She pointed at Tamsin. "You'll chop wood. The child will sit by my fire and hold their tongue, which I'm told comes naturally."

Wren, who held their tongue not out of obedience but because their tongue held nothing the old woman could use, sat by the fire.

It was a good fire. Small and disciplined, the way fires were supposed to be—not the roaring hungry gold of the city in their head, not the towers of flame that walked on legs of light through their worst remembering, but a sensible fire that heated soup and asked for nothing but wood and attention. Wren wrapped both hands around their knees and watched it and practiced the thing they practiced every night: *this fire is a fire. This fire is only a fire.*

The soup, when it came, had mushrooms in it and something sharp and piney, and it was the best thing Wren had ever eaten, or at least the best thing since the last time Mother Sorrel's kind fed them, except there had been no last time, because everything before the mountain was either gone or burning. They ate three bowls. Mother Sorrel counted the bowls with her eyebrows but said nothing, and pushed the bread board closer without being asked, and Wren decided, with the sudden fierce totality of a child's judgment, that they would die before letting anything happen to this old woman.

They did not know yet that this was how it always started. That was the whole trouble with them.

* * *

The storm kept them there two days.

Two days of chopping wood (Tamsin, complaining magnificently about it while secretly enjoying having something to do with their hands), of sorting dried leaves into piles that all looked identical to Wren but apparently were as different as kings and beggars ("That's feverfew, sprout, and if you mix it up with the tansy again I'll feed you the tansy myself"), of the rain hammering the shutters like someone with very urgent news and no manners.

Wren helped where hands could help. Hands could grind dried things with a mortar. Hands could carry water. Hands could hold still—this was the hardest one—while Mother Sorrel stitched the split in Wren's boot and muttered about people who let children walk mountains in bad leather.

But mostly Wren listened.

Listening was the strange new game the mountain had taught them. Tamsin spoke a language that should have been foreign and wasn't—slipped into Wren's ears like water into sand, meaning pooling everywhere—and nobody could explain why, least of all Tamsin, who explained everything else. And Wren answered in the only tongue they had, the old rolling consonants, the words shaped like riverbeds, and watched faces fall when they used it. Tamsin's face fell carefully, controlled, the way a thief controls everything. Mother Sorrel's fell honestly, once, and then she never let it fall again, which Wren understood was its own kind of gift.

On the second evening, Mother Sorrel spread a map on her table.

Not a fine map. A map drawn on waxed cloth in faded ink, patched in three places, the coast drawn with the affection of someone who had never seen it. She weighted the corners with stones and a spoon.

"So," she said, to Tamsin, in the voice people use when they've decided to stop pretending. "You're going south. Through the passes. Which means you'll want to know what I heard at the last market day, before you argue with me about whether you heard it too."

Tamsin's shoulders did a small thing. Wren had a name for that now: the guilt-shrug, the one Tamsin thought no one saw.

"Grey cloaks," Mother Sorrel said. "Six of them, maybe more, asking after a child that speaks the old tongue. Calling it a *vessel*. Offering amnesty for information." Her tea-colored eyes moved to Wren, unhurried, unafraid. "There are folk in the valley who'd sell their grandmothers for less than what they're offering. You know that."

"I know that better than anyone alive," Tamsin said quietly, and Wren heard the seam in it again, the fold where a fence named Ossler lived under the fold like a stone under a sheet.

"They're sweeping the high road," Mother Sorrel went on. "Which means you want the drowned road, if you're fools enough to take it, and looking at you, fool, I'd say you are." Her finger traced down off the map's edge, into the blank cloth where cartographers gave up, and tapped twice. "Under Thornfell. Old ways. My grandmother's grandmother used them, back when they still told the true stories instead of the Spire's ones."

"The drowned road leads to Vael Auren," Tamsin said.

"It leads past Vael Auren. Nothing leads *to* it. It drowned, dear. That's generally how that works."

Something happened inside Wren then, and they would spend years learning words for it, but that night they only knew that their chest went tight and hot and their hands began to shake, and the room tilted so that Mother Sorrel's finger seemed to be tapping not on cloth but on a wound.

*Home.*

They stood up so fast the stool went over. Both adults looked at them. Wren pointed at the map—at the blank place beyond the edge, where the ink gave up and the truth started—and said, in the old tongue, fast and desperate:

"That's where it sleeps. That's where the door went down. You have to understand, it doesn't drown, it *waits*, it has been waiting so long, and I am the only one left who knows the way in, and it is going out, going out, going out—"

Of course they didn't say it in any language Tamsin or Mother Sorrel knew. Of course it came out as the old rolling syllables, urgent and useless, a bell ringing in an empty church. Tamsin crossed the room and knelt and put both hands gently on Wren's shoulders and said, "Hey. Hey. Slow. What is it? What do you see?"

And Wren, who had spent every day since the mountain watching these people fail to understand them and loving them anyway for trying, felt something in them crack open along a seam no one had known was there.

Because Tamsin's hands were warm. Because the fire was only a fire. Because the old woman had fed them three bowls of soup and stitched their boot and never once let her face fall twice.

Because being understood mattered more, suddenly, than almost anything.

Wren opened their mouth, reached down into themselves past the old tongue, into the other language, the wet heavy borrowed one they had been soaking in for weeks like a root in rain—and pulled.

"*Fire,*" they said.

The word came out cracked and wrong-shaped, a fledgling falling out of the nest, and both adults went absolutely still. Rain filled the silence. Mother Sorrel's hand rose slowly to her mouth.

"*The fire is going out.*"

Each word cost Wren something—a shove, a heave, like pushing a boat off a mudbank—but the boat was moving now and they could not have stopped if they had wanted to, which they did not, they had never wanted anything so much.

"*I have to go home.*"

Then their legs gave out and Tamsin caught them, because of course Tamsin caught them, and Wren buried their face in a shoulder that smelled of woodsmoke and lies and found, to their astonishment, that they were crying, and that crying in this language, with these words finally out in the air, hurt less than any crying before it.

* * *

Later—after Mother Sorrel had pressed honey-water on everyone and declared, in the brisk voice she used for wounds and feelings alike, that shock made children talk nonsense and everyone was going to bed and that was final—Wren lay on a pallet by the fire and listened to the two adults argue in whispers across the room.

"—a vessel, Tamsin. That's what the cloaks called it. You don't put a child on a road to a drowned city because the child asked nicely."

"You didn't hear them."

"I heard enough. *Fire.* Children don't say *fire* like that. Like they're apologizing to it."

A long silence. Rain.

"They're scared," Tamsin said at last, and their voice did the thing it almost never did, the thing with no seam in it, all truth and no fold. "Not of us. Not even of the grey cloaks, I don't think. Sorrel, they're scared of the fire going *out*. What does that even mean? What kind of child grieves a fire?"

"The kind the Spire wants dead," Mother Sorrel said. "Or wants owned. Those are usually the same thing with different paperwork."

Another silence, longer. Wren kept their breathing slow and even, the way Tamsin had once taught them without meaning to, back on the pass: *sleep like you're somebody with nothing worth stealing.*

"And if it's true?" Tamsin said. Very quietly. "If there's really something dying down there under the mountain, and this kid is the only one holding the string?"

"Then you're in worse trouble than I thought," said Mother Sorrel, "and you'll need more than soup." Cloth rustled. Glass clinked. "Take these. Feverfew for the road-fever, yarrow for everything else, and this one—" a pause, as if she were deciding, "—this one's for grief. Don't ask me whose."

Wren slept, and dreamed, and for once the dream was not the burning city.

It was the city before. Small in the dream, the way true memories of childhood are small: a courtyard with a dry fountain, a woman's laugh from a high window, warm scales under one small palm like sun-warmed roof tiles, and everywhere, threaded through everything, the fire—not flame but presence, a warmth with a mind, humming under the streets like a cat too vast to see, and the hum said, without words, *you are ours and we are yours and it is morning.*

Wren woke with wet eyes and knew, with terrible clarity, exactly how long the dream had been getting smaller.

* * *

They left on the third morning, under a sky scrubbed clean and blue, and Mother Sorrel loaded them down like pack mules and pretended she hadn't.

"Through the Widow's Stair, down the goat track, and if the goat track forks, take the left, the right one goes to a village full of people who'd sell you for bootlaces." She pressed a parcel of bread into Wren's arms and looked at them directly, which few adults bothered to do. "And you. Sprout. Whatever's waiting down there under the water—you tell it Sorrel of the high woods says the world up here isn't done needing whatever it's keeping safe. Tell it to hang on."

Wren understood perhaps half. But they understood the important part, which was the grip on their shoulders and the fierce tea-colored eyes, and they did the only adequate reply: they flung their arms around the old woman's neck and held on.

Mother Sorrel made a sound like a hiccup disguised as a cough, patted Wren's back twice, and then shoved them firmly away before her face could do anything embarrassing. "Go on. Trouble travels faster standing still."

They took the goat track. At the fork, Wren stopped, looked right, looked left, and pointed left before Tamsin had even gotten their bearings—which earned them a long, narrow, grudging look from their guardian.

"How," Tamsin said. Not suspiciously anymore. That was the change, Wren realized; somewhere between the soup and the words and the crying, suspicion had quietly packed its bags. Now it was just wonder, wearing a thief's frown. "Never mind. Left it is. You're the map now, apparently. A map that eats all my bread."

Wren smiled their first smile in days and held out the bread parcel in mock offering, and Tamsin laughed, actually laughed, a sound Wren resolved privately to farm and harvest forever.

But at the top of the first ridge, where the path dipped toward the tree line and the drowned road's dark mouths waited somewhere below, Wren stopped again. This time not to point.

The feeling came up through their feet the way the fire's hum used to come up through the streets of Vael Auren—but thin now, threadbare, a blanket worn to gauze. Fainter than yesterday. Fainter than the mountain.

*Going out.*

They looked south, where beneath leagues of stone and floodwater a warmth older than kingdoms was guttering like Mother Sorrel's candle in the storm, and they gripped the bread parcel to their chest, and they said—clearly, in the wet borrowed language, because some things deserved to be said where they could be understood—

"We have to hurry."

Tamsin turned, and whatever was in Wren's face must have answered the question before it was asked, because Tamsin swore softly, shifted their pack higher, and started down the slope at a pace that had stopped being a stroll.

Behind them, far to the north along the high road they had avoided, a line of grey figures crested a ridge, paused like hawks adjusting for wind, and turned south.
