## 10. The Toll of Ash Bridge

The bridge announced itself long before they saw it, the way bad news always does.

First there was the smell—woodsmoke and wet iron, the particular stink of a garrison that had stopped pretending to be anything but a tollbooth with delusions of grandeur. Then the sound: a bell being rung badly, twice, by someone who clearly resented having to ring it at all. And finally, rising out of the morning mist like a jaw full of rotten teeth, Ash Bridge itself—a span of blackened stone thrown across the gorge where the River Sorn dropped two hundred feet into a chasm that swallowed sound whole.

Tamsin crouched behind a wind-bent pine on the ridge above and did what they always did before a negotiation: they took inventory.

One bridge, fortified at both ends. One gatehouse on the far side, squat and ugly, flying no banner Tamsin recognized, which meant the warden had either gone independent or been forgotten by whoever was supposed to be paying him. Six soldiers visible, four of them bored enough to be dangerous in the way bored men are—one careless shove from cruelty. And one warden, standing on the near tower with a spyglass he wasn't even looking through, because he'd already spotted them.

"Everyone can see us," Tamsin murmured. "That's the point of him standing there."

Wren stood beside them, small hands wrapped around the strap of their pack, watching the bridge with an expression Tamsin had learned to read over the past weeks: not fear exactly, but a kind of listening. The child's eyes went to the gorge, to the water thundering below, and something flickered across their face—recognition, maybe, or grief for a river that had once run clearer.

"He's going to ask for everything we have," Tamsin said. "So we're going to give him something worth more than everything we have, and it's going to cost us nothing but a lie." They paused. "I used to enjoy this part."

Wren looked up at them. Said nothing, because Wren never said anything—not in words Tamsin could use—but the look said *and now?* as plainly as speech.

"Now I'm going to do it anyway," Tamsin admitted, "because the alternative is swimming the Sorn in early thaw, and I've seen what the Sorn does to swimmers. It doesn't leave enough to bury." They adjusted their pack straps, checked the knife at their hip out of habit rather than need, and started down the switchback trail. "Stay behind me. Look pitiful. You're very good at it without even trying, which I mean as a compliment."

* * *

The warden's name, Tamsin discovered within the first minute of conversation, was Odric Vail, and he was the most transparently corrupt man Tamsin had met since the last corrupt man they'd bargained with, which had been three days ago in a market town whose name they'd already let slide out of memory like water through fingers.

Odric received them in the gatehouse parlor, which had once been a chapel—the alcove for a saint's statue still gaped empty above the hearth—and had since been furnished according to the principle that nothing says authority like furniture you stole from someone richer than you. He was a broad, soft man with a warden's chain worn over a merchant's velvet doublet, and he had the eyes of someone who had spent years learning exactly how much a traveler could be squeezed before they chose the chasm instead.

"Toll is ten crowns a head," Odric said, settling into his chair with the contentment of a man who enjoyed his own voice. "Twenty for the pair. Plus road tax, bridge maintenance levy, and—" he consulted a slate with great ceremony, "—the seasonal hazard surcharge. The gorge is treacherous this time of year."

"It's treacherous every time of year," Tamsin said. "That's why there's a bridge."

"That's why there's a *surcharge*." Odric smiled, showing teeth stained the brown of cheap tea. "But I'm a reasonable man. I take trade goods. Letters of credit. Information." His gaze slid past Tamsin to Wren, who stood very still by the door, hood up, and Odric's smile widened by a degree that made Tamsin's stomach tighten. "And sometimes I take interesting passengers off people's hands entirely. There are parties south of here paying well for—"

"They're my nephew," Tamsin said flatly. "Mute since the fever. You'll have to find your profits elsewhere."

"Mm." Odric didn't look convinced, but he also didn't look like a man who wanted to work that hard before lunch. He laced his fingers over his belly. "Ten crowns a head stands, then. Unless you'd care to make me a different offer. I hear things about travelers who come down out of Thornfell these days. Strange things. Things about grey cloaks asking questions in the lowland markets, and rewards posted for—"

"You want a memory."

"I want *value*," Odric corrected, pained. "A memory is value, isn't it? That's what they say about your kind. Brightwater, isn't it? The memory-thief of the high passes?" He leaned forward, and now the greed in his face was naked and almost childlike, which was somehow worse than the menace. "They say you can give a man anything. A first love. A victory. A night with a queen. My wife left me eleven years ago, Brightwater. Took the good silver and my best years. What could you give a man like that?"

And there it was. The opening, laid out neat as a snare line, and Tamsin felt the old craft rise in them like a tide—the pleasure of it, the *ease*, the beautiful mechanics of a mind waiting to be rearranged. This was the part they'd always loved, if they were honest, and they were trying very hard these days to be honest: not the coin, though the coin mattered, but the moment when another person's interior became clay in their hands. The intimacy of theft. It had never felt like violence. It had felt like being *let in*.

They thought of the toll-warden's wife, real or imagined. They thought of whatever poor soul Odric would lift that memory from to pay for it—because memories didn't come from nowhere; Tamsin's craft had always involved a certain creative borrowing, a reshuffling of other people's lives to furnish a client's fantasy. Somewhere downstream of this transaction, someone would wake with a hole in their afternoon and never know why.

They thought of Wren, watching from the doorway with those too-old eyes.

"One memory," Tamsin said. "Free passage for both of us, both directions, and your soldiers forget our faces. In exchange, you'll have the finest hour of your life."

Odric's grin split wide. "Done."

* * *

Tamsin worked with the door barred and the warden settled deep in his stolen armchair, wine at his elbow, eyes already glazing in anticipation. This part required touch—two fingers to the temple, light as a moth landing—and a careful, deliberate unspooling.

They built it the way they built all their best work: from true materials, honestly arranged.

An evening in late summer, in a garden that smelled of crushed thyme and warm stone. A woman with ink-stained fingers—Tamsin gave her Odric's jaw softened, Odric's own heavy laugh given to her, because clients trusted what they recognized—laughing at something he'd said, really laughing, head tipped back. A table between them with a jug of wine and a plate of figs. Fireflies starting up in the hedges. And the feeling underneath it all, the one Tamsin wove through everything like gold thread through rough cloth: *being chosen*. Being the person someone turned toward in a crowded room. Being, for one whole unhurried evening, enough.

Tamsin took the details from a hundred borrowed corners—a gardener's memory of thyme, a scholar's memory of fireflies, a widow's memory of being laughed with after years of being laughed at—and stitched them into a single seamless hour, and pressed it gently into the warden's mind beside the place where his bitterness lived, and watched his face go slack with wonder.

"Oh," Odric breathed. "Oh, she's—"

"She's laughing," Tamsin said quietly. "You told her the joke about the bishop and the mule. She's laughing."

The warden wept. Big, unembarrassed tears rolling down into the collar of his velvet doublet, his huge hands open on his knees like a man who had set down something heavy. For a long moment the gatehouse was silent except for the river below, roaring in its gorge, and Tamsin sat with two fingers still resting against a stranger's temple and felt—nothing like triumph.

This was the harm. Not the theft itself; theft, Tamsin could justify until the end of days, and often had. The harm was this: that Odric Vail would carry that garden with him for the rest of his life, would return to it nightly the way a dog returns to a buried bone, would measure every real hour of his shabby, extorting life against a fiction Tamsin had assembled from scraps of strangers' joy—and find it wanting. Tamsin hadn't given him a gift. They'd given him a wound dressed up as a gift, and he'd paid for it gladly, and he would never know.

"There," Tamsin said, withdrawing their hand. "It's yours. It won't fade."

Odric blinked at them, wet-eyed, radiant, ridiculous. "Brightwater. By the saints. I—" He stopped, gathered himself, and then, in a gesture that undid Tamsin completely, pressed a small purse into their hands along with the promised passes. "For the boy. Buy him something sweet on the far side. Life's short."

*Life's short,* Tamsin thought, walking out into the gray morning with Wren at their heel and blood-money jingling in their pocket. *And some of us spend it selling people counterfeit sunsets.*

The soldiers waved them across. One of them, a young one with a chapped face, smiled at Wren and got, in return, the solemn nod Wren bestowed on very few people, and the soldier looked absurdly pleased about it for the rest of the visible bridge.

Halfway across, with the gorge yawning below and the river's roar swallowing footsteps, Tamsin leaned close to Wren's ear.

"What I just did in there," they said, "that's the trade. That's what I've done hundreds of times. Beautiful lies, stitched from stolen pieces, sold to people hungry enough to buy." They watched the far tower grow closer. "I used to tell myself it was harmless. Everyone walks away happy, no one bleeds. But it's not harmless, little wren. It's worse than robbery, in a way. Robbery takes what you have. That—" they tapped their own temple, "—takes what you might have been. Fills the space where a real life should grow with something pretty and dead."

Wren's small hand found theirs. Didn't squeeze, didn't speak. Just held on, the way they did, and Tamsin found that the gesture landed harder than any sermon the Spire could have composed.

"Don't get sentimental," Tamsin muttered. "I'm still a thief. I just—" They exhaled. "I'm a thief who's started reading the labels. There's a word for that. I think the word is *worse*."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the high road, a mile past the bridge, sheltered by an overhang of granite that some ancient flood had undercut into a shallow cave. Tamsin got a small fire going—small, screened, the way Brannoc had taught them fifteen years ago—and divided hard bread and the last of the honey from the herbalist's stores, giving Wren the larger share and lying about why.

It was Wren who noticed the watchers.

The child went still mid-chew, gaze fixed on the dark slope above the hollow, and touched Tamsin's wrist—two taps, their private signal for *look, don't turn your head*.

Tamsin looked without turning their head. Two points of reflected firelight, high on the ridge, where no honest traveler would sit. Then a third, farther east, positioned to cover the road back toward the bridge. Positioned, Tamsin realized with a cold settling in their gut, not to intercept—but to observe. To count. To note which direction they took in the morning.

Grey cloaks wouldn't watch. Grey cloaks would come down the slope with chains and questions. These watchers sat patient as herons, and herons only waited that still when they knew where the fish had to swim.

"The bridge," Tamsin breathed. "He let us through too easily. Or—" and the pieces clicked together with an ugly, almost admiring click, "—he didn't let us through. Someone bought his cooperation before we ever arrived. Let the thief cross. Mark the road. Pick the ground *ahead*, somewhere quiet, somewhere with no witnesses and no walls." They stared into their small fire and felt the shape of the trap assembling itself in the dark country beyond the firelight. "Veyra doesn't chase. Veyra herds."

Wren's hand closed tighter on their sleeve. In the firelight the child's face was pale and grave, and their lips moved—shaping, as they sometimes did, words in the dead tongue, a phrase Tamsin had heard twice before and never translated:

*Vael Auren. Vael Auren.*

"Yes," Tamsin said softly. "I know. Home. We're still going." They banked the fire low and began, with great care, to plan a route that did not exist on any map—which was, at least, the sort of problem Tamsin had always been equipped for. "But tomorrow we stop being fish."

Far off in the dark, down the valley they would have to walk in the morning, something moved through the trees without hurrying at all.
