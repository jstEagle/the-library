## 11. Brannoc's Choice

Brannoc Hale had been following two ghosts through the high passes of Thornfell for three days, and the ghosts were better at it than he was.

He'd known they would be. That was the bitter joke of it. He had taught Tamsin Brightwater half of what they knew about vanishing—the other half they'd stolen from somebody's skull, no doubt—and now every trick he used to track them was a trick they already knew to counter. He'd find a cold fire ring and a snare set backwards to mislead him, and he'd sit on his heels in the snow and laugh until the laugh turned into something else that hurt his chest.

"Idiot," he told himself, for the hundredth time. "Absolute idiot."

The word didn't stick where he aimed it. It kept sliding off onto the memory of Wren's face in the lamplight of Brannoc's kitchen—mud-streaked, solemn, chewing bread like the bread might be revoked at any moment. He had sold that child. Had named a price. Had watched coin change hands and told himself it was business, that Brightwater would understand, that everybody sold something eventually and at least the child would eat.

Then he'd met the buyers' escort on the lowland road, and seen the grey under their traveling cloaks, and heard one of them say *vessel* the way Spire men said it, which was the way butchers said *carcass*.

Three days of snow and self-loathing later, here he was: fifty-two years old, knees like rusted hinges, chasing children up a mountain because his conscience had finally gotten louder than his debts.

The storm caught him at dusk on the fourth day, which was fitting. Storms always caught Brannoc at dusk. He sheltered under an overhang that some long-dead shepherd had cut into the rock, built a fire small enough to hide, and shared his last strip of dried mutton with a marmot that had clearly robbed travelers before and saw no reason to stop with him.

"You're a criminal," he informed the marmot. It ate faster.

That night he dreamed of Tamsin at nineteen, laughing on a rooftop in Marrowgate with someone else's wedding memory still bright in their eyes, saying, *Brannoc, everything can be carried if you pack it right.* He woke with frost in his beard and made himself a promise shaped like a question: *if you find them, what exactly are you going to say?*

He practiced versions of it while he walked. *I'm sorry* — too small. *I've come to help* — too generous to himself, as though helping were a thing he'd thought of first. *They're not buyers, they're inquisitors* — true, useful, and a fine way to avoid saying anything else at all.

In the end, of course, he didn't get to choose his words, because he stepped over a rise on the fifth morning directly into a crossbow bolt's worth of silence and found Tamsin Brightwater sitting on a boulder eating an apple, with the child behind them and a knife already out of its sheath and resting, very casually, against their knee.

"Hello, Brannoc," Tamsin said.

*

The clearing was small and walled with pine, chosen—if Tamsin had chosen it, and Brannoc would have bet his boots they had—so that the only ways out were the two ways in, both visible from the boulder. The child stood near the far pines with snow up to their shins, watching Brannoc with an expression he couldn't read and didn't dare guess at. Not afraid, exactly. *Weighing.* Like a horse deciding whether you were going to hit it.

Brannoc put his hands out from his sides, palms open, the old smuggler's greeting. "I'm alone."

"You were alone at the toll bridge too, I imagine," Tamsin said. They hadn't moved. The apple turned once in their fingers. "And alone when Veyra's people swept Marrowgate. And alone when my fence sold me out, though that one wasn't your doing. You're just standing in a long line of alone."

"I deserve that."

"You deserve worse than talk, Brannoc." But the knife went back into the sheath, and Tamsin hopped down off the boulder, and there they were: thinner than he remembered, wind-scoured, eyes with shadows under them that hadn't been there a season ago. Something in his chest did the painful folding thing again. "You took coin for us. Don't stand there looking wounded. I know exactly what I look like on a ledger. I've written enough of them."

"I didn't know who the buyers were." His voice came out lower than he intended. "I want that understood, and then I want you to ignore it, because it doesn't matter. I should have asked. I've spent thirty years knowing better than to take a job without asking who's paying, and I took this one because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because it was easy. Because you'd gone soft, is what I told myself. Thief takes in a stray, loses her edge, becomes a story people tell. And I could tidy the whole thing away with a purse of silver and sleep fine."

"And do you?"

"Sleep?"

"Fine."

Brannoc looked at the child. Wren was still watching him, head tilted slightly, and the wind moved the pine branches above them with a sound like slow breathing. "No," he said. "I sleep like everyone I ever cheated is taking turns sitting on my chest. It's very restful. I recommend it."

Something flickered at the corner of Tamsin's mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its winter stores. "Wren," they said, without turning around. "This is Brannoc Hale. He's a smuggler, a liar, and the only man in Thornfell who makes worse porridge than I do."

Wren said nothing. Of course they said nothing; Tamsin had told Brannoc about the dead tongue in a letter two winters back, back when letters between them had been jokes and route-markings instead of ledgers and apologies. But the child crossed the snow toward them—slowly, still weighing—and stopped an arm's length away and looked up into Brannoc's face with an attention so complete it was nearly a touch.

Then Wren reached out, took Brannoc's large scarred hand in both small ones, turned it palm-up, and traced one finger along the old burn scar that ran from wrist to thumb.

"That's from the Kestrel job," Brannoc said, stupidly, because the child couldn't understand him and it seemed important to fill the quiet. "Rope burn. I grabbed a line I shouldn't have."

Wren looked up at him and nodded once, gravely, as if this confirmed something. As if the hand itself had been vouched for.

"Well," said Brannoc, to nobody, feeling his throat go unreliable. "Well."

"Told you," Tamsin said quietly. "Worse than talk."

*

They sat by a proper fire after that—Tamsin insisted on building it themselves, in their own pattern, which Brannoc recognized and pretended not to—and shared the food neither party had much of. Brannoc produced hard cheese and a heel of black bread. Tamsin produced the apples, which they claimed to have found and which Brannoc chose to believe, because the alternative was remembering that orchards along this stretch belonged to a widow who paid him protection money.

"The buyers," Tamsin said, when the cheese was gone. "Tell me."

"Not buyers." Brannoc fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. Saying it aloud, to Tamsin's face, was harder than he'd rehearsed, and he had rehearsed it maybe forty times. "Grey cloaks under the wool. One of them wore a Spire signet on a cord under his shirt—he thought his collar hid it, but I've been cheated by better-dressed men than him. They called the child a vessel. They talked about extraction like it was ore." He made himself look up. "Veyra's people, Brightwater. The chief inquisitor's own chain of custody. Whoever those lowland lords I thought I was selling to were, they never existed. The sale was Spire bait from the first message, and I swallowed it like a trout swallowing a hook with the hook still in my own mouth."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked down through the branches. Behind them, Wren sat very still, watching the fire with an expression Brannoc had no name for—something older than a child's face ought to hold.

"How far ahead of us are they?" Tamsin asked at last. Businesslike. That was Tamsin all over; grief went in the ledger, and the road came first.

"Ahead? No." He shook his head. "That's the other thing. They're not ahead of you, they're *around* you. They let you pass the toll bridge—I know they did, because the warden sent a pigeon the hour you crossed, and I bought the pigeon's cousin's keeper a drink to hear about it. They're herding you, Tamsin. Every easy pass has been easy on purpose. There's a valley east of the Weeping Stair where three of their wagons are parked under canvas like pilgrims, and there are men on the ridgelines who are very good at not being seen, and the only reason you haven't seen them is they don't want you to yet."

"They want us somewhere specific."

"Don't you?" Brannoc spread his hands. "Think like a thief for a second—you're better at it than me. If you wanted a vessel of the old tongue delivered alive and unspoiled, where would you steer it? Somewhere the Spire's writ runs thin? Or somewhere the Spire's oldest stones are?"

Tamsin's apple-hand stopped midair.

"Oh," they said softly. "Oh, they're letting us run *home*. They think the child will lead us to—" They stopped themselves, glanced back at Wren, and Brannoc watched a whole conversation happen in the space of two heartbeats without a single word exchanged. Whatever Tamsin knew about dragons and drowned cities, they weren't going to say it in front of the fire with a stranger listening. Which meant, Brannoc realized, with a strange warm ache, that he was still a stranger.

Good, he thought. Keep it that way. Strangers can't be betrayed by strangers.

"There's more," he said instead. "The man I handed the child contract to—a broker called Ferren Mole, works out of Coldharbour—he's dead."

Tamsin's eyes came up sharp. "You killed him?"

"What? No! Brightwater, honestly. No, he's dead the ordinary way, which in his case means somebody else got to him first. Found floating in the harbor canal with his pockets full and his ledger missing. The Spire doesn't leave loose threads, and Ferren was nothing but thread." He hesitated, then decided that if he was going to spend his conscience he might as well spend it all at once. "Which means they'll come for me next. I'm the last living link to the sale. So understand what I'm telling you when I say I'm not here for forgiveness and I'm not here to hide. I'm here because I couldn't—" His voice cracked like green wood, and he glared at the fire as if it had done it. "Because I held that child's hand over a table and counted out what they were worth, and I have to spend the rest of my life making that number wrong."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above them a crow complained about the weather, at length, and with feeling.

It was Wren who moved first. The child got up, crossed the snow, and sat down on Brannoc's left side—close, deliberate, the way you'd sit by someone shivering—and after a moment leaned their shoulder against his arm. Not speaking. Just there. Warm through his coat.

Brannoc discovered that his eyes were doing something embarrassing and stared very hard at the flames until they stopped.

"They did that to me too," Tamsin said quietly. "First night. Knocked on my door half-frozen, walked in like they owned it, and I've been owned ever since." They tossed Brannoc the last apple. "Don't read too much into it. They're a terrible judge of character."

"I'm an excellent judge," Brannoc said thickly, "of apples," and bit into it before his face could betray him further.

*

They argued about the route until dark, which was how the two of them had always shown affection, and it was good, it was *almost* like before, except that twice Brannoc caught Tamsin glancing at Wren before answering—as if checking whether the truth was safe to say in front of the child—and twice Wren answered anyway, in that ringing dead language, pointing at the map scratched in the dirt with total certainty.

"She says no," Tamsin translated, the third time it happened. "She says the western fork floods with meltwater by week's end and we'd lose two days. She says—" Tamsin paused, and something complicated crossed their face. "She says the mountain remembers the way even when the maps forget."

"She says, does she." Brannoc looked at the child, who gazed back with the serene authority of a general reviewing troops. "Brightwater, I've run these passes since before you could pick a lock. There's no meltwater in the western fork. There's barely a *fork*."

"There is," Tamsin said simply. "There's a whole canyon. I've seen it in her memories. It's older than anything you or I have ever stolen, Brannoc, and I need you to trust that when she points, you point too."

And here it was, Brannoc thought. Here was the actual choice, the real one, the one he'd been climbing toward through five days of snow. Not forgive-or-don't, not help-or-hang. This: whether he could follow a mute child's finger into country no map agreed existed, on the word of a thief he'd sold and a magic he didn't believe in, because believing had cost him everything comfortable and following cost nothing but the last of his pride.

Pride, he reflected, was heavy anyway. Good riddance.

"Fine," he said. "But if the mountain remembers wrong and we all drown in a canyon that isn't there, I want it noted that I objected."

"Noted," Tamsin said. "Loudly. At length. Possibly in song."

"It's what I'd have wanted."

Wren made a sound then—a short, bright syllable in the dead tongue that needed no translating, because some things sound the same in every language ever spoken. It was, unmistakably, a laugh. The child laughed at him, pointed at the map, drew a little squiggle that was presumably Brannoc drowning, and laughed again.

"All right," Brannoc said, pointing at the child with his apple core. "All right, Your Majesty. We'll do it your way. But I'm keeping the map."

He smoothed the dirt flat with his palm and began, slowly, carefully, drawing the passes as he knew them—every smuggler's shortcut, every shepherd's lie, thirty years of Thornfell poured out into the firelight for two people to use and one mountain to correct. Above the pines the clouds were tearing open, and through the gap he could see the high peaks going gold with sunset, and beyond them, faint as breath on glass, a shape in the eastern crags that might have been a ruin and might have been nothing at all.

Wren went very still, looking at it.

"What?" Tamsin asked. "What is it? What do you see?"

The child didn't answer. The child had stopped seeing any of them at all—had gone somewhere else entirely, eyes wide, lips moving around a word too old for the air to carry. And down in the valleys below, so far off it was almost a rumor, Brannoc saw lights kindle in a line: lanterns, torches, watchfires. Grey cloaks, moving at last, closing the trap they'd spent weeks walking shut.

"They've stopped waiting," he said quietly. "Whatever she sees—we'd best hope the mountain remembers fast."
