## 12. The Drowned Road

The mouth of the cavern did not look like a door. It looked like the mountain had taken a bite of itself and thought better of it.

Tamsin stood at the edge of it, hip-deep in bracken, and consulted every memory they had stolen about the passes beneath Thornfell. There was nothing. That in itself was remarkable—Tamsin had memories of things that had never happened, memories bought off drunk pilgrims, memories lifted from a dead woman's sister about a wedding no one else remembered. But of this hole in the world: nothing.

"It's not in anyone's head," Tamsin said. "That should tell us something. Usually when nobody knows a thing, it's because nobody survived knowing it."

Wren stood at the water's edge where the stream spilled into the dark, and did not hesitate at all.

The child had been like this for two days now—not speaking, not since the hermit's hut and those six words that Tamsin still carried around like coins in a pocket they kept touching—but *pointing*. Pointing with the flat certainty of someone reading instructions off the inside of their own eyelids. Left at the split oak. Down past the shrine to a god whose name had worn off the stone. And now here, to this swallowed gap in the mountainside where the water went and did not come back.

"You understand," Tamsin said, crouching beside them, "that everything I know about not dying comes from going the other way from holes like this."

Wren looked up at them. In the grey morning light their eyes had that quality again—the one Tamsin could not name, the one that made them feel like a candle flame being considered by a great deal of wind. The child patted Tamsin's hand. Twice. Patiently. The way one reassures a large nervous animal.

"Oh, well. If we're doing gestures." Tamsin shrugged their pack higher and tied the lantern to their belt. "For the record, I want it noted that I have been talked into this by someone who is approximately the height of a fence post."

* *

The first hour was merely terrible, which lured Tamsin into optimism.

They followed the stream down through a throat of stone, the lantern throwing their shadows huge against walls slick as the inside of a throat. The air tasted of iron and old rain. Wren moved ahead without a waver, small feet finding purchase where Tamsin's slid, and twice stopped before Tamsin even saw why—a loose slope, a hollow that rang wrong underfoot—and steered them around it with a touch to the wrist.

"You've been here before," Tamsin said, somewhere in the second hour, and immediately heard how foolish it sounded. "Here. Beneath here. Somewhere in there—" they tapped their own temple, "—there's a map of a place you've never walked."

Wren considered this, walking. Then they made a gesture Tamsin had never seen from them: both hands open, turning outward, like someone offering up the contents of their palms. Not *yes*. Something more like *it was given to me*, or perhaps *I am remembering for someone else*.

Tamsin knew that feeling intimately. Half the cargo in their own skull belonged to other people.

"Right," they said quietly, and stopped talking about it, because some things go strange when you say them out loud in the dark.

* *

It was in the third hour that the carvings began, and it was the carvings that broke something loose in Tamsin's chest and left it swinging on a hinge.

The lantern found them first as scratches, then as lines, then—where the passage opened into a gallery wide enough that their voices came back changed, older—as whole walls of them. Figures cut deep into the living stone, worn but unmistakable, and Tamsin held the light up and forgot, entirely, how to breathe in an orderly fashion.

Dragons.

Vast coiling bodies rendered in maybe nine strokes each, the way a master hand can do more with less than a lifetime of fussy detail. They arched over the walls like weather. And beneath them, between their wings, along the same streets the artists had imagined—people. Small figures. Some carrying things. Some with their hands raised, and Tamsin's eye, trained by years of lifting other people's treasures, caught what the figures carried: purses. Sacks. A figure slipping a ring from another figure's finger while a dragon wheeled overhead, indifferent as sunrise.

"Wren," Tamsin said. Their voice came out sanded down. "Come look at this."

The child came. Stood before the wall. Put one small hand flat against the stone, fingers resting in a groove worn by some other hand, centuries or millennia ago, and Tamsin watched them mouth silent syllables in the dead tongue, reading.

Then Wren pointed—at the thief figure, the little pickpocket under the dragon's wing—and then, gravely, at Tamsin.

"I beg your pardon."

Wren nodded. Firmly. Then pointed at themselves, and then at the largest dragon, and then—here the gentle humor of it landed in Tamsin like a warm brick through a window—they pointed at the dragon's open jaws, and mimed, with great dignity, being eaten.

Tamsin laughed. It startled out of them, echoed down the gallery, came back sounding like several other people laughing too. "So that's the division of labor, is it? You get devoured by history and I pick its pockets?" They turned back to the wall, and the laugh faded into something else, something slower. "You know, I always thought I was old. Old trade, old roads, my grandmother's grandmother's tricks. I used to say memory-thieving was older than any kingdom, and I said it smug, like it made me gentry."

They ran a thumb along the carved thief's shoulder.

"But this was old before *old*. Whoever cut this didn't just live alongside dragons. They built us in. Thieves and dragons, side by side, in the founding stone." Tamsin stepped back, and the vertigo arrived—not of height, but of scale, the floor of their whole life revealing itself as a footnote in a book whose title they couldn't read. "Every road I've ever run, every fence, every toll, every smuggler I've robbed or saved—it all happens in the margin. Somebody wrote the actual page down here, under the mountain, and then everyone agreed to forget it."

Wren tugged their sleeve. Pointed onward, down.

"Yes, all right," Tamsin muttered. "Down. Where it's wetter. Naturally."

* *

The water came up to their knees, then their thighs, then—when the passage dropped through a broken stair into a flooded vault—it came up to everything, and they swam, or Wren swam and Tamsin clung to a floating timber beam and swore with creativity while the child paddled ahead like an indignant otter.

The vault above them was dry-lit.

That was the part Tamsin would not be able to explain later, and would not try to. A soft gold suffusion came from nowhere—from the stone itself, from veins running through the ceiling like the mountain had a circulatory system and something in it still, faintly, pulsed. It lit the water green-gold. It lit carvings on the vault walls, more figures, more dragons, and here the work was finer, closer, faces with expressions, and Tamsin saw among them—stopped swimming a moment to be sure—figures with hoods. Figures with knives. Figures holding small flames in cupped hands, offering them up.

"Thieves," Tamsin said softly, treading water. "They're all thieves. Or priests. Or—" they laughed once, breathlessly, "—or there wasn't a difference, back then. Maybe that's what got forgotten. Not the map. The *job description*."

Wren had gone still, out ahead, head tipped back toward the golden veins in the rock. And Tamsin saw the child's lips moving again, that silent reading, and saw—for the first time since the hermit's hut—fear on Wren's face. Not of the water. Of the light. Like someone watching a fire burn down in a hearth they are responsible for.

"Nearly there?" Tamsin asked, though the question answered itself; Wren was already swimming on, faster now, urgent, and Tamsin hauled themselves along the beam in the child's wake and tried not to think about how far below their feet the true bottom might be.

* *

They surfaced in a chamber that had once, indisputably, been a road.

A real one—wide, paved, running straight as a ruled line into darkness, its stones fitted so close that Tamsin could not have slipped a knife-blade between them, and Tamsin had spent a misspent decade learning exactly that trick. Water sheeted thinly across it, ankle-deep, mirror-still, so that they walked upon a second road hanging upside-down beneath them, and overhead the golden veins ran parallel to their path like lamps lit for a homecoming no one had expected.

Columns flanked the way. On each column, the carvings again, and here—fresh, almost unworn—Tamsin could finally read the whole story in sequence if not in sense: dragons descending. People fleeing. People returning. A figure small as a fingernail standing before a dragon's lowered head, and the dragon's fire pouring—not into destruction, but into the figure's hands, and the figure carrying it away, and the city behind them going dark, deliberately, like a lamp shuttered for the night.

"Hiding it," Tamsin whispered. "Someone hid it. The fire. They poured it into a person and put the person somewhere safe and let the whole world believe it burned." They looked down at Wren, dripping, small, solemn in the gold light, and finished the sentence they'd been dodging since a storm-night knock at their door: "Somewhere safe. Somewhere forgotten. Somewhere like Thornfell, where nobody knows anything because nobody survived knowing it."

Wren looked up at them. And nodded, once, and for the first time in all their days together the child reached out and took Tamsin's hand first—not guided there, not pointed, just took it—and they walked the drowned road together, thief and Heir, footsteps ringing off the columns in a rhythm that felt, horribly and wonderfully, like a choir answering a bell.

Tamsin's whole world had been a footnote. They were walking into the main text now, and the main text was waking up around them: the golden veins brightening as they passed, guttering like things remembering how, the water trembling with a pulse that had nothing to do with their steps.

And then the pulse stuttered.

The vein-light ahead flickered—not dimming, exactly. Flinching. The gold along the next column shrank back into the stone the way a hand pulls back from a stove, and the water's tremble changed rhythm, and far down the road, in the direction Wren had been leading them with such surety, Tamsin heard a sound that did not belong to any sleeping city.

Boots. Many of them, splashing in unison, disciplined as a hymn.

Tamsin pulled Wren behind the nearest column and snuffed the lantern with a squeeze of a shaking hand, and in the sudden half-dark they saw it stenciled into the flooding light of the veins ahead: grey cloaks, steel, and at the column's edge a tall figure raising a gloved hand, halting the line, listening.

Inquisitor Veyra had found the drowned road first.

"Well," Tamsin breathed into Wren's hair, heart hammering against the child's shoulder, "you wanted to go home. Question is whether we go through her, or find your front door the long way round—and I'd bet every stolen memory in my head she's already thought of the long way round too."
