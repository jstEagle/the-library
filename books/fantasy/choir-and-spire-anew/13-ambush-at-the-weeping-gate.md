## 13. Ambush at the Weeping Gate

The water found them before the light did.

Tamsin heard it first as a whisper in the dark—a sound like a hundred small mouths drinking—and then the tunnel opened around them and there it was: the Weeping Gate, a wall of pale stone seamed with springs, water sliding down its face in silver threads that caught the faint glow of Wren's cupped hands. The child had been carrying that glow since the flooded road began, a soft amber warmth between the palms, like holding a candle made of skin.

"You could have mentioned," Tamsin whispered, "that you were a lantern."

Wren glanced back. In the amber light their face was grave, and they touched two fingers to their own sternum, then pointed down—*it comes from below, from home*—and Tamsin's stomach did something complicated and unwelcome. Every hour underground, the child grew less like a stray and more like a compass needle swinging toward something enormous. Compasses didn't need feeding. Compasses didn't hold your hand when the dark got close.

*Stop it,* Tamsin told themselves firmly. *You're a thief. Thieves deliver goods. They don't get attached to the packaging.*

The Gate itself was a marvel nobody alive had carved: an archway twice the height of a man, its stone worn smooth by ten thousand years of falling water, and across its face a frieze so old the figures had melted into suggestion. Tamsin raised the little lamp they carried and traced the shapes. A dragon, wings folded, head bowed low. Beside it, smaller figures—people—with their hands open. Not in surrender. In *offering*.

"Thieves," Tamsin murmured. "Look at that one." A figure with a satchel slung across its back, caught mid-stride, glancing over its shoulder. "Somebody carved us into the walls down here. Somebody thought we belonged."

Wren reached up and pressed their palm flat against the archway, right where the water fell thickest, and for a moment the whole gate seemed to breathe—the springs brightening, dimming, brightening again, in time with something slow and vast beneath the mountain. The child's lips moved. No sound came out, but Tamsin had spent enough weeks inside that small skull to know the shape of the word.

*Home.*

"Almost home," Tamsin said, and hated how gentle their voice came out. "One more gate. Then I hand you off to whatever's waiting on the other side and go back to being a bad person full-time."

That was the plan. That had always been the plan, ever since Brannoc Hale had caught up with them on the ridge three days ago with rain running off his beard and shame all over his face, saying *the buyers are Spire, Tam, every last one of them; I sold you to the noose and I can't un-sell you but I can walk ahead of you.* He'd gone ahead that morning to scout the passage beyond the Gate. He should have been back by now.

Tamsin told themselves he'd found a dry spot and fallen asleep. Old men did that. It was practically his defining feature.

Then Wren went rigid, both hands flying shut, snuffing the amber light—and in the sudden blackness Tamsin smelled it under the clean mineral smell of the spring water: tallow, boot leather, and cold iron.

"Lamp out," they breathed, though it already was. They pulled Wren against the wall, into the shallow alcove behind the carved thief with the satchel, and put their mouth to the child's ear. "Whatever happens—whatever you see—do not make a sound. Not even the glowing. Especially not the glowing."

Wren's hand found theirs in the dark and squeezed once. It was not a frightened squeeze. It was the squeeze of someone who had watched cities burn and understood exactly what was about to happen, and who was choosing, deliberately, to be brave about it. Tamsin's chest hurt.

Down the flooded corridor, torchlight bloomed.

* * *

Veyra had counted the echoes.

She stood in the shallows before the Weeping Gate with forty Spire soldiers arrayed behind her in the black water, torches hooded, and she listened the way her mother had taught her to listen—to the way sound came back off wet stone. Two sets of footsteps. One heavy and adult, one light, arrhythmic, a child's uneven gait. And ahead of both, hours old now, the prints of a big man who walked like a man carrying regret: Brannoc Hale, sitting in her holding tent even now, guarded by six soldiers and his own catastrophically soft heart.

"He led them here," said Sergeant Okkel beside her, a slab of a woman with frostbite scars on her knuckles. "Just like you said he would."

"He led them here because he couldn't live with what he'd done, and this is the only kind of apology men like him know how to make." Veyra kept her voice flat. "It doesn't make him good. It makes him punctual."

She looked up at the Gate—at the ancient carvings the floodlights' edges barely touched—and felt the old anger rise in her throat, familiar as her own heartbeat. She had been born four valleys east of here, in a town whose name was now a legal designation: *Vael Auren Perimeter Settlement Seven.* Her mother had sung her songs in the old tongue before the Spire's edicts burned every book that held it. Her mother had died in the first week of the Ember Panic, when rumors of dragon-fire sent half the borderlands mad with fear and the other half mad with hunger, and soldiers of no banner at all had burned everything within thirty miles just to be sure.

She had been seven years old. She remembered the sky going orange. She remembered thinking, with a child's perfect logic, that if the fire was coming back, then someone had called it, and whoever had called it should be stopped.

Forty years later, the logic still held. It had simply acquired armor, a rank, and a warrant.

"The vessel approaches," she said. "On my mark, lanterns. Take the thief alive if it's convenient. The child—" She paused. Somewhere in the dark above them, water dripped from the ancient stone, patient as a metronome. "The child is not to be harmed. The child is to be *ended*, cleanly, and with dignity. There is a difference, and I will know if any of you forget it."

Okkel nodded. Forty torches stayed dark.

Veyra closed her eyes for one breath—one breath, no more—and let herself hear her mother's voice singing in a language the Spire had outlawed, and then she opened them.

"Now."

* * *

Light exploded across the cavern like the world cracking open.

Tamsin threw themselves sideways on pure thief's instinct, dragging Wren down behind the carved thief with the satchel, and even so the torch-glare seared afterimages across their vision. Figures in grey—forty? fifty?—rising out of the black water with crossbows levelled, and at their center a tall woman in the white-and-iron of the Spire's first circle, her hair cropped short and iron-grey at the temples, her voice cutting through the chaos like a blade through wet canvas.

"Hold! *Hold!* Nobody looses unless the thief runs!" The woman stepped forward through the shallows, hands empty and open, and Tamsin felt a flicker of professional admiration despite everything. She moved like someone who had never once needed to shout twice. "Tamsin Brightwater. Memory-thief, smuggler, liar of long standing. You're surrounded, exhausted, and standing in water up to your knees next to a child you cannot outrun anyone carrying. I'm going to offer you the only honest bargain of your life. Take it."

"And what's the honest price?" Tamsin called back, keeping Wren tucked behind them, keeping their voice steady through sheer decades of practice at sounding steadier than they were.

"The child dies. You walk away free. Full amnesty, signed by the Spire itself, plus whatever gold it takes to make sure you never have to steal another memory as long as you live." The woman tilted her head. "I'm told you have a sister-shaped hole in your ledger. I imagine you'd like to stop adding to it."

The words landed exactly where she'd aimed them, because of course they did; the Spire had files, the Spire had *everything*, and Tamsin felt the offer sink into them with horrible sweetness. Freedom. Gold. No more running, no more Brannocs wanting payment, no more looking over their shoulder into forever.

And Wren, dead behind them in the water. Small. Silent. Ended *cleanly, with dignity.*

"That's the bargain?" Tamsin said. "You've come all this way, inquisitor, and that's the best you've got?"

"It's the only one I've got. Everything else is theater." The woman's eyes—pale, tireless—moved from Tamsin to the child and something flickered across her face, quickly buried. "My name is Veyra. I was born in the ruins of the place your little passenger is walking us toward. I have watched what the old fire does when it wakes. I am not your enemy, Brightwater. I am the wall between a war and everyone you've ever sold a memory to. Give me the child."

Behind Tamsin, in the dark of the alcove, small fingers pressed against their back. Not pulling them away. Just *there*. Saying, in the only language the child had left: *your choice, thief. Whatever you choose, I'll believe you meant it.*

Oh, that was unfair. That was unforgivably unfair.

"I'll tell you what," Tamsin said loudly, buying seconds, mind racing through exits—the flooded corridor behind them, a mile of drowning; the narrow chimney they'd passed an hour back, too tight for soldiers, maybe; the— 

And then the ceiling of the passage behind Veyra's soldiers came down.

Not all of it. One section, precisely aimed, tons of rock and ten thousand years of patience crashing into the flooded corridor directly at the rear of the Spire line, sealing it, throwing a wall of spray across forty torches. In the roaring dark that followed, a bellow rolled through the cavern that Tamsin would have known anywhere—the voice of a man who had once talked his way past three toll-wardens while actively bleeding.

"*RUN, TAM!*"

"Brannoc," Tamsin breathed, and something in their chest tore neatly down the middle.

Because he hadn't been captured. Or—he had, and he'd gotten loose, or he'd let himself be taken, or—none of it mattered, none of it mattered *at all*, because the big fool was somewhere in that collapsing tunnel buying them seconds with his body, and the Spire line was breaking apart into shouting and lantern-light and Sergeant Okkel bellowing for order, and the only opening in the entire world was straight through the middle of it, past Veyra, through the Weeping Gate itself.

"Tamsin." Veyra hadn't turned. She stood in the chaos like a stake driven into a riverbed, and her pale eyes found Tamsin's over the water. "*Don't.*"

"Sorry," Tamsin said—and meant, absurdly, almost tenderly—"but I've never once in my life done what I was told by someone taller than me."

They ran.

* * *

*The water is loud and the light is loud and the man-woman in white says words like knives.*

*Wren runs because Tamsin runs. That is the whole of it. That is enough of it.*

*They splash through the shallows and the great gate is ahead, the gate that weeps, the gate that sang when Wren touched it, and behind them the noise becomes terrible—shouting, a crack of a crossbow, a scream that is not Brannoc's, thank the ash and ember, thank every dead name, not his—but then a sound that might be worse: the heavy, meaty sound of many hands taking one man down, and Brannoc's roar cut off mid-word, mid-*word*, and Tamsin flinches like they've been struck and nearly falls, and Wren catches their sleeve and hauls, small arms burning, because someone has to be strong now and it has apparently become Wren's turn.*

*Under the arch. Through the arch. The water-song rises all around them, joyful and awful, and the carvings flash past—the bowed dragon, the open hands, the little thief with the satchel—and Wren thinks:* *we are in the picture, we have always been in the picture* *—and then hands seize Tamsin from the side, a soldier, a grey cloak rising out of the pool, and Tamsin does something quick and vicious and learned in a hundred alleys, and the soldier goes down, and they are both through, both through, into the dark on the far side—*

*—and Tamsin stops.*

*Stops, at the mouth of the deep passage, with freedom sloping away below them and the battle raging behind, and turns around. Wren watches Tamsin's face do something terrible and honest: watch it want to keep running, and watch it lose that argument.*

"He's alive," Tamsin rasps. "They took him alive—I heard it—they took him and that means he's leverage, that means—" The thief drags a wet hand down their face. "That means every step we run, they tighten it. Oh, ashes. Oh, *ashes*, Brannoc, you stupid wonderful man."

*Taken. Taken alive. Because he chose it—because a man who wanted to die fighting would have died fighting, and Brannoc Hale had laid down in the water and let them bind him, so that the running ones could run.*

*Wren knows about debts. Wren is made of debts—owed to the dead, owed to the dying fire, owed to a city that sleeps under the water and dreams of wings. And now this loud, warm, guilty man has added himself to the ledger, paid in advance, for a child he met three days ago.*

*Wren takes Tamsin's hand. Points down the dark passage, where the water-glow is growing, where home pulls at their bones like the tide.*

*And then points back, at the chaos, at the place where Brannoc disappeared under grey cloaks, and presses their fist over their own heart, and holds Tamsin's eyes, and lets them see the promise in it:*

*I will come back for him. I swear it by the fire. When I am what I am meant to be, I will come back.*

*Tamsin looks at the child for a long moment. Something moves behind those thief's eyes—grief and guilt and a fierce, astonished tenderness, all fighting for the same doorway.*

"Yeah," Tamsin whispers. "Yeah, kid. Together, then. We come back together." They scrub at their face with both hands, and when they lower them, the thief's mask is back in place—almost. "Now run. Run like you owe me money."

*So Wren runs. Down, and down, into the breathing dark, where the amber light rises in their palms unbidden, where the water grows warm as bathwater, where far below something vast feels them coming and begins—slowly, sleepily, gratefully—to remember how to burn.*

*Behind them the Weeping Gate weeps on. Behind them, bound in wet rope in the ruins of a trap that half worked, Brannoc Hale starts, quietly, to laugh—because the thief got away, the child got away, and he is going to be hanged for it, and it was worth it, every bruised rib of it, worth it.*

*And behind them, standing alone in the shallows with her soldiers regrouping around her, Inquisitor Veyra watches the amber glow sink into the deep water like a sunset going the wrong direction, and does not give the order to pursue.*

"Not yet," she says, to no one, to the mountain. "You're going home, little heir. Good. So am I."

* * *

They swam the last stretch because there was no longer any choice: the passage dove beneath the rock, and the amber glow showed them air pockets like pearls strung along a drowned necklace, and Tamsin—who had once stolen a harbor pilot's memory of a wreck dive and never thanked him—kicked and gasped and surfaced at last into a space so large their stolen memories had no shelf for it.

A cavern like a buried sky. Pillars going up and up into darkness. Water below, black and warm. And everywhere, everywhere, the faint amber shimmer waking in the stone, answering Wren like a chorus answering its conductor.

Tamsin treaded water, coughing, shaking with cold and guilt and the image of Brannoc going down under grey cloaks, and looked at the small figure swimming steadily ahead of them, glowing, going home.

"Don't lose that light, kid," they whispered. "It's the only thing down here either of us can trust."

Ahead, in the deep, something answered—not in words, not yet, but in a pulse of warmth through the water that felt horribly, wonderfully like a heartbeat.
