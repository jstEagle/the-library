## 14. Vael Auren Rising

The water let go of Wren all at once, like a hand that had been holding too tight and suddenly remembered its manners.

They came up gasping, coughing, arms flailing against nothing—against air, sweet and cold and impossibly still. Tamsin surfaced beside them a heartbeat later, sputtering in a way that was very undignified for someone who claimed to be a professional.

"Right," Tamsin wheezed, treading water. "Note for the memoir: the drowned road is *extremely* well named."

Wren couldn't answer. Not because of their throat—the words had been coming easier lately, small rough common-tongue things they were still learning to hold—but because everything in them had gone quiet at once.

The cavern above the water was lit.

Not by torchlight. Not by the pale fungus-glow of the deep tunnels they'd walked for what felt like three days and might have been three hours. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, a soft gold that didn't flicker, didn't waver, didn't cast shadows so much as *suggest* them. It lay on the black water like oil on bread.

"Dormant dragon-fire," Tamsin said quietly. All the humor had drained out of their voice, leaving something Wren had never heard there before. Something almost like reverence. "I stole a memory of it once. An old man who'd seen it as a boy, before the Spire burned the last of it out of the lowlands. He said it looked like sunlight that had decided to stay."

Wren knew this place.

That was the terrible part. That was the part that made their chest hurt worse than the drowning had. They had never been here—not this body, not these small hands, not this short life spent in ditches and smuggler camps and Tamsin's drafty cabin—but they knew it the way you know your own name even when you're half-asleep. The water below them was not a flooded cave. It was a harbor. It had been a harbor when the mountains were young and the kingdoms were not yet kingdoms, and ships with sails like folded wings had come down the great channel from the sea to trade in glass and song.

*Vael Auren.*

The name rose in Wren's mind unbidden, and with it, for the first time since the burning spires, no fear came with it. Just the name, sitting there, patient as a stone.

"I can't see the far wall," Tamsin said. They had turned slowly in the water, and now they stopped turning, because ahead of them—where the gold light gathered thickest—something was rising out of the harbor. "Wren. Tell me you're seeing this."

Wren was seeing it.

A stairway, vast and shallow-sloped, climbing out of the water toward a gate the size of a cathedral door. It should have been drowned. Everything should have been drowned—the whole city had gone under the mountain lake a thousand years ago, every song agreed on that, every frightened history the Spire hadn't managed to burn. But the water stopped at the third step. It simply stopped, lying flat and obedient along an invisible line, as if the sea itself had been told *no further* and had never forgotten the instruction.

Tamsin swam for the stairs. Wren followed, arms aching, boots dragging like anchors. When they hauled themselves up onto the dry stone, the gold light soaked into their soaked clothes and—gently, absurdly—they began to steam dry.

"Well," said Tamsin. "That's new." They held up one arm and watched a droplet roll off their sleeve without wetting it. "The city's doing our laundry. I've had landlords who wouldn't do that."

Wren laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, half a sob, and they pressed both hands over their mouth.

Because the city had also done something else.

As the water steamed off them, sounds were rising from all around—faint, layered, overlapping. Voices. Hundreds of voices, thousands, speaking in the old tongue, in market-calls and lullabies and arguments about the price of fish, and underneath it all a music like a choir heard through a closed door. Not ghosts. Wren understood that immediately, the way they understood the stairs and the name. These weren't the dead speaking. These were *memories*, sunk into the stone the way dye sinks into wool, and the dormant fire was waking them, one thread at a time, because something had come home that the city had been waiting to remember itself for.

Something had come home. Wren's hands pressed harder over their mouth.

"Wren?" Tamsin crouched down to their level, dripping, wary, kind. "What is it? What are you hearing?"

Wren lowered their hands. The common words were still hard, still like carrying water in cupped palms, but they managed. "It knows me."

"Yeah," Tamsin said. "I got that impression." They glanced back at the impossible waterline, then up the stairway, at the enormous gate. "Question is whether that's going to be wonderful or horrible. In my experience it's usually both."

* * *

The gate was not locked. Of course it wasn't. Gates only lock against strangers.

It opened at Wren's touch—not with grinding stone but with a sound like a struck bell, deep and glad, and the doors swung inward on darkness that the gold light followed into, spilling across a floor of white veined with silver. Beyond stretched a hall so large that Wren's eyes refused at first to give it edges. Pillars rose like trees. Far above, where a ceiling should have been, there was only a soft glow, as if the whole city were lit from the sky downward.

And the voices grew louder.

Wren walked forward because their feet knew the way, and Tamsin walked beside them because Tamsin apparently could not stop themselves from walking into enormous dangerous glowing places, which explained a great deal about their life choices.

"It's louder in here," Tamsin murmured. "Can you tell what they're saying?"

Wren could. That was the horror of it. Every word arrived clear as rain.

*"—the Heir walks the Long Stair—"*

*"—look, look, the little one, the fire remembers—"*

*"—is it time? Is it finally time? We waited, we kept the lamps, we kept the lamps—"*

"They're happy," Wren whispered. Their own voice sounded wrong in the vastness, small and human. "They think I'm—" They stopped. The next word wouldn't come in the common tongue, so they said it in the old one, the word that had been following them through every dream since the burning spires: "*Heir.*"

Tamsin went very still. "The Dragonheir," they said. "The stories say the Dragonheir died when the city fell. All of them, line ended, fire scattered, war over." They looked at Wren with an expression Wren had learned to read on the road—the look Tamsin wore when a deal was more complicated than advertised. "Stories are written by people who want the war to be over."

The hall opened into streets.

That was the only way Wren could think of it. The great hall simply *became* a city, walls flowing outward into terraces and gardens and bridges between towers, all of it dry, all of it gold-lit, all of it waking. Fountains that had been dry for a thousand years began to murmur. Vines stirred in planters along the waysides, unfurling leaves that had been folded like sleeping hands. And everywhere, woven through the stone and the light, the memories thickened—a market assembling itself out of sound, a schoolyard's laughter echoing off walls that remembered children, a bell tolling somewhere high above with no rope and no ringer, just the memory of ringing.

"It's remembering itself," Wren said. They turned in a slow circle, and their heart did something painful and complicated, because it was beautiful, it was so beautiful, and every beautiful thing here was also a grave. "Tamsin. The city is remembering itself around us."

"I noticed," Tamsin said. Their voice was carefully light. "I'm choosing not to think too hard about what happens if it finishes."

But Wren was already thinking too hard about it. Because the memories weren't only markets and bells.

There were other threads in the weave. Darker ones. As they walked deeper into the waking city, those threads pulled at Wren like hooks, and each one came with a weight behind the sternum, a pressure like a hand pressing on the outside of their ribs.

*A tower falling. People running with bundles, with children, with nothing.*

*A dragon wheeling overhead—not the winged ships of the harbor memory but a true dragon, vast and grief-colored, breathing fire upward at the spires while people screamed in the old tongue: not in fear. In warning. In pleading. As if trying to make it stop.*

*A room. A small room. A woman singing a lullaby in the old tongue to a child with dark hair, and the smell of smoke coming under the door, and the song getting faster, and—*

Wren stopped walking. Stood in the middle of the golden street with their fists clenched at their sides and shook.

The dead were pressing. That was the truth of it, the thing they had no common words for yet. The city wasn't just remembering its fountains and its fish-markets. It was remembering everyone it had lost, and the losing, and it wanted—oh, how it wanted—to be *known*. A thousand years of silence, and now the fire was waking and someone had come home, and every memory that had ever sunk into this stone was rising at once like bubbles through deep water, all of them crying *remember me, remember me, remember me,* and they were all pushing toward Wren, because Wren was the one with ears to hear.

"Wren!" Tamsin caught their shoulders. "Hey. Hey. Look at me. You're shaking. What's happening?"

"They want to be remembered," Wren said. The words tore coming out. "All of them. Everyone who—it's so *heavy*, Tamsin, they're all waiting, they've been waiting so long, and I can feel every one of them, and I don't know how to hold—" Their breath hitched. "There were so many. There were *so many.*"

For a moment Tamsin said nothing. Then, awkwardly, gently, they did the thing they were worst at in the world: they knelt down in the golden street of a resurrected city and hugged a child they'd known for less than a season.

"Okay," Tamsin said into Wren's hair. "Here's what we know. One: you don't have to hold all of them tonight. Two: I have stolen a lot of memories, professionally, and I can tell you that memories don't actually need anything from you except to be witnessed. They're patient. The loud ones are just scared." A pause. "Three: whatever's making the light do this, it's probably at the center of the city, and centers of cities are where the important decisions live, and I would very much like to make some decisions before the decisions make us."

Wren laughed wetly against Tamsin's shoulder. "You always want to steal something."

"I contain multitudes," said Tamsin. "Most of them larcenous. Come on, Heir. Let's go meet the landlord."

* * *

The center of Vael Auren was a hill that had been a volcano once, ages before dragons or kingdoms, and the city had been built around it like a ring around a heart. Streets spiraled up its flanks. Bridges crossed its shoulders. And at its crown, visible from everywhere, stood a tower of black stone banded with gold—and the gold bands were beginning to glow.

The closer they climbed, the heavier the press of memory became, until Wren learned to walk with their eyes half-shut and their breath counted, the way the herbalist Maren had taught them to walk through storms. Tamsin stayed close, one hand hovering near Wren's shoulder, ready to anchor them. Once, passing a terrace garden, Wren stumbled and grabbed a stone balustrade, and the stone *answered*—a flood of a single afternoon, a gardener long dead humming while she tied back roses, contentment soaking through Wren like warmth through a window—and Wren gasped and let go and stood there trembling.

"It's not all bad," they said, wondering. "Some of them are just... ordinary. Happy. A lady tying roses."

"That's the part that gets me," Tamsin admitted quietly. "The ordinary ones. Any fool can be haunted by screaming. It takes craft to be haunted by roses." They flexed their fingers, and Wren saw—though Tamsin surely thought they hid it well—that the thief's hands were shaking too. The city was pressing on them as well, just deaf to most of it. "How much farther?"

Wren didn't answer with words. They answered with their feet, because their feet knew, the way they had known the stairs and the gates and the turning of every street. Up the spiral road. Through an archway carved with dragons and thieves side by side—the same carving from the drowned tunnels, older here, worn smooth by ten centuries of touching hands. Along a gallery where the gold light pooled deepest.

To the doors of the tower.

These opened too. Everything here opened for Wren; that was the whole unbearable truth of the place. Inside, a stair wound upward through the tower's heart, and the voices fell away—not silenced, but hushed, the way a crowd goes quiet. The gold dimmed to embers. And at the top of the stair, through one final archway, they stepped into a round chamber open to the sky, where a throne sat on a low dais of black stone.

It was not a grand throne. That was the surprise. It was small, almost plain, carved from a single piece of dark wood gone silver with age, worn smooth at the armrests by generations of hands. It looked like something a grandmother might sit in. Around it, set into the floor in a wide ring, burned—

No. Not burned. *Waited.*

Embers. Thousands upon thousands of embers, banked low, glowing faint orange in a perfect circle around the dais, and though they gave no heat, Wren felt them the way you feel a sleeping animal in a dark room. The dormant fire. The last dragon-fire in the world, all of it gathered here when the city fell, tended by no one, waiting for no one, for a thousand years.

Waiting for them.

"Oh," Tamsin breathed. All the irony had gone out of them entirely. They stood at the edge of the ember-ring and did not cross it, and Wren watched the thief's face do several things quickly and badly: hunger, terror, wonder, guilt. "That's it. That's the whole treasury of it. Do you feel how much there is? I can barely look at it. It's like looking at the sun through a keyhole."

Wren stepped forward.

Their foot crossed the ring of embers, and the world changed.

It did not roar awake—that was the strange mercy of it. It *recognized* them. Warmth moved up through the soles of their feet, through their legs, their chest, settling behind their sternum like a second heart, and every ember in the circle brightened by a fraction, and the hushed voices of the city exhaled all at once, a sound like wind through a forest, like ten thousand people saying *yes* without words.

And Wren understood—suddenly, completely, the way you understand a lullaby you've known forever—what this was. Not a weapon. Not a treasure. A *choir*. Every dragon that had ever lived had sung a note into this fire, and every keeper of Vael Auren had added theirs, and it was all still here, all of it, holding its breath.

It offered itself. That was the horror under the beauty. It offered itself *completely*—all of it, into whoever sat in the small silvered chair. Enough fire to raise the drowned city. Enough to wake every sleeper in the stone. Enough to burn the Spire to its foundations and remake the map of the world in a single afternoon. It asked nothing. It only waited, patiently, the way it had waited a thousand years, for someone to claim it.

Wren's hand found the armrest. The wood was warm as a living thing.

Behind them, Tamsin made a small sound—not quite a word—and Wren turned, and saw the thief standing rigid at the edge of the light, staring past Wren at the throne with an expression Wren had seen exactly once before, on the night at Ash Bridge, in the moment after they sold the false memory: the look of someone watching their own worst self arrive early.

"Wren," Tamsin said hoarsely. "Don't sit down yet. There's something I have to tell you first. And I—I need to say it out loud, before it eats me alive."

The embers brightened. Somewhere far below, through a thousand years of stone, the drowned road they had swum began, very softly, to sing.

Wren waited.
