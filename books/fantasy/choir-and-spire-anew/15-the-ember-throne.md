## 15. The Ember Throne

Wren had never seen a door that breathed before.

The gate into the deep hall was not wood or stone but a membrane of light, thin as the skin on cooling milk, and when Tamsin pressed their palm against it, the whole surface shivered and sighed. Warm air pushed through in slow pulses. In. Out. Like the flank of something enormous sleeping just beyond.

"It's waiting," Wren said. The words came easier now, common tongue sitting in their mouth like a shoe worn in again after a long winter. "It's been waiting a very long time."

Tamsin looked down at them—water still dripping from their hair, boots squelching with every step, face gray with exhaustion and the particular fear of someone who has swum through a drowned city to get here—and tried to smile. It came out crooked. "Waiting for what, exactly? Asking for a friend. The friend is me, and I'm terrified."

"For you," Wren said honestly. "For me. For whoever comes." They touched the light-door, and it rippled around their small fingers like it knew them. "It's been waiting for *me*."

* * *

The hall beyond opened so wide that the lantern-light died trying to find its walls. Wren stood at the top of a stair that descended in sweeping crescents, hundreds of steps, each one carved with figures so old their edges had gone soft as melted wax. And below—

Below was fire.

Not the hungry kind. Not the kind that ate houses and screamed people out of windows, which was the only kind Wren had ever known in the fragments of memory that weren't really theirs, except they were, they were, they were beginning to understand that they were. This fire lay coiled across the floor of the great chamber like a river deciding to rest, gold and deep amber and one strange color Wren had no word for in any language, a color like the feeling of being held. It moved slowly. It pulsed. In. Out.

Breathing. The whole city had been breathing this whole time, Wren realized. Through every flooded tunnel and broken spire, through the stone itself. Vael Auren wasn't dead. It was asleep.

And in the center of the fire-river, rising on a mound of fused glass where the flames had pooled for centuries, stood a throne.

"Right," said Tamsin faintly. "That's a throne. Made of fire. On an island. In the middle of a lake of also-fire." They rubbed their face with both hands. "I want you to know that in my professional opinion, we should leave immediately."

"You're a memory-thief," Wren said. "You don't have a professional opinion about thrones."

"I have a professional opinion about anything that can kill me, and that list is extremely long and starts with *everything*."

But they came down the stairs anyway, because Tamsin always came, that was the whole shape of them—that grumbling, complaining, deeply unwilling coming-along that Wren had learned meant *I would not let anything happen to you*. Wren counted the steps because counting helped when the memories pressed too close. Three hundred and twelve steps, and on each one the carvings showed dragons and people together: dragons carrying sheaves of grain, dragons curled around children, a dragon letting a tiny carved thief climb onto its nose while other tiny carved people shook their fists. Wren stopped at that one. Ran a thumb along the little thief's carved grin.

"That's you," they said.

"I have never in my life climbed on a dragon's nose," Tamsin said. "I've barely climbed onto a horse without complaining."

"But you would."

Tamsin paused. "...Yeah, all right. If the horse complained enough, I'd absolutely do it out of spite."

* * *

The heat grew as they descended, but it never burned. It soaked into Wren like sunlight through a window, like soup on a cold day, like the herbalist Maren's kitchen where they'd spoken their first common words. Their body remembered this place even though their mind had never been here—or their mind *had* been here, in the way that mattered, in the way that made their teeth ache with homesickness for a home that had been drowned before any kingdom Wren could name had existed.

At the bottom step, the fire parted.

It drew back from their bare feet like water from a stone, opening a path across the lake of flame, and Wren heard Tamsin make a small sound behind them—not quite a word, mostly air. Wren looked back. Tamsin stood at the edge with their toes right up against the boundary, arms wrapped around themselves, and their face did something complicated: fear, yes, but underneath it, shining through like a coin at the bottom of a well, want.

Wren knew that want. They'd seen it on smugglers and toll-wardens and once, terribly, flickering on Tamsin's own face by candlelight before it was confessed aloud. Wanting was the easiest feeling in the world to steal, Tamsin had told them once. That's why thieves exist. Everyone wants. Almost no one gets.

"Stay there if you're scared," Wren said.

"I'm not scared," Tamsin lied, magnificently.

"You are."

"I'm *professionally cautious*—"

"Stay there," Wren said again, gently, "if you're scared. But I have to go. The fire is going out. I have to go home." Their own words, given back to themselves. The first sentence they'd ever managed in the common tongue, and it was still true, truer now than ever, because here at the bottom of everything they could feel it: the great slow breath of the city, in and out, and each breath slightly shallower than the last. A candle in a room where all the doors were closed. Burning down.

They walked the path alone.

* * *

The throne was warm the way a mother's hands are warm.

Wren climbed the glass mound—three steps, four, five, their feet leaving prints that glowed briefly and faded—and sat down on the Ember Throne, and the world *opened*.

Later, they would not be able to explain it. There were no words for it in the dead tongue either, which surprised them, because the dead tongue had words for eleven kinds of silence and nine kinds of grief. The closest anyone had ever come was a phrase carved above the sanctuary gates: *the remembering of all things at once.* It felt like standing in the middle of a choir where every voice was a life. It felt like being very small and very large in the same heartbeat.

And it spoke to them. Or rather—it didn't speak, exactly. It *remembered at* them, and Wren understood, because understanding was the only language the fire needed.

*Here is what you are,* the fire said, in the voice of ten thousand mornings.

Wren saw. That was the only way to put it. They saw the Dragonheirs who had come before—a line of faces flowing backward like a river running upstream, some old, some young, one no older than Wren was now, a girl with soot on her cheeks who had held the throne through the worst winter of the old war and kept three cities from burning. They saw the bargain the throne offered, the same bargain it had offered every Heir since the beginning:

*Take us in.*

All of it. Every ember of the last dragon-fire in the world, poured into one small body, and that body would never be small again. They saw what it looked like—the vision bloomed generous and terrible: Wren grown tall in a single night, Wren whose skin shimmered like coal-bed light, Wren who could speak and mountains would listen. No more hunger. No more cold nights in smugglers' huts. No more running from grey cloaks, because grey cloaks would kneel, everyone would kneel, the Spire itself would kneel or become a smear of glass on the horizon. War would end—not because it was forgiven, but because it would be *impossible*, the way it's impossible for rain to fall upward. One child holding all the fire, and therefore holding all the peace, forever.

Forever, the fire whispered, gentle as a lullaby. *No one would ever hurt you again. No one would ever take anything from you again.*

And oh, Wren wanted it. That was the awful, honest truth of it, the thing they'd carry ashamed for the rest of their life however long that turned out to be: they were eight years old, maybe nine—they'd lost count during the running—and they had watched their city burn through borrowed eyes and slept in mud and eaten things they didn't want to think about, and here, finally, finally, was a voice saying *you will never be powerless again*, and every cell in their small tired body shouted *yes*.

Their fingers curled on the arms of the throne. The fire rose eagerly around them, licking at the air, brightening, hopeful as a dog hearing its name.

Then, beneath the wanting, Wren noticed something else. Because the throne remembered everything, and everything included the fine print.

*Take us in* had another edge. The Heir who took the fire became the fire's keeper—but keepers of fire cannot set it down. Ever. The girl with soot on her cheeks had ruled for sixty years and died on this throne, still holding it, because the fire could not be released once fully claimed; it could only pass, whole and burning, to the next Heir. Forever wasn't freedom. Forever was a throne with no getting-off. Wren thought of Tamsin describing their trade once, offhand, in the dark: *Every stolen thing gets heavier the longer you hold it.* Well. Here was the heaviest theft of all, gift-wrapped as glory.

And there was more, and this part the fire showed them shyly, the way you show someone your worst room:

There was another choice. The fire could simply be allowed to finish. To gutter. It had been dying for three hundred years anyway, breathing shallower and shallower, waiting for an Heir who might choose mercy instead of inheritance. Let it go out, and the kingdoms would lose nothing they still used—no army had flown on dragon-back in living memory, no border had been drawn by dragonfire since the old pacts crumbled. The fire's power was a story now, mostly. A reason for inquisitors to burn people. Let it gutter, and the Spire's great hunt would end, because you cannot wage holy war over an extinguished candle. The cycle would break. The last of the old world would sleep forever under clean water, and children like Wren could stop being vessels and weapons and prizes, and just be children.

The cost: everything the fire remembered would go dark. The songs, the names, the eleven kinds of silence. The war's dead—who pressed against Wren even now, thousands of voices murmuring at the edges of the choir, *remember us, remember us*—would be forgotten truly and finally. Peace bought with amnesia. An ending with no one left to tell it.

Two doors. Both locked from the inside. Both heavy as the world.

Wren sat on the Ember Throne, eight years old and holding infinity by the tail, and did the most childish thing possible, which was also possibly the wisest thing anyone had done in that hall in three hundred years.

They called for help.

* * *

"TAMSIN!"

Their voice echoed up the fire-lake and cracked in the middle, which embarrassed them even mid-crisis. The path across the flames flared bright, an invitation, and after a moment—after a long moment in which Wren could picture every terrible scenario Tamsin was surely inventing—there came the sound of boots on glass, running, and then Tamsin was there at the foot of the throne, panting, singed at the cuffs, looking up at Wren with an expression Wren had never seen on them before.

Awe, Wren realized. Plain, unguarded awe. Tamsin Brightwater, who stole feelings for a living and trusted none of their own, was looking at them like they'd hung the moon.

"Don't look at me like that," Wren said, wobbling between laughter and tears. "I need you to be sensible. You're the sensible one."

"I am *deeply* flattered and *deeply* concerned that you think so." Tamsin gripped the arm of the throne—their hand hissed faintly where it touched, and they snatched it back and stuck the fingers in their mouth, talking around them. "Ow. Okay. Report. What does the big scary chair want?"

"It wants to give me everything." Wren's voice came out smaller than they intended. "All the fire. I'd never be cold again. Nobody could ever hunt us, nobody could—" Their throat tightened. "It showed me. I'd be able to end every war just by existing. Everyone would be safe."

Tamsin went very quiet. And Wren, watching them closely the way children watch the adults they love, saw the want flare in their face again—that coin-at-the-bottom-of-the-well shine—and saw Tamsin see it too, and hate it, and say, hoarsely: "And?"

"And it doesn't come off." Wren spread their small hands. "Ever. Whoever takes it in holds it until they die, and then it jumps to the next one. It's not a gift, Tamsin. It's a chain that looks like a crown." They swallowed. "Or I can let it go out. All of it. And then nobody ever has to be the Heir again, and the Spire has nothing to hunt, and it's over. Really over."

"And the cost?" Tamsin asked, because Tamsin always asked about the cost, it was the first lesson of their trade. *Nothing is free, sprout. Especially the things people call free.*

Wren's eyes stung. "Everyone the fire remembers. All the dead. They'd be forgotten. Truly forgotten." Their voice dropped to a whisper. "Tamsin, they've been waiting so long to be remembered. Some of them have been waiting since before your kingdoms had names. If I let the light go out, they'll be like they never were at all."

Silence, except for the great slow breathing of the fire.

Tamsin sank down to sit on the lowest glass step, elbows on knees, staring into the amber depths for a long time. When they finally spoke, their voice had none of its usual armor on it.

"My sister's name was Lira," they said.

Wren went still. Tamsin never talked about her. Tamsin had erased her from their own mind to survive a purge, years ago, and told Wren about it in a cave once—told them the shape of the hole, if not the person. But somewhere along the road, in the nights by the fire and the mornings sharing hardtack, pieces had come back. Fragments. Enough to know her name.

"The Spire burned our village when I was fourteen. I survived because I forgot her—I paid a man to cut her out of me, and it worked, and I lived, and for twenty years I didn't even know what I'd lost." Tamsin turned their head and looked at Wren, and their eyes were wet and steady. "Do you know what I'd give to remember her properly? Just her laugh, one time? I found fragments later, on the road, in my own dreams. Little scraps. And those scraps are worth more to me than every pass and safehouse I've ever stolen combined." They gestured at the fire, the vast breathing dark. "That chair is offering you the opposite trade. Everything remembered, and you chained to the keeping of it. Or everyone freed, and the remembering lost." A ragged breath. "I don't know the answer, sprout. Anyone who tells you they know the answer is selling something. But I know this much: I spent twenty years paying interest on a debt I couldn't even see. Don't sign anything tonight. Chains look different in the morning."

Wren looked down at their hands, small on the arms of a throne older than language.

"There's a third thing," they said quietly. "Isn't there? There always is. You taught me that. Toll-wardens, contracts, bargains—there's always a third thing nobody says out loud."

Tamsin blinked. "...Go on."

"The fire needs an anchor if it goes out safely. Something freely given to hold the memory, so it doesn't just vanish." Wren had felt this in the choir of voices, a condition written deep in the old pacts, half-buried. "Someone has to give something. Freely. Not stolen. Not traded." They looked up. "Freely given, Tamsin. Do you know how rare that is? In your world? In *anyone's* world?"

Tamsin had gone pale. Not throne-fear pale. A different pale. The pale of someone seeing a bill arrive that they'd known, deep down, was coming.

"Wren," they said carefully. "What exactly would it need?"

And before Wren could answer—before the fire could finish explaining, before either of them could weigh the price that was already, in some corner of the future, waiting—far above, down through miles of stone and drowned halls, came a sound that did not belong to the sleeping city.

Boots. Many boots. And a voice amplified by Spire-craft, cold and clear, echoing down the ancient stair:

"Heir of Vael Auren. We know you're awake."

Tamsin stood up so fast they nearly slipped on the glass. "Oh, you have got to be joking," they breathed, and grabbed Wren's hand. "New plan. Same plan, actually. Run first, understand later—"

But Wren stayed rooted a moment longer, looking back at the throne—at the fire rising around it like a question, like an open door, like a patient, dying heart—and whispered, in the dead tongue, the oldest promise in the world:

*I haven't chosen yet. Wait for me.*
