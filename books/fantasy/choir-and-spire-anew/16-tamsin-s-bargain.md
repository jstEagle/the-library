## 16. Tamsin's Bargain

The Ember Throne did not look like a throne.

That was Tamsin's first thought, and they clung to it the way a drowning sailor clings to driftwood—because if the thing had looked like a throne, all teeth and grandeur and appetite, then wanting it would at least have felt like ambition. But it was only a low seat of black stone set in a ring of ash, and in its center burned an ember no bigger than a robin's egg, and it was the most beautiful thing Tamsin Brightwater had ever seen.

They hated that.

* * *

Wren slept curled against the throne's base like a cat against a hearthstone, which, Tamsin supposed, was exactly what it was. The child's breathing came slow and even, and around them the drowned sanctuary breathed too—a vast slow pulse of light through the water-walls, gold going to rose going to gold again, the rhythm of something enormous and patient that had been holding its breath for three hundred years.

Tamsin sat on a broken colonnade twenty paces away and tried very hard not to think about what they were thinking about.

It wasn't working.

Here was the trouble, laid out plain as a fence-post: Wren had told them, in that careful new voice of theirs, what the fire offered. *I can take it in,* the child had said, small hands spread over the ember's warmth. *All of it. It said so. It said I could carry it, and then nothing would ever burn down again unless I wanted it to.* And the fire had meant it kindly—the way a mountain means an avalanche kindly, perhaps, but kindly. It wanted a keeper. It had been waiting for one since the last one died screaming.

And Tamsin had stood there listening, and some small greased corner of their mind had whispered: *You're a memory-thief. You've lifted recollections from dying men and sleeping guards and once, memorably, from a bishop mid-sermon. The fire lives in memory. It speaks in memory. What is a birthright, really, except a memory the blood keeps?*

Steal it. That was the whisper. Not from the child—from the ember itself, or from the part of Tamsin that had never met a locked door it didn't want open. Take the inheritance out of Wren's head the way you'd lift a purse, tuck it behind your own eyes, and walk out of these caverns carrying the last dragon-fire in the world like a coal in a lantern. No more hungry winters. No more selling other people's yesterdays for bread. No more being small.

No more being *you*, said the whisper, and it didn't mean it as an insult.

"Stop it," Tamsin muttered aloud, to nobody.

The sanctuary pulsed. Gold, rose, gold. It sounded almost amused.

* * *

The trouble with a lifetime of theft, Tamsin reflected, pacing the colonnade, was that you got good at recognizing the shape of a heist—even when the mark was yourself. And this had the shape of one. There was the prize, glowing. There was the vulnerable party, sleeping. There was the rationalization already drafted and waiting: *it's for the best, the child shouldn't carry that weight, I'd be protecting them.* Every thief in Thornfell had sung that song. Tamsin had heard it from their own mouth often enough to know every verse.

Except—and here was the wrinkle that made them stop pacing and press the heels of their hands against their eyes—it wasn't entirely a lie. That was the filthy genius of it. Wren *was* six years old. Wren *shouldn't* carry a war's worth of dying fire in their chest. Any reasonable person could look at the situation and say the burden belonged on broader shoulders.

The fire thought so too. It had been murmuring to Tamsin for hours now, soft as sleet against a window. Not in words. In memories. That was how it talked—that was all it knew how to do. Little gifts, dropped along the edges of Tamsin's mind like coins along a road: the smell of Vael Auren before it fell, bread and brine and hot scale; the sound of ten thousand voices singing in a spire that could hold a note for a year; the feeling of being *trusted*, which was the one Tamsin kept flinching away from, because they'd never actually felt it and hadn't realized until now that there was a hole where it should go.

*I could give you that,* the fire said, without saying anything. *I remember what trust feels like. I can put it back.*

"Oh, you're worse than Brannoc," Tamsin told it. "At least he haggles honestly."

Behind them, a small voice said, "You talk to rocks."

Tamsin jumped hard enough to skin a knuckle on the colonnade. "Saints' teeth, Wren—"

"You were asleep," Wren said, unrepentant, padding closer on bare feet. They'd found dry clothes somewhere in the sanctuary's deep rooms, a child-sized robe of grey wool gone stiff with centuries, and they wore it with the dignity of a small emperor. Their dark eyes went from Tamsin's face to the distant ember and back, and something in them narrowed—not suspicious, exactly. Six-year-olds weren't suspicious. But they were *attentive*, in the way of animals that had learned to read weather. "You've been talking to it a long time. Since I slept."

"I've been talking to myself. It's a hobby. Very cheap."

"It answered you." Wren sat down on the colonnade beside Tamsin with the boneless finality of a child claiming territory, legs swinging above a floor built by people who had ridden dragons. "It talks different when it wants something. Like the toll-warden. He smiled too much."

Tamsin opened their mouth to deny it, and closed it again, because lying to this particular child had stopped working somewhere around the flooded road, and anyway there was a limit to how much hypocrisy a person could carry up a mountain. "Yes," they said instead. "It does."

"What does it want?"

And there it was. The door, standing open. All Tamsin had to do was walk through it smiling.

They thought about the smile. They thought about how easy it would be—how *natural*. Wren trusted them. That was the whole terrible architecture of it: a child who had crossed half a kingdom holding their hand, who had given them their first words, who believed—because children believe—that grown-ups mostly meant what they said. Tamsin could say *the fire thinks I should carry it, so neither of us has to be afraid*, and Wren would nod, and the ember would pour itself into a willing thief, and in a hundred years the kingdoms would learn a new word for whatever Tamsin Brightwater decided to be.

The fire pulsed. Gold, rose, gold. Patient as debt.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, and their voice cracked in the middle of it, which was undignified, and they found they didn't care.

Wren looked up. "No what?"

Tamsin rubbed their face with both hands. Somewhere below them, water moved through halls where kings had argued about mercy, and the sound was like slow applause, or like patience running out.

"When I was fourteen," Tamsin said, "I stole a pie."

Wren blinked. This was clearly not the sentence they'd expected. It was not the sentence Tamsin had expected either, but the confession was coming whether they liked it or not, and apparently it intended to arrive by way of baked goods.

"A meat pie, off a cart in Lowmarket. First thing I ever took that wasn't mine. And I remember—" Tamsin laughed, short and unhappy. "I remember standing in an alley eating it, and thinking, *this is the best pie I've ever had*, and knowing—I *knew*, even then—that it wasn't the pie. It was the taking. It tasted better because it was stolen. And I've been chasing that taste for twenty years, Wren. Passes, secrets, faces, names. Whole lifetimes, sometimes. And every single one tastes like that pie. Better than it should. Better than it has any right to. Because it isn't mine."

The sanctuary hummed. The ember across the hall brightened, just slightly, the way a listener leans forward.

"And right now," Tamsin said, and made themselves look at the child, because this part deserved eye contact even if it deserved nothing else, "that rock over there is offering me the biggest pie in the world. It's showing me what trust feels like. It's showing me what power feels like. It's telling me I'd be doing you a favor, taking your burden, and it's *right enough* about that that I nearly believed it, and the only reason I'm telling you this instead of doing it while you sleep is—" They stopped. Started again. "Because I promised myself, coming down that drowned road, that I wouldn't lie to you. Even about this. *Especially* about this."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Their feet had stopped swinging.

"You wanted to steal me," they said. Not a question. Not even, quite, an accusation. More like weather-reading. *Storm coming from the west.*

"I wanted to steal what's inside you. Which is worse, because at least a purse doesn't look at you afterward." Tamsin's throat was tight. "I didn't do it. I'm not going to do it. But I want you to know that I *wanted* to, because—because if I don't tell you, then it's still in me, sitting quiet, waiting for a bad night. And things that sit quiet waiting for bad nights are the only kind of thief I've never beaten."

* * *

He had expected crying. Or anger. Or that flat, wounded silence children produced when adults finally showed them the world's underside. Tamsin had rehearsed responses to all three on the walk back from the colonnade, and had prepared none for what actually happened, which was that Wren considered the matter with the gravity of a magistrate and then asked:

"Does it work? If you steal a memory. Does it stay?"

"...What?"

"The fire. If you took it out of my head. Would it stay yours?" Wren's brow furrowed. "Or would it eat through, like the rats ate through the hermit's flour sacks? Things that want to be somewhere come through."

Tamsin stared at him. "How old are you?"

"Six." A pause. "Almost."

"Almost six," Tamsin repeated faintly. "Right. Well. To answer the question: no. Stolen things don't stay. That's the whole dirty secret of my trade, Wren. People think a memory is like a coin—you lift it, you keep it. But it's more like a cutting from a living plant. It needs its soil. Put it in the wrong ground and it either dies or—" they gestured vaguely at their own skull, "—grows wrong. I've got a smuggler's knowledge of forty passes in my head and I couldn't find half of them anymore, because his feet aren't attached to my feet. Memories aren't things. They're *places you've been*. And I haven't been where you've been."

"But the fire thinks you could."

"The fire," Tamsin said, "is a liar with excellent manners."

Wren shook his head slowly. "It's not a liar. It's lonely." He said it simply, the way he said everything now that the words had come unstuck, and it landed harder than any argument could have. "Three hundred years under the water with nobody to talk to. It forgot how big it is. When it offers things, it's like—" he searched, "—like when I used to offer the hermit my stones. The pretty ones. Because I wanted him to stay. It wasn't a trade. I just wanted him to *stay*."

Tamsin felt something in their chest turn over, slow and painful, like a boat coming about.

"So when it tells you to take me," Wren finished, "maybe it's not tricking you. Maybe it just doesn't know another way to ask somebody not to leave."

For a long moment Tamsin couldn't speak at all. Across the hall, the ember flickered—not gold, not rose, but a small uncertain orange, the color of a candle in a draft, and Tamsin would have sworn on every saint they'd ever blasphemed that it looked embarrassed.

"Well," they managed finally. "That's—huh. That's considerably more charitable than anything I was going to say about it."

"You were going to call it names."

"I was going to call it *several* names."

"That's what Brannoc does when he's scared."

"I am not—" Tamsin began, and then, because honesty appeared to be contagious, sighed. "Fine. Yes. I'm scared. It's a very large rock full of very old fire and it likes me, and I have never once in my life been liked by anything that couldn't be pickpocketed. It's unsettling."

Wren giggled. Actually giggled, a bright sudden sound that went echoing off stone carved before the kingdoms existed, and Tamsin found themselves laughing too—helpless, slightly hysterical laughter, the kind that comes after fear has nowhere left to sit. They laughed until their ribs hurt, the two of them on a broken colonnade at the bottom of the world, and the ember glowed its modest orange, and for a few minutes nothing in the drowned dark wanted anything from anybody.

Then Wren leaned against Tamsin's shoulder, heavy the way tired children are heavy, and said, "You gave it back. The wanting."

"Hm?"

"The pie-feeling. You said it sits in you, waiting. But you told me, and now it's not sitting anymore. It's outside." He looked up, matter-of-fact. "That's how the hermit fixed nightmares. You tell them to somebody and they get smaller. He called it airing out."

Tamsin swallowed. "Your hermit sounds wiser than me."

"He smelled like goats."

"Most wise people do."

But the truth of it settled into Tamsin's bones and stayed there, warm as a hearthstone: the impulse *had* gotten smaller, said aloud. Not gone—Tamsin was too much a realist to believe in gone—but smaller, and named, and therefore armed against. Corruption resisted not by purity—they had never once been pure, and suspected purity was mostly a story the powerful told about themselves—but by honesty. By dragging the ugly thing into lamplight where it had to stand there squinting, ordinary, mortal-sized, instead of growing fat in the dark.

Twenty years a thief, and the first treasure they'd ever given away freely was their own worst thought. It figured. It absolutely figured.

* * *

They stayed on the colonnade until Wren's weight went slack against Tamsin's side, breath evening out into sleep again, and Tamsin sat unmoving so as not to wake him, watching the ember breathe its patient light.

*I'm not going to take him from you,* Tamsin told it silently. *And I'm not going to let him drown in you either. So we'd both better start thinking of a third option, because I promise you the Spire is coming, and Veyra doesn't negotiate, and I am extremely tired and running low on clever.*

The fire pulsed. Once. Twice. And then—slowly, reluctantly, like a dog deciding to trust—it dimmed itself down to a coal-glow, a banked and waiting warmth, and Tamsin chose to believe that meant *all right*.

From far above, muffled through fathoms of stone and water, came a sound Tamsin knew from a hundred bad nights on a hundred bad roads: the groan of rock shifting under deliberate pressure. Someone was opening the Weeping Gate.

Tamsin gathered Wren up—light, so light, far too light for everything he carried—and stood, heart hammering, eyes on the dark tunnel mouth where torchlight was beginning, terribly, to bloom.

"Company," they whispered to the sleeping child, to the fire, to anyone listening. "And I'd bet my whole stolen life I know whose."
