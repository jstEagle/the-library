## 18. The Last Gutter of Flame

The trouble with a city made of memory, Tamsin decided, was that it remembered everything—including how to fall down.

They had been in Vael Auren for less than an hour, and already the drowned sanctuary had woken around Wren like a dog hearing its name after twenty years. Streets that had been silt and shadow were streets again: lamplight blooming along the canal walls in slow amber pulses, bridges un-arch themselves from the water, windows kindling one by one up the faces of towers so tall their tops vanished into the cavern dark overhead. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. It was also, Tamsin was beginning to suspect, held together by nothing sturdier than a frightened child's attention.

"Wren," they said carefully, standing on what had been, moments ago, the flooded floor of a great hall and was now dry flagstone under their boots. "Sweetheart. Starlight. Whatever I did to deserve you finding my door in that storm—and I still don't know, by the way, I've checked every stolen memory I own—could you maybe try not to think quite so *loudly*?"

Wren didn't answer. The child stood at the center of the hall before a dais of black stone, where a throne waited like a banked hearth, its seat veined with lines of dull red light. Wren's small hands were pressed flat against their own chest, as though holding something in—or holding something together.

The red veins pulsed.

Tamsin felt it in their teeth first, then in the stolen memories behind their eyes—a pressure, a warmth, a vast slow turning-over, like a sleeper shifting toward waking. Every thief's instinct they had ever honed screamed at them at once. That was power in there. That was *wealth*. A single thread of true dragon-fire, carried out of this place in the vault of a mind, would buy a kingdom's ransom. Would buy forgiveness from Brannoc's buyers. Would buy a life with no more cold passes, no more dying smugglers' last hours pried open like oysters.

Tamsin looked at the throne and thought about money for exactly four seconds, and hated themselves for three and a half of them.

Then the ceiling began to sing.

It wasn't a pleasant sound. Stone groaning against stone, deep and structural, the sound a house makes when it has decided it is done being a house. Somewhere above them, in the dark of the cavern roof, something the size of a village shifted its weight.

"Right," said Tamsin. "That's not good."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about ancient magic: it is not dignified. It does not rise in stately columns and hum with purpose. It *panics*, because the thing holding it has panicked, and dragon-fire turns out to be no different from any other fire—it goes where the wind pushes it, and the wind, in this case, was a nine-year-old's terror.

Wren had been getting flashes of it since the surface. Tamsin had watched it happen on the long descent through the drowned road: the child stopping mid-step, eyes going wide and unfocused, lips moving in the dead tongue around words like *ash* and *choir* and a name that made the water tremble. The city's dead were pressing in on Wren, all those thousands of voices that had burned with the sanctuary and never been mourned properly, and each one wanted to be remembered, and Wren—who could barely remember to eat if Tamsin didn't put the bowl directly under their chin—was trying to hold all of them at once.

Now, standing before the Ember Throne, Wren had dropped the bowl entirely.

The flare came without warning. One moment the throne's veins were dull embers; the next, light poured out of the black stone like blood from a wound, and heat rolled across the hall in a wave that smelled of hot metal and, absurdly, of summer—the high sweet smell of a hayfield at noon, some ancestral memory of warmth baked into the fire itself. Tamsin threw an arm across their face and staggered back.

"Wren!" They fought through the glare. "Look at me! Not at it—at *me*!"

The child turned, and Tamsin's heart did something complicated and painful. Wren's eyes were full of reflected fire, and behind the fire was the expression Tamsin knew from a hundred market squares, a hundred collapsing bargains: the look of someone watching something precious come apart in their hands.

"I can't—" Wren got the words out in common tongue, which meant it was bad, because Wren only reached for Tamsin's language when the old one failed them. "They're all talking. All at once. And the fire wants to go *up*, it wants to go *home*, but home is—" The child's voice cracked. "There isn't a home. There's just me."

"Okay." Tamsin crouched, keeping their voice level the way you kept your hand level around a frightened animal or a loaded crossbow. "Okay. First: there is absolutely a home, and it has a leaky roof and a door that sticks and I have been meaning to fix that door for two years, so you're going to help me fix it, that's a promise, and Brightwater promises are worthless, which means I'll actually keep this one. Second—"

The ceiling sang again, louder. A crack ran across the hall's great dome like lightning frozen mid-strike, and through it sifted not dust but *water*—a thin bright rope of it, falling forty feet to splash against flagstone that hissed and steamed.

"—second," Tamsin continued, "we are leaving."

"The city will die." Wren's hands were shaking. Firelight crawled up the child's sleeves without burning the cloth, which Tamsin chose not to think about. "If I stop holding it, it goes out forever. If I hold it wrong, it—" They gestured upward, at the groaning dark, at the rope of lake-water threading down through the crack.

"Yes," Tamsin said. "I did notice the architecture voting."

Above them, the dome gave a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and a second crack opened, and then a third, and the lamplight along the walls began to gutter—not going out, Tamsin realized, but *flickering in time with Wren's breathing*, quick and shallow and scared.

Everything here ran on the child. The light, the dry floors, the whole resurrected dream of Vael Auren—it was all Wren's attention given shape, and Wren's attention was coming apart like wet paper.

Which meant, Tamsin thought, with the strange clarity that arrives precisely when you'd prefer hysteria, that the way out was not to run. Running would shake the last of the child's focus loose and bring the whole mountain down on both of them. The way out was to be worth looking at.

* * *

Tamsin Brightwater had spent fifteen years learning how to be memorable in exactly the ways that paid and forgettable in exactly the ways that kept them alive. Now they dug into the other skill, the older one, the one from before the trade—from a kitchen in a border town, from a woman who used to sing while she kneaded bread, from a sister whose face Tamsin had sold away to survive and had spent fifteen years not missing.

They dug down and found the voice that had once talked a toll-warden out of his post, a magistrate out of his chains, a grieving man out of jumping off Ash Bridge. Not the smooth voice. The real one underneath it.

"Hey," they said. "Wren. Look at me. Just me."

The child's blazing eyes found theirs.

"You remember the hermit's cottage? Maren's place, the one that smelled like boiled socks?" A flicker—almost a smile. Good. "You hated the soup. You ate three bowls and told me it was terrible in that dead-tongue word of yours, *shaveth*, and I said 'more?' and you said 'yes,' and that, starlight, was the moment I knew we were going to be fine. Because anyone who complains about soup and asks for seconds understands the world correctly."

The fire in Wren's eyes steadied. Slightly. The dome still groaned; the water still fell; but the flicker of the wall-lamps slowed to match a calmer breath.

"There you are," Tamsin said softly. "Now listen. This city isn't asking you to carry it. I know it feels that way—all those voices, all that weight—but I've carried memories my whole life, and I'm telling you the truth about them: they're not stones. They're birds. You don't hold birds. You open your hands and they sit there because they *choose* to, and when they need to fly, you let them."

"That's not how memories work," Wren whispered. "You steal them. You told me. You put them in glass."

"I lie professionally," Tamsin said. "It's how I afford the soup."

And then, because the dome chose that moment to shed an entire section of itself—a slab the size of a barn door crashing down thirty feet to their left, close enough that the impact knocked them both off their feet and filled the hall with choking glitter of powdered stone—Tamsin stopped being clever.

They grabbed Wren. Wren grabbed them back, arms locked around Tamsin's ribs with a strength no nine-year-old should have, dragon-strength, heir-strength, and the fire flared again—wild now, whipping off the throne in ribbons, licking up the walls, and everywhere it touched, the dying city *lurched*: a fountain coughed back to life, a bridge heaved itself upright, a bell somewhere rang once, a single deep bronze note that went through Tamsin's bones like grief.

"We have to go," Tamsin gasped into the child's hair. "The drowned road, the way we came—"

"It's flooding. I can feel it. The lake is coming in." Wren's voice was very small. "Tamsin. If I leave, it all goes dark. Everyone. Again."

Tamsin looked back at the throne.

This was the moment. They knew it the way they knew the weight of a purse by its sound. Behind them, the Ember Throne sat in its corona of flame, and threaded through that flame was knowledge—centuries of it, the true names of passes and kings, the craft of fire that had kept the peace of nations, enough stored wealth of mind to make every theft of Tamsin's career look like picking pockets at a funeral. All they had to do was touch it. One touch, one breath, and they could walk out of this collapsing grave carrying the most valuable cargo in the world inside their skull, and no inquisitor, no buyer, no god would ever be able to take it from them.

Their hand was already moving. That was the shameful part. Their body had made the choice before their conscience caught up, fingers reaching for the light—

—and Wren made a sound. Small. Not words. The sound a child makes when the one person they trust starts to turn away.

Tamsin closed their fist and pulled it back, and it was the hardest thing they had ever done, harder than the purge, harder than the sister, harder than every cold night on every high pass, and they heard themselves say, out loud, in a voice that shook:

"No. No, thank you. I've got what I came for."

Wren stared up at them through the smoke and the falling light. "*What?*"

"You." Tamsin lifted the child onto their hip—they were getting too big for it, they'd both pretend otherwise—and turned toward the far archway, where the drowned road sloped down into darkness and rising water. "Fifteen years I've taken things because taking was the only way anyone ever stayed. And you walked into my house in a storm and gave me everything for free, and I am *not*, do you hear me, I am *not* going to be the person who pockets the change."

The dome split. Water came through in earnest now, a white roaring sheet of it, and the floor tilted, and the throne's fire whipped sideways like a banner in a gale, and none of it mattered even slightly compared to the look on Wren's face—wonder, plain and undiluted, the same look from the first morning, mud and rain and a knock at the wrong hour.

"You're crying," Wren observed.

"I have ash in my eye."

"Both eyes?"

"It's *everywhere*," Tamsin said with dignity, and ran.

* * *

The drowned road was drowning in earnest. Waist-deep and climbing, the water black and cold as a debt, lit only by the fire that clung to Wren like a loyal cat—little tongues of it riding the child's shoulders, their hair, the crook of their arm, throwing wild gold across the carved walls where dragons and thieves danced side by side in stone, as they had since before kingdoms, as apparently they always would.

Tamsin waded hard, lungs burning, one arm locked around the child. Behind them the sanctuary came apart in slow thunderous sections, and with each collapse the light behind dimmed, and with each dimming Tamsin felt something tear in their chest that had nothing to do with wealth and everything to do with a city that had only just remembered its own name.

Halfway to the surface passage, the water hit chest-height and the current found its strength, and Tamsin went down.

Not far. But down—under, briefly, into cold and roar and blindness—and when they came up coughing, Wren was the one holding *them*, small hands fisted in their collar, dragging them toward the wall, planting them against the stone like a barnacle.

"Let go of the bag," the child said.

"What?"

"The bag." Wren pointed at Tamsin's satchel, half-submerged, snagged on a jutting carving—a stone dragon's outstretched claw, of all the ironies, holding fast to the thief. Inside the satchel: the vials, the tools, the glass memories of forty years of other people's lives. A fortune. An identity. Everything.

"If I let go, I lose it all," Tamsin said.

"If you don't, you lose *you*."

The current hauled at them both. The water was at their chins. Somewhere above, more of the mountain was deciding to become lake.

Tamsin Brightwater looked at the satchel. Thought about the ledger of their whole life, every entry written in other people's losses. Then they thought about a sticky door, and terrible soup eaten three times, and a small voice saying *shaveth* and meaning *more, please, stay.*

They let go.

The satchel tore free and tumbled away into the dark, and the current took them both instead—gently, suddenly, almost kindly, sweeping them up the sloping road toward the thin grey promise of daylight, Wren's borrowed fire streaming behind them like a comet's tail, and Tamsin laughed, actually laughed, breathless and terrified and lighter than they had been in fifteen years, because it turned out that giving things away weighed nothing at all.

They broke the surface outside into rain and evening, gasping on the rocks below Thornfell's shoulder, while behind them the mountain shuddered and glowed through its wounds like a lantern with a cracked shade.

Then the glow changed color. Went from gold to a deep, hungry red. And beneath the rain, faint but unmistakable, Tamsin heard footsteps on the path above—heavy, booted, many—and a voice calling down through the storm, cold as Spire steel:

"The Heir lives. Seal the lower road. *No one leaves the mountain.*"

Tamsin pulled Wren close against the rock, firelight and torchlight warring across the child's face, and understood that the choice they had just survived was only the first one.

The last gutter of flame was still burning. And someone up there meant to snuff it—along with everyone who had carried it this far.
