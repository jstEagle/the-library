## 19. The Price of Forgetting

The fire was dying, and it wanted to be remembered.

That was the truth of it, once Wren had pressed both small hands to the Ember Throne and listened. Tamsin stood at the base of the dais, soaked to the ribs from the flooded road, watching the last dragon-fire gutter in its cradle of stone like a candle in a drafty chapel. It had burned for a thousand years without a keeper. Now it had one—a mud-streaked child in an oversized coat—and the fire was asking for something no thief had ever taught Tamsin to give.

"It can't just go out," Wren said. The common words still sat strangely in their mouth, careful as a child carrying a full cup. "If I just let it die, it dies *angry*. It's been holding the kingdoms' anger all this time. All the oaths. All the broken promises." They looked up, and their eyes were too old again, the way they went sometimes, and Tamsin's chest did the thing it always did, the squeezing thing they'd stopped pretending wasn't love about four chapters of this disaster ago. "It needs somewhere to put the remembering. Or it will find somewhere on its own."

"Meaning what?" Tamsin asked, though they already knew. They were a memory-thief. They knew exactly what a mind could hold.

"Meaning it would pour itself into whoever was standing here," Wren said simply. "And that person would never be a person again. Just a wick."

Tamsin thought—briefly, shamefully, honestly—of themselves as a wick. Of the whole burning knowledge of dragons and drowned cities and the true names of old kings, all of it theirs, all of it sellable, all of it *power*. The thought arrived fully dressed and smiling, the way temptation does when you've spent your life opening doors to strangers.

They hated it. And then, because honesty was the only discipline they'd ever managed to keep, they said it out loud.

"I thought about taking it," Tamsin said. "Just now. You should know that. I stood here and I thought, *there's a fortune standing in that flame*, and my hands got itchy the way they do." They flexed those hands, thief's hands, quick and clever and suddenly very tired. "I'm not going to. But you deserve to know what's in me along with everything else."

Wren looked at them for a long moment. Then the child smiled—not the too-old smile, but the real one, gap-toothed and crooked.

"I know," Wren said. "I could hear you thinking. You think very loudly for someone who steals quietly."

"Rude."

"It's why I trust you," Wren said. "Liars are quiet inside."

* * *

The solution, when the fire finally explained itself through Wren's halting translation, was almost insultingly simple. Everything worth doing, Tamsin had learned, was either simple or impossible, and the simple things always cost more.

"The seal isn't a lock," Wren said, pacing the throne platform, small boots clicking on stone that glowed faintly where the child stepped, as if the city recognized its own. "It's an anchor. The fire has to be tied down by something freely given. A gift. Memories, given willingly—that's the only rope strong enough. Given, not taken." They stopped. They turned around slowly. "Tamsin. It has to be memories of *me*."

The words landed in the chamber and echoed off ten centuries of silence.

"No," Tamsin said.

"It has to be—"

"No." Tamsin heard their own voice crack and didn't care. "Find another way. Take mine of Brannoc. Take the toll-warden, take Veyra's face, take every miserable market morning I ever spent haggling over turnips. Take the lot. Not—" Their throat closed. "Not those."

"There are no others that will hold." Wren's voice was steady, which was somehow worse than tears. "The fire knows the difference between a bond and a transaction. It's been lied to before. That's why it's angry." The child came closer, and up close their eyes were wet and fierce. "You told me once—you told me in the cave, after the rockslide—that you gave me your sister's story freely, and it was the first thing you ever gave instead of took, and it changed something in you. This is that. Only bigger. Only *all of it*."

"You're asking me to forget you exist."

"I'm asking you to let the fire remember me so nobody has to become a god." Wren's chin trembled, and they mastered it, the brave little custodian, the frightened heir, the child who had crossed mountains on bare feet because the fire was going out and someone had to go home. "You always say it. Every time we run. You say, *nothing is free, little bird, the trick is choosing what you pay.*" A wet, hiccupping laugh escaped them. "So choose."

Tamsin turned away. Walked to the edge of the dais. Looked out over the drowned sanctuary—the sunken choir stalls furred with pale moss, the spires rising through black water like the fingers of drowned giants, the whole vast sleeping weight of a world that had ended before kingdoms had names. Somewhere above, Veyra was coming down the flooded road with soldiers and a captive Brannoc, and there was no time, there was never any time, time was the one thing even a memory-thief couldn't steal back.

They thought about the first night. The storm. The knock at the wrong hour. A mud-caked child on the doorstep speaking a dead tongue, and Tamsin's hand moving to that small brow on instinct, and the flood of fire and spires and a voice calling *Heir*—and beneath all that ancient terror, one small, stubborn, human thing: a child who was hungry, and cold, and alone, looking up at a stranger's door because it was the only door left.

They thought about stolen apples shared on a mountain pass. About teaching Wren to haggle ("never pay the first price, little bird, the first price is a lie wearing a hat"). About Wren falling asleep against their shoulder on the third night of flight, and Tamsin sitting rigidly still for six hours rather than wake them, and understanding, somewhere in the dark, that they had lost the war against their own heart without ever hearing it declared.

All of it. Gone. Every hour, every joke, every small fierce pride. They would stand in this room and watch a stranger walk away.

"Well," Tamsin said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Nothing is free."

"Tamsin—"

"Hush. Let me do this while I still know why." They knelt, so they were eye to eye with the child, and took Wren's face in their thief's hands, gentle as handling stolen glass. "Listen to me. In a minute I won't know you, and you'll know that, and it will hurt, and I'm sorry—I'll be sorry without knowing what for, which is the stupidest kind of sorry there is. But hear this before it goes." They leaned their forehead against the child's brow, the same gesture that had started everything, a thief's touch reversed into a benediction. "You were the best thing I never stole. Freely given. Freely kept. And whatever I am to you tomorrow—a stranger, a fool, a woman with itchy hands and no idea why her eyes are wet—some part of you remembers being loved. Memory is just the receipt, little bird. The keeping happened anyway. Nothing can take that back, because I gave it. Do you understand? *Given things stay given.*"

Wren was crying openly now, great silent sobs, and they threw their arms around Tamsin's neck and held on hard enough to bruise.

"I'll knock again," the child whispered into their hair. "Whatever happens. I'll find your door."

"Wrong hour or right one?"

"Wrong one. Obviously."

Tamsin laughed, and it broke in the middle, and they held on until the fire behind them flared bright and impatient, and there was nothing left to do but the doing.

They walked together to the Ember Throne. Wren climbed onto the seat, small hands finding the armrests worn smooth by dead kings, and the last dragon-fire rose to meet them like a dog greeting its master—eager, trembling, ancient beyond cruelty and kindness alike.

"Put your hands on the stone," Wren said. "Think of me. All of me. Everything, in order, if you can. The fire will take them as you give them. It will feel like—" They hesitated. "I don't actually know what it will feel like. Sorry. Very reassuring, I know."

"You're a terrible comfort, little bird."

"I'm a *custodian*," Wren said with dignity, "of a dying magical civilization. Comfort was not in the job description."

Tamsin laughed again—one last laugh, they understood, that belonged to the two of them—and laid both palms flat on the warm stone.

They began at the beginning. The storm. The knock. The mud and the dead tongue and the flood of fire. They gave it, deliberately, the way they had never given anything: not lifted, not eased loose, but *handed over*, root and branch, and the fire drank each memory like water reaching a root at last after a thousand dry years. The market mornings. The passes. The rockslide confession. The bridge, the false memory sold to the toll-warden, the shame and the self-knowledge tangled in it. The hermit's cottage and the first common words—*the fire is going out, I have to go home*—and how Tamsin's heart had cracked open along a seam they hadn't known was there.

The fire rose. The sanctuary shuddered awake around them, water receding from halls that had been drowned since before history, light climbing the spires like ivy, and the whole vast engine of the old peace turning over, groaning, and settling—not reignited, not destroyed, but *anchored*, tied down by a rope woven of one thief's entire love for one child.

It hurt more than anything. It hurt like being unmade politely, one warm hour at a time. Tamsin held on. They thought: *given things stay given.* They thought: *choose what you pay.*

They reached the last memory—the carved dragon, the smile, the promise of a knock at the wrong hour—and gave that too, and felt it leave like a breath they'd been holding for months.

And then there was a child on a throne, crying, and a stranger standing below the dais with wet eyes and no idea why, and a silence in their heart shaped exactly like someone they had never met.

"...Who are you?" the stranger asked.

* * *

**WREN**

Wren had watched a thousand years of memory pour into the fire, and none of it had prepared them for this: the moment Tamsin Brightwater looked up at them with a stranger's polite, baffled frown.

"My name's Tamsin," the stranger said, wiping their eyes and scowling at the wetness as though it had pickpocketed them. "Why am I crying? Did you do something?"

"You gave something away," Wren said. Their voice came out small. "A big something."

"Hm." Tamsin looked at their own hands—thief's hands, clever and quick, and now empty in a way Wren doubted anyone else could see. "Doesn't sound like me. I don't give things away. I *acquire* things." But they said it automatically, like a fence reciting a price list, and their eyes kept drifting back to Wren's face with an expression Wren recognized from mirrors: the look of someone trying to remember a word that's on the tip of the tongue.

Above them, the fire settled into its new anchorage with a sound like a cathedral exhaling. The light dimmed to a deep, banked ember-glow, patient now, tied down, safe for a thousand years or until the world found a better way. The drowned city stood dry and dark and quiet around them, its work done, its secret kept in the only vault that had never once been robbed: a willing heart.

Somewhere down the flooded road, boots were splashing. Many boots. And a voice Wren knew—Veyra's voice, iron over grief—calling orders in the dark.

"They're coming," Wren said, sliding off the throne. Their legs shook. "The inquisitors. And they have Brannoc."

Tamsin's head came up sharply. "Brannoc Hale? How do you—" The stranger frowned, pressing fingers to their temple. "I know that name. Why do I know that name? Why do I know *you*? Why do I want to—" They stopped. Their jaw set in a way that Wren knew, knew bone-deep, knew from a hundred escapes and a thousand miles: the look of Tamsin Brightwater deciding that questions could wait, because running couldn't.

"Later," Tamsin said. "Questions later. Running now. That much I apparently know how to do." They grabbed Wren's hand—hesitated, puzzled by their own instinct, and did it anyway—and pulled them toward the far passage, away from the approaching torchlight. "Stay close, kid. Whatever you are."

Wren's heart broke cleanly, the way a wish breaks, and held.

Because Tamsin's hand did not let go. Because the grip was sure and warm and *chosen*, chosen by muscles and instinct deeper than memory, the body remembering what the mind had paid away. Because as they ran, the stranger glanced down at them and said, gruffly, inexplicably, with no idea where the words came from:

"Never pay the first price, little bird. The first price is a lie wearing a hat."

And Wren, crying and laughing at once, squeezed that hand and answered in the old tongue and the new one both:

"I know. I'm going to spend the rest of my life proving it."

Behind them the Ember Throne dimmed to embers; ahead, torchlight bloomed at the mouth of the hall, and Veyra's voice rang out across the sleeping city—demanding, desperate, and about to meet the one thing she had ridden all this way to destroy: a Dragonheir with nothing left to burn, guarded by a thief with nothing left to lose but the choice to begin again.
