## 21. A Stranger at the Door

The storm came up the valley like an old creditor—slowly, then all at once, and demanding everything.

Tamsin Brightwater heard it first as a change in the sound of the pines below the cottage, a long low hiss rolling uphill, and set down the cup of tea they had been pretending to drink. Three weeks since Vael Auren. Three weeks since the fire went out of the world with a sound like an enormous sigh, since they had walked out of the drowned dark with wet boots and a head that felt, in certain places, like a house with rooms bricked shut.

They knew the rooms were there. That was the cruelty of it. They could stand in the doorway of their own mind and see the fresh mortar, feel the smooth blankness where something had been carved out—and not know what shape had lived in it.

The Spire's mages had called it *clean work*. Inquisitor Veyra herself, standing on the black shore while the last embers died beneath the water, had looked at Tamsin with something that was almost gentleness and said, "You won't even miss it."

"I'll know I'm missing something," Tamsin had answered.

"Yes," said Veyra. "That's what freely given means. It leaves a hole shaped like itself." And she had turned away, and carried her lie back down the mountain—the Heir is dead, the Heir is dead, let the kingdoms sleep—and Brannoc had gone east toward exile without so much as a wave, because Brannoc Hale had never in his life been good at goodbyes, and Tamsin had climbed alone through the rain to a cottage that smelled of cold ash and old tea.

Three weeks of that. Of waking at odd hours with the certainty that someone had spoken to them in the night. Of finding, twice now, small muddy footprints on the porch that matched no animal they knew and could only be explained by their own sleepwalking, which they did not believe for a moment but told themselves anyway, because the alternative was a grief they couldn't find the edges of.

So when the knocking came—three sharp raps, polite as a tax collector—they were already standing.

* * *

Lightning whitened the windows. Out in the yard, half-drowned in the downpour, stood a child.

Tamsin's hand found the iron poker by the door out of pure smuggler's habit, and then hung there, useless.

It was a boy, or a girl, or simply a child—rain-slicked, maybe nine years old, wearing a coat three sizes too big with the sleeves rolled and still swallowing its hands. Dark hair plastered flat. A satchel across one shoulder. And under the other arm, held with both hands the way you'd hold something that mattered more than staying dry: a wooden dragon, carved from pale mountain pine, wings swept back, tail curled, no bigger than a loaf of bread.

Tamsin knew that dragon. Had watched it take shape over a whole winter of bad nights, whittled by lamplight from a piece of scrap pine, for a child who—

For a child who—

The thought slid off, the way water slides off oiled cloth. There. The mortar again. Tamsin stood in their own doorway with rain blowing in and felt the hole-shaped absence press against them from the inside, and did what they always did with things too large to hold: made a joke of it.

"You're either very lost," they called over the wind, "or very committed. Either way, you're letting all my warm air escape."

The child smiled.

That was the worst part. Not the dragon, not the familiarity of the stance—one hip cocked, chin tilted up against the weather, entirely unbothered by a storm that would have sent a grown smuggler whimpering indoors. The smile. Because Tamsin had seen that exact smile before, they were sure of it, sure the way you're sure of a word on the tip of your tongue, and the knowing sat right on top of the mortar and refused to go through it.

The child shouted something. The storm ate most of it, but the shape of the sounds was wrong—not common tongue, not lowland trade-speech, not anything Tamsin had ever haggled in. A dead language, said a voice in Tamsin's memory that wasn't quite a memory. *A language no living kingdom uses.*

"Come inside before the mountain decides you're a sacrifice," Tamsin said, and stepped back, and the child came in trailing water and the smell of pine resin and lightning.

* * *

They got the coat off the child—it required negotiation, conducted mostly in gestures, the child cooperating cheerfully but refusing to surrender the dragon even to be unburdened, so that eventually Tamsin had to thread one arm out of a sleeve and then the other around the carved thing like a dance partner—and sat them by the fire wrapped in the good wool blanket, the one Tamsin had bought two winters ago and never used, because it had seemed like the kind of thing you kept for company.

Huh, thought Tamsin. Kept for company. Where had that come from?

The child drank hot tea with honey like it was a religious observance, both hands around the cup, eyes closed on the first swallow. Then opened them and looked around the cottage with frank, unhurried curiosity: the shelves, the bundles of dried herbs Tamsin didn't remember buying, the stack of whittling knives by the hearth worn smooth with use.

"You can talk," Tamsin said. "I assume. You just don't seem inclined to do it in any language I've got."

The child considered this. Then pointed at the dragon, then at themselves, then made a little walking motion with two fingers across their palm, and pointed north-ish, toward the high passes, and shrugged with the eloquence of the truly unconcerned.

"That explains nothing," Tamsin said, "and yet I understand you completely, which is worrying me."

The child grinned. Rainwater dripped off a curl of hair onto the floorboards. Outside, thunder walked across the peaks like something enormous changing its mind.

Tamsin should have asked more questions. Should have asked how a child came to be alone on Thornfell in a storm, where their parents were, whether anyone was looking for them, whether *anything* was looking for them—and the asking stuck somewhere behind Tamsin's breastbone, because every question circled back to the same hollow place. *Why does this feel like coming home? Why does the sight of that ridiculous dragon make my chest hurt?*

Instead they heard themselves say, "There's stew. It's mostly turnip. Don't look at me like that; beggars and memory-thieves can't be choosers, and lately I've mostly been the second, which pays worse than you'd think."

* * *

The child ate three bowls.

Between bowls, Tamsin talked, because silence in that cottage had gotten heavy these past weeks, and talking to a child who understood perhaps one word in ten was easier than talking to no one. They talked about the weather. They talked about the state of the roof. They talked, carefully, lightly, about nothing at all—and found themselves, somewhere around the second bowl, saying:

"—and there was a smuggler once, Brannoc Hale, built like a door and about as subtle, who owed me his life, and repaid the debt by trying to sell—" a pause, a swerve, "—by trying to sell *me* out, which is the traditional method of repayment in our profession, actually, we have songs about it. Terrible ones. Mostly about betrayal. It's a rich field."

The child looked up from the stew with gravy on their chin and an expression of such perfect, skeptical judgment that Tamsin laughed out loud for the first time in three weeks.

"Don't give me that. You don't know my life."

The child pointed at the dragon, then at Tamsin, then pressed one small fist against their own chest, and nodded slowly, gravely, as if to say: *don't I?*

The fire popped. Something in Tamsin's chest did too—a small clean crack, like ice giving way on a spring pond.

"I carved that," they heard themselves say. Their own voice sounded far away. "Didn't I. I don't—I know I carved it. My hands remember the knife-work. I can't remember making it. I can't remember who it was for." They looked at the child, really looked, and the hole in their mind ached the way a missing tooth aches, tongue returning and returning to the gap. "Who are you?"

The child set down the spoon. Wiped their chin with the back of their hand, which did nothing useful. And then, instead of answering, reached into the oversized coat—Tamsin hadn't taken it all the way off after all, just draped it over the chair—and drew out a folded square of oilcloth, and unwrapped it with the care of a priest handling relics.

Inside lay a strip of birch bark, curled with age, covered in dense lines of writing in a script Tamsin had never learned and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize a song you can't name. And beneath the writing, in careful common letters that wobbled the way letters wobble when written by a hand still learning them:

*I am going to forget you. This is how you will remember me.*

Below that, smaller:

*Ask me about the mountains. I will tell you true.*

Tamsin read it four times. The storm raged. The fire settled. Somewhere inside them, against the smooth brick of that sealed room, something knocked—three sharp raps, polite as a tax collector.

"Heir," Tamsin whispered. Not a question. The word arrived whole, unbidden, from whatever part of them the price had not touched. "Wren."

The child's face did something complicated—joy and grief holding hands—and they nodded, and pointed at themselves, and then shook their head, and pointed at themselves again, and spread their empty hands: *yes, and also no. The same, and a stranger.*

"The fire's gone," Tamsin said slowly, testing each word against the dark. "You gave it up. Or we did. Someone paid—" and their hand rose without permission and pressed flat against their own sternum, over the ache. "Someone paid for it. I don't remember paying. But I remember the receipt."

Wren—that was the only name Tamsin had, and it fit like a coat three sizes too big, and they wore it anyway—took up the birch bark and turned it over. More common letters, more wobbling:

*Veyra says you will try to give it back. She says you will try to steal yourself a memory to fill the hole. She says thieves always do.*

Tamsin laughed, and it came out wet. "She's right. I already thought about it. Standing right here, not ten minutes ago, I thought: *I could go back down, I could find a fragment, the city's sleeping but not gone, I could lift one memory of that child out of the dark and nobody would ever know—*" They stopped. Looked at Wren, dripping and grave and nine years old, holding a wooden dragon like a torch. "But she also wrote the rest of it, didn't she. Your letter. *Freely given.* That was the whole trick. The fire wouldn't seal for stolen things. It only sleeps if the anchor is honest."

Wren nodded.

"So if I stole it back, it would unmake everything. The truce. The sleeping city. All of it. One thief's nostalgia versus the peace of six kingdoms." Tamsin dragged a hand down their face. "Veyra would burn my cottage with me in it. Brannoc would write a terrible song. And you—" they looked at the child, at the stranger with familiar eyes, "—you wouldn't even know what you'd lost. Again."

Wren reached across the table and put one small, stew-warm hand over Tamsin's scarred knuckles, and squeezed, and said—in common tongue, halting, accented, clearly rehearsed for weeks against exactly this moment:

"Stay. Tell me stories. I will tell you mine. We will make new ones." A pause; a frown of concentration; then, with the triumph of a scholar nailing a thesis: "*Slowly.*"

And Tamsin Brightwater—who had spent a lifetime taking memories because keeping them meant losing people, who had built a whole philosophy out of traveling light, who had once believed that the safest heart was an empty one—sat in their own kitchen and felt that philosophy collapse like wet cardboard, and found they didn't mind at all.

"All right," they said roughly. "All right. Slowly." They stood, because standing was easier than crying in front of a child, and got down the extra bowl, the one they'd kept for company. "First story: there was once a fool who carved a dragon for a kid they'd known three days, which tells you everything about the state of the fool's judgment, and the kid kept it through a war, which tells you everything about the kid."

Outside, the storm was softening into steady rain. Inside, Wren laughed—a bright, startling sound, a stranger's laugh in a beloved register—and Tamsin thought: *I loved this child once. I don't remember it. I get to do it again.*

Somewhere far below the mountain, in the drowned dark, the old city slept without its fire, and did not stir.

And on the birch bark, beneath the letter, in letters Tamsin had not noticed until the firelight shifted—small, wry, unmistakably addressed to them—two final lines waited:

*P.S. The stew was never mostly turnip. You lied beautifully.*
*P.P.S. There are footprints on your porch. That was me. I've been coming for two weeks. I wanted to be sure you'd open the door.*

Tamsin looked at the child. The child looked at Tamsin, the picture of innocence, and took another spoonful of stew.

"Oh, you're going to be *trouble*," Tamsin said, and meant it as the highest praise they knew, and began—slowly, together, storm-bound and unhurried—to build something new out of everything that was left.
