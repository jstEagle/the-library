## 22. Choir and Spire Anew

The storm came in off the peaks on the last night of autumn, the way storms always came, as if Thornfell had been saving it up all season and couldn't wait a moment longer to spend it.

Tamsin Brightwater was sitting by the fire with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands when the knock came at the door—two small fists, three sharp raps, the exact rhythm of a child who had learned that grown folk answered faster if you knocked like you meant it.

Tamsin knew who it was before they opened the door. Knew it the way you know rain is coming by the ache in old scars, or the way you know a song's ending by its first note. There was no reason to know. The child on the doorstep was a stranger. That was the whole point of everything they'd done.

But Tamsin knew.

They opened the door onto a wall of wet grey wind, and there stood Wren—soaked through, mud to the knees, shivering like a leaf in a gale. In one small hand the child clutched a carved wooden dragon, its wings worn smooth from handling, its tail snapped off somewhere long ago and mended badly with twine.

Tamsin looked at the child for a long moment. The child looked back. Neither of them said anything, because neither of them remembered anything worth saying.

"Well," Tamsin said at last, stepping aside. "You look half-drowned. Come in before the storm decides to finish the job."

* * *

Here is what Tamsin Brightwater remembered of that autumn:

They remembered waking in Vael Auren with sand in their boots and an emptiness behind their eyes so vast it echoed. They remembered Brannoc Hale hauling them up the drowned road on his shoulders, cursing every god he could name and inventing two new ones for variety. They remembered Veyra standing at the Weeping Gate with her grey cloak plastered to her body, watching the last light of dragon-fire gutter out beneath the mountain, and saying, in a voice stripped down to bare stone: *"It's done. Go home. All of you."*

They remembered asking Brannoc, later, on the high passes: "What did I give up?"

And Brannoc—who had carried them, who had held the gate, who had spent three days in a Spire cell and walked away from his freedom because some debts are paid in blood and some are paid in worse—had looked at them with an expression Tamsin had no stolen memory to decode.

"You gave up a kid," he said. "A mute kid who talked in dead tongues. You gave up—" He stopped. Started again. "You gave up something you loved, Tamsin. That's all I can tell you. I never had it myself, so I don't know what it looked like from inside."

That was the shape of it. A hole shaped like a child, in a life shaped like a thief's.

Tamsin had gone home to the crooked house under the eaves of Thornfell. Had taken up the old trade, or tried to. But the trade tasted different now. Every mind they touched felt thin. Every stolen year felt borrowed twice over. They found themselves sitting up nights wondering what it was they'd lost, and whether grief for something you cannot remember still counts as grief.

It does. Tamsin could have told anyone who asked. Nobody asked.

And then, on the last night of autumn, in the teeth of a storm, a stranger knocked on the door carrying a broken dragon, and the hole in Tamsin's life sat down at their table, dripped on their floor, and asked—in careful, halting common speech—for tea.

* * *

Wren, it turned out, remembered nothing either.

The sealing had taken everything: the spires burning, the drowned city, the throne of embers, the war's whole long shadow—and along with it, every moment between the child and the thief. Wren knew only that there had been a storm, and a door, and a feeling like a hand pulling them toward this particular valley in these particular mountains, the way water knows downhill.

"I followed the road," the child said, three weeks in, wrapped in a blanket two sizes too big, watching Tamsin mend a fishing net by lamplight. "But I didn't know where I was going until I saw your chimney smoke."

Tamsin's hands went very still on the net.

"Is that right," they said, in the flat tone they used for prices and lies.

"You're the only person I remember at all," Wren said. "Isn't that strange? I don't remember my own name from before. I don't remember where I slept or what I ate. But I remember that someone would open this door."

Tamsin thought about the hole behind their own eyes, shaped exactly like this conversation.

"Not strange," they said. "Just how things go, sometimes. Drink your tea."

* * *

Building new memories, it emerged, was slow work. Nothing like theft. Theft was instant—a touch, a flood, a lifetime poured into you in the space of a heartbeat. Building was different. Building was teaching a child to gut a fish without gagging, and failing, and both of them laughing until the hermit herbalist Marta next door banged on the shared wall. Building was winter mornings spent arguing about whose turn it was to feed the goats (the goats were named Turnip and Turnip Two; naming them had been Tamsin's idea and they regretted it daily). Building was Wren learning letters by carving them into the tabletop, so that by spring the table read, around its rim, like a border inscription: *TAMSIN SNORES LIKE A ROCKSLIDE. THIS IS TRUE AND CAN BE PROVEN.*

"You carved that in my good table."

"It's true and can be proven."

"I'll sell you to the tinker."

"You won't." And here Wren smiled the smile that undid Tamsin completely, though they could not have said why. "You keep me."

Which was true. Which was the whole strange truth of it, and neither of them could account for it, and after a while they stopped trying.

Tamsin kept the trade, but changed its shape. Word went round the smuggler roads that Brightwater the memory-thief had gone soft—that they'd take a bad memory off your hands, a grief, a shame, a night you couldn't stop reliving, and carry it away for nothing but a meal and a bed. They became less a thief than a kind of rag-and-bone collector of other people's pain. It didn't pay. It paid in other ways. Old Halver the toll-warden wept on Tamsin's doorstep the day he handed over the false beautiful memory Tamsin had once sold him—the lie of a life he'd never lived—and asked, voice cracking, if he could please just have his own plain years back, even the ugly ones.

Tamsin took the false memory and burned it in the hearth while Halver watched, and the man went home lighter by thirty years of beautiful lies.

"That was a good thing you did," Wren said that night.

"Don't spread it around. I've got a reputation."

"Everyone already knows. Brannoc told the whole caravan route."

Brannoc came by twice a year, once going down to the lowlands and once coming back up. He'd taken exile rather than the Spire's reward, which meant he now ran honest cargo on dishonest roads, a distinction he maintained with great seriousness. He brought Wren honey-cakes and Tamsin news, and each visit he studied Tamsin with that same unreadable expression, as if checking a wound for signs of healing.

"You look better," he said, on the second visit, over the third cup of Marta's terrible cider.

"I look the same."

"You look like someone decided to stay put. It suits you. Like a boot that finally fits."

"Flatter me harder, why don't you."

He laughed, then grew quiet, turning his cup. "You ever wonder what it was?" he asked. "What you gave?"

"Every day."

"And you don't want to take it back? You could find someone who was there. Someone who remembers. One touch and—"

"No." Tamsin said it fast enough to surprise themselves. Then, slower: "No. It wasn't mine to keep. Whatever it was, I gave it. Taking it back would make the giving worth nothing."

Brannoc nodded slowly, and drank, and said no more about it. But at the door, leaving, he put his big hand briefly on Tamsin's shoulder and squeezed, and whatever passed between them needed no memory at all.

* * *

Veyra sent a letter once a year. It arrived by courier, sealed with the Spire's grey wax, and it was always the same length—one page, no more, as if brevity were a discipline she practiced like swordwork.

*The city sleeps,* the first letter said. *My surveyors confirm the fires are cold. The Spire has closed the matter. I have not. I think of it daily. I think, also, of a smuggler who chose wrong and right in the same breath. If you ever need a word spoken in high places, you have it. — V.*

Tamsin wrote back once: *Come see us sometime. Bring nothing official.* No reply came, but the following spring a package arrived containing a Spire-issue field knife, the finest steel Tamsin had ever held, and no note whatsoever, which from Veyra was practically a sonnet.

The mountains, meanwhile, kept their secrets. That much was in the bargain, though nobody had written the terms. Travelers spoke sometimes of lights deep under Thornfell, blue-green and slow, like lanterns in a drowned hall—but the lights were fainter every year, and by the second winter the shepherds stopped mentioning them at all. Somewhere below, Vael Auren lay dark and dreaming, its choir silent, its spire unlit, its carvings of dragons and thieves side by side slowly wearing smooth under the patient water.

Once—only once—Wren woke screaming in the night, and when Tamsin burst in, the child was staring at their own hands as if they belonged to someone else.

"I dreamed about fire," Wren whispered. "But it wasn't frightening. It was sad. Like a friend leaving on a boat."

"Dreams are just the mind sweeping its floors," Tamsin said, sitting on the edge of the bed. "Sweep it back out and sleep."

Wren reached up and caught their sleeve. "Stay till I'm asleep?"

Tamsin stayed. Of course they stayed. They would have stayed till dawn if asked, and part of them—the part with the echoing hole—suspected they had done exactly that, once, in another life neither of them owned anymore.

* * *

Spring came late that year, and when it came it came all at once, the way it does in the mountains—snowmelt roaring in the streams, the valley green overnight as if painted by a hurried god. Marta's herb garden exploded. Turnip Two escaped twice. Wren shot up half a hand's width and complained endlessly about sleeves that no longer fit.

On the first truly warm evening, Tamsin and Wren climbed the ridge behind the house, as they'd begun doing whenever the weather allowed—a ritual neither of them named, though both of them guarded it fiercely. From the ridge you could see the whole spine of Thornfell, purple in the dusk, and below it the crooked roofs of the market town, and beyond that, everywhere, the world going on.

"Do you ever think about what's down there?" Wren asked, chin on their knees, nodding toward the mountains' roots.

"Tunnels? Caves? Rocks being extremely old and smug about it?"

"The city."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. The wind moved through the pines with a sound almost like voices, almost like singing, if you let yourself imagine it.

"Sometimes," they admitted. "I dream about it. Water and stone. Something enormous sleeping. I try not to make too much of dreams."

"I think it's peaceful now," Wren said. "Whatever's down there. I think it finished being angry."

"That would be nice."

"I think—" The child hesitated, hunting for words, the way they still did sometimes, though the common tongue had settled into them like a bird into a new nest. "I think somebody made sure of it. Somebody paid for it. I hope whoever that was doesn't feel sad about it anymore."

Tamsin looked at the child's profile against the darkening sky—the stubborn jaw, the mud on their boots, the little scar from the fish-gutting incident—and felt the echo behind their eyes shift and settle, the way a house settles, the way grief settles when it stops being a wound and becomes a room you live in.

"Maybe they don't," Tamsin said. "Maybe they got a kid out of the bargain. Bad-tempered. Carves rude things into furniture."

"I heard that."

"You were meant to."

They sat together until the stars came out, and the mountain sang its almost-singing, and below them the town lit its windows one by one like a slow choir finding its notes.

* * *

The storm came again in autumn, because storms in Thornfell keep their appointments. Tamsin was at the fire with cold tea when they heard the door bang open below—Wren, home early from Marta's, shaking off rain like a dog, shouting up the stairs:

"Tamsin! The river's flooded the lower path! Marta says come quick, there's a caravan stuck on the far bank, and Brannoc's with them, and he says—" a pause, a grin audible in the pause itself, "—he says bring the good cups, because he's staying the week!"

Tamsin set down the tea. Went to the window. Watched the rain hammer the valley and the lightning stitch the peaks together, and felt—not the echo, not the hole, but something else, something built new, board by board, memory by small memory: a morning's laughter over fish guts, a table full of carved letters, a hand catching a sleeve in the dark.

Some things, they thought, aren't stolen and aren't given. Some things you build. And the building is the keeping.

"TAMSIN! The cups!"

"Coming, coming. Don't let him near my chair, he'll break it, he breaks everything—"

Below, the child laughed, and the sound filled the crooked house to its rafters, and outside, far under the mountain, the old city turned over in its sleep and dreamed of nothing at all.
