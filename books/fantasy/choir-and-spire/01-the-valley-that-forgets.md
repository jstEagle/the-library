## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bell rang at dusk, as it always did, and the valley exhaled.

Vesper Vane stood in the doorway of the guildhall with her hands folded and her face arranged into the mild blankness that had kept her fed and unnoticed for twenty-three years. Below her, Hollowmere performed its evening liturgy without knowing it was one. Lamplighters moved along Mill Row with their long poles. The baker's boy hauled his empty trays indoors. At the well, old Marta Brask paused mid-draw, head bowed, lips moving through whatever small prayer she offered the dark—*keep us, keep us, keep us*—the way other people said *goodnight*.

And beneath all of it, so low that no one else could possibly hear it, the ward hummed.

It was not a sound, exactly. It lived somewhere behind her teeth, in the soft tissue of her ears, a sustained note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. She had heard it for as long as she could remember. As a child she'd assumed everyone did—that the hum was simply part of the valley, like the smell of the tannery or the crookedness of the church spire—and she'd mentioned it once, casually, to her foster mother, who had looked at her with an expression Vesper still couldn't name and never spoke of it again. After that, Vesper learned to fold the sound away, to keep it behind her teeth where it belonged. Being forgettable was a craft. She had practiced it the way other children practiced whistling.

The bell finished its ninth stroke. Down in the lanes, people straightened, satisfied, and went about their suppers. None of them knew why the bell rang. None of them asked. That was the arrangement, though *arrangement* implied someone had sat at a table and agreed to terms, and Vesper suspected the truth was older and stranger than any bargain anyone alive remembered making.

She closed the guildhall door behind her and went up to Master Aldous.

* * *

He lay in the narrow bed in the east room, which had been her room once, before he took it because it caught the morning sun and he said a dying man deserved at least that much theft. He was very thin now. His hands, which had once coaxed music out of anything—a wineglass, a fence wire, the ribs of a cat—lay on the blanket like two shed gloves.

"You're late," he said, without opening his eyes.

"The bell ran long."

"The bell doesn't run long. You linger." His mouth twitched. "You always linger at the window. Twenty years, girl, and you think I haven't noticed you listening to things."

Vesper's stomach performed a slow, cold turn. She crossed to the basin, wrung out a cloth, and busied herself with his forehead. "You're feverish."

"I'm dying. There's a difference, and I have time now to appreciate it." He opened his eyes then, pale gray, sharp as ever. "Sit down, Vesper."

She sat.

For a while there was only the sound of his breathing, which had developed a rattle at the bottom of each breath, like water in a cracked jug. Outside, the valley settled into its nightly quiet. And beneath the quiet, the hum went on—but tonight, Vesper noticed, it wavered. Just slightly. The way a note wavers when the singer's breath is failing.

"How many of us are left?" Aldous asked suddenly.

"Of what?"

"Don't be coy. It's unbecoming and we don't have the hours for it." He turned his head against the pillow. "The guild. The Choir of Hollowmere. How many?"

Vesper looked at her hands. "You. Me."

"And before this winter?"

"You, me, and Brother Callum. And before last spring, Sister Ennet. And before—" She stopped.

"And before," Aldous agreed softly. "Say it. Say the whole ledger. A bookkeeper who won't read her own books is just a woman surrounded by paper."

"You, me, Brother Callum, Sister Ennet, Mother Ilse, Father Dorn, and Master Wren." Seven names. She hadn't spoken them aloud together in years; they tasted like dust and candle smoke. "Nine when I arrived. Eleven when you were young. The records say forty in your master's day. Two hundred at the founding."

"And how many people live in this valley, would you guess?"

"Four thousand. Perhaps four and a half."

"Mm." He stared at the ceiling. "One singer per five hundred souls. When the founding choir stood, it was one per twenty. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That we're understaffed."

Aldous laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and Vesper held the cup to his lips until the fit passed. When he could speak again, his voice had dropped to something almost tender.

"You are the most infuriating apprentice I have ever trained, and I trained Wren, who once tried to tune a ward-stone using a chicken bone and sheer confidence."

"What happened to the chicken?"

"We ate the evidence." He closed his eyes again. "Go down and bank the fire. Then come back. There's a thing I've been putting off telling you, and I find I can't put it off past tonight."

* * *

The guildhall at night was a different building than by day.

By day it was merely shabby: a long hall with a vaulted ceiling gone gray with dust, rows of benches nobody sat in, a dais at the far end where the full choir had once stood to sing the evening office. The townsfolk called it the Old Singing House and believed it was a church that had lost its congregation. They were not wrong, exactly. They were wrong in every particular that mattered.

But at night, when Vesper walked its length alone with a single lamp, the hall remembered. She felt it in the floorboards—in the way her footsteps fell into a rhythm not quite her own, a processional cadence worn into the wood by two hundred years of feet. She felt it in the acoustic shadows above the dais, where the vaulting still held, faint as breath on a mirror, the shape of harmonies sung so often they'd soaked into the stone. If she stood in the center of the dais and sang a single note, the hall would answer her, swelling her voice into something vast and mournful, and for a moment she could almost hear the missing voices around her—the basses rumbling in the floor, the descant flickering in the rafters like birds.

She had never told anyone this either. It went on the same shelf as the humming: the shelf of things Vesper Vane kept behind her teeth.

At the back of the hall stood the door to the undercroft, and beside the door, half-sunk into the wall itself, the first of the ward-stones.

There were nine of them in the valley proper—one at each compass point of the hollow, one at the pass, one at the millrace, and three whose locations were recorded only in the guild's sealed ledgers. They were waist-high, rounded by weather, and marked with spirals that might have been carvings or might have been growths. Every child in Hollowmere climbed on them. Every farmer cursed them when his plow struck one. Not one soul in four thousand had ever asked what they were for.

Vesper pressed her palm flat against the stone.

The hum leapt to meet her, climbing from behind her teeth into her bones, and for a moment she could read it the way Aldous had taught her to read weather in the ring of a bell: healthy, healthy, healthy—then a flicker, brief as a skipped heartbeat, where the note thinned to almost nothing and recovered. She snatched her hand back.

That was new.

Three months ago the flicker had come once a night. Last month, once an hour. Tonight, standing there with her heart going hard against her ribs, she counted four skips in the space of a minute, and the fourth one lasted long enough that she heard—she was nearly sure she heard—something on the far side of the silence. Not a sound. An attention. Like the pause between lightning and thunder, stretched thin over everything.

She banked the fire. She climbed the stairs. She made herself walk slowly, because Aldous was watching for her fear and she had learned long ago that fear shared is fear doubled.

* * *

He had propped himself up against the pillows, and he had the ledger open across his knees—the old one, the sealed one, bound in gray leather with the guild's mark stamped blind: a circle broken by nine points. No one but the two of them living could have opened it. The lock answered to song, and Vesper had never been taught the opening verse. She realized, with a small cold shock, that he must have sung it while she was below.

"You went into my ledgers," she said.

"I went into *my* ledgers. You inherited them the day you first hummed the evening office back to me, note-perfect, at age six, and pretended afterward that you hadn't." He patted the bed beside him. "Sit. And bring the lamp close. My eyes are going the way of the rest of me."

She sat. The pages, when he turned them, were dense with notation she only half recognized—the guild's cipher, mixing musical staves with characters that predated any alphabet used in the valley.

"Do you know why the ward holds?" Aldous asked.

"Because the choir sings. Sang. Because it was built to hold."

"No." He said it gently, the way he corrected her pitch. "The guild didn't build the ward, Vesper. You can't build a thing like that. Walls you build. This—" he laid his hand flat on the page, on the nine-pointed circle, "—this is a *bargain*. Something old lives under this valley, or around it, or through it—I've read six accounts and no two agree—and long ago our founders went to it and negotiated. Safety for the valley. Forever, or near enough. And the price was—"

He stopped. Not for breath. For courage, Vesper realized. She had watched this man set his own broken leg with a splint and a swear word, and he was gathering courage to finish a sentence.

"The price was memory," he said. "Paid forward. Each generation of the choir gives up some portion of what they are—the songs take it, that's what the songs are *for*, they're the mechanism of payment. Why do you think the records grow thinner as they go? Why do you think the guild forgot its own purpose, decade by decade, until we were left ringing a bell none of us could explain and tending stones none of us understood? We haven't been keeping the ward, girl. We've been *paying* it. And the payments have been getting smaller because the payers keep dying, and the thing on the other side has begun to notice."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Somewhere far off, too far to be real, Vesper thought she heard the hum skip again.

"Aldous." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "What happens when the payments stop entirely?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him reach over and grip her wrist, hard, with those glove-shed hands.

"Then the valley stops being safe," he said, "and starts being *noticed*. Now listen to me. There is a song—the binding, the true one, the one the founding choir sang—and I know half of it. Half a melody, half the words, and the half I know I only know because my master sang it to me once when he was dying, the way I'm singing it to you now, and gods forgive us both, we've let it wear down like a coin passed hand to hand until the face is rubbed smooth."

He began to sing.

His voice was wrecked—it scraped and frayed and dropped out entirely twice—but underneath the ruin Vesper could hear the architecture, the great load-bearing phrases of it, and her skin prickled because the hall below was answering, the stones answering, the whole valley's hidden machinery leaning toward the sound like grass toward sun. She memorized as she had never memorized in her life, carving each interval into herself, knowing even as she did it that half a melody was worse than none—a bridge that ends over the water.

He got to the seventh phrase. His eyes had closed. His hand still gripped her wrist, and she felt the grip change—not letting go, exactly, but loosening, the way a knot loosens—

"—and the eighth phrase rises," he whispered, "on the *fourth*, not the fifth, mind the fourth, it wants the fourth—"

And stopped.

The rattle in his chest went on a moment longer, out of habit, and then it didn't.

Vesper sat very still. The lamp burned. The ledger lay open on her knees, heavy as a sleeping animal. Outside, far below in the valley, the evening bell was silent because the evening was done, and the hum behind her teeth wavered, and skipped, and in the gap of the skip she heard—or dreamed she heard, and would spend the rest of her life unsure—something patient and enormous turning its attention toward Hollowmere, the way a man turns toward a door he has just heard unlatch.

She sang the seven phrases back into the dark, once, to make sure she had them.

Then she rose, and trimmed the lamp, and began, alone, to wonder what came after the fourth.
