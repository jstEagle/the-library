## 2. Answers

The rain came in the night, and by morning everyone in Hollowmere agreed it was a blessing.

Vesper heard them saying it at the well, at the bakery, at the shrine where old Marta left her usual candle and one extra for luck. *Answered at last,* they said. *The valley provides.* Old Cobb Hessel had prayed for rain on his barley for three weeks running—prayed aloud, at the shrine, the way people did when they wanted to be seen praying—and now his fields stood dark and dripping under a sky that had been cracked and dry since midsummer.

Nobody asked how. That was the thing about Hollowmere that Vesper had never been able to explain to herself until she was old enough to find it strange: nobody in the valley ever asked *how*. The bell rang at dusk and no one wondered who first hung it. The ward-stones circled the valley like gray teeth and no one wondered what they kept out, or what it cost to keep it out. Prayers were said, and mostly they went nowhere, and everyone knew that too—knew it the way you know the floor will hold you, without thinking about it—and so when one of them suddenly *didn't* go nowhere, the valley simply smiled and called it providence.

Vesper stood at her window with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and watched the water run down the lane, and she did not smile.

Because she had heard the ward all night.

She always heard it, faintly, the way you hear your own heartbeat: a low hum beneath everything, steady as a held note, the sound the guildhall made when the wind moved through its empty corridors. Her whole life it had been the same—one long unbroken tone, so constant she could sleep inside it like a stone sleeps inside a hill.

Last night it had stuttered.

Three times, just past midnight, the hum had thinned to almost nothing—a silence with edges, a held breath—and each time something on the far side of that silence had pressed against it, the way a tongue presses into a loose tooth. And each time the ward had caught itself, shuddered back up to fullness, and gone on humming as if nothing had happened.

Except that rain had fallen on Cobb Hessel's barley and nowhere else. She'd checked at dawn. The lane beyond his fence line was dry dust. The rain fell in a perfect circle around his fields, neat as a drawn boundary, and then stopped.

Prayers were supposed to go nowhere. The guild had spent four hundred years making sure of it. Master Aldous had told her once, half-asleep, that unanswered prayer was the guild's proudest work—that every soul in Hollowmere slept safe because their small hopes died quietly on the air instead of being *heard* by whatever lived outside the stones.

And now something out there was hearing them. And answering. Carefully. Neatly. Like a shopkeeper counting change.

Vesper drank the cold tea anyway, dressed, and went to see about the boy.

* * *

Tam Wren's cottage sat at the crook of the millstream, and there was a crowd outside it, which was never a good sign in a valley the size of Hollowmere. They parted for her without really seeing her—people rarely saw Vesper Vane; she had built a whole life out of being the girl who wasn't worth noticing—but she heard the story assembling itself in their murmurs before she reached the door.

*The widow Sela Wren. Dead two years this autumn. Fever. And there she was on the doorstep at first light, calling for her Tammy.*

The boy was nine. He had prayed at the shrine every night since the funeral—everyone knew that, everyone thought it sweet—and last night he had prayed harder than usual, because yesterday would have been his mother's birthday.

Vesper pushed through into the kitchen.

Sela Wren was sitting at the table.

She looked almost right. That was the first thing—the *almost*. She had Sela's broad hands folded on the wood, Sela's brown hair pinned back, Sela's faded blue dress with the mended elbow. But the dress was wrong in its details: the mend at the elbow was on the wrong arm. The hair was pinned the way you'd pin hair if you had only ever seen it from across a room. And the face—

The face was Sela's face rendered by someone working from memory. From *someone's* memory. A very good memory, but a memory nonetheless, and memories are soft in places photographs are sharp. The eyes were the worst. They were the correct color and the correct shape and they did not blink often enough, and when they did blink, both lids came down together like shutters.

Little Tam stood in the corner with his grandmother's arms locked around him, and his face was doing something terrible—not joy, not fear, but both at once, tearing him down the middle.

"Mama?" he kept saying. "Mama, is it—you came *back?*"

"I came back," the thing said, and its voice was Sela's voice played slightly too slow, like a song remembered rather than sung. "I heard you, my love. I heard every night."

Vesper's skin crawled. Because that was true. That was exactly, precisely true. Something had heard.

"Who are you?" she said.

The heads turned—all of them, neighbors and the grandmother and the boy—and the thing wearing Sela Wren looked at Vesper with those shutter-eyes, and for a moment something behind the face seemed to consider her. Weighing. Then it smiled, and the smile was warm and motherly and arrived a half-second late, like thunder after lightning.

"I'm Tammy's mama," it said gently. "Aren't I?"

"You're dead," Vesper said. "You died of fever two autumns ago. I helped dig the grave."

Murmuring behind her. The grandmother's grip tightened on the boy.

"Graves are for bodies," the thing said pleasantly. "I was never in the grave."

"*Out*," Vesper said, and her voice came out with an edge she didn't recognize, and to her own surprise the neighbors obeyed her—shepherded the boy and his grandmother toward the door while the boy wept and reached backward. The thing at the table watched them go without moving. It didn't try to follow. It only watched, and smiled its late smile, and said, to Vesper alone, in a voice gone quiet and thoughtful:

"You can hear it, can't you. The wall. You flinch when it stutters."

The door closed between them. Vesper stood in the yard shaking, telling herself she'd imagined the last part, knowing she hadn't.

* * *

The guildhall records were kept in the undercroft, in ledgers bound in gray leather that no one but Vesper had opened in thirty years. She took a lamp down the spiral stair past the empty choir stalls—four hundred years ago, forty voices had sung here nightly; now the benches gathered dust and her own footsteps sounded obscene—and unlocked the cabinet with the key Master Aldous kept on a cord around his neck, which he had given her last winter along with instructions she had pretended not to understand.

*If the humming changes, girl, you read the red ledger. Promise me.*

She found the red ledger by feel. Its spine crackled when she opened it.

It was not a history. It was a *log*. Entries in a dozen different hands across three centuries, terse as weather reports:

*Ward pressure high. Two singers posted at the north stones. Unanswered prayers holding.*

*Third stutter this season. Reinforced the evening round. Cost: acceptable.*

*Cost: acceptable.* Again and again, in margins growing more cramped as the centuries turned. And then, near the end, in Master Aldous's own shaking hand, dated eleven years ago:

*Stutters increasing. Ward thinning faster than the songs can hold it. The bargain requires renewal and there is no one left to renew it. If you are reading this, apprentice, then I am dead or dying and you are the last. Read the early entries. Understand what we bought and what we pay. Do not let them pray.*

The lamp guttered. Vesper sat down on the cold step with the ledger open across her knees and read until her eyes burned.

Here was what the guild had done, written plainly in ink gone brown: they had not built the ward. They had *bargained* for it. Something vast and patient lived beyond the pass—something the earliest entries called only *the Listener*, in handwriting that trembled even four hundred years ago—and the founders of Hollowmere had made a treaty with it. Your valley will be closed to us, the terms ran, in the oldest hand of all. In exchange, the toll is paid in memory, a portion each generation, taken from the singers who maintain the song. And no prayer raised within the stones shall be answered, for answered prayer is a door, and doors open from both sides.

*Toll paid in memory.* Vesper looked up at the dusty choir stalls and understood, suddenly, why the guild had dwindled. Why Aldous sometimes looked at her with blank, searching eyes, as though trying to remember why he'd taken an apprentice at all. Why the guildhall's own records had gaps—whole decades missing, pages razored out, as if the book itself had been paying its share.

The ward ate its keepers' yesterdays. Slowly. Politely. And when the keepers ran out, the ward began to starve.

And a starving wall develops holes. And through holes, things listen. And listening things answer.

She climbed the stairs slowly, the ledger under her arm, and paused at the top where the evening light came through the high windows and lay in gold bars across the silent nave. Somewhere out there, Cobb Hessel's barley drank its impossible rain. Somewhere out there, a thing with a dead woman's face sat patiently in a kitchen, waiting to be loved by a child whose grief had punched a hole in the world.

And somewhere beyond the pass—beyond the ward-stones, beyond the edge of everything safe—something old had noticed that the wall was thin, and had begun, very gently, to knock.

That night the bell rang at dusk as it always did, and Vesper stood in the guildhall doorway and listened to the ward hum, and counted the stutters.

Four.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would sit with Master Aldous and make him tell her everything, while he still had anything left to tell.

But when she reached his room, his bed was empty, and he was standing at the window in the dark, fully dressed, singing under his breath—a melody she had never heard, in a language she didn't know—with tears running silently down his face.

He turned to her, and his eyes were clear for the first time in months, and terribly afraid.

"It's time you learned the rest of the song," he said. "Before it learns ours."
