## 3. What the Bell Remembers

The pain came like weather now, rolling in from the east where my lungs had begun to drown in their own quiet. I had learned to read its moods the way a shepherd reads clouds. This morning's was a gray one—steady, patient, not yet the black squall that would take me. I had perhaps an hour of clarity left. Perhaps two if I was stubborn, and I had always been stubborn.

I lay in the narrow bed behind the guildhall's vestry, in the room where every Master of Hollowmere had died since before the stones were named. The ceiling above me was smoke-darkened oak, and carved into one beam, so high that only a dying man ever thought to look, was a small worn spiral. My master had shown it to me when I lay where Vesper now sat. *We are all of us wound tight around something,* he'd said. *The trick is knowing what.*

Vesper sat in the chair by the window with her hands folded, and she was doing the thing she did best: being nearly invisible. She had drawn the chair close without asking permission. She had brought water I did not ask for. She had opened the shutter exactly three fingers' width, letting in a blade of morning light that fell across the floor and stopped short of my bed, as though even the sun knew to keep its distance from what I was about to say.

She thought she was there to comfort me. Sweet child. She was there to be burdened.

"Vesper," I said, and her name came out of me like a stone pulled from a riverbed, cold and slick and heavier than it looked. "How long have you been able to hear it?"

Her stillness changed quality. That was the thing about Vesper Vane—she could fool the whole valley, but I had spent forty years teaching apprentices to listen, and listening is a language with no dialects. Her silence answered before her mouth did.

"The ward," I said. "You hear it humming."

"I don't—" she began, and then stopped, because lying to a dying man is a door most people can't bring themselves to open. "Since I was small," she said finally. "I thought everyone did. Then I thought no one did, and that I was broken somehow, so I stopped mentioning it."

"And what does it sound like?"

"A hum," she said. "Low. Like a finger run round the rim of a glass, but under the ground, under everything. Sometimes at night, when the bell stops and the valley goes quiet, it almost sounds like words." She hesitated. "It sounds tired, Master Aldous. Lately it sounds tired."

There it was. There was the thing I had been waiting six years to hear confirmed, ever since the last of the other listeners left or died or forgot, and I alone remained, and I began to wonder—with the particular terror of the last speaker of a language—what happens to a song when only one throat remembers it.

"It should sound tired," I said. "It has been singing for nine hundred years."

* * *

I made her help me sit up first. Pride, maybe. Or the simple animal wish to face what comes sitting rather than lying down, the way our ancestors faced wolves. She stacked the pillows with the careful competence she brought to everything, and I noticed—as I had noticed a hundred times and never said—that her hands were a listener's hands. Long-fingered, patient, always half-curled as though holding a note they hadn't been given permission to release.

"The guildhall is silent," I said. "You've noticed that. No lessons. No choir. The other guilds—the smiths, the weavers—they train their children. We dust."

"You said the songs were lost."

"I lied." I let that sit in the room between us. Outside, far off, someone's rooster complained about the hour. "Not entirely. A lie needs a true shape to stand in, and that one had one. Most of the songs *are* lost. But not because we mislaid them, Vesper. Not because fire or flood or neglect took them." I looked at her, and I made myself say it plainly, because plainness was the last gift I had to give. "We gave them away. Deliberately. One generation at a time."

Her brow furrowed, and beneath the furrow I saw the quick working of her mind, the mind I had privately called the finest in the valley for years and told no one, because telling her would have made her visible, and visibility in Hollowmere is a kind of wound.

"I don't understand."

"No. You wouldn't. You were raised inside the answer; you can't see it any more than you can see the air." I coughed, and tasted copper, and waited it out. "Tell me what you know of how the ward was built."

"That the founders sang it into being. That the first guild stood on the ridge and sang for seven days and seven nights, and the ward-stones rose out of the earth where their blood fell, and the valley has been safe ever since." She recited it the way children recite—the flat cadence of a story learned so young it feels less like knowledge than like bone.

"Beautiful," I said. "And false. Every word after 'founders.'" I gripped the blanket with both hands, gathering myself. "The founders couldn't sing a ward. No mortal voice can hold back what presses against this valley—not alone, not together, not for seven days or seventy years. What the founders did was find something that *could*, and bargain with it. And the price of that bargain, Vesper, the price paid every generation since the first, is memory."

She went very still. Stillness was her armor; I had watched her put it on a thousand times. But her eyes had gone bright and fast, moving across my face like a reader racing down a page.

"The ward isn't a wall," I said. "Walls are things. The ward is a *song*, sustained, sung by the stones themselves—and the stones need voices to remember them by. Each generation of the guild gives up its memories of the songs themselves. We teach an apprentice the melody, the apprentice sings it once at the stones, and then—we forget. Not the way old men forget. Completely. Cleanly. As though the song had never lived in us. That is why the guildhall is silent. That is why no record survives. The records were part of the payment. Every page that named the songs was surrendered too, and the ink faded within a season, and the paper crumbled, and no one grieved it because no one remembered there had ever been anything to grieve."

"But you remember," she whispered. "You're telling me."

"I am the last," I said. "And do you know why? Because sixty years ago, when I was younger than you are now, I committed a small act of treachery. When my turn came to surrender the songs, I kept one. Just fragments—I couldn't hold the whole of it, no single mind can—but I hid pieces of it inside other memories, the way you hide a key inside a house. I wrapped verses of it around my mother's face. Around the smell of bread. Around the day it hailed in midsummer. I wove the binding-song through the ordinary fabric of my life so that when the forgetting came for the song, it would have to take the whole cloth."

"And did it?"

"It took the whole cloth," I said quietly. "That is the cruelty of the bargain. It does not accept partial payment. I kept the melody, Vesper, but I lost everything it was woven through. I cannot tell you my mother's face. I cannot tell you the smell of bread. I know these things happened to me the way you know a stranger happened to you—by report, by inference, by the shape of the hole. Sixty years I have carried a song stitched through a life I cannot recall. Do you understand now why I let the guild go silent? Why I taught you nothing? I swore I would not pass this debt to another soul. I meant to let it end with me. Let the ward thin, let the valley learn what lives beyond the pass, let the world come in and be survived. Anything but one more pair of hands carrying this weight."

The light had crawled further across the floor while I spoke. It touched the foot of my bed now, warm as a hand. I found I didn't want it to reach my face. Dying men are allowed their superstitions.

"But," Vesper said.

I smiled. Even now, even hollowed as I was, she could find the hinge in any sentence. "But," I agreed. "Three nights ago, the bell rang wrong."

* * *

Everyone in Hollowmere knows the evening bell. They think it marks the hour. It has never marked the hour. The bell is the oldest instrument in the valley, older than the stones, cast from the same bronze the founders carried over the pass, and each night at dusk it rings thirteen times, and the thirteenth peal is the ward's own voice answering—a call and response, a question and confirmation, *are you there / I am here*, performed nightly for nine hundred years without interruption.

Three nights ago, the thirteenth peal came back a half-tone low.

No one else heard it. Of course no one else heard it. But I heard it, and I got up out of this bed, and I walked to the belfry on legs that had no business walking, and I rang the bell again myself, alone, at midnight, and listened. And the answer came back ragged. Frayed at the edges. A voice that has been singing too long without rest.

Then yesterday, Tam Whitlow's boy prayed for rain, and the rain came—out of a cloudless sky, warm, smelling faintly of something sweet and rotten underneath, like fruit gone past. And the miller's daughter prayed for her mother, dead these four winters, and woke to find a woman in her kitchen wearing her mother's face and getting the details wrong. The mole on the wrong side. The laugh pitched too high. The daughter ran to the shrine-warden, and the shrine-warden sent for me, and I looked at the thing stirring porridge in the miller's kitchen, and I felt the ward's hum through the floorboards, and it sounded, Vesper—it sounded *hungry*. Thin places let thin things through. That is the oldest lesson, and I had hoped never to teach it.

"So the debt comes due," I said. "Not just mine. The whole arrangement. The thing beyond the pass that holds up our sky has grown impatient, or grown hungry, or simply grown careless, and the ward is failing, and the only people who ever knew how to renew it forgot on purpose, generation after generation, until one cowardly old man hid the pieces in his own flesh and lost the life they were hidden in."

Vesper's eyes were wet but she wasn't crying. She was listening. God help me, she was *listening*, the way no one had listened in sixty years, and I understood suddenly that I had not failed to pass on the debt. I had merely delayed. All those silent years, all my fine resolutions, and the valley had gone ahead and produced the one person who could hear the ward without being taught—had grown her, quiet and forgettable and patient, like a seed the guild itself had planted without knowing.

"There is a song," I said. "A binding-song. It renews the bargain. It must be sung at the ward-stones on the ridge, at the turning of the season, by two voices—one to carry the plea, one to carry the price. I have the plea. Half of it. Fragments, wrapped in hailstones and bread-smell and a woman's face I can no longer see. The other half—" and here my voice did the thing dying voices do, thinned, went distant, became mostly breath— "the other half I never found. Perhaps there is no other half. Perhaps the song was always meant to be finished by whoever inherited it. Perhaps that was the design all along, and the forgetting too, and every Master before me stood in this room and realized, too late, that the guild's true work was not keeping the song but keeping the *need* for it alive."

"Master Aldous." Her voice was steady. Steadier than mine. "Teach me what you have."

So I began to sing.

It came out of me cracked and rusted, a voice like a gate in wind, but the melody rose through the ruin of it the way water rises through a broken pipe, finding its old channels. I sang the plea—the long descending line that asks the stones to remember the bargain, that names the founders, that offers the season's turning as surety. And as I sang, the fragments unwove themselves from my stolen memories one last time, and I felt them leave, and I felt—for the first time in sixty years—the spaces where my mother's face had been begin to fill with something, though whether it was grief or peace I could not say, and no longer needed to.

Vesper listened with her whole body. I watched her lips move silently, shaping the intervals. I watched her hands rise slightly off her knees, half-curled, holding.

I was four lines from the end of what I had when the black squall came, early, the way weather breaks its promises. The pain closed over me like dark water. But I had been a singer for sixty years longer than I had been a man, and a singer knows: you do not stop mid-line. You give the phrase its ending even if the ending kills you.

I sang the fourth line. I reached for the fifth—

*and the stones shall keep what we—*

The bell outside rang noon. Thirteen times. I counted, somewhere beneath the drowning, the way I had counted every noon of my life. On the thirteenth peal, the answer came back low again, frayed, hungry.

And Vesper Vane, kneeling at my bedside with her hands full of half a melody, leaned close and finished the line for me.

I never heard how she ended it.
