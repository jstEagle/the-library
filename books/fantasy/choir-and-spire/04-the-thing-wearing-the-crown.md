## 4. The Thing Wearing the Crown

I buried Master Aldous on a Tuesday, in the manner of the guild: no prayer, no marker, just the bell rung backward thirteen times and the grave dug shallow, because the guild had always said the dead of Hollowmere should stay close to the skin of the world. Then I went home to an empty guildhall that smelled of tallow and camphor and him, and I sat in his chair with his half-melody folded in my pocket like a knife I didn't yet know how to hold.

I stayed three days. I told myself it was mourning. It was cowardice wearing mourning's coat.

On the fourth morning I packed what mattered: the ledger of answered prayers, the songbook with its burned pages, bread, a water skin, and the copper bell-clapper Aldous had once pressed into my hands without explanation. I dressed in the gray wool of a traveler instead of the brown of an apprentice, because if I was going to do this thing, I was not going to do it as a ghost.

The ward-stones stood at the western edge of the valley where the road narrowed between two shoulders of hill. I had passed them ten thousand times. I had fetched mushrooms from the slope beyond them as a child, gathered kindling, chased a runaway goat halfway to the pass before Tam-of-the-village—no, that was another life's confusion; the boy who helped me catch her was called Denny—before Denny caught her for me. I had crossed that line more times than I could count.

I had never once noticed the stones hum.

That was the first horror: standing before them and realizing they had been singing all my life, and I had heard them the way you hear your own heartbeat, which is to say never, until something goes wrong. Now, with Aldous's melody lodged in me like a splinter, I could not *not* hear them. They sang the fourth line over and over, worn smooth by centuries of repetition, and beneath the song there was a silence shaped exactly like a fifth line.

A hole in the roof of the world, the size of one unfinished phrase.

I touched the nearest stone. It was warm.

* * *

Crossing was like being born.

I mean that literally—I have no gentler comparison. One step I was Vesper Vane, apprentice of a dead guild, and the next the whole world turned its face toward me and began to speak at once.

Magic did not come back to my senses. It *flooded* them. The ward hadn't kept magic out of Hollowmere; it had kept me from perceiving it, and perception, once opened, cannot be politely closed. Light arrived with texture. The wind had opinions. Every living thing within sight announced itself—the grass in green murmurs, the hawks overhead in thin silver screams—and underneath all of it ran the deep current of the land itself, vast and slow and utterly indifferent to me, a river of meaning so large that my mind skated across its surface like a dropped stone.

I fell to my knees on the far side of the stones and was sick into the bracken.

When I could stand, I understood two things. First, that every guildsinger before me had learned to bear this, which meant learning to bear it was possible. Second, that Master Aldous had spent forty years carrying this weight inside a failing body while pretending to be a tired old man ringing a bell, and I would never be able to hate him again for how slowly he climbed stairs.

I walked toward the pass with my senses screaming, and the terrible thing—the thing I will not pretend I didn't feel—was that it was beautiful.

I had been told, in the way valleys tell children these things, that beyond the ward the world was dangerous, fallen, wrong. No one had said it was *gorgeous*. The light through the high pines came down in visible shafts, thick as honey, each one threaded with motes that were not dust but something older, drifting with purpose. A stream argued with itself in three-part harmony. When a fox crossed the road ahead of me it left a brief afterimage of amber script hanging in the air, some record the world insisted on keeping, and I stood watching it fade with tears running freely down my face, grieving for all the years I had lived in a muted world and called the silence peace.

The wider world was more beautiful than I had imagined. That was the trap of it. I could see now why someone might open a door to a god, if the god knocked beautifully enough.

By dusk I reached Cairnlow.

* * *

Cairnlow was the border town, the last settlement before the king's road climbed into the heartlands, and it had the look of a place that had stopped believing in tomorrow. Not ruined—ruin takes violence. This was worse. Cairnlow was *tidy*. Streets swept. Shutters mended. And everywhere, everywhere, people smiling.

Not the smiles of the contented. I knew contentment; I'd grown up among farmers whose faces knew how to rest. These smiles were held. Fixed. Faces arranged around an absence the way a glove is arranged around a missing hand. In the tavern yard a woman laughed at nothing, the laugh rising and cutting off at exactly the same length as the laugh before it, and I realized I'd been listening to her for a full minute and she had laughed eleven times, identically, like a bell struck by something patient.

I pulled my hood up and found a corner in the taproom and listened, because listening was the only craft I had.

They talked, the people of Cairnlow. That was the second horror: they weren't mute or vacant. They spoke of harvests and prices and a wedding in spring. But their talk had no roots. Ask a man about his father and he'd smile and say he'd been a good man, and his eyes would slide off the memory like rain off slate. Ask a woman where she'd learned her weaving and she'd say she'd always known, always, always. The past lay over this town like snow, smooth and featureless, and no one thought to wonder what it covered.

And their prayers—gods, their prayers. I heard them now, with the ward no longer muffling me. Every soul in Cairnlow leaked want the way a bad roof leaks rain, little bright threads rising from the town into the darkening sky. *Let the calf live. Let her love me back. Let me sleep without dreaming.* And above the town, coiling down to meet those threads like smoke drawn backward into a chimney, came—

answers.

I saw one arrive. I will describe it as well as I can, though language was built for a world where this doesn't happen. A thread of wanting rose from a shuttered window—a man praying, I think, for his sick daughter—and out of the evening sky something leaned down along that thread. It had no shape I could fix on. My eyes wanted to call it tall and my skin wanted to call it cold and my memory, afterward, refused entirely. It touched the prayer the way a finger touches a fly on syrup.

Then it left something behind, and the something slipped under the door, and inside the house a child's voice cried out—not in fear, in *joy*—and a woman's voice answered, warm and wrong, a voice saying all the right words in an order slightly too perfect, like a song learned from hearing it sung once through a wall.

The answered prayers walked around Cairnlow in their mothers' faces and their brothers' faces, doing kindnesses, curing fevers, mending roofs. The town loved them. That was the worst part. The town was *grateful*.

I did not sleep. I sat in the corner of the taproom with my back to the wall and watched the door all night, and near dawn, when the sky over the eastern hills turned the color of a healing bruise, the king came.

* * *

He came up the king's road with a company of twelve riders, and even at that distance—even a quarter mile off—I felt him before I saw him, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. Every prayer-thread in Cairnlow bent toward him at once, gently, like grass leaning into wind. He was not drawing the threads. He was simply the direction they grew.

He rode a white horse that did not sweat.

I want to write that he was beautiful, because he was, in the way a cathedral is beautiful: immense, proportioned, designed. Tall beyond any natural man, crowned, robed in a blue so deep it drank the dawn. He raised one hand to the townsfolk gathering in the street and they wept with joy, all of them, simultaneously, a field of wheat bowing.

And he smiled at them.

I have seen smiles all my life. I know the machinery of them—the eyes, the mouth, the small honest delays. His smile had no delays. It arrived fully formed, wide and warm, and it did not move. It did not deepen or fade. It was painted on the moment and hung there, and behind it his eyes performed the same trick: bright, benevolent, and absolutely still, two windows lit in a house where no fire burns.

Then he turned his head to scan the crowd, and I saw the wrongness entire. The turn was too smooth. No weight shifted in him; the horse's gait never registered in his spine. He moved like a puppet viewed through water, like something remembering, imperfectly, how bodies work. For a heartbeat his head went too far—a degree past the neck's honest limit—and corrected, and the smile never wavered, and I understood that whatever wore the king understood smiles the way I understood the word "ocean": completely, and having never touched one.

He was hollow. Aldous had told me true. Something vast was reaching down through that crown the way a hand reaches into a glove, and the kingdom below the pass had not been conquered. It had been *worn*.

At the edge of the square, apart from the weeping crowds, stood others. I counted seven. They looked like pilgrims, hooded and plain, but they cast no afterimages—no amber script, no record, nothing. The world declined to remember them even as they stood in it. Answered-prayer creatures, then, the god's own household, attending their king the way priests attend a relic. As I watched, one of them turned its hooded face toward me.

Across a quarter mile of dawn-lit street, through a crowd of hundreds, it looked directly at the corner where I sat behind my hood, and it tilted its head, curious, the way a child tilts at a locked box.

I did not wait to learn what happened next. I left coin on the table for bread I hadn't eaten and went out the back, through the yard, over a fence, my new-opened senses shrieking with every touch of the world, and I did not stop until the town was behind me and the road forked—east toward the capital, north into wild country.

I leaned against a milestone, shaking, and made myself say it aloud, because saying things aloud was the guild's oldest discipline and I needed a discipline badly.

"The king is a glove." My voice cracked on the second word. "The god is the hand. And the song needs two voices, and I don't have the fifth line, and I don't have a second voice, and I am one forgettable girl standing between a valley of strangers and a thing that eats yesterday."

The milestone said nothing. Milestones are honest that way.

But as I stood there, deciding—which way, east or north, flight or folly—I heard hoofbeats on the road behind me. One rider, moving fast, not in the smooth gliding wrongness of the court but in the ordinary jarring rhythm of a frightened man on a tired horse.

He reined in beside the milestone, and he was gaunt and ink-stained and looking over his shoulder more often than forward, and when he saw me his eyes went wide—not with desire or suspicion, but with recognition, sudden and total.

"You," he said. "You're a singer. I can see it—you're *humming*, girl, you've been humming since Cairnlow and you don't even know it." He fumbled at his coat and drew out a leather tube, a scroll case, clutched it like scripture. "My name is Tam. I was the royal archivist, before I read what I wasn't supposed to read. And I think you and I need to compare notes about a lullaby."

I stared at him. He stared back. Somewhere far to the east, beneath a crown, something ancient turned its borrowed head.

"Start talking," I said.
