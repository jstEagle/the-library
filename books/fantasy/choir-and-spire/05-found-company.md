## 5. Found Company

Vesper had been walking for three days when she realized someone was following her.

She'd left Cairnlow before dawn, slipping out through the sheep-gate with the half-melody written on a scrap of paper folded against her ribs. The road north climbed through country that made her teeth ache—magic soaked into everything here, thick as fog after rain. Every hedgerow hummed. Every stream sang a note just slightly wrong. She had learned to keep her hood up and her eyes down, because looking too long at anything let it look back.

The follower was not subtle. She'd caught the sound of him twice yesterday: boots too heavy for the soft ground, a cough he tried to swallow. This morning there were footprints in the frost beside hers whenever the path narrowed, and once, when she stopped abruptly behind a windbreak of hawthorn, she heard him stop too—too late—and then swear under his breath in the particular exhausted way of a man who has been sleeping badly for a long time.

She could have kept walking. She was good at being unremarkable; it was practically her craft. But the half-melody sat against her ribs like a stone, and Aldous's last words sat behind it: *It takes two voices, girl. It was always meant to take two.* And somewhere between Cairnlow and this frozen hillside, she had begun to understand that she could not carry the song alone—not because her voice was small, but because the melody itself had a gap in it, a silence shaped exactly like another person.

So instead of walking on, she stepped off the road, put her back against an oak, and said to the treeline, "You can come out now or you can spend another night freezing in the bracken. I don't care which."

A long pause. Then, from a stand of hazels twenty yards off, a man rose up like something reluctantly resurrected—tall, thin, wrapped in a clerk's coat gone green at the elbows, with ink-stained fingers and a face that looked like it had been designed for reading by candlelight and was suffering outdoors on principle. He had a satchel slung across his chest and a crossbow he was holding entirely wrong.

"You knew," he said. His voice was hoarse. "Since when?"

"Since yesterday morning. You sneeze when you're nervous."

"I do not—" He sneezed.

Vesper waited.

The man sighed, pushed through the bracken, and stood in the road with his arms slightly out, the way people stand when they want you to see they're unarmed even though they are visibly holding a weapon. "Tam," he said. "Tam Wrenlow. Formerly second archivist to the Royal Collection at Dunmarrow Hall. Currently"—he gestured at the wilderness around them—"this."

"Vesper Vane." She studied him. Up close he looked worse than from a distance: shadows under his eyes like bruises, cheeks hollowed, a healing cut along his jaw. A man running from something, and running badly. "Why are you following me?"

"Because you walked into Cairnlow asking questions about answered prayers, and then you stood in the market square for an hour staring at the king's procession with your mouth open like you'd never seen a man ride past before—which, incidentally, nobody else did, which is its own horror—and then you left town the moment he'd passed, heading north toward the old shrine roads." He swallowed. "I've been alone with what I know for four months, and every night I tell myself I imagined it, and every morning I see the king smile again and know I didn't. And then you arrive, looking at him like *that*, and I think: either she's mad, or she knows. And if she knows—"

"I know," Vesper said quietly.

The relief that crossed Tam's face was so naked it embarrassed them both. He turned away, busying himself with his satchel strap. "Right," he said. "Right. Good. Well." He cleared his throat. "There's a shrine about two miles on with a roof that mostly holds. We shouldn't talk on the open road."

*We*. He'd said it without noticing, and Vesper noticed herself not minding, and she hated that a little—the ease of it, how quickly the word fit. She had spent her whole life being forgettable, invisible, safe inside her own silence. Companionship was a door, and doors swing both ways.

She followed him anyway.

* * *

Tam's POV:

The shrine had been a waystation for pilgrims once, back when pilgrims still came this way. Now it was a stone shell with a collapsed eastern wall and an altar some traveler had been using as a table. Tam got a fire going with the efficiency of long practice while the girl—Vesper—stood in the doorway watching the dark come down over the hills like she expected something to walk out of it.

He found himself talking more than he meant to. That was the trouble with solitude; it made the dam weak.

"I was the one who catalogued the royal correspondence," he said, turning a flatbread on a stone near the fire. "Eleven years. You learn a king's hand the way you learn a friend's face. Three years ago the letters started changing. Not the words—the words were fine, better than fine, the king's diction improved dramatically—but the *pressure*. The way the pen moved. And the seals were right but the wax was poured left-handed." He shrugged, helpless. "Small things. Stupid things. I told myself it was a new secretary. Then famine year came, and the granaries filled overnight, and the prayers started getting answered—actual answers, actual miracles, whole harvests appearing where blight had been—and the court wept with joy, and I sat in my archive with a stack of correspondence and thought: *who signed these? Who is signing these?*"

"And?" Vesper asked.

"So I did something no sensible archivist does. I pulled the private records. Medical journals. Confessor's notes. And eleven months ago I found a physician's entry, dated the week the miracles began. It describes an audience with His Majesty in which the king failed to recognize his own daughter, then recovered, laughed, embraced her—and afterward the physician wrote, in Latin so the servants wouldn't understand: *"I have served three kings and buried two. This man is a coat that someone else is wearing.""*

The fire cracked. Vesper had gone very still, her face lit orange on one side.

"I took the entry and ran," Tam said. "Which makes me a coward and a traitor, depending on whose gallows you ask. I've been drifting since. Watching. Counting." He met her eyes. "How many of you are there? The guild. The ward-singers. How many left who can see what I saw?"

"One," Vesper said.

The word dropped between them like a coin down a well. Tam felt it land in his own chest, because he knew—he'd known for months—that he was the only one in the capital who'd seen it too. Two people in all the world carrying the same terrible knowledge. It wasn't company, exactly. It was more like two survivors of the same shipwreck washing up on the same beach.

"Show me what you have," he said. "You came north for a reason. Nobody walks into that market square and stares at the king like they're memorizing him unless they've got a reason."

Vesper hesitated. He watched her weigh it—watched the instinct to hide war with something newer and hungrier. Then she reached into her coat and unfolded a scrap of paper, worn soft at the creases, and handed it across the fire.

On it, in a shaking hand that had clearly belonged to a dying man, was a line of music. Notation above, and below it words in a language Tam didn't know, transcribed phonetically.

"What is it?" he asked.

"The guild's song. My master died singing it. He only got halfway." Her voice was carefully flat. "I hoped—I don't know what I hoped. That there'd be a copy somewhere. An archive. Anything."

Tam stared at the notation, and his skin began to crawl, because he *did* know it. Not the language. The structure. He'd spent eleven years among the oldest documents in the kingdom, and some of those documents predated the kingdom—contracts, bindings, covenants, things the founders had locked away in lead-lined cases and labeled *do not read aloud.*

"This isn't a hymn," he said slowly. "Look at the structure. Call line, response line, call line—but the response lines are empty. Not missing. *Empty.* Scored for a second voice that was never written down, because—" he stopped, heart hammering.

"Because what?"

"Because the second part was never supposed to be written. Binding songs aren't performed, they're *enacted*. The second voice learns by answering. You sing the call, and whoever stands with you sings back whatever the binding requires of *them*—it's different every time, it's shaped by what they offer. That's why there's nothing on the page. The page only holds half the ritual because the other half is a person." He looked up. "This is a binding song, Vesper. A two-voice binding. Your master didn't die mid-hymn. He died mid-*contract*, and he left you holding the signature end."

Vesper's POV:

They worked through the night, and Vesper discovered something worse than dread: she discovered she was *good* at this—with him.

It happened without her permission. He'd spread six more pages across the altar floor, pages he'd stolen from the archive in his flight—fragments of covenant law, marginalia, a partial glossary of the old tongue—and suddenly the two of them were leaning over the scraps together, shoulders almost touching, finishing each other's deductions. He knew the grammar of power; she knew the sound of it. When he translated a phrase, she heard how it wanted to be sung. When she hummed a passage, he'd go still and say, "Again—slower—there, right *there*, that word means *anchor*, I'm certain of it," and she'd feel the melody settle into place like a bone resetting.

By the grey hours before dawn they had it. Or nearly.

"It's a cage-song," Tam said, rubbing his eyes. His voice had gone rough with exhaustion and something like awe. "Woven to hold something that feeds. The call lines define the prison. The answer lines..." He trailed off.

"Are the lock," Vesper finished. "And the lock is made of what the second voice gives up." She'd understood it hours ago, in the place below thinking where melodies live. The empty staves weren't waiting for notes. They were waiting for *weight*. Memories, probably. Offerings. The song needed fuel the way a fire needs wood.

"That's why the ward works," Tam murmured. "That's why Hollowmere stays hidden. Someone paid. Generations of someones."

Vesper said nothing. Aldous had told her the price was paid each generation. She hadn't yet asked herself what her own payment would be, and she was careful not to ask now, here, with this stranger-turned-ally watching her with kind, tired eyes.

Instead she said, "You should sleep."

"So should you."

"One of us should."

They argued about it briefly, pointlessly, and ended up sharing the watch in shifts, and it was during her shift—sitting by the low fire while Tam slept curled in his clerk's coat like a comma—that Vesper admitted the thing she'd been refusing to admit since the hazel thicket: that she had spent three days alone on the road telling herself she preferred it, and had believed it right up until tonight. Until someone said *we* and meant it. Until someone looked at her knowledge and called it valuable instead of strange.

Companionship was a door, and doors swing both ways, and she was already standing in the doorway and could feel the draft.

She hated how much she needed it. She hated more that she wasn't going to give it up.

Near dawn, Tam woke with a gasp, sat bolt upright, and stared at the wall of the shrine as if the stones had spoken.

"What?" Vesper said. "What is it?"

"The glossary," he breathed. "The entry I couldn't place—the word that keeps recurring in every covenant fragment, the one I translated as 'god' but the usage never quite fits—" He grabbed his satchel, hands shaking, and began digging through it. "It doesn't mean god. It means *guest*. Vesper—it means *invited guest*."

He looked up at her, and the firelight made his face into something out of an older, crueler story.

"Somebody opened the door. Somebody asked it in."
