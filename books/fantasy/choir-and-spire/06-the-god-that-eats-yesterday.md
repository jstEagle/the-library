## 6. The God That Eats Yesterday

The archive beneath the Chantry of Saint Vellum had not been opened in forty years, and it smelled like it: dust and tallow, old leather gone soft as skin, and beneath it all a faint sweetness Tam could not name until he realized it was rot—paper rot, the slow sugar-death of ink. He lit three candles because two felt insufficient to what he was about to do, and one felt like an apology.

Tam Wren had been the royal archivist of Cairnlow for eleven years before he fled it. Eleven years of cataloguing decrees and harvest ledgers, of restoring water-damaged genealogies for nobles who wanted their lineages older than they were. He had been good at his work. He had been *proud* of his work. And then, eight months ago, he had pulled a box of coronation records from the reign of King Aldric the Second—records that should have filled four boxes—and found them nearly empty.

Not lost. Not damaged. *Excised.* Pages cut away with a razor so fine the remaining stubs gleamed like teeth. The inventory lists still existed, cataloguing documents that no longer were. Someone had wanted the absence itself to be invisible.

He had taken the inventory with him when he ran, along with a copy of the succession rolls, and he had spent eight months telling himself he was a coward. Now, with Vesper asleep upstairs in the chantry's guest cell—she slept like someone who had never once been able to afford to sleep deeply—he went back down into the dark to find out what kind of coward.

*If you want to know what was taken,* he told himself, lighting the third candle, *find out who did the taking.*

* * *

The trick of research, Tam had learned, was not reading everything. It was reading *around* everything. The excised pages left shadows. A decree dated the third of Thawmonth referenced "the terms agreed upon at the winter court" — but there was no record of any winter court. A grain ledger from the famine year showed imports arriving from a source listed only as "per arrangement." A bishop's letter thanked the king for "your wisdom in the matter we discussed, which shall not be written."

Fourteen references to something unwritten, scattered across thirty years of records like holes in cloth. Tam laid them out on a reading table in chronological order and stared at them until the candle flames doubled in his tired eyes.

The famine year was the hinge. He had lived through its tail end as a boy—the bread queues, the winter they buried his grandmother, the spring when the king rode out to the villages and people wept and called him blessed. Everyone remembered the king ending the famine. That was the story. That was the *only* story; Tam had never heard another version, not whispered, not drunk, not in forty years of gossip in a capital city that ran on gossip.

That was wrong. That was *architecturally* wrong. Every other event in Cairnlow's history had at least two versions and usually nine. But the end of the famine had exactly one, polished smooth as a river stone, repeated identically by everyone.

Identically. Tam sat down slowly on the reading stool. People did not remember things identically. Memory varied the way handwriting varied. Unless—

Unless the memory had not grown naturally. Unless it had been *installed.*

His hands were shaking when he reached for the oldest box, the one he'd been avoiding since midnight: the personal papers of Archivist Corvel, his predecessor's predecessor, the man whose marginalia Tam had grown up respecting like scripture. Corvel had been meticulous, skeptical, and—according to the official record—retired suddenly to a coastal monastery, where he died peacefully two years later.

Corvel's last notebook was bound in grey calfskin, unmarked. Tam broke its seal with a knife he'd bought for cutting apples.

*They tell me I am ill,* the first entry read, in a hand shakier than Corvel's famous script. *They tell me my memory fails. I say: my memory is the only thing in this palace that has not changed.*

* * *

He read until the candles burned down to stubs, and then he lit more, and the night outside the little window went from black to bruise to pale iron while Tam Wren learned what his kingdom had done.

Corvel had noticed it first in the small things. Servants who could not recall their own mothers' faces. A chamberlain who introduced himself, twice in one hour, as though meeting Tam for the first time. Courtiers with beautiful, empty smiles and no childhoods—they spoke of their pasts the way actors speak of roles, fluently and without a single specific detail. No scraped knees. No dead dogs. No first kisses under bad weather.

And the king. Corvel had served three monarchs. He wrote of Aldric the Second the way a man writes about watching a house burn from inside it: *He remembers nothing before the famine year. Nothing. Not his coronation, not his wife, not the daughter who died of the fever. When I brought him her portrait he looked at it with courtesy, as at a stranger's child. And yet he is serene. He is the most contented man I have ever met. Contentment without memory is not peace. It is the absence of anything to be unpeaceful about.*

Then, near the end, the entries turned frantic, scrawled sideways up the margins:

*I found it. In the sealed chapel under the east tower—a text older than the dynasty, copied by the first guild-singers before they went into exile. It names the thing. OUROD. The Eater of Yesterday. It does not devour flesh or souls. It eats MEMORY—the past itself, the having-been. A mind fed upon becomes a vessel. An obedient, grateful, smiling vessel.*

*It cannot take what does not exist. That is why the valley wards hold. Hollowmere was made deliberately plain—the guild sang the ward to keep magic out, yes, but also to keep the valley POOR in enchantment, thin in wonder, forgettable even to itself. A meal with no scent. Ourod starves at the ward-stones because there is nothing inside worth eating.*

*God forgive me—I understand now why the famine ended. The king did not pray for rain. He prayed for something that answers. And it answered. It took the kingdom's past as its fee—every hard year, every grief, every debt of gratitude owed—and in exchange the fields bloomed and the people forgot they had ever been hungry, forgot they had ever been afraid, forgot the price.*

*They are not enslaved. That is the horror. They are HAPPY. They would not thank you for rescuing them. What do you rescue a man from, when you would have to give him back his dead daughter, his starving winters, his shame?*

*Tomorrow I will go to the monastery. If I am quiet, perhaps it will let me keep enough of myself to die as Corvel.*

The last entry was a single line, pressed so hard the nib had torn the page:

*IT WAS A RATIONAL CHOICE. THAT IS THE WORST PART. I WOULD HAVE MADE IT TOO.*

* * *

Tam climbed the stairs on legs that belonged to someone else and stood a long time in the doorway of Vesper's cell, watching her sleep. She had insisted on taking the cot nearest the door—"old guild habit," she said, though she claimed to remember almost nothing of guild practice—and she slept with one hand curled around the strap of her satchel the way other people held hands.

He should wake her. This was hers as much as his. The half-melody Aldous had died singing was folded in his coat pocket, and last night, humming it against Corvel's transcription of the binding song, they had matched—two halves of one throat, a song built for two voices, a lock built for two keys.

But he didn't wake her. Some knowledge you had to carry alone first, to see if your legs would hold.

Because here was the thing that kept him standing in that cold doorway while the grey light came up over Cairnlow's rooftops: Corvel was right. It *had* been rational. Tam had run the arithmetic himself, sitting among the candle stubs. One kingdom's past, weighed against ten thousand lives during the famine. Every historian he had ever admired would have signed the bargain. He might have drafted the contract himself, in his neat archivist's hand, and gone home satisfied.

Ourod had not invaded. Ourod had been *invited*, by a desperate king making the only merciful choice available to him, and the god had honored its side of the bargain perfectly. The fields bloomed. The famine ended. The people smiled.

And now the god was hungry again—it had eaten the kingdom's yesterday and found it sweet, and sweet things make appetites—and it was reaching past the border for more. The answered prayers. The thing wearing a dead mother's face in Hollowmere. The thinning ward. All of it was appetite. All of it was a guest who had finished the meal and begun looking at the furniture.

Vesper stirred and opened her eyes, and for a moment, before she was fully awake, she looked at him with total trust—an expression he suspected she would deny ever wearing—and something in Tam's chest hurt in a way he decided not to examine.

"You've been down there all night," she said, voice rough with sleep. Then she saw his face and sat up fast. "What did you find?"

"Everything." His voice came out strange. He cleared his throat. "I found everything, and I wish to God I hadn't." He sat on the edge of the washstand chair and told her all of it—Ourod, the bargain, the excised pages, Corvel's last scrawled confession—and she listened the way she seemed to listen to everything, completely, as though the words were being stored somewhere load-bearing.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Then: "The half-melody. Aldous's song. You said it matches the binding text."

"It does. Two voices. A lock and a key." He hesitated. "There's more. The binding requires an anchor—memory, offered freely. That's how you bind a thing that eats memory. You feed it a meal so heavy it cannot move." He watched her face. "Vesper, the anchor has to come from a singer untouched by magic. From the valley. From you."

She looked at her own hands for a long time—those capable, forgettable hands—and Tam braced for fear, or refusal, or the practical questions he knew she'd ask eventually.

Instead she said, quietly: "Then we'd better make sure it's worth eating. Tell me about the king. If part of him is still in there—if he's fighting—we need to know how to reach him before we're anywhere near that throne."

Tam blinked. Of all the responses he had imagined, he hadn't imagined *mercy*, not aimed at the man who had sold them all.

"There are stories," he said slowly. "Court stories. Things the hollowed nobles sometimes say—fragments. Children's rhymes they shouldn't know. Corvel thought the deepest memories sink farthest and surface last." He paused. "There's one rhyme. A counting rhyme about the bell in Hollowmere. The court children sing it and nobody knows where it came from."

Vesper had gone very still.

"What rhyme?" she asked.

Tam sang it. Four lines, nonsense words, a tune that snagged in the ear like a fishhook—and he watched Vesper's face change as she recognized it, watched her go white around the lips, watched her whisper the fifth line, the one the court children had never known, the one that wasn't in any version sung south of the pass:

"The bell remembers what the singer forgets."

"That's not a children's rhyme," she said. "That's the guild. That's the *ward-song*. Someone in that palace is singing our music—and if the king's buried self is humming the binding melody, then he isn't just a prisoner." She stood, slinging her satchel across her chest, eyes bright and terrified and certain all at once. "He's a choir member waiting for his cue. We have to get to the capital, Tam. Before Ourod finishes its meal—and finds us on the menu."
