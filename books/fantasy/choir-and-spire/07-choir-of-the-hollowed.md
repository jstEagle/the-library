## 7. Choir of the Hollowed

They took her at the ford.

Vesper had known, in the way one knows a storm is coming—by the taste of the air, by the hush of birds—that Tam's plan to slip past the capital's outer villages would fail. The roads were too watched. And so when the riders emerged from the mist like ink blooming through water, she did not run. Running would have meant leaving Tam behind, and she found, to her own quiet horror, that this was no longer an option she possessed.

The riders wore no livery. That was the first wrongness. A king's men should have worn his colors; these wore only gray, gray cloaks, gray hoods, and beneath each hood a face that was smooth as a river stone—not young, not old, just *smooth*, as though memory itself had been sanded off them along with their expressions. Their horses made no sound on the stones.

"Vesper Vane," said the foremost rider, and its voice was pleasant, and it was the most terrible thing she had heard since the thing with her neighbor's face had smiled at her over the fence. "You are expected."

"I'm expected," she repeated. Her mouth was dry. "By whom?"

"By everyone," it said, and smiled, and the smile fit its face the way a glove fits a hand: perfectly, and with nothing inside.

* * *

The capital of Cairnlow was beautiful the way a corpse arranged for viewing is beautiful.

She saw it first from the crest of the last hill, riding bound at the wrists—not cruelly bound, nothing so crude as pain, merely bound, as one binds a book—and the city spread below her in the gold of late afternoon: white walls, slate roofs, the great spike of the palace rising from its heart like a needle through fabric. Banners hung motionless in a windless sky. The streets, even at market hour, were full and *quiet*.

No shouting vendors. No children shrieking through the alleys. No argument between a fishwife and a customer over the freshness of the catch. She realized, as they clattered down the cobbled avenue, that she could hear individual footsteps, individual coughs, because no one spoke above a murmur, and no one hurried, and no one lingered either. People moved through the streets like water finding its level: smooth, purposeless, serene.

"They're happy," Tam whispered beside her. He had been taken too, of course—they hadn't even pretended he might escape. "That's what I couldn't make anyone understand, back before I ran. They're not miserable. They're not suffering. They've been *tidied*."

A woman stood at a fruit stall, arranging apples into a perfect spiral. Vesper caught her eye as they passed. The woman smiled—a warm, genuine smile—and Vesper felt her stomach drop, because there was nothing behind the smile but the smile. It was like looking into a lit window of a house where no one lived.

"What did they lose?" Vesper asked quietly.

"Everything that hurt," Tam said. "And everything that didn't." His voice cracked on the last words. He was watching the woman with an expression Vesper had never seen him wear—not the archivist's cool appraisal, not the fugitive's flinch. Something rawer. "I had a sister here, once. Maren. When I came to say goodbye before I fled, she looked at me and asked why I was crying. She remembered my name. Nothing else. Not the winter we shared a bed during the fever years. Not how our father—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Ourod doesn't eat cruelty, Vesper. It eats *yesterday*. All of it. The grief and the joy, together, because together is the only way they exist."

* * *

The palace received them without ceremony, which was somehow worse than chains.

Guards—stone-faced, stone-voiced—took her satchel, and Vesper's heart lurched as the half-melody, Aldous's dying gift, written in her own careful hand, vanished into a gray sleeve. They walked her down corridors of pale marble where servants glided like fish through clear water, and every servant paused to look at her, and every look held the same mild, vacant curiosity: *what a strange thing, to be so full.*

Because that was what she felt like among them—an overfull cup among empty ones. She had spent her whole life being forgettable, cultivating invisibility like a gardener cultivates moss, and now her memories pressed against her skin so loudly she was sure they could see them: the bell tower at dusk, Master Aldous's cough, the smell of tallow and old paper, Tam's terrible cooking, the ward-hum under everything. Every step deeper into the palace, she clutched them tighter, silently, the way you clutch a child's hand in a crowd.

They gave her a room. A lovely room, with a window overlooking a garden where gardeners moved among roses that had no thorns. There was even supper: bread, cheese, wine, laid out as if for an honored guest.

"You will dine with His Majesty tomorrow evening," the smooth-faced steward told her. "He so looks forward to meeting new friends."

"And until then?"

The steward tilted its head, considering the question as though it were a sum slightly beyond it. "Until then," it said gently, "you may be anything you like."

* * *

She did not sleep. Toward the small hours, she heard the singing.

It drifted up through the floor like smoke: a choir of voices, dozens of them, blended so seamlessly it might have been one voice multiplied. They sang in the throne hall below—or somewhere below—and the song had no words she knew, only long vowel tones that rose and fell like breathing. But underneath the vowels, woven through them, ran a melody.

Vesper sat bolt upright in bed, blood turning to ice water.

She knew the melody. It was the ward-song. *Their* song—the guild's song, the one that had been sung into the valley stones generations ago—warped, slowed, inverted in places, sung *wrong* the way a reflection is wrong. Ourod wasn't just eating the kingdom's memories. It was making the hollowed court sing the binding song backward. A counter-ward. A leash made of music, holding the god's feast in place while it dined at leisure.

And somewhere in that choir—

Vesper crept to the door. Unlocked. Of course unlocked; where would she run? She followed the corridor down, down, past sleeping doors, following the sound to a gallery balcony above the throne hall, and looked down through moonlit dark at the court of Cairnlow assembled in rows, singing.

The king stood among them.

At this distance he might have been any tall figure in a crown, swaying gently with the music. But the crown sat crooked, and his hands—his hands were fists, knuckles white, and they did not sway. Everything about him swayed except his hands, two small islands of resistance in a body given over to the tide, and Vesper understood with a jolt that went through her like a spear: *he was fighting.* Some buried fragment of him, some cell of himself too deep for even a memory-eating god to reach, was fighting the song from inside his own throat.

Then his head turned. Slowly. Unnaturally slowly, like a man wading against a current. And he looked up at her across the moonlit dark of the throne hall.

His eyes were wrong—clouded, filmed, the eyes of the returned mother-thing at the fence. But behind the film something moved, frantic, like a moth beating against glass. His lips moved. No sound came out at first, and then, threaded under the choir's drone, so faint she had to read it more than hear it:

*Ring the bell, ring the bell,
the valley keeps what none can tell.
One voice low and one voice high—*

He faltered. His jaw clenched. Somewhere deep in him, something seized the words and dragged them under, and his face smoothed into placid contentment, and he turned back to the choir, and his hands unclenched, and he sang.

Vesper stood gripping the balcony rail until her fingers ached.

*Aldous's rhyme.* The nonsense verse every Hollowmere child learned with the evening bell—except it wasn't nonsense, it had never been nonsense, it was the binding song broken into pieces small enough for children to carry without knowing what they carried. And the king of Cairnlow knew it. Knew it the way she knew it: *from inside the ward, from childhood, from before.*

Which meant the king had once been a child of Hollowmere.

She stood in the dark a long time, listening to the inverted ward-song rise around her, and felt pity arrive first—pity for the man whose hands still made fists—and then, hardening beneath it like frost forming on a window, resolve. Ourod had not conquered this kingdom. It had been *invited*, fed, housed—and the king who had opened the door was now locked in the cellar of his own body, humming the way out through a nursery rhyme.

The enemy was also a prisoner. And prisoners, Aldous had taught her, could be freed—if you were willing to pay what freedom cost.

Tomorrow she would dine with the god that ate yesterday. Tomorrow she would need Tam, the stolen melody, and a plan stitched from almost nothing.

Tonight she stood alone in the gallery and finished the rhyme under her breath, both voices, hers and the ghost of a dead man's:

*"One voice low and one voice high—
sing the sleeper lullaby.
What was given comes again:
the bell remembers. Amen."*

Below her, unhurried, the choir sang on—and far off in the dark of the palace, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep, and began, softly, to listen.
