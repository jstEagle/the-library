## 8. Counterweight

The bells of Cairnlow were still ringing the alarm when Tam pulled Vesper through the drain-gate beneath the archive's east wall, and he would remember that sound for the rest of his life, however long or short it proved to be: bronze and iron snarling over the rooftops, the city discovering in real time that one apprentice had slipped its throat.

He had done this before. Fled, that was. He was, if nothing else, an expert on running. But he had never fled *with* anyone, and it changed everything—the pace, the weight, the terrible arithmetic of a companion who could not climb as fast as he could, whose breathing he now counted in the dark the way he'd once counted catalogued volumes.

"Left," he hissed at the fork in the culvert, and she went left without question, which frightened him more than the bells did. She trusted him. He had not been trusted by anything except a filing system in eleven years.

They came up out of the drainage channel two miles downstream, in a stand of birches with their roots combed white through the mud, and Vesper doubled over, hands on knees, and laughed—a ragged, disbelieving sound.

"We got out," she said. "I sang in front of the throne of Ourod and we *got out*."

"Don't say that name out loud," Tam said. "Not yet. Not until we're somewhere it can't hear."

"It hears everything."

"Yes." He looked back toward the distant smudge of the capital, the spire where a hollowed king sat smiling with a smile like a door left open. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

The safehouse was Tam's own, though he had never thought of it that way until tonight: a fire-watcher's hut on the shoulder of Mount Ceren, three days' walk from anywhere, stocked with stolen books and one bed, one chair, and a floor. He gave her the bed. She argued. He won by pretending not to hear the argument, which was, he reflected, the first useful thing his years as an archivist had taught him about people.

For two days they did almost nothing but sleep and eat smoked trout and let their hands stop shaking. On the third day, Tam spread the guild records across the floor—what remained of them, the pages he'd lifted from the royal archive fifteen years ago when he first understood what the king was, the pages Vesper had carried out of Hollowmere wrapped in oilcloth against her heart—and began, for the hundredth time, to read them properly.

"Say it again," Vesper said. She sat cross-legged opposite him, her half-melody scratched in charcoal on a board between them. "From the top. What does the song actually do?"

"It doesn't kill him." Tam turned a page. The old guild-hand was cramped and water-stained; some of it he could only guess at. "Ourod. You can't kill a hunger. Hunger isn't alive enough to die. The song is a binding—an *unbinding*, really. It unpicks the knot that ties the god to the king, and once it's untied, the valley's ward can push the god back past the stones. Back into the thin country where it belongs."

"And the price."

"You keep going straight at it, don't you."

"I watched a woman wear my mother's face wrong," Vesper said quietly. "I don't have patience left for circling."

Tam set down the page. Outside, wind moved through the birches below the hut with a sound like pages turning, and he made himself say it plainly, because plainness was all he had to offer and because he had already decided something and did not want her to see the decision arrive.

"The binding has to be anchored. A song that unmakes a bargain has to be paid for out of the singers themselves—that's the whole grammar of these workings. The god trades in memory; so does the counter-song. Two voices, two anchors. Each singer offers memories to the knot. The song spends them like coin."

"How many?"

"That's not written." He tapped the page. "It says *as the knot requires*. Which means as much as the god weighs. And Ourod has eaten a kingdom's worth of yesterday, so I'd guess the answer is: a great deal."

Vesper was silent. Then: "Mine are the only ones it would want anyway. Memories of inside the ward—it's never tasted those. That's the point of me. Isn't it."

"It's one of the points of you," Tam said, and stood, abruptly, and went to the window, because it was easier to say the rest facing away. "But there's a condition. The anchor-voice—the voice that pays the heavier share—has to be untainted. Never touched by magic. Not born under it, not marked by it, not fed on it. The song needs one clean thread to tie everything off with, or the whole working just... feeds it. We'd be delivering a meal instead of a chain."

"And you've been touched."

"Fifteen years in an archive full of answered prayers." He tried to make it a joke; it came out flat. "I dreamed in the king's dreams for a decade, Vesper. I know things I shouldn't know. There's rot in me the song would find and follow. You're the only thread it can't unravel—Hollowmere made you deliberately bland, deliberately closed. The ward kept magic out of you the way a cellar keeps out frost. You're the clean voice. It has to be you paying the larger share."

The silence behind him went on long enough that he turned around. She wasn't crying. She was doing arithmetic—he could see it, the same tight-faced calculation he'd watched her make over the guild records, over escape routes, over everything. Counting what she had. Counting what the knot might take.

"My mother teaching me the bell rope," she said, as if reading from a ledger. "Aldous calling me 'girl' for six years before he ever said my name. The taste of the festival bread. Which stones wobble on the bridge path. Every single face in the valley." Her jaw tightened. "And if it takes all of it, I walk back into Hollowmere a stranger."

"Yes."

"And you? What does your share take?"

"Some of the archive. My mother's voice, probably—I've been hoarding that one. Whatever else it fancies." He shrugged, and hated how light he made it sound, and knew she saw through it. "Small coin next to yours."

"Don't." Her voice cracked like ice in a pond. "Don't do the brave-little-sacrifice voice, Tam. I've been the invisible girl my whole life; I know what it looks like when someone hides the size of a thing." She stood, crossed the small room, and stopped an arm's length away, glaring up at him with eyes that had heard a god breathe. "Tell me honestly what you're afraid of losing."

So he did. Standing in his own doorway with the wind worrying the roof, Tam told her about his mother singing in the kitchen of the archivist's quarters, off-key, always off-key, and how her voice was the last intact room in his memory and he visited it every night before sleep. He told her that if the song took it, he would wake tomorrow knowing a woman had existed and feeling nothing, and that this frightened him more than the god did—more than being eaten, more than dying. He said all of it in a level archivist's tone until the last sentence, which came out in no tone at all.

"There," he finished. "Now you know the shape of mine. It's smaller than yours. But it's honest."

Vesper reached out and took his hand. Her grip was cold and very strong.

"Good," she said. "Then we're both terrified, and we can work."

* * *

They practiced.

That was the thing no ballad ever mentioned, Tam thought on the fourth day, hoarse and furious in the birch grove while snow began to sift through the branches: heroism was mostly rehearsal. The completed melody—which was Aldous's half and Tam's reconstruction of the other half from fragments in the royal archive, fitted together like a broken bowl—had to be sung in true harmony, two lines that met and parted and met again, and neither of them could hold a line alone for more than eight bars without sliding.

Vesper had the guild training and no confidence. Tam had perfect pitch and no technique. Together they were, in his professional assessment as keeper of His Majesty's records, catastrophic.

Again. From the seventh bar. Again.

On the fifth day it caught—not beautifully, but truly, the two lines locking like gears, and for eleven seconds the air above the charcoal score shimmered the way heat shimmers over a road. They stopped. Looked at each other. Snow in her hair, blood on his lip from biting it.

"Eleven seconds," Tam said. "We need ninety."

"Then we keep going until our throats bleed." Vesper wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. She looked, he thought, less afraid than she had since the throne room—fear burned off by sheer stubborn repetition, the way fog burns off a field. "Tam. If the knot takes everything—if I come out the far side empty—will you tell me?"

"Tell you what?"

"Who I was." Her chin came up, defiant, as though daring him to pity her. "After. When we're back. Someone will have to teach me my own name. I'd rather it was someone who knew what it cost."

The words landed in Tam's chest and stayed there, heavy, and he understood that he had just been given a task more frightening than any in the archives: to become another person's memory. To carry her whole life in his ordinary, rot-flecked head and hand it back piece by piece to a stranger with familiar eyes.

"I'll tell you everything," he said. "Whether you want to hear it or not. I'm an archivist. Hoarding is what I'm *for*."

She laughed—wetly, but she laughed—and picked up the score.

* * *

On the seventh night, with the melody holding at forty bars unbroken and their voices raw, Tam woke past midnight to find Vesper sitting at the window, watching the north.

He joined her. Far off, past the black teeth of the mountains, the sky above Hollowmere's pass glowed faintly, wrongly—a bruised luminescence, like moonlight that had forgotten the moon. The ward, burning itself down to the wick.

"It's thinner every night," she said. "I can feel it from here. Like a tooth I've lost—I keep touching the gap."

"So we go tomorrow."

"So we go tomorrow." She didn't look at him. In the low light her face was the face of someone standing at the edge of a high place, and Tam realized she was memorizing it—this hut, this mountain, this moment, him—as though she could bank memories against the coming withdrawal, as though loving a thing in advance might hide it from the scales.

"Vesper." He waited until she turned. "Whatever the knot takes—you will still choose them. After. You'll learn their names and choose them again, because choosing is the part of love that memory never owned in the first place. I believe that. I have to believe it, and I only believe maybe four things in this world."

She considered that for a long moment, snow ticking against the shutter.

"Write it down, then," she said at last, handing him the charcoal. "All of it. So that after, when I'm empty and doubting, there's a record. You're good at records."

He wrote it. He wrote it carefully, in his best archival hand, and dated it, and folded it into the oilcloth with the guild pages, and did not tell her that his own hands were shaking too badly to feel brave about any of it.

At dawn they shouldered their packs and started down the mountain, toward the pass, toward the valley that had made her clean, toward the throne where a buried king waited to spend his last fragment holding a hungry god's gaze while two frightened friends sang for their lives.

The ward flickered overhead like a guttering lamp—and beyond it, faint but unmistakable, something vast drew breath to sing along.
