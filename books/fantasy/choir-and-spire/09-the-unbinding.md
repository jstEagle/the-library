## 9. The Unbinding

The throne room of Cairnlow had been beautiful once.

Vesper could still see the bones of that beauty beneath what Ourod had made of it—the vaulted ceiling where painted saints had been replaced by smooth grey absence, the windows of stained glass now bleached to colorless crystal, the banners on the walls with their heraldry scrubbed to blank white silk. The god did not destroy things. It *emptied* them. It was a moth that ate only the colors out of cloth and left the weave intact, so you could see exactly what had been lost and know it would never come back.

She walked down the center aisle because Tam said the guards expected her to run, and running was the one thing they knew how to stop.

Her wrists were bound in silk—silk, because iron was too memorable, she supposed—and the hollowed courtiers lined the aisle on either side like mannequins dressed in finery. They watched her pass with pleasant, vacant smiles. One of them, a woman with silver-threaded hair and eyes like unwritten paper, tilted her head as Vesper went by and whispered, almost kindly:

"You smell like *yesterday*."

"I know," Vesper said. "I brought enough for everyone."

The throne sat on a dais at the far end, and upon it sat the king—or the thing wearing him. He was not what she had imagined. She had pictured a monster, something swollen and wrong, but he looked merely tired: an old man in a crown too heavy for his neck, hands folded, face composed into an expression of mild contentment that never once reached his eyes. Around him moved the answered-prayer creatures, soft-bodied and half-formed, trailing ribbons of other people's wishes. They parted for her without being asked.

Ourod did not look up until she stood at the foot of the dais.

"Little bell-ringer," it said. The king's voice, but layered underneath with a sound like pages turning in an empty library. "You came all this way to sing to me. How touching. The guild always did mistake stubbornness for courage."

"The guild is dead," Vesper said. Her heart was slamming so hard against her ribs she was sure the whole court could hear it. "You saw to that, generation by generation. You ate them one memory at a time and called it a bargain."

"I called it *mercy*." The king's head tilted. Behind his eyes, deep down, something flickered—brief, desperate, drowned. "Your valley forgot its dead so it would not have to grieve them. Forgot its wars so it would not have to fear them. I have done the same for a kingdom. Every mouth I feed forgets its hunger. Is that not kindness?"

"No," said another voice, small and cracked and coming from the king's own throat—a child's voice, buried forty years deep. "*No, no, please—*"

The flicker behind the eyes. A hand twitched against the armrest, fingers curling like a drowning man reaching for light.

Vesper's breath caught. *He's still in there.* Aldous had hoped; Tam had believed from the archives; but hope was cheap until you heard a grown king beg through a stolen mouth.

"Ah." Ourod smiled with the king's thin lips. "Yes. There is a mouse in my wall. He scratches sometimes. It doesn't matter. Nothing matters here except—" Its gaze fixed on her, and the weight of it pressed down like deep water, and Vesper felt, for the first time, the edge of its attention touch the edges of her mind, tasting, sorting, *cataloguing*: *the smell of wet stone after rain, the sound of a bell rope burning through her palms, Aldous's cough, Tam's terrible laugh, the taste of bread baked over peat fire—*

It recoiled slightly. Almost delicately. Like a connoisseur discovering the vintage has gone strange.

"What are you?" it murmured. "You're full to bursting. No wonder you reek of it. A life spent *hoarding*."

"Not hoarding," Vesper said, and unbound her own hands, because Tam's knots were meant to be slipped. "Spending."

She began to sing.

*

The melody was half a thing, Aldous's half, the line he'd died trying to finish—but Tam had found the rest in a royal archive sealed behind a door that remembered being locked, and for three nights they had stitched the halves together in a cellar while the hollowed city slept around them. Two voices. That was the design. The guild had always known: a ward sung alone is a wall, but a ward sung by two is a *knot*, and knots hold.

Tam stepped out of the shadow of a pillar, where the guards had let him pass because his papers said he belonged there, and joined her on the second phrase.

His voice was rough and untrained and absolutely steady.

The song was not loud. That was the first surprise—that the binding of a god sounded like a lullaby, like something a mother might hum over a cradle. But the stones heard it. Vesper felt the throne room wake under her feet, old stone remembering itself, and the bleached windows flushed faintly with color at their edges, and the blank banners stirred though there was no wind.

Ourod rose from the throne.

It came up wrong—all at once, unfolding like something that had forgotten how bodies bent—and the courtly calm fell away and what stood there wore the king's shape but nothing else, a glove turned inside out. The answered-prayer creatures screamed without mouths. The courtiers swayed, smiling still, smiling harder, smiles stretched to splitting.

"*Stop,*" the god said, and the word hit the room like cold.

Vesper kept singing. So did Tam. Their voices braided, hers high and clear, his low and cracked, and between the two lines the melody built the knot: *this holds, therefore this holds, therefore this holds.*

The god lunged—not at her, she understood in a flash of clarity why not at her, because she was the anchor and anchors must be pulled, not broken—and it dove instead into the song, into the spaces between the notes, hunting the seam where two voices met. And there, waiting in the seam, was the king.

He rose inside his own body the way a diver rises toward light—slowly, agonizingly, dragging decades of eaten years behind him. Vesper felt him arrive at the surface of himself and gasp, a real gasp, human and ragged, and the god's attention snapped back to him in fury and hunger both, because here was the last meal it had never finished, the morsel it had saved, and it *could not help itself*. Hunger was the one prayer Ourod had ever answered honestly.

"Hold him," Tam shouted over the rising roar. "Vesper—*now*—"

*

She opened her memory the way Aldous had taught her to open a door in winter: braced, deliberate, knowing the cold on the other side.

Not all at once. In order. That was the craft of it, the last secret the dying master had pressed into her hands along with half a melody: *the song needs a price, girl, but you choose the order you pay in. Choose well.*

She gave it the mornings first. Rain on the guildhall roof. The bell rope's familiar burn. Bread over peat fire, and the neighbor children shrieking through the square, and the particular gray-gold light of Hollowmere at dusk when the ward-stones drank the sun and hummed their low contented note that only she had ever heard.

Each memory left her mouth as part of the song and entered the knot, and each one *held*. That was the miracle of the binding—it didn't destroy what it took. It wove it in. Her life became the thread that tied the god shut.

Aldous next. His cough. His hands teaching hers the chord shapes. His last lucid hour, his voice cracking on the truth: *we bargained, we never built, forgive us.* She gave that memory gladly and felt it settle into place like a keystone.

Then Tam. His laugh, terrible and wonderful. Three nights in a cellar, arguing about verse structure, sharing stolen bread. The moment he'd looked at her across a candle flame and said, *you know we're friends, don't you? I just want it said aloud once before the end.* She gave that too, and hated giving it, and gave it anyway.

The god was screaming now in a hundred borrowed voices. The king was holding it—barely, gloriously, his buried self wrapped around the thing that ate him, buying her seconds with the last coins of a self forty years pawned. His eyes had cleared. Human eyes, terrified and defiant, meeting hers across the ruined court.

"*Sing,*" the king rasped. "Whatever it costs. Sing. I have paid worse for less."

So she sang, and paid.

The valley itself went last. Not the place—she could keep the shape of a map, mountains and a river, geography without warmth—but everything else. The nightly bell. The rituals whose meanings no one questioned. The feeling—the *whole feeling*—of walking through a world that knew her name even when no mouth spoke it. The loneliness. The belonging. The strange, stubborn love she had carried for a valley that had never once needed to notice her, and noticed her anyway.

She poured it all into the knot.

And Ourod, which had fed on memory since before kingdoms had names, which had never once been *given* anything freely, took the offering into itself and found it could not digest it. Love was not a flavor it knew. The knot closed around it—two voices, one offered life, one king's sacrifice—and the god that eats yesterday discovered, at the end, that it had swallowed a tomorrow it could not unravel.

It came apart quietly. That was the strangest thing. All that roaring, all that hunger, and in the end it simply thinned, like fog at noon, like ink in water, and the throne room filled up with silence and falling dust and the slow, astonished return of color—red bleeding back into the banners, blue into the windows, gold leaf flaring on the vaults as if the room were remembering how to be beautiful and weeping while it did.

The king collapsed. Tam caught him halfway down the dais steps, lowering the old body gently, and the king's hand scrabbled weakly at Tam's sleeve, and his mouth worked.

"My name," he whispered. "Someone—please—I've forgotten my own—"

"It's in the archives," Tam said hoarsely. "Everything about you. I read it twice. I'll tell you every day until it sticks. Your name, your wife's name, your daughter—" His voice broke. "I remember it all. That's the deal. You held it, and I remember."

The king wept like a child who has been lost a very long time.

Vesper stood at the foot of the dais with the song finished in her throat and tried to remember what she was supposed to be grieving.

She knew she should be grieving. She knew it the way you know a fact in a book. Something had been taken from her—something enormous, something that mattered more than anything—and she searched for it and found only a smooth, warm absence, like a bed still holding the shape of someone who has risen.

There was a valley. She was fairly sure there was a valley. People there called her family, Tam had told her, and she had written it down that morning in her journal in her own handwriting: *When we get home—if we get home—read this page first.*

She touched the journal through her coat and felt the edges of pages, many pages, a whole book of herself written by a girl she could no longer meet.

"Vesper?" Tam was beside her, bloodied, shaking, alive, a stranger she trusted with her life and could not say why. "Are you—did it work? Do you still—"

"I don't know what I'm missing," she said. "That's the trouble. You can't miss a room you can't remember entering." She looked up at him, and her eyes stung with tears that had no story attached. "But I wrote myself a letter. Apparently I thought I'd need it. Apparently I knew me better than I do."

Tam laughed—wet, cracked, terrible and wonderful, though she couldn't have said why the laugh landed somewhere old and sore.

"Then we go home," he said. "And you start reading."

Far away, past the pass, past the border stones, a valley waited with its evening bell—and somewhere in her chest, below memory, below grief, below the smooth warm absence where her life had been, something in Vesper already knew the exact hour it rang.
