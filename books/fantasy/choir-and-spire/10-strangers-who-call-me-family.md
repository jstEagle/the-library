## 10. Strangers Who Call Me Family

The road down into Hollowmere was a stranger to her.

Vesper knew this the way she knew the ache in her feet and the taste of dust on her lips—immediately, without argument. She had walked for eleven days from the capital, past Cairnlow's smoking chimneys and the king's broken banner flying at half-mast over an empty throne room, and every mile of it was new. Every mile except this one, which should have been new too, and wasn't.

Her body knew the way. That was the first thing she learned about herself on the far side of everything: she was a collection of habits wearing a woman's shape. Her feet turned left where the path forked without consulting her. Her hand rose to push back a branch that hung exactly where her arm expected a branch to hang. When the ward-stones appeared at last, gray and lichen-eaten in the evening light, she stopped before them the way one stops before a door in one's own house—and felt nothing. No hum. No song beneath the silence. Whatever she had once heard there, whatever had let her feel the ward breathing, it had gone with the rest of her life into Ourod's long throat.

She touched the stone anyway. It was warm. That was all she could say about it.

*You lived here,* Tam had told her, the night before they parted at the crossroads outside Cairnlow, his voice rough with a grief he couldn't name because half of what they'd grieved together had been *hers*. *You have people there. They'll know you.*

*I won't know them,* she'd said.

*Then learn,* he'd said, and smiled, and it had been the bravest thing either of them did in that whole terrible year—braver than the singing, braver than the throne room, braver than standing in front of a god with nothing but a melody and a memory to feed it. *Learning is just loving slowly. You're good at slow things, Vesper. I remember that much about you.*

He remembered more than she did now. He remembered all of it—the guildhall, Master Aldous, the night the child's prayer was answered by something wearing its mother's face. He carried her life for her like a man carrying water across a desert, doling it out in sips when she asked. She had asked him, once, on the road: *Tell me who I was.* And he had talked until dawn, and she had listened, and none of it had caught. It slid off her like rain off slate. Stories about yourself are only stories, even when they're true. Even when everyone else swears by them.

So here she stood, at the border of a valley full of strangers who loved her, holding nothing but her hands' knowledge and her voice's, and the bell tower rising above the rooftops like a promise someone else had made.

She went in.

* * *

The valley smelled of woodsmoke and wet earth and bread. That, at least, needed no remembering; some things are simply true everywhere, and hunger is one of them.

They came out of their houses as she passed. Word traveled ahead of her somehow—she would learn later that a tinker had seen her on the high road and run ahead through three villages shouting her name—but that first evening it felt like magic, which was funny, because the whole point of Hollowmere had always been that there wasn't any. Doors opened. Faces turned. And they called her name—*Vesper, Vesper Vane*—with a warmth that made her chest hurt precisely because it meant nothing to her. Strangers, calling her family. A whole valley of people who had known her since childhood, greeting her home from a war she couldn't recall fighting.

An old woman reached her first. Small, bent, quick-eyed, with flour on her apron and tears already running freely down a face deeply lined with laughter.

"Oh, child," she said, and took Vesper's hands in both of hers, and squeezed them as if testing whether she were real. "Oh, look at you. Look what you've done."

"I'm sorry," Vesper said, because it was the only sentence she had. "I don't—I don't know you."

"You do," the old woman said gently. "You just don't remember yet. Or ever. Maren Ashby. I baked you your first honey-bun when you were four years old and stole it off my windowsill, and I never once scolded you for it." She looked at Vesper's face, searching it the way one searches a familiar room after a burglary—for what's missing. "It's all right," she said, and Vesper realized she was saying it to herself as much as to her. "It's all right. You came back. The rest we can build."

The rest of the evening happened to her rather than with her. There was a feast in the guildhall—a hall she recognized the way one recognizes a face in a dream, dimly, from underneath—and she sat at its head because no one would let her sit anywhere else. There were toasts. There were songs, and here was the second thing she learned about herself: when the valley sang the old harvest rounds, her voice joined them. Not hesitantly. Perfectly. Harmonies she had no memory of learning rose out of her throat like birds out of a hedge, and the table went quiet, and then someone laughed with joy, and then everyone did, and Vesper sat in the middle of their joy like a stone in a river, feeling it rush past her on every side.

A young man with soot-dark hair and his father's stubborn jaw kept refilling her cup and asking questions. *Do you remember the flood year? Do you remember teaching me knots? Did you really once ring the midnight bell for a dare?* She answered honestly each time: *No. Tell me. Was I good at it?* And he would laugh and tell her, and she would listen, and it would not stick, and he would tell her again the next day anyway, cheerfully, endlessly, as if repetition were a form of love. Which, she was beginning to suspect, it was.

His name was Colm. She wrote it down.

That was the third thing she learned, and the most important: names could be written. Memory failed her nightly like a tide going out, but ink held. That first night, sitting alone in the guildhall's small cold chamber—her chamber, they said, hers since she was twelve—she found a blank book in the desk drawer, and a quill, and she began.

*Maren Ashby. Baker. Honey-buns. Lost a son to the fever years ago; do not ask her about sons.*
*Colm Ferris. Smith's boy. Stubborn. Teaches me the same jokes daily and laughs when I don't get them.*
*Tam. Archivist. Friend. Carries my memories for me. Visits at solstice. Keep the window seat clear for him.*

She fell asleep mid-sentence, pen still in hand, and dreamed of nothing at all—which was, she supposed, fair. She had traded her dreams along with everything else. The god had eaten well.

* * *

Spring came, and with it the work of beginning.

Her hands surprised her daily. She took up the bell rope on her first morning back—drawn to it, she could not have said why—and her fingers found the pulls and pauses of the evening peal without instruction, ringing out a pattern the valley hadn't heard since Aldous died, and old Maren stood in the square below and wept and said, *That's his rhythm, girl. That's exactly how he rang it.* Vesper did not know who Aldous was. But her wrists did. Somewhere in tendons and muscle, a dead man's lesson survived the death of the man who received it, and every evening when she pulled the rope she rang not just the hour but a ghost's handwriting, and the valley heard it and was comforted, and she let them be comforted. It cost her nothing. It was the one inheritance she could spend freely.

There were harder days. Days when a child ran up to her in the market crying *Vesper! Vesper!* and hugged her legs, and she felt the warmth of it and the hollowness of it simultaneously, love arriving addressed to someone she no longer contained. Days when she caught herself watching other families—at supper, at the well, at funerals—with the detached envy of a traveler looking through windows. Days when she woke reaching for a melody in her sleep, humming half a phrase that made her heart clench and break open and clench again, a song about two voices, a binding, a price paid, and she would lie in the dark knowing only this: that she had sung it once, with someone, for everyone, and it had worked, and it had taken everything, and she would do it again.

That last part she was sure of. That was the strange mercy of it. Whatever Ourod had taken, it had not taken the shape of her choices. She could not remember choosing these people. But given the same life again, empty-handed, she chose them anew—daily, deliberately, the way one chooses to pull a bell rope or plant a field or keep a promise whose reasons are lost. Love, it turned out, was not a memory. It was a practice. And practices could be rebuilt from rubble.

Tam came at solstice, as the journal instructed her to expect him. He arrived dusty and grinning and stood in her doorway like a question, afraid she wouldn't know him—and she didn't, not truly—but she had written *keep the window seat clear*, and it was clear, and she offered it to him, and he sat, and they talked until the candle drowned in its own wax. He told her stories. This time some of them caught—not as memories, but as seeds. She wrote them down. *I was brave. I was lonely. I was loved. I am loved.* By midsummer the journal filled a shelf. By autumn, children in the square were calling her *the Rememberer*, though she remembered less than anyone in the valley, because she was the only one writing it all down.

One evening in late autumn, ringing the bell, she paused between pulls—her hands knew the pause belonged there, though no one alive could say why—and in that pause she heard it, faint as breath: a hum under the world. The ward, singing. Not to her. Not anymore. Just singing, the way a hearth sings, because it was tended and fed and loved by people who kept it without understanding it.

She finished the peal. She went down into the square, where Maren waited with honey-buns and Colm with questions and a dozen others with names she'd written and rewritten until the ink wore grooves in the paper, and she ate, and laughed, and got the joke wrong on purpose this time, because Colm liked explaining it.

Later, alone, she lit a candle and opened a fresh page and wrote at the top, in a steady hand:

*I don't remember them. I choose them. Every day, I choose them. That has to be worth more than remembering—because remembering is something that happens to you, and this, this is something I do.*

Below it, after a moment, she added one more line—the only entry she ever made twice, word for word, page after page, year after year, as if insuring against a loss that had already come:

*And if I forget this too: begin again. The rope will know my hands. The song will know my voice. The valley will know my name. Begin again.*

Outside, the bell settled into silence. Under the silence, faint and faithful, the ward hummed on—and somewhere beyond the pass, in a kingdom learning to remember itself again, a man with a crown and a broken smile was teaching his court an old rhyme, two voices to a line, keeping time for a return no one had promised.

But that was another story. Here, in Hollowmere, the honey-buns were warm, the journal was open, and Vesper Vane—last apprentice, first Rememberer, stranger and daughter—blew out her candle and slept, dreamless and content, in the valley that had forgotten how to forget her.
