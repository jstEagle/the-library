## 1. The Dying Cartographer

Grayhollow after dark smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone, and beneath that — if you knew how to listen with your nose — the faint green scent of things that grew where they shouldn't. Beatrix Vasquez had spent twelve years learning exactly which illegalities a person could sell in the night market without drawing the Wardens' attention, and she had arranged her stall accordingly: candle-stubs charmed to burn an hour longer than honest wax (harmless, technically; a cheat of time rather than magic), knotted red thread for travelers who wanted to find their way home (a superstition, the Wardens said, and superstition was permitted), and little folded paper birds that would flutter once in your palm and then be only paper again.

She never sold anything that drew. Nothing that mapped. She was careful even with her own hands — ink stained under her fingernails like any scrivener's, ordinary black, bought from the same chandler who supplied half the city's clerks. If you looked at Beatrix Vasquez, you saw a tired woman of middle years selling trinkets under a lantern turned down low so the Warden patrols would pass her by without slowing. That was the whole architecture of her life, and she had built it brick by careful brick until even she sometimes couldn't see through it.

The lantern guttered. She trimmed the wick with her thumbnail and did not think about the dream she'd had three nights running: a road unrolling beneath a mountain, white as a scar on dark skin, and at the end of it a door shaped like something she'd lost.

"You're burning your candle wrong," said the moth-seller from two stalls down, an old woman named Hesper who had been old when Beatrix first came to Grayhollow and would be old when the city itself gave out. "Wick's crooked."

"The wick is fine."

"Everything about you is crooked, girl. That's why I like you." Hesper's moths stirred in their cage, wings like ash with eyes painted on them. They were real moths, merely ugly. Even so, one of them pressed its face to the wire as if it could smell the lie Beatrix lived inside.

The market moved around her in its usual tide — fishwives haggling over the last of the day's catch, a knife-grinder working his wheel, children too poor for beds darting between legs and stealing warmth instead of coin. Somewhere down the row, someone laughed too loudly at a joke that wasn't funny, the kind of laughter that meant fear looking for somewhere to put itself. The Wardens had been thick in Grayhollow all month. Three arrests on Copperlane. A woman dragged from her own bakery with her hands still dusted in flour because a neighbor swore the bread rose *wrong*.

Beatrix kept her eyes down and sold a love-knot to a boy with no idea what he wanted, which was most boys, and a paper bird to his sister, and pretended her heart didn't lift — just slightly, just traitorously — every time the bird flapped its wings.

*Twelve years,* she told herself. *You have been safe for twelve years.*

Then the crowd went strange.

It happened the way weather happens: not all at once but all together, a pressure change, heads turning in the same direction like iron filings finding their north. Down the mouth of Lantern Row, where the market spilled toward the tram tunnel and the river-smell beyond, people were stepping aside. Not fleeing. Stepping aside, the way you step away from a sick animal or a debt collector.

The man who came up the row was tall and ruined. His coat had once been good — she could tell by the cut, by the ghost of silver braid at the cuffs — and was now more mud than wool, torn along one sleeve in a way that suggested it hadn't caught on anything so innocent as a nail. He walked like a man carrying something breakable, each step placed with terrible care, and it took her a moment to understand that the breakable thing was himself.

He was bleeding. She saw that when he was close enough to touch: a dark bloom spreading low on his side, soaking through coat and shirt in slow pulses, and his lips were gray as the underside of leaves.

"Hesper," Beatrix said quietly, without turning her head. "Get the watch?"

"And tell the Wardens there's a bleeding man at a charm stall?" Hesper snorted. "They'll burn us both and thank themselves for it." But she was already moving, herding the curious away with her cane and her temper.

The man reached Beatrix's stall and stopped. He did not reach for the table. He did not ask for help. He looked at her face — not her hands, not her wares, her *face*, the way you look at a place you buried long ago and dreamed of finding again — and he smiled, and the smile frightened her worse than the blood.

"You kept the lantern low," he said. His voice was cracked clay. "Smart. You always were smart, Trixie."

The name went through her like a hook through cloth.

No one alive called her that. No one alive *knew* it. It belonged to a kitchen in another country, to a girl with skinned knees who drew maps in flour on the table while her mother scolded and her sister laughed, and that girl had gone into the ground of the past twelve years ago, the same year Beatrix Vasquez had been invented to replace her.

"I don't know you," she said.

"No." He swayed. "But I knew your mother. And I promised her I'd find you before—" He stopped, swallowed, started over. "Before I lost the chance."

He reached into his coat then, and half the market must have seen the motion, because Beatrix heard the collective intake of breath behind her. What he drew out was not a knife. It was a fold of heavy paper, square, sealed with wax that was not wax — it moved when the light touched it, the way oil moves on water — and it was *warm*. She felt the warmth from a foot away, like standing near a hearth.

He pressed it into her hands and closed her fingers over it with both of his, and his hands were freezing except where the map warmed them between.

"It's the last of it," he whispered. "The fire's memory. Everything it ever saw, everything it ever loved, drawn down into paper so it wouldn't die with the body. Do you understand what I'm giving you? Nobody else could carry it. Nobody else could *read* it. That's why I came. That's why I crawled halfway across the—"

"Don't." Her voice shook. "I don't want it. Whatever this is, whatever you think I am—"

"Trixie." He said it gently, the way you say a word to calm a startled animal. "Look at what you are. Look at your stall. Knots and birds and candles that lie about the hour. You've spent twelve years doing magic with one hand tied behind your back, and you did it so well you almost convinced the hand didn't exist." His breath rattled. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry it has to be tonight. I tried to get here sooner. I tried for *years*."

His knees went.

She caught him — dropped the map on the table, caught him under the arms, lowered him down against the stall's leg as gently as she could while her mind screamed at her to let go, to step back, to shout for help and become a bystander in her own story. His weight was nothing. When had he eaten last? When had anyone been kind to him?

"Stay with me," she said. "Press on the wound, like this—"

"There isn't time for wounds." Blood at the corner of his mouth now, and still he smiled, patient as an old teacher. "Listen. The map knows the road under the mountain. There's a court there. Old bargains. They keep things in glass, Trixie. Small lights in small lanterns." His fingers found her wrist, gripped with the last strength he had. "One of those lights is yours. It has been yours since you were nineteen years old and desperate. I know. I was the one who—"

And then his voice failed him entirely, and what came out was barely sound at all, and she bent close to catch it, close enough to feel the cold coming off him like winter off a stone:

"*Trix. Run. Don't let the King read your name.*"

He died between one heartbeat and the next, the way a candle dies when pinched — here, and heat, and then simply absence. His eyes stayed open on the underside of her awning, and the smile did not leave his face, and around them the night market resumed as markets do, the tide closing over the space where a man had been, because that is what crowds do with death when the Wardens might be listening.

Beatrix sat back on her heels. Her hands were red to the wrists.

The map lay on her table where he had left it, and it was humming.

Not metaphorically. Not the way a memory hums. It made an actual sound, low and layered, like a struck bell heard through a wall, like a choir very far underground, and the wax seal had broken open of its own accord, and along the fold's edge she could see color moving — not printed color, not dye, but *light* moving under the paper's skin, gold and ember-red, turning slowly like something asleep that had begun to dream.

She should drop it in the river. She should stamp it into the mud and walk away and tomorrow open her stall and sell knots and birds and lying candles and be safe, be nobody, be a woman whose worst crime was a crooked wick.

Her hands closed around it anyway. It was warm as a living thing, warm as the stranger's hands had been cold, and through her palms the hum climbed into her chest and settled there, and she recognized it the way you recognize a lullaby sung in a language you've forgotten — not the words, but the shape of them. It hummed, and somewhere far below the city, something answered.

Hesper returned, cane tapping, and stopped dead at the sight of her: kneeling in the mud, bloody hands full of impossible paper.

"Girl," Hesper said slowly, "what in the deep dark have you got there?"

"I don't know yet." Beatrix stood. Her legs held. Twelve years of practice held. But when she spoke again, her voice came out in her mother's accent, in the old country vowels she had sanded off herself grain by grain: "It knows my name."

Down Lantern Row, past the fishwives and the knife-grinder, a patrol of Wardens rounded the corner in their gray lacquered coats, and every lantern in the market seemed to dim as they passed — and Beatrix, moving before she had decided to move, blew out her own light, folded the humming map against her heart, and slipped into the dark between the stalls like smoke going home.

Behind her, the dead man's eyes still smiled at the awning. Ahead of her lay the attic workshop, the locked chest under the floorboards, and twelve years of silence about to crack wide open.

The map pulsed once against her ribs, patient as a second heartbeat, and began, very softly, to unfold.
