## 3. A Voice for Locked Doors

Three years ago, Sera Vane had sung for the Warden-General himself. She had stood in the marble throat of the Cathedral of Order with forty voices behind her and the whole city listening through the open doors, and when she reached the high phrase in the Litany of Stillness — the one note that was supposed to hang like a held breath — the great iron door at the back of the nave had groaned on its hinges and swung wide.

It had been sealed since the Reckoning. Sealed by three separate Warden-masons with salt mortar and cold iron and a blessing spoken backward so it could never be unspoken.

Sera's voice had opened it in four seconds.

She hadn't even meant to. That was the terrible part, the part they never believed when she tried to explain: she hadn't pushed anything into the note, hadn't done the thing she'd learned as a girl from her mother, the folding of sound into seams. She had simply been *true*, purely true, for one note longer than was safe, and the door had heard her and remembered that it used to be wood and hinges and not a prison, and it had opened out of sheer homesickness.

They banned her voice the same week. Not her singing — Grayhollow loved its music, as long as it stayed small and harmless and dead inside — but her *voice*, specifically, formally, entered into the ledger of restricted instruments alongside thunder-charms and whispering mirrors. She was permitted to perform only songs already catalogued, already worn smooth, already sung ten thousand times by throats the Wardens had inspected. If she deviated — if she improvised, if she ornamented, if she did any of the hundred small living things a singer does without thinking — she would be fined, then silenced, then taken.

So now she sang in the Drowned Bell, a tavern sunk half a story below street level where the ceiling sweated and the lamplight came up amber through bottles nobody could afford, and she sang the same eleven sanctioned songs in rotation, flattening every note into obedience, and she collected coppers in a chipped cup, and she went home to a room above a tannery that smelled of everything she'd lost.

Tonight the crowd was thin. Rain outside — real rain, not the usual gray drizzle, but a hard drumming rain that drove even the beggars under awnings and made the gutters run bright with lamplight and something else, something faintly green that Sera chose not to look at too closely because looking closely was how people disappeared in this city.

She finished *The Miller's Patient Daughter* — song number seven, approved for public performance, its third verse officially shortened on the grounds that the original contained an unresolved minor cadence, which the Office of Harmonic Safety had determined might induce melancholy or, worse, longing — and she set down the cup and let the silence be silent.

That was when the woman sat down across from her.

Sera had noticed her earlier, or rather had noticed *not noticing* her, which was harder. The tavern was half empty and this woman should have been easy to clock: dark-haired, ink-stained fingers, a charm-seller's shabby coat with sawdust in the seams, sitting alone at the corner table doing nothing suspicious whatsoever, which was itself suspicious. But every time Sera's eyes drifted toward her, some gentle fog slid over the thought and she looked away again, mildly, like a person who had simply decided the wall was more interesting.

A veil-work. A good one. Sera had grown up in a house where her mother wove veils out of humming thread, and she knew the taste of one on the air — like the moment before remembering a word.

Now the woman was sitting directly across from her, close enough that Sera could see the ink stains went all the way up under her cuffs, and there was no fog anymore, just a pair of steady brown eyes and an expression of someone about to do something they'd already decided to regret.

"Drink?" the woman said, and signaled the barkeep before Sera answered. Two cups of the plum wine arrived, the kind that tasted mostly of syrup and slightly of forgetting. "On me."

"I don't drink with strangers."

"You sang the seventh verse of the Miller's Daughter tonight." The woman turned her cup slowly on the table. "The full version. You put back the line about the river keeping what it loves."

Sera went very still. "That's the sanctioned text."

"The sanctioned text ends the verse at 'the mill wheel turns.' Yours didn't." The woman looked up. Her voice dropped to almost nothing, pitched beneath the rain and the clink of glasses, a voice meant for one listener only. "You hid a whole extra line inside your breath, between two notes, quiet enough that only someone who knew the original would ever catch it. I grew up on that song. I caught it."

*The river keeps what it loves.* Sera's mother had sung that line while mending nets, while dying, while — in the end — being carried out of the house on a board with coins on her eyes. It wasn't in the catalogue. It had never been in the catalogue. It existed only in kitchens and fish-markets and the memory of women who were gone.

"Who are you?" Sera asked.

"Beatrix Vasquez. I sell charms in the night market. Harmless ones." A pause with weight in it. "Mostly harmless ones."

"And you're going to ruin my life over a folk lyric."

"No," Beatrix said. "I'm going to ask you for something worse, and you're going to refuse it, and then I'm going to show you why you won't."

Sera laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound, the first laugh she'd produced off-stage in months. "Charm-sellers don't usually open with threats."

"It's not a threat. It's a request." Beatrix leaned in. The lamplight caught the fine lines around her eyes; she was younger than her tiredness suggested, maybe early thirties, with the particular exhaustion of someone who had spent twelve years holding still. "One song. Sung at the gate of the mountain road, three nights from now, at moonrise. One song, and then you walk away and I never trouble you again."

"The mountain road is closed. Has been closed since before I was born. There's nothing up there but Wardens and rock."

"There's a door under there," Beatrix said. "And doors are what you're for."

The words landed in Sera's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the echo and hated how long it took.

"I don't know what you've heard—"

"I know exactly what happened three years ago. I read the incident record. I have a friend who copies things he shouldn't." Beatrix's voice stayed low, even, almost gentle. "One note, Sera Vane. Four seconds. And a door that three Warden-masons swore shut came open like it missed you personally. Do you know what that is? That's not a crime. That's a *vocation*. And they took it away from you and gave you eleven dead songs and a chipped cup."

"They also would take my hands if they found me helping you," Sera said. "The penalty for unauthorized resonance is silencing first. Then the rest."

"Yes," Beatrix agreed. "I know. I've spent twelve years hiding too, so believe me when I say I'm not asking anyone to be brave. I'm asking you to want something enough that bravery stops mattering." She paused, and for the first time her steadiness flickered, showing the fear underneath. "I can't tell you what's behind the door. Not yet. Telling is dangerous for both of us. But I'll tell you what I *can*: whatever's down there has been waiting a long time, and I think — I think it knows your name too."

"That's not comforting. That's horrifying."

"Yes," Beatrix said again, and almost smiled. "I find that most true things are."

Sera sat back. Around them the Drowned Bell murmured on — dice rattling, rain drumming, some drunk two tables over weeping quietly at his own reflection — and none of it touched the little bubble of silence the two women occupied, which felt less like privacy than like the held breath before a high note.

"No," Sera said. It came out steadier than she expected. "Whatever you're doing, whoever died to put you up to it — and I can see it in your face, someone did die — the answer is no. I spent three years learning to sing *small*. Small keeps me alive. Small keeps my hands attached. I am not going to stand at some cursed gate and crack open a mountain for a stranger with ink on her hands and grief on her breath, no matter how well she quotes folk lyrics." She drained the plum wine, grimaced at the sweetness. "Thank you for the drink. Don't come back here. They watch singers closer than they watch anyone."

Beatrix nodded slowly, and Sera watched the hope drain out of her face by degrees, and hated herself for being the one to pour it out. "I understand," Beatrix said. She rose, dropped a few coppers beside the cups, and pulled her hood up. "For what it's worth — the line about the river. My mother sang it too. Different river, same line. Isn't it strange, the things that survive the Wardens?"

She turned toward the stairs.

And that was when the humming started.

It came from inside her coat — from the flat canvas satchel slung against her ribs, the shape of a folded document. At first Sera thought it was Beatrix humming, some absent habit, and then she realized the sound had no breath in it at all. It rose pure and sourceless, threading through the rain-noise, and the glasses behind the bar began to ring softly in sympathy, each one finding its own thin note, and the lamp flames leaned toward the sound like flowers toward sun.

Beatrix froze halfway to the stairs. Slowly, she drew the bundle out — a folded square of heavy paper, tied with waxed cord — and the moment it cleared her coat, the humming resolved.

Into melody.

Into *her mother's lullaby.*

Not the sanctioned version. Not any version in any catalogue. The real one — the one with the descending third at the end that wasn't written anywhere, the one her mother had made up on the nights the storms came off the water, the one Sera had last heard whispered through the boards of a sickroom fourteen years ago. *Sleep, my gull, the tide will turn, the sea gives back what boats return—*

Every hair on Sera's arms stood up. The melody was imperfect, searching, like something remembering rather than reciting — but it knew the third. It knew the third *exactly*, and the third was the part her mother had improvised, the part that existed nowhere in the world except in Sera's own head, unless—

Unless it had lived somewhere else too. Somewhere below. Somewhere kept.

"Sera," Beatrix said quietly, holding the warm, humming fold of paper between them like a coal. Her eyes were wide and frightened and certain. "I need you to understand something. This map has been leading me all week. To places. To people." She swallowed. "It has never once led me to a *song*."

The lullaby climbed to its highest phrase — the one her mother always softened, the one Sera had spent three years refusing to reach for — and hung there, unfinished, waiting, the way a held door waits.

Sera Vane reached across the table and untied the cord.

* * *

Three streets away and one story up, in a room above a shuttered debt-broker's office, Dov Fen was counting other people's money when he heard, faint through the rain, a snatch of melody that stopped his hands mid-coin.

He knew that tune. Everyone from the harbor wards knew that tune. And everyone from the harbor wards knew it had died with the women who sang it.

He looked at the coin in his fingers, thought of a man with gray in his beard who had once bought a boy out of a broker's cellar, and felt the old debt shift its weight against his chest like something turning over in its sleep.

"Right," he told the empty room, and reached for his coat. "Right. Fine."
