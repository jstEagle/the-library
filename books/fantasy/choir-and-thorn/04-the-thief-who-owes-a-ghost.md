## 4. The Thief Who Owes a Ghost

Dov Fen had three rules, and he'd kept all of them for six years without breaking so much as a seam.

Never work the same pocket twice. Never trust a man who smiles before he pays. And never — *never* — look too long at the dead.

He was breaking the third rule now, standing in the doorway of the Saltcellar tavern with his collar up and his heart going like a trapped bird, watching the ghost of Aldric Vess sit at a table he could not touch, in light that no one else seemed to notice.

The dead stranger. The one the whole night market was whispering about. Bled out at a charm-seller's stall two streets over, they said, right into the gutter, and the Wardens had come sniffing by morning.

Dov knew him. That was the trouble. Dov knew his face the way you know a face you've tried to forget — from below, from the bad years, from the debt-broker's cellar on Cindermarrow Lane where Dov had spent four months of his fourteenth year counting cracks in the ceiling and learning that freedom had a price tag and someone else's name on it.

Aldric Vess had been the name on it.

Not that Dov had known it then. The man had simply appeared one gray afternoon, laid a purse heavy enough to make the broker's eyes go wet, and said, "The boy. The Fen boy." No explanation. No conditions. He'd walked Dov out into the rain and vanished, and Dov had spent six years telling himself it was luck, or charity, or a mistake someone would eventually come to collect on.

Now here was the collection: a ghost in the Saltcellar, thin as smoke held to a candle's shape, sitting across from the charm-woman — Vasquez, the map-seller, the quiet one with ink-stained fingers — and pointing, patiently, at things she couldn't see him pointing at.

*Never look too long at the dead.* But Dov looked. He looked until the ghost turned its head, and its eyes found his across the crowded room, and there was nothing terrible in them at all. Only recognition. Only something that might have been relief.

Dov's rules lasted another four seconds. Then he crossed the room and sat down at their table like a man diving into cold water, because the only way through it was fast.

"You're Vess," he said to the ghost, though the ghost couldn't answer, though half the tavern probably thought he was mad. "You bought me off Cindermarrow. Six years ago. Rain coming down sideways. You never told me why."

The ghost's mouth moved. No sound came out. But its hand rose, slowly, and pressed flat against its own chest — over the heart, or where the heart had been — and then pointed, gently, at Beatrix Vasquez.

She was staring at Dov like he'd grown a second head. Up close she looked tired in the way stone is tired, worn smooth by weather, but her eyes were quick and dark and missed nothing. "You can see him," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone can see him," Dov said. "He's sitting right there."

"No." She glanced around the tavern — at the dockhands, the knife-grinders, the woman selling roasted chestnuts near the door — none of whom gave the empty chair so much as a glance. "No, they can't. Only I could. Until now." Her fingers curled around the edge of something wrapped in oilcloth on the table between them, and Dov felt it — felt it the way you feel a struck bell through the floorboards — a hum, low and alive, rising up through the wood and into his teeth. "Why did he buy your freedom?"

"I asked myself that every day for six years." Dov pulled his cap off, worked it in his hands. "Figured I'd find out when I stopped being afraid of the answer."

The ghost leaned forward. Its outline flickered like a draft worrying a flame, and for a moment — just a moment — Dov thought he heard it, a single word dragged up from somewhere very far away:

*—tired—*

Then it was gone, and the ghost sat back, and the hum from the oilcloth bundle swelled once, softly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Beatrix Vasquez looked from the ghost to Dov, and Dov watched her decide things — watched it happen behind her eyes the way he watched marks decide whether to clutch their purses. "The Wardens burned the charm-market last night," she said. "They're looking for this." She rested her hand on the bundle. "They'll be looking for me. And I need to get under the mountain, and every road above ground has Warden eyes on it now."

"Under the mountain," Dov repeated.

"The court beneath. Where souls are kept." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *the fish market*, and somehow that made it worse.

Dov laughed, because the alternative was screaming, and a thief learns early that laughter costs less. "Right. Well. There are ways down. The old tram tunnels, the drowned cisterns, the hollows under Cindermarrow itself — the city sits on the mountain's roots like a drunk on a fence post. I've been in most of those tunnels. I've slept in some of them." He turned his cap over. "But I want it said plainly first, charm-woman, because I don't do debts twice. Whatever's down there — whatever he—" he nodded at the ghost, whose stillness had the particular quality of a man holding his breath, "—wants with you, I'm not walking into it blind again. I walked out of one cellar owing my life to a stranger. I'm not walking into another."

The ghost raised both hands then, and Dov went quiet, because the ghost was doing something it hadn't done before: it was *drawing*. One finger traced shapes on the tabletop — slow, careful, deliberate — and the shapes were lines, and the lines were a road, and the road ran down and down beneath the city, past junctions Dov knew and passages he didn't, into a darkness marked with a small, urgent circle.

"That's the tram line," Dov said slowly. "Under the river. Nobody's run trams since the Wardens flooded it. How does he know it?"

"He made maps," Beatrix said quietly. "Before Grayhollow outlawed them. Before he—" She stopped. Her hand tightened on the bundle. "He carried this map for a long time. It remembers what he knew."

The ghost's finger came to rest on the circle at the bottom of the drawn road, and then lifted, and pointed — at Dov's own chest, this time, straight and steady, and Dov felt the gesture go through him like a fishhook.

"You're asking me," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "You bought me free and now you're asking me to walk you back down into a cellar. Is that it? Is that the joke?"

The ghost didn't flinch. But its hand opened — palm up, empty, an old gesture, older than language — and Dov understood it anyway, because he'd seen it in dreams for six years: *no debt. No debt at all. A door, not a chain.*

And that, in the end, was what broke him. Not guilt. Guilt he could carry; he'd carried it up out of Cindermarrow and it fit nicely in the same pocket as everything else he'd stolen. What broke him was simpler and more shameful: he was *tired*. Tired of flinching at gray-haired men in doorways. Tired of checking his shadow for collectors. Tired of owing a kindness he'd never been allowed to repay, wearing it like a stone sewn into his coat, heavier every year.

Six years was long enough to be tired.

"Fine," Dov said. He put his cap back on, pulled it low, and stood. "The flooded tram line. There's a grate behind the tannery on Sloe Street — third brick from the left is loose, and the drop's thirty feet, so nobody tell Sera Vane if she scares easy."

"Sera Vane?" Beatrix blinked. "The singer?"

"She sings in the Gilded Gill these nights. For coppers and cabbage soup." Dov shrugged, already moving toward the door, already feeling strange and lighter and terrified all at once. "You want doors opened under the mountain, charm-woman, and there are doors down there that aren't doors at all. I've heard them humming. If anyone can sing a way through, it's her — assuming she hasn't drunk her voice away."

Behind him, he heard the scrape of her chair, the soft rustle of the oilcloth bundle being gathered, and — faintly, impossibly — the sound of footsteps that belonged to no one, following.

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the table where the ghost still sat. "Vess," he said. "When we get down there — whatever this is, whatever you couldn't say while you were breathing — say it plain. You've got a voice now even if it doesn't carry. Don't waste it on gestures."

The ghost smiled. It was a terrible thing, a wonderful thing, a smile six years overdue, and Dov turned away from it fast, before it could undo him entirely.

Outside, the rain was coming down sideways, exactly as it had that afternoon on Cindermarrow Lane, and Dov Fen stepped into it with two strangers and a dead man at his back, and for the first time since he was fourteen years old, he wasn't running alone.

He just hoped, as the tannery stink rose up to meet them and the loose brick waited three streets on, that whatever waited beneath the mountain was kinder than the thing waiting above it — because even now, even here, he could hear the bells of the Wardens' watch beginning to ring, and they were ringing wrong.
