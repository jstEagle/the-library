## 5. What the Wardens Burn

The fire came at dusk, as fires in Grayhollow always did — politely at first, then all at once.

Beatrix was restocking her stall when the first torchlight bloomed at the mouth of Tanner's Row, and for one foolish heartbeat she thought it was a festival. The night market loved its lanterns. It loved paper suns and oil-lamp moths and the little glass votives she sold with no magic in them at all, nothing but colored glass and the suggestion of wonder. She had built twelve years on that suggestion. Twelve years of harmless charms, of ink stains instead of spark-scarred fingertips, of a smile calibrated so precisely to blandness that even the Wardens' informants yawned when they passed her table.

Then the torches turned, and began to touch things.

She smelled it before she understood it: tallow smoke, yes, but under it something sweeter and fouler — burning sage-wrappings, burning charm-paper, burning the particular resin the hedge-witches used to seal their sachets. Warden fire was not ordinary fire. It had been blessed against enchantment, soaked in hymn-water and iron ash, and where it landed on anything that remembered magic, it clung like guilt and ate until there was nothing left to remember.

"Market's closed," said a voice made of official boredom. A Warden-sergeant walked the row with a brand in each hand, touching them to awnings as casually as a man lighting streetlamps. "By order of the Provost's Office. Anyone holding unlicensed thaumaturgical materials will be processed."

*Processed.* The word had a sound like a door closing.

Around Beatrix, the market came apart. Old Mother Quill grabbed her tray of luck-knots and ran with it held over her head like a crown. The candle-boy slipped under his own table and vanished, which was a small magic in itself. Someone was screaming two stalls down — not in pain, Beatrix realized, but in grief, the raw animal grief of watching your livelihood catch light and knowing you cannot spit on it fast enough to matter.

Her hands were moving before her mind caught up. She swept her wares into the canvas satchel beneath the counter — the glass votives, the spools of gray thread, the little folded paper birds that were only paper, *only ever only paper*, she had checked a thousand times. Her fingers found the thing at the bottom of the satchel without looking for it.

The map was warm through the canvas. Warm, and faintly pulsing, like a bird's breast under a cupped hand.

It had hummed all night in her attic. It had drawn itself while she slept — she had seen the fresh lines at dawn, a road going down and down beneath a mountain rendered in strokes that looked less like ink than like vein-work, like something circulatory. And in the middle of it, a small square cell, and inside the cell a shape drawn in trembling silver that her eyes kept sliding off of and her heart kept refusing to.

The shape looked like a soul. The shape, if she was honest with herself — and she had spent twelve years being scrupulously honest with herself about everything except this — looked like *hers*.

Now the map pulsed against her hip as though it could smell the Warden-fire coming, and Beatrix understood with terrible clarity what the fire was for. Not the market. Not the charms. The market was kindling around one particular warmth. They were hunting the map, and everything between the Wardens and the map would burn because it happened to stand there.

Twelve years. Twelve years of being small and gray and safe, and here was the bill come due, presented in flame.

A hand closed on her arm. Sera Vane, tall and smoke-eyed, already pulling her back from the stall. "Beatrix. *Beatrix.* It's gone. Let it go."

"It's not—" Her voice cracked. Behind her awning, the fire found her stock of gray thread and the flames went briefly, horribly blue, hungrily blue, and she understood that somewhere in twelve years of careful nothing, she had let one true thing slip in. The thread had been spun by her own hand, from her own gift, on a night early enough that she'd almost forgotten she had one. "That was mine," she whispered. "That whole stall was mine."

"Yes." Sera's grip tightened. "And you're more than a stall. Come *on*."

The ghost was waiting at the end of the row.

He stood in the lee of the cooper's shop, half-lit by the advancing fire, and he was pointing — he was always pointing — down the alley behind the chandler's, where the smoke ran thin and the dark ran deep. In life he must have been an ordinary man; in death he had the urgency of a man late for something for years. His mouth moved. Nothing came. But his finger did not waver.

"He wants us to go with him," Dov Fen said. The thief materialized out of the crowd the way he probably materialized everywhere, sideways and apologetically, a satchel of someone else's goods already over his shoulder. "He's been following me all evening making that face. I thought it was gas."

"You can see him?" Beatrix asked.

"I can see everyone. Occupational hazard." Dov grimaced toward the ghost. "Also he owes me an explanation about the afterlife, so we're even."

The alley swallowed them just as the Warden-sergeant reached her stall. Beatrix looked back once, only once, and saw the fire take the striped awning she had sewn herself, the little brass scale she'd haggled for across nine winters, the sign that read BEATRIX VASQUEZ — PAPER CHARMS, NOTHING MORE. The words curled black and lifted away on the heat like a flock of tiny escaping birds.

Nothing more. She had written that herself. She had lived inside that sentence for twelve years, and now it was ash, and she found — walking into the dark with a disgraced singer's hand on her elbow and a dead man's ghost leading the way — that she did not know whether to weep or breathe.

* * *

The alleys behind the chandler's were old Grayhollow, pre-warden Grayhollow, the bones of the city before it learned to be afraid. Smoke pooled in them like fog with intentions. Somewhere behind them the market roared; ahead there was only the ghost, gliding between fallen crates and rusted drainpipes, patient as a lighthouse.

"He knows where he's going," Dov murmured. He had taken up the rear, walking backward half the time, watching the mouths of cross-alleys the way other men watch weather. "That's the third turn he's flagged before we needed it. There's a patrol coming up Rope Street, by the way. Left, left, through the arch."

"How do you—"

"I hear boots. Also I hear them saying 'the cartographer,' which I'm guessing is new and unpleasant for you."

Beatrix's stomach dropped through the cobblestones. *The cartographer.* Not the charm-seller. Not Vasquez, nothing-more. Someone had told them who she was. Which meant someone had known, which meant her twelve years had been thinner than she'd ever let herself believe — a curtain, not a wall.

They cut through the arch. The patrol's torchlight slid past the gap behind them like a searchlight across water, and the ghost stopped before a wall.

Not a remarkable wall. Brick, soot-stained, mortared sometime in the last century, absolutely solid. The ghost put his hand against it and looked back at Beatrix, and though he had no breath and no voice, she heard the question anyway.

"There's nothing there," Sera said flatly.

"There's something there," Dov said. He had pressed his palm flat to the brick and was frowning. "Draft. Feel it. There's airflow, and airflow means space, and space means—"

"The tram tunnel." Beatrix stepped forward, heart hammering. Thirty years ago, before the Wardens, before the Ordinance, Grayhollow had run trams beneath its hills, and when magic was outlawed the tunnels were sealed — not bricked, *sealed*, warded shut by the same hymns and irons that now soaked the fire eating her market. She had mapped their ghosts a hundred times in her head, the way she mapped everything, because her gift never asked permission. If any passage in this city still remembered being open, it was this one. "The old Eastern line. It runs under the river. Under the mountain's roots, if the maps in my head are right. And they're sealed with—"

"With singing," Sera said quietly.

They all looked at her. The fire-light from the alley mouth painted half her face gold and left the other half in shadow, and Beatrix watched three years of self-imposed silence war across it.

"No," Sera said. "No, absolutely not. You know what happened. The archive door. Forty seals, and I cracked every one of them like an egg, and they took my name off the choir roster and my voice out of every licensed hall in the city. I don't sing *at* walls. I don't sing at anything that doesn't ask for coppers first."

The ghost pointed at the wall. Insistently. Almost rudely.

"Sera." Beatrix kept her voice low, gentle, the voice she used for frightened customers. "Behind us there are men with blessed fire who know my name. Ahead of us is a wall. I don't have another option, and I don't think you came this far because you do either."

Sera stared at the brick. Her jaw worked. When she finally spoke, her voice was barely above the crackle of distant flame. "Three years ago I sang a door open and it was the worst night of my life. Everyone I sang with was disgraced beside me. Do you understand? My voice doesn't open doors. It *unmakes* them. There's a difference."

"I know," Beatrix said. "I've spent twelve years pretending my gift didn't exist either. We're both very good at doors that stay shut." She hesitated, then said the true thing, because the burning city had burned away her talent for anything else. "I'm not asking you to save me. I'm asking if you want to walk through."

For a long moment Sera Vane said nothing. Then she rolled her shoulders back the way Beatrix imagined she once had before ten thousand seats, lifted her chin, and set one gloved hand against the mortar.

"If this wakes the whole Warden garrison," she said, "I am blaming the ghost."

The ghost, obligingly, bowed.

She began so softly that Beatrix thought at first it was the wind in the tunnel-mouth — a single held note, low and warm, more felt than heard, settling into the brickwork like water into a dry cloth. Then a second note joined it, a third, and Beatrix felt the hairs on her arms rise, felt her teeth ache gently, felt the world lean. It was not a song in any language she knew. It was older than language, or younger — a song shaped like the memory of opening, of a door deciding to be a doorway.

The bricks shivered.

Mortar dust sifted down like gray snow. Between one note and the next, the wall lost its conviction — the bricks slid apart with a groan like a sleeper turning over, revealing a black archway beyond, rimmed with thirty years of undisturbed dark, exhaling air that smelled of river-stone and rust and deep, deep distance.

Sera stopped. Staggered. Dov caught her elbow.

"Oh," she said, and laughed, a broken, astonished laugh. "Oh, it still *works*. Three years and it still works, and I hate that it works, and—"

"Later," Dov said, but gently. "Celebrate in the murder-tunnel."

They went through, all four of them — the living three and the dead one — and behind them the wall drew itself closed again like a healing wound, shutting out the glow of the burning market, the shouts, the twelve-year sentence Beatrix had served on herself and called safety.

* * *

The tram tunnel was a throat of dark, and the dark had texture. Beatrix's eyes adjusted slowly: rails furred with rust, tile walls glazed with old advertisements for a city that no longer existed, the occasional skull-smile of a side-passage branching off toward places unmapped by anyone living. The map against her hip had begun to pulse faster now, eager, and when she pulled it out — she couldn't not — the ink had shifted again. New lines crawled along the underground road, drawing the tunnel ahead of them before they walked it, and each new stroke seemed to cost the paper something. Or the something it fed on was not paper.

She didn't want to think about what it fed on. Not yet. Not down here, where her sister's name was suddenly, unbearably close to her tongue.

Dov walked ahead with the ghost, arguing amiably about directions neither of them needed to argue about. Sera drifted beside Beatrix, humming under her breath — not the door-song, something small and private, relearning her own instrument.

"That wasn't a disgrace," Beatrix said quietly. "What you did back there."

Sera was silent long enough that their footsteps filled it. "It felt like the opposite," she said at last. "It felt like coming home to a house that burned down." Then, softer: "Your stall. I'm sorry about your stall."

Beatrix looked back the way they'd come, though the wall and the fire and the whole gray city were sealed behind thirty feet of stone. She thought of her striped awning curling into black birds. She thought of the sentence *nothing more*, burning off its own sign.

"I spent twelve years building something small enough that no one would bother to burn it," she said. "And they burned it anyway. So." She found, to her surprise, that she was almost smiling. "I suppose the lesson is to build something worth the trouble."

Ahead, the ghost stopped at a junction and pointed down — down and inward, toward the mountain's buried root, toward the road the map had drawn in silver. The map hummed against Beatrix's palm, warm as a living thing, and far off in the dark, so faint it might have been the tunnel dreaming, something answered it.

Something vast. Something old. Something that breathed.

"There," the ghost said.

And this time, all three of them heard it.
