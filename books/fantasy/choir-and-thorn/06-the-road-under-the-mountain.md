## 6. The Road Under the Mountain

Dov Fen had spent his whole life learning what things cost.

A stolen apple cost a bruised wrist and a night in the cold. A name given to a debt-broker cost eleven years of running. A man's life — the stranger's life, the ghost's life, though Dov still couldn't say his name because no one had ever told him it — had cost whatever it was that made a man bleed out in a night market pressing a folded map into a stranger's hands like he was handing off something holy.

So when Dov noticed what the map was eating, he recognized the currency immediately.

It happened on the second day under the mountain, or what Dov thought of as the second day, because down here there was no sun and no sky and only Sera's little lantern-flame to mark the hours. They had walked out of Grayhollow's tram tunnel into dark that didn't end, and Beatrix had unrolled the living map across her knees and pressed her palm flat against it, and the ink had moved like something waking up.

That was how it worked. He'd watched her figure it out with growing horror she tried to hide behind a cartographer's calm. The map could draw them a road through the unmapped dark — but it needed material. It needed *her*. Every mile they traveled, the ink drank from her, and what it drank were memories.

He saw the first one go on the second day. She'd been talking about her sister — Lira, her name was Lira, though Dov hadn't known that until then — about how Lira used to braid grass crowns by the river and crown every stone along the bank so none of them would feel left out. Beatrix laughed telling it, a real laugh, rusty as an old gate. And then she stopped mid-sentence. Her hand went to the map where the ink was still settling, wet and gleaming, drawing a fork in the tunnel ahead: two roads, one marked safe, one marked drowned.

"What was I saying?" she asked.

Sera looked at her. Dov looked at the floor.

"Grass crowns," Sera said gently. "You were saying your sister made grass crowns."

Beatrix frowned, reaching for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue. "Did I say why?"

"You said she crowned the stones."

"That sounds like her." But there was a hole in her voice where the rest should have been, and Dov watched her press her fingers to her temple like she could hold the memory in through sheer pressure, and the map at her knees glowed faintly warm, satisfied, fed.

* * *

By the fourth day, Dov had learned the pattern.

The map took memories in order, roughly — not all at once but in layers, the way water takes a shoreline. Small things first. The color of a kitchen wall. A dog's name. Then bigger ones, richer ones, the ones with weight to them, because Dov supposed even magic had taste, and grief was the richest meal there was.

They walked. The tunnels were old beyond old, older than Grayhollow, older than the Wardens' city above, walls carved sometimes with spirals that predated language and sometimes with nothing at all, just raw stone sweating in the dark. The air tasted of iron and deep water. Somewhere far below them, always, a sound like breathing — vast, slow, patient. Sera said it was the mountain dreaming. Dov said nothing, because he'd grown up in the undercity and he knew what breathing meant, and it wasn't dreams.

At each junction, Beatrix would kneel and open the map and give it what it asked for, and the road would bloom across the parchment in lines of gold and blue, and she would read their way forward, and she would lose another piece of herself to pay for it.

She tried to hide it. That was the worst part. She'd go quiet after each feeding, sit very straight, catalog her own head like inventory. *I remember my mother's hands. I remember the smell of the shop. I remember—* and sometimes the list came up short and she'd have to fake her way past it, and Dov, who had built a whole life on noticing what people pretended not to feel, watched her do it six times before he did anything.

The seventh time, he acted.

* * *

Here is what Dov knew about lying: it was a tool, same as a lockpick. You didn't use it for cruelty. You used it to open doors that shouldn't be closed.

The junction ahead split three ways, and Beatrix knelt with the map already unrolled, and Dov crouched beside her and put his finger on the left-hand passage before the ink finished drying.

"This one," he said. "I know this stretch. Ran contraband through it when I was fourteen. It comes up behind the flooded stair."

It wasn't true. He had never been this deep; nobody from the surface had. The left passage was marked on the map in a thin uncertain line, which meant it was a guess, which meant it might kill them.

But the right-hand passage was drawn thick and confident, glowing fresh, and Dov had done the arithmetic over the last day of walking: the more certain the route, the more the map had drunk to draw it. The thin lines cost less. The guesses were cheap. So he started feeding her cheap roads and calling them certain, and every time she gave the map a memory, he made sure it was a small one.

"The left," Beatrix repeated, doubtful. "Are you sure? It looks barely mapped."

"Flooded stair, collapsed archway, then a long gallery with white stone." He recited it like scripture, inventing as he went, watching her face. "Trust me. I've never gotten lost underground in my life. It's the one thing I'm good at."

It was a lie stacked on a lie — he'd gotten lost plenty — but she smiled, tired, and took the left passage, and paid the map a memory of a rainy market day and the smell of chestnuts, small coin, small coin, and Dov felt something in his chest ease by a single degree.

Behind them, the ghost flickered.

Dov glanced back. The stranger's ghost had been fading since the third day — he'd started solid as any living man, gray-edged but present, and now he was translucent as breath on glass, and the deeper they went the thinner he got, like the mountain itself was drinking him too. He walked at the rear, always, keeping watch over their backs, and when Dov caught his eye the ghost shook his head slowly: *don't tell her.*

*Don't tell her,* Dov translated. Not about me. About the map. About what it costs.

But the ghost's eyes went to Beatrix's back, and then to Dov, and something passed between them that Dov chose to read as *thank you*, because he was tired of owing and wanted, just once, to be owed something instead.

* * *

On the sixth day, Sera figured it out.

She was sharper than people gave her credit for — a choir-singer who'd survived disgrace in a city that outlawed music-that-mattered had to be. She'd been humming to keep time, one of her tavern songs, and Beatrix had joined in without thinking, and then stopped, because the song was one Lira used to sing, and Beatrix couldn't remember learning it.

Sera caught the shape of the silence the way she caught wrong notes. Later, at a rest stop, while Beatrix slept with the map rolled tight beneath her head like a pillow, Sera sat down next to Dov and said, quietly, "How many?"

Dov kept whittling his stick. "What?"

"How many has it taken. Don't insult me, thief. I've watched her check her own pockets all week. Only the pockets are inside her skull."

Dov turned the stick over. The firelight moved on his knife. "More than she knows," he said finally. "Fewer than it wants."

"And you've been steering us down the thin roads."

"Guessing, mostly. Lying, some." He shrugged. "Costs her less."

Sera was quiet a moment. Down the tunnel, the mountain breathed its slow vast breath. "You know that's not mercy," she said. "Right? Letting her spend herself blind. That's just theft with better manners."

"I know." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "But if I tell her, she'll stop using the big memories, and the map will starve, and we'll be lost down here forever, and her soul stays in a lantern under this rock either way. At least this way—" He stopped. Started again. "At least this way some part of her gets home. Even if it's a smaller her than the one that left."

Sera looked at him for a long moment, firelight in her eyes, and then she reached over and squeezed his shoulder once, hard, the way you grip a rope. "Then we carry it with her," she said. "Whatever she loses, we remember it for her. That's what a company's for. You don't let someone pay alone. You split the debt."

She stood, went to where Beatrix slept, and began, very softly, to sing — the lullaby the map had hummed at her, the one Beatrix's mother must have sung, note for note, so that even if Beatrix lost the original, the copy would live in someone else's throat.

Dov sat in the dark and whittled and listened, and found that his eyes were stinging, and blamed the tunnel dust.

* * *

On the eighth day, the road ended at a door.

Not a door like doors above — no wood, no hinges. A seam in the mountain itself, tall as a cathedral, veined with light the color of lantern-glass, and through the seam came warmth and music and the smell of honey and something underneath it, faint and wrong, like flowers rotting in a beautiful room.

Beatrix unrolled the map one last time. Dov watched her brace herself — he saw her jaw set, saw her choose, deliberately, the biggest memory she had left, because the door deserved it, because they were close now and she would not arrive at her own soul's prison having paid in pennies.

"Lira," she whispered, giving it up willingly, "in the hospital bed, holding my hand, telling me not to cry because—" and the ink flared gold, and the seam shuddered and began to open, and Beatrix's face went smooth and puzzled, like a woman who had woken from a dream she almost remembered.

"Because?" Sera prompted softly.

"I don't know," Beatrix said. "Was there a Lira?"

And Dov stepped forward and took her arm, and Sera took her other side, and between them they walked her toward the opening light while the ghost — nearly transparent now, a man-shaped absence with eyes full of old grief — drifted ahead of them through the seam, into the court beneath the mountain, where somewhere in the dark a king made of dusk turned his head as if he'd felt them coming.

"Stay close," Dov murmured to Beatrix. "We've got you. Whatever you're missing — we brought it with us."

The door closed behind them like a held breath finally let go.
