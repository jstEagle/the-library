## 7. The Court Beneath

Sera Vane had spent three years learning what her voice could not do.

It could not sing in the Warden's cathedral, where the acoustics had once loved her. It could not sing above a whisper in any room with a sealed door, because sealed doors had opinions, and hers had a way of arguing with them. It could not, she had told herself a thousand times in a thousand cheap rooms, be trusted.

But it could do this.

The tunnel mouth had been singing back at them for an hour before they understood it was a tunnel at all. The stone ahead of them gave off a low, resonant hum, and when Sera matched it — barely, a thread of tone pushed through closed lips — the hum changed. It leaned toward her. The rock parted the way a crowd parts for a funeral.

"Stop doing that," Dov said, from somewhere behind her in the dark. His voice was steady, but his boots had stopped making noise, which meant he was frightened. Dov made no noise when he was frightened. "You're going to wake something up."

"Everything down here is already awake," Sera said. "It's just been waiting for someone to say so."

The map had led them true, or as true as a living thing could lead. Beatrix walked at the front with it cradled against her chest like a sleeping child, and Sera watched the firelight move across her face and thought: *she has been paying for this the whole way down.* She had seen it in the way Beatrix paused at nothing, reaching for a memory that wasn't there — a name, a face, a sister's laugh — and found smooth blank stone instead. She had said nothing. There was nothing yet she could do. But she had begun, quietly, to compose.

That was the other thing her voice could do, the thing no Warden had ever banned because no Warden had known it: she could build a song out of anything. Out of guilt. Out of a ghost growing fainter with every mile. Out of a woman trading pieces of herself for a road.

The tunnel opened, and Sera forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was not dark. That was the first wrongness. Sera had expected dark, had expected the wet black of the undercity tunnels, but the cavern opened above them like a sky that had been told about skies only secondhand. It glowed. Thousands — no, tens of thousands — of small lights hung in the vaulted dark, suspended on silver chains that descended from heights the eye could not follow, and each light burned inside a globe of glass no larger than a heart.

Lanterns. A sky made of lanterns.

And beneath them, a city. Not ruins — a court. Bridges of pale stone arched over streets that had never seen weather, and the buildings rose in slender spires carved with figures that seemed to move when looked at sideways. Water ran in channels along the roads, but it ran upward, climbing the spires in silver threads and vanishing. There were people, or the shapes of people, moving through the streets with the unhurried grace of a dream. Their clothes were beautiful. Their faces, when one turned to look at the intruders, were smooth with a patience so complete it was worse than hostility.

"Beautiful," Dov said flatly, "and wrong. Both things. Don't forget the second one."

"I never forget the second one," Sera said. "The second one is the only thing I've ever been good at remembering."

The ghost drifted ahead of them, and Sera saw him clearly now, or as clearly as a fading thing can be seen. He had been growing thinner since the tunnels, his edges going soft, his outline losing its argument with the air. But here, in this light, he looked almost solid again, and she wondered — with a small, cold horror — whether the lanterns recognized him. Whether he had bargains here too.

He raised one translucent hand and pointed down the great avenue toward a structure at the cavern's heart: a hall of black stone crowned with a spire that wept. Water fell from its peak in a slow, endless ribbon, and where it struck the ground it did not splash but *sounded*, each drop ringing out a note, and the notes together were a song Sera knew.

Her mother's lullaby. The same one the map had hummed to her in the tavern, the one that had cracked her refusal open like an egg.

"They're playing it," she whispered. "They're playing it to us."

"No," Beatrix said quietly. Her face was pale in the lantern-light, and her arms were tight around the map. "They're playing it to *you*. This whole place is a door, Sera. Everything down here wants to be let in."

They walked. There was nothing else to do. The people of the court watched them pass without turning their heads, which was somehow worse than staring, and the lanterns overhead — Sera made herself look up, made herself see — the lanterns were not lights.

They were souls. You could tell if you looked long enough, if you let yourself see the way each globe held something that moved like a person moves in sleep, turning, reaching, settling. Some burned steady and warm. Some guttered. One, near enough to read, flickered in a rhythm Sera recognized from a hundred sickbeds: the rhythm of someone calling a name over and over and being answered by no one.

Her voice rose in her throat before she decided to let it. Not a song — a sound, low and fierce, the sound she made when a sealed door needed to know it was sealed no longer. The nearest lanterns brightened. All down the avenue, in a spreading wave, the souls turned toward her like flowers toward a sun that had finally come.

The street went silent. The patient people stopped walking.

And the doors of the black hall opened.

* * *

The Grieving King was not what Sera expected, which was, she supposed, the point of him.

He sat on a throne of woven silver at the heart of the hall, beneath the spire that wept its endless lullaby, and he was beautiful the way the court was beautiful — completely, and wrongly. He was young and old at once, his face unlined but his eyes carrying the specific exhaustion of someone who has watched a very long dusk and expects no dawn. His crown was made of glass, and inside it, small flames moved. Dragon-fire, Sera thought, though she had no way of knowing how she knew. It looked like the memory of warmth rather than warmth itself.

He did not rise. He did not speak at first. He simply looked at Beatrix with an expression so raw and so human that Sera's composer's heart broke a little — it was the look of a man seeing a letter he had written decades ago and never sent.

"You brought it back," the King said. His voice filled the hall the way her voice filled a room, but with a grief instead of a music. "Twelve years I have listened for it. Twelve years, and it walks in wearing a coat."

"It's not yours," Beatrix said. Her voice was steady. Sera admired her enormously and feared for her completely.

"Nothing here is mine," the King said, and there was such terrible sincerity in it that Sera understood this was the most dangerous thing about him. "That is the whole of my kingdom. I only keep what others have surrendered. I only light the dusk with what the living chose to sell." He gestured, one pale hand, and the hall's far wall — Sera had thought it a wall — resolved into shelves, and the shelves into thousands upon thousands of lanterns, receding into dark. "Every one a bargain kept. Every one a choice someone made because the world gave them no better price. I do not steal, cartographer. I *receipt*."

"You kept mine," Beatrix said.

"You sold it," the King said gently. "There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of the law beneath the mountain. But." He leaned forward, and the dragon-fire in his glass crown flared, and for one moment his face was only a man's face, tired and hungry and alone. "I am not without mercy. I am, in fact, famous for it. So here is my offer, made once, in front of your witnesses."

He extended his hand, and a lantern descended from the dark above on its silver chain, slowly, slowly, and came to rest in the air before Beatrix.

It was small. Its glass was old, its flame the color of candlelight at a childhood window. And inside it, turning in her sleep, reaching, settling, was a girl of nineteen with dark hair and an expression Sera knew from the taverns and the market stalls and every mile of the road down: the expression of someone who has just paid too much and is telling herself it was worth it.

Beatrix made a sound Sera had never heard from her. Not a word. Something older than words.

"Your soul," the King said, "as it was on the day you sold it. Unspent. Unaged. Whole." His voice softened into something that was almost kind, which was the cruelest register Sera had ever heard a voice take. "And all I ask in return is the map. The last memory of the last dragon-fire. Give it to me, and the girl goes home with you — not to Grayhollow, not to your attic, but *home*, to the summer she sold it, with her sister still alive in the next room and every unmapped road still ahead of her. You will not even remember this place. You will simply be nineteen, and whole, and it will be a good year."

The hall was silent except for the weeping spire, ringing out its lullaby.

Sera looked at Beatrix and saw the temptation take her the way a tide takes a swimmer — not suddenly, not violently, but completely. She saw her eyes go soft. She saw her arms loosen around the map. She saw her take one step toward the lantern, then another, and the girl inside the glass turned toward her, and pressed one small hand flat against the inside of the glass, the way children press their hands to windows.

*That's you*, Sera thought, singing silently, desperately, a note without a voice. *That's who you were. It isn't who you are.*

The ghost moved. He drifted between Beatrix and the lantern, faint as breath on glass, and he pointed — first at Beatrix, then at the map, then at the road they had come by. *You. This. Forward.* His face, what was left of it, was pleading.

But it wasn't the ghost that stopped her. It was Dov, who had gone very still by the door, and then said, in the flat, careful voice he used when a mark was watching:

"The lanterns are going out."

Sera looked up. He was right. All along the great shelves, here and there, a soul-light had begun to gutter — not the natural flicker of flame, but the stutter of something being *drawn on*, the way a candle gutters when a door opens somewhere and the draft finds it.

The King's eyes did not follow Dov's gaze. That was the tell. That was the thing Sera would carry up out of the mountain and sing about for the rest of her life: the Grieving King heard his kingdom falter, and he did not look, because he already knew, because something down in the dark beneath his beautiful court was burning through its last fuel, and every bargain in every lantern hung from that one flame.

"Consider my offer," the King said, and his voice was still gentle, and his hands were folded, and he did not look up. "You may keep the lantern with you tonight. Sleep beside your soul, cartographer. In the morning, we will speak again."

The lantern drifted into Beatrix's hands. The girl inside turned over in her sleep, and her small hand left a print on the glass that did not fade.

And far below the hall, so deep that Sera felt it in her teeth rather than heard it, something enormous exhaled — a long, ragged, failing breath, like a fire that has been burning for a thousand years and has finally, finally begun to go out.
