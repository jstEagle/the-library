## 9. The Last Dragon-Fire

Dov Fen had stolen a great many things in his life — purses, rings, a silver candlestick off a Warden's own altar, once, and the Warden's boots besides — but he had never stolen anything from a sleeping king.

The court beneath the mountain made it easy to forget that theft was possible here at all. Everything gleamed. Everything was beautiful in the way of things that had never been told *no*: archways of black stone threaded with veins of dim gold, corridors that curved when you weren't looking so that you always arrived somewhere slightly different than you intended. Lanterns hung from ceilings that might have been sky, each one holding a soft amber light, and Dov had learned on their first terrified walk through these halls not to look too closely at what drifted inside the glass. Sometimes it looked like smoke. Sometimes it looked like a face.

Tonight the lanterns flickered. All of them. Out of rhythm, like a heartbeat losing its place.

"That's her," Sera whispered beside him. Her voice, even whispered, did something strange to the air — it bent toward her, listening. "Down there. Under us. She's waking it up just by being here."

"Then we'd better hurry," Dov said, "before whatever's happening down there happens without us."

The ghost walked ahead of them.

It had grown fainter with every mile of the road under the mountain, and now it was barely more than an outline, a man-shaped absence in the amber dark. It still pointed, though. It had been the broker of this court once — that was the thing Dov kept turning over in his head as they moved through the sleeping halls. The stranger whose life had bought his own out of a debt-broker's ledger had spent his living years selling souls to a king, and his death trying to give one back. And now, translucent and nearly gone, he led them through the palace he'd built his fortune on like a man walking home in the dark by memory alone.

He stopped before a door that didn't look like a door. It looked like a seam in the wall, thin as a knife cut, and it was warm. Dov felt the heat of it against his palm before he touched it, and drew back.

"He says the King sleeps at the turning of the dusk," Sera murmured. She had her ear tilted toward the ghost, though Dov knew she couldn't hear him any better than Dov could. She was reading him the way she read everything — by the shape of the silence around him. "He says the whole court dreams with him. That's why the bargains are kept down there instead of up here. Nothing guards the vault because nothing has ever needed to."

"Nothing with a keyhole," Dov said. He crouched, produced his picks, then looked at the seam again and put them away. There were no locks here. There was only stone pretending very hard to be solid.

"Sera," he said. "You want to do the thing?"

She laughed — a small, cracked sound, half fear. Three years since the sealed door of the Warden's hall had split down its seams at the first note of her voice, three years of taverns and thrown coppers and singing only songs that meant nothing. She looked at the ghost, who stood with his fading hands folded, waiting.

"I've been wanting to do the thing for three years," she said.

* * *

Sera Vane had thought, for a long time, that her voice was a wound. That was how they'd framed it in Grayhollow: the singer who broke things, the voice that was banned, the disgrace. She had learned to sing small, to sing shut, to hold every note like a bird with clipped wings.

Standing before the seam in the mountain's heart, she unclipped the wings.

She began low, lower than the tavern crowds ever heard her go — a note that was less a sound than a permission. The stone listened. She could feel it listening, feel the mountain leaning in like a great animal hearing its name spoken kindly for the first time in centuries. She sang the door open the way you'd sing a child awake: gently, and without apology, and with the absolute certainty that it would open.

The seam widened. Gold light spilled out, but it was not the pale amber of the lanterns above — it was deeper, redder, older. Heat rolled over her face like breath.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it's *real*."

The vault below them was vast beyond sense, a hollowed chamber where the mountain's roots should have been, and it was filled with glass. Thousands upon thousands of lanterns hung in tiers descending into darkness, each one burning with a soul's small stubborn light — bargains, all of them, every cruel little contract the Grieving King had ever struck, kept alight by the single flame at the very bottom of everything.

And there it sat: the last dragon-fire.

It was smaller than Sera had imagined, a coil of flame no larger than a hearth's worth, guttering in a basin of black iron, dimmed to embers wrapped around one exhausted tongue of red-gold fire. A thousand years of feeding every bargain in the court, and this was what remained of something that had once, she understood without being told, filled skies.

"It's dying," Dov said behind her, hushed. "It's been dying this whole time, keeping all this lit."

"No," Sera said, and her voice came out in harmonies she hadn't chosen, the vault's acoustics catching her words and giving them back grander than she'd meant them. "It's *waiting*. There's a difference. Ask anyone in a tavern."

They went down together, stair after stair through tiers of glowing souls, and the heat rose to meet them, and the nearer they came to the basin, the more the surrounding lanterns flickered — nervous, Sera thought, or hopeful, the way candles lean when a window opens.

The dragon-fire lifted its head when they arrived. It had a way of moving that suggested eyes it did not have. It regarded them for a long moment, guttering.

Then it turned its attention upward — through all that stone, through all that dusk — toward the room where Beatrix Vasquez waited with her soul in a glass lantern and a map full of memories in her hands.

"She's coming," Sera said gently, kneeling at the basin's rim. "She chose us. She's choosing you too, right now, whether she knows it yet or not." She swallowed. "But she can't choose anything if the King wakes first. So we have to do our part. Can you hear me?"

The flame leaned toward her voice the way the mountain had. Everything leaned toward her voice; it had only ever been a question of whether she trusted what it did next.

So she sang the fire awake.

Not a lullaby this time. Not a door-song either, nothing so small as opening. She sang the way the choir-masters had wept over when she was seventeen, before Grayhollow decided wonder was a crime — she sang dawn, sang the first morning after a long dark, sang *rise*, and the last dragon-fire caught each note like kindling.

It grew. Slowly at first, a tongue strengthening, then faster, red going gold going white at the heart, heat washing the tiers of lanterns in waves, and all around them ten thousand souls burned brighter, steadier, as if remembering what they were fuel for. The vault roared softly with light. Somewhere far above, Sera felt rather than heard the mountain shudder — the King's dusk flickering at its edges.

"Dov," she called over the growing rush. "*Now.*"

* * *

Dov had found the lantern two tiers up, exactly where the ghost had pointed before fading back against the dark like breath on cold glass. It hung apart from the others, on a hook of its own, as if even the court had known better than to store it among common stock.

Inside the glass, the light was unmistakably a person. Small, curled, flickering — and familiar, somehow, in the set of the shoulders, the tilt of the head. Nineteen years old and terrified, doing arithmetic with her own existence because magic always demands a price and her sister was dying.

Beatrix Vasquez, sold. Twelve years of ink and silence and harmless charms, and here was the part of her that had been held in pawn the entire time.

Dov pressed his palms flat against the lantern glass and felt it hum.

"You don't know me," he told it quietly. "That's fine. I didn't know you either, not really — a woman who sells charms and draws maps and looks at everyone like she's memorizing the exits. But a dead man bought my life with his, and he spent his last breath putting yours back where it belongs, so." He laughed, shaky. "I'm tired of owing. You understand? This is me paying."

Behind and below him, Sera's song reached its height, and the dragon-fire stood up in its basin — truly stood, a pillar of white-gold flame tall as the vault itself, and every lantern in the chamber flared at once, and the seams in the walls began to sing in answer, and Dov understood that the whole court was one held breath about to be released.

He lifted the lantern high.

"For the broker who couldn't forgive himself," he said. "For the sister. For the twelve years." He grinned, fierce and frightened and free. "And mostly, Beatrix, for you."

He hurled it down into the fire.

The glass burst mid-fall, and the light inside it — the small curled nineteen-year-old soul of Beatrix Vasquez — came out like a spark leaving a bonfire, and the dragon-fire received it, and *knew* it, and for one impossible instant the entire vault was silent, every flame stilled, every soul holding its breath.

Then the first lantern shattered.

Then the second, and the tenth, and the hundredth — bargains unraveling all at once, every contract the King had ever struck tearing loose from the flame that had enforced them, souls bursting free of their glass like birds from a broken coop, rising in rivers of light up through the splitting stone. The vault groaned. The tiers collapsed into cascades of glittering shards. Above them, muffled through a thousand feet of mountain, Dov heard the sound he'd been dreading and rejoicing in equally: the Grieving King, waking, screaming.

Sera grabbed his arm. Her face was streaked with tears and lit white-gold, and she was laughing.

"The court's coming down," Dov shouted over the roar. "We have to get to Beatrix!"

The dragon-fire coiled down out of its pillar and became, briefly, something with wings — a shape of flame vast and brief as a struck match, a memory of flight — and it turned toward the stairs, toward the upper halls, toward the room where a cartographer waited, and it inclined its burning head to them both.

*Follow*, it seemed to say, in a language older than names.

And then it poured upward through the collapsing dark like dawn through a torn curtain, and Dov Fen ran after it, toward the King, toward Beatrix, toward everything.
