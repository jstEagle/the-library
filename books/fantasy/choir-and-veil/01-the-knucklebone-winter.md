## 1. The Knucklebone Winter

The knucklebone had been in the pot three days when it started to sing.

Theo Reyes pulled it out with tongs, set it on the scarred pine workbench, and waited for the voice to settle. They always took a while — the newly dead especially. They came in loud, all their unfinished business still boiling off them like steam off a butcher's carcass, and the first hours were nothing but grief and confusion and names repeated over and over until Theo wanted to drive an awl through his own ears.

This one had quieted faster than most. That was usually a bad sign. The quiet ones had something to say.

"Theo," the bone said, in Maren Kost's dry paper voice. "Theo, are you listening."

"I'm listening." He turned the knucklebone under the lamp. It was a good specimen — dense, yellow-white, no hairline fractures for the voice to leak out of sideways. He'd bought it off her son for two coppers and a promise, which was how he bought everything these days. *A keepsake for the family,* that was what he told people. *Something she can rest in.* Nobody in Greyhollow needed to know the rest was less like sleep and more like being trapped in a small room with a talkative stranger.

Except they did know. That was the whole point of him.

"She's fading," the bone said — Maren talking about herself, which was a habit of the dead that Theo had never gotten used to. They spoke about their own dissolution the way travelers spoke about weather. "Another day, maybe two. Carve me before I go stupid."

"You won't go stupid."

"I'm already going stupid. Yesterday I couldn't remember my sister's face. Today I couldn't remember if I had a sister." A pause, the particular crackling silence of a ghost gathering itself. "Carve me into something small. Something she can hold. She has bad hands now, Theo. Arthritis up both wrists. Nothing too fine."

He picked up his finest gouge and began.

The workshop was one room above the chandler's on Drover's Lane, twelve feet by twenty, and it held more dead than the Greyhollow cemetery did. Not whole dead — pieces. Knucklebones and fingerbones, slivers of rib, one entire patella that had belonged to a ferryman and never stopped complaining about water. Each carved piece held its voice the way a bell holds its ring: dormant until struck, then singing out whatever the dead had left unsaid. Theo's gift — his livelihood, his curse, the thing that made him both indispensable and quietly avoided at market — was that he could shape the bone so the voice came out gentle. Shaped right, a grandmother could tell her granddaughter where she'd buried the deed to the orchard without screaming once about the cold.

Shaped wrong — or shaped honest, depending who you asked — a piece of bone would tell you exactly how it felt to die.

Maren Kost watched him work. He could feel her attention the way you feel sun through a window. She'd been a wool merchant in her living years, sharp-eyed and sharp-tongued, and death hadn't softened her so much as slowed her down, like a blade going from a whetstone to a strop.

"You've lost weight," she said.

"Everyone says that."

"Because everyone can see it. You're carving like a man with debts."

He didn't answer. The gouage whispered along the bone's curve, raising curls of dust that smelled faintly of the lime pot. Outside, Greyhollow went about its evening under four feet of old snow: sled runners creaking, someone beating a rug, the smith's hammer keeping its patient time. A town of nine hundred souls folded into a mountain fold like a coin dropped in a pocket, and every soul of them knew what Theo was and what he cost and mostly kept their distance except when they needed him.

That was neutrality. That was the trade. He carved for anyone — smugglers and magistrates, the pious and the profane — and took no side in anything, because a man who could hear the dead could hear things that got other men killed, and the only reason Theo Reyes had lived to thirty-four was that nobody had ever been sure whose side he was on. Including, most days, himself.

"Sefa Marun came by yesterday," Maren said. "While you were at the potter's."

His hands didn't stop, but they wanted to. "Did she leave a message?"

"She left a look. Sat in that chair for an hour and looked at your ledger where you keep it under the loose board like it's a secret." The ghost made a sound that might have been laughter in a throat that still worked. "One-eyed women don't sit for an hour, boy. She's counting the days on her debt."

"I know what she is."

"Do you? Because I remember her brother, and I remember how he ended, and I remember that Sefa does not forgive and does not forget and writes everything down in that little book of hers." Maren's voice thinned, frayed at the edges. Death was eating her from the outside in now; another day and there wouldn't be enough of her left to hold a grudge. "Pay her, Theo. Sell the ferryman's kneecap if you have to. Debts to smugglers don't gather interest. They gather knives."

"I'll pay her."

"Liar." But it was soft, almost fond. "Carve faster."

* * *

He worked until the lamp burned low, and what came out of the knucklebone was not beautiful, because Maren had forbidden beauty. It was a small rounded thing, fist-sized, smooth as a river stone, sized for arthritic hands. When he set it in the light and tapped it twice against the bench — the striking, the waking — Maren's voice came out even and unhurried, with none of the static of dying that had fuzzed her all week.

"There's a false bottom in the cedar chest. The deed, the wedding silver, and forty crowns in mixed coin. Tell Anja the combination of my heart is her name, which she'll understand, and which will make her cry, and I want that. I want her to cry exactly once, and then get angry, and then take the money and leave this town, because—" and here the ghost paused, and Theo felt the weight of the last thing coming the way you feel thunder before it arrives, "—because there's a war coming down those passes, whatever the council says, and Greyhollow is on the wrong side of it, and the crown lies, Theo. The crown lies. Tell her the crown lies."

The workshop went still. Even the ferryman's patella, which never shut up, held its peace.

"Maren," Theo said carefully. "What do you mean, the crown lies?"

But she was guttering, spending her final substance on that one message the way the dying spend their last coins, and all he got back was a whisper thin as wind under a door: *the crown lies, the crown lies*, and then, fainter, almost amused: *well. At least I said it.*

And the bone was just bone.

He sat with it a long time. This was the part of the trade nobody paid for — the after, the empty room where a voice used to be. He wrapped the keepsake in wool, wrote ANJA KOST on it in his careful carver's letters, and set it in the delivery box by the door, where it sat among six other finished pieces waiting for families too frightened or too proud to come collect them.

Then he climbed the ladder to his sleeping loft, did not sleep, and listened instead to the rumors that had blown into town with the last caravan before the passes closed.

They were all versions of the same story. In the south, the free cities were arguing — councils and leagues and merchant princes, each certain that compromise would save them and war would ruin them. And in the capital, King Aldric — who had ruled for thirty years, who had ended the Weeping Plague, who had been photographed and painted and sung about until he was less a man than a mural — had stopped appearing in public. A regent spoke in his name. Edicts came sealed with the crown's mark but written in a new hand. And the Graycloaks, the king's own watch, had doubled in number and started asking questions in towns like Greyhollow: *who travels south, who trades with the free cities, who remembers the old songs.*

Nobody in the taproom had said the word *war*. Mountain people didn't waste words on things they could avoid naming. But Maren Kost, who had died of her heart in her own bed like a respectable woman, had spent her last breath on a warning, and the dead did not waste breath. The dead had nothing left to be polite with.

"The crown lies," Theo said to the dark ceiling.

He thought about his ledger under the floorboard. Fourteen crowns owed to Sefa Marun, plus the interest she swore she wasn't charging, plus the unspoken debt of a favor from nine years ago that she had never once mentioned, which was worse than any interest. He thought about the passes closing — they closed every winter, but this year the closure would have Graycloak seals on it, Graycloak inspectors at the Salt Stair, and Sefa's blind paths would become worth more than gold, and she would collect accordingly.

He thought about staying neutral. He was good at it. He'd built a life out of it the way other men built houses, plank by careful plank.

Below him, in the workshop, something ticked.

It was a soft sound, familiar, almost domestic: bone settling in the lime pot, ice contracting in the water jar, the chandler downstairs stacking tallow. He knew every sound that room made. He lay still and catalogued them, one by one, the way he catalogued voices, and found them all accounted for except one — a faint, rhythmic resonance, like a bell struck very far away, coming not from the pot or the jars but from the wall of finished pieces in their wool wrappings, the six uncollected keepsakes, each one holding its dead.

They were humming.

Not speaking. Humming — a low chord, three voices and four, rising and falling together like breathing, like a congregation finding a hymn's key. Theo had heard bones do many things in fourteen years of this trade. He had never heard them harmonize.

He came down the ladder slowly. The workshop was blue with snowlight. He crossed to the shelf, lifted the first wrapping, and touched the little bird carved from a child's fingerbone — a keepsake for a mother who couldn't yet bear to hear what her son had said at the end.

The hum deepened under his fingertips. Not words. Not quite. More like the feeling of words, the shape of meaning pressing against the inside of the bone, the way a moth presses against glass. And underneath the hum, so faint he might have invented it, a single syllable repeating in a language he didn't know, older than the trade, older than Greyhollow, older than anything the dead in his care had ever carried.

Outside, the wind shifted, and the first flakes of a whiteout came hissing against the shutters.

Through the storm, faint and impossible, Theo heard the chandler's dog begin to bark at something on Drover's Lane — bark and then stop barking and start to whimper, the way dogs do at things that are neither wolf nor man.

He stood in his workshop full of quiet dead, and for the first time in fourteen years, he was afraid of what might knock on his door.
