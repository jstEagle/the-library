## 2. A Child at the Door

The storm came down off the passes two days early, the way debts and bad news always did.

Theo heard it before he saw it — a pressure in his back teeth, a low complaint from the shelf of jawbones he'd carved in October. Weather spoke through bone if you listened long enough. Snow meant silence coming, a muffled hush over Greyhollow that would quiet even the dead for a few blessed hours, because the living stayed indoors and stopped walking over their ancestors.

He was wrong about that. The snow didn't quiet anything. It brought the thing that would end his quiet forever, though he wouldn't know it until the door opened.

He spent the morning on the knucklebone. The woman's ghost — Maren Ostrek, dyer, dead nine days of a fall on the ice stairs — had finally gone hoarse with repeating herself, and her last words had settled into something Theo could work with: *tell my sister the blue cloth wasn't stolen.* He carved the confession into the bone in letters small as grain, sealed it with wax, and set the keepsake on the finished pile between a child's first tooth mounted in silver and a widow's ring carved from her husband's heel-bone. Honest work. Clean grief. The kind of transaction that never touched the war everyone in the lowlands kept whispering about.

"You can rest now," he told the empty room, because Maren's ghost had thinned to almost nothing after the carving, the way they always did. Given away. Spent. "Whatever's next, you go there lighter."

Nothing answered. There never was an answer, only the fading warmth of a voice leaving. People in town thought his gift must be company. They didn't understand it was more like being a ferryman — everyone who crossed wanted something, and none of them stayed, and he stood on the water alone between every crossing.

The light went gray-green around noon. Then the wind arrived like something thrown.

By three o'clock the whiteout had swallowed the street outside his window. Greyhollow sat high enough that winter storms came sideways here, ice riding the wind, and this one had teeth. Theo banked the stove, lit the second lamp, and tried not to count how much lamp oil he owed the coopers' factor for. Tried not to think about the other debt either — Sefa Marun's name in his ledger, four hundred crowns against a favor she hadn't yet decided to collect. Smugglers were patient creditors. That was the frightening part.

He was reaching for the next piece of stock — a femur, donor unknown, bought lawful from the anatomist in Vessany — when the bones began to ring.

Not speak. *Ring.*

Every carved piece on every shelf took up a tone at once, a chord, high and clear, like someone had dragged a bow across all of them together. The femur hummed in his hand so hard he nearly dropped it. In eleven years of hearing the dead whisper and mutter and beg, Theo Reyes had never once heard bone *sing*.

He put it down. The chord held. And underneath it, worse than the ringing, he noticed what was missing: the dead themselves. Gone. All of them. The low murmur of Greyhollow's recent dead that lived under his hearing like riverbed water — silenced as if a hand had closed over the whole town.

His breath fogged the window. Outside, nothing. White on white, the street lamps drowned, the mountain gone.

Then, through the white, a small dark shape came up his path.

* * *

He watched it come and could not make sense of it. A child, small, walking steadily into wind that had knocked grown men flat an hour ago. Barefoot. Bareheaded, bare-armed, wearing some kind of shift that wasn't wool or linen or anything he could name. The snow did not land on her. It bent around her, the way water bends around a stone in a stream, and where she stepped the ground behind her showed bare earth for a heartbeat before the white rushed back in.

Theo stood with his hand on the latch and did not open it. Every instinct he owned — instincts honed by years of staying out of other people's trouble — said *don't*. Whatever walked unharmed through a killing whiteout was not lost. It was sent.

The knocking nearly made him jump out of his skin, which was absurd; he'd known she'd knock. Small fist, three soft strikes. Polite. As if she were a neighbor come to borrow flour.

Through the door he said, "Go home, child."

No answer. Three more knocks.

"The Ostreks are the third house down. Go there. Knock there."

Silence, except the wind — and under the wind, the chord. The bones were still singing. Louder now. He looked at the shelves and saw dust jumping off the lids of the storage boxes, fine grains dancing in the lamplight, and understood that whatever stood three feet from his door was doing that. Was doing it *to* them.

He opened the door because the alternative was standing in his own workshop listening to that sound until it drove him through the wall, and he told himself afterward, for years, that this was the reason. Not the bare feet. Not the smallness of her.

Cold hit him like a slap. The girl looked up.

Eight years old, maybe. Dark hair cut raggedly, as if someone had done it running. Her eyes were ordinary brown, red-rimmed, exhausted — the most ordinary thing about her, and somehow the most terrible, because they were the eyes of a tired child and everything else said she was not one.

She was shivering. That undid him faster than the magic had. Whatever she was, she was also eight, and cold, though the cold couldn't seem to touch her — the shivering looked almost like memory, a body remembering how it was supposed to respond.

"Inside," Theo said, and stood aside.

She crossed the threshold and the bones went silent.

All of them. At once. Like a bell struck and instantly gripped. Eleven years of murmuring dead, and for the first time since he was a boy kneeling at his mother's deathbed hearing her last words rise up out of her wedding band — for the first time in his life, Theo Reyes stood in absolute silence among the remains of the dead.

It should have been relief. It was the loneliest sound he had ever heard.

* * *

He got her into the chair by the stove, wrapped her in the good blanket, the one he'd been saving to sell, and put water on to warm. She let him do all of it with a stillness that unsettled him more than struggle would have. Children who had been through what she'd clearly been through usually either clung or flinched. She watched. Everything — his hands, the stove, the shelves of bone, the knife rolls hanging by the bench — with the careful attention of someone cataloguing exits.

"Can you talk?" Theo asked. "What's your name?"

Her mouth moved. What came out was not any language he knew.

It wasn't babble. That was the first thing he understood, and the understanding arrived in his sternum before his head. The sounds had grammar. Cadence. Stress falling in deliberate places, syllables shaped with care, a sentence — no, several sentences — delivered patiently, as if surely *this time* he would understand. When he shook his head, she paused, tried again, slower, adjusting something in the structure the way a craftsman adjusts a joint that won't sit.

And the bones rang with every word.

Softly, this time — she seemed to be speaking quietly, almost apologetically — but each syllable lifted a faint harmonic off the shelves. The jawbones hummed. The femur stock trembled. The little silver-mounted tooth chimed like a struck glass. Bone remembered voices; that was the whole of his gift, the whole of his curse, bone holding what the dead left in it. And this child's language was made of exactly the stuff that bone remembered. Speaking it, she plucked every relic in the room like a harpist testing strings.

"Stop," Theo said, and then, gentler, when she flinched: "It's all right. Just — quieter. Please."

She pressed her lips together. The harmonics died. The silence came back, total, enormous, and Theo realized he was shaking.

He made tea she didn't drink and porridge she ate slowly, methodically, all of it, scraping the bowl — hungry, then. Hungry was human. While she ate he worked up the nerve to look at the thing at her throat.

She wore it on a cord: a locket the size of a thumbnail, dull gray-white, carved with a seal he had seen maybe twice in his life and hoped never to see again. The crowned stag of House Aldric. The royal seal of the king across the mountains.

Except royal seal-work was done in seal-bone — the charmed osseous stuff the crown monopolized, cut from sources Theo had heard whispered about but never named — and Theo dealt in bone the way a moneylender deals in coin. He knew what he was looking at from across the room. He leaned close anyway, and the moment his eyes traced the stag's antlers, the locket gave off a single low note, sweet and mournful, and every hair on his arms stood up.

Royal seal-bone. Old. Older than Aldric's reign by centuries, if the resonance was anything to judge by — and it was; resonance never lied to him, that was the one rule of his life that had held. This wasn't a signet struck this generation. This was a thing carried a long way through a great deal of time.

The girl's hand rose and closed over the locket, protective, automatic. First voluntary motion she'd made since sitting down.

"It's all right," Theo lied. "Nobody's taking it."

She searched his face. Whatever she found there — exhaustion, debt, dread, and yes, he supposed, some residue of decency — she apparently decided was sufficient, because her shoulders came down half an inch and she stopped watching the knife rolls.

Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, the silence of the dead lay over everything like deep water. Theo cleaned his tools without cleaning them, moving blades over the strop for the shape of the motion, trying to think.

Here is what he knew. A child walks through an impossible storm untouched. Her language rings bone like a hammer on bronze. She wears the royal seal on old, old bone. And the dead — the dead who had never once, in thirty-eight years, given him a single hour's peace — have fled from her, or been driven out, or been *forbidden*, and he did not know which possibility frightened him most.

Here is what he knew about himself. He had spent the war's whole approach being no one. Neutral. Useful in small ways to small people. He carved griefs for farmers and kept his mouth shut about smugglers' cargo and had never, not once, picked a side, because sides got men like him nailed to doors in the lowland towns. He owed Sefa Marun four hundred crowns and intended to die old and solvent and forgotten.

The girl finished her porridge. Set down the spoon with both hands, precise as a courtier. Then she slid off the chair, crossed the floor, and stood in front of him, looking up, and held out the locket.

"Don't," Theo said, though she hadn't spoken a word of anything. "Keep it. I don't want it."

She insisted. Took his hand — small fingers, warm, impossibly warm after that walk — and pressed the locket into his palm and folded his fingers shut over it. Then she looked at him with those ordinary, exhausted eyes and said one word in the ringing tongue.

The locket sang in his fist. The whole workshop resonated, one vast soft chord, floorboards and rafters and every grave-gift on every shelf, and for a moment Theo felt the chord pass *through* him, felt it find the one piece of bone he carried that no one knew about — the sliver of his mother's fingerbone sewn into his coat lining, the one relic he had never carved, never opened, never dared listen to —

— and then it was over. Silence again. The girl withdrew her hand, satisfied, as though a message had been delivered and receipted.

"I don't understand you," Theo said quietly. "I don't know what you want from me."

She pointed at the locket in his fist. Then at herself. Then she pointed south, through the wall, through the storm, through the mountain itself, toward Thornfell — a place Theo had spent his whole life not thinking about — and made a gesture like walking. Two fingers, patient steps across the table.

Then she looked at the door, and her face changed, and Theo saw fear enter it for the first time — real fear, animal and immediate — though there was nothing outside but white.

Somewhere down the valley, muffled by the roar of the storm, he thought he heard dogs. Or horns. Or both.

The girl turned back to him and took hold of his sleeve. Two fingers, walking south across the table again. Slower this time. A question in it.

Theo Reyes stood in the ringing silence of his workshop, holding a royal seal he could not put down, and understood with perfect clarity that his careful, neutral, forgettable life had just walked out the door into the whiteout and was not coming back.

"Yes," he said, to a child who couldn't understand him, and heard the word cost him everything it was going to cost. "All right. South."

She exhaled — the smallest breath of relief, a child's sigh — and the bones of the dead, silent for the first time in his life, shivered once all together, like a held breath let go.
