## 3. What the Dead Refuse to Say

The girl slept in Theo's bed like a stone dropped into deep water.

He had given her his blanket, a bowl of barley porridge gone cold because she wouldn't eat until he ate first, and the one question he could not stop circling: *how did you get up my mountain in a whiteout with no boots?* She hadn't answered. She didn't seem to understand him at all — or she understood something else entirely, because when he spoke she tilted her head as if listening past his words to whatever lived underneath them.

Now she slept, and Theo sat at his workbench with a lamp turned low and asked his workshop the only question that mattered.

*Who is she?*

The dead of Greyhollow had been silent since she crossed his threshold. Not quiet — silent, the way a forest goes silent when something large moves through it. For six years Theo had lived inside a low murmur of the departed: old Marta complaining about her son's choice of wife from the shelf where her fingerbones sat in a cedar box; the avalanche men who came down off the pass each winter to grumble about the cold they still felt; a hundred small ghosts knuckled into keepsakes and reliquaries, all of them talking, always talking, a tide he'd learned to sleep through the way a harbor master sleeps through surf.

Now nothing. The silence had weight. It pressed on his ears like altitude.

"Marta," he said aloud, because saying it aloud was an old habit, a way of making the gift feel like company instead of affliction. "You've been quiet long enough. Tell me what walked into my house."

Nothing. The cedar box sat where it always sat. If there was a voice behind the bone, it was holding its breath.

He worked through the shelf piece by piece. The avalanche men — three brothers, carved into a locket their sister had never come back for — gave him nothing but a faint ache in his molars, the bone equivalent of a locked door. A child's milk tooth he'd kept out of sentiment rather than trade hummed once and went flat. Even the nameless scraps in his scrap jar, the bits too small to hold much of anyone, felt *absent*, which was a word Theo had never needed before. Dead things were never absent. Dead things were the one constant of his life, more reliable than weather, more reliable than money.

The girl shifted in her sleep and murmured something in that language that wasn't a language — older than any kingdom, older than the bones he worked, a sound that made the lamp flame lean toward her like a man leaning across a table.

And every relic on the shelf shivered at once.

Not resonated. *Shivered.* The way a dog shivers when thunder is still an hour away. Theo stood very still and watched a dead woman's knucklebone — yesterday's commission, the one that had told him *the crown lies* — rock gently against its velvet lining without being touched.

"All right," he said quietly. "That's an answer of a kind."

It told him this: whatever the girl carried, the dead recognized it. And whatever the dead felt about it, it was not comfort. It was the feeling prey has for predator, or servant for king, or — and this thought arrived unwelcome and stayed — the feeling the living have for the dead.

He looked at her again. Eight years old, maybe nine. Bare feet, unchapped. Hair the color of wet slate. In the lamplight her face had the terrible smoothness of a face that had never been afraid of anything small — no fear of the dark, no fear of hunger, no fear of cold. Only, when she'd first looked at him, a fear so large it had no room left for anything lesser.

She was afraid of something the size of a kingdom.

Theo poured himself a finger of plum brandy he couldn't afford and drank it standing up, which was how he took all his worst decisions.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the storm spent, the world outside his window scoured white and blue. Theo left the girl sleeping — she slept like something recharging rather than resting — and went down through Greyhollow's crooked lanes to see if the storm had brought anything besides snow.

It had brought the post rider, half-frozen, and with the post rider a notice.

He saw it pinned to the board outside the Wayhouse before he saw anything else: fresh paper in a town where paper was scarce, ink still sharp, and above the text a seal pressed in gray wax that Theo knew without wanting to. Not the seal of any free city. Not the seal of any kingdom he'd sworn to, because he'd never sworn to one. The seal of the crown — the true crown, the old crown, the one that supposedly sat on a king's head in the far south and had nothing to do with mountain towns like his.

Except now it did.

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN**, the notice read, **RECOVERY OF LOST ASSET. A girl-child, approximately eight years, dark of hair, pale of eye. Answers to no known tongue. Carries property of the royal household. Reward: two hundred crowns silver, payable at any Graycloak garrison. Report all sightings. Concealment of crown property is treason.**

Two hundred crowns. Theo did the arithmetic the way he did everything, grimly and precisely. Two hundred crowns was four years of carving. It was Sefa Marun's debt paid twice over with change left to buy back his mother's ring from the pawnbroker in Vessany. It was a life without winter debts, without smuggler favors accruing interest, without lying awake doing sums against the spring.

*Treason,* the notice said, in letters neat as knife-work.

A knot of townsfolk stood reading it. Old Piet the tanner squinted at the wax seal. "Graycloaks in the passes already? That's south-law. That's got nothing to do with us."

"War's got everything to do with everyone lately," said the post rider, warming his hands. "They sealed Salt Stair yesterday. Whole caravan turned back. Sefa Marun's people are sitting at the bottom of it cursing."

"Looking for this girl, you think?"

"Looking for something." The rider shrugged. "Crown doesn't spend ink on children."

Theo read the notice a third time. *Carries property of the royal household.* He thought of the locket she wore against her breastbone, the one he'd glimpsed when he'd peeled off his coat to wrap her — a locket of bone, and not just any bone. He'd touched it once, briefly, and his gift had flinched like a hand pulled from a flame. Seal-bone. Bone cut from a king's own hand, or made to remember that it had been. Property of the royal household, yes. The way a heart is property of the body it lives in.

He walked home the long way, taking his time, doing a different kind of arithmetic. If he carried the girl down to the garrison at the pass foot — if it even existed yet, if the roads were open — he would be paid, praised, and left alone forever. No more smuggling debts. No more looking over his shoulder at warrants. The Graycloaks wanted order, and order was simple: things belonged to whom the law said they belonged.

And the girl would go wherever crowns send runaway assets. He had seen what happened to assets. Assets were used until used up.

"The crown lies," he said to the empty lane, quoting a dead woman, and heard how thin the words sounded spoken by a living coward.

* * *

That afternoon he tried the dead one last time.

He cleared the bench, set out his oldest pieces — the ones whose voices had been with him longest, worn smooth with handling — and built a small fire though the day wasn't cold. Ritual mattered. His mother had taught him that much before she died, or rather before she stopped speaking to him, which for a boy who heard the dead was its own kind of dying. Ritual was how you asked politely. Politeness was how you survived the asking.

"I need names," he told them. "I need to know what she is. I need to know what's coming down the passes after her. That's all. Names and one honest answer."

Silence.

He picked up the avalanche brothers' locket. Nothing. He held Marta's knucklebone to the firelight and asked her directly, the way you ask an elder, with respect and a little desperation. The bone warmed in his palm and then cooled, and the cooling felt deliberate, like a door closed gently in his face.

Even the dead were choosing sides. Or choosing survival. Theo supposed he couldn't blame them. They had, after all, already lost everything once.

Except one.

He almost missed it, because the voice came from the corner of the workshop he never used — the shelf where he kept the failures, the pieces nobody claimed, the carvings that had come out wrong or whose dead had said nothing worth keeping. Among them hung a small wooden-and-bone charm, a gallows-token, the kind smugglers wore to mock the law that hanged them. Its owner had been a runner named Corvo Hesh, caught two winters back with crown contraband in his saddlebags, hanged from the bridge beam at Greyhollow's edge while the whole town watched because watching was mandatory. Theo had bought the token off the executioner for a bottle, as he bought most of his raw material, and Corvo's ghost had lived in it ever since — a thin, sly, complaining presence, forever grumbling about the cold, the rope, the indignity of it.

Corvo spoke now, and his voice was different. Stripped of its slyness. Quiet as a man in a sickroom.

"Carver." A pause, like a man gathering courage he shouldn't have needed, being already dead. "You should not have let her in."

"Too late for that. What is she?"

"She's—" The voice frayed. Theo gripped the token and poured warmth into it, the way you cup a match in wind. "She's a vessel. You know that word. You've seen the shape of it in your work — the way some bones hold more than they should."

"A vessel of what?"

"Of him." A longer pause. Outside, wind worried the eaves. "Of the king. What's left of the king. What he cut out of himself before the thing in the crown could eat it. She's what he saved. She's the proof and the remedy and the—"

"Corvo." Theo's mouth had gone dry. "What thing in the crown?"

"You know what wears the crown isn't the king. Everyone south of the passes knows it and no one says it. They call it the Veil, the ones who know. The ones who made it. It came in during the plague years, when the king was desperate, when the whole court was desperate, and it came in wearing mercy like a cloak." The voice dropped lower, and Theo leaned close to the token as if closeness could make the words clearer. "It eats grief, carver. That's what it does. That's what it *is*. It eats a kingdom's grieving one village at a time, and it calls the eating peace. And when it finished with the king's sorrow it started on the rest of him. Memory. Name. Self. The king has been dying inside his own crown for years, and near the end he grew her — grew a vessel out of his own severed memories, a spare self, a seed of Aldric hidden in a child's body — and someone ran with her. Someone got her out. And now the Veil wants its meal finished, and it will burn every road between here and the sea to finish it."

The workshop seemed to tilt. Theo put a hand on the bench.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "Why you, why now, when the others won't speak?"

"Because I was hanged for carrying crown secrets I never understood," Corvo said, "and I'd like, just once, to carry one that matters." A breath that no lungs made. "There's a word, carver. The Graycloaks use it among themselves. I heard it through the rope, in the minute after, when the dead come close to the newly dead. They said the vessel must be recovered before it reaches Thornfell. They said the vessel is Veilborn's key. They said—"

The token cracked.

A hairline fracture, then a deeper one, running through the bone core with a sound like ice on a pond. Theo felt Corvo's presence thin even as the ghost hurried on, faster, the words tumbling:

"—Veilborn, they called it, Veilborn, and it knows the vessel listens for the dead, it knows she'll run to the ones who hear us, so it silences us where she—"

The fracture widened. The voice stretched like rope under load, and then, with a soft pop of parting fiber, Corvo Hesh was simply gone — not faded, not sunk into sleep, but *gone*, the way a candle is gone when it's pinched, and the little gallows-token lay in Theo's palm in two clean halves, empty for the first time in two years.

Empty. He had never held an empty piece before. He hadn't known emptiness had a texture. It felt like a socket where a tooth had been.

The others stayed silent. All of them. Every ghost he owned, every voice he'd lived inside for thirty years, holding their breath in the dark of their bones — and now Theo understood why. Not fear of the girl.

Fear of the thing hunting her. The thing that could reach even here. The thing that silenced the dead ahead of her path like a man walking through a field snapping every stalk.

*Veilborn.*

He set the broken token down with a care that surprised him. On the bed, the girl stirred. Her eyes opened — pale, calm, ancient in a way no eight-year-old's eyes should be — and found him across the room, and she said something in the old tongue, soft, questioning. Asking, he thought, whether he was still going to keep her. Whether the arithmetic had come out right.

The bounty notice's words sat in him like swallowed glass. *Treason.* Two hundred crowns. Four years of freedom, weighed against a child who was the last living piece of a murdered king, hunted by a parasite that ate nations' sorrows and called the eating peace.

Theo Reyes had spent his whole life being neutral. Neutral was a warm room with the shutters closed while the world shouted outside. He had told himself neutrality was wisdom, was craft, was the discipline of a man who worked with the dead and owed nothing to factions of the living.

But the dead had gone silent for her. One of them had died — again, truly — to give him the word. And somewhere south, passes sealing, an army was climbing toward a place called Thornfell with patient love in its heart and a child's name on a list.

Theo crouched by the bed so their eyes were level.

"We're leaving," he said, in the tone he used for prices: final, non-negotiable. "Tonight. Before the roads close all the way. There's a woman named Sefa Marun who owes me, and I owe her worse, and between the two debts we might just get over the mountain."

The girl watched his mouth move. Then she reached out and touched his wrist — lightly, deliberately, the way you touch something to claim it — and nodded once.

Outside, far down the valley, so distant it might have been thunder, something began to seal the passes.
