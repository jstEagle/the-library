## 4. The Smuggler's Ledger

The ledger was bound in goat hide gone soft as earlobe, and Theo had kept it under the loose floorboard beneath his workbench for nine years without opening it twice.

He opened it now by lamplight while Ilset slept in the back room with both his coats piled over her and a carved sparrow tucked against her throat — not because it would do anything, but because the dead woman whose knucklebone had become that sparrow had liked children, and Theo found he wanted something of hers near the girl.

The pages were Sefa Marun's handwriting: slanted, economical, each entry a small act of treason. *Three crates salted eel to Vessany, no manifest. Two kegs Thornfell red through the Salt Stair, duty unpaid. One man, wounded, no questions.* Nine years ago Theo's name sat on that last line, in ink faded to the color of old tea. He'd come down out of the hills with a broken arm and a warrant with his name on it — a young carver who'd made the mistake of carving for the wrong widow, a magistrate's widow, and putting too true a voice in the bone. Sefa had hidden him in an eel barrel for six days between Greyhollow and Vessany and never once asked what he'd done.

She'd said, when she let him off at the river docks: *You owe me a favor, bone-man. Not money. A favor. I'll know it when I need it.*

Nine years. He'd half hoped she'd forgotten. Half hoped, and half been ashamed of the hoping, because Sefa Marun did not forget things. It was why she still had her caravan and most of her fingers, and why she'd lost everything else.

Ilset made a sound in the back room. Not words — she hadn't spoken a word he understood yet, though last night, half-asleep, she'd murmured something in that other tongue and every piece of finished work on his shelves had hummed like a struck tuning fork until he'd wrapped them all in felt. He'd slept sitting up with a mallet in reach, which was absurd, because she weighed less than a sack of flour and had eaten his entire store of dried plums with the solemn gratitude of someone who'd never been given food before. Or who'd been given food before, long ago, by people who were gone.

He closed the ledger. Wrapped it in oilcloth. Put it inside his coat, where the warrant used to sit.

Then he went and stood over the sleeping child and watched her breathe, and tried to feel like a man making a plan instead of a man running, because those were different things, and he needed very badly to be the first one.

* * *

Greyhollow's market square was half empty in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

Theo noticed it before he noticed anything else: the fish stalls shuttered, the wool merchant gone, the cobbler's awning rolled up and nailed shut. And then he saw what filled the space the town had emptied. People. Strangers, moving south in family clots, pushing handcarts stacked with mattresses and birdcages and one entire loom. A woman walked past him carrying a goat kid like a baby. An old man led two horses loaded so high their bellies nearly dragged. Nobody haggled. Nobody talked much at all. They moved with the flat efficiency of people who had already done their crying somewhere behind them and couldn't afford to do it again here.

"Passes are closing," said Marta Ostrow, who sold lamp oil and opinions in equal measure, appearing at his elbow the way she always did. She nodded at the traffic. "Started three days ago. Graycloaks at the Salt Stair, checking papers, turning folk back north. Turning some folk *in*, if you take my meaning." She lowered her voice to the register she reserved for sedition. "They say the king's sealed the high roads. Say there's plague in the lowlands."

"There's no plague," Theo said. "There's never been plague."

"No," Marta agreed comfortably. "But there's a war coming, and wars need reasons, and 'plague' is cheaper than the truth." She looked at him sideways. "You're carrying something, Almeida. You've got your walking face on."

"I'm going to sell some carvings downvalley."

"You're going to do something stupid," Marta said, "and I'm going to pretend I didn't see you, because you fixed my mother's memorial-bone for free and it sings her name every dusk and my father weeps when he hears it." She pressed a paper twist of candied ginger into his hand. "For the road. Don't die stupidly."

It was as close to love as Greyhollow gave anyone, and Theo took it.

He found the caravan at the far edge of the square, or rather found its wake: eight wagons being loaded by a crew of hard-faced men and women who moved like they expected to be shot at from any of four directions. The wagons were painted dull brown and looked, at a glance, like ordinary freight haulers. At a second glance you noticed the iron bands, the false floors, the drivers' benches wide enough to sleep three. At a third glance you stopped looking, because the crew had started noticing you noticing.

Sefa Marun stood on the box of the lead wagon with a tally slate in her hand, calling weights to a man below.

She was smaller than Theo remembered, or the world had gotten bigger around her. Sixty now, maybe more — grey braid coiled at her neck like rope, shoulders still set square enough to carry a doorframe. Her left eye was a puckered seam below a leather patch, and the right eye was the same flat brown it had been nine years ago, missing nothing, forgiving nothing, cataloguing everything at a fair price.

She saw him before he reached the wagon. Of course she did.

"Well," she said, and jumped down from the box with a crack of her boots that meant she'd done it ten thousand times. "The bone-man. Come up out of his hole." Her gaze went over his shoulder, quick and professional, cataloguing the street behind him. "Alone?"

"Yes."

"Liar." She said it without heat, the way you'd note weather. "You walk like a man with cargo. Where is it?"

"Sefa—"

"You called in a debt or you didn't," she said. "If you didn't, buy a carving and go home. If you did, don't stand in the open telling me about it." She turned and barked at her crew — *finish the water barrels, Rask, we're not wintering here* — and jerked her chin toward the alley between the tannery and the chandler's, where the smell would cover conversation and the walls would cover sightlines.

Theo followed her. His heart was doing something he resented.

* * *

In the alley, out of the wind, Sefa leaned against the tannery wall and waited. That was the thing about her. She never pulled. You came to her at your own pace or you drowned.

"The crown lies," Theo said. He'd decided to start there, since it was where the dead woman had started. "That's the message. That's what the bones keep saying. Every relic I've carved this season, the dead come through saying the same thing — *the crown lies* — and then they stop talking, like something's cut the line. And three days ago a child walked into my workshop out of a whiteout, barefoot, no frostbite, speaking a language older than anything I know, and when she speaks my bones ring like bells and the ghosts go silent. All of them. For the first time in my life. There's a bounty notice naming her crown property. And I think whatever wears the crown isn't a man anymore, and I think she's the only proof of it that can walk and talk and bleed, and I think the Graycloaks will burn this whole valley to get her back."

He ran out of breath. Sefa hadn't blinked.

"That's the whole of it?" she asked.

"There's more but it gets stranger."

"It usually does." She rubbed her jaw, working something over. "The dead don't lie, bone-man. Everyone knows that. It's the one honest thing left in the world." She pushed off the wall. "Where's the child now?"

"Hidden. Fed. Asleep."

"And you want passage south. Through Thornfell. With a fugitive the crown wants back." She smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile; it was the smile of a woman doing arithmetic. "You want me to spend eight wagons, twelve people, and thirty years of blind paths on a ghost story and a barefoot girl."

"I want you to spend a favor," Theo said. "One favor. You named the price yourself. I'm just paying it."

Something moved behind her eye. He'd scored, and she knew he knew it, and she hated him mildly for the accuracy.

"A favor," she said slowly, "was for a *favor*. Get me out of trouble, carry a thing, carve me something true. This—" she gestured at the invisible shape of everything he'd just described, "—this is a war. You're asking me to pick a side in a war."

"The war picked itself. You've seen the refugees."

"I've seen refugees. Refugees mean nothing. Refugees are weather." But her voice had shifted, and Theo, who spent his life listening for what people didn't say, heard it: she'd already decided. She'd probably decided in the alley's first ten seconds, maybe at the market square, maybe — and this thought arrived unbidden and strange — nine years ago, when she'd written *one man, wounded, no questions* in a ledger she kept precisely so that someday she could hold it over the world.

"Why?" he asked. "Honestly. Why are you even considering this? You told me once sentiment was for people who could afford it."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. The wind came down the alley smelling of tanbark and smoke, and somewhere beyond the rooftops the refugee column creaked southward like a slow river.

"My brother," she said.

Theo waited.

"Hallan. Younger than me by two years. Smarter than me by a mile, which I never forgave him for." She said it evenly, recitation-flat, a story told so many times internally that saying it aloud cost almost nothing and therefore must have cost everything. "Twenty-two years ago he ran letters for Aldric's court. Real letters — the court's private correspondence, before the Veil, before everything went grey. He got taken by the crown's secret police. The Quiet Office, they were called then. I lost the eye getting him out of their holding house in Vessany, and I still lost him. They executed him for treason." Her hand drifted up, touched the patch, an old unconscious gesture. "Treason. Hallan, who carried letters for the king they claim he betrayed. The record says treason. The record is signed, stamped, filed, and it is a *lie*, and everyone who could correct it is dead or bought."

"So the ledger," Theo said quietly. "Your ledger. The entries—"

"Every crime the crown ever paid me to move or paid me to ignore, cross-referenced with dates, routes, names, and the Quiet Office's own seals where I could lift them." She patted her chest, where — he realized — a book-shaped weight sat under her coat, twin to the one under his. "Twenty-two years of receipts. Because one day the record gets read aloud somewhere that matters, and Hallan's children — he had two, did I ever tell you? No. No one asks smugglers about their families — his children get to hear the word *murder* instead of *treason*." She straightened. "That day hasn't come. But you just walked into my alley and told me the crown is a lie wearing a lie, and that you have a living witness the dead themselves vouch for."

"I don't know if she's a witness. I don't even know if she can testify. She doesn't speak—"

"She's alive," Sefa said. "Alive beats testimony. Dead men make poor evidence, bone-man; believe me, I've checked." She was moving now, back toward the square, talking fast, decisions landing like stamps. "Here's what happens. You and yours ride in the third wagon, the salt-fish one — it stinks, which keeps searchers brief. We go south tomorrow, before dawn, not tonight; tonight the roads have eyes and my crew needs sleep. We take the Blind Cut through Thornfell, the path that isn't on any map because I burned the only map. Three days to the river, then Vessany if the bridges are open."

"And the passes?"

Her step slowed. For the first time since the alley began, Sefa Marun looked tired.

"Sealed," she said. "Or sealing. My cousin runs a tollhouse on the Salt Stair — ran, past tense — sent word yesterday that Graycloak companies are chaining the gates and building checkpoint timber. They're not collecting tolls. They're counting heads and taking names, and the names they're most interested in—" she glanced back at him, one-eyed, level, "—are children. Girls, specifically. Eight years old, give or take. Traveling with a man, alone or near-alone. Word's come down every pass in the last week. Somebody wants somebody back very badly, and whoever it is has paid enough to make half the bounty-men in the free cities rich."

Theo felt cold settle into him, patient and thorough, like snowmelt through a boot seam.

"They're hunting her on every road."

"They're hunting her on every road," Sefa agreed. "Which means the roads are dead, and we're not taking roads. We're taking the paths the mountain forgot." She climbed back onto her wagon box and looked down at him, and the arithmetic was done; he could see the total in her face. "Third wagon. Dawn. Bring the girl, bring whatever evidence you've got, bring your nerve. And bone-man—"

"Yes?"

"If this goes wrong, if my crew bleeds for your ghost story, that ledger of mine comes out in front of whatever council still has a spine, and your name goes in it next to mine. You understand? I'm not spending Hallan on a feeling."

"You're spending him on the truth," Theo said.

Sefa Marun held his eyes for a moment longer. Then she snorted — the closest thing to laughter he'd ever gotten from her — and turned away, calling weights to Rask, and Theo walked back across a square full of fleeing strangers with a favor spent, an alliance bought, and the taste in his mouth of a man who'd just promised a dangerous woman that the truth was worth what it would cost.

Behind him, in the alley shadow, he could have sworn he heard it again — faint, distant, threaded through the wind off the pass: the dead of Greyhollow, all together, in one voice.

*The crown lies.*

And underneath it, newer, quieter, worse:

*Hurry.*

* * *

Ilset was awake when he got back. She stood at the workshop window with her hands on the sill, watching the refugee river crawl south, and when Theo came in she turned and pointed one small finger down the road, then at herself, then at him.

Then she pointed north — toward the passes, toward the sealed gates, toward whatever wore the crown — and drew her thumb slowly across her throat.

Theo set down the candied ginger.

"Packed by dawn," he said, uselessly, in a language she didn't speak. But she nodded anyway, once, solemn as a magistrate, and began gathering her plums.
