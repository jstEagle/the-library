## 5. Voices in the Bone Road

Ilset counted things to keep herself from screaming.

She had learned counting in the palace, in the years before she had a name. Count the stairs: ninety-one from the nursery to the queen's garden. Count the windows in the long gallery: forty, and three of them cracked. Count the seconds between the king's laughter and his weeping, because the space between those two things was shrinking, and when it closed entirely, she would have to run.

Now she counted the bones in the road.

The smuggler's cart had a floor of rough planks, and under the planks — she could feel them through her bare feet, which never got cold — there were relics sewn into the lining like seeds. A finger bone. Two ribs. Something small and knuckle-shaped that hummed when the cart went over stones. The bone-carver had packed her among them as if she were cargo too, and in a way she was, and in a way she was the most dangerous thing that had ever ridden in a smuggler's cart, and she held both truths in her chest like two hands cupped around a moth.

The cart lurched. The knucklebone hummed. Ilset pressed her palm flat against the planks and listened with the part of her that was not a girl of eight winters but something older and thinner, poured into a shape the priests had grown in vats of memory and milk.

*Four dead,* she counted. *One finger, two ribs, one jaw, one knuckle.* The jaw was the loudest. It kept repeating a word in the trade-tongue of the lowlands, over and over, like a man knocking on a door no one answered: *debt, debt, debt.*

She understood it. That was the problem with being made of a king's severed memories. She understood everything, all at once, in languages she had never been taught, in griefs she had never earned. Aldric's memories were in her the way water is in a sponge — every year of his reign soaked through her, and when she squeezed, even a little, things came out that should have stayed inside him.

That was why they hunted her.

Not for running away. Vessels ran away sometimes; the gardeners said so, the quiet ones with soft voices who fed her and measured her skull with silk ribbons. *They always come back or they die,* one gardener had told another, thinking she was asleep. *There is nowhere in the world a vessel can be empty.*

But Ilset was not empty. That was the secret she carried down the mountain in a smuggler's cart: she was *full*. Full past the brim. And somewhere behind her, wearing her father-king's face like a glove, the Veil had finally noticed what was missing from its meal.

* * *

The world through her eyes was not the world other people saw. She had known this since before she had language for it.

Other people looked at a tree and saw *tree*, brown and green, done. Ilset looked at a tree and saw the rings inside it, the way the memory of every dry summer was written in its wood, the way its roots remembered the shape of the rock they grew around. Everything remembered. Everything was full of the past, layered like sediment in a riverbed, and she could not stop reading it any more than a literate woman could stop reading a sign nailed to a door.

The mountains read themselves to her as they rolled beneath the cart. Stone remembered pressure. Snow remembered falling. The road itself was a scar in the world's skin, and scars remember how they were made, and this one had been made by feet — thousands of them, generations of smugglers and soldiers and pilgrims, and under all of it, deeper, older, a single line of marching men in armor the color of dusk, going north to a war whose name Aldric knew and she could almost taste on the back of her tongue: *the Salt War, the Salt War, four thousand dead at the fords—*

She put her hands over her ears. It didn't help. The memories weren't sound. They were more like weather.

"You're shaking," said the bone-carver.

Theo Reyes sat across from her in the swaying cart, wrapped in a coat too thin for the pass, his hands — those terrible, wonderful hands — folded in his lap like he was afraid of them. He had kind eyes and tired ones. In the workshop, when the dead had gone silent around her, he had looked at her the way a drowning man looks at a plank.

"I'm listening," she said. Her common tongue was good; the king had spoken it, so she did too.

"To what?"

"The road."

He waited. He was better at waiting than anyone she'd met. The gardeners hadn't waited; they'd written things on slates and measured her and never once asked what she heard, because asking might make her say it, and if she said it, someone would have to decide whether to believe her.

"Dead people," she said finally, because he deserved some of the truth. "In the bones you packed."

His face did something complicated. "You can hear them?"

"They're very loud." She frowned, trying to find the words. "But quiet. Inside your workshop everything stopped. Like holding your breath. Out here—" the cart hit a rut, and the knucklebone sang a bright anxious note, "—out here it's like a market day. Everyone shouting their last thought over and over."

"What are they saying?"

Ilset looked at him. He was a grown man with a carver's calluses and a smuggler's debts and a warrant with his name on it somewhere, probably, and he had taken her in off a whiteout steppe without asking a single question that mattered, and she decided — the way Aldric had once decided, in a memory she carried like a stone in her belly — to give him something true.

"That they don't want to go where they're going," she said.

* * *

At night they stopped in a hollow under an overhang, and Sefa Marun built a fire small enough to hide from the stars, and Ilset lay between the two adults and did not sleep, because sleep was where the Veil could reach her.

It had found her in dreams twice already since the workshop. Both times it wore the king's voice, gentle as bathwater: *Come home, little vessel. You are leaking. You will spoil. Come home and we will pour you out properly, into the crown, where memory belongs.*

She had woken both times with her mouth open and old words coming out of it, the high ceremonial language of the binding-song, and Theo had sat up fast with his knife half-drawn until he saw it was only her.

Tonight she fought sleep by watching the fire and reading it. Fire remembered being a forest. That was her favorite kind of remembering — fire was honest about it, crackling out its whole history in one bright evening, the oak and the pine and the lightning all at once, nothing hidden, nothing hoarded.

Unlike kings.

Aldric had hoarded. She knew that now from the inside. She was built from what he cut away — the memories too heavy for a ruling man to carry, the doubts, the shames, the whole second kingdom of himself he'd amputated and grown into flesh so that someone else could hold it. *A spare vessel,* the gardeners called her. Made in case the Veil outgrew the first host.

Except the Veil had outgrown him faster than anyone guessed. And the night it began to eat the parts of him that were still *him* — the night the king stood in the long gallery under forty windows and wept without knowing why, and then laughed without knowing why, and then looked straight at little Ilset in her nightgown and said, in the Veil's smooth borrowed voice, *which one of you is the real one?* — that was the night she ran.

Ninety-one stairs. Forty windows. She had counted them going down, barefoot, carrying nothing but the locket of seal-bone against her sternum, because the seal-bone was the one thing in the palace that remembered the king before the crown, and she could not leave it behind. It was proof. It was the difference between *the king is ill* and *the king is eaten*.

If she died in these mountains, the proof died with her, and the Veil would wear Aldric until it had worn out the whole kingdom's grief, village by village, and no one would ever know there had been anything underneath to save.

So she did not scream. She counted.

*Two hundred and six bones in the human body,* she counted. *I have all of mine. I am not a relic yet.*

*Three days since the workshop. One river crossing. Eleven carts passed us going north, all of them full of furniture and fear.*

*One fire. Two living people who do not know what I am.*

*Zero places in the world a full vessel can be empty.*

* * *

On the fourth morning the road changed, and Ilset felt it before she saw it, the way you feel a storm change in a tooth.

The cart topped a ridge and the land opened below them — a long valley stitched with the gray ribbon of the road, and along the road moved people. Not carts. People. Whole families on foot, bundles on poles, children carried and children walking and one old man pulling a handcart with a goat tied to it, and the goat looking back the way refugees look back, which is the only direction that matters.

Sefa reined up at the crest and swept the valley with her single eye, and her eye was not comforted. "Refugee traffic," she muttered. "Out of the northern valleys. Graycloaks are burning ahead of themselves again." She spat over the wheel. "Or behind. With that lot it's hard to say which end is the mouth."

Ilset read the valley instead.

It roared. Not the road — the *ground*. The whole valley was steeped in leaving. Every stone by the roadside had been stepped on by ten thousand fleeing feet, and the memories stacked up in her like water behind a dam: a woman dropping a shoe and not stopping for it; a boy carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, carrying it for miles, carrying the cage because the cage was the shape of hope; a soldier walking south against the current of the fleeing, walking with his head down, walking toward the thing everyone else fled from, and his boots remembering every step—

Ilset's nose was bleeding.

She didn't notice until Theo did. He turned from the valley and his face changed completely, all the tiredness burned off it at once.

"Ilset. Your nose."

She touched her upper lip. Red. Bright and strange against her fingers, and the strangest part was that she was surprised — as if some part of her still believed she was only a girl, and girls get nosebleeds from crying, from cold, from ordinary things, and not from standing in a valley where ten thousand griefs were shouting at once through the ground.

"It's loud here," she said.

Theo pulled a rag from his coat and tipped her chin up with one finger — careful, always careful, the way he handled bone — and pinched her nose, and held her, and looked at Sefa over her head with an expression Ilset couldn't read because it wasn't a memory of the king's. It was purely his. That was rare. Most faces she could match to something Aldric had seen. This one was new.

"How loud?" Theo asked quietly. "Loud like the workshop before you came? Or louder?"

"Louder." Blood tickled in her throat. She swallowed it; swallowing was counting too. "The workshop was a room of dead people. This is a country of them. They haven't all died yet, but they've all started leaving, and leaving remembers the same way dying does." She looked up at him, blood on the rag, her eyes watering from the pinch. "Something is coming down that road behind us. Something that makes people leave."

Sefa had gone very still. "Graycloaks burn villages," she said. "That's not new."

"No," Ilset said. "This isn't burning. Burning leaves ash and ash forgets fast." She wiped her lip, looked at her own blood, and said the truest thing she had said in four days: "This leaves *quiet*. Villages that aren't destroyed. Villages where everyone just stops grieving. I felt the edge of one before the palace gates. A town called Marenholt. Nobody in Marenholt was sad anymore. Nobody in Marenholt remembered being sad. It was the worst place I have ever stood in my life, and I stood in it for eleven minutes, and I ran for nine days after."

The wind came up the valley, smelling of snow and far smoke.

Sefa looked at Theo. Theo looked at the road south, then north, then at the locket's chain visible at Ilset's collar, and Ilset watched him do arithmetic she couldn't follow — smuggler's math, roads and risks and weights — and watched him arrive at an answer he clearly hated.

"We're not stopping," he said. "We drive through. No fires tonight, no stops except the horse. If the traffic's thick, we're just another cart."

"And if they're inspecting," Sefa said, "at the Salt Stair, where the road narrows to one cart wide and there's a Graycloak post with a brazier and a captain with good eyes—"

"Then I'll show him a relic worth bribing for," Theo said, "and pray he can't count."

Ilset lay back in the cart bed as they rolled down into the flood of refugees, and she pressed the seal-bone locket against her sternum where it hummed its low royal note, and she added to her count:

*One Graycloak post ahead. One forged relic. One captain who counts.*

And underneath, quieter, in the king's own buried voice — the piece of Aldric that lived in her marrow and was not afraid, the piece the Veil had never managed to eat:

*They are close now, little one. Hold the proof. Hold the song. Whatever they offer you, whatever they take — hold.*
