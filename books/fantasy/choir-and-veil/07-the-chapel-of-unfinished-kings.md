## 7. The Chapel of Unfinished Kings

The chapel announced itself as a tooth in the dark — a broken arch of pale stone jutting from the hillside where the smuggler path bent east, away from the relayed fires. Sefa spotted it first, or rather her mule did; the animal's ears went flat and it refused to pass the arch until she dismounted and led it through by the bridle, muttering something about beasts knowing what men forget.

"Unfinished kings," she said, when Theo asked. "Old cult ground. Before the free cities, before the crown, before any of the names they make children memorize. They used to bring claimants here and crown them with river-stone, and if the stone cracked, the claimant went home alive and landless." She spat into the dark. "Honest system, really. More honest than the one we've got."

The roof was gone. Whatever had held the tiles up had rotted or been pulled down centuries ago, and the walls stood to shoulder height in places, to nothing in others, so that the chapel felt less like a building than a suggestion of one — a floor of packed earth and fallen stone open to a sky thick with cloud. At the far end, the apse had survived, a half-dome of fitted masonry, and beneath it stood the font: a basin of black river-stone, cracked clean through, exactly as Sefa had promised.

Theo set Ilset down on the apse steps and she sat the way she always sat — perfectly still, hands in her lap, watching everything and offering nothing. The cold didn't touch her. It had been four days since the Salt Stair and the cliff path and the smuggler named Doss who had died buying them thirty seconds with a knife in his fist, and in four days the girl had not shivered once, not in the whiteout, not in the river shallows Sefa had waded through cursing while Ilset crossed on stones that shouldn't have held her weight. Theo had stopped trying to make sense of it. His ghosts hadn't.

Because the silence had held. Four days, and the dead in his head — the constant murmuring crowd he had lived with since he was nine years old, the dead of every relic he'd ever carved, the dead who never stopped, never slept, never let him alone — the dead were quiet. Not absent. He could feel them the way you feel a room through a shut door. But they would not come near the girl. Whatever she carried, they wanted no part of it.

He should have found that comforting. Instead it made his skin crawl, because in his experience, when the dead refused to speak, it was because whatever they had to say was worse than silence.

Sefa built a small fire in the nave, screened behind a fall of wall so the light wouldn't show from the path. She fed the horses, checked the mule's shoe, walked the perimeter with her one eye and a knife she never seemed to put away, and then she sat with her back to the broken wall and said, "Sleep in shifts. I'll take first."

Theo meant to take his shift awake. He meant to keep watch on the girl, on the path, on the dark. But four days of fear and cold and the specific exhaustion of carrying a child who weighed less than she should have — he sat down on the apse steps beside Ilset, and the fire's warmth reached him like a hand on the shoulder, and sleep took him between one breath and the next.

* * *

He dreamed of a hall he had never seen.

He knew it was a dream because the light was wrong — gold without a source, falling from everywhere and nowhere, the light of memory rather than of sun. And he knew it was not his memory because the hall was a throne room, and Theo Reyes had never stood in a throne room in his life except once, drunk, on a dare, in Vessany's council house, and had been thrown out before he reached the doors.

This hall was older. The stone was the same pale stone as the chapel around him — he understood, distantly, that the chapel and the hall were the same place, separated by centuries, the roof on, the walls whole, the apse not a ruin but an altar. Banners hung from the clerestory, deep green and silver, and the crowd that filled the hall was dressed in the fashion of sixty years ago, the high collars, the long coats, and they were all looking at the altar.

A man stood at the altar. He was young — younger than Theo — and he wore the look of a man carrying something heavy up a long stair, the careful posture of someone who has decided not to show the weight. He had dark hair going gray at the temples too early, and a face that would have been ordinary except for the eyes, which were tired in a way that no man of twenty-five should be tired. A plague tired, Theo thought. The tiredness of a man who has spent two years burying his country.

The crowd hushed. An old priest came forward bearing a cushion, and on the cushion lay a crown.

Theo had seen the crown before. Every soul in the free cities had seen it, stamped on coin and warrant and gallows-notice: the iron circlet with its seven points, plain, almost brutal, the crown the usurper wore. But in the dream-light it looked different. It looked new. And it looked — Theo's stomach turned — it looked *hungry*. The way his carved bones sometimes looked hungry, when he'd cut them from bone that remembered violence. The way a door looks when something on the other side is leaning against it.

The young man — Aldric, Theo knew it was Aldric, knew it the way you know a thing in a dream, without being told — the young king looked at the crown for a long moment. And then he did something no coronation should include.

He flinched.

Small. Almost nothing. A tightening around the eyes, a half-step back that he converted, with the skill of a man trained all his life to stand still in front of crowds, into a bow of his head. But Theo had spent twenty years reading the bodies of the dead, the last honest gestures of people at the end of things, and he knew a flinch when he saw one. Aldric was afraid of the crown. He was afraid, and he was going to put it on anyway.

*Why?* Theo thought, and the dream, which had been showing him the hall, turned to show him the reason.

The plague. It came to him not as explanation but as experience — the dream was generous that way, or cruel. The gray fever, they'd called it in the songs, the sorrowing sickness, the grief-plague. It had come out of the east in the king's second year and it had not killed the way plagues kill. It killed the will. The sick did not waste; they *stopped*. Stopped eating, stopped speaking, stopped grieving — that was the horror the songs got wrong, the plague didn't cause grief, it *ate* it. Villages full of people who had lost children and could no longer remember why the small clothes folded in the chest mattered. Mothers who looked at empty cradles with mild, pleasant faces. A whole kingdom forgetting how to mourn, and the forgetting spreading like frost across a window, and the sick lying down in the fields in rows because they could not remember why standing was worth the effort.

Theo had heard the dead talk about it. Old ghosts, the ones who'd died before the wars, sometimes muttered of the gray years the way sailors mutter of a storm. But hearing about it was nothing. *Feeling* it, even secondhand, even dreamed — the vast soft nothing of it, the tenderness of it, the way it didn't tear but *soothed* — he understood in that moment why the histories called it a mercy, and why the histories were written by the survivors of something that should never have been called mercy.

The court had tried everything. The dream gave him that too, in fragments: physicians burning herbs that made the sick cough and change nothing; priests ringing bells over villages until the bells cracked; the king himself riding from town to town, holding the dying, weeping for people who could no longer weep for themselves, and catching nothing, because the plague did not pass body to body. It passed *sorrow* to sorrow. The more you loved, the faster it found you.

And in the last months, the story went — the story every child in the free cities knew — the king had gone up into the mountains alone, to a place of old stones, and had prayed, and had come down wearing the iron crown, and the plague had broken within the season.

The story said it was a miracle. The story said the gods had answered.

The dream said otherwise.

Because now the dream stood Theo at the altar's edge, close enough to touch the young king's sleeve, and the priest was lifting the crown, and Aldric was speaking — not the coronation words the crowd heard, but something under them, a whisper, a *bargain*, words said so low that only the stone itself and one bone-carver sixty years unborn could hear:

*"You promise they keep their grief. You promise the sorrow stays theirs — the mourning, the memory, all of it, everything that makes the loss bearable. Take only the dying part. Take the part that spreads. Nothing else."*

And the crown answered.

It did not answer in words. It answered the way the plague had answered — softly, sweetly, a warmth against the skin of the mind, an assent so gentle it felt like being forgiven. And Theo felt, with a sick lurch, the shape of the lie in it, the fine print written in a language of warmth: *yes, yes, all of it, everything* — and beneath the yes, folded small as a seed, the truth the crown did not say: *I will keep their grief. I will keep it all. Every sorrow in this kingdom will pass through me, and I will hold it, and I will hold it, and I will hold it, and what holds a flood becomes the flood.*

The crown came down.

Theo heard it settle on the king's brow — a sound like a key turning in a lock the size of a country — and Aldric's face changed. Not much. Not the rictus of possession the puppet-shows liked, not the black-eyed sneer. Just a stillness. The tiredness smoothed away, the fear smoothed away, everything smoothed away, and what looked out through the young king's eyes was calm, and patient, and *loving*, and it was the lovingness that made Theo's hands shake. It looked at the crowd and loved them the way a man loves a wound he is cleaning. It looked at them and began, gently, without malice, to take.

The hall went gray at the edges. The banners faded. The crowd's faces softened into pleasant blankness, one by one, like lamps going out — and Theo shouted, or tried to, because he had seen this before, hadn't he, in the villages they'd walked through, in the faces of the refugees on the road who spoke of the Graycloaks' mercy with mild grateful smiles — he had been seeing the crown's work for weeks without knowing he was looking at a coronation still in progress, sixty years long, still being performed —

* * *

He woke on the apse steps with Ilset's hand on his face.

Her palm was cold, colder than the night air, and it lay against his cheek with a weight that was not quite the weight of a child's hand. Her eyes were open and fixed on him, and her mouth was moving, and sound was coming out of it — the old language, the one that made bones ring, the one the scholars in Vessany would want — but this time it was not the flat recitation he had heard from her before. It was *sung*. Low, half-voiced, a melody with the bones of a lullaby and the structure of something much older, and as she sang, the chapel sang back.

The stones resonated. Theo felt it through the apse steps, through the floor, up through his spine: the fitted masonry of the half-dome humming in sympathy, a deep chord like a struck bell that never quite decays. The cracked font at the altar buzzed like a hive. And in the sound — Theo's gift, his curse, his lifelong affliction, reached for the first time in four days — and found *voices*. Not the dead of his workshop, not the smugglers and grandmothers whose knucklebones he'd carved. These were older. Thousands of them. Packed into the stone like sediment, like the pressed layers of a coal seam, and they were singing the same song Ilset sang, had been singing it, he understood with dawning horror, for sixty years, on loop, without pause, the binding-song, the coronation-song, the song that held the crown's bargain in place —

And beneath the song, one voice, separate from the chorus, close, as close as a man kneeling beside him.

It was tired. It was the tiredness from the dream, the plague tired, the tiredness of a man who had carried a country up a long stair. It spoke in the common tongue, in the accent of the capital, and it spoke directly to Theo Reyes, bone-carver, debtor, neutral man, and it said:

*"I asked for this."*

Theo could not move. Ilset's cold hand lay on his cheek. The stones hummed. The voice went on, and there was something in it he had never heard in sixty years of listening to the dead, because the dead, whatever else they were, were always *finished* — and this voice was not finished, this voice was still happening, still being lived, somewhere under the crown, sixty years deep:

*"They were dying. Not the fever-dying. Worse. They were lying in the fields and they could not remember their children's faces, and I could, I remembered all of them, every face in every village, I carried them, and I thought — God forgive me, carver, I thought, let me carry it instead. Let me hold what they cannot. It seemed such a small thing to trade. A crown is only iron. A king is only a man. I asked for this."*

The voice caught. When it resumed it was quieter, and it was the quietness that undid him, the sound of a man admitting the thing he had spent sixty years not admitting:

*"I was wrong."*

The chord in the stones bent. Somewhere in the humming, thousands of voices shifted key, and the sound that came out was not a song anymore. It was grief. Sixty years of it, a kingdom's worth, held and held and held until the holder and the held were the same thing, pouring through the cracked font, through the apse, through the small cold hand on Theo's face.

Ilset stopped singing.

She took her hand from his cheek. She looked at him with her strange still eyes, and then she did something he had never seen her do in all the days since she appeared barefoot at his door in the whiteout.

She wept.

Silently, the way the plague's victims couldn't — no, Theo thought, the way the plague's victims *would*, if the crown had kept its bargain, if the grief had stayed theirs — she wept without sound, tears running down a child's face in a ruined chapel at midnight, and Theo understood that the tears were not hers. Or they were hers, all of them, sixty years of them, every tear the kingdom had been unable to cry since the crown came down, stored in a vessel grown from a king's severed memories, waiting.

He gathered her in. She came stiffly at first, the way a thing comes that has not been held, and then all at once, her small body shaking against his chest, and over her head Theo looked at the cracked black font and the humming apse and said, aloud, to a king he had never met and had been running from for a month:

"I hear you."

The stones went quiet. The chorus settled back to its loop, its endless patient binding-song. But the close voice, the unfinished one, said one word more before it folded back under, one word that was not a plea or a confession but a warning, and the warning was:

*"Run."*

* * *

Sefa's boot in his ribs woke him a second time, properly, and the gray pre-dawn light showed him the nave, the dead fire, the mule, the girl asleep on the apse steps exactly where he'd left her — a child, only a child, dreaming whatever vessels dream.

"You were talking in your sleep," Sefa said. Her one eye was unreadable. "Loud. And the girl was singing, and the stones were — " She stopped. Sefa Marun did not stop often. "The stones were singing back, carver. I've slept in a hundred ruins on this road and stones don't sing back."

Theo looked at the cracked font. In the gray light it was only a broken basin, old cult furniture, honest stone.

"We can't stop here again," he said. "Not anywhere old. She — " He didn't know how to say it. *She's a door. The whole country is a door and she's standing in front of it.* "We need to move."

"Moving." Sefa crouched, stirred the ash, fed it a twist of kindling until a flame stood up. "Funny you say that. While you were communing with the architecture, I walked the path south." She looked up at him, and in the firelight her missing eye was a shadow and her living eye was very bright. "There are campfires in the valley. A lot of them. Graycloak vanguard, moving up from the river, and they're not sweeping for us, carver. They're not even looking. They're marching on Thornfell. All of them. The whole army."

Theo's blood went cold. "They know where we're going."

"Everybody knows where you're going." Sefa stood, shouldering her pack. "Question is whether we get to the choir before the crown does. And carver — " she jerked her chin at the sleeping girl, at the cracked font, at the whole patient humming dark — "after what I heard in this chapel tonight, I'm thinking we should know something before we run one more step. What exactly is it you're carrying her toward?"

Theo opened his mouth to answer, and found he had no answer, because the truth was that he no longer knew whether he was carrying Ilset to the choir to free a kingdom — or to deliver her to it.
