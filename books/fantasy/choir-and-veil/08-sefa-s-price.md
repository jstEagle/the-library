## 8. Sefa's Price

Twenty years ago, on a night so cold the river smoked, Sefa Marun carried a crate of sealed bones across the border at Cael Ford and told the checkpoint guard it was salted fish.

The guard was young. He had chapped hands and a mother he wrote to every week, and he wanted to be somewhere warm, so he lifted the lid an inch, wrinkled his nose at the brine-soaked sacking she'd laid over the top, and waved her through. Sefa thanked him in the flat accent of a fishwife and drove on with her heart beating like a fist against her ribs, because under the brine and the sacking, packed in wool like eggs, were forty human knucklebones cut from the hands of executed traitors — and every one of them still whispering.

That was the work, back then. That was the crown's work.

They called themselves couriers of the Royal Commission of Antiquities, which was a lie stitched into real paperwork by men in clean rooms who never smelled river mud or blood. What they actually did was move relics. Bones that remembered. Rings that dreamed. A bishop's jawbone that, when held to the ear, recited confessions no living man had dared speak aloud. King Aldric's court had discovered, early in his reign, that the dead made excellent witnesses and even better weapons, and there was a whole quiet industry built around harvesting them — smugglers like Sefa running the roads, carvers preparing the vessels, and above it all a spymaster named Corven Hale who signed his orders with a stamp shaped like a closed eye.

Sefa had been twenty-three, one-eyed in neither sense, quick on a mule's back and quicker with a ledger. Her brother Dain rode rear guard. He was nineteen and laughed too loudly for a man in their trade, and he kept a list in his head of every town where a girl had smiled at him, which was most towns, which was why he was useless at tradecraft and indispensable anyway, because people trusted him the way they trust weather that has always been kind.

The commission paid well. Too well, if she'd been honest with herself, but honesty about money is a luxury you can't afford until after it's gone.

* * *

The job that broke everything came down from Hale's office in the eleventh month of the year the plague reached the capital.

It should have been routine: a reliquary out of the palace vaults, moved north to Thornfell, no questions. But the reliquary was wrong. Sefa knew bone the way other smugglers knew coin weight, and what sat in that case wasn't carved work at all — it was raw, unworked royal seal-bone, heavy as sin, and when she pressed her palm flat against its lid she heard not a voice but a *crowd*, a hush of thousands of dead all breathing at once, like wind through a canyon that used to have a name.

Dain heard it too. She watched the color leave his face as he stood with his hand on the case, and she watched something worse follow the color out: curiosity.

"What is it?" he asked her later, on the road, in the dark, in the voice of a boy asking why the sky is the color it is.

"None of ours," Sefa said. "That's what it is. We don't ask."

But Dain asked. Of course he asked. He asked a customs clerk in Marrowgate, and the clerk asked a priest, and the priest — whether from fear or conscience or simple arithmetic — sent word up the chain, and the word arrived at a desk with a closed-eye stamp on it, and beneath the stamp someone wrote two lines:

*The courier Sefa Marun is essential. The courier Dain Marun knows too much.*

She learned the shape of those two lines only afterward, reconstructed them over years the way you reconstruct a face from a skull. At the time, all she knew was that on the last night before delivery, at a waystation below Thornfell where the pines grew black and close, the king's own men came for them. Not soldiers. The Quiet Guard — gray-cloaked, soft-booted, faces wrapped. They took Dain first because he was sleeping nearest the door, and they would have taken Sefa too except she'd developed, somewhere in six years of smuggling, the animal habit of waking when the night went silent, and the crickets had stopped.

She went out the window with nothing but her knife and the clothes she wore and heard, as she ran, her brother calling her name exactly twice. Not screaming. Calling. As if he believed, right up until the second call, that she would come back for him. That was the thing that lived behind her eye socket ever after — not the sound of his death, which she never heard, but the sound of his *trust*, which she did.

They caught him. They tried him in a room with no windows, and the record says treason — conspiracy to sell the crown's secrets — and the record is a document, and documents are written by whoever holds the pen. They hanged him at Marrowgate on a market morning so the crowd could learn from it, and they made his sister watch, because they suspected she'd run, and they were right, and they wanted the lesson to reach her.

What the lesson actually taught her was patience.

* * *

The eye she lost three years later, to a Graycloak sabre on the Salt Stair, in a fight that started because she refused to hand over a coffin she was legally carrying. By then she was no longer anyone's courier. She ran her own road now, blind paths through Thornfell that existed on no map because she had erased them from every map she could buy or burn. Smugglers called her the Ledger, half a joke about her bookkeeping and half something older and more careful, and the free cities' councils called her when they needed things moved quietly, and the crown called her nothing at all, because the crown had a warrant with her name on it and a bounty that hadn't been claimed yet, though men had died trying.

One eye. Half a face of scarring. A ledger of debts owed to her and by her, kept in a cipher of her own devising, and at the very back, past the columns of figures, a single page that was not arithmetic at all.

*Dain Marun. Executed Marrowgate, falsely recorded. Witnesses: none living. Evidence: none surviving. Verdict required: murder, not treason.*

That was the whole entry. Twenty years old and unwritten-upon, because there had never been anything to write. The Quiet Guard left no witnesses. The commission burned its papers when Aldric's court reorganized. The judge who pronounced sentence died comfortable in bed. Every thread had been cut, scorched, buried.

And then, in the worst winter in a generation, a bone-carver from Greyhollow — a neutral, careful, debt-riddled nobody who had spent a decade perfecting the art of being beneath everyone's notice — turned up at her fire asking for a road south with a mute child beside him who wore a locket of royal seal-bone, and whose silence, when Sefa leaned close, sounded less like muteness and more like a held breath.

* * *

Now, in the present, on the blind road south of Greyhollow with refugee carts creaking ahead of them and Graycloak outriders reported at the Salt Stair, Sefa sat her mule and watched Theo Reyes sleep badly against a pack frame, and let herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the workshop.

The child was a vessel. The carver didn't fully understand what that meant yet; Sefa could see it in how gently he handled her ignorance, the way you carry a jar you've been told is full of oil but suspect is full of lightning. And whatever the child contained — the king's memory, the true history, the unburned record — it meant that for the first time in twenty years there was a witness. Not a paper trail, not a bribed clerk's recollection, not a grave to dig up. A *witness*. Living, walking, breathing the court's secrets out through pores.

If the Veil could be exposed — if whatever wore Aldric's crown could be dragged into daylight before it finished eating the kingdom — then the trials would come. They always came, after. And when they came, the records would be opened, and among the reopened files would sit a case marked *treason*, and Sefa Marun intended to stand in whatever passed for a courtroom and say, in front of gods and councils and anyone else who cared to listen: *my brother was nineteen and curious, and curiosity is not treason, and the men who killed him are either monsters wearing a king's face or a monster wearing a king outright, and either way the verdict reads murder.*

She didn't want revenge. Revenge was a fire that ate the house it warmed. What she wanted was a *word*. One word changed on one line of one record. Treason to murder. It seemed so small, spelled out like that. It had taken her twenty years to earn the right to chase a single syllable, and she would spend the whole ledger — every debt owed her, every favor banked, every blind path she alone remembered — to collect it.

Theo stirred. "You're staring," he said, without opening his eyes.

"I'm checking my cargo."

"We're not cargo."

"Everything on this road is cargo." She nudged her mule forward a step. "The child sleeps?"

"Finally. She dreams loud, though. Talks in a language I don't know, and the bones in my pack answer her." He rubbed his face. "You haven't asked me what she is."

"No."

"Why not?"

Sefa looked south, toward the pass, toward Thornfell's dark shoulder against the stars, toward a waystation ruin where pine trees had grown tall around the spot where she had last heard her brother say her name. "Because I already know what she is," she said. "She's evidence. And I've spent twenty years learning that evidence doesn't survive unless somebody bleeds for it." She turned back to him and let him see, for once without any craft at all, what was behind the eyepatch. "I'm not doing this for you, carver. Don't mistake it. You're the road. She's the reason. Get us both to Thornfell alive and maybe we'll both get what we're actually carrying delivered."

Theo studied her a long moment, the way he probably studied bone before the knife touched it. "And what happens," he asked quietly, "when the evidence starts talking? When it has a voice and a name and looks at you like you're supposed to save it?"

Ahead of them, in the wagon, the child cried out in her sleep — a short, sharp sound, the same word twice, in that old tongue — and every relic in Sefa's carefully padded cargo rang once, softly, like struck glass.

Sefa's hand found her knife.

"That," she said, "is a question I'm hoping I never have to answer. Ride. The Salt Stair by dawn, before the Graycloaks seal it — and carver, pray your evidence stays asleep till we're past the checkpoint."
