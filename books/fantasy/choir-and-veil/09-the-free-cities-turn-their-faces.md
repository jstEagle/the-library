## 9. The Free Cities Turn Their Faces

Vessany announced itself by smell before sight: river mud, tanneries, frying onions, and beneath it all the faint mineral tang of the aqueduct that fed the city's hundred fountains. Theo had forgotten how loud a free city was. After the passes, after the chapel and the snow and the long silence of the dead in his satchel, the noise of ten thousand living voices struck him like weather.

Ilset rode the cart's bench beside him with her hood up, watching the walls rise out of the morning haze. She hadn't spoken — she never spoke, not in any tongue he knew — but her hand found his sleeve as they passed under the gatehouse, her fingers pressing the wool there like a reader marking a page.

"I know," Theo said. "Big. Loud. Full of people who'll want to sell you something."

The gate guards barely looked at them. That was the promise of Vessany, wasn't it? Six cities on the river road, none of them swearing to any crown, all of them too rich and too stubborn to be conquered. The Graycloaks couldn't march here. The usurper's writ stopped at the toll bridges.

*So why does it feel like we've run from one fire into another,* Theo thought, *and this one's just slower?*

Sefa had peeled off at the Salt Fork with half the caravan, taking the trade goods south where they'd fetch better price, but she'd given him a name before she went: Master Ondrel Quay, Second Chair of Antiquities at the Vessany Athenaeum, a man who owed her for three crates of "ceremonial" artifacts that had never seen a ceremony in their lives.

"Tell him the ledger balances," Sefa said, working her eye patch loose and resettling it against the wind. "He'll know what I mean. And Theo —" she'd caught his arm, her grip stronger than her frame suggested, "— whatever the scholars tell you, don't let Ilset out of your sight in that city. Free cities are free because everything's for sale."

Now, in the shadow of the Athenaeum's buttresses, he understood her warning completely.

* * *

The Athenaeum smelled of dust and lamp oil and old arguments. Its reading hall rose four stories, ringed with galleries, and every surface that could hold a book did. Theo stood in the entrance with bone-dust still under his fingernails and felt the way he always felt around institutions: like a dog being measured for a cage.

Master Quay received him in a side chamber stacked with unlabeled crates. He was a narrow man, sixty-odd, with ink-stained cuffs and the particular patience of someone who had spent decades being right slowly.

"Sefa Marun's carver," he said, before Theo could introduce himself. "She wrote ahead. Which means either you're carrying something extraordinary or something dangerous, and in my experience the categories overlap." He gestured to a stool. "Show me."

Theo set the locket on the table between them.

Quay didn't touch it at first. He leaned over it the way a physician leans over a wound, close enough that his breath stirred the dust. Then he took out a lens on a chain, held it above the seal-bone, and went very still.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get royal seal-bone?"

"It came with the child."

"The child." Quay lowered the lens. His eyes, when they found Theo's, had gone sharp and careful. "Describe her."

"Barefoot in a whiteout. Eight years old, maybe. Unharmed by cold. Speaks a language my bones answer like bells. And every ghost I've ever carried went silent the day she walked through my door." He paused. "I carve relics, Master Quay. I hear the dead through what they leave behind. It's a gift, or a curse, or a trade — depends on the day. And I'm telling you that girl is louder than every dead thing I've ever touched, and she isn't even dead."

For a long moment Quay said nothing. Then he crossed to the door, closed it, and dropped the bar.

"You should understand," he said, turning back, "what you've walked into. Three weeks ago the Council of Nine voted on whether to receive envoys from the crown. Last night they voted again. This morning the vote stands at five to four in favor of negotiation." He sat down heavily across from Theo. "You've arrived in the city at the exact hour it decided to make peace with the thing wearing Aldric's face."

"They call him the usurper here?"

"They call him *King Aldric*, restored and hale, thank the gods, ending the war before it costs us anything." Quay's mouth twisted. "The free cities have never been brave, carver. They've been comfortable. Comfort is a kind of cowardice with good accounting."

"And you? Where do you stand?"

"I stand," Quay said, "where the evidence stands. Show me more of it."

* * *

It took two days.

Two days of Quay cross-referencing the locket against seals recorded in court inventories two centuries old. Two days of Ilset sitting in the corner of the scholar's chamber, drawing in the dust with one finger — spirals, mostly, and a repeated glyph that made Quay's hands shake when he finally matched it to a fragment in his collection. Two days of Theo sleeping badly in a rented room with Ilset on a pallet beside him, waking at every footstep in the hall, listening for boots.

On the second evening, Quay laid out six fragments of stone and fired clay across his table like a man dealing cards, and told Theo what he'd spent thirty years piecing together from scraps the courts had tried to burn.

"The old tongue has no surviving name for itself," he began. "We call it Choir-Speech, because every text we have in it is a song, a binding, or an account of both. It predates the kingdoms. Predates the free cities. There is exactly one institution in recorded history that spoke it fluently, and they built their house on a mountain called Thornfell."

"The choir," Theo said. He'd heard the word from the smuggler ghost, from Sefa, from the dead woman's knucklebone whispering *the crown lies*. It kept surfacing like a drowned body.

"The choir." Quay aligned two clay shards so their broken edges met. "Here is what the fragments say, and I warn you it will sound like madness. Some centuries ago — the dating is contested — the choir-singers bound something into a crown. Not forged it into the metal. *Bound* it. Sung it in, note by note, the way you'd pack a wound with moss. A parasite, a hunger, a veil — the texts use all three words. They called it the Veilborn, and they did not kill it because they believed it could not be killed. Only contained. Only sung down."

"And the crown?"

"Went to the kings. Passed hand to hand for generations, dormant, or quiet enough that no one noticed. Until Aldric." Quay looked up. "There was a plague, carver. Not of the body — of memory. A sickness that ate grief out of people, left them hollow and docile. Whole villages smiling through their children's funerals. The court physicians despaired. And somewhere in that despair, Aldric made a bargain. We don't have the text of it. But we have this." He tapped the locket. "Seal-bone. Bone taken from a crowned head, cut at coronation, per the old rite. It remembers its wearer. And what it remembers—" his voice dropped, "—is a voice inside the king's own skull, patient and loving, saying: *give me the grief, and I will carry it for you.*"

Theo's hands had gone cold. "The plague. The Veil caused it."

"The Veil *was* the plague. Or the plague was the Veil, reaching out, softening the kingdom the way you'd soften bread in milk. Making the bargain easy. And Aldric — grieving, desperate, a good man in a bad year — said yes." Quay sat back. "That's the shape of it. The rest is inference. But the inference says the Veil doesn't wear the crown the way a man wears a hat. It wears the way a worm wears an apple. Slowly. From inside. Feeding on what the host feels, growing fat on a nation's sorrow, and when the host runs dry—"

He stopped. Both of them looked, without meaning to, at the girl in the corner, drawing spirals in the dust.

"When the host runs dry," Theo said, "it needs a new vessel."

"That would be the inference, yes."

* * *

"Can it be killed?" Theo asked. "That's the question. Everything else is history."

And here Quay did something Theo had never seen a scholar do. He took off his spectacles, cleaned them thoroughly, put them back on, and then poured himself a cup of wine and drank half of it in one swallow.

"No," he said. "I want to be wrong. I have spent thirty years hoping to be wrong. But the binding-texts are unambiguous on one point, and it's the point everyone burned the libraries to bury. The Veilborn cannot be destroyed because it is not, properly speaking, *alive*. You can't kill a hole. You can only fill it, or starve it, or — and this is the word the texts actually use — *unbind* it. Take apart the song that holds it. Note by note, reversed. And the unbinding can only be done where the original binding was sung, because the song lives in the stone as much as in the throat. The resonance has to match. Thornfell. The ruined choir beneath it. Nowhere else."

"Then we go to Thornfell."

"With what army?" Quay spread his hands. "The usurper's vanguard is already moving up the river valleys. The Council voted last night to open negotiations — which means, practically, that they will send ambassadors, and the ambassadors will come back with terms, and the terms will involve extradition. Anyone the crown names as a fugitive asset." His eyes flicked to Ilset. "A runaway vessel, I believe the warrant said. The Graycloak notices have reached the river cities; money talks even here, especially here."

"The councils won't fight."

"The councils will *negotiate*, which is worse. Fighting at least requires someone to admit there's an enemy." Quay stood and went to the window, looking out over the lamplit city, the fountains, the barges on the dark river. "Do you know what the fourth vote was about yesterday? Not the treaty. A motion to fund evacuation of the border villages. It failed eight to one. The reasoning, recorded verbatim: *evacuation implies alarm, and alarm may prejudice the negotiations.*" He laughed, short and bitter. "They are going to feed villages to that thing one at a time and call each meal diplomacy."

Theo looked at Ilset. She had stopped drawing. She was watching Quay with an expression Theo had learned to read over the hard miles — not fear exactly, but recognition. The look of someone hearing her own nightmare described accurately by a stranger.

"There has to be another way in," Theo said. "To Thornfell. Off the roads, off the ledgers. Someone who knows the blind paths."

Something moved behind Quay's face — reluctance, then resignation. "Sefa Marun," he said. "Which you already know, because she sent you to me, which means she already knows what I'm going to say, which means that woman has been orchestrating this since before you arrived." He rubbed his eyes. "Yes. There are ways. Smuggler roads, the old salt stairs, paths that don't appear on any map the army buys. But carver — hear me. Even if you reach the choir. Even if the texts are right and the place still holds the song. The unbinding requires a living voice fluent in Choir-Speech. A singer. And the last singers died centuries ago."

"Except," Theo said quietly, "the language didn't die. It's been sleeping in one vessel after another, waiting." He turned to the girl. "Haven't it, little one?"

Ilset looked at him. And in the lamplight, for just a moment, her lips moved — no sound, no breath, just the shape of a word formed and released, like a fish rising and sinking in dark water.

Quay saw it too. He sat down again, slowly, like a man whose legs had stopped agreeing with him.

"Gods below," he whispered. "It's *her*. The texts talk about vessels — spare vessels, grown from the king's severed memories, kept against the day the Veil outgrew its host. I thought it was allegory. It's inventory." He stared at her. "She's not running *from* the palace, carver. She's what the palace was saving. And if the Veil finishes with Aldric, the next crown it wears will be fitted to *her*."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere out in the city, a bell rang the late watch, and Theo thought about the Council of Nine voting five to four to shake hands with a hunger, and the border villages staying put because evacuation might *prejudice the negotiations*, and a warrant with a child's description on it and a price beside it.

"Then we leave tonight," he said. "Before the vote becomes an edict and the edict becomes soldiers at the gates."

Quay was already moving, pulling a crate toward the wall, revealing a cache beneath the floorboards: papers, seals, a purse heavy enough to be heard. "Sefa's ledger balances," he said. "Tell her the antiquities sold well. And carver—" he pressed the purse into Theo's hands, along with a folded sheet covered in his cramped notation, "—this is everything I have on the binding-song's structure. Fragmentary. Contested. Probably useless."

"Probably," Theo agreed, and took it anyway.

At the door, Ilset paused. She looked back at the scholar, at the six fragments on the table, at the great dark library beyond — all that collected human knowing, bought and shelved and bar-coded by committee — and she did the first deliberate thing Theo had ever seen her do toward anyone outside their small traveling company.

She bowed. Deeply. The bow of one singer acknowledging another.

Then she took Theo's hand, and they went down into the streets, where the fountains ran and the barges loaded and the free cities turned their faces, five votes to four, away from the mountain.
