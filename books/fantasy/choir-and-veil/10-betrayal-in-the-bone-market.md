## 10. Betrayal in the Bone Market

The fence's name was Odo Vetch, and he had the eyes of a man counting coins inside a coffin.

Theo had known him for six years, back when Theo still came to Vessany twice a year to sell reliquary work under a false name. Odo dealt in the gray market between the legitimate bone-traders and the outright grave-robbers — carved saints' fingers, martyr's teeth, the occasional knucklebone of a minor hero whose family had fallen on hard times. He never asked where things came from. That was the whole of his virtue, and Theo had once mistaken it for trustworthiness.

Now Odo turned Ilset's locket over in his soft hands and his one honest thought was visible on his face like a price tag.

"Seal-bone," he said. "Royal seal-bone. Do you know what this is worth?"

"I know what it's worth," Theo said. "That's not why I'm here."

"Everything is worth something. That's the first law of markets." Odo set the locket down with exaggerated care, as if it might bruise. "The second law is that everything worth something is worth more to someone else."

Behind Theo, Ilset stood very still among the hanging bones of Odo's shop — wind-chimes of rib and radius, mobiles of vertebrae that clicked in the river draft. She had been silent all morning in the way she was always silent: not empty silence but full silence, a held breath. Since the scholar's house, since the word *unbound* and *choir* and *only there*, she had gone quiet the way deep water goes quiet.

"The Graycloaks are asking after a girl," Theo said. "Eight years old. They're paying for information. I want to know how much, so I know how careful to be."

Odo smiled. It was a good smile, practiced, warm at the edges. "And you came to me. The man best positioned to sell you."

"I came to you because you hate them. They burned Hasker's Row last spring. Your cousin kept a shop there."

"My cousin kept a shop there," Odo agreed. His thumb moved across the locket, back and forth, a small compulsive tide. "And the Graycloaks paid me four hundred crowns for names after the fire, and I gave them three, and my cousin's shop stayed burned anyway." He looked up, and for one moment the fence fell away and something older and sadder looked out. "You see the problem, carver. I am not brave. I am comfortable. Bravery is expensive and comfort is cheap, and I have chosen cheap every day of my life."

"Then don't sell me."

"I haven't sold you yet." He pushed the locket back across the counter. "Take it. Take it and go out through the tannery lane, not the square. And carver—" he raised one finger, "—understand that I will be offered four hundred crowns again by nightfall, and I have already told you exactly what I am."

* * *

They went out through the tannery lane, which stank of lime and old hides, and Theo hated himself for believing it would matter.

Sefa was waiting at the Cutwater Stair with the barge, arms crossed, her single eye tracking the market crowd above like a hawk counting rabbits. She'd moored the *Kindly Stranger* under a load of salted eel barrels, invisible to anyone not looking for a smuggler's hull, and she looked at Theo's face and knew before he spoke.

"He'll sell us," she said.

"Maybe not."

"He already has." She spat into the brown water. "Men like Odo don't decide to betray you, Reyes. They just stop deciding not to. Get the girl aboard. We run tonight, after dark—"

The bells started at midday.

Not the hour-bells. The alarm bells, from the Salt Gate tower, and then answering bells from the Harbor Watch, and then the flat brazen voice of a crier rolling down from the high town: *By order of the Crown Regent, seal the gates. A fugitive of state within the walls. Reward declared. Four hundred crowns.*

Four hundred crowns. Odo hadn't even waited for an offer.

"They were already in the city," Sefa breathed. "The bounty's not new — they just rang it. Gods' teeth, they've been sitting on it, waiting to see who'd lead them to you." Her eye found Theo's, and there was no blame in it, which was worse. "You walked your question past every ear in Vessany, carver. The scholar's house. The council hall. And now the fence."

Below the Cutwater Stair, the market was boiling. Theo could see it from the landing: the great bone market square where Vessany had traded its relics for three hundred years, stalls of yellowed ivory and whale-rib arches, and now the crowd tearing itself apart as gray-cloaked figures moved through it in pairs, unhurried, certain, holding up a paper that bore — he knew without seeing — a charcoal sketch of a girl and a description of a man with carver's hands.

"We can't reach the barge," Sefa said. "They'll watch the water. We go through the market, we go now, while they're looking for people running *away* from it, and we come around to the north stair after dark." She grabbed his sleeve. "Reyes. Look at me. You carry anything they can use?"

Theo's hand went to the satchel against his ribs. Inside: charge-bones, three days' rations, the forged relic that had fooled a captain at the Salt Stair, and a dozen small carved pieces he'd made on the road south — keepsakes, memorials, the work of his hands done in inns and campsites to pay for bread. Bones of the dead, each one carrying what remained of someone.

"I carry the dead," he said. "I always carry the dead."

"Then let's hope they're on our side."

* * *

The market swallowed them.

It was the wrong instinct and the right move at once — pushing *into* the panic instead of away from it, two travelers swept along with hundreds of others trying to reach home before the gates closed. Theo kept Ilset's hand locked in his and her hood up and his shoulders hunched around her like a wall. She moved without resistance, light as a bird on his arm, and only once did she look up — at a stall where a vendor was abandoning his goods, ivory birds scattering underfoot — and make a small sound in her throat.

"Don't look at anything," Theo murmured. "Eyes down. We're just poor and frightened like everyone else."

They were thirty feet from the north stair when the Graycloaks stopped being a rumor.

A pair of them stood at the stair mouth, checking faces, turning back anyone alone or armed. Beyond them, at the far end of the square, another pair had begun working the crowd methodically, and Theo understood with cold clarity that the market wasn't sealed yet — it was being *combed*. Someone had told them the fugitive was here, and now they intended to walk the square until they found her.

He turned. Too fast. A fishwife stumbled, swore, and Ilset's hood slipped back for half a heartbeat — half a second of pale hair and a face too calm for a child in a riot — and twenty feet away a Graycloak sergeant went still the way a pointer goes still.

"There," the sergeant said. Not loudly. That was the terrible thing about them; even hunting children they didn't shout. "There she is."

The square broke.

Not all at once — first a ripple, heads turning, the animal arithmetic of crowds deciding whether danger is theirs — and then the fishwife screamed and the ripple became a wave and the wave became a stampede, and Theo pulled Ilset off the open ground and behind a whale-rib stall as the first crossbow bolt buried itself in the rib with a sound like a tooth cracking.

"Run," he told her, and shoved her toward the alley behind the stall, and ran the other way.

It was the oldest trick and it worked for exactly as long as it took the sergeant to realize the fleeing man was the lesser prize. Theo heard boots behind him, two pairs, closing, and he ducked through the tanner's arch into the narrow lane where Odo's shop sat shuttered and innocent, and there he stopped, because there was nowhere left to go, and because the dead in his satchel had begun to sing.

He felt it through his palms first — the ache he'd known since boyhood, the resonance of remains near their end of usefulness, except this was different. This was every piece he carried crying out at once, not in grief but in *answer*, the way soldiers answer a call. The hanged smuggler's vertebra. The plague-dead woman's finger-bones he'd carved in an inn outside Vessany. The grandmother's knucklebone he'd never managed to sell. Twelve small deaths, awake, and beneath them something vaster: the market itself, three centuries of bone-trade, relics and remnants and saint's-tooth trinkets from a thousand graves, all of it humming now like struck glass.

Because he wanted them. Because for the first time in his life he wanted the dead to do something, and they heard it.

The Graycloaks came around the arch at a run and stopped.

The lane was full of ancestors.

They rose out of the stalls and doorways and gutters of the bone market — not ghosts, nothing so polite, but bone given motion, given *purpose*: a rib-arch wrenching free of its merchant's frame and sweeping like a scythe; a saint's forearm dragging itself up a wall on fingers of carved ivory; vertebrae streaming together into spined shapes that had never been animals and were terrifying anyway. And at their center, orbiting Theo like moons, the twelve from his satchel spun in a slow ring, clicking, singing in frequencies only he could hear.

The sergeant made a sound that was mostly prayer.

"Go," Theo said, and his voice came out doubled, harmonized, himself and everyone he'd ever carried speaking at once. "Tell your crown the carver is done hiding."

They fled. Of course they fled. Men trained to fight living things have no doctrine for the dead standing up.

Theo stood in the ringing lane with his arms shaking and his gift blazing openly for the first time in front of witnesses — dozens of witnesses, the whole market watching from windows and doorways, a fishwife on her knees, a boy laughing with delight, an old ivory-carver staring at the moving bones of his own trade with his mouth open. There was no taking it back. Every soul in Vessany's bone market had just seen a bone-carver raise the dead, and by nightfall the story would be in every tavern, and by week's end in every Graycloak garrison from here to the capital.

*Seen,* he thought. *Costs.*

Ilset appeared at the archway, drawn back by whatever current ran between them, and the bones settled around Theo like snowfall, gentle now, laying themselves down one by one with a tenderness that made the fishwife weep. He scooped them into his satchel with hands that wouldn't stop trembling.

"Reyes!" Sefa's voice, raw, from the north stair — she'd fought her way *to* him, gods help her, leaving the barge with a boat-hook in her fists and blood on her knuckles that wasn't hers. "Water! Now!"

* * *

They went down the north stair three steps at a time with the alarm bells still clanging overhead, and the Graycloaks did not follow, because the Graycloaks were busy being afraid.

The *Kindly Stranger* slipped her mooring before the harbor chain could rise — Sefa had greased the link-pin weeks ago, because Sefa greased everything — and Vessany fell away behind them in the smoke-colored afternoon, its towers and its bells and its four-hundred-crown bounty shrinking into the haze.

Theo sat in the bilge with Ilset asleep against his side, her hand curled around the royal locket, and watched his own hands shake. Wordless, Sefa put a cup of plum brandy in them and sat down across from him, her eye on the receding city.

"You know what you did," she said. Not a question.

"I know what I did."

"Every cloak in the province will know your face by the frost-moon. And hers." Sefa drank. "There's no more sneaking, carver. No more blind paths and forged papers. From here to Thornfell we're not travelers anymore. We're the story."

Theo looked down at Ilset, at the small steady breath of her, at the locket that held the beginning of everything.

"Good," he heard himself say, and meant it, and was frightened by how much he meant it. "Let them come knowing. We'll be at the choir before they understand what they're chasing."

Sefa laughed — short, harsh, admiring. Then her eye went past him, to the eastern shore, where the foothills of Thornfell should have been green above the river bend.

They weren't green. They were smoking.

Three fires, maybe four, burning in a line along the low country like lit fuses — and drifting across the water, faint but unmistakable, came a sound Theo had never heard before and knew instantly, the way you know a name called in a dream.

Singing. Thousands of voices, in perfect unison, coming downriver against the current.

The Veil's army was already in the valley, and it was singing the binding-song — backwards.
