## 11. Ilset Speaks

The river moved wrong.

Ilset had watched water all her life — the palace fountains, the cisterns beneath the keep where the memory-vats breathed in the dark, the slow gray veins of meltwater on the mountain — and none of it had prepared her for a river that refused to hold still long enough to be remembered. It kept remaking itself. Every second it was a new river, and it did not care who knew.

She sat on the deck of Sefa's boat with her knees pulled up, watching the brown water slide beneath them, and tried to think of herself as a river. New every second. Not the thing she had been made from.

"You're doing the staring again," Theo said from the stern.

He was mending a net with the smuggler woman, his hands moving in their carver's rhythm even around someone else's rope. He had beautiful hands. Courtier hands, said a voice in her that was not hers. Hands like Master Ellery's, who strung the king's lute.

"I'm not staring," she said. The common tongue still felt like walking in borrowed shoes. "I'm *watching*. There is a difference."

"Is there."

"In the old tongue there are nine words for watching and only one for staring." She held up one finger. "Watching is when you keep what you see." A second finger. "Staring is when what you see keeps you."

Theo's hands paused on the net. The smuggler woman — Sefa, one-eyed, sharp as a fish hook — snorted and went back to work, but Theo looked at Ilset a moment too long, the way he had begun looking at her lately. Like he was checking whether she was still home.

She was still home. Mostly. That was the problem. Home was getting crowded.

* * *

It started small, that night, moored in the reeds where the channel forked. She dreamed the way she always dreamed — in layers, other people's yesterdays laid over her own like wet cloth — but this time the dream did not stay behind her eyes.

She woke standing at the bow.

The moon was down. The reeds hissed. And her mouth was saying words in the common tongue, clear and complete, in a voice pitched lower than her own: "*Darling, the western gate — you must not sign the western accord, they will bleed you by winter—*"

She clapped both hands over her mouth. The words stopped. Her heart slammed. Behind her, in the shelter of the overturned boat, Theo slept sitting up with his arms around his satchel of bones, and he did not wake, and she stood very still until the sky went the color of ash, and then she went back to her blanket and lay with her eyes open and told no one.

But it happened again. Twice more before Vessany sank behind them and the hills took over. Once she called out a chamberlain's name in her sleep — *Corvan, Corvan, seal the letters* — and once she woke to find her fingers moving in the air, tracing the shapes of a signature she had never learned, a great looping royal hand, and when she looked at what she had written on the misted boards of the hull it was not her name at all.

It was *Aldric*.

She scrubbed it off with her sleeve before the others woke. It came away pale, like something that hadn't fully committed to being real.

* * *

On the fourth day upriver she lost an hour.

They were portaging around a weir, Sefa hauling the boat, Theo carrying Ilset over the slick stones, and then — nothing. No stones. No cold. She was in a hall hung with green silk, and rain was coming down outside in sheets, and a woman was laughing.

Ilset knew the laugh. She knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, which is to say: not at all until you notice it, and then completely.

The queen was young. Younger than Ilset had ever seen her in the memories — and that was the first wrongness, because the memories Ilset carried were supposed to be old ones, sealed ones, the deep archive, the things cut away before the Veil got hungry. This was recent. This was bright. This was *leaking*.

"*You'll catch your death,*" the queen said, holding out her hand in the green-lit hall, and Ilset took it, and the hand was warm, and then the stones were under her feet again and Theo was setting her down on the far bank and looking at her face and going white.

"Ilset. Ilset. Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"They're—" He stopped himself. Sefa had come around the boat and was watching with her one eye narrowed. "Gray," Theo said carefully. "Just now they went gray. All the way through. Like river fog."

"They do that," Ilset said, because it sounded better than *whose eyes would you like them to be?*

That night she could not pretend anymore, so she did not try.

* * *

She waited until Sefa took the watch and Theo banked the fire, until it was just the two of them and the dark and the sound of the water pretending to be the same water twice.

"Theo," she said. "Who is Maren?"

His hands stopped on the fire-stick. In the ember-light his face did something complicated — grief and confusion fighting for the same ground.

"There's no Maren," he said slowly. "My mother was Lissette. Why—"

"The tall one," Ilset said. Her mouth kept going without her permission, and this time she let it, because she wanted him to understand and she had no words of her own big enough. "Dark hair going silver at the temples. Laughs before the end of jokes. Sings badly and knows it. Wore a blue coat to the harvest festival three years ago and you told her it was your favorite color so she'd wear it again, and she did, every festival after, and then the fever year came and she didn't."

Theo had gone very still. The kind of still the dead go.

"She used to call you little wren," Ilset whispered. "She's not here anymore, Theo. I can't find her. I've been looking through everything I carry and she isn't anywhere, and I don't know why that hurts me but it does."

The fire cracked. Somewhere across the river an owl asked its one question.

"That's the queen," Theo said finally, hoarse. "Isn't it. You're talking about the queen."

"Maren Aldricane," Ilset said. "Yes. I weep for her every night, Theo, only I don't know I'm doing it until I wake with a wet face. She died two years before the Veil came, and he never once said her name aloud afterward, and so the Veil couldn't eat the grieving, because he'd locked it somewhere even he couldn't reach. He locked it in the part of himself he cut away." She pressed her fist against her own chest. "He locked her in me."

Theo came across the ground between them on his knees, slowly, the way you approach a bird. "Ilset—"

"I'm leaking," she said. "I know what leaking is. I was made to hold and now the seams are going, and everything he stored in me is coming out sideways into your world, and I can't—" Her voice broke and it broke in two voices at once, a child's and a king's braided together, and she saw Theo hear that. "I called you Master Ellery yesterday morning. Did you notice?"

"You said good morning twice," Theo said quietly. "In different accents."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Just tell me. Tell me all of it. Whatever it is, it goes easier shared. That much I've learned from the dead."

So she told him. All of it, in the common tongue because the old tongue was too honest and she needed somewhere to hide.

* * *

"When the plague came," she said, "the king was already afraid. Fear makes people do bargains; grief makes them do worse ones. But before any of that — before the crown and the choir and the thing that wears him now — there was a decision made in a room with no windows. The archivists came to him and said: the working you propose will require a vessel. Memory cut from a mind must live somewhere or die. If the binding holds, you will need somewhere safe to store the pieces of yourself most precious to you, so the Veil cannot reach them. And if the binding fails — if the Veil grows too large for one man —"

She stopped. Made herself finish.

"— then it must have somewhere else to go. Something to eat instead of the kingdom. A spare."

The fire had burned low. Theo wasn't breathing right.

"They grew me," Ilset said. "From what he cut away first. From his childhood and his mother's face and his love of bad singing and the name of his wife. They put those things in a child grown quick in vats under the keep, and they taught her a language older than kingdoms because the old tongue is what memory is spoken in, and they set her in a room below the throne room to wait." She smiled, and it was a terrible small smile. "Do you know what I thought my whole life? I thought I was a library. I thought I mattered because of what I held. And then the night the Veil began to chew through the last seals, the archivist who helped me run — she was crying, and she said *I'm sorry, little dove, I'm so sorry* — and I understood. Libraries aren't loved. They're *used*. I'm not a vault, Theo. I'm a pantry. I was made to be consumed. When the Veil finishes with the king, they were going to bring it a child."

"No," Theo said. Flat. Immediate. Like correcting a carving that had gone wrong.

"It's true—"

"I don't care if it's true." His voice shook, and Ilset realized, with a strange floating wonder, that he was angrier than she had ever seen anyone, and that none of the anger was aimed at her. "Listen to me. Whatever they made you *for*, that's not what you *are*. Things don't get to be what they were built as. Bones don't get to be knuckles forever. Sometimes they get carved into something else entirely, something nobody planned." He took her hands. His were warm and nicked with old blade-marks and they smelled faintly of the workshop she had changed his life in. "I decide what I am. Sefa decides. And you decide. Nobody else. Not even the dead."

"The king might disagree," Ilset whispered. "He's in here too. More of him every day. Some mornings I wake up and I'm eighty years old and my feet ache and I miss my wife so much I can't see straight, and it takes me a minute to remember I'm eight."

"Then we'll carry him too," Theo said simply. "But you first. You always first."

And Ilset, who had been made in the dark to be eaten, who had never once been put first by anyone in either of her lives, found that she was crying — her own tears this time, she was almost sure, salt from her own small sea — and that Theo was holding her, awkwardly, fiercely, the way you hold the thing you have decided to protect whatever it costs.

Over his shoulder, at the edge of the firelight, Sefa stood with her one eye shining and said nothing at all.

* * *

She woke before dawn to gray light and gulls, and for one blessed moment she was only Ilset — small, cold, hungry, ordinary. Then she opened her mouth to call Theo and what came out was not her voice and not quite the king's either. It was layered, both of them, resonant as struck bone, and it spoke in the old tongue, and every relic in Theo's satchel sang back in answer, a soft chord rising from the canvas like a congregation.

Downriver, thin with distance, something answered.

Not a bell. Not a bird. A long, patient note, like a throat clearing in an empty cathedral, and every hair on Ilset's arms rose, because she knew that sound. She had been grown beside it. It was the sound the Veil made when it turned its attention toward something it intended, eventually, to swallow.

Sefa was already at the tiller, cutting the mooring line with her knife.

"They found us," she said. "Or it found us. Same difference. Hold on to something, little dove."

The river bent north, toward Thornfell, and the patient note followed.
