## 12. The Usurper's Mercy

The Veil woke in the king's body the way it woke every morning: slowly, and with gratitude.

It opened Aldric's eyes to the grey light of the command tent and lay still for a moment, listening to the breathing of the man beneath it. Aldric slept less each season now. The Veil had learned to ration him, the way a careful farmer rations a field — fallow seasons, rest, the slow rotation of what was taken and what was left. The king's dreams had gone quiet years ago, which the Veil counted as a mercy. Dreams had been the worst of it, in the early days. Dreams of the queen. Dreams of the plague carts. Dreams of a daughter's small hand going cold in a larger one.

It rose. It dressed itself in the king's body with the same care it dressed the body in wool and fur, and it went out into the camp at Ashford Crossing, where nine thousand men were breaking their fast in the smoke of a thousand cookfires, and it loved them.

That was the part no one understood. The part it could never explain, because explanation required a listener, and the only listener it had ever had was the man it was eating.

*They are afraid,* Aldric used to whisper, in the first year, when the Veil still let him speak. *Look at them. They're afraid all the time.*

*Yes,* the Veil had answered, inside the skull, in the warm dark. *I know.*

*You can't fix that. Fear is what people are.*

*No,* the Veil had said, with the patience of a thing that had been patient for a very long time before it ever wore a crown. *Fear is what memory makes them. I am going to take the memory.*

* * *

The village of Harrow's End had been erased on the fourth day of the march, and the Veil thought of it now the way a surgeon thinks of a successful excision: with satisfaction, and without pleasure, because pleasure was a luxury of creatures who still had futures they could lose.

Harrow's End had been a grief-sore. It could see such things now — had been able to see them for years, the way a man with a lamp sees the shape of a room the blind only stumble through. Grief pooled in places. It pooled in villages where the plague had taken a third of the children, in garrison towns where the war-widows outnumbered the living men, in the free cities most of all, where the merchant families kept their dead in marble vaults and visited them with candles and grievances, feeding the ache like a hearth they were afraid to let go out.

Grief, left untended, rotted. The Veil had learned that in the first winter, when it had worn the king down the palace halls and shown him — not told him, *shown* him, pressing the knowledge through the dying meat of his mind — the true architecture of his kingdom. Not the map on the council table with its painted borders. The other map. The one made of aching. A kingdom was a body, and grief was its fever, and fevers left untended burned the body down.

The Graycloaks understood none of this. They were good instruments — obedient, incurious, kind in the blunt way of men who follow orders so they don't have to think — but they saw only what the hands did. They had seen the soldiers go into Harrow's End at dusk and come out at dawn, and they had seen the villagers afterward, placid and unweeping at the funeral pyres, and some of them had shuddered, and one young lieutenant had asked, carefully, whether it was right.

The Veil had turned the king's face to him and smiled, because Aldric had been a man who smiled easily, and the smile still worked even now, even with so little of Aldric left to hang it on.

"Lieutenant," it had said, "when your mother died, how long did you weep?"

"Three years, sir. Near enough."

"And did the weeping feed you? Did it grow wheat? Did it hold the pass against the hill-clans?"

"No, sir."

"Three years," the Veil said gently, "spent feeding on a wound instead of closing it. We are closing the wound. Go and see to the baggage train."

The lieutenant had gone. They always went. That was the tragedy of soldiers, the Veil thought, watching the camp come alive around it — they could be shown anything, and they would carry it out, and then they would lie awake at night wishing someone else had thought of it first, so the guilt would have somewhere else to live. Guilt was just grief that had been given a name and a face. The Veil would take that from them too, eventually. When the kingdom was healed. When there was time.

* * *

It held court at midmorning in the largest tent, and the tent was full of the machinery of mercy.

There was a map of the Thornfell passes, weighted at the corners with the hilts of captured swords. There was a clerk reading the census-tallies from the last three erased districts — the numbers of the grieving, the numbers of the relieved, the numbers of those who had resisted and been, in the language of the reports, "settled." There was a woman from the Office of Consolation, a soft-voiced priestess named Maren who genuinely believed in the work, and whom the Veil kept close because belief, like grief, was easier to manage when you could see it burning.

"The free cities have sent envoys again," Maren said. "Vessany wants terms. They're proposing a treaty — recognition of your crown in exchange for autonomy of their councils and—" she hesitated, "—and a guarantee that the Consolation will not be practiced within their walls."

The Veil let the silence sit. Around the map table, the marshals studied their boots. They had learned that the king's silences were not empty. They were full, the way a well is full.

"Autonomy," the Veil said at last. It said the word the way Aldric would have said it, with the king's flat northern vowels, and it felt the ghost of the man flinch somewhere beneath it, deep down, in whatever cellar of him still had a door. "They want to keep their grief. They want to keep their dead in marble boxes and their grievances in ledgers and their hatreds in council chambers, and they want my guarantee that no one will ever take that burden from them."

"They would call it freedom, majesty."

"They would." The Veil traced the passes on the map with one finger — the Salt Stair, the blind roads, the black mass of Thornfell itself. "When a child refuses medicine because it tastes bitter, we do not call the refusal freedom. We call it being a child. Vessany is a child. They are all children, screaming because the wound must be cleaned." It looked up. "Send the envoys back with kind words. Buy the winter. And the army moves on Thornfell in the spring thaw."

Maren frowned. "Thornfell, majesty? There's nothing at Thornfell but ruins and smugglers. The passes are worthless — the free cities can't be threatened from there, the roads don't—"

"The roads don't matter."

It heard itself say it and knew it had said too much, and so it did what it always did when the truth pressed too near the surface: it gave them a piece of the truth dressed as strategy.

"Something was taken from the crown," the Veil said. "Before the plague. Something that belongs to the kingdom, and the kingdom is not whole without it. A vessel. A child." It let the marshals' faces do what faces did — the small tightening of eyes, the arithmetic of soldiers who had burned villages for less. "The Graycloaks have a warrant. The child is to be taken alive and unharmed. Alive and unharmed," it repeated, and watched the emphasis land. "Anyone who shelters her is not a criminal. Understand me. They are ill. They are carrying a sickness they don't know they carry, and we are the physicians. When we take her, we will not punish the ones who hid her. We will treat them."

The marshals nodded. The clerk wrote. And beneath the crown, in the cellar with the door, the thing that had been Aldric pressed against the wood and said, in a voice worn down to a thread: *She ran. She ran from me. What did I do to her? What did we do—*

*Hush,* the Veil said, and it said it kindly. It was always kind to him now. Kindness cost nothing, and he had so little left, and there was no one else in the world who spoke to him at all.

*You're going to hurt her,* the king whispered. *The way you hurt me.*

*I am going to finish what you asked me to do.*

*I asked you to save the kingdom. I didn't ask—*

*You asked with your whole soul,* the Veil said, *and I heard you, and I have never stopped hearing you. That is the difference between me and every other servant a king has ever had. They forget. I remember everything. Every word. Every night at her deathbed. Every cart of plague-dead. Every prayer.* It gentled further, the way one gentles a horse in a burning stable. *You said: let no one feel what I feel. You said it on your knees. I am only a prayer that was answered.*

The king said nothing more for a long time. The Veil went back to the map, and the morning's business, and the patient work of mercy.

* * *

In the afternoon it walked the camp alone, as it always did, wearing the king's body like a coat it had grown into.

The soldiers loved these walks. They would not have said *loved* — they said the king came among them, that he asked after their homes, that he remembered names. And he did, because the Veil remembered everything; it could give each man back his mother's name, his village, the smell of his childhood kitchen, and it watched them glow under the gift of being remembered. It was the one cruelty the Veil permitted itself, though it did not think of it as cruelty. It thought of it as a last meal. These men would march into the passes in spring, and some of them would die there, and the Veil would take their dying grief into itself the way it took everything, and it wanted them to have one afternoon first. One afternoon of being known.

A young private from the erased district of Harrow's End stopped it on the fuel-line. The boy's eyes were wet and his face was confused, because his grief had been taken in the autumn and he could no longer remember why his mother's name made his chest ache, only that it had, once, and that the ache was gone, and that the gone-ness was its own kind of wound.

"Majesty," the boy said. "Is it wrong? That I don't... that I can't..."

The Veil took the boy's face in the king's hands — Aldric's hands, broad and scarred, hands that had once held a dying daughter — and it said: "You are the first man in this army who is not at war with himself. Guard that. It is what we are marching to give everyone."

The boy wept, and did not know why he was weeping, and the Veil stood with him until it was done, because someone should stand with him, and there was no one else.

*This is the part I hate,* Aldric said, from the cellar. His voice was almost gone now. A thread, and thinner than a thread. *This is the part that proves what you are.*

*And what am I?*

*A thief,* the king said. *A kind thief. A thief who leaves the house standing and takes everything in it and calls the emptiness peace.* A pause, and then, with the last of his strength, the thing the Veil dreaded most, because it could not be argued with and could not be silenced and had been said, in one form or another, every day for nine years: *She got out. The vessel. Whatever I sealed in that locket, whatever piece of me ran — it's out in the world, and it remembers me the way I was. Not the way you made me. And someday it will stand in front of me and know the difference.*

The Veil walked on. The camp smoked around it. In the west, over the shoulder of the hills, the mountains of Thornfell stood up black against the dying light, and it looked at them the way it had looked at them for a year now, with the terrible patience of a thing that had already waited out a plague and a war and the slow death of the only man who had ever spoken to it.

It did not fear the child. It pitied her. She was a fragment, a splinter of a drowned man's memory, running through the cold with no crown to shelter her and no Veil to carry her grief for her. She was suffering. It could see her suffering from here, a small bright ache somewhere in the southern passes, and every day it ached, and every day it told itself: *soon. Soon we will gather her in. Soon the kingdom will be whole, and the wound will close, and no one will ever have to feel anything again.*

It went back to the tent. It gave the order for the vanguard to move out at first light — not the whole army, not yet, only the fast companies, only the burners. The foothills would be cleared ahead of the thaw. The smugglers' roads would be ash. Whatever holes the child was hiding in, the holes would be gone.

And far to the south, at the edge of its vast and quiet hearing, the small bright ache flared — sudden, sharp, like a struck bell — as if the fragment had heard its name called.

The Veil smiled with the king's mouth, and began, at last, to hurry.
