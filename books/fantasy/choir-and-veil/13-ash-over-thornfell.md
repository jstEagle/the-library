## 13. Ash Over Thornfell

The smoke found them before the fire did.

Theo smelled it first as a sweetness under the cold — pine resin cooking somewhere below, then the sourness of thatch and barley, then the fat-slick stink of a barn going up with the animals still inside it. By midmorning the southern sky had gone the color of an old bruise, and when the wind shifted, ash came down on them like gray snow, settling in Sefa's braids, in the folds of Ilset's cloak, in the seams of Theo's knuckles where no amount of rubbing would drive it.

They were three days above the foothills now, climbing the old smuggler staircut into Thornfell's shoulder, and the world below them was learning to burn. Village after village — Halvern's Ford, the mill at Cresset Low, the terraced orchards above Sorrel — went up in sequence, west to east, like someone reading down a list and striking each name through as they finished with it. From the height of the pass you could watch it happen in real time. A village would go dark at its heart, one orange point, then bloom outward until the whole valley floor held its shape in fire, a map of itself drawn in flame, and then the wind would take the shape apart and leave only the smoke standing where the roofs had been.

Ilset watched from the ledge with her hands pressed flat against the stone, and she did not cry, which frightened Theo more than weeping would have. She had stopped crying two days ago, somewhere past the second burned village, and since then she had gone quiet in a way that wasn't silence so much as *containment* — a child holding something enormous behind her teeth and refusing to let it out because letting it out might be the end of everything. She ate what Sefa gave her. She slept when told. She walked without complaint up switchbacks that would have broken a grown soldier's wind. And all the while, Theo knew, the king's memories were stacking themselves inside her like snowfall on a roof, weightless one flake at a time, until the whole load came down at once.

He'd seen the first crack that morning. They had paused to breathe at a bend in the stair, and Ilset had looked down at the burning orchards and said, in the old tongue, four words. Then she'd turned to Theo and said, in his mother's voice — not a voice like his mother's voice; *his mother's actual voice*, the one he hadn't heard in nineteen years, dredged up out of whatever sediment of memory the king's mind carried or Ilset's own strange architecture held — she'd said: *the trees are people too.*

Then she'd blinked, come back to herself, looked at Theo with her own ordinary eight-year-old eyes, and asked if they could rest.

Theo stood on the ledge for a long moment afterward, ash sifting onto his shoulders, feeling the ground tilt under him in a way that had nothing to do with altitude. He thought about the workshop in Greyhollow. He thought about how he'd spent fifteen years listening to the dead and calling it a curse, and how he would have given anything, in those years, for one hour of true quiet — and how now, with the dead crowding in around him thicker than he'd ever known them, all he wanted was for the voices to stop meaning what they meant.

Because they did mean it. That was the thing he couldn't explain to anyone who hadn't stood inside his skull. The ghosts weren't noise. They were testimony. And the testimony was getting louder.

* * *

It started subtly, the way water rising in a cellar starts subtly — a damp smell, a dark line on the stones, nothing you could point to. On the first day above the foothills, Theo heard maybe a dozen voices in the rock around him, faint as breath: travelers who'd died on this stair over the centuries, smugglers caught in whiteouts, a shepherd boy who'd slipped on the ice in some winter before the war, before any war, back when the worst thing a mountain did was kill you honestly.

By the second day there were hundreds.

By the third — today, the day of ash — there were thousands, and Theo could feel them the way you can feel a crowd pressing against a door from the other side: not as sound anymore but as *pressure*, a weight in the stone itself, a density. The mountain here was full. It was full the way a riverbed is full of water, the way a bone is full of marrow. Every hand-span of rock they climbed past held somebody. He put his palm against a boulder at the third switchback and it rang under his touch like a struck bell, and the bell said, in forty overlapping voices, *we are here, we are here, we died here, do not forget we died here* — and beneath that chorus, deeper, older, a single thread of singing. The binding-song. Faint, looping, patient, coming up from far below like heat from a cellar. Like the choir was already humming to them through six hundred feet of mountain, saying: *you're close. You're close. Come down.*

Sefa noticed him stopping. She always noticed. One eye and twenty years of blind paths had made her a reader of men the way Theo was a reader of bones.

"You've gone strange," she said, coming back along the line of mules. "You've got the look. The one you get when the dead are talking business."

"They're not talking," Theo said. "They're *counting*. I can hear them counting." He didn't fully understand what he meant by that himself, except that the voices had changed register — less grief, more arithmetic. Thousands of dead doing sums. "Something down there is keeping a ledger."

"Everything down there keeps a ledger," Sefa said dryly. "That's why we're climbing it instead of walking around it."

But she said it lightly, and lightly wasn't what the moment was. Theo tried to tell her what the moment actually was — that the mountain was a sediment of the dead, packed layer on layer like chalk, like compressed snow, every era laid down over the last, and that near the choir the layers got denser and denser until the stone itself was more dead than mineral — and Sefa listened, because Sefa always listened, but she didn't flinch, and Theo realized partway through explaining that she already knew. Of course she knew. She'd run these paths for twenty years. She'd lost her brother to the crown's secret police and buried what was left of him where the ground allowed, and she had never once needed a bone-carver to tell her that mountains remember. She just hadn't known anyone else could hear it.

"The thing I don't understand," Theo said finally, "is why louder. Why now. If the dead are always here, why does the mountain get *fuller* the closer we walk to the place where the Veil was bound?"

Sefa chewed on that for the length of a switchback. Then she said, "Maybe because they know what's coming. Maybe the dead can read the same smoke we can. Or maybe—" and here she slowed her mule until they were riding abreast, close enough that their stirrups nearly touched, "—maybe they're not louder because there are more of them. Maybe they're louder because they're *waiting*. Because for six hundred years nobody has come up this stair who could actually do something, and now here you come, bone-carver, with your gift and your girl and your forged relics, and every soul packed into this rock can smell it on you the way the Graycloaks can smell it. Hope has a scent. Dead things remember scents."

Theo laughed, short and humorless, mostly because the alternative was to stand very still and let the size of what she'd said settle onto him like the ash was settling.

"You think I'm hope?" he said. "I'm a man with a chisel and a debt."

"I think you're a man the dead have been waiting for," Sefa said, "and I think you know it, and I think that's why you've been climbing slower every day and pretending it's the mules."

She wasn't wrong. He had been slowing them. Not much — a few minutes an hour, an extra rest at each bend, arguments about the weather that were really arguments about arrival. Because every step closer to the choir was a step closer to the answer he'd been circling since Vessany, the answer the scholar had given him plainly and he had refused to hold properly in his mind: the unbinding needed a living voice fluent in the old tongue. Ilset's voice. Singing it would empty her. And Theo had spent nine days telling himself there would be another way, that the scholar was wrong, that the dead would offer an amendment, that mercy would find a loophole — and the dead, with their thousands of voices rising out of the stone around him, had offered no amendment at all. They had offered only the song, looping, unchanged, exactly as it must have been sung the day the Veil went into the crown. No loophole. No amendment. Just the price, quoted again and again, patiently, in every voice the mountain owned.

The dead weren't cruel about it. That was almost worse. They sounded *certain*.

* * *

The vanguard reached the tree line on the fourth morning.

Theo saw it happen from the high col — a thin dark line coming up out of the burned country, moving along the river road toward the mountain's base, and then spreading, the way spilled ink spreads, into the first stands of surviving pine. Torches. Small ones, disciplined, spaced evenly, an army doing its work with the methodical calm of a harvest crew. They weren't charging. They were *clearing*. Burning the forest back from the lower slopes so the mountain would have nowhere to hide its defenders, nowhere to hide its paths, nowhere to hide anything — including, though the soldiers surely didn't know it, a mute child and a bone-carver and a one-eyed caravan mistress with three mules and a grudge.

Sefa saw them too, and her reaction was immediate and unsentimental: *up*. Everything up. Break camp in the dark, cut the mules' loads down to essentials, abandon the tent, abandon the spare feed, abandon everything that weighed more than a child's fear. She drove them hard that day, harder than she'd driven anyone in Theo's hearing, up switchbacks glazed with early ice, through a saddle where the wind came across the rock like a blade drawn sideways, and Theo understood that she wasn't running from the Graycloaks. She was running *ahead* of them, buying distance the way she'd once bought silence, spending effort now so they wouldn't have to spend blood later.

The plan, such as it was, lived in her head whole: climb through the night, cross the shoulder of Thornfell above the tree line where no army could follow quickly, descend the far side into the hidden combe where the sunken chapel-mouth opened onto the stair down to the choir. Two days, if the weather held. Three, if it didn't. Five, if the Graycloaks sent climbers — and they would send climbers, Sefa said, because Captain Verren did not write reports that ended in *the vessel escaped over terrain my men could not cross.* Verren wrote reports that ended in heads.

So they climbed. And the mountain climbed with them, its dead rising in density under their feet like a tide coming in, and Theo lay awake at the cold camps listening to voices he could now barely separate, a wall of sound with faces behind it, and somewhere in that wall — he was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of a name spoken in your own childhood kitchen — was his mother. Not her ghost. Her ghost had gone wherever ghosts go, years ago, released or dissolved or moved on; he'd felt the moment of her leaving as a silence shaped exactly like her. But the king's memories, the sediment Ilset carried, held a record of everyone the court had ever touched, and Theo had begun to suspect — had begun to *fear* — that somewhere in that archive was a woman named Maren Reyes, a carver's wife from Greyhollow, whose son had left home at seventeen and never written enough. If the Veil offered him her voice at the choir — and the Veil offered bargains, that was its nature, that was how it had gotten into the crown in the first place — Theo did not trust himself to refuse. He wanted to refuse. He'd rehearsed refusing. But wanting and trusting were different animals, and he knew which one he was.

He told Sefa this on the second night, in the lee of a boulder, while Ilset slept between them wrapped in both their cloaks. He told her because she was the only person alive who wouldn't flinch, and because he wanted, for once, to say a true thing out loud before the mountain could say it for him.

"If it offers," he said, "I don't know what I'll do."

"Yes you do," Sefa said.

"No. I don't. That's the honest answer. Nineteen years I've carved other people's mothers into keepsakes. I've held a thousand strangers' griefs in my hands and sold them back to them polished. And I have never once — not once — heard my own mother say my name. Do you understand what that's worth? What it would cost me *not* to buy it, if the price were a child?"

Sefa sat with that. The wind screamed past the boulder. Ilset slept. Somewhere below, the forest burned, and the light of it stained the clouds overhead a deep arterial red, so that the whole sky looked bruised from underneath.

"I'll tell you what I know," Sefa said at last. "I know a woman who spent twenty years hating a crown for killing her brother, and who discovered, somewhere on this stair, that hate wasn't heavy enough to carry her up a mountain in winter. Something else was carrying her. She didn't have a word for it for a long time. She has one now."

"What word?"

Sefa looked at him. One eye, steady as a fixed star.

"You," she said. "The word is you, Theo. Has been since Vessany, probably since the checkpoint, probably since the ledger, and I was too busy being useful to say it, and the mountain was too loud, and there was always another switchback, another burned village, another reason to keep the important things unsaid until the summit. Well. We're near the summit. And tomorrow somebody holds a rearguard, and I'm the one who holds rearguards, it's the oldest entry in my ledger, and I am done leaving true things unsaid on the theory that there'll be a better room later. There isn't a better room. There's just this one, and the red sky, and you."

Theo didn't move. For a long moment he didn't even breathe right. He'd imagined confessions a hundred times on this climb — usually his own, usually silent, usually addressed to a mother's grave — and he had never once imagined receiving one, here, in the open, at eleven thousand feet, from a woman with one eye and a mule and a criminal's laugh, in the plainest language he'd ever heard spoken at altitude.

"Sefa," he said, and then ran out of words, which for a man who dealt daily in the exact weight of other people's last words was its own kind of confession. So he said the small true thing instead of the large one: "I've been afraid to want anything on this mountain. Wanting felt like a luxury the dead couldn't afford me. But I—" he stopped, restarted, stopped again, and finally let it out the way you let air out of a sealed jar, all at once, ugly and clean: "I love you. I've loved you since the Salt Stair, when you lied to a captain with a straight face to protect a child you'd never met, and I watched you do it and thought, *that's the bravest thing I've ever seen a person do*, and also, in some back room of myself I didn't open for weeks because opening it felt like treason against the mission — I thought, *I want to be the kind of man who deserves to stand next to that.*"

"And are you?"

"I'm finding out. Daily. Usually the hard way."

Sefa laughed — that laugh, the one that started as a cough and ended as a kind of victory — and leaned her shoulder against his, and for perhaps a minute, in the red-lit dark, with an army climbing the mountain below them and a parasite wearing a kingdom above them and a child sleeping between them like a coal that hadn't decided whether to go out, they simply sat together, two people saying a true thing in a place built entirely of untrue ones.

Then she pulled her coat straight, checked the mules' pickets, and said, "Rearguard's mine at dawn. Don't make it weird."

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of char.

The Graycloak climbers hit the lower switchbacks an hour behind schedule — Sefa had guessed they'd waste time on the burned forest's edge, and she'd guessed right — but they came, thirty of them roped in teams, moving with the grim competence of men who'd been promised bonuses per head. Theo watched them from the col through Sefa's spyglass: small figures in gray wool, torches doused now in daylight, axes slung, climbing in that awful orderly way armies climb, as if the mountain were a staircase in a house they owned.

The plan was simple and Sefa delivered it simply: she'd take the two armed men they'd picked up at Vessany — brothers, ex-carabinieri, loyal to her ledger rather than to any flag — and hold the narrows at the fifth switchback, the one place on the route where three climbers could make ten feel like fifty. Theo, Ilset, and the lead mule would push ahead over the shoulder without stopping, without looking back, without — and she said this twice, firmly — *without coming back for her*, because coming back was how rearguards became massacres, and she had buried enough brothers-in-trade to know the arithmetic cold.

Theo argued. Briefly. Badly. He argued with the specific desperate logic of a man who has just been told the person he loves is volunteering to stand between danger and everyone he's responsible for, and who knows, with terrible clarity, that arguing will cost time and time costs lives and the argument is therefore itself a betrayal of the person he's arguing for. Sefa cut it off in four sentences: *I've held worse narrows with fewer men. You have the vessel. The vessel is the mission. Go.*

So they went.

Theo took Ilset's hand and climbed. The dead rose around them like floodwater — he could feel the density changing under his boots with every hundred feet, the mountain thickening, voices layering over voices, the binding-song growing from a thread to a chord — and he climbed through it the way you wade a river, head down, hand gripped, listening despite himself. And the dead, who had counted for days, began to do something else. They began to *watch*. He felt it as attention, as a turning, as ten thousand buried faces orienting toward the same point on the stair. Toward Ilset. The girl the mountain had been waiting for. Some of the voices said her name — not *Ilset*, which was the name Sefa had given her on the road, but something older, syllables Theo half-recognized from the locket's inscriptions, the vessel-name, the one the palace records had used. The dead knew her. The dead had *always* known her. She was theirs in some way he couldn't parse yet, a seed of the king's memory planted in living flesh, and the mountain received her passing the way soil receives rain.

Behind them, at the narrows, the fight started.

Theo heard it before he could see it — heard it partly with his ears and partly, horribly, through the stone, because the dead at the narrows transmitted it, relayed it upslope through six switchbacks of packed souls like a chain of signal fires made of ghosts. Steel. Shouts. A scream that cut off. Another that didn't. Sefa's voice, giving orders in smuggler-sign, clipped and level, and then — this was the detail that would live in Theo's chest for the rest of his life — Sefa's voice *changing*, halfway through the fight, from command to something rawer, and then the sound of the narrows themselves, the rock ringing under his palm when he pressed it, ringing with the dead's account of what was happening below: *three holding thirty, three holding thirty, one down, two holding, the one-eyed woman is laughing, the one-eyed woman is laughing while she fights, they cannot believe her, they keep dying—*

Ilset heard none of it. Theo made sure of that. He kept her moving, kept her talking — reciting the binding-song's opening phrases, anything, words, words, a wall of ordinary speech between the child and the battle below — and she climbed well, better than well, small and fierce on the ice-glazed steps, and only once, near the shoulder, did she falter. Only once did she turn and look back down the stair into the smoke, and Theo saw her face do something no eight-year-old's face should know how to do: she looked back *knowingly*. She looked back the way you look back at a door you've already decided to walk through later. And then she took his hand again and kept climbing, and Theo understood, with a dread that settled into his bones like the ash, that the child had heard everything after all — that she'd been hearing it for days through channels he didn't have, king's channels, vessel's channels — and that she had already done the arithmetic Sefa had done, and had reached the same total, and had quietly, without a word to anyone, accepted it.

They crossed the shoulder at dusk.

Below them, the narrows went quiet. The relay of dead fell silent, switchback by switchback, like lamps going out, and the last transmission up the chain was not a report at all but a single sentence passed mouth to mouth through the sediment, ghost to ghost, until it reached Theo's hand on the stone:

*She bought you the night. Use it.*

And then, a minute later, fainter, almost lost under the binding-song's loop:

*Tell him the ledger's in the green case. Tell him it was never the eye I minded losing.*

Theo knelt on the frozen shoulder of Thornfell with Ilset's hand in his and the red burn-light staining the clouds below and the choir humming somewhere under his knees, six hundred feet of dead between him and it, and he did not weep, because the dead around him were watching and he had learned on this mountain that grief performed in front of witnesses becomes testimony, and he was not ready to testify. He was not ready. He would be. Tomorrow, in the sunken dark, with the ledger in the green case and the song waiting and the child already knowing the price — he would be ready, or he would stand beside her anyway, which amounted to the same thing.

The night held. The army regrouped below. And at first light, at the mouth of the hidden combe, where the chapel ruin crouched over its drowned stair, Theo lifted the green case from the mule's back, set it down beside his daughter-of-the-road, and looked down into the earth that had been singing to them for nine days.

The stair waited. So did everything else.
