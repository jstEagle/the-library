## 14. The Choir Beneath

The stair went down farther than any stair had a right to.

Theo counted the first hundred steps out of habit, the way he counted knots in a length of sinew, and then he stopped counting because the numbers stopped meaning anything. The walls sweated. The air came up cold and mineral, tasting of iron and old water, and beneath it — under it, woven through it like thread through cloth — ran the song.

He had been hearing it for nine days, faint as a hairline crack in glass. Down here it was not faint. It filled the stairwell the way water fills a lung.

It was not a pretty song. That was the first thing that unsettled him. He had expected something like the voices in his bones — the murmured confessions, the last words, the small human wreckage the dead left behind. This was older than wreckage. It was vowels without language, stacked in ranks, rising and falling in a pattern that never resolved and never changed. A loop. A knot tied in sound and asked to hold forever.

Sefa paused two steps above him, one hand on the wet wall, her bound shoulder dark where the bandage had seeped through. "How far?"

"Ask the stone," Theo said. "It's the one singing."

Behind them both, Ilset put her bare foot — she still would not wear the boots Sefa bought her in Vessany — onto the next step, and the song *shifted*.

It was slight. A half-tone, maybe less. But Theo felt it in his teeth, in the plates of his skull, and every bone in the green case on his back rang once, softly, like forks in a drawer when a door slams. Ilset looked up at him with those eyes that were not quite an eight-year-old's eyes and said nothing, because she had already understood what he was only now grasping.

The choir knew she was here. The choir had been waiting for her.

* * *

They came down out of the stair into the cavern near noon, though noon was a word that meant nothing in a place the sun had never touched. Sefa struck her storm-lantern, and its little circle of light seemed almost rude, a stranger pushing into a temple.

Then Theo saw the scale of it and forgot to breathe.

The cathedral was sunken, drowned in its own mountain, and it had not sunk empty. The nave stretched away into darkness beyond the lantern's reach, and its pillars rose from black standing water, each one carved — no, *grown*, there was no chisel-mark anywhere — in ranks of figures ascending toward vaulted heights that the light could not find. Men and women, their faces worn to suggestion, their mouths open in the universal shape of song. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Sediment of the dead, packed into stone like shells into chalk.

And they were all singing.

Not the carvings. The carvings were only stone. But the dead themselves were here, pressed into the rock the way pressed flowers are pressed into books, and Theo heard them the way he had heard the dead his whole life — except that this was not one voice or a hundred. It was all of them at once, layered and looped, singing the binding-song back and forward and back again, and they had been singing it since before Greyhollow had a name.

"Theo." Sefa's voice came out flat and careful, the voice she used when the caravan was spooked. "Tell me what I'm hearing. And don't say nothing."

"You couldn't hear it if you weren't meant to," he said. "Most people would walk through here and feel cold and want to leave."

"And us?"

"We were always going to be meant."

Ilset walked to the edge of the black water and crouched, the way she crouched at everything — completely, without fear, a child's body making a scholar's posture. Where her reflection should have been, the water showed only ripples. She dipped one finger in, tasted it, and made a face that was, for one second, purely eight years old.

"Salty," she said, in the old tongue. Then, in her broken common: "Tear-water."

"Charming," Sefa muttered.

But Theo had gone still. Because the moment Ilset spoke, the song shifted again — more than a half-tone this time — and for the space of three heartbeats, he heard *words* in it. Not the endless vowel-stack. Words. A line of melody breaking free of the loop like a fish surfacing, saying something urgent and specific and then drowning back under:

*—the crown, the crown is the knot, the knot must be sung loose by a throat that—*

And then the loop closed over it and the song was only sound again.

"What did it say?" Ilset asked. She had turned to look at him. She always knew when the dead spoke to him; she said their voices moved his face like wind moves wheat.

"It didn't finish." He set the green case down on the first dry flagstone and knelt beside it. His hands, he noticed, were shaking. He was a bone-carver; his hands did not shake. "It started to say how the unbinding works. Something about a throat."

"Whose throat?" Sefa demanded.

He didn't answer, because he was afraid he already knew, and saying it would make it load-bearing.

* * *

They made camp on a wide ledge above the waterline, and Theo worked, because working was how he thought. He laid out the relics he had carried nine days and three hundred miles: the fragments of Aldric's testimony he had gathered from sympathetic ghosts along the smuggler roads, wrapped in wool; the coronation-bone Ilset had carried out of the palace in her locket; and the plain river-bone he used for listening, unremarkable, worn smooth by twenty years of his thumbs.

He held the listening-bone to his ear the way a child holds a shell, and let the choir pour through him.

It was too much. It was like drinking a river. In his workshop he had handled four, five ghosts at a time, each with a name and a grievance and a final sentence. Here there were thousands, and they were not individuals anymore; they had been singing together so long they had fused into a single instrument, and the instrument was playing one piece of music, forever, and had forgotten everything else.

Except it hadn't forgotten. That was the horror of it. When he listened closely, he could feel the whole of them straining against the loop the way a sleeper strains against a dream — each dead soul still separately aware, still buried alive inside the chorus, still *himself* or *herself*, forced to sing the binding over and over because the binding required singers and no one had ever thought to ask what it cost the singers.

The Veil had been bound here. And the price of the binding had been paid, in advance, in perpetuity, by the dead.

"Oh," Theo said aloud, very quietly. "You poor bastards."

The song faltered. Just for a beat. As if thousands of throats had stumbled on the same syllable — surprised, after centuries, at being pitied.

Sefa sat down across the lantern from him, cradling her wounded arm. "You've got a face on you. The bad kind. The kind you had before the checkpoint, and before Vessany, and before every other disaster you've walked us into."

"I think I know how it ends," Theo said. "The unbinding. I think I know why nobody's ever done it."

"Say it anyway."

He looked past her, to where Ilset slept — actually slept, truly, deeply, in the way she rarely allowed herself — curled against a pillar carved with a woman whose open mouth had worn smooth. Even asleep, the girl's lips moved faintly, shaping syllables of a language older than kingdoms.

"The binding was sung into the crown," Theo said. "Live voices, the old tongue, a choir of the willing. To unbind it you have to sing the same song reversed. Un-weave the knot instead of tying it. Same power, run backward." He turned the listening-bone over in his fingers. "But the song can't be sung from memory or notation. There's no notation. It has to be sung by a living voice fluent in the old tongue — fluent the way she is fluent, from the inside. And the song isn't just sounds. It's the binding itself. Singing it means *being* it. Pouring yourself into the knot so you can pull it apart."

"So the singer—" Sefa started.

"Gives everything. Everything she is. The song empties the cup to fill the knot." He heard his own voice go distant, clinical, the voice he used to quote the dead because quoting was easier than feeling. "She'd sing herself out. Whatever's left afterward wouldn't be Ilset. It might not even be anyone."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere out over the black water, deep in the nave, the choir resumed its loop, patient as geology.

"She volunteered already, didn't she," Sefa said. Not a question. "Back at the pass. You told her the plan and she nodded and you thought she didn't understand, and you've been telling yourself that ever since."

"She's eight."

"She's a vessel grown from a king's memories to be consumed. She told you that herself, on the river. She was *made* to be poured out, Theo. The only difference between the Veil's plan and ours is that ours asks her permission first." Sefa's one eye held him, unblinking, terrible. "That's not comfort. That's the arithmetic, and I've watched lawful men do arithmetic their whole careers and call it mercy."

"Don't." Theo's voice came out harder than he intended. "Don't you dare make this clean."

"Nothing about it is clean. That's my point." She leaned back against the pillar and closed her eye. "You've got one night to find another way, carver. So find one. Or get ready to stand in front of that child tomorrow and tell her the truth, which is that you brought her three hundred miles to be spent."

* * *

He did not sleep. He walked the ledge instead, past the sleeping shapes of Sefa and Ilset, down to the water's edge, and he listened — really listened — opening himself to the choir the way he had never dared open himself to anything.

And the choir, sensing him, did something it had not done in centuries.

It changed the song.

Not much. One thread pulled loose from the loop, one voice rising clear above the rest — a single dead man, or the memory of one, an old voice with a young man's grief still folded inside it. The founder, maybe. The first singer. And the voice sang him a fragment, just a fragment, of the binding-song rendered slow and separate, and Theo understood it not with his ears but with his gift, the way he understood every ghost who had ever passed through his hands:

*We tied the knot with living breath because only life can bind. We did not know that only life can loose it. Forgive us. We saved the world and chained ourselves to the winch.*

"Why her?" Theo whispered to the water. "Why does it have to be her? She's a child. She never asked for any of this. She's the only innocent in this whole rotten story."

*Because she is what we made her to be,* the voice sang back, gentle as ash falling. *A vessel. A spare. We grew her from the king's severed memory against the day the crown failed — not to save her, but to save everyone else. We are sorry. We have been sorry for six hundred years, and sorry has changed nothing.*

"There has to be another way."

*There is always another way,* the dead sang. *Every way costs. Show us yours, bone-carver, and we will sing it with you.*

And Theo stood at the edge of the tear-water sea, in a cathedral of the chained dead, and reached for another way, and found—

His mother's voice. Sixteen years dead, and he had never heard it, not once, not through all his gifts. He found it now, imagined it, built it out of nothing in the dark, and he understood with sudden terrible clarity that he would trade anything, *anything*, for one more way that didn't end with a child on an altar.

But there wasn't one. There was only the loop, and the child asleep on the ledge, and morning coming down the drowned stair.

Behind him, soft on the stone, he heard small feet. He turned.

Ilset stood at the mouth of the ledge in her nightshirt, awake, having heard none of the dead but somehow all of it anyway, looking at him with eyes old enough to know exactly what he had been asking the water, and exactly what the water had answered.

"Theo," she said, in the old tongue, in the voice that made bones ring. Then, carefully, in common: "I sleep now. Before, I listen." Her small hand found his. "I am not afraid. Teach me the reverse."

Out over the black water, the choir began, very slowly, very gently, to sing her the first line.

It sounded, Theo thought, like a lullaby.

It sounded like a knife being sharpened.
