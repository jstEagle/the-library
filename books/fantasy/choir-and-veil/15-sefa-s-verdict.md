## 15. Sefa's Verdict

The wound had gone through her like a spear through wet canvas — clean entry under the collarbone, ugly exit below the shoulder blade, and everything between it torn and leaking. Sefa Marun knew wounds the way other people knew weather. She'd catalogued them across twenty years of blind paths and burned caravans: the arrow that clipped a rib and sang out the other side, the knife that opened her forearm on the Salt Stair, the fever that nearly took her leg at Vessany's docks. She knew what this one was. She'd watched men die of it. She'd watched them die slowly, which was worse.

She lay on a pallet of pine boughs in the back hollow of a shepherd's hut above Thornfell, wrapped in wool that smelled of smoke and someone else's sweat, and she refused to die quickly because dying quickly would be convenient, and Sefa Marun had never once in her life done the convenient thing.

The hut belonged to no one now. The shepherd had gone south with the last of the foothill refugees three days ago, driving his sheep ahead of him like a man pushing a boulder uphill against the tide of history. The vanguard had passed through the valley below, burning as they went — not the disciplined burn of soldiers clearing ground, but the patient, methodical erasure of the Veil's mercy, village by village, memory by memory. Sefa had seen it from the ridge with her one eye before the fighting took her down. She had seen a granary full of people who did not remember why they were hiding in it. She had seen a woman stand in her own doorway and smile at the Graycloaks coming to unmake her, smiling because they had already taken the grief out of her, taken the dead child from her heart the way you'd take a splinter from a hand, gently, gently, until there was nothing left to hurt and nothing left to hold.

That was the thing about the Veil that no one understood who hadn't watched it work up close. It didn't look like an invasion. It looked like relief.

* * *

Theo sat beside her pallet, working a whetstone along a carving knife with the absent rhythm of a man whose hands needed occupation or they'd start shaking. He'd been at it for an hour. The stone whispered against steel, whispered, whispered, and Sefa listened to it the way she used to listen to rain on caravan canvas — counting the intervals, waiting for the pattern to break.

"You're going to ruin the edge," she said finally. "You've been at it so long the blade's thinner than my patience."

"It keeps my hands busy."

"I know what it does." She shifted against the boughs and immediately regretted it; the pain came up through her chest like a tide finding its mark, and she rode it down instead of crying out, because crying out would cost breath and breath was currency now, and Sefa Marun balanced her books to the last coin. "Ask me what you came to ask me."

Theo set the stone down. His hands went still on his knees, and stillness looked strange on him, like a man wearing someone else's coat.

"You're dying," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen the wound. He'd packed it himself with the moss and the boiled wine, and he'd seen enough of the dead in his bones to know the difference between a body that would heal and a body that was already being claimed. "I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

"Good. I hate pretending."

"The choir needs a voice. A living voice, fluent in the old tongue. That means Ilset sings the binding-song reversed, and singing it empties her — whatever she is, whatever she carries, poured out into the stone." He said it flatly, the way he'd rehearsed it, probably to the ghosts, probably all night. "Sefa. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me there's another way."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the pass with a sound like a crowd murmuring in a language just below hearing, and she thought: even the mountain is full of voices now, and none of them are hers.

"You want me to tell you it's wrong," she said. "Not that it's wrong. You want permission to feel what you already feel."

"Maybe."

"Then here it is." She pulled herself up on her good arm, slow, grinding, refusing help when he reached for her, because if she was going to say what she'd come twenty years and one eye and one brother to say, she was going to say it standing upright in her own body, even lying down. "It's wrong. Not the way you think. Not because the child suffers. Children suffer in every war ever fought, and kingdoms have always spent them like copper coins — one child for a city, two children for a border, a generation for a peace. The arithmetic always balances. That's what makes it evil. If the arithmetic didn't balance, we could call it tragedy and grieve and move on. But it balances. It always balances. That's why it never stops."

Theo's hands curled closed.

"I ran cargo for Aldric's court," Sefa said. "Twenty years ago. Before the Veil, before the crown went rotten — or while it was going rotten, which amounts to the same thing, since rot doesn't announce itself, it just smells sweet a little too long. There was a winter when the court needed grain moved through passes the law had sealed, and the law seals passes during famines, did you know that? Seals them tight, so the hungry can't reach the food and the food can't reach the hungry, because movement is disorder and disorder is expensive. So the crown hired smugglers. Hired me. And the arithmetic balanced beautifully — grain moved, famine blunted, no one counted the villages that starved anyway because the roads were closed to everyone but us." She stopped. Breathed. The pain rose again and she let it, because some things deserved to be said through pain; it proved they cost something. "My brother Dario ran the east road. The secret police took him in spring. They called it treason. They executed him in the square at Vessany with a paper in his hand proving his innocence, and the paper was burned afterward, and the execution was entered in the rolls as lawful, and lawful is the word that lets everyone sleep at night. Lawful. The most dangerous word in any kingdom. I lost my eye to the same raid that took him. I've had twenty years to decide what I think about lawful arithmetic, Theo Reyes, and here is my verdict—"

She pointed at him with two fingers, the old caravan gesture, the one that meant *listen and don't interrupt.*

"—a law that spends children is not broken. It's working exactly as designed. And justice outside the law begins the moment you refuse to do the math."

* * *

He didn't answer right away. Sefa hadn't expected him to. Theo Reyes was a man who processed the world through his hands and his ghosts, and both of those were quiet now — the ghosts scattered or silent since the child arrived, the hands holding nothing but a knife and a whetstone and the terrible weight of a choice that had no clean edge to carve along.

She watched him the way she'd once watched avalanches build on the high cornices: reading the load, reading the angle, knowing the release was coming and not knowing what it would take with it when it went.

"They'll say it's necessary," he said at last. "The councils. The scholars. Anyone I ask. They'll say one vessel, one child grown for exactly this purpose, against a nation being eaten alive. They'll say she was made to be spent."

"She was." Sefa let that sit. "And the man who made that decision — whoever grew her in whatever cold room of the palace, whoever decided that a spare vessel was cheaper than a solution — that man was doing arithmetic too. You understand? The Veil didn't invent the spending of children. It inherited the practice. It found a kingdom that had been balancing its books on small bodies for centuries and it simply became the newest entry in the ledger." Her voice roughened, and she let it. "I keep a ledger myself. Have for twenty years. Every crime of the crown I witnessed or carried or cleaned up after. Names, dates, roads, orders signed. Dario's execution warrant — I bought a copy off a clerk with three years of smuggling profit. The famine routes. The villages erased by the Veil's mercy, village by village, with the dates the grief went out of them. It's all written down. Because here's what I learned losing an eye and a brother to lawful men: the dead don't need avenging, Theo. The dead need *naming.* A name spoken aloud, entered into record, witnessed. That's the only immortality that matters and the only justice that scales."

"And you want it read at the unbinding."

"I want it *spoken* at the unbinding." She turned her head on the pallet, and her one eye caught the low firelight and held it. "When the Veil comes apart — when the king dies human and the choir goes quiet — there will be one moment, one only, where every ear in that cavern belongs to the truth. The dead will be listening. The living will be listening. Even the parasite, unraveling, might listen — parasites hate ledgers, they live on forgetting. In that moment, someone must stand in the stone room where a king made a bargain to end a plague, and say aloud: here is what your crown did while it saved you. Here is who paid. Here are the names. Not to damn Aldric — God knows I've made my peace with pitying that man — but to damn the *practice.* To make the free cities hear that the bargain was never singular. That every negotiated peace they're voting on right now in Vessany is the same bargain, the same arithmetic, dressed in new silk. One child. One village. One memory at a time. Balance, balance, balance."

Theo was looking at her the way he sometimes looked at bone before the first cut — seeing not the object but the shape inside it, the thing waiting to be released.

"Sefa," he said, "you sound like a woman writing her own eulogy."

"I am." She smiled, and it was the crooked half-smile that had unnerved customs officers and charmed dock thieves for two decades. "A eulogy is just a verdict delivered to an audience that finally has to listen. I've been drafting mine for twenty years. It's short. It goes: *Sefa Marun, smuggler, one-eyed, unlawful, correct.*"

* * *

She made him wait until dark to give him the ledger. Not for drama — though Sefa Marun was not above drama, drama was a tool like any other, a pry bar for stubborn minds. She waited because the giving itself had to be done properly, and proper things required darkness and firelight and no witnesses but the ones who mattered.

The ledger lived in a oilcloth wrap against her belly, where she'd carried it through checkpoints and river crossings and one memorable week hiding in a pickle barrel outside Vessany. She'd sewn it into her coat lining after Dario died and unpicked the stitches only twice a year to add pages. It was fat as a psalter now. Forty years of crown crimes compressed into a smuggler's cramped hand — she wrote left-handed since the raid took her depth perception, and her script slanted like a road running downhill, but every line was legible, because evidence that can't be read is evidence that doesn't exist.

She worked it free of the wrapping with fingers that wouldn't fully close anymore, and the effort cost her more than she showed, and she showed him nothing, because that was the deal she'd made with her own body: *you may fail me, but you will not embarrass me.*

"Hold out your hands."

He held them out. Carver's hands, scarred at the knuckles, steady as a mason's. Good hands. Hands that had spent years listening to the dead and were about to spend one night learning to speak for them instead.

"This is yours now," Sefa said, and laid the ledger in them. "Not loaned. Given. I'm past the age of lending things I mean to keep."

"It should go to the councils. To Vessany, to the archive—"

"It will. After. But first it goes where I can't carry it anymore." She pressed his fingers closed over the cover, both her hands around his one pair, a smuggler's grip, the grip you used to pass contraband under a table while the customs man looked elsewhere. "Listen to me, Reyes. At the unbinding, when the song is sung and the Veil unravels and the king is dying human in the stone — you will be tempted to make it beautiful. You're a maker; beauty is your trade. You'll want the moment clean. Quiet. Forgiveness without testimony, mercy without accounting, the catharsis without the bill. Don't you dare. Beautiful endings are how kingdoms launder their crimes. The Veil itself wanted a beautiful ending — that's what its mercy *was*, a beautiful ending, grief dissolved, accounts closed, no one left to collect. If you let the unbinding be beautiful and silent, you've built the parasite a monument instead of a grave."

"Then what do I do?"

"You open the ledger. You read the names. All of them, or as many as the hour allows, starting with Dario Marun, executed unlawfully in the square at Vessany, in the spring of the sealed roads, and entered in the rolls as treason." Her voice cracked on her brother's name and she let it crack, deliberately, because a crack in a voice is a hook, and hooks catch listeners, and listeners are what the dead have been owed for twenty years. "Read them until the free cities' delegates in that cavern understand that their negotiated peace is a receipt for the same crime. Read them until the dead in the stone stop singing the binding-song and start hearing something else for the first time in a generation: their own names, spoken by a living mouth, in a room where it counts."

"And if there isn't time?"

"There's time." She said it with the flat certainty of a woman who had moved cargoes through impossible windows her whole life. "There's always time for the true thing and never time for the safe one. That's not fatalism, Reyes, that's scheduling."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the fire had burned down to a red memory of itself and Theo had fallen asleep sitting up with the ledger under his coat like a man guarding a heartbeat that wasn't his own — Sefa lay awake and did her final inventory.

Her eye: spent. Her brother: named, at last, in a document that would outlive the rolls that burned him. Her caravans: scattered, her routes known now to Graycloak scouts, her smuggling days ended not by capture but by arithmetic, the wound having finally presented a bill she couldn't dispute. Her body: closing its books page by page. She could feel it happening the way she could feel weather in the old arrow-scar — a cooling, a settling, a tide going out. Two days, maybe three. The moss and the wine had bought her that much and no more.

Two days was enough. It had to be. Because the vanguard was in the foothills, and the main host was behind it, and somewhere in that host rode a crowned thing that genuinely believed it was saving everyone, and the passes would not stay open long, and the choir beneath Thornfell would not wait forever.

She thought about Ilset sleeping in the hut's far corner, small and breathing, wrapped in the shepherd's second blanket, dreaming — if vessels dreamed — dreams made of a dead king's memories, coronations and plagues and a queen's voice. Sefa had watched the girl for three days through her one eye. She had watched her carry water without being asked. Watched her sit beside Sefa's pallet for hours, saying nothing — the girl couldn't say anything, not in any tongue the living used — and once, only once, reaching out to touch the bandage over Sefa's ruined eye with two fingers, feather-light, the way you touch a wound you recognize because you carry its twin somewhere deeper.

A child grown to be consumed, who touched a dying woman's wound like a sister touching a sister.

And the councils in Vessany would spend her. The scholars would spend her. Half of Theo's own ghosts, if they still spoke, would argue for spending her — the dead are practical, the dead have always been practical, death is the great simplifier and it loves a clean equation. One vessel, emptied willingly, against a nation's grief. Balance. Balance. Balance.

"No," Sefa said aloud, to the dark, to the mountain, to whatever was listening in the sediment of the stone. "No more balance."

She had spent her whole life moving goods the law forbade, and she had learned the difference between unlawful and unjust, and it was this: the law asks *what may be done,* and justice asks *what must be refused.* Every smuggler worth the name understands refusal. Refusal is the whole trade. You refuse the checkpoint, the tariff, the seal on the road, the paper that says a man is a traitor. You refuse, and you carry the true cargo through anyway, over the blind paths, in the dark, at cost.

Ilset was the true cargo. The last one. And Sefa Marun, dying, one-eyed, unlawful, intended to see her delivered — not emptied, not spent, not balanced — delivered, whole, to the far side of this war, walking out of Thornfell under her own name with her own voice in her own throat.

Whatever it cost. The bill was already rendered. She'd signed for it on the ridge with a Graycloak spear.

Toward dawn, Theo woke, and saw her watching him, and started to speak, and she stopped him with two raised fingers.

"One more thing," she said. "Before the Graycloaks come down the mountain and we run out of hours. When this is done — when the choir is quiet and the Veil is ash and the girl is singing in whatever tongue she chooses — find the clerk who sold me Dario's warrant. He's still alive. He drinks at the Anchor and Chain in Vessany and he hates himself in a useful way. He kept a second copy of the execution order. The original, unsigned, with the true charge written on it before someone above him changed it to treason." She paused, and her breath caught, and she rode the pain down one more time, slower now, the tide going further out. "Get it. Add it to the ledger. Let the archive bear both our names."

Theo's jaw tightened. "You'll give it to them yourself. When we come back down."

"Reyes." She smiled at him in the firelight, the crooked smile, the last one she had in stock. "I'm a smuggler. I know exactly what I'm carrying and exactly how far it goes."

Outside the hut, far below in the black valley, watch-fires bloomed along the foothill road — one, then three, then a constellation of them, a slow bright tide climbing toward the passes. The Graycloaks had found the trail. The mountain had maybe a day left in it, perhaps less.

Sefa heard Theo count the fires under his breath, and heard him stop counting, and heard the silence he made instead, which was the silence of a man picking up a weight he'd hoped never to lift.

"Good," she said to the dark. "Now we hurry."
