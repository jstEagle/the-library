## 16. The Graycloaks Descend

The mountain had three roads into it, and Theo had spent the night learning all of them by heart.

Sefa had drawn them in the ash of the hearth with the burnt end of a stick: the Salt Stair they'd already climbed, closed behind them now with a Graycloak picket at its foot; the Shepherd's Cut, wide enough for mules, which was where the vanguard would come; and the Kettle, a choked gash of scree above a frozen lake that no army in its right mind would attempt.

"They'll take the Cut," Sefa said. "They're not in their right minds. They're in his." She tapped the ash-map where the Cut crossed beneath the Hanging Wall, a cliff face eight hundred feet of banded gray stone, loaded — as every cliff in Thornfell was loaded — with snow from the last three storms. "One push at the right place and the whole slope comes down."

"One push," Theo said, "at the right place." He turned his satchel over and let the bones spill out across the table between them: a winter's worth of carvings, each one still faintly warm, each one still whispering. "I don't have powder, Sefa. I have knickknacks."

"You have something better than powder. You have the dead who know this rock better than any engineer alive." She hooked her one eye on him. "How many avalanches has this mountain thrown down since men started digging in it? Where are those dead now?"

He hadn't thought about it that way, and he hated that he hadn't, because it meant he'd been thinking of his gift as a thing he carried rather than a country he stood in. Thornfell's high passes had been killing travelers since before the free cities were cities. Every one of those deaths had gone somewhere. He'd always assumed nowhere.

He picked up the oldest bone in his kit — a femur head he'd carved in Greyhollow from a grave-gift traded to him years ago, its original owner a miner whose name even the ghost had forgotten — and held it against the cold stone of the hut's floor, and listened.

The mountain was not quiet. He'd known that since they'd climbed into it; the ghosts here came thicker every hour, like sediment settling around them, thousands of voices packed into the stone the way silt is packed into a riverbed. But when he put his palm flat and *listened*, properly listened, the sediment shifted. Voices sorted themselves. And out of the sorting rose a chorus he recognized by its texture before he understood it by its words: the crushed, the buried, the ones who had died under snow and rock and never been dug out.

Miners. Herders. A mail-carrier whose route had crossed the Hanging Wall for thirty winters. An entire wedding party from two hundred years ago, caught in a slide on the Cut, still — in whatever way the dead persisted — walking down that slope under white cloaks.

"How many?" Sefa asked, watching his face.

"More than I can count," Theo said. "And they remember exactly how it's done."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, and it was the strangest work of Theo's strange life.

A charge-bone, as the ghosts taught him to make it, was not a weapon in itself. It was an *invitation*. He carved snow-loads into sheep femurs, carved fracture lines into deer antlers, and into each one he set a name — because the avalanche-dead would not move for geometry alone, but they would move for one of their own. The mail-carrier, whose name surfaced at last as Doss, took up residence in the first bone and taught Theo the mountain's grammar: where the Hanging Wall's base was honeycombed with melt-hollows, where the wind had under-cut the cornice above the Cut, where a single sharp report at the right seam would unzip eight hundred feet of slope like a coat.

"Here," Doss said through the bone, in a voice like gravel poured slowly. "Not there. There kills your friends too. Here only kills the road."

Theo carved until his fingers cracked. Sefa, who should have been lying down — the wound in her side had opened twice during the climb, and the pad over it was stiff and rust-dark — sat propped against the wall directing him with her chin, correcting his map in the ash, arguing with Doss through Theo's mouth like a woman haggling over freight rates. *You want the whole road or half the road? Then give me the seam, don't tell me a story about the seam.*

Ilset sat in the corner and watched.

That was the part Theo kept trying not to notice. She didn't sleep. She sat wrapped in Sefa's spare cloak with the locket of seal-bone open in her lap, and she watched him carve, and she listened — he could tell she was listening to more than he was saying, because sometimes her lips moved, shaping words in the old tongue, and when they did every loose bone in the hut hummed faintly, like wineglasses near a sung note.

Twice he caught her watching the choir-bones he'd brought from below — the fragments of binding-song he'd pried from the sunken cathedral's resonant stone, wrapped in wool, singing their looped endless song even through the wrapping. Twice he caught her hand drifting toward them. Twice she pulled it back when she saw him looking, and smiled the small adult smile that was so wrong on an eight-year-old's face, and said nothing, because she couldn't.

Near dawn he realized she wasn't pulling her hand back because she was afraid of the bones.

She was pulling it back because she didn't want to worry him yet.

* * *

The vanguard came into view an hour after sunrise, and it was worse than fires in a valley.

From the shoulder above the Shepherd's Cut they watched them come: three hundred, maybe four, no banners, no baggage, moving at a pace that made Sefa swear softly in three languages. Graycloak regulars marched like men; these moved like weather. Ranks closing over gaps without command. No scouts ranging ahead. They didn't need scouts. Somewhere in that column, patient and terribly loving, the Veil wore its king and knew exactly where it was going.

"It's not hunting us," Theo said. The realization arrived cold. "Look at the pace. It's not hurrying. It knows we can't leave the mountain."

"It's not hunting you," Sefa agreed. "It's *arriving*. There's a difference. Hunting means you might get away. Arriving means it's already decided how this ends and it's just walking here to attend." She spat over the edge, deliberately, into the wind. "Arrogant bastard. Good. Arrogant men march under cliffs."

They placed the charge-bones through the morning, and this was the work Theo would remember longest: climbing down the goat tracks to the base of the Hanging Wall with a satchel full of singing bone while four hundred Graycloaks closed at a walk, and setting each carving into a melt-hollow or a seam-crack with hands that shook only the first time. The avalanche-dead came with him. He felt them gather the way you feel a crowd gather behind you at a window — pressure, attention, company. Doss walked beside him the whole descent, invisible, talking constantly in that slow gravel voice, and it was the most either of them had been talked-at in years.

"There," Doss said. "Under the wedge. That stone fell when my grandmother's grandmother was alive and it's been waiting to fall ever since. Put the little one there. Yes. Now the antler, in the cut where the water goes in spring. You're learning, carver."

"I'm improvising," Theo said.

"Same thing, if you do it fast enough."

By midday eleven carvings lay seeded along a quarter mile of cliff base, and the vanguard had reached the frozen lake below the Cut, and Sefa called it: *Now or never.* She positioned herself at the trigger point — a ledge above the western end of the wall, out of the slide path, within earshot of every bone at once — and Theo knelt beside the first charge with both hands flat on it, and reached down through the stone into the packed sediment of the dead.

He had animated relic-bones once before, in Vessany, in terror, in public: a shield of ancestors rising out of a market stall. That had been shouting. This was something else. He opened himself the way you open a door in a storm, and the avalanche-dead came through him — hundreds of them, the crushed and the buried, the wedding party in their white cloaks, miners and herders and one very confused pilgrim — and he felt them pour into the seams of the Hanging Wall like water finding cracks, and the mountain *shivered*.

"Now," Doss said, from everywhere. "All together. For the road."

Theo struck the first charge with the flat of his carving knife.

The sound was small — a knock, a knuckle on a door — and then the mountain answered it. The wedge-stone Doss's ancestor had been waiting under for five generations let go, and the let-go traveled. Theo struck the second charge, the third, ran the line of them like a man playing a grisly instrument, and the Hanging Wall unzipped.

He had heard avalanches from far away, all his life, a soft long rumble like the mountain clearing its throat. He had never stood above one. The noise was not sound; it was a change in what sound was. Eight hundred feet of snow and banded rock leaned out over the Shepherd's Cut, hung for one impossible heartbeat that Theo would spend the rest of his life hearing in silence, and came down.

The lake broke first — the ice shattering white, the black water leaping up to meet the fall. Then the cloud swallowed the vanguard's center ranks whole. The scream of it went up and up and did not stop, and through it, faintly, Theo heard the avalanche-dead singing — a work-song, a road-mender's song, old and rough and almost glad — and he understood that to them this was not slaughter. This was the work they'd died doing, finished at last, done *right*, with warning given and their own kind steering. The Graycloaks at the column's edges scrambled clear. The rest —

The rest were under a hundred feet of new mountain.

The cloud rolled up the valley, ate the watch-fires, ate the sound of the army, and settled into a silence so total that Theo could hear his own heart misbehave. Snow-dust sifted down on the ledge like flour. Sefa gripped his shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"That," she said hoarsely, "was a full day bought. Maybe two, for anyone digging." Then she looked at his face and her expression changed. "Reyes. How many?"

"At the edges? Hundreds got clear. It let them." His voice came out strange. "It wasn't a weapon, Sefa. It was a *road closing*. They just wouldn't stop walking down it."

"Then the dead have more mercy than I do," Sefa said, "and I've lost the argument with myself about whether that's comforting." She turned away toward the summit track. "Come on. Two days. We spend them getting down to the choir."

* * *

It was halfway up the summit track, lungs burning in the thin air, that Theo finally said it out loud, because he had promised himself he would and because cowardice postponed things indefinitely.

"Ilset. When we reach the choir — the unbinding needs a living voice in the old tongue. You heard the dead explain it below. You know what that means. Singing it will empty you. Everything you are, everything the king put in you, poured out through the song. I need you to understand that I am not asking that of you. I will find another way. I will *build* another way—"

She stopped on the trail ahead of him and turned around.

She had taken the locket off. It hung in her open palm, seal-bone dull in the gray light, and her other hand rested — he saw it now, saw it with a lurch — on the choir-fragment she had taken from his pack. Not stolen. Taken the way you take a thing that belongs to you.

*I know,* her eyes said. And then, because she was Ilset, and because being understood was not enough for her, she set the choir-fragment against her throat and hummed — one note only, half a breath of the binding-song reversed, the way the dead sang it backward in the sunken dark — and the whole mountainside rang. Every bone in Theo's satchel tolled like a church at a coronation. Loose stones buzzed. Sefa swore. The note lasted three seconds and left the air feeling flayed, and Ilset lowered the fragment, swayed, steadied herself against the rock — and looked at Theo with an expression of such calm, such terrible readiness, that his protest died in his throat.

She had been practicing. In the nights he'd thought she slept.

"No," Theo said, and his voice cracked on it. "No. Listen to me. There has to be — Sefa's ledger, the testimony-bones, the councils, some other way to bind it, some—" He heard himself and stopped, because he sounded exactly like every desperate ghost he had ever consoled, bargaining with the irreversible.

Ilset climbed back down two steps to stand level with him. Small, barefoot-hearted, steady. She took his hand and pressed it flat against the choir-fragment, and through the bone, clear as speech, came the dead of the choir — thousands of them, still looping their song after centuries — and underneath the song, for the first time, something new: they were waiting. They had been waiting for her. The vessel grown from the king's memory, the spare made to be consumed, and every ghost in that sunken stone knew her name.

*We chose her,* the bone seemed to say. *She chose us. Stand aside, carver.*

"She won't be talked out of it," Sefa said quietly, from above. "I've watched her for weeks, Reyes. She decided in Vessany, when the scholars told her the price. She's not marching to her death. She's finishing her errand. There's a difference, and it's the difference, and you know it is."

Theo looked at the child who was not quite a child, holding the memory of a dead king against her throat, ready to sing herself empty to unmake the thing that had eaten him. He thought of his mother's voice, lost before he ever learned to listen. He thought of the Veil's bargain he hadn't yet refused, and knew it was coming, and knew what it would offer.

He could not stop her. He understood that now, fully, the way you understand gravity. What he could do was refuse to make her do it alone.

"Then we go together," he said. "You sing. I carve. Whatever it costs—" his voice failed, restarted, "—we pay it side by side, and neither of us gets to sneak off and sacrifice themselves politely. Agreed?"

Ilset studied him for a long moment with the king's patience in her young eyes. Then she nodded once, sealed it, and — quick as a thief, before he could react — leaned forward and hugged him, brief and fierce, her cheek against his chest, listening, he realized, to his heartbeat the way he listened to bones.

Then she turned and led them up the summit track toward the ruined choir, humming under her breath, and the mountain rang gently with every step.

Behind and far below, through the settling snow-dust of the buried pass, Theo heard something he had hoped not to hear for two days: the deep, unhurried, patient rhythm of an army that did not dig.

It simply walked around.
