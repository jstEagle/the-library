## 17. Audience with the Crown

The dead told him first.

Theo woke in the dark of the choir's outer gallery with his heart already hammering, the carved bones in his pockets ringing like a house of struck bells. The sediment of the dead pressed in around him — thousands of voices packed into the resonant stone, singing the binding-song in their endless loop — but beneath the singing, a new sound had entered the mountain. Footsteps. One set. Unhurried. Coming down.

He crawled to the gallery's edge and looked into the great throat of the cavern below, where the sunken cathedral opened like a mouth of pale stone. Torchlight from their small camp threw long shadows across the singing walls. Sefa lay where they'd left her, propped against a fallen pillar, her wound bound in Ilset's torn cloak, her one eye open and fixed on the stair. And Ilset — Ilset stood at the center of the choir floor with her head tilted, listening, the way Theo had spent his whole life listening.

"She's coming," the girl said. Not in the old tongue. In common. She had been speaking more and more of it lately, as if the king's memories were bleeding through her in words Theo could understand, and every word frightened him more than the language she'd arrived speaking. "Alone."

"That's not possible," Sefa said. "The vanguard's a day behind us. I watched them burn the foothills."

"She left them at the pass." Ilset's voice was flat, distant, a courtier's voice reporting to a throne. "She wants to see the vessel that fled. She wants to see *me*."

Theo's hands found the knife at his belt, the charge-bones in his satchel, the small carved relics he'd made from Aldric's testimony-bones — the king's memories given shape, hidden in the lining of his coat. None of it would matter. He knew that the way he knew the weight of a knucklebone in his palm. You cannot carve a defense against a thing that wears a crown and a king's body and a patience older than the kingdom it eats.

"Get her behind the pillar," Sefa said. "And you — carver. Whatever it offers you, it will offer you something you want. That's how it took a kingdom. Not with armies. With *yes*."

The footsteps came down the last stair.

* * *

It wore Aldric's body, and it wore him well.

Theo had seen the king only in dreams and in the memories Ilset leaked like light through a cracked jar, but he knew the face from the coins, from the wanted-notices, from the chapel frescoes: a broad, weathered man, a soldier-king with grey coming into his beard. The body that descended into the choir was that body, still. But the way it moved was wrong — too smooth, too economical, each step placed like a word chosen from a dictionary. The Graycloak uniform was immaculate. The crown was a thin circlet of black iron, and where it touched the king's brow, the skin had gone the color of ash.

It stopped at the edge of the torchlight and looked at Sefa first.

"Sefa Marun," it said, and the voice was Aldric's voice, warm and ruined. "You were my brother's quartermaster once. Before. I'm sorry about the eye. That was Rennick's cruelty, not mine. I never gave that order."

"Liar," Sefa said.

"No," it said, with something like gentleness. "I simply wasn't there. There's a difference, and you've spent twenty years refusing to learn it." Its gaze moved past her, up the gallery wall, and found Theo. "And you. The bone-carver of Greyhollow. The man who hears the dead. Do you know how long I've wanted to meet you?"

Theo came down into the choir because there was no sense hiding. His legs felt like borrowed things.

"Ilset," he said. "Stay by Sefa."

The girl did not move. She stood in the center of the singing floor with her chin lifted, and for a moment — one strange, luminous moment — she looked less like a child than like a queen reviewing a soldier.

"You left your army at the pass," Theo said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Why?"

"Because armies frighten children, and I have frightened this one enough." The thing in the king's body folded its hands, a courtier's patient gesture. "And because what happens next is not a matter for soldiers. It's a matter for singers and carvers. For the dead and the ones who hear them." It turned, slowly, taking in the cavern — the walls veined with resonant stone, the sediment of centuries packed into the singing dark. "Do you know what this place is, Theo Reyes?"

"A tomb."

"A *throat*," it said. "The first throat. This is where the binding was sung into my crown, in the years of the plague, when the dead were rising in the fields and the living were drowning in their own grief. Your king asked for a mercy. I was the mercy he was given." It smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, and it was terrible. "I have been keeping that mercy ever since. Village by village. Grief by grief. Do you know what I found in the foothills on my way here? A woman who had buried three sons in the war. She couldn't speak. She couldn't eat. She was dying of remembering. And my Graycloaks — " the smile widened fractionally — "my Graycloaks touched her brow, and she smiled, and she asked them to thank the king. That is what I do. That is all I do."

"You erase them," Theo said. "You eat the memory and leave the shape of the person behind."

"I *relieve* them." For the first time, something moved under the smooth surface — a flicker, a genuine thing. "You of all people should understand. You sit in a workshop full of the dead, night after night, carrying their last words like stones in your pockets. You know what remembering costs. I am the only mercy this world has ever built that *works*."

"And it's eating the king."

The flicker again — annoyance this time, or something wearing annoyance's face. "Aldric is tired. Aldric was always tired. When he is done, there will be others. There is a whole kingdom of the grieving, carver. I will never run out of hosts. I will never run out of *work*."

* * *

Then it looked at Ilset, and Theo saw the hunger come into the king's face like a tide coming into a harbor.

"But you," it said softly. "The spare vessel. The memory I set aside in case I outgrew my first home. Do you know how it grieved me when you fled? I felt you go the way a man feels a tooth leave his mouth. And now here you are, walking back to me, into the one place in the world where I am strongest." It extended a hand toward her, palm up, gentle as a father's. "Come home, little vessel. The king is so tired. You were made for this."

Ilset did not move. Her small hands were fists at her sides. And Theo heard her say, in the old tongue, one word — and the whole cavern resonated with it, the singing walls catching it and throwing it back, and Theo didn't know the word but he knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a knife in the dark.

*No.*

The Veil's hand lowered. When it looked up again, it looked at Theo, and its expression had changed — the courtier's patience giving way to something more intimate, more dangerous. Interest.

"You love her," it said. "Not as a father. As a *carver*. You look at her and you see something unfinished that you could keep whole." It stepped closer, and the torchlight made the ash-grey skin of its brow glow like a scar. "Let me offer you a trade, then. A carver's trade. Bone for bone."

"I don't want anything from you."

"Everyone wants something from me. That's why I'm still wearing a kingdom." It raised one hand, and the cavern *answered* — the dead in the walls shifting, the binding-song bending around its gesture like water around a stone. "I have been listening to the dead of this mountain since I entered it. Do you know what they say about you? The workshop-ghosts you've carried. The woman whose knucklebone you carved. The smuggler who hanged. And one voice, deeper than the rest, one voice you have never once let yourself listen to — "

"Don't," Theo said.

" — a woman's voice. A young voice. It stopped when you were nine years old. It has been waiting in you ever since, the way a song waits in a sealed room." The Veil's voice dropped, went soft, went *kind*, and the kindness was the cruelest thing Theo had ever heard. "I can give her back to you. Not an echo. Not a carving. Her. Her voice, her laugh, the thing she was saying when the fever took her mid-word — I hold every grief this kingdom ever surrendered, and hers is in me, whole. I can put it back in your ear, carver. You could hear your mother finish her sentence. You could hear her for the rest of your life."

The choir went very quiet around him. Even the dead in the walls seemed to lean in.

Theo stood in the torchlight with his hands hanging at his sides, and he let himself feel it — all of it, the whole weight of thirty years, the sealed room, the unfinished word. His mother's voice had been the first voice he'd ever heard and the first the gift had taken from him, because the dead he loved best were the dead he couldn't bear to listen to, and so he had walled her off, and the wall had never come down, and every carving he'd ever made had been made in the silence she left behind.

He could have her back. One word. One small, terrible *yes*.

And Ilset would walk up the stair in the king's body, barefoot and unharmed by cold, a vessel going home to be emptied.

"No," Theo said.

The word came out of him the way the girl's word had come out of her — with the whole cavern behind it, with thirty years of sealed rooms breaking open at once. He heard his own voice crack in the middle of it, heard everything he was refusing crack with it, and he kept standing there, and he kept saying it.

"No. You don't get her. You don't get *her voice* — " he pointed at Ilset, his hand shaking — "and you don't get *mine*. You've been feeding on this kingdom's grief for twenty years because grief is what people will pay. But I have carried the dead my whole life and I will tell you what they never once said to me, not one of them, not ever: they never said *forget*. They said *remember*. They said *the crown lies*. They said *I was wrong*. You are not a mercy. You are a debt collector wearing a dead man's face, and I am done paying."

The silence that followed was absolute.

And then the Veil — the thing that had worn a king for twenty years, the thing that had ordered villages erased with a terrible patient love — the Veil *flinched*.

It was small. It was over in a heartbeat. But Theo saw it, because he had spent his life reading what the dead could not hide: the moment the mask slips and the thing underneath shows through. The king's face convulsed. The ash-grey brow darkened. And from somewhere deep inside the body — from beneath the smooth courtier surface, from the sealed room where a tired soldier had been drowning for twenty years — a voice came out that was not the Veil's voice at all.

"*What did you say.*"

It was Aldric. The true king. Ragged, drowned, half-eaten — but *there*, and awake, and looking out of his own eyes for the first time in years as if through a window flung open by a gale.

"What did you — " the king's voice broke, and the body staggered, one hand flying to the black circlet as if to tear it off — "*what did you say to it* — carver — *what is your name* — "

"Aldric," the Veil said, and the two voices ground against each other in one throat, the smooth and the ruined. "Aldric, sleep. Aldric, you are tired, I am keeping you safe — "

"You *lied* to me," the king said, and now he was weeping, great ugly sobs of a man surfacing from deep water, and the crown smoked where his fingers clawed at it. "Twenty years — you said there was no other way — you said the kingdom would *die* — "

"The kingdom *will* die — "

"Then let it die *awake* — "

The body dropped to its knees on the choir floor. The torchlight guttered. In the walls, the binding-song faltered for the first time in centuries — the dead stumbling in their loop, thousands of voices losing their place at once, and Theo felt the whole mountain hold its breath.

Sefa was on her feet against the pillar, her one eye wide. Ilset had not moved from the center of the floor, but her fists had opened, and she was looking at the kneeling king with an expression Theo had never seen on her face — not fear. *Recognition.* The vessel looking at the host. The memory looking at the man it had been cut from.

The Veil got one hand under itself and lifted its head, and for a moment the two faces were both there at once — the courtier's patience and the soldier's grief, layered like a carving gone wrong, two figures in one bone.

"You should have taken the bargain, carver," it said quietly. Both voices. Perfectly together.

And then it stood, and straightened the Graycloak uniform with two precise tugs, and the king's face closed like a door.

"The army comes at dawn," the Veil said. "I will not offer again."

It walked back up the stair, unhurried, one set of footsteps in the dark.

Theo stood in the failing torchlight with his heart slamming and his hands shaking, and Ilset crossed the floor to him and took one of them in both of hers. Her palms were warm. Her eyes were wet.

"He's still in there," she said in common. "Theo. He's still *in there*. I heard him."

"I know," Theo said.

Behind them, in the walls of the sunken cathedral, the dead began to sing again — but differently now. Faster. Louder. The binding-song turning over in its sleep like a thing remembering what it was for, and beneath it, faint and ragged and unmistakable, a new voice had joined the choir: a drowned king, singing from underneath, keeping pace.

Dawn was coming. And for the first time since the plague years, the binding had a crack in it — and something on both sides of the veil, dead and living, pressing at the break.
