## 18. The Unbinding

**Theo**

The crack in the binding was the size of a coin. It sounded like a cathedral.

Theo felt it through his palms before he heard it — he was kneeling on the choir floor with his hands flat against resonant stone, and the whole cavern had become an instrument someone was tuning too fast. The dead in the walls sang louder. Under them, ragged and human, Aldric kept pace, a drowned man swimming toward a light that might be a lamp or might be a hook.

Ilset stood at the center of the choir, barefoot on the stone — she never seemed to feel cold, but this morning she'd put her boots back on, and Theo understood why. What came next, she wanted to do as herself. As a girl. Not as a vessel.

"Sefa's still bleeding," he said. It wasn't a reason to stop. He said it anyway.

"She's sleeping," Ilset said. "Marta's watching her." Marta was one of the avalanche-dead who had followed them down from the pass, a gray woman in a gray coat who stood where Sefa slept and did not leave. The dead had been doing that lately — gathering. Coming down out of the stone like sediment settling into shape.

"And if it empties you?"

"Then it empties me," Ilset said. She said it the way children say things that are true and unbearable, plainly, without drama, because they haven't learned yet what adults use instead of plainness. "You can't stop me. You said so."

"I said I couldn't stop you. I didn't say I liked it."

She smiled at him then — quick, crooked, eight years old for one whole second — and it broke his heart more cleanly than anything the Veil could have done. "Stand beside me," she said. "That's all you have to do."

He took out his knives. Nine of them, laid on a cloth: bone-handled, bone-bladed where he could manage it, each one carved over the years with names of the dead he'd carried. Beside them he set the locket of royal seal-bone, open, and inside it the curl of pale material that had been King Aldric's severed memory — grown strange over twenty years, threaded through with silver like frost in wood.

The memories would need somewhere to live. That was the plan, such as it was, assembled from a scholar's letter, a dying smuggler's certainty, and a dead king's song: Ilset would sing the binding backward, and when the Veil came loose from crown and host, Theo would carve fast enough to give the king something true to die into. A body of witness. Bone that remembered him as he was, not as the parasite had worn him.

"You'll have minutes," Ilset said. "Maybe less."

"I've carved keepsakes at funerals while the ghost was still talking," Theo said. "I know how to work quickly."

"That's the most depressing thing anyone has ever said to me."

"Welcome to Greyhollow humor."

But she didn't laugh, and neither did he, and above them — far above, past the collapsed pass, past the burning foothills — something patient and loving was coming down the mountain alone.

* * *

**Ilset**

The old tongue did not live in her mouth. It lived deeper than that, in the place where she had been made, which was not a place at all but a decision — a queen weeping over a plague of forgetting, a king saying *take my memory, take my grief, let me be the one who pays*, and alchemists of the crown turning that sacrifice into a vessel. A spare cup. Her.

Ilset had fled the palace carrying a language older than any kingdom, and now she opened her mouth and found it waiting, patient as water.

She began with the first line of the binding-song, sung backward.

The effect was immediate and physical. The walls of the sunken cathedral flexed. Dust fell in long threads from vaults that had not shed dust since before the free cities existed. And the choir — thousands of dead packed into the stone like sediment, singing their loop for twenty years — faltered, staggered, and then turned.

They sang the second line with her.

That was how the reversed binding worked; she understood it now, understood everything now, the knowledge arriving like tide rather than thought. The binding had been sung by many voices into one crown. To unmake it, one voice had to lead and the dead had to follow — willingly, freely, each one choosing to release the hold they had helped establish. Consent, given backward through time. The dead were choosing. They were singing the king loose.

And beneath them, fainter now, the drowned voice stopped keeping pace.

It rose.

* * *

**Theo**

The first memory came up through his hands like a fish taking a hook.

He was carving — seal-bone, the locket's twin blank he'd brought from Greyhollow without knowing why — and the bone went hot under his knife, and then he wasn't in the choir anymore. He was in a garden above a plague city, twenty-two years ago, holding a young queen's hand while she cried.

*"I can't watch another village go quiet," she said. "Not the way it goes quiet. Like the people were never there."*

*"Then don't watch," said a man's voice — younger than any king's voice Theo knew, warm and tired and terrified. "Let me carry it. If grief is the door this thing comes through, let me be the door. Let me stand in it."*

*Aldric. Young Aldric, before the crown. Before.*

The knife moved. Theo's hands knew what they were doing even as his mind drowned in a stranger's life: the coronation, the crown settling on his brow like cold water, and the moment the Veil entered — not a scream, not a battle, just a bargain being honored. Something vast and gentle sliding into the space where his memory of his mother's face used to be, filling it like water fills a print in sand.

*I asked for this,* Aldric had told him in the chapel. *I was wrong.*

Now Theo carved the wrongness. He carved the garden. He carved the queen's hand going slack, the first village the Veil erased in its master's name, the exact moment Aldric understood what he had invited in and found he no longer had the memory of objecting — the Veil had taken that too, tidily, the way it took all grief, including its own.

The bone filled. He reached for the second blank.

* * *

**Ilset**

Halfway through the third verse, she began to empty.

She had expected darkness, dissolution, some dramatic unraveling. Instead it was subtraction. Colors went first — the choir's green glow draining to gray, then to a gray so pure it wasn't a color at all. Then warmth left her feet. Then the sense of her own edges.

She kept singing. That was the whole discipline of it, the entire heroism available to an eight-year-old girl standing in a hole in the world: keep singing. The king's memories were leaving her the way water leaves a lifted cup, pouring through her mouth into the stone, into the choir, into the hands of the carver who knelt nearby shaping them into something that would outlast her.

She was glad. That was the strangest part. She had been made to be consumed — a spare vessel if the Veil outgrew the king — and every story she had ever been told ended with the cup being filled again. No one had ever planned for the cup to pour itself out on purpose, into a better shape.

Somewhere in the subtracting gray, Theo looked up. His mouth was moving. Sound reached her late, warped, like speech underwater:

"—stay with me—"

She couldn't answer. Singing was all she was now. But she found, in the last of herself, one word of the common tongue — the language she'd learned on smuggler roads and mountain paths, the language of the living — and she folded it into the end of the verse like a secret stitched into a hem.

The word was his name.

* * *

**Theo**

Four blanks became five became six, and the bone was drinking the king faster than Theo could shape him, and still it wasn't enough — you cannot carve a whole man, only the parts that matter, and Theo was learning which parts mattered at a speed that felt like blasphemy. The garden. The objection the Veil stole. The night Aldric tried, exactly once, to tear the crown from his own head, and his arms wouldn't obey him. The twenty years of watching his kingdom's grief get eaten and calling it mercy because the alternative was admitting he'd damned them all.

The seventh piece of bone Theo carved differently. Not a scene. A sentence.

*It was murder,* the bone said, in the shape of Aldric's own remembered voice. *What was done to my kingdom was done to me first, and I consented to neither. Let the record show I objected. Let the record show my objection was stolen. Let the record show I am not my parasite.*

Sefa's ledger sat in his satchel against the choir wall, wrapped in oilcloth. This was hers too, that sentence. Justice outside the law meant refusing lawful arithmetic — refusing to spend children to save kingdoms — and it also meant naming things plainly, in evidence, in bone that could be held.

The choir floor shuddered.

Above them, far above, the mountain rang once, like a struck bell — the sound of something vast setting foot on stone it recognized.

"He's here," Theo said. His voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Ilset. He's here."

She sang louder.

* * *

**Ilset**

The Veil came down into the choir the way dawn comes — gradually, then all at once, and impossible to argue with.

She felt it arrive without seeing it: a presence at the edge of the gray, enormous and gentle, radiating something that was genuinely, terribly love. It had crossed the mountain alone. It had left its army at the pass because this was not army work. This was family business. This was the parasite returning for the cup that had been made to hold its overflow, and it was sad about the necessity, and its sadness was real, and that was the worst thing Ilset had ever known.

*Little vessel,* it said, in the old tongue, in a voice like snowfall. *You were made for this. Why do you run?*

Because, she thought, in the last colors of herself, *made-for is not the same as want-to.*

And the dead agreed.

They came off the walls — not bodies, nothing so crude, but presences, thousands upon thousands, the sediment of twenty years rising out of the stone all at once, and they did not attack the Veil. Attacking it would have fed it; it drank conflict the way it drank grief. Instead they did the one thing nothing had ever done to it in its long patient existence.

They stopped singing.

Silence hit the cathedral like a landslide. The binding-song — the loop, the engine, the prison and the anchor both — simply ceased. Twenty years of continuous sound, ended in one chosen instant, by consent, by every dead voice in the mountain agreeing at last to let go.

The Veil, unbound from crown, unbound from host, unbound from song, had nowhere to stand.

It unraveled.

Not screaming — it was never cruel, and even its ending was quiet — but folding, layer over layer, like cloth falling away from a body that was never there. And the choir of the dead, instead of tearing it apart, opened around it. Held it. Sang one final note — not the binding-song, something older, something like a lullaby — and let it rest among them, the way you lay a tired child down.

The gray drained out of the world.

Color crashed back into Ilset like a wave, and warmth, and edges, and weight — weight most of all, wonderful, ordinary weight — and she was on her knees on the stone, empty as a rung bell, alive, and the first thing she saw was Theo catching the crown as it rolled off the center of the choir floor, catching it and flinging it away from himself like it was fire, because it was.

In the middle of the circle of bone pieces lay an old man.

* * *

**Theo**

Aldric died the way the scholar had promised and Theo hadn't dared believe: humanly.

He was small. That was Theo's first thought, kneeling beside him — kings are supposed to be large, and this man had been worn down to almost nothing by two decades of tenant, a house hollowed by its guest. His eyes were brown and unfocused and then, suddenly, focused, and they found Theo's face with the desperate clarity of a drowning man finding a rope.

"The bones," Aldric rasped. "Did you — I felt myself being written. Is it true? Am I —"

"In there," Theo said. He gathered the seven pieces and pressed them into the king's hands, closing the fingers over them. "All of it. The garden. Your objection. The theft. It's yours. Nobody can wear it, nobody can edit it. It's evidence, and it's you."

Aldric looked down at his own hands holding himself, and something in his face came apart — not grief, exactly, though grief was in it, but relief so large it had nowhere to go except outward, as tears, as a shaking Theo could feel through the stone.

"My wife," he whispered. "There was a queen. I couldn't — for twenty years I couldn't remember there was a queen."

"There was," Ilset said.

Her voice came out rusted, half-ruined, barely a whisper — but it was common tongue, every word, and Theo's chest hurt hearing it. She crawled to the king's other side and took his hand, the girl who had been made from his severed memory holding the man she'd been cut from.

"She sang," Aldric said. "At the binding. I remember — I'm allowed to remember — she sang the first line, and I thought, whatever comes of this, she'll have her villages back—"

"It worked," Ilset lied gently. "For a little while, it worked. Rest now. We have your witness. We'll finish it."

"I asked for this," Aldric said, and the words were different now, lighter, a confession instead of a curse. "I was wrong. Tell them I said so. Tell them I was wrong, and tell them—" his grip tightened on the bones, on the girl's hand, on the last minute of being a man instead of a throne— "tell them the crown lies."

The choir, silent for the first time in twenty years, stayed silent.

Aldric died between them in the gray morning light of the sunken cathedral, and the dead did not sing him out, and nothing wore him, and it was — Theo would spend the rest of his life failing to find a better word for it — it was peaceful. Just that. Peaceful. A man dying among strangers who had become witnesses, holding his own name in his hands.

Ilset laid her forehead against the dead king's shoulder and wept, quietly, in a language that was finally, fully, entirely hers.

Theo sat back on his heels. His hands ached. His knives lay in a row like spent soldiers. Above them the mountain held its breath, the Graycloak vanguard hours away at best, Sefa bleeding two chambers back with a dead woman standing guard over her, and a kingdom's worth of justice waiting upstairs in an oilcloth bundle.

"We have to move him," Theo said softly. "And her. And us."

Ilset lifted her head. Her eyes were swollen and red and clear as well-water.

"Not yet," she said. "Just — stay. One minute. Stay."

So he stayed. Outside, dawn came over Thornfell, and for the first time in twenty years, nothing in the mountain was singing — and everything in it, dead and living alike, was listening.
