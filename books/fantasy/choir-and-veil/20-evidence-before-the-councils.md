## 20. Evidence Before the Councils

The council chamber of Vessany had been built to hold three hundred voices and had never once held a truth.

Theo stood at the floor's center, on a mosaic of the nine free cities arranged in a wheel, and felt the weight of the room the way he felt the weight of bone before the first cut — all of it waiting, all of it dense with what it might become. The galleries were full. Merchants in dyed wool, harbor captains, a delegation of Graycloak deserters kept apart behind a rope like something contagious. Above them all, the nine council seats rose in a crescent, and nine faces looked down at him with the particular patience of people who had already decided.

He had seen that patience before. He had worn it himself, for years, in a workshop in Greyhollow, carving keepsakes for the grieving and telling himself that neutrality was a virtue. He knew its texture. It was not patience at all. It was fear wearing a respectable coat.

At his feet, on a table of black oak, sat two objects.

The first was Sefa Marun's ledger. Twenty years of entries in her cramped, left-slanting hand: dates, routes, names, payments. Payments taken and payments made. Bribes to port inspectors. Sums funneled to the crown's secret police. And in the back pages, in a different ink, a different kind of record — the executions. Forty-one names. Men and women who had carried for the court and been silenced for knowing it. The last name in that column was Torv Marun, her brother, hanged at the Salt Stair with a charge of treason read over him by an officer whose salary, the ledger showed, had been paid from the same purse that had hired him.

The second object was smaller. A cedar box, and inside it, a spine of carved bone no longer than Theo's forearm. Aldric's testimony. He had cut it himself in the weeks after the unbinding, working in the quiet of a borrowed room while Ilset slept, shaping the dead king's final memories into something that could speak to people who had no gift to hear them. It was the hardest work of his life, and it was the last work his gift would ever do. The bones held the king's voice now the way a bell holds its strike. Anyone could hear it. That had been the point.

"You understand," said Councilor Veyra of Vessany, her voice carrying the flat courtesy of a woman managing an inconvenience, "that the accords are signed. The regency has been recognized. The army stands down on terms. What you bring us asks us to unmake a peace that has already bought us a season without funerals."

"I bring you a peace that was bought with lies," Theo said. "I'm asking you to price it honestly."

Murmurs. Somewhere in the galleries a woman began to weep quietly, and the sound traveled the room like a current. Theo did not look for her. He had learned, in the last months, that grief in a crowd was not one thing but many, and that each of them needed its own moment.

"Councilor." He turned to face the crescent of seats. "I was a bone-carver in Greyhollow. I wanted nothing from any of this. I will tell you plainly: if you send me home today, I will not fight you. I have no army. I have a child, a dead woman's ledger, and a dead king's confession. That is the whole of my force. So hear them, and then do what you like."

He opened the cedar box.

He did not need to touch the bone. He had built the speaking into it — a resonance, a held note in the grain that answered the room's own silence. When he lifted the spine free, the chamber's acoustics took it, and the dead king's voice filled the vaulted dark as if the stone itself remembered how to sing.

*My name is Aldric, fourth of that name, and I was king of the Thornlands. What follows is a confession, and I ask that it be entered as evidence, not as plea.*

The room went still in the way rooms do when a dead man speaks with more composure than the living.

*I made a bargain to save my people from the gray plague. I know now what the bargain cost, because I was made to pay it slowly, from the inside. The thing that wore my crown — I will not excuse it, and I will not let you excuse it either by calling it a monster and nothing more. It believed it was merciful. It erased grief village by village because grief was the door the plague came through. It burned the foothills of Thornfell with the same patience a surgeon brings to a limb. And I consented to the first cut. Hear that. The first cut was mine.*

Theo watched the councilors as the voice moved through them. Veyra's face did not change, but her hands, folded on the arm of her chair, had gone white at the knuckles. Old Corvath of Drenmark, who had argued loudest for the accords, leaned forward despite himself. And at the crescent's far end, a young councilor from the river towns — Theo had learned her name was Ashka, and that she had lost a brother at the Salt Stair — sat with her eyes closed, listening the way you listen to music, letting it pass through.

*The crown's secret police did not serve me. They served the thing that wore me. The executions recorded in the ledger before you were murders dressed in law. I name them so. I name the officers. I name the purses that paid them. I was king, and I was the first victim, and I am dead, and none of these three facts excuses the others.*

The voice ended. Theo set the bone down gently, the way you set down something sleeping.

Silence held the chamber for the length of ten heartbeats. Then Corvath of Drenmark said, heavily, "A confession from a dead man. Charming. And the ledger — a smuggler's book. Forged, altered, who can say? We are asked to tear up signed accords on the word of bones and a carver's paper."

"The ledger is in Sefa Marun's hand," Theo said. "Her hand can be verified by anyone she ever bought a drink for, which is half the harbormasters on this coast. And the king's bone can be tested as bone. Cut it. Date it. You will find the grain grew in the Thornlands royal preserve, and you will find in it no lie, because there is nothing left in it that can lie. That is more than you can say of any living witness in this room, including me."

"And what is it you want, carver?" Veyra again, sharp now. "Say it plainly. No one tears up a peace for sentiment."

"Three things." Theo held up his fingers, one by one. He had rehearsed this in the borrowed room, and Ilset had listened, and when he finished she had said, *Say it slower. Grown people hear slow.* "First: the forty-one names in that ledger are read into the record as murders, not treasons. Their families are told the truth. That costs you nothing but shame, and shame is cheap at the price you paid for this peace.

"Second: the officers named — the ones still living — stand trial. Not before the regency's courts. Before a joint tribunal of the free cities. If you will not do it for the dead, do it because the regency signed your accords with those men still on its payroll, and you should want to know what else it signed with.

"Third: the smugglers. All of them. Every name in every column of that book, the living and the dead. Pardoned. Posthumously where it must be. They carried for the crown when the crown asked and were hanged when the crown needed quiet, and the free cities called them criminals because it was convenient to have a class of people whose deaths meant nothing. Their deaths meant something. I am here because one of them spent her last blood making sure they would."

He stopped. The room was very quiet. Somewhere above, a pigeon shifted in the rafters, and the sound was enormous.

Corvath laughed — a short, ugly sound. "Pardons for smugglers. Tribunals for officers. You understand that half the council took those officers' money over the years? You are asking men to try their own bribers."

"I know," Theo said. "I've met the men."

That drew a sound from the galleries — not quite laughter, not quite a roar. Veyra raised a hand for order and did not get it immediately, and Theo understood, watching the crescent fracture into murmuring clusters, that the vote was already happening, out loud, in front of everyone, the way real decisions always happen before they are made official.

* * *

They recessed for six hours. Theo spent them in the colonnade outside, on a bench worn smooth by a century of waiting petitioners, with Ilset beside him working a piece of scrap bone with a file too big for her hand.

She was eleven now, or the world had decided she was eleven, which amounted to the same thing. She had grown a hand's width since Thornfell. Her hair was cut short and practical and she wore a carver's apron over a harbor-girl's coat, and when the council pages went by she did not look up from her work. She had learned, in the months since the choir, to be unremarkable in public, and Theo hated that she had learned it and said nothing, because he had spent forty years perfecting the same skill.

"You're doing the wing wrong," he said.

"I'm doing the wing my way."

"It'll stall in the air."

"It's not going in the air. It's going on a shelf." She blew bone dust off the little form. "Did they believe you?"

"Some did. Some didn't."

"Which are you?"

He looked at her. She had a way of asking questions that went past the polite layer of a thing straight to the wet bone of it. "I think I believed me," he said. "That's what I've got."

"That's enough," Ilset said, with the absolute authority of a child, and went back to her wing.

Sefa would have agreed with her. Theo still caught himself turning to say something to Sefa — a joke about councilors, a memory of the pass — and the turn was always into empty air. She had died at Thornfell with her ledger finished and her brother's name spoken aloud by a king, and she had died, Theo was fairly sure, content, which was more than most of the living managed. But contentment in the dead did not fill the space they left. He had spent his whole life hearing what the dead left behind. Now he carried it in the ordinary human way, in the chest, in the throat, at odd hours, and there was no gift in it at all.

A page came for him at dusk. The council would vote within the hour.

* * *

The chamber at night was lit with oil lamps, and the nine faces floated above the light like masks.

Veyra read the terms. The murders would be named. The tribunal would sit — Drenmark abstaining, Vessany and the river towns and the coast cities carrying it. The pardons passed last, and passed narrowest, and Corvath of Drenmark rose during the reading to say, formally, for the record, that he believed the whole proceeding was a farce performed on the corpse of a convenient peace, and that history would judge them.

"History," Theo said from the floor, before he could stop himself, "has already testified. You were just too well paid to listen."

Corvath went the color of ham. The galleries did laugh, then, fully, and Theo understood that he had spent something he would not get back — his careful neutrality, the last of it, spent in a single sentence in front of nine councils and three hundred witnesses. It was, he decided, a fair price.

The war dissolved the way ice dissolves — not in a battle but in a thaw. The regency, faced with a tribunal that would reach into its payroll and a free-cities alliance that no longer needed to fear its army because that army's officers were busy hiring advocates, simply came apart. Garrisons went unpaid and went home. The Graycloak order was formally disbanded in the spring; some of its members stood trial, and some of them, Theo noted without much satisfaction, sat on tribunals judging their own juniors, which was the way of institutions everywhere. The foothill villages that had burned began to rebuild, and the free cities sent grain instead of soldiers, because it was finally cheaper.

It was not justice. Theo knew the shape of real justice, had held it in his hands in a choir beneath a mountain, and it had been small and human and had cost everything. What the councils produced was a document. Forty-one families received letters. Some of them wrote back. One woman came to Vessany to find him — Torv Marun had been her cousin's husband — and she did not thank him. She stood in the colonnade and looked at him for a long time and said, "Sefa always said someone would read it out loud someday," and then she left, and Theo sat on the bench for an hour afterward, holding that sentence the way you hold a coal.

Some accepted. Some didn't. The dead did not rise from their pardons. The officers who hanged themselves before trial were mourned by no one whose mourning mattered. And the accords stood, amended but standing, because the free cities were free cities, and would trade with the devil's own regency if the tolls were right.

But the ledger bore Sefa Marun's name on its archive plate now, in the public records of Vessany, where any clerk or child could ask for it and read the truth of forty-one deaths. And in the margin of the first page, in the archivist's careful hand, were the words: *Entered as evidence by Theo Reyes, bone-carver, on behalf of the dead.*

On behalf of the dead.

He read those words more times than he would admit. Once, he would have heard them spoken by the dead themselves — would have felt Sefa's ghost lean against his shoulder and mutter something dry about the spelling of her name. Now there was only the archive's dust and the lamplight and his own voice, reading aloud, because Ilset had asked him to, because she said the dead should be read out loud at least once a year, by someone living, so the words stayed warm.

He did it every year. It was, he supposed, his gift now. Not hearing. Remembering.

* * *

They left Vessany in early summer, on a river barge with a hold full of dye-wood, and Ilset stood at the rail the whole first day watching the city shrink.

"They didn't all agree," she said, finally.

"No."

"Corvath didn't. He said history would judge."

"He did."

"Will it?"

Theo thought about it. The river slid by, brown and patient, carrying them south toward Greyhollow, toward a workshop with a cold hearth and a wall of finished keepsakes waiting for hands that no longer heard what they carved.

"History's not a judge," he said. "It's a ledger. It just writes down what we did and waits for someone to read it out loud." He nodded at the city, already small behind them. "Sefa wrote her part. The king wrote his. The councils wrote theirs — some of it true, some of it bought. Now it's our page."

Ilset considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, and then she held up the little bone bird she'd been carving since the colonnade, wings wrong, perfect anyway.

"Then let's write it slow," she said. "And read it out loud."

Theo laughed — his first real laugh since the mountain — and the barge carried them on down the brown water toward home, toward the workshop, toward whatever quiet work a man's hands could do when his ears held nothing but the living world, and the living world, that evening, sounded like a child humming an old song she no longer knew the words to, getting them wrong, and not caring at all.

But that is another chapter, and the river was long, and the summer was just beginning.
