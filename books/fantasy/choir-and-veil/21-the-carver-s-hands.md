## 21. The Carver's Hands

The road into Greyhollow was longer than Theo remembered, or perhaps he had simply forgotten how long roads were when you walked them without a caravan, without a smuggler's shortcuts, without the dead murmuring directions in your ear. Just him and Ilset and two mules and a cart of tools wrapped in oilcloth, climbing the last switchbacks as autumn came down off the peaks like a slow gray tide.

He had left Greyhollow a coward with debts. He came back a man whose name appeared in council dispatches from Vessany to Salt Stair, which was not the same thing as coming back a hero, though some people seemed determined to treat it that way. He hated it. He had spent his whole life being nobody on purpose, and now innkeepers refused his coin and children pointed at him in the street and an old woman pressed her hand to her heart when he passed, as if he'd done something other than stand next to a girl while she sang.

"You're scowling," Ilset said.

"I'm thinking."

"Your face does the same thing for both."

She was eleven now, or nearly — they had settled on a birthday somewhere in the spring, mostly so she could have one. She rode the lead mule with her hood down because she liked the cold, because cold was just weather to her, ordinary weather, and after everything she had been she took a fierce proprietary joy in ordinary things: chapped lips, sore feet, the taste of river water. Her hair had grown out past her shoulders. She still spoke softly, but she spoke constantly, a running commentary on the world, as if she were making up for eight years of silence and intended to finish the debt by winter.

"The pass looks different," she said.

"Fires did that."

"I know what fires do." A pause. "I meant it looks *better*. Like a scar instead of a wound."

Theo looked up at the ridgeline where the vanguard's burning had blackened the pines two summers ago. She was right, damn her. Green was coming back through the ash in stubborn threads. He grunted, which she correctly interpreted as agreement, and they crested the last rise, and there was Greyhollow below them in its bowl of stone and smoke, smaller than he remembered, more beautiful than he deserved.

* * *

The workshop smelled wrong when he opened the door.

Not bad — wrong. Three years of dust and cold ash and the particular staleness of a room where nothing has happened. His bench sat under its sheet like a body under a shroud. The racks of finished work along the wall — keepsakes, buttons, the little carved animals he'd sold to travelers — were exactly where he'd left them, and looking at them was like reading letters from a stranger.

Ilset stood in the doorway behind him and didn't come in.

"It's quiet," she said.

"That's the point."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "It's quiet like the choir was quiet. After."

He understood then what she meant, and it landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. For him, silence was new — three years new, still raw at the edges, a missing tooth his tongue kept finding. But she had been born into a different kind of noise entirely. She had carried a king's memories in her skull since before she could walk. Silence, for Ilset, was something that had to be survived too.

So he stepped aside and said, "Pick a corner. Any corner. It's yours."

She picked the corner by the window, naturally, the one with the best light, and within a week she had dragged a stool there and a scrap of blanket and a chipped cup she used for holding bone scraps, and the workshop began, slowly, to smell like work again.

* * *

The first thing he carved was nothing special. That was the point of it.

A button. Plain horn-colored bone, four holes, sanded smooth. He sat at his bench on the first morning and held the blank in his hands and waited — out of habit, out of thirty years of habit — for the voice that lived in every piece he touched. The dead woman's knucklebone. The hanged smuggler's finger-bone. Every relic had come to him talking, and he had talked back, and between them they had made a living and lost a life.

The button said nothing. Bone is only bone. Calcium and memory-shaped nothing, a material like any other, and his hands remembered what to do with it even though his gift was gone: the turn of the knife, the pressure, the patience. His hands had always known how to carve. The hearing had just been riding along on top, loud as a second heartbeat, and he had mistaken it for the whole craft.

He carved six buttons that day. Then a comb. Then a set of dice for the innkeeper's boy, who paid for them in bread and gossip. Plain work. Honest work. Work that would never whisper to anyone, least of all him.

Some nights the silence got so large he couldn't sleep in the loft above the shop and had to come down and sit at the bench with a lamp lit, doing nothing, just sitting where the voices used to be. He grieved them. There was no honest way around it. People assumed he'd been freed, and he let them assume it, because explaining was worse. But he had loved the dead. Not their wisdom, not their warnings — them. The dead woman who'd sung lullabies in a dialect no living person spoke. The avalanche men who'd argued about card games for a decade. Even the frightened ones, even the angry ones. Thirty years of company, and then the choir went quiet and released them all, and he was alone in his own head for the first time since he was nine years old.

His mother's voice had been in there once. He'd never found it. He'd told himself, in the choir beneath Thornfell, that refusing the Veil's bargain meant he'd made peace with never hearing her — and he had, he had, he'd do it again a thousand times. But peace and absence are different rooms in the same house, and some nights he woke in the small hours reaching for a voice that wasn't there, and lay in the dark learning, again, how wide a man could be hollowed.

Those nights, eventually, stopped being nights he endured alone. Because around the second month, he started hearing footsteps on the stairs, and Ilset would appear with two cups of barley tea and sit across the bench from him without a word, and they would drink together in the silence, and it was — he could admit this now — a better kind of company than any ghost had ever been. The living, showing up. Choosing to.

* * *

He started teaching her in the third week, formally, with a contract written out on good paper because she insisted contracts made things real. *Apprentice: Ilset. Term: seven years. Master: Theo Reyes, carver.* She signed it with a careful hand and pinned it above the window corner, and looked at it every morning like a talisman.

The problem — the interesting problem, the one that got him out of bed — was that everything he knew about teaching carving was tangled up with hearing. He'd chosen bones by listening for the ones that wanted to be worked. He'd read grain and density through resonance, through the way a piece hummed against his palm. None of that transferred. Ilset couldn't hear what he'd heard. Nobody ever would again.

So they built a new method from scratch, the two of them, and it turned out to be a good method, maybe better than his had been. She learned to read bone with her eyes and thumbs: the striations, the weight, the faint oiliness of fresh-cut versus the chalkiness of old. She learned which bones forgave mistakes and which ones didn't. She learned that a knife is a conversation you have with yourself as much as with the material.

And here was the thing he hadn't anticipated, the thing that undid him a little every time: she carved without grief. Every piece he had ever made had been shadowed by its source — a keepsake was a death worn close, a memento mori polished pretty. Ilset carved birds and fish and little round-bellied horses, and when she sold them at market, people bought them because they were *lovely*, full stop. Children bought them with saved coppers. No one wept over them. They were objects that existed purely to be enjoyed, and Theo, watching a farmer's daughter laugh over a carved wren, felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since before the war.

"You're staring," she said, not looking up from her knife.

"Your sparrow's wing is crooked."

"It's not crooked, it's mid-flap." She turned it toward him. "Birds don't hold still, master Reyes."

He took the knife from her, adjusted the wing a hair, handed it back. "There. Now it's mid-flap *correctly*."

She stuck her tongue out at him, eleven going on twelve going on insufferable, and went back to work, and the lamp burned low, and outside the snow came down soft on Greyhollow, and Theo thought: *this*. This was what he'd been avoiding the war to protect, without knowing it. Not his own life. This small lamplit room. A child arguing about wings.

* * *

In the spring, word came that Sefa Marun's ledger had gone public.

It arrived the way most things arrived in Greyhollow — late, secondhand, attached to a merchant who wanted a commission carved. But this time the merchant barely mentioned the commission. He stood in the workshop turning his hat in his hands and said, "You've heard, then? About the archive?"

"Tell me."

"They've opened it in Vessany. The whole ledger. Public reading room, free to any citizen, scribes making fair copies for the other cities." He hesitated. "They named it for her. The Marun Archive. There's a plaque. My cousin saw it — says her eye's carved into the stone above the door, the missing one, rendered honest. And the pardons—" He swallowed. "Forty-one names read aloud on opening day. Smugglers. Her brother among them. Treason struck from the record, murder entered in its place. The crown's murders, named as murders, in front of everyone."

Theo set down his knife.

He had presented the ledger himself, a year ago, standing before the councils with Aldric's testimony-bones in a case beside him and his voice shaking only at the start. Some councils had accepted. Some hadn't. He'd called it imperfect, earned vindication, and tried to believe it. But vindication was abstract. This — a building with her name on it, a plaque with her honest eye, forty-one names spoken aloud while crowds wept — this was Sefa made permanent. Grief weaponized into purpose, and the purpose achieved, and the woman herself not there to see it, having died in the high passes holding a rearguard action so a child could reach a choir and sing.

"She'd have hated the ceremony," Theo said. "Loved the ledger. Hated the ceremony."

"Everyone says that."

"Because it's true." He picked the knife back up. "I'll take the commission. What do you need?"

"A pendant," the merchant said. "For my mother. She's alive," he added quickly, and then, softer, "but my father isn't, and she doesn't talk about him, and I thought—"

"Plain work?" Theo asked. "Or something that remembers?"

The merchant thought about it for a long moment. "Something that remembers," he said finally. "But gently. If that can be done."

"It can be done," Theo said, and it could. He'd learned that over the past year. He couldn't hear the dead anymore, but the dead didn't need him to hear them for a keepsake to hold them. He carved the father's initial into the pendant's back, and a small boat on the front because the man had been a fisherman, and when the merchant held it he cried a little, quietly, the way grown men cry in workshops, and paid double, and Theo gave half back.

That was the shape of the work now. Not voices. Just hands, and memory, and the living carrying the dead gently, which was, he suspected, all anyone had ever needed from a carver anyway.

* * *

Summer brought pilgrims.

He should have expected it. The choir beneath Thornfell was famous now; the story had been told and retold until it barely resembled itself, and people came up the mountain wanting to see the sunken cathedral where a king died human and a monster unraveled into song. Most of them were harmless. Some of them found their way to Greyhollow, because the story included a bone-carver, and bone-carvers were interesting, and one of them had given up a miraculous gift, and wouldn't he demonstrate—

No. He wouldn't. Politely, firmly, and increasingly less politely as the season wore on.

One afternoon a scholar arrived — a genuine one, gray-robed, dusty, trailing the smell of ink — and asked not for a demonstration but for an apprenticeship referral, saying she'd heard the carver of Greyhollow had developed new methods for working bone, methods that didn't rely on the old resonant traditions, and the universities wanted them recorded before they were lost.

"They're not going to be lost," Theo said. "My apprentice knows all of them."

"Then may I speak with your apprentice?"

He almost said no. It was reflexive, the old instinct — keep her hidden, keep her small, keep the world's attention off the girl who had once been a vessel. But she was eleven, and getting taller, and the Veil was dead, and hiding her had stopped being protection sometime around the second year and become, if he was honest, a habit of his own fear.

"She's in the garden," he said. "Ask her yourself."

He watched through the window as the scholar interviewed Ilset among the bean rows, the girl gesturing with a trowel, explaining thumb-reading and forgiveness-of-material with the grave authority of someone twice her age. The scholar took notes. At the end she bowed — actually bowed — and Ilset went pink to her ears and pretended it hadn't happened, and came inside and said, "I'm going to write a book," in the tone other children announced they wanted a pony.

"Are you."

"*Methods of the Silent Bench.* That's the title. The scholar says the university will print it."

"The university will want payment in copies for their library."

"Obviously." She was already hunting for paper. "Master Reyes, how do you spell *striation*?"

He spelled it for her. Outside, the summer light came gold through the window glass, and fell across the bench, and across the plain honest bones waiting to be worked, and Theo thought about a mute child who had once been built to be consumed, standing in a garden deciding to write a book. The Veil had eaten nations' grief. Ilset was going to spend her life making things that fed people instead. Somewhere, he hoped, whatever remained of King Aldric knew, and was glad.

* * *

Autumn again. Two years, now, since the choir.

On the anniversary they climbed to the ridge above town — their tradition, established last year without discussion, maintained this year the same way — and ate apples and looked down at Greyhollow lighting its lamps one by one as dusk came on. Below them the town glowed like embers in a grate. Somewhere down there was the workshop, and the contract pinned above her corner, and the half-finished chess set he was carving for the innkeeper, and the small bird Ilset had been working on in secret for weeks, hiding it in a cloth whenever he came near, badly.

"One more year of apprenticeship," he said, "and I'll have to start paying you wages."

"You'll have to start paying me wages *now*. I did the entire Kessler order while you were in Vessany."

"The Kessler order was sanding."

"Sanding is work." She bit her apple with a crack. "Sefa's ledger says so. Page sixty. *All labor toward the movement of goods is labor,*" she recited, "and sanding moves goods toward being goods."

"You've actually read the ledger."

"Twice." Quieter: "Her handwriting gets worse near the end. You can tell when she knew she was dying. The entries get shorter but they don't get less true." She looked out at the lights. "I think that's the best thing I know how to say about anything. Shorter, but not less true."

Theo didn't answer. There wasn't anything to add. Below them the last lamps came on, and the first stars came out above the pass where the fires had been, green threading through the black, a scar becoming landscape.

"Come on," he said, standing, brushing off his coat. "Cold's coming down. And I promised Marta I'd deliver her dice tomorrow, which means tonight I have to finish them, which means tonight you have to help me finish them, which means—"

"Which means sanding," Ilset said, groaning, delighted, already scrambling up.

They went down the mountain side by side, toward the light, toward the bench, toward the plain and patient work of hands — and neither of them heard anything in the dark around them but wind, and each other's footsteps, and that, Theo thought, was the whole miracle, and it was enough.
