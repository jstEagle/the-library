## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bees woke before Ivo did, as they always did, and by the time the light came down over the eastern ridge like water poured through cloth, the hives were already humming their low, indifferent hymn. Ivo counted them by sound. Six hives, six hums, each with its own pitch, its own temper. The third hive from the wall was always angriest in late summer. The one nearest the herb garden sang flat, as if mourning something. Twenty years of listening had taught Ivo the grammar of bees, and it was a comfort to know that at least one language in Correnvale asked nothing of its speaker but patience.

Ivo pulled on the gloves, tied the veil, and went out into the morning.

Correnvale lay in a bowl of hills like a coin dropped into a cupped palm. It was not a large valley—two hours' walk from the southern spring where the water rose cold and mineral-bright, to the northern pass where the road gave up and became goat track, then bracken, then stone. A river ran through it, shallow and quick, too busy to reflect anything properly. Three farms clung to the lower slopes, a mill with a wheel that turned even in winter because the current never froze, and a chapel whose bell had cracked sometime before Ivo arrived and had never been replaced. The valley folk said the crack was from frost. Ivo knew better, though it made no difference now. Nothing rang true here. That was the whole point of the place.

Magic could not follow a person into Correnvale. That was what the old maps said, the ones drawn after the war, when the crown's cartographers marked certain places with small careful symbols meaning *hollow*, meaning *dead ground*, meaning *nothing grows here but ordinary things*. Whether it was the iron in the hills or the way the wind moved through the passes or some older exhaustion sunk into the soil like blood into a floor, no one living knew for certain. What was known was simpler: a swordspeaker who crossed the ridgeline felt their gift go out of them like heat leaving a bath. The words they had spent lifetimes mastering went silent in the throat. The power that had once moved armies became, in Correnvale, only breath.

Ivo had crossed that ridgeline twenty years ago with burned hands, a dead name, and a sword wrapped in sacking on their back, and had felt the silence come down over them like snowfall, and had wept—not from grief, or not only from grief, but from the terrible relief of it. To be mute in the only tongue that mattered. To be, finally, no one.

Now Ivo was the keeper of bees, the mender of the mill's smaller gears, the person the valley folk called on when a calf came wrong or a roof beam split. They called them *hermit* behind their back and *neighbor* to their face, and both words were true enough. Ivo kept to the stone house at the valley's southern end, grew leeks and onions and lavender for the hives, sold honey in wax-sealed crocks at the autumn market, and never once, in twenty years, spoke aloud a single syllable of the Old Speech.

Not in prayer. Not in anger. Not once in the depth of a fevered winter when the coughing came and Ivo half-hoped the words would rise up unbidden and finish the work the purge had begun.

Silence was the oath. Silence was survival. Silence, Ivo had learned, could become a kind of home, the way an empty room becomes a home once you stop flinching at the echoes.

* * *

Mornings followed their liturgy. Water from the well, heated on the banked coals; bread set to rise; a slow walk of the hive-row with smoke in hand, lifting each lid only far enough to see that all was well. Then the whetstone.

The sword hung on two iron pegs inside the bedroom door, still wrapped in its oiled sacking, and once a month—Ivo allowed themselves this much ceremony, this one indulgence dressed as maintenance—the wrappings came off and the blade came into the light.

It was a beautiful thing, which was part of why it should have been melted down. A single-edged falx of dark steel, slightly too long for any scabbard Ivo owned, the grip wrapped in leather gone glass-smooth with handling. Along the flat, characters were etched so fine you needed raking light to find them: the old script, the command-script, the letters that had once been cut into the gates of cities to warn kings what lived behind them. *We speak, therefore kneel.* The blade itself held no magic—nothing in Correnvale could hold magic—but it held memory, and memory was heavier than any enchantment.

Ivo drew the whetstone along the edge in long even strokes, matching breath to stroke, and did not read the letters, and did not remember the sounds they made, and lied to themselves about both things every single time.

Twenty years since the Night of Ashes. Twenty years since the order of the swordspeakers—four hundred voices strong, keepers of the Old Speech, the last heirs of a language older than any throne—had been called to assembly at their mountain cloister and answered, because the summons carried the seal of their own Grandmaster, and because loyalty was the flaw bred deepest in them. The fires had been visible from the lowlands for three days, the histories said, though the histories were written by the crown and the crown wrote carefully. What Ivo remembered was smaller than fire. A novice named Ellery, fourteen years old, pushing Ivo toward the drainage tunnel with both hands and saying *go, go* in the plain tongue because she knew—Ivo would understand this only years later—that her own voice was already lost. What Ivo remembered was running down a mountainside with ash in their hair like early snow, and the taste of the oath in their mouth as they took it alone, in the dark, with no witness but a river: *I will not speak. I will not answer. Let my voice die in me before it kills again.*

Four hundred voices. One survivor who knew of, though Ivo had stopped looking for others decades ago, because hope was a luxury the oath did not cover.

The whetstone whispered. Outside, the third hive droned its angry note. Ivo finished the edge, oiled the steel, wrapped it again, and hung it back on its pegs, and the room was just a room again, and Ivo was just a beekeeper with scarred hands and a stiff shoulder, and the morning went on being ordinary, which was the only prize the valley offered and the only one Ivo had ever wanted.

That was the day. That was every day. Ivo had believed, right up until the hour the child came through the gate, that it would go on being every day until the last one.

* * *

The light was going amber and long when Ivo heard the gate latch lift.

No one used the gate. The valley folk came to the kitchen door around the side, as neighbors did, and strangers almost never came at all—the road beyond the northern pass led nowhere anyone wanted to be, and everyone south of the ridge knew Correnvale for a poor bend in a poor valley good for honey and nothing else. So when the latch clicked, Ivo's hands went still over the honey crocks, and something older than thought pulled the shoulders square and turned the head, slow, the way prey animals turn.

A child stood in the gateway.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, perhaps nine—small for either. Barefoot on the packed earth, though the evenings had taken on the first bite of autumn and the ground held its chill till midmorning now. She wore a wool dress of good weave, dyed a deep madder red, hem thick with road dust, torn at one knee. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, the way hair is cut by someone who loves a child but has never held shears with purpose. No pack. No shoes. No escort.

And she was humming.

The sound drifted across the yard ahead of her, thin and wandering, the aimless melody children make without knowing it—but Ivo's body heard it before Ivo's mind did, and every muscle locked. Because it was not a song. It had never been a song. The intervals were wrong for song, arranged in the falling thirds and suspended fifths that belonged to one music only: the cadence of the Old Speech chanted at half-voice, the way novices hummed their drills to sleep, the way soldiers of the speaking orders steadied their nerves on the eve of battle, the way a mother of the order might soothe a child who wept in the dark.

She was humming the Lament-Cadence. Third descent, the form reserved for naming the dead.

Ivo did not move. Did not breathe. Somewhere behind the ribs, the heart performed a slow, traitorous stumble, because the syllables—she was shaping actual syllables now, half-voiced, slurred the way a sleeper slurs, but unmistakable—were not random. They were the opening formula. *Ashen-vel, ashen-kele, tor-*—and Ivo's throat ached with the effort of not finishing it, because the next word was *vahra*, and the mind supplies what the mouth denies, and there it stood, complete and shining, the first line of the Rite of Witness, the oldest working in the canon, the one they taught first and buried last.

The girl reached the middle of the yard and stopped. She looked up at Ivo standing frozen among the honey crocks, and her eyes were ordinary eyes, brown, red-rimmed, exhausted, the eyes of any child at the end of a long road—and yet she tilted her head, and her lips moved around the final phrase once more, softer, questioning. As if asking directions in a language she did not know she spoke.

Then she swayed on her feet, and sat down in the dust as though her strings had been cut, and began, very quietly, to cry.

Routine reasserted itself the way routine always does, grateful for any emergency that lets it operate. Ivo moved without deciding to—lifted the girl, who weighed nothing, who was burning with fever, whose bare feet were split and gray with miles—carried her inside, set her on the settle by the hearth, brought water, brought bread softened in warm milk. She ate with the blind mechanical hunger of the half-fainting and did not wake fully, did not speak in the plain tongue, could not, Ivo discovered when she tried and produced only a husky clicking, some injury or long disuse having closed that door. Mute in the valley's speech. And yet her lips, as her eyes finally shut and sleep took her down, kept moving.

Humming. Always humming. Falling thirds, suspended fifths, the Lament-Cadence circling on itself like a hawk over a field.

Ivo sat across the room with cold hands and did not sleep. The sword hung on its pegs in the next room, wrapped and silent. The bees settled into their evening hum. Outside, the valley performed its ancient trick, pressing its quiet down over everything, insisting: *nothing here, nothing here, nothing here.*

But Ivo knew what the child was, or suspected, and the suspicion was a splinter working deeper with every hour. Magic did not follow into Correnvale. The old maps promised it. Twenty years of silence had proven it.

And yet a barefoot girl had walked through the gate carrying a dead language in her mouth like a coal cupped in bare hands, and the coal was still burning. Which meant she had not brought it across the ridge.

Which meant it was waking *here*. In the hollow ground. In the one place in the world that had agreed to forget.

Ivo watched the sleeping child shape syllables older than crowns and understood, with a certainty that felt like cold water rising, that the valley had just stopped forgetting—and that everything Ivo had buried for twenty years had heard the sound, and turned toward it.
