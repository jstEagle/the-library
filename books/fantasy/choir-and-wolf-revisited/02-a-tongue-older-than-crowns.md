## 2. A Tongue Older Than Crowns

The child slept in the loft above the smoke-room, on a pallet of straw and sheepskin that Ivo had made ready in the old way—corners turned down, a sprig of dried wormwood at the head, a bowl of water set beneath the frame to catch whatever dreams might spill. The last of these was superstition. Ivo had not believed in the old protections for twenty years, and yet their hands had performed them anyway, moving through the motions like a bell-ringer who no longer believes in bells but cannot bear to let the tower fall silent.

Ivo sat below in the dark with a single rushlight burning low and did not sleep.

Three nights now the girl had been under this roof. Three nights of honey-bread and barley porridge, of baths that turned her pale skin pink and drew the road-grime from between her toes, of patient attempts at speech that failed in both directions. She could not—or would not—speak the valley's tongue. When Ivo asked her name she only looked up with those grey eyes, wide and unafraid, and pressed two fingers to her own throat as though to say: *it is not there.* And when Ivo spoke to her in the slow, careful Correnvale dialect that the farmers used at market, she tilted her head like a bird hearing rain on a distant roof, attentive but unmoved.

But at night she sang.

Not sang, precisely. The first night Ivo had thought it a child's dreaming hum, the wordless noise all children make in sleep. Then the shapes beneath the hum had resolved, and Ivo's blood had gone cold as well-water in January.

*Vesseth-karai. Vesseth-karai, il domm.*

*Stand fast. Stand fast, and do not break.*

A sentry's cadence. A wall-word. The kind of phrase spoken by soldiers holding a line while their comrades died around them, spoken in a voice trained to carry over screaming. This child, this barefoot scrap of a girl who could not say *bread* or *thank you*, was reciting the commands of a battlefield in her sleep, in a language that had not been spoken aloud in this world for twenty years except once, by Ivo, alone, into a pillow, on the worst anniversary of every year.

Ivo had not answered. That was the discipline. That was the whole of the discipline, really—the oath reduced to its simplest and cruelest form: *never answer.* Not never speak, though speaking was forbidden too, but never answer, because the Old Speech was a conversation, and a conversation required two, and what one voice began another voice could finish. Ivo had lain in the dark below the loft with their heart hammering like a fist on a door and had not answered, and in the morning the girl had woken smiling, hungry, ordinary, remembering nothing.

The second night she had spoken longer. The third night—Ivo glanced up through the floorboards as if sight could pass through wood—she was speaking still.

Ivo rose, took the rushlight, and climbed the ladder.

* * *

Up close, asleep, the girl looked younger than her seven or eight years, small-boned and fine-featured, with the chapped lips and dirty fingernails of a child who had walked a long way alone. Her hair was the color of wet flax, cut raggedly, recently, as if someone had sheared it in haste or grief. Her feet, even bandaged, were a ruin. Whoever had put her on the road north had not expected her to survive it.

And her mouth moved. Words came out of it in a whisper-thin stream, syllables falling one against the next with the liquid precision of water finding its way downhill. Ivo knelt beside the pallet and listened, and twenty years fell away like a cloak shrugged off.

*...karai veth, karai veth, sul ammen dor...*

*Hold fast, hold fast, we are not yet undone.*

Ivo knew the line. Knew it the way one knows a scar. It came from the Lament of Harrow Field, sung—or spoken, for the swordspeakers did not sing, they *spoke* their griefs and made them true—by the survivors after the third day of the purge, when the order's strength had gone from nine hundred blades to fewer than forty. Ivo had been eleven years old when they first heard it, standing in the great hall at Cairn Ammen with cold hands and a hot throat, already sworn, already learning.

An eleven-year-old did not write battle-laments. An eight-year-old did not dream them whole.

Ivo sat back on their heels. The rushlight guttered. Below the floorboards the bees slept in their winter cluster, humming their own small language of warmth, and Ivo thought: *she is a vessel. Someone poured this into her. Or something did.*

There was a way to know. There had always been a way to know, and Ivo had spent two decades pretending otherwise.

The testing of a student was called *the opening of the door*. It was done at night because truth kept different hours than lies. It was done with questions so simple they seemed like nothing: a name asked twice, a number counted backward, a door described and then described wrong. The student answered from memory or from understanding, and the difference between the two was everything. Memory could be planted. Understanding could not.

Ivo had administered the test forty times, to forty children, in another life. Their mouth remembered the words even if their oath forbade them.

*I will not speak it,* Ivo told themselves. *I will only listen. There is no harm in listening. Listening is not answering.*

It was a lie and they knew it was a lie, and they leaned close to the sleeping child anyway.

"Sefa," Ivo whispered—not in the Old Speech, only the name the girl had scratched into the dirt of the yard on her first morning, the one thing she had given them. "Sefa. Wake."

She did not wake. But her breathing changed, deepened, slowed into that long tidal rhythm that the old teachers had called *the tide-door*, the state between sleep and waking where the mind's guards stood down. Ivo watched her eyelids flicker. Watched her hands curl loosely at her sides, fingers splayed, palms open—the receiving posture, the first thing taught to every novice: *be open, be empty, be a cup.*

Cold walked up Ivo's spine. No one had taught her that. No one alive had taught her that.

"Answer me," Ivo said, very softly, in the valley tongue. "What is your name?"

Her lips moved. What came out was not Correnvale speech. It was a single syllable, round and resonant, filling the little loft the way a struck bell fills a tower: *Sef-a-a-a*, but deeper, older, the name pronounced in the grammar of command, a name spoken *at* the world rather than *to* it. The rushlight flame bent toward her. The bowl of water beneath the bedframe shivered into rings.

Ivo's hands went numb.

"Count for me," Ivo whispered, and here they broke the letter of the oath without breaking its spirit—or so they told themselves, because the words they used next were numbers, and numbers were neutral, numbers belonged to everyone. "Five. Four. Three."

She followed. Down past one, down past one into the place beneath numbers, and her voice changed again, dropped into that register that Ivo had heard last from dying men on a burning field, the register that made the air itself attend.

"What are you?" Ivo asked. Valley tongue. Harmless words. "Where do you come from?"

And the child, eyes closed, mouth barely moving, answered in the Old Speech with perfect fluency, perfect cadence, a sentence of perhaps ten words that Ivo understood completely and would remember until they died:

*I am the door they built when all the doors were broken.*

The fire in the hearth below went out. Not dwindled—went out, all at once, like a held breath released. The bees' winter hum stuttered and resumed. In the bowl, the water stilled to a black mirror, and in the mirror Ivo saw, for half a heartbeat, not the rafters but a hall of white stone and nine hundred swords hanging on nine hundred pegs, and then nothing, only darkness and their own gaunt face looking back.

Ivo pulled the blanket up over the girl's shoulders with hands that shook. She sighed, turned her face into the sheepskin, and slept on, an ordinary child again, a foundling, a stray, warm and small and utterly unaware of what was living inside her mouth.

Ivo descended the ladder and sat in the cold smoke-room until dawn, and did not light the fire, and did not speak, and understood at last that the silence they had tended for twenty years like a garden was not a garden at all. It was a dam. And somewhere north of here, someone had broken it open and poured a river into a child and pointed her south, whether she knew it or not, whether anyone knew it or not.

*They will have felt it,* Ivo thought. *The crown's listeners will have felt it. And others. Others who did not die at Harrow Field but went north, and waited, and grew teeth.*

Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges. In the loft, the girl murmured once more in her sleep—a single word this time, soft as snowfall—and Ivo caught it despite themselves, caught it the way a wound catches a knife:

*Ammen.*

The old name of the fallen hall. The word for *home*.

She was calling for a home that had been ash for twenty years. And Ivo, sitting in the dark with their oath lying broken at their feet like a snapped bowstring, realized that some part of them had already answered—that the test had not been for the child at all, that the door the Old Speech opened swung both ways, and that somewhere in the night, without meaning to, without saying a single word of power, Ivo Quintana had answered her.

The bees began to stir. Dawn came thin and cold over Correnvale, and far off along the ridgeline, where no road ran and no traveler had any honest business being, something moved against the brightening sky—small, deliberate, watching.

Ivo saw it and felt no surprise at all. Only the old, familiar dread, wearing at last its true face: recognition.
