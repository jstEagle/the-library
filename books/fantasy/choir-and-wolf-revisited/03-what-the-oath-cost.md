## 3. What the Oath Cost

The valley spoke to Sefa in fragments.

That was how she had come to think of it—not in words, exactly, because words were what she lacked, but in pieces of knowing that arrived the way light arrived through a shutter: slanted, partial, gone when she turned to look. The bees knew her. The stones under the eastern wall knew her. The old sword above the hearth, which Ivo never touched and never let her touch, knew her best of all, and it hummed at night the way a kettle hums before it boils, a thin silver thread of sound that only she could hear.

She could not say any of this. That was the cruelty of Correnvale: it was a valley full of things that understood her, wrapped around the one person she could not make understand. When she tried to speak the valley's tongue, the sounds came out wrong, thick and blunt, like stones dropped in water. Her mouth had been shaped for something older. When she opened it, what wanted to come out was not *bread* or *water* or *please* but syllables that made the air itself lean in to listen, and she had learned—quickly, in her first weeks, from the way Ivo's face went white as bone—to swallow them back down.

So she was mute in the way that mattered, and fluent in the way that was forbidden, and she had begun to suspect that this was not an accident. She had been *made* this way. She didn't have the words for that suspicion yet either. But she felt it in her bones the way she felt weather coming, three days before the clouds.

* * *

The warmth was the strangest part.

Ivo was like a wall with a fire behind it. Sefa had known hearths, had known the particular heat that comes through stone from a room where something burns, and that was what living near Ivo was like. Nothing showed. The face was weathered and closed, the hands busy with beeswax and bread dough and the endless small maintenance of a house that wanted to be forgotten. But put your palm flat against the wall of Ivo's silence and you could feel it: a vast heat, banked and buried, pressed down under twenty years of ash.

Some nights she pressed her hand against the wall of Ivo's actual bedroom, cold plaster under her palm, and felt the warmth come through anyway. And some nights—Ivo dreamed loudly, though she didn't know it—the warmth spoke.

That was when Sefa heard the massacre.

She didn't know the word for it yet. What she had was the dream itself, which came to her through the wall like heat through stone, and which she walked through night after night because she had no choice and, in some shameful corner of herself, because she didn't want one. In the dream she was tall. In the dream her hands were scarred and strong and held a sword that sang. In the dream there were ninety-one voices around her, and every one of them was speaking at once, and the speech was *hers*, the old syllables, the ones that made the air lean in.

Then the dream turned.

It always turned the same way. Fire coming down a mountainside that was not fire—something brighter, something that ate the words right out of the air, so that the ninety-one voices went silent one by one, not because their owners died but because their *language* died first, was pulled out of them like thread from a hem, and then they fell, all of them, into a silence so total that Sefa woke gasping with her hand pressed over her own mouth.

And in the dream there was always the running. Down through burning terraces, over a bridge, into a green valley where the air was thick and dead and *nothing could follow*—and the tall dream-self falling to their knees in the shallows of a cold river, holding a sword that no longer sang, and speaking one sentence over and over into the water, a sentence Sefa could never quite catch, though she heard it every night, though it was the last thing the dream said before it let her go.

She began, without deciding to, to leave her hand on that wall longer each night. Not because it was kind. Because it was the only place in the world where she was not alone, even if the company was a massacre.

* * *

In the days, she watched.

She watched Ivo tend the hives with bare hands, unafraid of stings, murmuring to the bees in the valley's blunt tongue—*stay, sisters, stay, the frost is late this year*—and Sefa understood that the bees were not what they seemed, or not only. They settled on Ivo's arms like small golden soldiers reporting to a general in exile. Once, when a wasp raid came up from the southern hedges, Ivo had stood among the hives and the bees had risen in a single moving cloud and driven the wasps off like a wall of stings, and afterward Ivo had stood very still for a long time, looking at their own hands as if the hands belonged to a stranger.

She watched Ivo sharpen the sword. Once a month, on the dark of the moon, the whetstone came out, and the blade caught the firelight, and Ivo worked the edge in long slow strokes with an expression Sefa had no name for—grief and discipline and something like prayer. The sword never sang in the daytime. But Sefa noticed that after the sharpening, Ivo slept badly, and the wall grew hotter, and the dream came more vividly, and once—only once—she had heard, through the plaster, a single syllable escape into the dark room. Just one. A word of the old speech, spoken so quietly it was barely breath.

The house had shivered. The bees had hummed in unison. And Ivo had lain awake until dawn, and so had Sefa, her heart going like a trapped bird, because she had recognized the word. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat. It meant *remain*. It was the first word of the oath every swordspeaker swore, and Ivo had spoken it to the dark like a man checking that a wound still hurt.

* * *

She tried, once, to give something back.

It was on an evening in late autumn, when the light came long and gold across the valley and the bees had gone quiet in their boxes. Ivo sat on the step mending a net, and Sefa sat on the step below, and the silence between them was the comfortable kind, the kind they had built together out of all the words Sefa couldn't say and Ivo wouldn't.

She reached out and touched the back of Ivo's hand—the scarred one, the one with the burn shaped like a spread wing—and she hummed. Not the forbidden syllables. Just a melody, wordless, that she had carried with her from wherever she had come from, from before the gate, before the valley. A cradle-song, maybe. A marching song. She didn't know. But she felt Ivo go still under her fingers, still the way the valley was still, and when she looked up, Ivo was crying.

Not weeping. Ivo didn't weep. But two lines of water stood in the seams of that weathered face, and the mending had stopped, and the hands in Sefa's grip were trembling very slightly, like a wall trembling with the fire behind it.

Ivo looked at her for a long time. And Sefa, who could not speak, looked back, and tried to say with her eyes what she had no tongue for: *I hear it. I hear what's behind the wall. I'm not afraid of it. I'm not afraid of you.*

Ivo reached up and touched her hair, once, awkwardly, the way a person touches something they have decided not to want and want anyway.

"You should have gone to anyone else," Ivo said quietly, in the valley's blunt tongue, and then, almost inaudibly, in the old speech, the word for *child*—and the air leaned in to listen, and Sefa's whole body rang like a struck bell, because it was the first time anyone had ever spoken her language *to* her.

Then Ivo stood, and went inside, and did not come out again that night, and the wall between their rooms was so hot Sefa could not keep her hand on it.

* * *

That night the dream changed.

The fire still came down the mountain. The ninety-one voices still went out one by one, thread pulled from hem. But this time, when the dream-self fell in the cold river, the sentence over the water was clear. Sefa heard every word of it, and the words were the old words, the true words, and they were:

*I remain. I remain. Let no one ever again be made to carry what I could not save.*

And then the dream did something it had never done. It turned and looked at *her*. The tall scarred dream-self, kneeling in the river, lifted its head and looked directly at her across the burning distance of twenty years, and its mouth opened, and it said—in her language, in the language that made the air lean in—a single word that Sefa did not know, had never been taught, and understood completely:

*Run.*

She woke with the taste of snow in her mouth, though no snow had fallen. The window was full of grey pre-dawn light. And far off, at the northern edge of the valley, where the ridgeline cut the sky, she could feel something coming—something that was not fire, and not words, and not wolves, not yet, but the *space* where those things would be, the way you feel a rider before you hear the hooves.

Down in the yard, Ivo was already standing at the gate, looking north, one hand resting on the gatepost and the other, unconsciously, on the empty place at their hip where a sword had hung for twenty years and did not.

Sefa pressed her palm flat against the cold window glass, over the distant ridgeline, and felt the warmth in the house behind her, and the cold coming on the road ahead, and she understood, in the wordless way she understood everything, that the valley's long forgetting was over.
