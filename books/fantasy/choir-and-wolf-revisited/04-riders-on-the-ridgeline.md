## 4. Riders on the Ridgeline

The riders came down from the pass at midmorning, when the light was thin and the frost still held the grass in its silver teeth, and Ivo knew them before they were more than a smudge against the ridgeline—not by their banners, which they had wisely left furled, but by the way they rode. Soldiers ride like men who trust the ground beneath them. These rode like people listening to it.

Six of them. Five in the grey-and-gold of the Crown's Commission for the Suppression of Resonance, and one at the front whose seat in the saddle Ivo would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of a well, twenty years drowned and all.

Vess Marrow.

Ivo's hands did not shake. That was the first miracle of the day, and Ivo noted it with a kind of distant professional interest, the way one notes that the bees have begun clustering early, or that the well water tastes of iron. The hands were steady. The heart was doing something else entirely—something arrhythmic and old, a drumbeat from another country—and Ivo set it aside the way one sets aside a tool that has no use today.

"Sefa," Ivo said quietly, without turning. "Go to the root cellar. Take the lamp but don't light it. Take bread and the wool blanket from my bed. If I call your name twice, you stay there until spring if you must."

The girl was already in the doorway, barefoot on the cold stone, her eyes fixed on the riders with an expression Ivo recognized and hated: recognition. She could feel them, Ivo realized. Whatever she carried, whatever slept in her blood and spoke through her in the night, it had felt them coming the way a struck bell feels the hand approaching.

"They're loud," she whispered. It was the longest sentence she'd given him in three days. "Not with mouths."

"I know," Ivo said. "Cellar. Now."

She went. She didn't run, which was worse somehow—a child who runs is only afraid; a child who walks away with her chin up has already decided something about the world.

Ivo closed the door, stood a moment in the dim kitchen with the smell of honey and beeswax and last night's smoke, and performed the small treacheries of survival: pulled on the patched coat instead of the good one, smeared a thumb of ash along the jaw as though from the forge, took up the beekeeping veil and draped it over a chair where a visitor would see it. A harmless hermit kept bees and mended tools. A harmless hermit had dirt under his nails and a stoop beginning in his spine. Ivo assembled this person piece by piece in the glass of the window, the way one once assembled a stance, a grip, a breath before speaking—and then went out to meet the past at the gate.

* * *

They reined up twenty paces off, which was correct protocol and also correct tactics, and Ivo noted both. The five troopers sat their horses with the rigid boredom of men who had ridden hard for days and been told to look menacing without being permitted to be so. Their leader swung down lightly, unhurried, and pushed back the hood of a riding cloak the color of wet slate.

Twenty years had put silver at Vess Marrow's temples and lines around eyes that were still, infuriatingly, the same grey-green as the valley's first thaw. The Commission uniform fit like a second skin, tailored, immaculate, the captain's bars worked in gold thread at the collar. Everything about Vess said: *I have become exactly what I intended to become.*

"Good morning," Vess said. The voice hadn't changed either. Low, even, with that faint northern burr that the capital had never managed to sand away. "I'm looking for the keeper of this apiary. The village at Corren Ford said a hermit keeps bees above the vale and sells wax to the shrine at Aldermoor."

"You've found him," Ivo said. His voice came out right too—roughened just enough, deferential just enough, the voice of a man who had spent two decades being nobody. He had practiced being nobody the way he had once practiced forms. "Though I'll warn you the autumn harvest's spoken for. Shrine takes half, and the other half sees me through winter."

"Then we won't trouble your ledger." Vess smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and Ivo thought: *you learned that smile in the same schoolyard I learned to hold my tongue.* "Captain Vess Marrow, Crown Commission. We're surveying the high valleys—road conditions, bridge repairs, that sort of thing." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "And resonance."

"There's no magic in Correnvale," Ivo said. "Never has been. That's why people settle here. Why do you think the shrine built its hospital at the ford? Sick folk come to be somewhere quiet."

"Mm." Vess walked a slow half-circle, looking—not at Ivo, but at the yard. At the hives ranked along the south wall. At the smithy shed with its door propped open on a rusted plow blade. At the sword.

It hung inside the shed doorway, because a hermit might plausibly own his grandfather's blade and never wear it, and hiding it entirely would have been the louder lie. An old arming sword, plain crossguard, oiled and sharp and never drawn. Vess looked at it the way a musician looks at an instrument in a stranger's house.

"Family piece?"

"My father's. He kept bees too."

"He must have been proud of you."

Something moved behind Ivo's ribs, old and specific. *You were the best of us,* Vess had said once, nineteen years old, both of them bleeding from training drills under a sky the color of hammered tin. *When they finally let you speak, the whole order will kneel.* And Ivo had laughed and said the whole order could wait, because Vess had promised to show him the sea.

"The dead don't keep score," Ivo said aloud.

"No," Vess agreed softly. "We do that for them."

* * *

Vess did not leave. That was the second thing Ivo had known would happen, watching the six riders descend, and knowing it had not made it easier. The captain accepted tea—accepted it with genuine pleasure, complimented the honey, asked intelligent questions about hive weights and winter losses, and meanwhile the five troopers made a polite circuit of the property with the loose, professional gait of men who had done this a hundred times in a hundred valleys and found something worth finding in perhaps one of them.

Ivo poured tea and lied with every muscle.

It was astonishing, really, how much of the old discipline survived. The swordspeakers had trained for interrogation—Ivo remembered the cold rooms beneath the Chapterhouse, the masters asking questions in the Old Speech while novices struggled not to answer, not to flinch, not to let truth move anywhere visible on the face. *The word is a weapon,* Master Callen had said, *and the body is its sheath. Control the sheath.* Ivo had been the best in his year at controlling the sheath. It had saved his life in the purge and it was saving him now, sitting across from the only person alive who had known him before, passing the honey jar with steady hands while inside him everything screamed.

Because Vess was not searching the yard. Vess was searching *him*. Every question about bees was a probe; every pause was a listening. And underneath the questions, underneath twenty years of rank and distance, Ivo could feel the other thing—the thing that had no name they'd ever been allowed to give it, the thing that had made two teenagers sit too long by the training-yard fire, knees almost touching, saying nothing in a language that forbade everything.

"You've done well here," Vess said at last, quietly, when the troopers had drifted out of earshot toward the tree line. "This life. It suits you."

"It's a life," Ivo said. "It fits me the way any coat fits after enough winters."

"That's not what I meant and you know it." Vess set down the cup. For a moment the captain's mask slipped—not into softness, but into something warier, more tired. "Ivo. If it's you. If some part of you is still—" A breath. "There are orders. Not mine. Above mine. Something woke in the north eighteen months ago and the Commission has been chasing echoes ever since. Echoes that answer back." The grey-green eyes came up, and they were not the eyes of a friend, and they were not the eyes of an enemy, and that was the worst of all possible things. "If you hear anything in this valley—if anything *speaks*, even in dreams—you send for me. Before anyone else. Promise me that much."

"I'm a beekeeper, Captain."

"Promise me anyway."

Ivo held the gaze, and gave nothing, and understood that giving nothing was itself an answer, and watched Vess understand it too.

* * *

They stayed the night in the barn, which protocol demanded and which Ivo could not refuse without becoming interesting. Ivo spent the evening doing chores that needed no doing—splitting wood already split, checking hives by lantern-light—while inside his skull a single sentence repeated like a bell struck over and over: *echoes that answer back.*

Sefa.

He got to the cellar after full dark, moving through the house on feet he'd taught silence twenty years ago. She was awake, of course. She was always awake now, it seemed, sitting in the dark among the turnips and the cider casks with the unlit lamp beside her, her eyes catching what little light there was.

"Was it her?" Sefa signed. She had invented half her signs herself, quick and strange and precise. "The one with the cold voice. Was it her?"

"How do you know she has a cold voice?"

"She dreamed here last night. In the barn." Sefa's hands stilled. "She was dreaming about you. About someone named Ivo. But she couldn't say the name out loud, even in the dream. Especially in the dream."

Ivo sat down slowly on a cider cask, and the cellar was very dark and very cold, and somewhere overhead the troopers' boots crossed the floorboards, and somewhere beyond the walls the wolves were silent in the trees, and the child looked at him with the patience of the buried.

"What else did she dream?"

Sefa's hands moved carefully, choosing each sign like a step across ice. "A field. Fire coming down a hill. Everyone shouting in the true tongue, all at once, and then stopping, one by one, like lamps going out." Her fingers hesitated. "And she was calling a name into the fire. Over and over. Your name. And the fire answered her instead."

Above them, distantly, Captain Vess Marrow turned over in a soldier's blanket in Ivo's hay, twenty years and one impossible valley away from the person she had been calling, and slept, or did not sleep, and dreamed—or did not dream—of fire.

Ivo reached out and took the child's hand, because there was nothing left to say in any language at all.

In the morning the riders would ride east, and Vess would look back once at the gate, and Ivo would stand in the yard wearing the face of a hermit until the horses crested the ridge and were gone. But that night, in the root cellar, holding the hand of a girl who heard dreams, Ivo Quintana finally admitted what the day had made undeniable:

The valley had stopped forgetting. And it had started with him.
