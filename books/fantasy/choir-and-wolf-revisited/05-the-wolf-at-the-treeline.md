## 5. The Wolf at the Treeline

The first wolf came on a morning of thin frost, when the beehives wore caps of white and the valley breathed smoke from every chimney. Ivo saw it from the herb garden, a shape at the treeline where the pines began their dark climb toward the pass. It stood the way no wolf should stand—upright in its attention, head level, eyes fixed not on the hives or the goats but on the cottage itself. On Ivo.

It was too large. That was the second wrongness, after the stillness. A grey wolf of Correnvale's high forest might reach Ivo's hip; this one stood nearly to the chest, its coat the color of wet slate, its muzzle scarred along one side like something had once tried to carve it and failed. It did not move for the better part of an hour. It simply watched, patient as winter, until the light changed and it turned without hurry back into the trees, the way a man leaves a room he intends to return to.

Ivo stood among the frosted rosemary with dirt under their nails and cold climbing their spine, and thought: *they found us.*

Twenty years. Twenty years of silence worn smooth as river stone, of being nobody, of tending bees and mending fences and never once letting the old syllables rise past the teeth. The crown had forgotten Correnvale—that was the valley's whole gift, its whole covenant. Magic could not follow them here. Whatever resonance the Old Speech carried, the valley drank it down like rain into sand. That was why the swordspeakers' scattered survivors had drifted toward it, generation by generation, until only Ivo remained, a hermit with a sword they never drew and a tongue they never used.

But wolves did not need magic. Wolves remembered.

That night Ivo barred the door early and sat awake with the unsheathed sword across their knees—not to use, never to use, but because the weight of it was the weight of everything they had been, and some nights the hands needed reminding. The blade was called Meridian. It had been forged for a speaker who died at Redford Bridge, and it had passed through four hands before coming to Ivo's, and it had not tasted air outside the scabbard in two decades. Its edge was oiled and honed regardless. Hope, maybe. Or habit. Or the quiet knowledge that habit is all that stands between a person and the person they swore never to become again.

* * *

Sefa heard the wolves before she saw them, though she did not know that was what she'd heard.

She heard it in her sleep, which was where most things reached her first now. A voice below her window, low and layered, many throats braided into one sound—and underneath the animal howl of it, words. Real words. Words she understood the way she understood warmth or hunger, without translation, without effort. They were speaking to her. She was almost sure of it. They were saying something about *coming*, and something about *home*, and one long phrase that made her chest ache as if someone had pressed a thumb gently against a bruise she hadn't known she carried.

When she woke, the frost on the glass held a single paw print pressed against the outside of the pane, large as her spread hand, five toes and a pad, already going soft at the edges as the sun warmed the valley.

She didn't tell Ivo. Telling Ivo things had become complicated lately, the way water becomes complicated when it starts to freeze—still water, still theirs, but with a new brittleness running through it that neither of them named. Since the riders had come and gone, since the night Ivo had tested her by candlelight and then sat afterward with their face in their hands like a man counting coins he wished he'd never been given, there was a carefulness between them. Sefa felt it the way she felt weather coming in her knees, which was another thing she'd stopped mentioning.

So she kept the paw print to herself, and kept listening, and three nights later she got up out of bed while the moon stood over the ridge like a watchman's lamp, and walked out the door Ivo had begun locking, and crossed the frozen garden barefoot, and went to the treeline.

She did not feel the cold. That was the strangest part. The snow took her feet and gave nothing back, no bite, no burn, and she walked through it the way she imagined other people walked through dreams, except that she was wide awake inside the walking—or not awake, exactly. Awake the way a bell is awake when it has just been struck.

At the edge of the pines, the wolves were waiting. Four of them, sitting in a half-circle in the snow like elders at a fire, and their eyes caught the moonlight and threw it back amber, and their breath rose from their jaws in slow silver columns. The big scarred one sat at the center. When Sefa stopped at the border where field became forest, it inclined its head—a small motion, deliberate, courteous—and spoke.

Not growled. Not howled. *Spoke.* And the language it spoke was the language of her dreams, the one Ivo sometimes let slip a half-syllable of before catching it behind their teeth like a moth in a fist. Sefa understood every word, and the understanding felt less like learning than like remembering, and what the wolf said was:

*"You are late, little vessel. But you are not lost. We have been counting the winters."*

She answered. She didn't decide to answer; the answering simply happened, the way blinking happens, the way crying happens when grief gets too large for the body holding it. Her mouth opened and the Old Speech came out of her fluent and full-voiced, every rolled consonant and long vowel in its proper place, and she said—she would remember this part always, even after—"I don't know where home is. Nobody will tell me."

And the scarred wolf said, gently, terribly: *"They cannot tell you. You were not born to a home. You were made."*

Then the moon went behind cloud, and Sefa was lying in her bed with snow melting in her hair and her feet blue-black with cold, and Ivo was kneeling over her with a candle, shaking her shoulder, calling her name in the valley's plain speech, and her feet hurt so badly when they thawed that she cried, and Ivo held her hands and said nothing about anything, and the silence between them filled up the whole cottage like floodwater.

* * *

Ivo knew what the wolves were. Knowing was the problem.

There had been two survivals, after Redford Bridge. Two ways the order endured its own ending. The swordspeakers who fled south and west had chosen silence—had buried the Speech so deep it couldn't be found, had scattered into valleys where magic starved, had traded power for persistence. Ivo was the last of those, the last sworn, the last silent.

The others had gone north. Into the pinewastes, beyond the crown's maps, where the witch-hunters' writ ran thin and the winters did the hunting for them. And there, they had made a different bargain. They had not buried the Speech. They had fed it—to the wolves. Old blood-pacts, older than the order itself, older than crowns: the northern packs had always spoken alongside the speakers, two voices for one command. So when the purge came, the survivors had poured what remained of themselves into their brother-beasts and vanished into them. The wolves *were* the cult now. Fur and fang and fluency. They had survived the way a song survives its singer.

Ivo had believed them dead for twenty years. Believing it had been a kind of comfort, the way believing oneself the last of anything is a comfort—it makes the silence holy instead of empty.

Now the wolves sat at the treeline, watching the cottage, watching the child, and the comfort curdled into dread. Because the wolves had not come for Ivo. Ivo understood that with terrible clarity. The wolves had come for Sefa—the beacon, the waking voice, the little vessel, whatever she was becoming. And where the wolves gathered, the crown could not be far behind, because Vess Marrow had spent her career learning to read the north the way sailors read weather, and wolves at a southern treeline in autumn were a storm front if ever there was one.

The past was closing in from both directions, and it had chosen the same doorway.

Ivo began sleeping in the chair beside Sefa's bed, sword across their knees, and began tying a cord from the girl's wrist to the bedframe each night—not cruelly, they told themselves, just a bell-cord, just a warning—but every morning they untied it with shame sitting in their stomach like a swallowed stone. On the fourth morning the cord hung cut. Snipped clean, laid coiled on the pillow like a returned gift. And beneath it, pressed into the floorboard dust, a paw print again, and again the size of a hand, and this time Ivo knelt and put their own hand into it and their fingers did not reach the edges.

That day Ivo walked to the treeline alone and stood where the field met the pines and said, aloud, in the valley's plain speech, to whatever waited in the shadows between the trunks: "She is a child. Whatever she carries, she did not choose it. If you take her, you take her from the only quiet she has ever known."

The forest said nothing. But the wind moved through the high branches in a long descending sigh, and Ivo—who had spent twenty years learning to hear what speech sounded like when it wasn't speech—heard in that sigh a shape, a grammar, an answer forming somewhere out of sight. And the shape of it was patience. The wolves were not asking. They were informing. They had all the time that winter offered, and winter had come early.

* * *

Sefa dreamed again, and this time she dreamed of the massacre.

She had never seen Redford Bridge. She had not been born; she had been *made*, though she did not yet know the word for what she was, only the ache of it, the sense of being a cup shaped for someone else's wine. But the dream came anyway, complete and merciless: the bridge in fog, the banners of the purge burning green, the speakers standing in ranks on the far shore and speaking in unison, and the words doing nothing, nothing, because the crown had learned to unmake sound itself, had learned to drink commands out of the air before they landed. She watched them fall. She watched one figure wade backward into the river with a sword and swim away downstream, and the dream pressed its face close to hers and whispered: *that one is still alive. That one is in the next room.*

She woke knowing two things she had not known at dusk. That Ivo had run from the bridge. And that Ivo had never told her, and never would, unless something broke.

Something was breaking. She could feel it in the cottage the way she felt storms in her knees. Ivo had stopped humming. Had started watching the treeline from the window with the curtain pulled back a finger's width, hour after hour. Had burned something in the stove one evening—a paper, a letter, a page covered in the old script—and stood over the flames longer than the burning required.

And the wolves kept singing at the forest's edge, closer each night, their voices threading through her sleep, saying her name—or the name the Speech had given her, which was not Sefa, which was a word like a key turning—and each night she woke a little closer to the door.

On the ninth night, Ivo woke to find the bed empty, the cord cut, the door open, and snow falling thick and silent over the garden, filling the small footprints even as Ivo followed them. The tracks led straight, unhurried, certain, toward the treeline. Toward the pines. Toward the amber eyes waiting in the dark between the trunks, and the voices in the snow saying, in the oldest language there was, that the vessel was coming home.

Ivo took down Meridian, and for the first time in twenty years, drew it.
