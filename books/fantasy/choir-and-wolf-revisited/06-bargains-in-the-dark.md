## 6. Bargains in the Dark

The snow did not stop falling. It fell all through that night and into the morning, soft as ash, patient as debt, until Correnvale wore a foot of white like a shroud laid over something not yet dead.

Ivo walked back from the treeline with Meridian bare in one hand and Sefa's small hand in the other, and the strangest part of it was how ordinary her grip felt—chapped knuckles, cold fingertips, the reflexive squeeze of a child who had been caught doing something forbidden and expected to be scolded. She did not remember walking out. She said so twice, in the valley's plain tongue, which she had begun to learn in fragments: *I didn't go. I was here. I was sleeping.* The third time she stopped arguing and simply pressed closer to Ivo's side, and they finished the walk in silence, two figures dragging a single line of prints through the garden gate while the wolves watched from between the pines with eyes like banked coals.

Ivo barred the door. Then, after a moment, unbarred it, because a barred door had never once stopped anything that mattered, and went instead to the hearth and built the fire up high.

"You were talking to them," Ivo said.

Sefa sat with her knees drawn up, hands wrapped around a cup of honeyed milk she was too tense to drink. "I don't know what I was saying."

"I do." Ivo kept the voice level. Twenty years of practice at keeping voices level. "You were speaking the old tongue. Fluently. You told them you were coming."

"Was I?" Her face did something complicated—fear and longing fighting for the same ground. "It doesn't feel like saying things. It feels like... remembering them. Like the words are already there and I just let them out." She looked into the fire. "They weren't frightening, though. Out there. They sounded like—" She struggled. "Like someone calling me by a name I haven't heard yet."

Ivo set Meridian against the wall where it could be seen. A message to the child; perhaps also a message to whatever else might be listening. *The sword is awake now too.*

"Sleep in my room tonight," Ivo said. "Door open. Fire lit."

"And you'll be awake all night watching me."

"Yes."

Sefa almost smiled. It was a thin, tired thing, but it was hers. "That's the first honest thing anyone's said to me since I came here."

* * *

Halda came at dusk three days later, when the light had gone the color of dirty wool and the valley lay blue and hushed beneath its snow.

Ivo saw her first from the bee-yard, standing at the fence with smoke still drifting off the hives—the bees slept deep in winter cluster, but the ritual of tending them was the last piece of the old life Ivo had refused to surrender, and rituals mattered most when everything else was coming apart. Halda stood at the gate exactly where a guest would stand, which was itself a kind of threat. The wolf-cult did not knock. The wolf-cult arrived, and the world rearranged itself around their arrival.

She was tall the way winter is tall—all vertical lines and gray—and she wore a cloak of undyed fleece over armor that had been made for someone larger and refitted without apology. At her hip hung no sword but a horn, banded in silver gone black with age. Around her throat, on a cord, hung a tooth Ivo recognized and wished very much not to: the long curved canine of a dire-wolf, the mark of a speaker among the northern pack, one who carried words the way soldiers carry banners.

"Ivo Quintana," Halda said. Her accent bent the old name into shapes the valley had forgotten. "Twenty years, and you keep bees."

"Somebody has to ask the flowers politely."

Something moved at the corner of Halda's mouth—not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "May I come inside, swordspeaker? Or will you make an old friend stand in the snow?"

"We are not friends."

"No," Halda agreed pleasantly. "We are survivors. It's nearly the same thing, and far more useful." She waited. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it away, and Ivo understood that she would stand there until spring if it suited her, and that every minute she stood there was a minute spent reminding Ivo that patience was a weapon too.

Ivo opened the gate.

Inside, Sefa sat by the fire with a slate, copying letters Ivo had drawn for her that morning—valley letters, plain trade-script, the alphabet of people who had never commanded anything. She looked up when Halda ducked through the low door, and Ivo watched the child's whole body change: spine straightening, chin lifting, some posture rising out of her that no foundling orphan had any right to know. Halda looked at her the way one looks at a fire that has finally caught.

"*Vessel,*" Halda said, in the Old Speech, and the word filled the room like water filling a cup.

Sefa answered before she could stop herself—a single syllable of acknowledgment, formal, correct—and then clapped both hands over her mouth, horrified.

"There," Halda said softly, to Ivo, in the trade tongue again. "You see. You can bar the door, burn the books, bury the language under twenty years of honey and silence. But she answers. She was made to answer. And what is made cannot be unmade by kindness." She removed her cloak at last and sat, uninvited, taking the chair nearest the fire as if she had sat in it all her life. "So let us talk business, Quintana, because I have walked nine days through your beautiful dead valley to say one sentence to you, and I would rather say it warm."

Ivo stayed standing. "Say it."

"Give us the child. Freely given, freely taken—that matters, in the old law, and you know it does. In exchange, the pack turns north at the ridgeline and never crosses into Correnvale again. Not this winter, not any winter. Your valley stays magic-dead and magic-safe. Your bees keep their flowers. No hunters, no cult, no war at your gate." Halda spread her hands, palms open, the gesture of a merchant displaying honest goods. "You have spent twenty years buying silence with exile. I am offering to let you keep the silence and lose only the exile's loneliness. It is, I think, the kindest bargain anyone will offer you this season."

Through the window, beyond the garden wall, the treeline stood dark and full. Ivo did not need to see the eyes to know they were there.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we take her anyway, eventually, and the valley learns what the rest of the world learned about crossing the pack." Halda said it without heat, without cruelty, the way one reports weather. "But I hope you won't refuse. I knew your teacher, did you know that? Ashkane trained two winters with our speakers before the crowns decided your order was a plague. She said you were the best listener she ever taught." The gray eyes settled on Ivo, ancient and unhurried. "Listen now. The crown's riders are not here for the child. Not truly. Vess Marrow's orders are to find the vessel and bring it back breathing, because the war-mages want a weapon that speaks commands, and a grown weapon can be pointed. But the child herself? The child is a fuse, Quintana. Everything that wakes her wakes the war. Every day you keep her here, hiding her, teaching her letters, loving her—I can see it, don't insult us both—you are shortening the fuse. Give her to us, and the pack keeps her in the far north where no crown can reach and no mage can point. She lives. The valley lives. Everyone loses a little. That is what peace is."

The fire popped. On the slate, Sefa had stopped writing. Her chalk rested mid-letter, and she was looking at the floor, and Ivo realized she had understood more of the trade tongue than either of them had assumed.

"She's a person," Ivo said quietly. "Not a parcel."

"She's a person shaped like a key," Halda said, rising, taking up her cloak. "And keys do not get to choose which doors they open. Only who carries them." At the door she paused, and for one moment the emissary's mask slipped and something older showed through—weariness, or grief, or simply the long fatigue of the surviving. "Three days, swordspeaker. Then the pack stops waiting politely. You have my word on the old law, which is the only word I have left."

She left. The snow took her footsteps within the hour.

* * *

Vess Marrow came alone, past midnight, and did not knock either—but then, she had never needed to. She had known this house when it was a garrison house, before the fall, before the purge, before the oath. She let herself in the way one enters a memory, carefully, expecting it to be smaller than remembered.

She stood dripping in the entryway, mail frosted white, and looked at Ivo across twenty years and one extinguished war, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"You drew your sword," Vess said at last. "Word reached camp. A farmer saw you carrying it bare through the snow, chasing a child toward the pines." Her hand rested on her own hilt—not drawing, just resting, the way one touches an old scar. "Do you know what I reported? I reported a mad hermit with a stick. Because the alternative was reporting that the last swordspeaker of Correnvale had broken silence, and then I would have had to arrest you, and I have spent twenty years being very good at not arresting you."

"Vess—"

"The crown wants the girl alive." She said it fast, flat, the way you pull a splinter. "Not captured alive—*kept* alive. There is a difference, and the difference is a cage in the capital and mages who will feed her words until she speaks armies into the field. Marrow, they said. Bring us the vessel. And I rode out here to tell you, off the record, out of earshot, in the middle of a cursed snowstorm, because apparently I am still—" She stopped. Jaw tight. "Because apparently I am still whatever I was."

Ivo crossed the room slowly. Up close, Vess looked older than her years and younger than her rank, and exhausted in the specific way of someone who has been holding a door shut with her shoulder for two decades.

"The wolves came to me first," Ivo said. "They've offered a trade. The child for the valley's safety."

"And you're considering it."

"I'm considering everything. That's the problem." Ivo laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "If I give her to the pack, I save the valley and sell the only— the one person in twenty years who has made this place feel like a home instead of a hiding hole. If I keep her, I doom the valley and hand her to whichever army arrives first. If I run with her, we freeze in the passes, and she talks in her sleep, and everything that hunts her follows the sound. Every road out of this betrayal ends somewhere worse, Vess. There is no clean choice. There is only choosing who we fail."

Vess was quiet for a long time. Snowmelt dripped from her cloak onto the flagstones, ticking like a slow clock.

"When we were young," she said finally, "you told me the Old Speech had no word for *betray*. That it was a language built for truth, and truth couldn't turn on itself." Her mouth twisted. "I thought it was romantic nonsense. Now I look at you, and at that child, and at the wolves in your trees and the crown at my back, and I think: maybe the language was right. Maybe there's no such thing. Maybe there's only truth, arriving late, and everyone it cuts on the way."

She turned to go, then paused at the door, her back to Ivo, her hand on the frame.

"They're moving up the timetable," she said. "Orders changed tonight. We ride for the treeline shrine at dawn. All of us." A breath. "Whatever you're going to do, Ivo—do it before the sun."

The door closed behind her. Outside, the snow went on falling, and between the dark trunks of the pines, amber eyes opened one by one, like lamps being lit along a road that led nowhere Ivo wanted to go—and everywhere at once.
