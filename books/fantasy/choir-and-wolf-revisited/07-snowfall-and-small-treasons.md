## 7. Snowfall and Small Treasons

The snow came the night Sefa learned what she was.

It had been waiting all through the thin weeks of autumn, she thought later—holding its breath above the valley like a held word—and when it finally fell, it fell with the patience of something that meant to stay. By morning Correnvale was white to the rooftops, and the bees' hives wore little caps of snow like sleeping pilgrims, and the world had gone so quiet that Sefa could hear her own blood moving in her ears.

She stood at the kitchen window with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands, watching Ivo cross the yard. The old swordspeaker moved slowly now, favoring the left knee when the cold came into it, and they paused at the gate to look north, toward the treeline, the way they always did. The wolves had not shown themselves in nine days. That was worse than if they had.

Behind Sefa, on the table where Ivo had left it, lay the letter.

She had not meant to read it. She told herself that, and the telling was true, but truth did not stop her hands from having already unfolded it while Ivo was out checking the hives, nor her eyes from having already fallen on the words written there in a cramped, careful hand—the hand of Halda, the wolf-woman who smelled of pine and old iron and spoke the Old Speech as easily as breathing.

*The vessel is ripening,* the letter said. *You know what she was grown for. You know who grew her. When the voice wakes fully, she will be called home—to us or to them. There is no third road, Quintana. Choose for her before the choice is taken.*

Sefa had read it three times before her mind would accept it. *Vessel.* Not foundling. Not orphan. Not even, properly, a child. A made thing. Something shaped in some northern dark by people who wanted a mouth for their old words, a throat for their war, a girl-shaped jar to carry the language until it was needed again.

She looked down at her own hands then, at the small scars on her knuckles from the beekeeping lessons, at the chapped skin around her nails, and for the first time in her life she wondered whether her hands were hers at all—or whether they had been built, joint by joint, the way a carpenter builds a door. Whether the door had ever been asked what it wished to hold.

The tea had gone cold in her cup. She drank it anyway, because Ivo had taught her never to waste warmth, and because doing ordinary things with extraordinary hands was the only way she knew to keep from screaming.

* * *

Ivo came back in smelling of smoke and wax and cold, stamping snow off their boots, and Sefa watched them across the kitchen and understood, suddenly, everything she had only half-understood before. Why Ivo flinched sometimes when she laughed too loudly. Why the old swordspeaker tested her at night with whispered syllables and went gray-faced when she answered correctly without knowing why. Why Ivo's love came wrapped in fear like a blade wrapped in cloth.

They had known. All along, they had known what she was, and they had let her believe she was a lost child who had wandered barefoot out of the mountains, humming words she didn't understand.

"You read it." Ivo's voice was quiet. They didn't look surprised. That was the worst part—that nothing about her betrayal surprised them, as if even her small treasons had been foreseen, planned, accounted for in whatever ledger they kept of her.

"You knew," Sefa said. Her own voice sounded strange to her, thinner than usual. "You knew I wasn't real."

Ivo set down the hive tool with exaggerated care, the way they did when their hands wanted to shake and they refused to allow it. "Sit down, Sefa."

"Am I even called Sefa? Or is that just—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Is that just what you named the vessel? Like naming a bucket?"

For a moment Ivo closed their eyes, and Sefa saw twenty years pass behind their face like weather. "You are the child who arrived at my gate with frostbitten feet," they said, "and ate six honey-breads and threw up in my garden and cried yourself to sleep in my chair. Whatever anyone made you to be, that is what you have been here. That is what you are."

"But it isn't what I *was*."

"No," Ivo admitted. And then, because Ivo had never once lied to her, not even kindly: "No. It isn't."

Sefa looked at the window, at the falling snow, at the dark line of the forest where the wolves lived in the space between trees. *There is no third road,* Halda had written. But Sefa had spent two years learning the Old Speech in her sleep, and one thing the old tongue taught, over and over, in every dream: roads are made by walking. Someone had decided there were only two roads for her—the crown's road, the cult's road—and no one, not once, had asked her which way her own feet wanted to go.

Well. Her feet could decide now.

"I'm going out," she said.

"Sefa—"

"The storm's coming harder tonight. If I'm not back by full dark—" She heard herself speaking the valley's flat trade-tongue, and hated how it sat in her mouth like a borrowed coat. "Don't come after me. Please. For once, don't."

Ivo took one step toward her, and Sefa saw the whole argument rise in their face—the oath, the fear, the memory of wars—and she also saw, beneath it, the thing that had kept her alive through her first winter here: Ivo loved her. Loved her the way the valley loved its river, quietly, constantly, without needing to say so.

So she did the cruelest kind thing available to her. She answered the question Ivo had been asking in their heart since the day she arrived, in the only language that couldn't be mistaken for anything else.

"*Veth-kara i domm,*" she said softly. *Stay.*

And Ivo—who had sworn silence for twenty years, whose body had been trained past instinct to obey that word—stayed. Stood rooted in the middle of their own kitchen while Sefa pulled on the too-big coat, the wool mittens, the boots two sizes too large, and walked out into the snow.

She was crying before she reached the gate. She didn't stop walking.

* * *

The storm met her halfway up the pasture.

It came down off the mountains in a white wall, erasing the world behind her step by step, and Sefa walked into it with her chin down and her arms wrapped around herself, letting it erase her too. Let it erase the vessel. Let it erase the made thing. Somewhere under this snow, maybe, was whatever small original self had existed before the makers got hold of her—a seed, a spark, a girl who belonged to nobody's purpose—and if she walked far enough into the white, maybe she'd find her.

Or maybe there was nothing underneath. Maybe she was all vessel, all the way down, a jar painted to look like a person. The thought opened in her chest like a wound, and she let it bleed, because the pain at least was hers. She was almost sure the pain was hers.

The wolves found her at the stone wall below the birch wood.

She heard them first—not growling, nothing so crude—but a low harmonic hum in the air, the sound the Old Speech made when it gathered itself, and then shapes resolved out of the whiteness: five of them, six, standing in the deep snow as though it were summer grass. They were too large, as they had always been too large, their coats the color of ash and iron, their eyes holding a patient amber light. The biggest one stepped forward, and Sefa recognized her—the emissary, Halda's shadow, the wolf who had watched the farm for a season.

In her sleep, Sefa had spoken with these wolves. In her sleep, they had answered.

"*Kessa ven-arrha?*" the great wolf said aloud, in a voice like wind through a door left open. *Why do you walk alone, little mouth?*

Little mouth. Even the wolves used the name the makers gave her function instead of a name for her self. Sefa's throat closed. Snow gathered on her lashes and melted.

"I don't want to be a mouth," she said—in the Old Speech, because the trade-tongue had failed her, because this was the one language her body knew and she hated it and needed it both. "I don't want to be a vessel. I don't want to be carried anywhere. I want—" Her voice cracked. "I want to belong to myself. Is there any place in your north for a thing like that?"

The wolves regarded her. Snow settled on their backs and did not melt, and Sefa understood dimly that they were deciding something, conferring in frequencies below hearing, weighing her the way she had been weighed her whole life.

But then the lead wolf did something none of the makers, none of the crown, none of the dreaming cult had ever done.

She waited for Sefa to finish. Waited through the silence after. Did not reach in and take.

"*Arrhess kessa vennu,*" she said at last. *Then walk beside us, and speak for yourself.*

And Sefa—half-frozen, hollowed out, more frightened than she had ever been—felt something in her chest unclench for the first time since the letter. It wasn't belonging, not yet. It was the doorway to it. She took one step toward the treeline.

Behind her, faint through the storm, someone shouted her name.

* * *

Ivo should have stayed. The word had held them; she had felt it hold them, had felt the terrible obedience of it dragging at the old swordspeaker's bones like an anchor. But Ivo had spent twenty years practicing disobedience against their own order, and apparently a girl's single syllable was not enough to hold what twenty years of discipline had built.

Sefa turned and saw them coming up the pasture through the whiteout—a dark figure, sword belted on though they had never once drawn it in her presence, moving with the stubborn, limping determination of someone who had decided that some promises mattered more than others. Snow plastered their grey hair flat. Their breath tore away in ribbons.

"*Sefa!*"

"Don't!" she screamed back, and the word came out doubled—in the valley tongue and beneath it, involuntarily, in the Old Speech, so that the storm itself seemed to lean aside. The wolves' heads swung toward Ivo. Low sounds started in their chests.

Ivo stopped, ten paces off, hands open and empty, snow whirling between them. And Sefa saw the moment the old swordspeaker understood exactly what she had learned today—saw it land in their face like a blow—because Ivo looked at her standing among the wolves, at the letter's truth made flesh, and mouthed one word that the wind stole away but that Sefa's other ear, her sleeping ear, caught perfectly:

*Vessel.*

They knew she knew. And still they had come. Still they had broken her word to chase her into a blizzard, because that was the kind of foolish, faithful, impossible creature Ivo Quintana was, and Sefa loved them for it, and that was precisely why she could not go back. Every road that led home led through being claimed.

The lead wolf paced forward to stand at Sefa's shoulder, warm as a hearthstone, and looked at Ivo with ancient amber patience.

"*Vennu, arrhess,*" she rumbled. *Choose, little speaker. The snow is deep, and the night is long, and both armies are already moving.*

Out on the ridgeline, faint and wrong against the falling white, Sefa saw torchlight. More than one torch. Coming down.

Ivo saw it too. And slowly, deliberately, in front of the wolves and the storm and the girl they had raised, Ivo Quintana's hand closed around the hilt of the sword they had not drawn in twenty years.
