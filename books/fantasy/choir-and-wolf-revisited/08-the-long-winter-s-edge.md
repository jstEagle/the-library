## 8. The Long Winter's Edge

The shrine at the treeline had no roof, and in winter it needed none. Snow fell straight through the broken arches where a god had once been worshipped, settling on the altar stone like flour sifted over bread. Ivo had passed it a thousand times on the long walk to the high meadows, and a thousand times it had been nothing—a ruin, a landmark, a place to rest the shoulder against cold granite. Now, with the wolves standing in the shadows between the pillars and Halda waiting with her hands folded like a woman at market, the old stones remembered what they were.

They had always known, Ivo thought. It was Ivo who had forgotten. Forgetting was the whole of Correnvale's gift; the valley forgot so its people could sleep. But nothing here slept tonight. The snow came down without wind, in great slow flakes that hissed faintly where they touched the altar, as if the ground itself were murmuring.

Sefa stood at the edge of the shrine with her back to the forest, small and bareheaded, her breath pluming silver. She would not come closer to Halda. She would not go farther from the wolves. She stood in the exact middle of everything, the way she always did now, and watched Ivo with eyes too old for her face—eyes that had learned, in one terrible afternoon, what she was made for.

*Come away*, Ivo wanted to say. *We'll go back to the house. There's honey-bread. We'll wait out the storm.* But the words would have been lies wearing kindness like a coat, and Sefa had learned to smell a lie faster than any witch-hunter. So Ivo said nothing, and walked instead to the altar, and set both palms flat on the freezing stone, and let the silence do what words could not.

"You came," Halda said.

"You asked."

"I asked." The wolf-woman's voice was low and unhurried, roughened by years of northern wind. She was older than she'd seemed among the trees—grey shot through her black braid, lines carved deep around her mouth—and there was something in her face that Ivo recognized with a jolt: the particular patience of someone who has buried many people and expects to bury more. "I didn't expect you to answer. Twenty years ago you would have run."

"Twenty years ago I had somewhere to run *to*."

Halda smiled without warmth. "And now?"

"Now I have a child who talks in her sleep." Ivo turned from the altar. The snow was gathering on their shoulders, melting slowly through wool, and Ivo let it. Cold was honest. "You said you'd tell me why. Why she dreams in the old tongue. Why the wolves crossed three hundred miles of dead country to find her. You'll tell me now, or we all stand here until the spring thaws us out together."

Around the pillars, the wolves shifted. They were not animals—Ivo had understood that since the first night they appeared at the treeline, too large, too watchful, their eyes catching light the way a cat's never quite did—but seeing them up close still raised the hair on the back of the neck. They did not growl. That was worse. They simply waited, the way soldiers wait, which is to say the way people wait who have already decided how this ends and are merely curious about the details.

Halda looked past Ivo, at Sefa. Something moved across her face—not softness exactly, but the ghost of it, the memory of softness in someone who could no longer afford the thing itself.

"The war never ended," she said.

"I know that."

"No." Halda shook her head slowly. "You know it the way a man knows he's dying. Abstractly. In the general way. I mean it the way the wounded know it." She stepped closer, boots crunching frost, and when she spoke again her voice dropped into the register of confession. "Your order believed the war ended at Redford Bridge. Your order died believing it. The crown believes it ended too—that the swordspeakers are ash, that the Old Speech is a story told to frighten children, that the last of the commandments burned with the last of your brothers and sisters." Her eyes flicked to Sefa. "But the ones who survived in the north, we knew better. A war doesn't end because one side stops fighting. It ends when one side stops *wanting*. And neither side has ever stopped wanting."

"Wanting what?"

"The words." Halda spread her hands, and the snow seemed to lean toward her. "Command is power, swordspeaker. Not metaphor. Not poetry. A single true word can stop a heart, turn an army, unmake a wall. Your order hoarded it. The crown feared it. And when the purge came, both sides learned the same lesson: it isn't enough to kill the speakers. The speech itself survives them. It goes... looking. For a throat."

Ivo's stomach turned over slowly, like a stone rolling in dark water. "She's not a foundling."

"She was never born," Halda said quietly. "She was *grown*. Somewhere north of here, in the years after Redford Bridge, there are still makers with the old craft. They shaped a vessel and filled it with every fragment of the speech they could gather—every syllable salvaged from the burning. She isn't a girl who learned the words in her sleep. She's the words, walking around in the shape of a girl, waiting to wake up."

The snow hissed on the altar. Somewhere behind the pillars, a wolf breathed out, long and slow.

"And when she wakes," Ivo said. Her own voice sounded far away, thin, a thread pulled taut across twenty years. "When the voice wakes fully. What happens?"

"She calls," Halda said simply. "That's what the speech is *for*. It calls, and it commands, and things come. Both armies, swordspeaker. The crown will hear her before the crown knows it's hearing—their hunters are already riding, following a resonance they don't have a name for anymore. And our people will come down out of the north, every survivor of the purge and their children and their children's children, because they have waited twenty years for a voice to finish what the bridge started." She met Ivo's eyes. "One way or another, this valley is where it ends. The only question left is who is standing beside her when she speaks."

Ivo looked at Sefa. The girl hadn't moved. Snow had settled on her dark hair and she hadn't brushed it away, and she was watching Halda with an expression Ivo knew intimately, because it had lived on Ivo's own face for two decades: the look of someone listening to their own death sentence read aloud in a language they almost understood.

"I won't let them use her," Ivo said. "Not your people. Not the crown. Not anyone."

"No," Halda agreed softly. "I don't think you will. That's why I'm telling you this instead of taking her tonight. We could have taken her, swordspeaker. Any night since the treeline. My people don't sleep, and yours does." She inclined her head, almost a bow. "I wanted you to choose first. The old oaths said you couldn't. Let's see what you are without them."

It was then that Ivo heard the horses.

* * *

They came up the valley road in a column, torches guttering orange against the blue-black snowfall, and Vess Marrow rode at their head with her cloak thrown back from her shoulders and her sword loose in its scabbard. Twelve soldiers. Enough to take a child. Not enough, perhaps, to hold a shrine full of wolves—but Vess had never been a woman who let arithmetic decide for her.

Ivo stepped out onto the road ahead of the shrine, alone, hands empty and visible, snow whirling between the torchlight and the dark. Behind, half-hidden among the broken arches, the wolves had gone absolutely still. Sefa had vanished into the shadow of the altar stone, and Ivo prayed—to whatever forgotten god the shrine had once held, to the valley itself, to the stubborn dead of Redford Bridge—that she would stay there.

Vess reined in ten paces away. Up close, by torchlight, she looked older than the last time Ivo had seen her at the gate playing the harmless hermit—older and more tired, and underneath the tiredness, something raw that she was holding down with both hands.

"Step aside, Jude." Her voice was quiet. She used the old name deliberately, and Ivo felt it land like a knife laid flat against the skin—not cutting yet. "The captain who visited you in autumn was doing her duty politely. The officer who's here tonight is doing it thoroughly. The crown has confirmed resonance in this valley. A living source. You know what that means."

"I know what it means to *you*," Ivo said. "Tell me what it means to the crown."

Vess's jaw tightened. Around her, the soldiers shifted, spears rising slightly, eyes tracking the darkness beyond the shrine where shapes moved that should not have been so large. "It means she comes to the capital. Alive. They want her trained, not burned—there are voices at court saying a weapon like that ends wars before they start." A muscle jumped in her cheek. "Other voices say a weapon like that starts them. I've argued for her, Ivo. I've spent whatever credit I had arguing for her. This is the result. Bring her out, and I swear on my mother's grave she reaches the capital unharmed."

"And then?"

Vess didn't answer. She didn't have to. Ivo had seen that silence before, twenty years ago, on the faces of good officers carrying orders they hadn't read closely enough—or had read too well.

"They'll use her voice," Ivo said. "Point her at a border, a rebellion, a rival throne, and open her mouth, and let her speak. And everything my order died trying to contain walks out into the world wearing a child's face." Ivo took one step forward, and the nearest soldier flinched, spear leveling. "You trained beside me, Vess. Before the fall. You know what a true word costs. You watched Redford Bridge burn. Are you telling me you believe the crown has learned restraint in twenty years?"

"I believe I keep my oaths," Vess said, but her voice cracked on *oaths*, just slightly, just enough, and Ivo saw it—the fault line running through her, old love and old duty pulling in opposite directions until something had to give.

"Then here's mine," Ivo said, "the only one I have left."

And Ivo walked back toward the shrine—not fast, not slow—past Vess's puzzled frown, past the leveled spears, to the altar where the old sword lay wrapped in oilcloth where it had rested, undrawn, for twenty years. The bundle was stiff with cold. Ivo's fingers shook, not from fear but from something older, something that had waited two decades in the marrow like water in stone, and unwrapped the cloth with the care of someone opening a grave.

The blade came out black and clean. It caught the torchlight and threw it back wrong, the way blades forged for speakers always had—drinking light instead of shining—and along the fuller, faint as breath on glass, the old letters woke. Ivo had not drawn it since Redford Bridge. Had sworn, hand on this very steel, never to draw it again while anything lived that could be harmed by it. The oath broke now, quietly, completely, like ice underfoot.

But the hand that raised it did not point it at Vess. Did not point it at Halda, who had emerged from the arches with her wolves flowing silent around her, or at the soldiers, whose spears wavered as the great grey shapes paced into the torchlight, close enough to touch.

Ivo planted the point in the snow between all of them, both hands on the pommel, head bowed over it like a farmer over seed, and stood.

"I draw it for no one," Ivo said, and the valley heard, and the snow seemed to lean in. "Not for the crown. Not for the north. Not for the dead, who would understand, or the living, who won't. I drew it once for an oath and it bought me twenty years of silence and a valley full of graves I couldn't dig deep enough. So hear me—all of you—" and here Ivo lifted their head and looked at Vess, at Halda, at the small pale shape of Sefa rising from behind the altar with her eyes wide and wet, "—whatever comes next, whatever she becomes, whoever claims her, they go through me first. Not because an order tells me to. Because I *choose* it. That's the only oath left worth the name."

The snow fell. The torches spat. And in the ringing silence, Vess Marrow looked at the black blade standing upright in the drifts—at the old letters waking along the steel, spelling themselves out letter by letter in light that had no business existing in a magic-dead valley—and her face went white as the ground.

Because the letters were rearranging. Writing themselves anew. And Ivo felt it too, rising up through the sword, through the frozen earth, through twenty years of buried silence: the Old Speech, stirring, answering, gathering around the girl at the altar like a tide around a lighthouse.

Sefa opened her mouth—and began, softly, terribly, to speak.
