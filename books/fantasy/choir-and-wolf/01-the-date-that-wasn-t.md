## 1. The Date That Wasn't

I had exactly one night off a season, and I'd decided to spend it making a fool of myself in front of Sable Fenwick.

The Godless Valley didn't have much, but it had the autumn fair every year — three nights of lanterns strung between the granary and the mill, honey wine sold by the ladle, and a fiddler who only knew six songs but played them like each one was a declaration of war. The whole company had turned out in civilian clothes, which mostly meant that I kept noticing how everyone carried themselves like they were about to be ambushed by a turnip cart.

Sable was waiting by the well, wearing a coat with actual buttons.

That's the kind of detail an archivist notices, I suppose. It's the kind of detail a mercenary notices too, because buttons mean someone who doesn't expect to grab a blade and run. Sable Fenwick moved through the world like nothing in it had ever needed grabbing. Ink on their fingers, spectacles slightly crooked, hair doing whatever hair does when its owner has spent the day with their nose in a ledger. They were holding two cups of honey wine and looking pleased with themselves in the way of a person who has already won an argument in their head.

"You're late," they said.

"I'm punctual for drills and early for everything else. You're the one who's early."

"I'm an archivist. Early is where all the good things are." They handed me a cup. "Also where the good seats are. Also where the wine hasn't been watered yet."

I took a sip to have something to do with my mouth. Honey wine at the fair is always sweeter than you remember and stronger than anyone admits, and the first swallow went down warm and sat behind my ribs like a small animal settling.

"So," Sable said, rocking on their heels. "This is courting, yes? I want to be sure we're using the same word for it. In the municipal records there are eleven distinct terms for pre-contractual romantic arrangements and I'd hate for us to be operating under different definitions."

"Sable."

"Yes?"

"Drink your wine."

They drank their wine. Around us the fair did what the fair did: kids shrieking through the crowd playing knife-tag with wooden knives because this is a mercenary town and even our games come with edge training; old Sergeant Bray losing money at the ring-toss and pretending not to care; somebody's dog stealing an entire roast ear of corn with the dignity of a professional. Lantern light turned everything the color of good ale. Above the valley walls the sky was going dark the way it always does here — fast, total, no lingering — because the valley doesn't hold light any better than it holds anything else.

That's the thing people from outside never understand about the Godless Valley. Magic doesn't work here. Hasn't for a hundred years, since before the first settler put up the first fence. Blessings don't take, curses slide off, charms go dead as river stones. People came here to get away from all of it — gods, witches, prophecy, the whole screaming circus of the unseen. My mother came for that reason. Her mother before her. I grew up thinking of the silence overhead the way fish think of water.

Then at nineteen I took an oath anyway. Not to anything listening — nothing listens here — but to the memory of something that used to. The Sworn of the Quiet Choir, an order older than the valley, older than the Silence, keeping faith with a god who stopped answering a century back. We keep the hymns. We keep the forms. We train our bodies because the god can't strengthen our souls. There's a brand on my left forearm, a small spiral like a fern tip uncurling, and it has been cold and mute since the day it was inked, the way every brand in the order has been cold and mute for four generations. An oath to a silent god is just a promise you make to yourself with extra steps.

But it's mine. That's the part I've never known how to explain to anyone, least of all the person currently watching me drink honey wine like I was a puzzle worth solving.

"You're staring," I said.

"I'm observing. Staring is undisciplined." Sable pushed their spectacles up. "You look nice tonight. I wanted to say that earlier but you arrived mid-sentence and I lost the thread, and then I got a new thread about wine terminology, and—"

"You look nice too," I said. "Buttons and everything."

They looked down at their coat, delighted, like they'd forgotten the buttons and were being reminded of a gift. "Aldous in the records office says courting couples should exchange compliments at intervals or the arrangement stalls. He's been married four times so his sample size is—"

"Sable. You're doing the thing."

"The thing where I talk when nervous? Yes. I'm aware. I've catalogued it." They took a breath, let it out. "The fiddler's playing the third song. I believe that one's danceable."

It was danceable the way a bar fight is negotiable — technically, if you committed. And I should say here, plainly, since I'll have cause to doubt everything else later: that evening was good. Genuinely, cleanly good. We danced badly among people dancing worse. Sable counted steps under their breath in the manner of someone indexing them. I stepped on their foot twice and apologized once, which by fair standards was chivalrous. We bought candied apples neither of us finished. We watched Bray finally win at ring-toss and cheer like he'd taken a city. The lanterns burned low and were replaced, and the night got that soft late-fair feeling where the noise turns into a kind of warmth you can lean against.

I remember thinking: I would like a great many more of these. Nights, I mean. With this person. I even got as far as composing the sentence, which for me is roughly equivalent to other people's grand declarations.

Which is, of course, when Sable ruined everything. Lovingly. With a book.

* * *

"Here," they said, after the fourth song, hauling a satchel around from behind their back with the guilty energy of someone presenting either flowers or a stolen relic. "I brought you a present. Sort of. Present-adjacent."

"It's a book."

"It's *always* a book with me, yes, I know, Aldous warned—" They caught my expression and laughed, and I felt that laugh somewhere inconvenient. "All right. Context. We finished the Halloway estate catalogue last week — the family that died out, left the manor and everything in it to the archive. Fourteen hundred volumes, most of them tax ledgers, some of them genuinely terrible poetry. And this." They drew out a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, handled it like it might bite, which should have told me something even then. "Bound in vellum I can't identify. No author. No title. The cataloguing convention is to date items by internal evidence, so — look at the flyleaf."

They opened it on the flat top of the well and turned it toward the nearest lantern.

The paper was heavy, cream-colored, oddly smooth. The hand that covered the flyleaf was small and precise, the kind of script that costs money to learn:

*Set down in the year 1001, being the hundredth year of the Silence, that what was foreseen may not find us unprepared.*

"One thousand and one," Sable said softly. "That's — Vesper, that's exactly a hundred years ago. A book written the very year the god went quiet, found in a cellar forty miles from where the Silence begins. Do you understand how unlikely—"

"You brought a century-old mystery book to a fair."

"You're missing the best part." Their eyes had gone bright, that specific archival fever I'd learned to find charming right up until it wasn't. "It's a prophecy cycle. Or claims to be. Most of it's the usual fog — kings, winters, a wolf, choirs, the standard symbolic furniture. But some passages are *specific*, Vesper. Names. Dates. And the handwriting changes halfway through, which suggests multiple contributors over time, except the additions are in inks consistent with the original binding period, which shouldn't be possible unless—"

"Sable."

"—unless someone wrote the whole thing at once and *pretended* it accumulated, which is its own fascinating problem, but here, here, read this passage, tell me what you see—"

They turned pages with two fingers, quick and reverent, and stopped near the middle of the book, angling it to the light.

I read it. I read it twice, because the first time my eyes slid off it the way eyes slide off a lie.

*In the hundredth year, in the season of falling leaves, the wolf shall walk abroad unaware,* the page said. *Amberdale, sworn and unwarning, whose brand sleeps; and Fenwick, reader of the dead, who opens the door that was shut. Together they shall stand at the choir door, and the crown shall sing through the throat of a stolen king, and the winter that eats shall begin its long meal. What the wolf breaks, the reader mends. What the reader writes, the wolf keeps.*

My name was on the page.

Not *a* name like mine. Not Amberdale the family, though we've farmed this valley since before the Silence and the surname is old. *Vesper.* And *Fenwick.* Two surnames that exist, as far as I knew, in exactly one place each: standing at a well in a lantern-lit square, holding half a cup of cold honey wine.

"Sable," I said. My voice came out level, which I was grateful for. Drill does that; your body keeps working while the rest of you reorganizes. "When did you catalogue this?"

"Tuesday."

"And when did you first read this passage?"

"Tuesday. I read it, and I checked the parish registers, and I checked the muster rolls, and I checked the valley census going back sixty years, and I told myself it was coincidence — a common enough name paired with a common enough name, transcribed wrong, misread, something." They were talking faster now, the words coming out in the flat careful cadence of someone who has rehearsed disbelief until it wore thin. "And then I couldn't stop thinking about it all week, and tonight seemed — I thought if I showed you and we laughed about it, it would—" They stopped. Pushed their spectacles up. Their hand wasn't quite steady. "It wouldn't go away, Vesper. All week it wouldn't go away."

Around us the fair went on. The fiddler scraped into the fifth song. Somewhere a kid screamed with joy. Bray was arguing with the ring-toss man. The lanterns burned, ordinary and orange and utterly indifferent, and I stood there with a century-old book under my eyes and my own name staring up at me like it had been waiting.

*Whose brand sleeps.* Nobody outside the Sworn knows I carry a brand. Nobody in the valley knows the order still exists — we keep it that way deliberately, four generations of deliberate quiet. And the phrase itself, *sworn and unwarning* —

"Read me the rest of the page," I said.

"There isn't much more." Sable turned the leaf. "It moves to another verse. 'The reader opens the door that was shut' — that's clearly this passage continued — and then there's a marginal note, same hand, smaller." They leaned close, tracing above the line without touching it. "'The book is patient. Let no one read aloud beneath an open sky.'"

We both looked up. At the same moment, without meaning to, without deciding anything, we looked past the lanterns, past the mill roof, up at the sky over the valley.

It was fully dark. It was open. It was the same empty, magic-dead, perfectly ordinary sky I'd lived under my whole life, and for the first time in twenty-six years it looked less like home and more like a held breath.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. This was the Godless Valley. Nothing had happened here for a hundred years; it was practically our civic religion.

I closed the book myself — gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone's sleeping — and Sable wrapped it back in the oilcloth, and for a while we just stood at the well not saying anything, shoulder to shoulder, while the fiddler played and the fair ignored us, which is one of the better things fairs do.

"Coincidence," Sable offered finally, without conviction.

"Two names. Out of a whole century."

"Names recur. I told you, I checked the—"

"*Sworn and unwarning,* Sable. Whose brand *sleeps.*"

They were quiet. Then, in a smaller voice: "I wanted tonight to be about honey wine."

"I know." I looked at them — the crooked spectacles, the ink stains, the buttoned coat, the face I'd spent three seasons of one-night-off-per-season trying to work up to. Something in my chest had gone hard and cold and mercenary, the part of me that maps exits, and I hated it for arriving at the fair. "Here's what we're going to do. You're going to take that book home. You're going to lock it in the deepest drawer you have. Tomorrow, in daylight, with coffee, we'll look at it again and we'll find the mundane explanation, because there's always a mundane explanation, and then — " I made myself smile. "Then maybe the sixth song. I hear the fiddler's got a seventh he's been sitting on."

Sable looked at me for a long moment. Whatever they saw in my face made them reach out and take my hand — the sword-callused one, not the branded one, and I noted even then that they'd chosen it deliberately, and loved them a little for it.

"All right," they said. "Tomorrow. Daylight. Coffee."

They tucked the book into the satchel, and we turned back toward the lanterns and the noise, and I was three steps along and almost believing my own exit-mapping when Sable stopped dead beside me and said, in the precise, toneless voice of a person reading their own obituary:

"Vesper. The flyleaf. Read me the flyleaf again."

"It said written in the year 1001, hundredth year of the—"

"After that. The line underneath. I never read it aloud because it seemed like decoration."

I hadn't seen a line underneath. Neither, clearly, had they, ten minutes ago, or they'd have mentioned it. But when we set the satchel down on the well again and unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that had gone clumsy, and turned to the front by lantern-light, there it was, in that same small precise hand, directly below the date, fresh as morning:

*Begun in the year 1101, in the season of falling leaves, at a fair, beside a well, by two who thought they were only reading.*

The ink glistened.

It was not dry.

Behind us, unnoticed by anyone, the fiddler's sixth song wound down, and the whole square fell briefly, perfectly, silent — and in that silence, from somewhere above the valley walls, came a sound I had never heard in the Godless Valley in my life.

Something answering.
