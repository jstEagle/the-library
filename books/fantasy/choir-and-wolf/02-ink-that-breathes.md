## 2. Ink That Breathes

The book was warm.

That should have been my first clue. Books aren't warm. Books are the temperature of the room they've been sitting in for however many years, which in the Amberdale valley archive meant the temperature of a cellar with opinions about damp. But when I pulled *The Choir Unbroken* off the cart where I'd left it that morning — third shelf, between a tax ledger from the founding era and someone's doomed attempt to catalogue folk songs by key signature — it was warm as a sleeping animal, and it hummed against my palms like a struck bell that hadn't quite finished ringing.

"Sable," Vesper said behind me. "You've got your face on."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a face you make when you're about to do something archivists shouldn't do." They were leaning against the doorframe of the archive annex, still dressed from the fair, honey wine on their breath and a paper cone of sugared chestnuts balanced in one hand like a lantern. The lamplight caught the oath-brand at their throat — the small grey spiral every Sworn carried, dormant now for so long most people mistook it for a birthmark. "You made it twice tonight. Once when you won the ring toss, once when you looked at me over the rim of your cup."

"That second one was just looking."

"It was scheming-looking." They came in anyway, because Vesper Amberdale has never once in their life walked away from me doing something unwise. That's either devotion or poor judgment, and I've decided not to investigate which. "Show me."

I set the book on the reading table and opened it to where I'd stopped that afternoon. The page was dated a hundred years past — before the Silence, before the Godless Valley was godless, before either of our grandparents had learned to walk. And there, in ink that had no business knowing anything, in a hand that looped and slanted like it was in a hurry:

*And the wolf at the choir door shall come bearing the reader, who opens what was sealed, and together—*

"Vesper Amberdale," I read aloud, because I couldn't help myself, because some part of me had been waiting all evening to say it in front of them. "*Vesper Amberdale* shall stand at the threshold of the hollow crown."

They went very still. Vesper does stillness better than anyone I've ever met — it's the mercenary in them, the part that understands that movement draws eyes — but this wasn't trained stillness. This was the stillness of an animal hearing something wrong in the brush.

"That's not funny," they said.

"I'm not laughing."

"And this?" They touched the next line with one finger, careful, the way they handle things they might need to break quickly. Below the prophecy, in smaller script, cramped into the margin like an afterthought or an apology: *the reader follows the wolf down, and the wolf cannot go back without them.*

"My name's not written here."

"Not yet." Vesper's jaw tightened. "Sable. It says *not yet.*"

I want to be honest about what I felt in that moment, because later — after everything — people assumed I must have been frightened too, that we were both swept up helpless in something vast. And yes, eventually, fear arrived. But in that first moment, standing in a cold archive with wine on my own breath and a hundred-year-old book breathing under my hands, what I felt was *vindication*. Ten years of cataloguing other people's leavings. Ten years of being told the valley's archives were a dead end, a backwater, a place where history came to stop moving. And now a book that knew Vesper's name. A book that knew *me*, or would.

"The ink's fresh," I said, and heard my own voice do the thing it does when I'm excited, going quick and bright and slightly ahead of my brain. "Look — the descenders haven't fully dried into the fiber. Whoever wrote this used a high-gum iron gall, see the sheen? That's a recent batch. Vesper, this page is *new*. Someone wrote a prophecy about us last week."

"Or," said Vesper, "it wrote itself last week, which is worse."

"Or the book is older than the valley and has been waiting for us specifically, which is *best*."

"Sable Fenwick." They said my full name the way they say it at the end of arguments they're losing, which is to say, with love and exasperation in equal parts. "It is the night of the autumn fair. We have had exactly one evening off in four months. I bought you chestnuts. I was going to walk you home the long way past the orchards. Can the century-old doom book wait until morning?"

And I should have said yes. Any reasonable person says yes. But I'd already turned back to the page, and the light had shifted — I noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather through a window — and the letters had begun to move. Not smearing. Not blurring. *Reading themselves.* Each word darkened as my eye passed over it, then faded behind me, like footprints filling with snow.

"*Together they shall unmake what the crown has made,*" I read, faster now, because the text was pulling, because mid-sentence felt suddenly like a dangerous place to stop. "*And the winter shall learn the shape of spring again, and the silent one shall—*"

The room tilted.

Not the room. Everything. The floor dropped out from under the world and the book didn't fall because the book was the floor now, the book was the air, the book was everywhere, wet-ink smell and lamplight gone amber and Vesper shouting my name from very far away and also right inside my ear —

Mid-sentence. We fell mid-sentence. I've thought about that a great deal since. Whatever took us didn't wait for the page to finish. It needed the wolf and the reader, and it needed them *now*, and grammar could sort itself out afterward.

The last thing I saw of home was Vesper's hand closing around mine, hard enough to hurt, and the chestnuts scattering across the floorboards like small brown stars.

* * *

Snow.

That was the first fact. Snow falling fat and slow through a violet dusk, catching in my eyelashes, hissing faintly where it landed on bare skin. Snow deep on rooftops, snow curtaining the alleys, snow packed gray-brown along a cobbled street that ran downhill toward — I squinted, blinking flakes away — walls. High black walls crowned with towers, and above the tallest tower a pennant snapping in a wind that tasted of iron and woodsmoke.

The second fact: we were standing in the middle of that street, in plain view, in clothes that fit too well.

I looked down at myself. Gone were the fair-day boots and Sable's-one-good-coat. In their place: a long scholar's robe of deep green wool, lined with fox fur, buttoned to the throat, cut close through the shoulders in a way that suggested it had been measured for me by someone patient. My satchel hung at my hip — my actual satchel, the one with the cracked strap, except the strap wasn't cracked anymore and the bag was heavier than I'd ever known it, stuffed with tools I didn't recognize. On my hands: fingerless gloves, ink-stained at the fingertips. Ink-stained. As if I'd been wearing these clothes for years.

Beside me, Vesper stood in armor.

Not parade armor — traveling mail, dull and oiled, a sword at their hip with a worn grip shaped to a hand that was unmistakably theirs. Their coat bore a sigil I'd only seen in woodcuts in the oldest folios: a closed eye inside a spiral. The mark of the Sworn of the Quiet Choir, from before the Silence, from when the order had a capital chapter-house and a hundred blades instead of a hunted handful hiding in the hills.

"Vesper," I said. My breath fogged. "Don't panic."

"I'm not panicking." Their voice was flat and controlled, which for Vesper is the loudest panic there is. "I'm doing threat assessment. There's a difference." They turned a slow circle, taking in the street, the walls, the snow. "This is the capital."

"What?"

"This street. The processional way. It runs to the palace gates." They pointed downhill with a gauntleted hand. "I've never been here, but Mother Cassia drilled us on the old maps until we could draw them blindfolded. That's the palace. That's—" they hesitated, and I watched certainty arrive on their face like a bruise, "—that's the year it happened. The snow, the lockdown, the last winter before the Silence. We're a hundred years from home, Sable. Give or take."

A door banged open somewhere above us. I flinched; Vesper didn't, but their hand found their sword grip, easy as breathing.

An old woman leaned out of a third-floor window, shaking out a rug, and looked straight down at us. For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then her face broke open into something between relief and reverence.

"There you are!" she called down. "Blessed evening to you! The whole quarter's been asking — the Sworn blade and the Reader, come at last! My mother waited her whole life to see the pair the Choir sang about!" She pressed a fist to her heart, to the spot where a brand would sit if she'd had one. "Go well, wolf. Go well, keeper of pages. The city needs you."

She withdrew before either of us could answer. The window shut. Somewhere down the block, another door opened, and voices — excited, hushed, urgent — began to carry through the falling snow.

"Wolf," Vesper repeated. Their voice had gone strange.

"'The wolf at the choir door,'" I quoted. My mouth was dry. "'Shall come bearing the reader.'"

"They *know us*." Vesper's hand hadn't left the sword. "A hundred years ago, in a city I've never stood in, strangers know my face. Sable, that woman wasn't surprised. She was *waiting*." They finally turned to look at me, and snow had gathered in their hair, and underneath the flat mercenary calm their eyes were doing arithmetic they hated. "How long has the book known?"

"I don't know."

"How long have *you* known?"

I wanted to lie. I want to say, for the record, in whatever version of this story gets told — I wanted to, and I didn't. "Since the fair. Since you won the ring toss and I saw your name in the margins while you weren't looking. I was going to tell you. I was going to show you gently, over wine, make it a curiosity—"

"You brought me to a cursed book on purpose."

"I brought you to a *prophecy* on purpose, and it turned out to be a door." I spread my gloved hands, and the snow kept falling between us, and somewhere a church bell began to toll — six strokes, deep and cracked, like the bell itself had a cold. "Vesper. Look around. Look at *us*. Whatever this is, it's not random. It fitted us for clothes. It gave me a satchel of tools. It put a hundred-year-old sword in your hand and a sigil on your coat. Somebody — something — prepared for us. Down to the stitching."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you feel *aimed*." I stepped closer, close enough that our breath mingled white in the narrow space between us. "There's a difference. Random means nothing we do matters. Aimed means everything we do matters enormously. As an archivist, I promise you: aimed is better."

For a long moment Vesper just looked at me. Then, slowly, they laughed — one short, disbelieving breath of a laugh, fogging the air — and shook snow out of their hair like a dog coming out of a river.

"Four months I've waited for one evening off," they said. "One fair. One dance. You owe me a dance, Fenwick. Across a hundred years, across whatever this is — you owe me."

"Noted. Witnessed. I'll put it in the ledger." I dared a smile. "First, though — do you feel that?"

They went still again. Because I did feel it, had been feeling it since the moment we landed and had mistaken it for the cold: a pressure behind my sternum, rhythmic, vast, like standing near a cathedral organ with all its stops drawn. And beside me, Vesper's hand had drifted up without their seeming to notice, to the grey spiral at their throat.

Their brand — dormant their whole life, dormant a hundred years in the valley's magic-dead air — was glowing.

Faintly. Faint as embers under ash. But glowing, pulsing, in time with the pressure I felt in my own chest, and Vesper was staring down at it with an expression I had never seen on them before and hope never to see again: the look of a person whose own body has become evidence.

"It's awake," they whispered. "Sable, it hasn't done that since — it's never done that. The god's been silent my entire life. The whole order's been praying into a well with no bottom." They looked up at me, and the snow kept falling, and down the street more doors were opening, faces gathering in the blue dusk, murmuring *wolf, wolf, the wolf has come.* "What did we just walk into?"

Ahead of us, downhill, the palace walls rose black against the violet sky. And as I watched — as we both watched — light kindled in the highest tower, one window, warm gold against the dark, and threading down through the wind and snow came sound. Distant, thin, almost lost. Singing.

A hymn. I knew the melody from a dozen valley lullabies, from funeral rites, from the few surviving pages of the Quiet Choir's songbook. A hymn to the Silent One.

But sung wrong. Every phrase inverted, every rising line bent downward, like a prayer played backward through a dying man's teeth.

Vesper heard it too. I watched the recognition move through them like a shudder through a rope.

"We walk into that," they said quietly. Not a question. Their hand fell from the brand to the sword, and they started downhill, and I followed, because following Vesper Amberdale into terrible ideas has always been, apparently, my calling.

Behind us, in the snow where we'd landed, half-buried already, lay a single sheet of paper neither of us had carried. When I think of it now — and I think of it often — I can still recite what it said, in that looping, hurried hand:

*The reader follows the wolf down. The wolf cannot go back without them. Neither can the world.*

The ink was still wet when the snow covered it.
