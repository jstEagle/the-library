## 3. A City Holding Its Breath

The city of Corvale did not sleep so much as it held its breath.

Vesper noticed it the second morning after they fell out of a book and into a century that wasn't theirs. They'd woken in the narrow room above the cooper's shop — the one the innkeeper's wife had pressed on them with the certainty of someone handing keys to old friends — and lain still for a long while, listening. In the Godless Valley, mornings had sound. Hammers, dogs, Sable muttering over a ledger, the drill-yard calling cadence. Here there was only a hush so complete it had texture, like wool packed into the ears.

Then, beneath the hush: footsteps. Hundreds of them, all moving at the same careful pace.

They dressed in the clothes that had come with them — the book's idea of them, apparently, down to the worn left knee of the trousers and the sword belt that sat exactly where Vesper's hips wanted it — and went down into the street to see what a city looked like when it was afraid of its own shadow.

What it looked like was this: shutters half-closed even at midmorning. Market stalls open but sparse, sellers standing behind their goods like men behind barricades. Guards at every corner in armor the color of old milk, pale plate gone grey at the joints, faces hidden behind visors that showed nothing but a slit of dark. And everywhere, chalked on doorposts, on well-sides, on the cobbles themselves where rain hadn't yet eaten it away: a spiral. A closed spiral, wound inward, ending in a point.

Sable would have known what it meant. Sable knew what everything meant; it was the most infuriating and most comforting thing about them. But Sable had spent the morning in the great library, having talked their way past three doors Vesper would have had to kill their way past, and so Vesper walked alone through a capital city that flinched from its own king, and tried to remember how homesickness was supposed to feel when home was a hundred years and a closed valley away.

It felt like hunger with nowhere to put the food.

* * *

"You're staring," said the woman at the pie stall, not unkindly. She was sixty if she was a day, flour to the elbows, eyes that had done arithmetic on every customer she'd ever served. "You've been staring at my pies for a quarter hour and you haven't bought one."

"I'm deciding."

"Nobody decides that long over mutton pie." She leaned across her counter and lowered her voice, though the street was empty enough that lowering it seemed almost ceremonial. "You're not from here."

"Is it that obvious?"

"You walk like the guards owe you money." She wrapped a pie without being asked and pushed it across. "Two coppers. And you can pay me back in gossip. Where'd you come from, that you don't know better than to stand in the open gawking?"

Vesper paid. The pie was excellent, which felt like an ambush. "We came up from the south," they said, which was true in a way that would take too long to explain. "Is there a reason not to stand in the open?"

The woman's eyes went, briefly, to the palace hill — visible over the rooftops even from here, a black crown of walls against a sky the color of dishwater — and then back, quick, like touching a stove. "Curfew's at dusk now. Was at dark, before the Frost Edict. And you don't sing in the streets after dark." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *don't put your hand in the fire*, a rule so settled it had stopped needing reasons. "And if you hear singing, you get inside and you bar the door, and you don't listen."

"Hear singing from where?"

She looked at Vesper the way people look at children who ask why the sky is up. "From the palace, love. From the king."

"The king sings?"

"He hasn't been seen since the Feast of Lanterns. Five months." She began stacking empty trays with sharp little movements, as if tidiness could tidy the conversation too. "But some nights — clear nights, cold ones — you can hear him. Through the walls, through everything. Singing." Her hands stilled. "My mother sang that song to me when I was small. A lullaby, it was. Old words. Nobody knows them anymore." She resumed stacking, faster. "Only he sings it wrong now. Like a song played backward. Like something learned by a man who never had a mother to teach him gentle."

"What happens to people who listen?"

"No one knows." She met Vesper's eyes at last, and there was something under the fear that took Vesper a moment to name, because they'd seen it so rarely outside the drill-yard: it was defiance, banked low but burning. "That's the worst of it. The Pale Guard takes them — anyone out after curfew, anyone caught listening, anyone who asks questions like yours. And no one taken has ever come back to say." She jerked her chin at the chalked spiral on the wall behind her stall. "You see that mark? That's the Church's new sign. The Regent-Cardinal says it wards off the winter. Says the Silence brought the frost, and the Spiral holds it back." Her mouth twisted. "He says a lot of things."

"And you don't believe him."

"I believe winter comes early this year, and rivers freeze while they're still running, and birds fall out of the sky dead with no mark on them." She pushed a second pie across the counter. "On the house. You look like you'll be needing your strength, and I look like I'll be needing someone to remember I was kind, when whatever's coming gets here."

Vesper took the pie. It was the first gift they'd been given in a century that wasn't theirs, and it sat warm in their hand like a coal.

"Thank you," they said. "What's your name? For remembering."

"Maren." She almost smiled. "Maren Ostwick. Pies and treason, that's me."

* * *

The ache started at dusk.

Vesper was crossing the Tanner's Bridge when it came — not sudden, but arriving the way weather arrives, a pressure change you feel in your teeth before you know to call it a storm. Their left forearm, below the skin, where the oath-brand lived: a slow, deep throb, like a second heartbeat waking up after a hundred years of pretending to be a scar.

They stopped in the middle of the bridge and gripped the rail.

The brand had been silent since before Vesper could remember anything else. Every Sworn child got it at seven years old, inked in ash and blessed water, a small coiled mark over the pulse that warmed once in a lifetime — the day the god touched you, or so Mother Cassia used to say, back when the order still had mothers and cassias and more than a handful of members. Vesper's had warmed once, at nine years old, during a fever that should have killed them. They'd woken healed and marked forever with the knowledge that something vast had leaned close, considered them, and moved on.

Then the god went silent — had gone silent a century ago, Vesper corrected themself, the timeline still catching in their throat like a fishbone — and the brand had been just ink ever since. Dead weight. A souvenir of a god who had nothing left to say.

Now it beat. Slow and hot and unmistakable, in time with something Vesper could not yet hear.

They turned toward the palace hill without deciding to. Their feet made the decision the way trained feet do, carrying the body toward trouble with the calm of long habit. The brand pulsed harder with each step, and Vesper let it lead, up streets growing emptier as the light died, past doors already barred at dusk, past a patrol of the Pale Guard whose visored heads tracked them with the smooth synchronized turn of weathervanes finding wind.

The guard didn't stop them. That was its own kind of wrong. Vesper walked within ten feet of four armed soldiers of a lockdown regime, and they parted around them like water around a stone, and one of them — Vesper would have sworn it, later — inclined its head. As if they were expected. As if the spiral on the walls and the ache in their arm were two ends of the same sentence, and everyone but Vesper had already read it.

They turned down a side street instead, heart hammering, and found a gap between a chandler's shop and the city wall where the shadows pooled deep, and pressed their back to cold stone, and breathed.

The brand beat on. Patient. Insistent. Pointing.

Up the hill, the palace stood black against the last grey light, and Vesper stared at it and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when you are far from home and cannot go back, that the thing pulling at their arm was the same thing that had pulled them out of their own century. The book had opened a door. Something on the other side had reached through and taken hold.

Not the prophecy. Prophecies were paper. This was older than paper. This was the tug of a hook set long ago, in a fevered child's arm, by something that had then gone quiet for a hundred years — quiet the way a man goes quiet when he's holding his breath underwater.

*Come on, then,* Vesper thought at it, at the hill, at whatever waited up there wearing a king. *Say what you want.*

The brand pulsed once, hard, and went still — not silent, Vesper realized, but satisfied. It had gotten what it wanted. It had made them look.

* * *

Sable found them at the cooper's shop after full dark, letting themselves in with the key the innkeeper's wife had given them and the total absence of caution that Vesper had long ago stopped trying to correct. They were flushed with library-joy, arms full of borrowed folios, spectacles fogged from the cold walk.

"You will not believe what the restricted collection—" They stopped. Set the folios down. Looked at Vesper properly, the way they did when the archivist in them gave way to the person who had learned to read Vesper's silences the way other people read print. "What happened?"

"How do you know something happened?"

"Because you're sitting in the dark eating cold pie with your boots on, and you only do that after you've decided to worry about something alone, which we agreed — after the book, after everything — we weren't going to do anymore." Sable knelt, took Vesper's face in both hands, thumbs cool against their jaw. "Talk."

So Vesper told it: Maren and her pies. The curfew and the Frost Edict. The spiral chalked on every wall. The king unseen for five months, heard only at night, singing a lullaby backward. And the brand — they pushed up their sleeve and held their forearm to the candle, and Sable bent close, breath catching.

"It's warm," Sable whispered. "It's — Vesper, it's *glowing*. Barely, but—"

"A hundred years it's been dead ink. Tonight it woke up and dragged me halfway across the city by the arm." Vesper pulled the sleeve down. "Toward the palace."

Sable sat back on their heels. Vesper watched them do the thing they did — the rapid eye-flicker, the invisible page-turning, the whole archive of their mind riffling itself — and braced for the excitement, because Sable's excitement was bottomless and often badly timed.

It came, but quieter than usual. "A king who hasn't been seen in five months. Singing an inverted hymn. Your oath-brand waking after a century of silence, pointing at him like a compass needle." Sable removed their spectacles and polished them on their sleeve, which was what they did instead of saying *I'm frightened*. "In the chronicles I read today — the ones they let me read — there's a gap. Everything about the year the god fell silent is either missing or so bland it reads like it was written by someone being watched. Someone razored pages out of the official record, Vesper. Centuries-old books, cut with a blade, carefully. You don't do that to history unless the history is dangerous."

"To whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Sable put the spectacles back on, and behind the glass their eyes had gone from frightened to lit, which was the most Sable thing about them: fear and fascination sharing one flame. "Tomorrow I'm going back. There's a sub-level the head librarian pretended didn't exist until I mentioned the word *prophecy*, and then pretended very hard existed while looking at me like I'd asked to see her firstborn's sins. Whatever was cut out of the public record is probably down there." They hesitated. "And you'll be—"

"Going up the hill," Vesper said.

"Alone?"

"For now. Two strangers asking about the king draws less notice than one stranger asking and one stranger sneaking." Vesper reached out and straightened Sable's crooked collar, an old habit, a small tenderness. "Besides. If your sub-level librarian doesn't eat you, meet me at Maren's stall at dusk. Pie stall, market row. Even a century away, I know where to find you."

Sable covered Vesper's hand with their own and held it there a moment. "Be careful. The guards—"

"Let me worry about the guards. You worry about the razor-cuts." Vesper managed a grin, and it mostly worked. "We fell into a prophecy, Sable. Let's at least be thorough about reading it."

After Sable slept, Vesper sat by the window with the candle out, watching the palace hill, and the brand lay quiet under their sleeve like a sleeping animal — but not, they understood now, a dead one. Somewhere up there in the dark, something was singing to a city that had learned not to listen. And somewhere in the silence between the notes, a god who had chosen quiet as a warning was waiting for someone stubborn enough to finally hear it.

Vesper had sworn an oath at seven years old to a voice that had never spoken to them directly in their entire life.

Tomorrow, they thought, they'd climb the hill and find out why it had started whispering now.

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it did now, the Frost Edict's heralds walking the streets ringing bells and crying *inside, all inside, by order of the Regent.* Vesper was already at Maren's stall when the last bell faded, leaning against the counter with two coppers ready, watching the palace hill go gold, then rose, then black.

Maren saw them coming and had the pie wrapped before they arrived. "Back again. Told you you'd need your strength." Then her eyes flicked past Vesper's shoulder and her flour-dusted hands went still. "Friend of yours?"

Vesper turned. At the mouth of the market row, where the lamplight gave out, stood a figure in a hooded cloak the grey of old ash — and flanking the alley mouths on either side, four more, pale armor dulled to shadow, visors fixed on the pie stall with the patience of things that had all night.

The brand under Vesper's sleeve woke like a struck match.

"Not yet," Vesper said quietly, to Maren, to the hill, to whoever was watching. "But I think it's about to be."
