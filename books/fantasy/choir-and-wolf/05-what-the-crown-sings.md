## 5. What the Crown Sings

The brand woke Vesper an hour past midnight.

Not the old ache — the brand hadn't done anything but sit under their skin like a scar for a hundred years' worth of inherited silence. This was different. This was a low, pulling heat, like a hand closing around their wrist from the inside.

They sat up in the narrow room above the cooper's shop that Sable had found them — found *them*, which was still a sentence Vesper couldn't finish without laughing at how it sounded — and pressed two fingers to the mark on their inner forearm. The Quiet Choir's sigil: three vertical lines crossed by a single horizontal stroke, like a mouth closed over song. It had been inked into them at seventeen by Mother Ivenna's own hand, back when the order still had mothers and novices and a god who answered.

It had never hurt before. It had never done anything before.

Now it pulled, faint but insistent, toward the north wall of the city. Toward the palace.

"No," Vesper told it, out loud, the way they'd talk to a horse about to do something stupid.

The brand pulled harder.

* * *

They'd been in this century — this *place*, this snowbound capital wearing clothes that fit too well — six days now, and Vesper had learned exactly three things worth learning.

One: the winter was wrong. Not cold-wrong, not early-winter-wrong, but wrong the way a body is wrong when something is eating it from inside. The river had frozen mid-current on their second night, whole eddies locked mid-swirl like insects in amber. The birds came down out of the grey sky dead, unmarked, as if they'd simply been switched off. The old woman who sold chestnuts near the Tanner's Gate crossed herself twice whenever she said the word *palace*.

Two: nobody had seen King Aldric in four months. The king's voice, yes. The king's singing, every night, through the palace walls, thin and carrying across the frozen rooftops like smoke from a fire no one could find. But the king himself — nothing. The Regent-Cardinal Ossric ruled in his name, pale-faced and soft-voiced, and the city obeyed because the alternative was thinking too hard about what was behind those walls.

Three: Sable Fenwick could find trouble in a library faster than Vesper could find it in a tavern, and Vesper had once started a riot in a tavern by accident.

Sable was asleep across the room — or pretending to be, curled on the pallet with one arm flung over their eyes, a stub of candle burned down beside a stack of notes they'd sworn they weren't taking anymore. Six days, and they'd already filled a notebook with observations about a century they'd fallen into through the pages of a book. Six days, and Vesper had already stopped being surprised by anything except how much they didn't want Sable walking into whatever the brand wanted from them tonight.

Vesper dressed in the dark. Boots, coat, the knife at the hip because habits were the only luggage they trusted. At the door they paused, looked back at the shape of Sable breathing slow under the blanket, and made themselves leave anyway. If the brand was going to drag them somewhere, it could drag them alone. Whatever was pulling at the oath-marks of the Sworn, it wasn't going to use the person Vesper loved as bait if Vesper could help it.

The door clicked shut behind them. On the other side of the room, very quietly, Sable opened one eye.

* * *

The city at night was a held breath.

Snow fell without wind, straight down, in fat unhurried flakes that landed without sound. Vesper moved through streets where every window was shuttered and every door was barred, past shrines where candles had guttered out and no one relit them, and felt the brand pull steadily stronger with each block north. By the time they reached the Street of Bells — empty, its bell-tower dark, because apparently even bells were afraid — the heat under their skin had become a rope.

And then the singing began.

It came up out of the palace like water rising through stone. Vesper stopped in the middle of the street, breath fogging, and listened, and the hair rose along both arms beneath the coat.

Because they knew the song.

Every child raised Sworn knew the hymns of the Quiet One — not sung, never sung aloud, that was the whole point of the Quiet Choir; the hymns were *breathed*, mouthed in silence, a devotion made of held breath. Vesper had spent ten years of childhood mouthing the Lamentation of the Falling Snow every dusk, hands cupped, lips shaping words without sound, feeling the god's attention settle over the valley like a hand on the head. They knew every syllable. They knew where the melody bent, where the long note broke into three short ones, where the singer was meant to fall silent and let the silence itself be the offering.

What drifted from the palace was the Lamentation of the Falling Snow.

Sung backwards.

Not reversed note-for-note — subtler than that, crueler than that. The syllables were inverted, each phrase turned inside out so the meaning curdled while the melody stayed sweet. Where the hymn said *come rest, come rest, the snow forgives all things*, the palace version said — Vesper felt it rather than translated it, the way you feel a blade before you see it — *stay awake, stay awake, the snow devours all things*. Where the hymn offered silence as worship, this thing demanded silence as tribute. Every held breath in the hymn had been stretched until it became a strangling.

Someone down there was singing a prayer to Vesper's silent god. Sung wrong on purpose. Sung the way you'd sing if you wanted the prayer to be a leash.

The brand flared white-hot against Vesper's forearm, and for one vertiginous second they weren't standing in a snowy street at all — they were somewhere vast and dark and wet, somewhere warm like the inside of a throat, and something enormous was turning toward them with an interest that had no kindness in it—

Vesper bit their tongue hard enough to taste copper, and the vision snapped shut like a trapdoor.

They stood gasping in the snow. Above the rooftops, the song went on, serene, patient, wrong.

"Okay," Vesper whispered, to the brand, to the night, to whoever was listening. "Okay. I hear you."

They climbed.

* * *

The roof of the dye-house on Bellringer's Row put Vesper level with the palace's outer wall, forty yards of open air between them and the royal wing. Close enough. From here, crouched behind a chimney crusted with ice, they watched the lit windows slide past below the eaves and listened to the hymn pour out of the king's chamber — the one tall window at the corner of the highest tower, glowing amber, curtains half-drawn.

And for the first time, they saw him.

King Aldric stood at the window with his back to the glass. Even at forty yards, even through frost-feathered panes, Vesper could see what four months of rumor had been trying and failing to describe: the king looked *worn*. Not ill, not starved — worn, the way a glove goes worn, stretched at the knuckles, thinned at the palm. His posture was perfect. Too perfect, spine straight as a spear-shaft, arms hanging with the stillness of something hung rather than standing. And he swayed, very slightly, in time with his own singing, the way a man sways when the song isn't coming from him but *through* him, the way a reed sways when the wind plays it.

His mouth sang the inverted Lamentation. His eyes were open, and they were looking directly at Vesper.

Forty yards. Night. Frost on the glass. There was no way — there was *no way* —

Aldric's eyes did not move off them. And in the middle of the false hymn, between one inverted syllable and the next, the king's mouth faltered. Just a hitch, a half-beat stumble, the kind of mistake no possessed thing would make. For a single instant his face changed — cracked, the way ice cracks, one clean line of something desperate running through the mask — and his right hand came up off his side and pressed flat against the inside of the window, fingers spread, pressing toward the street, toward the roofs, toward Vesper, as if a man underwater might press his palm to the surface of a lake.

*Help me,* said the hand. No sound. No sound at all — and Vesper understood, with a lurch, why the hymn was sung inverted, why every syllable was turned against itself. The thing in the crown had taken the god's own language and used it to build a cage of song around its prisoner. Aldric couldn't cry out. Any word he tried to speak came out as the hymn. He had spent four months screaming prayers and the whole city had heard music.

Then the moment passed. The hand dropped. The eyes glazed over again like windows fogging, and the sweet terrible voice resumed, seamless, as though the man inside it had never surfaced at all.

Vesper stayed on the rooftop a long time after the song ended — it ended at the exact hour of matins, precise as a clock, and the amber window went dark — crouched in the snow with their heart slamming and the brand throbbing in time with it, slower now, satisfied, like a dog that had led its master where it wanted.

They thought about the valley. About the Godless Valley, where magic couldn't follow, where the Sworn had fled a century ago when their god went quiet — fled, everyone said, because the god died and the magic died with it. Vesper had grown up on that story the way children grow up on the floor of a house: it was just the ground, just true. Their oath-brand was a relic. Their order was a memory. The Silent One was dead, and grief was the only rite left.

But gods didn't die quietly. Gods got *devoured*. And the thing doing the devouring was up there in a crown, wearing a king like a glove, digesting a divinity so slowly that the digestion had weather. A hundred years of winter, falling over the realm one season at a time, and everyone blaming the calendar.

Vesper pressed their thumb hard against the brand and felt it pulse back, warm, alive, *reaching*. Not a scar after all. A thread. The last warm thread of a god at the bottom of a dark place, pulling toward anyone who still carried its mark.

"You picked a hell of a messenger," Vesper murmured. "I'm a mercenary. I break things for money."

The brand pulsed again, gentle, almost amused. Almost fond. And underneath the horror and the cold and the taste of copper still on their tongue, Vesper felt something crack open in their chest — not fear, though there was plenty of that, but the older, worse thing underneath fear. Recognition. Somewhere in that tower a man was trapped in his own body singing a hymn to the god that had swallowed him, and somewhere deeper than that a god was trapped in the thing that wore the crown, and both of them were reaching for the same person on a freezing rooftop in a city that had forgotten how to pray.

Compassion was a tactical liability. Every commander Vesper ever served had said so. Compassion got soldiers killed.

Vesper stayed on the roof until dawn-grey touched the snow, memorizing the guard rotations at the palace gate, the gap in patrols at the chapel stair, the drainage culvert beneath the east wall that the frost had split wide enough for a slim body in dark wool. Then they climbed down, walked home through the waking city with murder in their stride and grief in their teeth, and let themselves into the room above the cooper's shop.

Sable was sitting up on the pallet, notebook in their lap, candle relit, eyes bright and furious and terrified all at once.

"Don't," Vesper said, before they could speak.

"Don't *what*?" Sable's voice cracked. "You snuck out at midnight following a *pain in your arm* into the one part of this city that eats people, and I'm supposed to say nothing? I heard it, Vesper. The singing. I heard it stop and start again — right when you would've been under the wall — and I sat here for an hour imagining—"

"It's Aldric." Vesper stripped off their gloves finger by finger, not looking at Sable yet. "The king. He's alive. He's *in there*. And the thing wearing him is singing our god's hymn backwards, and my brand nearly took my head off getting me there to hear it." They finally met Sable's eyes. "Something ate the Silent One, Sable. A hundred years ago. And it's still chewing."

Sable had gone very still, the particular stillness of an archivist who has just realized which shelf the answer lives on. "The chronicles," they breathed. "The year of the Silence — the restricted archive — I told you yesterday, whole sections razored out—"

"I know."

"If the god wasn't killed, if it was *fed on* — then someone did the feeding, and whoever it was would have left records, or had records destroyed, and either way the destruction itself is a record—"

"*Sable.*"

"—because no one razors pages out of every copy of everything unless the truth is load-bearing—"

"*Sable.*" Vesper crossed the room and took their face in both cold hands, and Sable stopped talking. Up close their eyes were red-rimmed; they hadn't slept either. "You're not going into that archive alone. And I'm not going back to that palace alone. Whatever this is—" Vesper lifted their branded forearm between them, the sigil still faintly warm, "—it's been waiting a hundred years for someone dumb enough to follow it. I'd rather be dumb with company."

For a moment Sable just looked at them, and some of the fury drained out of their face and left behind the other thing, the thing that had started all of this at an autumn fair with a book neither of them should have opened.

"Company," Sable agreed quietly. "Fine. But I'm telling you now — if your arm starts pulling you somewhere at midnight again, you wake me first. That's the deal. That's the whole deal."

"Deal," Vesper said, and meant it, and was already lying to themself about how easy it would be to keep.

Outside, the sun came up the color of weak tea, and somewhere far to the north, behind palace walls, a king who could not scream began rehearsing tomorrow night's hymn.
