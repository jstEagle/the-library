## 6. Company of the Sworn

The Sworn of the Quiet Choir did not advertise. That was rather the point of them.

I learned this young, in the Godless Valley, where my mother had sworn me to a god who had already stopped answering. The brand on my forearm — a small circle with a single vertical line through it, inked in ash and honey when I was nine — had never spoken since. No visions. No warmth. Just the ache of a question no one alive could answer: *why would a god go quiet and leave its people holding the phone?*

But the order had survived the silence the way cockroaches survive anything: by getting small, getting quiet, and getting very good at not being found.

Finding them again, a century after their last recorded chapter house burned, required skills I hadn't used since my mercenary days went respectable. I needed rumors. I needed old men who drank too much and remembered too much. I needed the kind of tavern where the barkeep watered the wine and doubled the price for asking why.

Sable wanted to come with me. I said no, and I meant it, and I felt terrible about both.

"You're the one they'll be hunting," Sable argued, arms crossed in our borrowed rooms above the chandler's shop, wearing that particular stubbornness that made me want to kiss them and strangle them in equal measure. "You walk into a cell of paranoid heretics alone, you're one bad conversation from a knife in the dark."

"That's exactly why I go alone." I sat on the windowsill, watching snow fall on a city that should have been weeks from first frost. "They've spent a hundred years being betrayed by faces they trusted. A stranger is dangerous. Two strangers are a conspiracy. And you—" I hesitated, because the next part wasn't fair, but fairness is a luxury of people whose names aren't written in prophecy books. "You're the reader. The chronicles we pulled apart at the archive, the pages Sable razored out of history — if any of that leaks, it points at an archivist. If the Sworn hear *archivist* anywhere near their safehouses, they'll scatter like starlings."

"So I'm supposed to sit here and embroider while you get interrogated by scarred fanatics?"

"Take notes. You're good at notes." I tried a smile. It came out crooked. "And if I don't come back by the second bell tomorrow, burn the copied pages and get out of the city. Don't be clever. Don't be brave. Be gone."

Sable looked at me for a long moment, and I watched them decide — deliberately, the way they decided everything, weighing it like a manuscript against its provenance — to hate the plan and follow it anyway.

"Fine," they said. "But write down everything they tell you. Verbatim. I want the dialect notes."

Only Sable Fenwick would demand field linguistics from a heresy hunt.

* * *

The rumor trail started, as all good trails do, with a lie.

A fishmonger near the river gate told me his cousin had seen Sworn begging alms at the shrine of Saint Vellum — which was nonsense, because the Sworn don't beg; they tithe *to* the poor, an inversion that made them beloved in the slums and hated everywhere else. But the lie had a shape. Lies about hidden things usually point away from the truth, and this one pointed away from the river district, so I went the other way.

Two days of walking. Snow thickening. The king's song drifting through the palace walls each night like something drowning and singing about it. My brand aching worse every time the wind came off the palace hill — a dull hot pulse now, like a bruise learning to throb. Whatever slept under that roof knew I was in its city, and it didn't like me.

On the third evening I found what I was looking for in the last place a sensible person would look: a tannery.

The tannery stank of lime and rot and rendered fat, and the foreman was a broad woman with a butcher's apron and eyes that catalogued everyone who walked past her door. I bought a hide I didn't need, overpaid visibly, and asked — casually, the way you ask about weather — whether she'd heard anything about the Quiet Choir.

She looked at me the way a hawk looks at a rabbit that has just made eye contact.

"Heard what about them?"

"That they still exist."

"They don't," she said, and sold me the hide, and as I turned to leave she added, without looking at me: "Tanners' Row runs behind the old fullers' yard. There's a door there that isn't a door. Knock wrong and you'll wish you hadn't knocked at all."

Then she went back to scraping fat off skin, and I stood in the falling snow feeling like a man who'd just been handed a live grenade with the pin labeled *good luck.*

* * *

The door that wasn't a door was exactly that: a section of wall behind the ruined fullers' yard, mortared to match the stones around it, with no handle, no hinge-line visible, nothing but a hairline crack where the mortar met in a pattern too regular to be accident. I'd have walked past it ten times. Maybe I already had.

There was no knocker. Instead, low down, at knee height, three stones were set into the wall: two pale, one dark.

I thought about it for a long time. Then I pressed the dark stone twice and the pale stone once, because the hymn rhythm of the Quiet Choir — the only prayer I'd ever been taught, back when the valley still pretended the god could hear — ran *rest, rest, rise.* Dark, dark, light. It was either the right answer or a very poetic way to die.

The wall swung inward on counterweights so smooth the stones didn't scrape.

Inside: darkness, warmth, and the smell of beeswax and woodsmoke and, underneath it, faintly, unmistakably, *honey* — the same smell as the rite that had branded me twenty years ago. My whole body reacted before my mind caught up. Twenty years collapsed. I was nine years old again, standing in a chapter house that smelled like this, waiting for a god to answer a question it had already decided not to answer.

A voice came out of the dark ahead of me. Female, older, roughened like rope left out in weather.

"Name your mother's mother, and the word she whispered when the Silence began."

I stood very still. This was the problem with being raised in a scattered order: everyone's proof-of-life questions were local. Mine were valley questions, and this cell had clearly been capital-born.

"I can't," I said honestly. "I'm Amberdale-sworn. Valley line. We got different questions."

Silence. Long enough to be ominous.

Then footsteps, and a lantern, and a face.

Mother Cassia was perhaps sixty, though the scarring made it hard to read. The burn had taken the left side of her jaw and most of her ear and left the skin smooth and waxy, pulled slightly toward the throat, so that even at rest she seemed to be turning to listen to something behind her. Her remaining eyebrow was white. Her eyes were grey — the same grey, I realized with a jolt, as the eyes painted on the broken icon in the archive's crypt level. The god's eyes. Or what the Church claimed were the god's eyes, before the Church decided the god had never existed at all.

She held the lantern up beside her own face, deliberately, letting me see all of it. It was a courtesy and a warning at once: *this is what they did to me. Understand what you're walking into.*

"The valley line," she said. "We thought the valley cells died out forty years ago."

"They did. Mostly. I'm what's left."

"And you came looking for us. In the middle of a purge. With winter coming early and the Regent-Cardinal offering fifty crowns a head for Sworn ears." She lowered the lantern. "Either you're the bravest fool I've met this decade, or you're bait."

"Could I be both?"

That earned half a laugh, dry as tinder. "Come in, wolf. Mind the step."

*Wolf.* I hadn't been called that yet by anyone in this century. I filed it away and said nothing, and the fact that I said nothing — that I didn't flinch, didn't ask how she knew, didn't perform surprise — seemed to matter to her more than anything else I might have said.

* * *

The safehouse was a converted wine cellar, and it was fuller than I expected.

Not with fighters. With *people.* Old men mending vestments by candlelight. A woman teaching six children their letters using a hornbook with the god's sigil carved where the alphabet should crown the page. A boy of maybe fifteen practicing the true hymn on a reed flute, softly, the melody rising and falling in patterns that made my brand ache in a completely different way than the palace did — a homesick ache, a *recognized* ache.

Thirty-one souls. That was the entire surviving cell. Thirty-one people keeping a hundred-year-old promise to a god who had never once, in living memory, said thank you.

Cassia led me to a table at the far end, away from the others, and poured me tea that tasted of pine and something bitter and medicinal. Then she sat, laid her hands flat on the table — I noticed two fingers missing from her right hand, another gift from whatever fire had taken her face — and looked at me with the patience of someone who has outlasted better interrogators than you.

"Tell me why you're here. Not the cover story. The real one. Start wherever's truest."

I took a breath and told her the truth, or as much of it as I could carry: the fair, the book, the page dated a century back, the pull into the snowbound capital, the clothes that fit too well, the strangers who greeted us by names out of legend. I told her about the razored chronicles. About the king singing at night. About my brand waking up after twenty years of sleep, aching toward the palace like a compass needle finding north.

I did not tell her about the wolf. I wanted to see if she'd bring it up herself.

She listened without interrupting, which in my experience marks either a saint or a trap-setter, and Cassia had the eyes of both.

When I finished, she was quiet for a while. Then:

"The book. Describe the binding."

"Dark leather, cracked at the spine. Iron corner-guards gone green. No title."

"Clasp?"

"Broken clasp, brass, shaped like—" I stopped. Shaped like a circle with a line through it. Like my brand.

Cassia nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a suspicion she'd carried for years. "There were nine of those books. Written in the last decade before the Silence, by a scribe named Ondrey, who claimed the god dictated them *in advance* — a record of things that hadn't happened yet. Eight were destroyed by the Church. The ninth vanished. Every Sworn elder I ever served under believed that if the ninth book surfaced, it would mean the end times had started." She turned her lantern-warm gaze fully on me. "You're telling me it surfaced in a valley archive fair, opened itself to your name, and dragged you here."

"Not dragged. Pulled. There's a difference. Dragging doesn't ask permission."

"Did it ask yours?"

I thought about it. "No. But it asked Sable's. They touched the page first."

Cassia's scarred mouth twitched. "The reader touches first. Yes. That matches." She said it lightly, but I saw the weight land in her hands, which had gone very still on the table. "Vesper Amberdale. Do you know what our fragmentary copies call you?"

Here it comes, I thought. Here's where she tells me the thing I've been circling for days.

"No," I said. "Tell me."

She leaned forward. "The prophecy names a figure at the turning of the age. Not by birth-name — the text uses titles, the way prophecy does, the way it used 'the Reader' for whoever opened the book. The figure is called *the wolf at the choir door.*" She let that sit between us. "It says the wolf comes from outside the walls, from a place the god's voice cannot reach. It says the wolf carries an oath older than the Silence. And then it gives two lines, and the two lines contradict each other outright, and every scholar who's ever touched the fragments has bled over the contradiction."

"What are the lines?"

Cassia recited them, and I could hear she'd recited them many times, in hiding, in fear, maybe aloud to dying Sworn as a comfort or a curse:

*"The wolf at the choir door shall break the crown and free the voice."*

*"The wolf at the choir door shall break the choir and still the voice forever."*

Same verb. Same object, almost. Break the crown, break the choir. Free the voice, still the voice. As if the prophecy itself couldn't decide — or as if, and this was the thought that crawled up my spine cold, *as if the outcome depended on something the text refused to name.*

"Which one am I?" I asked. My voice came out flatter than I intended.

"That's the question, isn't it." Cassia sat back. "The Church teaches its version, naturally: the wolf is a destroyer, a heretic's weapon, the reason good folk must report strangers with brands. Our own dead elders split on it. Mother Edeleth — who trained me, who lost those fingers and that side of her face in the same fire that nearly took me — believed the wolf was salvation. Her rival, Father Anselm, believed the wolf was the god's *second death*, the final insult. They hated each other for thirty years over two lines of text neither of them could verify." She paused. "Edeleth died believing she was right. Anselm died believing he was. Neither of them ever met anyone the prophecy might actually describe."

"And now you have."

"Now I have." Her grey eyes fixed on me. "And I'll be honest with you, Vesper, because you strike me as someone who'd rather have honesty than comfort. I don't know which you are. I've known you for an hour. What I know is this: you walked into a hunted cellar during a purge, unarmed, and asked for nothing. Destroyers usually ask for something."

The tea had gone cold in my cup. I set it down.

"What do you need me to say?" I asked. "To prove which wolf I am?"

"Nothing you can say." She shook her head. "Words are cheap. The Church's spies are eloquent. No — the prophecy will prove itself, or it won't. All I can do is watch what you do next, and pray Edeleth was right." She rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked down at me from her full unimpressive height. "In the meantime, you'll stay. You'll work. You'll mend vestments and haul water and teach the children the true hymn if you remember it, and you'll do it under a false name, and you will not leave the cellar until I say. Because if the Regent's hunters find a branded stranger asking after the Choir, they'll find us within the week, and thirty-one people will burn for your homecoming."

"Understood."

"One more thing." She turned back at the curtain that divided her sleeping alcove from the common room, and the lantern light caught the wax-smooth scar tissue, and for a moment she looked less like a woman than like an icon of some fiercer saint. "The boy with the flute. Tam. He's the best ear this order has produced in three generations. He can hear the king's night-song from three streets off and transcribe it. Last month he brought me the transcription." Her voice dropped. "Vesper — the inverted hymn the thing in the palace sings? It isn't just the true hymn backwards. There's a second melody buried inside it. Underneath. Something the Singing King is being forced to carry along with the corruption, like a message sewn into the lining of a stolen coat."

My brand pulsed. Hard. Once.

"We can't make sense of it," Cassia said. "Tam's too young; he's never heard the original melodies complete. But you were valley-sworn. You learned the old songs before the Silence finished settling." She watched my face carefully. "Do you remember them?"

I did. God help me, I did — fragments, hummed by my mother, corrected by a deaf choirmistress who'd learned them from her grandmother, melodies I'd carried for twenty years without knowing why, the way you carry a key to a door you've never seen.

"There's something else," Cassia said, and now her tone changed entirely, and the warmth drained out of the room by degrees. "Something I haven't told the others. Something about the book, and the reader, and the reason the ninth volume surfaced *now*, in *your* century, in *your* hands." She drew the curtain aside. "Get some sleep, wolf. Tomorrow I show you what Tam heard. And then you'll understand why Father Anselm might have been right to be afraid — and why, if Edeleth was right instead, you're the only person alive who can finish what a silent god started a hundred years ago."

The curtain fell shut behind her.

I lay down on a straw pallet in a cellar full of strangers who shared my faith and doubted my face, and above me the snow kept falling, and somewhere across the frozen city the hollow king sang his broken hymn into the dark — and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, that second melody, waiting for someone who remembered the words.

I fell asleep humming mine.
