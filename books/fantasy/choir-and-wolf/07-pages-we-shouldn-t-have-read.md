## 7. Pages We Shouldn't Have Read

Mother Cassia gave me a candle, a stool, and a warning.

"The Sworn don't keep libraries anymore," she said. "Libraries get people burned. What you see in this room stays in your skull or nowhere."

The room was a cellar beneath a tannery — I could tell by the smell, which had soaked into the stone over decades and would soak into me the same way if I stayed long enough. Shelves of salvaged books lined the walls: hymnals with their covers pried off, psalters rebound in sailcloth, loose quires wrapped in oilskin like contraband, which I suppose they were. A heretic's archive. My whole professional soul ached at the cataloguing it needed.

"I need everything your people saved from the year of the Silence," I told her. "Chronicles, court records, anything with dates."

"We have three chronicles. Fragments." Her scarred mouth twisted. "The Church burned what it could find. Why do you think we're hiding in a cellar?"

"Because razored pages leave scars," I said. "And scars can be read."

She looked at me for a long moment, the way an archivist looks at a book she suspects is more valuable than its cover admits.

"Vesper trusts you," she said at last, which was not quite the same as trusting me herself. "Don't make them wrong."

* * *

I worked until my candle was a puddle and my fingers were grey with ink.

The three chronicles were court annals, written by different hands across the same terrible year — the year the god went silent, the year the winter came early and stayed. Each one had been mutilated. Not burned, not censored with a stamp: *razored*, carefully, by someone who knew exactly which passages mattered. Whole seasons were missing from each volume, but the razoring wasn't random. It followed a pattern, and patterns are the one language I speak fluently.

Here is what I found in the margins that survived.

The high priest of the Quiet Choir that year was named Aurelian Voss. He appears constantly in the surviving text — dedicating altars, leading processions, praised in the fawning way court chroniclers praise powerful men. Then, four months before the Silence, his name simply stops appearing. No death notice. No retirement. Nothing. In a court chronicle, where they recorded how many swans the king ate at midsummer, a man does not vanish without a line.

Unless someone wanted him to vanish.

I laid the three chronicles side by side on the tannery floor and matched their wounds. Where Chronicle One had been cut, Chronicle Two often kept a fragment — half a sentence, a date, a name bleeding off the edge of a page. Archivists call it collation; thieves call it piecing. By midnight I had reconstructed maybe a tenth of what had been removed, and that tenth was enough to turn my stomach cold in a cellar that was already cold.

The passages weren't about enemies. There was no invasion, no heresy from outside, no rival god rising out of the sea. The missing text was about a *rite*. A feeding rite. The fragments used words I'd only seen in medical texts and one banned grimoire I'd catalogued at nineteen and never stopped thinking about: *siphon*, *vessel*, *communion inverted*.

Aurelian Voss hadn't fled the choir. He had turned the choir inside out. The hymns that fed devotion *to* the god — he had learned to sing them backward, to pull instead of give, to drink. And the chronicle fragments made clear he hadn't done it in secret. He'd done it at the altar, in front of the court, disguised as greater piety. Longer services. Deeper devotions. The god grew quiet not because it was wounded by strangers but because it was being *eaten from within its own house*, and no one thought to look there, because who looks for a thief behind the altar?

The last fragment I reconstructed was two lines long, from the hand of a chronicler whose style I recognized as frightened — cramped letters, ink blotted in haste:

*"His Holiness sings nightly in the king's chambers. The king has not spoken in nine days. His Majesty's eyes—"*

And then the razor.

I sat back on my stool. The candle had drowned. In the dark I could hear the tannery vats dripping above me, slow as a clock, and somewhere far off, muffled by snow and stone, the hollow king singing his inverted hymn — and I understood now why it sounded wrong. It sounded wrong because it was old. It was *Aurelian's* song, a century stale, sung by whatever remained of him, still feeding, still patient.

Something wearing Aldric's crown had been eating a god for a hundred years, one winter at a time.

And the Regent-Cardinal — Cassia had said his name was Voss. I hadn't thought anything of it. Half the nobility shared six surnames. But priests pass things down. Doctrines. Grudges. Recipes.

I relit the candle with hands that only shook a little.

* * *

It was near dawn when I found the third chronicle's true treasure, and I found it the way you find most important things in archives: by accident, while looking for something else.

The binding had been repaired badly, sometime in the last century, by someone in a hurry — new thread punched through old holes at the wrong angle. When I flexed the spine to check the sewing, the boards gapped, and a slip of paper slid out from between the flyleaf and the cover. Thin, brittle, folded twice. Hidden, not lost. Someone had put it *there*, inside the mutilated book, knowing that anyone thorough enough to study a ruined chronicle might be thorough enough to find it.

Or knowing that *one particular person* would.

I unfolded it under the candle. It was a single page, cut from a larger document — the paper stock matched nothing in the chronicles themselves. The handwriting was elegant, antique, the kind taught in scriptoria a hundred years gone.

At the top, a heading: *To the Second Reader.*

My mouth went dry.

*If you have found this, then the book has done its work twice,* the page began. *Once for the wolf, once for the reader. Do not ask how I knew you would come. The god whispered it to me in the last hour it could still whisper, and a god's last whisper is not a guess.*

I read it three times. The cellar dripped. The king sang. My own heartbeat sounded loud and stupid in my ears.

*I am the hand that razored these chronicles,* the letter continued. *Not the Cardinal's men — mine. I served in the royal archive when Aurelian completed his communion, and I saw what the king became, and I understood that truth survives only if someone buries it properly. So I cut the record. Every account of the rite, every mention of Voss, gathered here where the Sworn would one day shelter — because the Sworn would be blamed, and the blamed keep secrets well.*

*I left the wounds readable on purpose. A razored page tells you what was taken. Collate them, and you will learn what I could not write aloud: that our god was not slain. That it was supped upon. That the eater sits enthroned.*

*But hear the rest, second reader, because the rest is yours alone.*

*The god's last whisper named two. The wolf at the choir door — the oath-bearer, the breaker, the one whose vow will crack the crown. And the reader — the one who comes after, who keeps what is kept, who carries the true hymn through the fire. It did not tell me your name. Names mean little to something that large. It told me your *shape*. A keeper of dead pages. A lover of the wolf. Someone who chooses truth when truth is expensive.*

I stopped reading. My eyes had blurred, and I wiped them with the back of my wrist, and the smear of soot and ink on my skin seemed suddenly very fitting, very much the shape of me, and I laughed — one short, unsteady note in an empty cellar — because what else do you do when a dead archivist describes you across a century?

*I hid the completion of the prophecy where no heretic-hunter would dare disturb it,* the letter went on. *Where the crown itself would guard it without knowing. Seek the royal crypts. The pages sleep with the queen, sewn into her shroud — she was Voss's first victim, though the chronicles will never say so, and her husband has mourned her for a hundred years inside his own stolen mouth.*

*Beware the Regent-Cardinal. The Voss line tends the rite generation after generation. They believe themselves stewards. They are livestock who have learned to love the farmer.*

*One more thing, and then I will let you work.*

*The book that brought you here — the one with your names in it — was written by no prophet of ours. It was written by the god itself, in the last of its strength, poured through a sleeping scribe the way a dying man dictates a will. It is not a prediction. It is an instruction manual, addressed forward, because the god could not save itself but could, perhaps, arrange its own rescue. It chose its rescuers the way a drowning man chooses which hand to reach for.*

*It chose yours.*

*— Brother Edmun, last librarian of the Quiet Choir*

I set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something that might still be alive.

Then I sat on the stool in the dying light of a heretic candle and let the vertigo take me, because it wanted taking. The book knew. Before Vesper was born, before I was born, before the valley was even mapped — some vast, starving, dying intelligence had looked down the long throat of a century and reached for *me*. Not a hero. Not a saint. An archivist. A woman whose greatest ambition, three weeks ago, had been getting the autumn fair's honey wine stall to stop watering the casks.

It had reached for my hand specifically. And I had taken it without thinking, the way you take a offered hand, and now I was standing a hundred years from home holding a dead man's confession and a map to a queen's shroud, and there was no version of me anywhere in any timeline that put the page down and walked away.

That was the vertigo's sharpest edge, in the end. Not fear. Recognition. All my life I had believed I chose books. It turns out a book had chosen me first, and waited patiently on a shelf for a hundred years for me to catch up.

"Archivist."

Cassia's voice at the stair. Grey light was leaking down past her shoulders — real morning, the false winter's pale sun.

"You've been down here all night," she said. "Vesper's been pacing a trench in my floor. They're convinced the Pale Guard took you."

"They should know better than to worry," I said. "I'm exactly where a book told me to wait."

She came down the stairs slowly, sword-hand loose, and I held up Brother Edmun's page and the collated fragments spread across the floor like the shed skin of a century.

"Cassia," I said. "Your order wasn't destroyed by enemies either. You were *harvested* — scapegoats, season after season, so no one would ever audit the crown's accounts. And the proof your god was eaten by its own priest has been sitting in a cellar under a tannery for a hundred years, waiting for someone with my exact set of neuroses." I heard my own voice go bright and hard, the fury-voice I usually only aimed at mislabeled folios. "I know where the missing pages are. I know how the unbinding works. And I know the Regent-Cardinal's family recipe."

For the first time since I'd met her, Mother Cassia sat down on a heretic's crate like her legs had voted independently.

"Say all of that again," she said, "slower. And then say the part about the crypts again, because I think I heard the name of a place no living Sworn has entered since the Silence, and I want to be sure my heart is breaking for the right reason."

* * *

We climbed the stairs together into the white morning, and the city was wrong.

I knew cities. I knew the specific hum of a city deciding whether to panic — the pitch of market argument, the tempo of footsteps. This was a city past deciding. Smoke stood up straight from a hundred chimneys because there was no wind at all, which is its own kind of omen. The canal at the tannery's back gate had frozen *mid-current*, ice sculpted around water that was still moving underneath, like the river had been interrupted mid-sentence. A fishmonger stood at the ice hole with a net and a face like a man watching his gods drown. Birds — I counted five before I stopped counting — lay stiff on the cobbles along the wall, fallen out of a sky that had simply closed beneath them.

And everywhere, in doorways, in windows, chalked on shutters: the Church's new sigil, the crowned sun. Painted over it, wherever hands dared: the circle-and-silence of the Choir, and beneath those marks, fresh scratches, the word *heretic* crossed out and rewritten as *truth*.

"The riots started an hour ago," Cassia said quietly. "Grain stores are empty. The Church says the Sworn cursed the harvest. There's a procession forming at the Cathedral square — the Regent-Cardinal is going to address the city." She looked at me sideways. "He doesn't give sermons in bad weather, archivist. If Voss is speaking publicly, something is beginning."

Somewhere across the rooftops, bells began — not the measured hour-bells but the fast, ugly rhythm I'd read about in civil-annal fragments and never hoped to hear: *alarm, alarm, alarm.* The crowd-noise of the city rose to meet it, a tide coming in.

And beneath it all, faint and patient, the hollow king began to sing.

I grabbed the arm of a passing Sworn runner — a boy of maybe fifteen with frostbitten ears — and pressed Edmun's page into his hands. "Take this to Vesper Amberdale. Word for word. Tell them the crypts, the queen, the shroud — and tell them I've gone to the cathedral square."

The boy stared. "You're going *toward* it?"

"I'm an archivist," I said, pulling my coat tight against a cold that was starting to feel less like weather and more like appetite. "When something tries to burn the record, I stand between."

The square was six streets away. I felt the winter lean toward me the whole way there, like something inhaling — and in my pocket, copied out in my own careful hand, the true hymn waited in the dark like a drawn knife.
