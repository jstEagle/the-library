## 8. The Long Winter Begins

**Vesper**

The river froze mid-current.

Vesper saw it from the bridge on the third morning of the hard cold — saw it happen, which was the part that made their skin crawl afterward. The Millwater had been moving a moment before, dark and muscular, carrying slush toward the sea. Then a sound like a struck bell rolled up from under the ice at the banks, and the whole river stopped. Not slowed. Stopped. The current hung in the water like a thought interrupted, and frost climbed it from bank to bank in a single breath, and the Millwater became a road.

People cheered. That was the worst of it. A fisherman's boy ran out onto the ice and slid, laughing, and the crowd laughed with him, and Vesper stood at the rail with their hand pressed flat against the frozen stone and thought: *nothing natural stops like that.* Rivers don't hold their breath. Rivers don't *decide*.

Their oath-brand ached under their glove. It had ached for three days now, a low toothache of a pain, deep in the meat of the forearm where the Sworn's mark sat like a brand of cooled iron. It had been silent for a century — everyone's had, all across the valley, the whole order's marks gone quiet the day the Quiet One went silent. Vesper had grown up thinking of the brand as a scar, a family heirloom of grief. Now it was waking up like an old dog hearing a whistle only it could hear.

And the whistle was coming from the palace.

"You're doing the face," said a voice beside them, and Vesper startled, because Sable moved like a librarian even in a borrowed winter coat, silent as a page turning.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided the world is ending and you're the only one who's noticed." Sable leaned on the rail, breath fogging. Around them the crowd was still celebrating the frozen river. Someone had already set up a stall selling hot wine at the bridge's foot, which Vesper felt was a very capital-city response to an apocalypse. "Have you decided the world is ending?"

"I've decided the river is wrong."

"The river is very wrong." Sable said it lightly, but their eyes weren't light. Three days in this century — this *snowbound, wrong-century* version of a city that wouldn't exist for another hundred years — and Sable had gone from thrilled to something quieter and harder. They'd stopped taking notes two days ago. Vesper had noticed. Archivists don't stop taking notes. "The birds this morning. Did you see?"

Vesper had. They'd been coming out of the tenement where the Sworn's contact had put them up — a cobbler's widow with a brand hidden under her sleeve and a fear of windows — when the starlings came over the rooftops, a thousand of them, wheeling south the way starlings do. And then, all together, without a hawk or a shot or any reason at all, they dropped. Fell out of the sky like a handful of thrown stones, landing in the streets and the frozen gutters and one unlucky man's hood, and the whole flock lay still in the snow, and the silence afterward was the kind of silence that has weight.

"Frozen mid-flight," Vesper said. "Not starved. Not scared. Frozen. Mid-flight."

"Mm." Sable's breath came out in a long plume. "In the chronicles, the winter of the Silence started the same way. I checked. Rivers first, then birds, then—" They stopped.

"Then what?"

"Then people started blaming each other." Sable nodded across the square, and Vesper followed the nod, and saw the bonfire.

It was being built on the steps of the Cathedral of the Resplendent Sun, which was a joke of a name in this weather, because the sun hadn't shown its face in six days. Priests in white and gold were stacking pallets and old pews and — Vesper's stomach tightened — books. Stacks of books, armloads of them, and among the books, Vesper could see, even at this distance, spines they recognized. The Sworn's liturgies. The Quiet One's hymnals, printed in the old days when the order had been tolerated, before the Silence had made them heretics by absence.

A priest stood above the pile, and his voice carried across the frozen square the way voices carry in hard cold, flat and clear.

"—the heretics of the Quiet Choir, who turned their backs on the Sun and invited this darkness! Who whispered to their dead god while the realm froze! The Crown has been patient. The Crown has been merciful. But the Sun's patience is not infinite, and neither is the people's!"

The crowd was listening. That was the thing Veper couldn't look away from. The crowd was *listening*, because people were hungry and cold and frightened, and frightened people will eat any story you hand them if it comes with someone else to blame.

"The Sworn are still here," the priest said. "Hidden in your streets. Sleeping under your roofs. And every day they go unfound, the winter deepens."

The bonfire caught. The books went up with a smell Vesper knew from their childhood — the smell of the one time the valley's chapel had been burned, when they were seven, though the valley had no priests and the fire had never been explained. They watched hymnals curl and blacken, and their brand ached so hard their fingers went numb, and they understood with total clarity that the thing in the palace was not just eating a god. It was eating the god's memory. Burning it out of the world one book at a time.

"We need to move," they said. "Today. If the Sworn are being hunted—"

"Cassia's cell knows we're coming. We have the sign." Sable was already moving, and Vesper followed, and that was the last easy minute they had for a long time.

* * *

**Sable**

The riot started, as far as Sable could ever reconstruct it, over a loaf of bread.

They had been crossing the Cornmarket with Vesper, heads down, moving with the crowd toward the tanner's lane where Mother Cassia's people were supposed to be waiting, when a baker's boy had come out of his shop and announced there was no more flour and there would be no more bread, and a woman in the queue had said, in a voice Sable would remember for the rest of their life, "Then we eat the rich," and it had been meant as a joke, Sable thought, or half a joke, the way desperate things are half jokes. And then someone else had said it, not as a joke. And then the queue had become a crowd and the crowd had become a flood, and the baker's shutters had gone in with a crack like ice breaking, and the whole street tilted into violence the way a table tilts when you lean on one end.

Sable had studied riots. They had read accounts of the Bread Riots of the Interregnum, of the Guild Wars, of a dozen other moments when cities came apart. They had thought, in the smug way of people who study things, that they understood the shape of one.

They understood nothing. Nothing in the chronicles conveyed the *sound* — the layered roar of it, hundreds of voices braided into one animal noise. Nothing conveyed the smell, woodsmoke and spilled beer and the copper tang of the first blood, which happened somewhere to their left and was over in seconds. Nothing conveyed the sheer *physics* of it, the way a crowd stops being people and becomes a fluid, and you are not walking in it, you are being *poured*.

"Vesper!" Sable shouted it and heard it swallowed whole.

Vesper had been a half-step ahead, shoulder cutting the current, one hand hooked in the back of Sable's coat — Vesper had done that all day, a small constant anchor, and Sable had privately catalogued how it made them feel and filed the entry under *later* — but then the crowd surged sideways, a whole mass of it breaking toward a grain cart that had appeared at the street's mouth, and the hand on Sable's coat was torn away like a leaf off a branch.

"Vesper—"

For one second Sable saw them: Vesper's face above the crowd, sharp with the same fear Sable felt, mouth shaping Sable's name. Then a shutter came off a window and hit the snow, and a knot of men surged between them, and Vesper was gone. Just — gone. The way a page slips back into a closed book.

Sable did what archivists do. They got against a wall. They made themselves small in the lee of a chandler's doorway, and they breathed, and they thought.

*Think. Facts. One: Vesper can fight. Vesper has fought professionally for a decade. The crowd is a danger but not, for Vesper, a lethal one. Two: the destination is known to both of us — the tanner's lane, the sign, the widow's cousin. If separated, regroup. Vesper will go there. Three: you are a scholar in a city that has just declared your religion heresy, and you are carrying—*

Sable's hand went to their coat, to the inner pocket, and found the pages.

They had copied them two nights ago by candle stub: the mutilated chronicle passages, the razored-out history, the fragments they'd pieced together in the archive before the archive had started to feel like a trap. The pages that said the god had not been killed. The pages that said it had been *fed upon*. They were against Sable's ribs right now, and if the crowd tore the coat, if the wrong eyes saw the old Sworn script—

A window broke above their head. Glass rained into the doorway. Sable pulled their hood down and moved, because staying still had stopped being an option; the flood was coming around the chandler's like water around a stone.

They went with it. That was the only rule of a riot, they realized, learning it in the body rather than the book: you do not fight the current, you *are* the current, you go where it goes and you get out where it thins. It thinned at the mouth of Tanner's Lane, because tanners' lanes smell like tanners' lanes even in winter, and the riot had no appetite for that.

Sable stood in the sudden quiet, chest heaving, hood up, alone in a century that was not their own, with stolen pages against their heart and no idea where Vesper was.

The brand on their own arm — the small one, the scholar's mark the Sworn gave to allies, the one they'd gotten in a ceremony that had felt, at the time, like a delightful piece of living history — ached.

Not like Vesper's. Sable was not Sworn. But it ached, faintly, in time with something. Like a tuning fork near a struck bell.

They looked down the lane. At its end, a door with a tanner's wheel chalked above it, and under the wheel, fresh in the snow, a second sign: three small lines, like a bird's footprint.

The sign they'd agreed on.

Sable went.

* * *

**Vesper**

They lost Sable in the Cornmarket and it took everything Vesper had not to turn back into the flood after them.

Everything, and then some. Because turning back was what the fear wanted, and the fear was loud, and Vesper had spent a lifetime learning that the fear is always loud and always wrong. You go to the rally point. You trust the plan. You do not feed the flood another body.

So they let the crowd carry them east, and they fought when they had to — twice, briefly, men with hammers and no targets, and Vesper put them down with the flat economy of a decade's drill and kept moving — and they got out at the fish gate, where the frozen canal made a road and the riot had no interest in following, because there was nothing on the ice to burn.

Then they stood on the canal in the grey light, alone, and the fear caught up with them all at once.

Sable. *Sable,* who had never been in a fight, who had never been in a *riot*, who had walked into this century with the joy of a child walking into a story and had spent three days watching the story turn into the other kind. Sable, whose last sight of them had been Vesper being pulled under by a crowd.

Vesper's brand was burning now. Not aching — burning, a wire of heat from wrist to elbow, and underneath the pain, faint as a voice through a wall, something else. A pull. North. Toward the palace.

*Not now,* Vesper told it, and the brand, being what it was — being the last living thread of a god — did not care what Vesper told it.

They ran for the tanner's lane.

* * *

**Sable**

The door at the end of Tanner's Lane was opened by a boy of maybe twelve with a tanner's burn scars on both hands, who looked at Sable's sign-gesture, looked at their face, and said, "You're the one from the book," with the flat certainty of a child repeating something he'd been told, and Sable's blood went cold in a way the winter hadn't managed yet.

"Which book?" they asked.

The boy just stepped aside.

Inside was a cellar, and in the cellar were eleven people, and at the center of the eleven was a woman with a scarred jaw and eyes like a hawk deciding — Mother Cassia, whom Sable had met for exactly one hour in the valley of their own century, a hundred years from now, when Cassia was old and the order was nearly gone. Here she was young. Here she was *formidable*. Here she looked at Sable and said, "Where's the wolf?"

"The—" Sable caught up. The prophecy's name for Vesper. *The wolf at the choir door.* "Separated. In the Cornmarket. They'll come here."

"They'd better." Cassia jerked her chin at the cellar's low ceiling, beyond which the roar of the city came and went like surf. "You heard what they burned this afternoon."

"The hymnals. We saw."

"Not just the hymnals." Cassia's voice was level in the way Vesper's went level, the trained levelness, the levelness that costs something. "They burned the *registers*. Every record of every Sworn in the capital. Names, addresses, faces. The Church collected them for a century 'for safekeeping.'" Her mouth did something that was not a smile. "Now we know why they kept them."

Around the cellar, Sable saw the eleven people hear this — most of them had already known, but hearing it said aloud was its own wound. A woman with a baby wrapped against her chest closed her eyes. An old man began, very quietly, to pack a satchel.

"They'll come for us tonight," Cassia said. "Pale Guard, probably. They'll come for the whole lane, because the baker's boy saw me at the well this morning and the baker's boy's mother talks to the Cathedral. So we move at dark. North cells. And your wolf—" the hawk eyes came back to Sable, "—had better be here by dark, or your wolf is going to have to find us the hard way, in a city that's about to start hunting us house to house."

* * *

**Vesper**

The city found its scapegoats before dark.

Vesper heard it coming from three streets away — not the riot's animal roar this time but something worse, something organized: drums, and chanting, and the particular cadence of a crowd that has been *given* its targets. They pressed into a doorway on Cordwainer Street and watched the procession go by: white-robed penitents at the front, then the Pale Guard in their bone-colored armor, moving with a discipline no riot ever had, and behind them a mob with torches, and above the mob carried on poles the things they had come to burn.

Coats. Boots. A chair. Household things, torn from houses — and among them, held up like a trophy, a banner of the Quiet Choir, grey silk, the old mark stitched in the corner, and Vesper's brand answered it like a struck bell answers its twin, and they had to put their teeth together and hold still.

The chanting resolved into words. *Find the Sworn. Find the Sworn.* And then a new word joined it, a name, and the name was *Fenwick*, and the world went very small and very cold.

*—the heretic archivist, the Crown's criers had said, that morning, in the square Vesper had crossed without listening — the heretic archivist who stole from the royal archive, who carries the old lies, who was seen at the Cornmarket—*

They had Sable's name. They had Sable's *description*. Vesper had watched, three days ago, Sable sign the archive's visitor ledger in a beautiful looping hand, full name, in ink, in front of witnesses, because Sable was an archivist and archivists sign things.

The procession turned south, toward the archive quarter, and Vesper came out of the doorway and followed it, because the plan said go to the tanner's lane, and the plan could go to hell. The fear was loud and for once the fear was *right*.

* * *

**Sable**

They came for the lane at dusk, and Cassia's people were already gone — all but the rear guard, all but Cassia herself, who had waited at the door with a hand on Sable's shoulder and said, "The wolf will come here looking for you. Be gone before the Guard does. North cells. The cobbler's widow knows the way," and had gone out the back into the snow.

Sable had gone north alone through streets that were curfew-quiet and curfew-lit, every lamp burning, every door shut, the whole city holding its breath between the riot and the hunt. They had reached the widow's house. The widow had taken one look at them and said, "Not you, not tonight, they're watching the north cells, everyone knows it," and then, seeing Sable's face, had relented enough to add: "The archive quarter. That's where they're sweeping. If your person is missing, that's where missing persons are."

So Sable had turned around, and gone back south, into the teeth of it, and that was how they came to be standing in the mouth of Archive Row at full dark, watching the royal archive of the capital of a kingdom a hundred years dead burn like a festival bonfire.

The Pale Guard had cordoned the street. The crowd was allowed to watch. The crowd was, Sable understood with a sick, scholarly clarity, *the point*. You don't burn a library secretly. A library burns secretly in the dark of a siege. You burn it in front of everyone, in the light of a hundred lamps, so that everyone learns the same lesson at once: *there is no truth but the Crown's truth, and the old truth is ash.*

Sable stood in the crowd with their hood up and their stolen pages against their ribs and watched a hundred years of records — the very chronicles they'd razored through, the very shelves they'd walked three days ago in scholarly rapture — go up.

And they thought, with a strange detachment, like reading someone else's account: *this is the night I stop being a visitor.*

The junior librarian had gotten out. Sable saw her — a girl of sixteen, soot-faced, held by the arm by a Guard who was half-dragging her down the row toward the questioning house — and saw the girl clutching something to her chest with her free arm, a bundle wrapped in her apron, and saw the Guard notice the bundle, and saw the Guard's hand go to it.

Later, Sable could never fully account for the next thirty seconds. There was a scream in the crowd — someone else's, a woman's, a diversion that Sable would spend years suspecting had been arranged, though by whom they never learned — and the crowd surged, and the Guard turned, and Sable was through the cordon and their elbow was in the Guard's throat, an archivist's elbow, a scholar's elbow, the single most violent act of Sable Fenwick's life, and then they were running, with the girl, with the bundle, into the tangle of lanes behind the row while the fire roared behind them and someone shouted the word *Fenwick* like a curse.

They didn't stop until the archive quarter was a glow behind them and the girl was sobbing against a wall. The bundle, when Sable finally made her open it, was books. Six of them, small, scorched at the edges. Sable knew the spines.

Chronicles. The mutilated ones. The ones with the razored years.

"You saved these," Sable said. "Out of the fire. Why?"

The girl looked at them with soot-streaked eyes and said, "Because someone had to," and Sable felt that sentence go into them like a blade going into a sheath, and they knew — the way you know a thing you've read a hundred times and only now lived — that this was what the work was. Not the shelves. Not the ledgers. *This.*

Then, from up the frozen canal, a sound: running footsteps, fast and uneven, and a shape coming out of the dark with its hood back and its brand-lit face tight with fear, and Sable was moving before their mind caught up, and then Vesper's arms were around them, hard enough to hurt, and Vesper was saying their name into their hair like a word in a language only the two of them spoke.

"You're alive," Vesper said. "You're— they were burning your name off the city, I heard it, I—"

"I'm here." Sable held on. Around them the winter deepened; the frost crept up the walls; somewhere a bell tolled the hour, and the sound of it froze, Sable would have sworn, a half-second too long in the air. "I'm here. But Vesper — the archive's gone. Everything I came to find. Everything except—" they touched their ribs, the pages, the scorched books under their coat, "—this."

Vesper pulled back enough to look at them, at the soot and the shaking hands and the elbow that was going to bruise spectacularly, and something in their face shifted — pride, Sable thought, actual pride, filed under *later* with everything else.

"Then tell me on the move," Vesper said. "Because the Guard knows your name now, the whole city knows your name, and we have until dawn before this winter stops being weather and starts being a hunt."

Behind them, down the canal, the ice sang — a long, ringing note, like a struck bell, like a held breath.

The winter was listening.

And it had heard their names.
