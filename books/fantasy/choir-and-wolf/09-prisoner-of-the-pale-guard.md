## 9. Prisoner of the Pale Guard

They lost each other at the Tanner's Bridge.

Later, Vesper would replay it the way you replay a bad fall — every detail lit too bright, every mistake obvious, none of them avoidable. The crowd on the bridge had been running both directions, which is never a good sign; crowds only do that when nobody knows where the danger is, so everyone is running *from* everything. A cart had overturned somewhere behind them, spilling salted fish across the cobbles, and the fish were freezing as they scattered, and people were slipping on them, and through all of it Sable's hand was in theirs, grip like a promise.

Then the pale guard came down the bridge from the far end in a wedge, shields locked, moving with that terrible unhurried patience that said they didn't need to run because running was for people who could lose.

Vesper had done the math in half a second. Two exits off the bridge. One toward the river stairs, one toward the ropewalk. They'd squeezed Sable's hand twice — their old signal, *split and meet* — and shoved them toward the stairs, toward the dark, toward the archive tunnels Sable knew better than the guard ever would.

Sable had gone. That was the part that sat wrong in Vesper's chest afterward. Not that they'd gone — that was the plan, that was right — but that they'd gone *looking back*, mouth shaping words Vesper couldn't hear over the screaming, and Vesper had spent the next three days turning those silent syllables over like a coin worn smooth. *Meet me. Meet me. Don't be stupid.*

They'd been stupid anyway. But not on the bridge. On the bridge they'd been perfect.

The mistake came an hour later, in a chandler's cellar off Rope Lane, when Vesper went back for them instead of out of the city. Because the signal meant *meet*, and meeting required two, and Vesper Amberdale had never once in their life left anyone at a rendezvous point. The Sworn drilled it into children before they drilled sword work: *the oath is the company, the company is the oath.* You did not leave your people in a burning city to wait alone in the dark.

The cellar was empty except for candle stubs and a cat with opinions. And when Vesper climbed back up into the alley, the pale guard was already there — six of them, no wedge this time, spread loose like fishermen who'd felt the line go taut hours ago and had simply waited for the fish to tire.

"Amberdale," said the one in front. Not a question. A reading, like off a ledger.

Vesper put their back to the wall and took inventory. Six guards, pale-lacquered armor, the kind that looked like bone until you got close enough to see it was just very good metal. Shortswords. No crossbows — crossbows in an alley that narrow would be stupid, and whatever else the Pale Guard was, they weren't stupid. Behind Vesper, the cellar hatch. Through it, another cellar. Through that, a courtyard Vesper had scouted yesterday and now, with the specific cruelty of hindsight, remembered was a dead end in winter because the drain grates were iced shut.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Vesper said. "You know my name."

"The whole city knows your name." The speaker's voice was flat and young and terribly calm. "The archivist burned the archive. Your name was in the ledger with hers. Twice, actually. Once for the heresy charge and once for unpaid harbor fees from your first week here, which I confess surprised me."

"That was a clerical error."

"So it says." The guard tilted their head, and the bone-white helm caught the light of the burning archive still glowing on the southern horizon, three nights old and refusing to die. "You can fight us. Six of us, in this alley, and you'd take maybe two of us down and we'd take your arms off at interesting angles. Or you can come quietly, and I give you my word — my actual word, sworn and witnessed — that you arrive unharmed."

"And after?"

"After is above my pay," the guard said. There was something almost human in it, almost apologetic. "But unharmed is unharmed. It's more than most get this winter."

Vesper looked at the six of them. Looked at the iced grate behind their own eyes, the dead-end courtyard, the arithmetic. Somewhere in this city Sable was hiding with an armful of stolen books and a wounded librarian and a map to a dead queen's shroud, and every hour Vesper stayed free was an hour the Guard spent hunting *them* instead of following the trail back to Sable.

That decided it, more than the math did. The math just made it respectable.

"Unharmed," Vester said. "Sworn and witnessed."

"Sworn and witnessed." The guard extended a manacled hand, courteous as a dance partner. "Walk with me."

* * *

They took Vesper not to a dungeon but upward, which told Vesper more than any interrogation could have. Down was where you put people whose answers didn't matter yet. Up — up the terraced streets, past shuttered markets, through checkpoints where ordinary soldiers wouldn't look directly at the pale armor — up was where you put people whose answers mattered *immediately*.

The palace rose out of the frozen city like something grown rather than built, black stone furred with frost, its towers sheathed in ice so thick the windows glowed blue-green from inside, like looking into deep water. Vesper had seen it from a distance a dozen times since arriving a century out of place. Up close it was worse. Up close you could hear it — or rather hear what it wasn't. No sound of household. No clatter of kitchens, no dogs, no laughter from the gardens. Just wind moving through the ice sheathing with a low tonal moan, and beneath it, faintly, if you let yourself believe you heard it, singing.

The ache started in Vesper's brand before they passed the outer gate.

It had been dormant for a hundred years — for a lifetime, really, since before Vesper was born, since the god went quiet and the Valley went godless and everyone agreed to stop talking about it. A smooth patch of skin over the sternum, warm as an old scar, meaning nothing. Now it woke the way a limb wakes after sleeping wrong: pins and needles first, then heat, then a deep wrongness, like a door being forced open in a house Vesper had thought empty.

By the time they crossed the inner court, it hurt.

By the time they reached the doors of the Cardinal's wing, it *sang* — a thin answering thread pulled taut between Vesper's chest and whatever was behind those doors, and Vesper understood with cold clarity that this was not an accident. The brand wasn't aching near the palace. The palace was pulling on the brand. Something in there knew exactly what Vesper carried, and wanted it close.

The guards stopped at the doors. The young one — their name, Vesper had learned during the long walk, was Ferren, and Ferren had a habit of apologizing with small courtesies, a steadying hand at an icy step, a cup of water held out without comment — Ferren hesitated.

"I'll say this once, off the record," Ferren murmured. "He will offer you something. He always offers. Refusing him costs nothing he'll admit to and everything he won't. Do you understand me?"

"Not remotely."

"Good." Ferren pushed the doors open. "Neither does he."

* * *

The Regent-Cardinal received prisoners in a room that had clearly once been a chapel and had been renovated within an inch of its life. The altar was gone; in its place stood a desk of black walnut, immaculate, bearing papers squared to angles a surveyor would approve of. The saints' niches along the walls had been filled with ledgers. Ledgers, hundreds of them, spines outward, arranged by some private taxonomy. Where other men kept icons, this man kept records.

He was smaller than Vesper expected. Grey-robed, silver-haired, with a scholar's stoop and a face like a folded map — all creases and faint old lines of travel. His eyes were the problem. They were pale grey, almost white, the color of the winter outside, and they moved over Vesper with an appraiser's patience, cataloguing, pricing.

"Sit," he said, indicating a plain chair. "Or stand. You've been walking for hours; standing is defensible."

Vesper stood.

"I am Cardinal Ossian Vaunt," the old man said, "Regent of the realm, Keeper of the King's Peace, servant of the Throne Eternal." A dry pause. "And great-grandson, four times removed, of High Priest Malachi Vaunt, whom your order murdered."

"We didn't murder anyone," Vesper said. "Your ancestor—"

"—betrayed nothing, stole nothing, and died at the hands of fanatics who then rewrote history to flatter themselves. Yes. I know the version you were raised on." He said it without heat, the way a man corrects a child's arithmetic. "I have read both versions, Ms. Amberdale. I have read *all* the versions. That is rather the point of me." He folded his hands. "Do you know how many chronicles survive from the year of the Silence? Before last month, I mean."

Vesper said nothing.

"Forty-one," the Cardinal continued. "Now fewer. Your friend saw to that. A librarian, burning books — there's a sermon in that, if I believed in sermons." He opened one of the ledgers on his desk, turned it around, slid it across. "I believe in ledgers."

The page was written in a fine clerical hand. It was a list. Names, dates, locations. Vesper found their own name a third of the way down, followed by a notation in the same tidy script: *Valley-born. Oath intact — anomalous. Retain.*

"'Retain,'" Vesper read aloud.

"An old instruction. Written a hundred years ago, by my predecessor's predecessor's predecessor, who inherited it from the man himself. My family has been retaining things for a century, Ms. Amberdale. Names. Pages. People. We are very good at waiting." The pale eyes lifted. "Which brings me to why you're here, and why you're sitting in a warm room instead of a cold cell. I'm going to offer you a trade. I want you to listen to all of it before you refuse, because refusals given early are cheap, and I find cheap refusals insulting to us both."

Vesper leaned against the wall. Deliberately. Let him watch them get comfortable. "Say your piece."

"The Silent One is dead." The Cardinal spoke the words gently, almost kindly. "Not sleeping. Not hiding. Dead, and has been for a hundred years, and everything your order has endured since — the hunts, the purges, the shame — has been grief curdled into superstition. I am prepared to end that. Publicly. Formally. The Church will declare the Silence final, the Sworn dissolved with honors, every surviving member pardoned, pensioned, *named*. No more heresy. No more hunting. Your people come in from the cold, Ms. Amberdale. All of them. Mother Cassia's little flock in the sewers gets to grow old."

He watched Vesper the way snakes watch warmth.

"In exchange," he said, "you renounce. Here, now, in writing, in your own hand. Renounce the oath, renounce the brand, renounce the fiction of a god who abandoned you. One page. And the war ends."

The silence stretched. Outside, wind worried the ice on the windows, and under it — so faint Vesper might have invented it — the king's song threaded through the stones.

*Hymn,* Vesper thought. *Our hymn. Sung wrong.*

"You want my renunciation," Vesper said slowly, "as a document. As evidence. As a trophy."

"I want it as *proof*," the Cardinal corrected. "Proof that the last living Sworn of the Quiet Choir, born in the Godless Valley itself, branded by the rite and faithful since childhood, looked at the truth and chose the truth. When your people read that page — and they will read it; grief makes readers of everyone eventually — they will finally allow themselves to stop waiting for a voice that is never coming." Something moved behind the pale eyes, and it took Vesper a moment to identify it, because it was so wildly out of place on that folded-map face.

It was longing. Terrible, exhausted longing.

"Do you know what it costs," the Cardinal said quietly, "to keep a lie alive for four generations? To raise sons knowing they'll inherit a vigil? To burn archives and call it mercy? I am the fourth Vaunt to hold this office, and I am *tired*, Ms. Amberdale. Give me one page of ink and I will let the whole rotten century lie down."

For one dangerous moment, Vesper almost pitied him.

Then the ache in their brand flared — hot, insistent, *pointing* — and Vesper thought about a god that had chosen silence instead of screams, that had let its own faithful call it coward and corpse rather than tell them the thing that would break them, that had swallowed a century of horror to keep one warning intact: *it wears the crown, it eats what loves it, run.*

A god that loyal deserved better than a signature.

"No," Vesper said.

The Cardinal sighed, and the longing folded away like a map being closed, and what remained was just a tired old clerk facing a discrepancy in his accounts.

"I did ask you to hear all of it."

"I did. It was generous. It was even sincere, mostly. And it was built on a lie, so it doesn't matter how generous it was." Vesper straightened off the wall. "Your ancestor didn't die a martyr and my god didn't die at all. I've read pages you razored out of forty-one chronicles, Cardinal. I know what's wearing the king. I know what the winter *is*. And I know why your family has been 'retaining' names for a hundred years — because the thing your great-grandsire became needs feeding, and needs covering, and needs people like you to keep the ledgers while it sings hymns backwards through the walls."

The temperature of the room changed. Vesper couldn't have said how — no visible movement, no gesture — but the air went thin and cold, and the frost on the window glass grew, visibly, fern-patterns racing across it, and the Cardinal did not move at all.

"There it is," he said softly. "The wolf at the choir door. I wondered how long the animal in you would stay leashed." He rose, unhurried, and came around the desk. Up close he smelled of ink and camphor and, underneath, something older and sweeter that made Vesper's brand recoil like a hand from a stove. "Let me tell you what happens now, since negotiation has concluded. In nine days, the realm celebrates the Feast of the Long Night, and the king appears before his people for the first time since autumn, and the crown is renewed, and the winter becomes permanent, and none of it requires your cooperation. What your cooperation would have purchased was mercy. For you. For your archivist. For Mother Cassia's ragged congregation."

"You don't have her."

"No," the Cardinal agreed. "She is annoyingly difficult to hold. Which is why I am pleased to settle for collateral of higher quality." He produced a stylus and a small slate, wrote three characters on it with a clerk's economy, and held it up.

On the slate, glowing faintly like frost forming in fast motion, was the shape of Vesper's own brand.

"Nine days," the Cardinal said. "The altar is hungry, and it has learned to prefer oath-flesh. Yours is the oldest intact oath in the realm, Ms. Amberdale. Do you understand what that means? When the crown renews itself, it will drink you the way a drunkard drinks the first cup of the evening — and the Silent One's last thread will snap, and even if some miracle frees the king, the god dies *this time*, truly, finally, unmourned." He set the slate down. "Unless, of course, someone rescues you. Someone clever. Someone with a talent for finding things in archives." The pale eyes glittered. "I do hope your friend comes. I've kept a cell ready for weeks."

Guards opened the doors behind Vesper. The brand screamed, pulled, sang toward whatever lay deeper in the palace — toward the throne, the crown, the hunger wearing a hymn.

Ferren was waiting in the corridor, and would not meet Vesper's eyes.

"Nine days," Vesper said to the Cardinal, not turning around. "Long enough."

"For what?"

"For my archivist to read everything." Vesper smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and they were glad the man couldn't see it. "You really should have burned more books."

The doors closed. The frost kept growing. And far below the palace, in the dark under the ice, something ancient turned its attention — patient, starving, and suddenly very interested — toward the sound of an oath that refused, even now, to break.
