## 10. The Library Burns

The smoke reached the archive before the fire did.

Sable smelled it first as a thread of something sweet and wrong threading through the familiar smell of vellum, dust, and lamp oil — the smell of a book burning where it shouldn't be. They looked up from the mutilated chronicle spread across their worktable, quill still in hand, ink still wet on the transcription they'd been making of the razored margins, and thought, absurdly: someone has left a brazier unattended.

Then the junior librarian came running down the aisle between the stacks, skirts hiked in her fists, and Sable understood.

"Tobin," Sable said. "Tobin — what is it?"

"Men." Tobin Ashgrave was nineteen, freckled, chronically overworked, and currently the color of tallow. "In the lower gallery. Gray cloaks. They had a warrant — the Regent's seal — they said it was an inspection of restricted holdings, and then one of them struck a flint against the *Canticles of Merren* like he was lighting his pipe—"

"*The Canticles*?" Sable was already moving, sweeping the chronicle off the table and into their satchel with the reflexes of a person who had spent a lifetime rescuing books from leaks and damp and mice. "*That's a first-edition*—"

"Sable." Tobin caught their sleeve. Her hand was shaking. "There are nine of them. They're not inspecting anything. They're going stack to stack."

Outside, beyond the archive's tall leaded windows, the early dark of the false winter pressed close, snow falling in fat silent flakes that never seemed to land properly — that fell, Sable had noticed lately, slightly wrong, as though the sky itself were unsure what weather was supposed to do. Somewhere across the city a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The alarm.

"They'll burn it all," Tobin whispered. "Everything. Every chronicle, every record, every—" She swallowed. "Master Ellery told me once, when I asked why the Silence years were all cut up. He said: *the pages weren't lost, girl. Pages that are lost can be found.*"

"And where did Master Ellery learn that?" Sable asked. "Where is Master Ellery?"

"Gone since morning. He went out before dawn and didn't come back, and then the gray cloaks arrived an hour later, and I think—" Tobin's voice cracked. "I think he told them where we keep things."

*Or he tried not to,* Sable thought, *and they found out anyway.*

Above them, through the floorboards, came the sound of boots. Measured. Unhurried. The boots of men doing a job they expected to finish.

Sable had spent their whole life among books, and there was a doctrine every archivist learned in their first year, older than any oath, drilled deeper than any catechism: **a library does not belong to the people who guard it. It belongs to whoever comes after.** You are not its owner. You are its hands for a while. Your only duty is to pass it on.

They looked at Tobin, who was watching them with the terrible hope of the young — the belief that the older person in the room knows what to do.

"We're not going to fight them," Sable said quietly. "We're going to be faster than them. That's all. That's the whole plan."

"It's a terrible plan."

"Yes. Come on."

* * *

The restricted gallery sat behind a door that most of the archive's staff believed was bricked shut, because Master Ellery had told everyone so for forty years, including, presumably, the gray cloaks. It was not bricked shut. It was behind the map case of the northern provinces, which swung outward on a counterweight if you lifted the case's brass meridian ring and turned it a quarter-turn east — knowledge Sable now understood had been passed to them not as trivia but as inheritance, the way you'd pass a child to a lifeboat.

Inside: three shelves of the true Silence-era material, the things too dangerous even to razore. Master Ellery's own annotations. A bundle of letters tied in ribbon, correspondence between the royal chaplaincy and the crown in the year the god fell silent, sealed with wax stamps Sable recognized from the chronicle margins — the same inverted sun.

And at the back, wrapped in oilcloth, a slim folio that made Sable's breath stop.

It was not the prophecy book. It was a commentary *on* the prophecy book — a scholar's marginalia, generations deep, and on the final leaf, in a hand Sable did not know but whose ink had browned with a century of age, a single paragraph:

*Of the pages removed from the prophet's text: let no archivist seek them in vault or strongroom, for the Regent's line has searched both for a hundred years and found nothing. The prophet herself hid them where only love would look. She sewed them into the winding-sheet of Queen Isolde, who died beloved, that they might lie upon the king's own heart until such time as a reader came who could bear them.*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, lips moving.

*The king's own tomb.*

The royal crypts lay beneath the palace. Beneath the palace where Vesper had been walking toward, alone, four nights ago, saying *I'll be back by morning, don't wait up*, and hadn't come back, and where Sable had been telling themselves ever since that mercenary oaths were sturdy things and Sworn soldiers got captured and escaped all the time, it was practically a hobby—

The door above them boomed.

Not opened. Boomed — struck, hard, from the outside, hard enough to shake dust from the ceiling beams.

"The map case," Tobin breathed. "They found the map case."

Boots crossed the floor above. Voices, low and professional. And then, unmistakably, the smell again — that sweet wrong smell, closer now, and beneath it the crackle of flame catching dry paper, and a sound Sable had heard only once before, in a flooded church cellar as a child: the sound of a room full of books beginning to die. A soft rushing roar, like wind through wheat, like a crowd inhaling.

"They're firing the stacks," Sable said. Their voice sounded very far away to themselves. "They're firing the *stacks*."

"Sable—"

"Take the letters. Take the folio. Move."

They came out of the hidden gallery into heat. The main reading room's far wall was already orange — the great window glass crazing, bulging, running — and down the central aisle the shelves burned in ranks like candles on an altar, flames climbing spines, pages curling open as they blackened, as though the books were trying to read themselves one last time before the end. Smoke rolled along the ceiling in a slow black tide.

And in the doorway at the head of the aisle stood a man in a gray cloak, holding a torch, watching the fire with the mild satisfaction of a gardener surveying a well-kept bed.

He saw them. He did not reach for a weapon. He simply said, in a voice pitched to carry over the roar:

"Archivist Fenwick. The Regent extends his compliments. He notes that you have been reading things that are no longer extant." A thin smile. "Nothing personal. History is being *corrected*."

"You're burning ten thousand volumes," Sable said. "For one page."

"I'm burning ten thousand volumes for *one word*, actually." The man tilted his head. "But you'd know better than anyone how much trouble one word can cause. Do give my regards to your soldier friend, when you see him next. If you see him next."

Behind him, further down the aisle, a second gray cloak turned — and hesitated, because Tobin had stopped running.

She had stopped in front of a fallen shelf, where a burning book had landed open on the stones, and she was staring at it with her hand pressed over her mouth, and Sable realized with a cold drop through the stomach that she wasn't frozen in fear.

She was frozen in *grief*. Nineteen years old, cataloguing since she was twelve, and watching a century of human thought go up like chaff.

The gray cloak raised his torch toward her.

Sable did not decide anything. Later, they would remember it as a gap in time — a blank space between seeing the torch move and finding themselves already across the room, satchel swinging, the heavy brass meridian ring from the map case (they were carrying it; they would realize this much later) connecting solidly with the man's wrist. The torch clattered away. The man swore, staggering. His companion lunged, and Sable grabbed Tobin's collar and *ran*, hauling her down the side aisle toward the loading stair as the shelf behind them went up in a sheet of flame.

"THE STAIRS!" Sable shouted. "TOBIN, THE STAIRS!"

They burst out the loading door into snow and blessed freezing air, coughing, eyes streaming, and behind them the archive's windows lit gold from within, one after another, like a building deciding to become a lantern. Snow hissed on hot stone. Across the square, bells kept ringing. People were coming out into the street to watch, faces upturned, and Sable understood with sick clarity that some of those faces were afraid, and none of them were surprised.

This was what the long winter was for. Not weather. *Erasure.*

Tobin was bleeding from a cut on her temple — a falling shelf strut, maybe, or a thrown torch, she couldn't say — and shaking so hard her teeth clicked. Sable got an arm around her, checked the satchel — the letters, the folio, the chronicle, all there, all safe — and felt absolutely nothing about it, because their mind had snagged on one sentence and wouldn't let go.

*The winding-sheet of Queen Isolde. The king's own tomb.*

Vesper had been taken toward the palace. Vesper might be *under* the palace. And the missing pages — the rite, whatever the rite was, the thing the prophecy demanded — were sewn into a dead queen's shroud directly above—or below—wherever Vesper was.

"Can you walk?" Sable asked Tobin.

"Are you going back in?"

"No." Sable looked up at the burning archive, at the snow falling into the flames and dying with little hisses, at the crowd gathering to watch history be corrected, and felt grief move through them like weather — huge, and cold, and then, strangely, settling into something harder underneath. Something with edges. "I'm done losing things to these people. Come on. We need somewhere the Regent's men won't look."

"Which is where?"

Sable pulled the folio from the satchel, held it under a streetlamp's guttering light, and read aloud, voice steady, the scholar in them rising through the smoke-grief like a spine straightening:

"'Let no archivist seek them in vault or strongroom.'" They met Tobin's eyes. "So we don't look in either. We find the last cells of the Sworn of the Quiet Choir — Mother Cassia's people. Because if anyone alive knows the way into the royal crypts, it's the order that's been hunted underground for a hundred years."

"And if they won't help us?"

Sable looked at the fire one more time. Ten thousand voices, gone. Master Ellery, gone or worse. Vesper, gone into the dark under the palace.

"Then I'll show them the page," Sable said, "that says the wolf at the choir door has a reader standing right beside him. And I'll tell them the book knew I was coming." They shouldered the satchel. "Because it did. Now run."
