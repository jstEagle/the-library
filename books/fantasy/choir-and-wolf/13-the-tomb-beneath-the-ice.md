## 13. The Tomb Beneath the Ice

### VESPER

Cassia's map was drawn on the back of a funeral shroud, which Vesper tried not to think about as they spread it across the floor of the safehouse attic. The ink was faded brown — not the brown of old ink, Sable had said, but the brown of old blood, mixed into the pigment so the map could pass for a burial cloth if it was ever found.

"She hid it in plain sight," Sable murmured, tracing a line with one finger. "The crypts run under the whole palace hill. These galleries here — the old ones — they're not on any surviving plan of the palace. The archive had fragments. I catalogued three of them myself and never understood what I was looking at." They looked up, and in the candlelight their eyes had the particular shine they got when a puzzle resolved. "I was looking at the underside of the world."

"The underside is frozen," Vesper said.

"The underside is *frozen*," Sable agreed, with what Vesper considered an inappropriate amount of delight.

The winter had reached the crypts first. Of course it had. Cold sank; the dead got the weather before the living did. Cassia's map showed two ways down: the ceremonial stair from the palace chapel, guarded by men in pale armor, and a sinkhole shaft behind the tanneries where the hill had slumped a generation ago and no one had bothered to repair it. The shaft it was.

They went down at dusk, when the pale guard changed watch and the city's curfew bells drove everyone indoors. Vesper went first, boots finding holds in the iced-over rubble, the rope humming with both their weights. Below them the dark opened like a throat.

Halfway down, Vesper's brand began to ache.

It had been aching for days now, a low toothache of the soul, worse the closer they came to the palace. But here, suspended over a hole full of dead kings, it did something new. It *warmed*. A thin thread of heat, like a coal breathed on, like a hand closing around theirs in the dark.

"Vesper?" Sable's voice, tight, from above.

"It's all right." Vesper's own voice sounded strange to them, thickened. "It's — the oath. It's awake down here."

They descended into a gallery of ice.

Not snow — ice, clear as glass, sheeting the walls of a corridor built for kings. Their candlelight went through it and lit the far side of the stone, and Vesper saw that the walls were carved with a procession of figures, and that the ice had not obscured them but *preserved* them, every face, every fold of robe, sharp as the day the chisel left them. A choir of the dead, marching toward a door.

"The original crypts," Sable breathed. "This is pre-Aldric. This is — Vesper, this is *centuries* older than the palace above us. They built the palace on top of a holy site and forgot it."

"Kings are good at forgetting," Vesper said, and touched the wall.

The ice was warm.

Not the brand-warmth of their own skin — the ice itself, under their glove, faintly, impossibly warm, like a body that had only lately gone cold. And through the contact, faint as a word spoken in another room, something reached for them.

*— the wolf —*

Vesper snatched their hand back. "Sable."

"I felt it." Sable had gone pale. "The whole wall just — did you hear—"

"The god." Vesper's heart was hammering somewhere up around their teeth. "It's not just in the crown. It's soaked into the hill. It's been bleeding down here for a hundred years."

They stood in the ice-lit corridor, two small warm things in a throat of frozen stone, and Vesper understood that they had not come down into a tomb.

They had come down into a wound.

* * *

### SABLE

Sable had spent their whole life believing that knowledge was the safest thing in the world. A fact, properly recorded, could not burn. A page, properly catalogued, could not lie. The archive was a fortress and Sable its garrison, and nothing in it had ever reached out and *touched* them.

The wall had touched them. The wall had said *the wolf*, in a voice like a bell rung underwater, and every certainty Sable owned had come loose in their chest like teeth.

They kept walking. That was the thing about dread — you could walk through it if you had a map, and Sable had a map, and a lantern, and a person in front of them whose shoulders were set like a fortress gate. Vesper walked the way Sable wished they could feel: certain. As if the dark were a formality.

The gallery opened, and opened again, and then the ceiling rose and the candle could not find the top of the room, and Sable understood they had entered the great crypt proper — the old one, the true one, the one the palace had been built to bury.

Tombs lined the walls in tiers, but not the marble monuments of the upper royal crypt. These were stone sarcophagi, plain, each carved with a single symbol Sable knew from a hundred fragmentary chronicles: a closed mouth. The sigil of the Quiet Choir, a century before the Choir was ever hunted.

"They buried their dead here on purpose," Sable said. "Close to the god. This was a *sanctuary*. The hill was holy before there was ever a palace on it." Their scholar's mind was running ahead of their fear now, laying rails into the dark. "Which means the crown-thing didn't just kill the god. It moved into the god's *house*. It's been sleeping in the bed of the thing it ate."

"Charming," Vesper said. "Which way?"

Sable checked the shroud-map against the tiers of tombs. Cassia's ink showed a spiral, descending, and at the center of the spiral a chamber marked with two symbols: the closed mouth, and beside it, in different ink, newer ink, a small drawn star.

The star was the mark the Sworn used for *hope*.

"Down," Sable said. "To the center."

The spiral took them past a hundred closed mouths. The cold deepened but the strange warmth did not fade — it pooled, it *thickened*, until Sable could feel it against their face like standing near an oven, a heat with no source, a heat that was somehow colder than the ice around it because it was the heat of something *alive* in a place where nothing should be.

At the center of the spiral, a door.

It was not grand. It was a plain stone slab, and it stood ajar — a hand's width of darkness between it and the frame, as if someone had gone through a hundred years ago and never bothered to close it behind them.

Or as if someone had left it open *on purpose*. Waiting.

"Prophecy," Sable said quietly. "The book pulled us here. Maybe it's been pulling here longer than it pulled us."

They went through.

* * *

### VESPER

The chamber beyond the door was small, round, and warm — truly warm, the first true warmth Vesper had felt since the valley, and the shock of it nearly buckled their knees. It was the warmth of a hearth in a house you'd been locked out of for a century.

In the center of the chamber lay a sarcophagus, and on it, carved deep and worn smooth by hands, was the closed mouth — but this one had been *opened*. Someone had chiseled the lips apart, long ago. Beneath the parted lips, in letters Vesper's body knew before their mind caught up, ran an inscription in the old liturgical script of the Sworn:

*HERE SLEEPS THE ONE WHO LISTENS. SPEAK, AND BE ANSWERED.*

Vesper's brand was singing now. Not aching — singing, a note held so long and so low it had become a kind of silence of its own, and Vesper stood before the opened-mouth sigil and understood that they were standing in the god's grave.

Not the crown. The crown was a theft. This was the *grave* — the place the god had been laid down when it chose silence, the place its body had been left while the thing that ate it wore its voice like a stolen coat.

"Sable," they said. "The queen."

Sable was already at the sarcophagus, lantern raised. The lid was stone, and it was heavy, and it was also — Vesper saw it at the same moment Sable did — *cracked*. A seam ran its length, old and deliberate, mortared over and mortared again, the way you'd seal something you meant to open only once, at great need, by people who knew they might not live to need it.

"It's not a queen in here," Sable said. Their voice had gone very quiet. "Vesper. It was never a queen in here. Look at the shroud-map."

Vesper looked. In Cassia's newer ink, the star at the center of the spiral had a label, small and fierce, in the Mother's own hand:

*THE FIRST SWORN LIES HERE. THE PAGES SLEEP WITH HER. TAKE THEM AND FINISH IT.*

"Not the queen," Vesper said. "The first of the order. The one who wrote the prophecy down before it happened." They set their hands on the cracked lid. "She hid the truth inside the god's own grave because it's the one place the crown-thing would never look. You don't ransack the corpse you're digesting."

"Are we—" Sable hesitated. "Is it all right? To open it?"

Vesper thought about the warm ice in the corridor. About the voice like a bell underwater. About a god that had gone silent for a hundred years and left one warm thread of itself trailing through the dark, waiting for someone to follow it down.

"She's been waiting," Vesper said. "Both of them have. The god and its first follower. A hundred years, holding the door open." They looked at Sable, and something in their chest went very still and very clear. "We're not grave-robbers, love. We're the answer to a letter."

Together they broke the seal.

* * *

### SABLE

The lid came away with a sound like a held breath released, and the warmth in the chamber *rose*, a tide of it, and Sable had to grip the sarcophagus edge to stay upright.

Inside lay a woman. A century dead and uncorrupted, or near enough — brown as parchment, small, folded with her hands over her breast, and sewn into the shroud that wrapped her, Sable could see the shapes of pages. Dozens of them. Stitched into the cloth in rows, like scales, like feathers, like armor.

"She made herself into a book," Sable whispered. They had read a thousand accounts of hidden texts — buried, burned, memorized and destroyed, smuggled in the soles of shoes. Never this. Never a person choosing to become the archive. "She sewed the prophecy into her own burial shroud. The one thing the crown could never burn, because the crown doesn't come down here. The crown is *afraid* to come down here."

They cut the stitches with a knife Vesper held steady, and the pages came free one by one, soft as moth wings, and Sable read by lantern light with their hands shaking and their mind, for once, perfectly still.

The first pages were the prophecy they knew — the wolf at the choir door, the reader, the crown, the winter. But then came the pages the chronicles had lost, the pages the Regent's razors had hunted through a century of archives, and Sable read the truth in a dead woman's careful hand:

*The rite of unbinding is written here, and I have checked it against every text I trust. It is simple. That is its cruelty.*

*A binding made by a broken oath can only be broken by a broken oath. The priest bound the god with the vow he swore at his own consecration — the vow of service, spoken aloud at the altar, and then betrayed. So the god must be freed by a vow of service, spoken aloud, and then broken — at the crown itself, in the thing's hearing, while the true hymn is sung.*

*It must be a true vow. Not words. The rite will know the difference. The breaker must be someone who meant it, every day of their life, and must break it knowing what it costs, and must break it anyway.*

Sable stopped reading.

"Vesper."

"I can read over your shoulder," Vesper said. "I'm a mercenary, not an idiot."

"Vesper." Sable's voice came out wrong, small. "It has to be an oath. A real one. Spoken aloud and broken at the crown." They looked up. "It has to be yours."

The chamber was silent. The warmth breathed around them like a sleeping animal.

"I know," Vesper said.

"You don't — the cost. The oath is your strength, your healing, your—" Sable heard their own voice climbing and made it stop. Made it flat. An archivist's voice, a voice for facts. "The Sworn who break their vows don't survive it, Vesper. The order's own records say the oath-breakers die within a year, or wish they had."

"The Sworn who broke their vows broke them *falsely*," Vesper said. "Or in despair, or to save their own skins. Read it again. *Knowing what it costs, and breaking it anyway.*" They were quiet a moment. "There's a difference between dropping a sword and giving one up."

"That's not comfort. That's a eulogy."

"It's a plan," Vesper said, and there was that terrible steadiness again, the fortress-gate shoulders, the certainty Sable had envied an hour ago and now wanted to shake apart with both hands. "It's the only plan. The rite accepts any true vow — you told me you'd stand in. I'm telling you no. Not because I don't—" They stopped. Started again, plainer. "Because it's mine. The god chose me before I was born. The least I can do is choose it back."

Sable looked down at the pages in their hands. At the dead woman in the stone bed who had written *that is its cruelty* a hundred years ago and then sewn the truth into her own skin so it would survive to be read by exactly two people, in exactly this room, at exactly this hour of the world.

The prophecy had not trapped them. It had *trusted* them. That was the thing Sable finally understood, standing in the god's grave with the unbinding rite trembling in their fingers. The book hadn't pulled them here to be used. It had pulled them here the way you hand someone a letter — because you believe they'll read it, and do what's right, and grieve what it costs.

"Then we do it right," Sable said. "All of it. The true hymn sung inside the false one — that's the choir. That's you. And the vow broken at the crown — that's you too. And I'll be there with every page she left us, and if the rite so much as *stumbles*, I'm stepping in, and you don't get a vote, because you had your vote and you spent it on being noble."

Vesper looked at them for a long moment. Then they smiled — tired, real, the first true smile since the valley.

"Agreed," Vesper said. "Now help me close the lid. She's earned her sleep."

They were lowering the stone back into place — the warmth settling, the brand-song softening to a hum — when Sable felt the pages shift in their arms. One leaf, loose, that had been stitched deeper than the rest, at the dead woman's heart. Sable unfolded it by lantern light and went cold in the middle of all that warmth.

It was not prophecy. It was a letter. Addressed, in a firm and ancient hand:

*To the reader who comes after —*

*One thing more. The crown can be broken. But the thing inside it was a man once, and a man it remains, in the place it keeps its name. The god cannot unmake what it loved. Only a living voice, speaking the priest's true name at the moment of the breaking, can give him leave to let go.*

*I did not know his name. I was not there. Find it, or the crown will crack and the thing inside will simply find another.*

*— the first of the Sworn*

Sable looked up at Vesper, and the lantern light seemed suddenly very small.

"We need his name," Sable said. "The priest's true name. And I know exactly one place left in this city where a name like that might still exist." They swallowed. "The Regent-Cardinal's private chapel. Where the consecration records are kept. Where the *family* records are kept."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone and ice, the palace bells began to ring — not the hour, not the curfew.

The coronation peal. The hollow king would be crowned in three days.

"We have three days," Vesper said, "to steal a name from the man who's wearing it."
