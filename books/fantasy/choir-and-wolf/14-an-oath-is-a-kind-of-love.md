## 14. An Oath Is a Kind of Love

The crypt stairs went down further than any crypt had a right to.

We'd counted two hundred steps when the cold stopped feeling like cold and started feeling like a presence — the kind of cold that watches you. The queen's shroud was behind us now, folded into Sable's satchel along with the missing pages, the ink still faintly warm, as if the words had been waiting a century for someone to touch them. Behind us, too, was the tomb's long silence, the kind of silence that isn't empty.

Ahead was the door. Above the door, the city. Above the city, the crown.

I sat down on the steps because my legs decided the matter for me, and because I wanted one more minute of being underground, where nothing could hear what I was about to say.

Sable sat beside me without asking why. That's the thing about archivists. They know when to stop reading and just be present in the room.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Sable said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you go quiet and start rehearsing something, and your jaw does that little clench, like you're chewing on a sentence that keeps fighting back." Sable pulled their coat tighter. In the grey light leaking up from below, their face looked carved out of paper. "I've catalogued enough of you by now, Vesper. Talk."

So I talked.

"When I was nine years old," I said, "Mother Cassia put her hand on my chest and asked me if I was willing to be a door. That's what the oath is. Not a sword, not a shield. A door. The god pours through you when it wants, and you hold the frame. I said yes. I was nine, and I said yes, and it was the truest thing I've ever done."

Sable said nothing. Waiting. Archivists.

"The rite needs a broken vow," I went on. "A willing one, spoken aloud at the crown, with the whole truth of it. Fine. I can do that part. I've broken bones for less. But here's what the pages don't say in so many words, because whoever wrote them was either a coward or kind." I turned my hand over. The oath-brand sat in the meat of my palm like a coin pressed too deep, the sigil of the Quiet Choir, cold as it had been for a hundred years of silence. "A door that's been holding up a house for thirty years doesn't get to just walk away. You pull it out, the house comes down with it."

"The house being you."

"The house being everything I am. The strength — that's the god's, loaned through the oath. The healing — same. You've seen me take a blade in the shoulder and be drilling the next morning. That wasn't toughness. That was borrowed grace, and when I break the vow, the loan gets called in." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Cassia used to say the Sworn don't retire. They just get smaller."

Sable's hand found mine. Not the branded palm — the other one, the ordinary one. They held it like it was a first edition. Like it was the last copy of something.

"How much smaller?" they asked.

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. Maybe I lose the edge off my sword arm. Maybe I lose the arm. Maybe I lose—" I stopped, because the next word was *myself*, and saying it out loud made it a fact instead of a fear.

"Say it," Sable said gently. "I'll write it down later. Facts are safer once they're written down. That's the whole trick of my profession."

"Maybe I lose myself. The parts of me that are Sworn. Which — Sable, I've been Sworn longer than I've been anything. Longer than I've been a mercenary. Longer than I've been a friend, a lover, a person with a favorite kind of honey wine. It's not my job. It's the shape of me. And I'm going to crack that shape open on the steps of a palace, in front of a hollow king and a thing that eats gods, and I don't know what's left standing when the pieces stop moving."

The cold breathed around us. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone and frost, the city went on being afraid.

* * *

Sable was quiet for a long time. I know their silences now. There's the silence of a person thinking, the silence of a person hurt, and the silence of a person assembling an argument with terrible precision. This was the third kind.

"The rite accepts any true vow," they said at last. "That's what the shroud pages say. *Any vow freely given and truly held.* It doesn't have to be yours."

I knew where this was going. I'd known since the tomb, since Sable read that line twice and their eyes did the thing they do when they've found a door in a wall everyone else thought was solid.

"No," I said.

"You didn't let me finish."

"You're going to offer to stand in. You're going to say the rite would take your vow instead — your vow to the archive, or to truth, or to me, whatever you've got that's true and heavy enough. And it would work, because the rite doesn't care about swords and brands, it cares about love, and yours is—" I stopped. Started again. "It would work. And then the cost lands on you, and you don't know what you'd lose, and I do know, because I just spent five minutes telling you, and I am not going to sit here and watch you pay my bill."

"Vesper."

"No."

"Vesper Amberdale, mercenary of the Godless Valley, let me finish one sentence." Their voice had that edge it got in the archive, the one that made junior librarians stand up straight. "You've spent your whole life being the door. The shield. The one who walks in front. I have watched you take three wounds in the last two weeks that were meant for me, and you did it without asking, because that's what you are. But this isn't a blade. This is a bill, and it's addressed to you, and I am asking you — once, plainly — to let someone else open the envelope."

"The rite needs a vow of equivalent weight," I said. "Mine's thirty years old. It's been fed on every day of my life. What's yours?"

Sable looked at me. Really looked, the way they look at a page they're about to memorize.

"You," they said. "Obviously. You absolute disaster of a person. My vow is you."

* * *

I want to tell you I handled that well. I want to tell you I said something worthy of the moment, something a ballad could use.

What I actually did was stop breathing for about four seconds, and then say, "That's not — vows have to be formal, there are rules—"

"I've read the rules," Sable said. "I've read every rule ever written down in this kingdom, including several that were supposed to be lost. A vow is a sentence said with your whole soul behind it. That's it. That's the entire technology. Everything else is ceremony, and ceremony is just fear wearing nice clothes." Their thumb moved over my knuckles. "I could stand on these steps right now and vow my life, my work, my truth, and my love to the unbinding of the crown, and the rite would take it, and it would hurt, and I would survive it, because I am the person who carried four hundred books out of a burning building one armful at a time and I do not put down what matters."

"And what if it costs you the archive?" I said. "Your memory? The thing you love second-most?"

"Second-most," Sable repeated, and something in their face went soft and dangerous at the same time. "You've been keeping count of your own losses this whole descent. Have you once counted mine? Because here's mine, plainly: if you break that vow and it hollows you out into someone I don't recognize, that's a cost I pay too. Every day, forever. If you die on those palace steps because you went in weakened and alone, that's a cost I pay. If you survive but you're smaller — if the man who dances badly at fairs and hums the true hymn under his breath and looks at me like I'm the last warm thing in the world — if he gets subtracted from the universe, then the rite took *me*, Vesper. It just did it through you."

The cold pressed in. Down in the dark below us, something that might have been the god shifted in its sleep, and the temperature dropped by a degree, and I felt my brand ache in sympathy like a dog hearing a whistle only it could hear.

"That's not fair," I said.

"No," Sable agreed. "It's not. Love is spectacularly unfair. It's the least fair thing ever invented, and it's the only thing down here that isn't frozen." They turned to face me fully, knees scraping stone. "So let's be unfair together, instead of taking turns being unfair at each other. Which is, incidentally, what we've been doing since the fair."

* * *

I looked at my palm. The sigil stared back, patient as it had always been. A hundred years of silence, and it had never once stopped being there. Even when the god was being devoured in a crown above us, even now — the door still held. That was the whole tragedy of the Sworn. Our god went quiet and we kept the frame anyway, because an oath isn't a contract. A contract ends when the other party defaults. An oath is what's left when there's nothing left to be loyal *to*, and you're loyal anyway.

And that, I realized, sitting on a frozen step under a dead queen's house, was exactly why it had to be mine to break.

"Sable." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "If I let you stand in — if I let the rite take your vow instead of mine — what am I? I'm a man who handed his one great act to someone else because he was afraid of the price. I'd still be standing. I'd probably still have my sword arm. And I would spend the rest of my life knowing that when the moment came, I flinched and let the person I love most in the world bleed for me."

"That's not how I'd remember it."

"I know. That's the worst part. You'd remember it as love. I'd remember it as cowardice, and I'd be right, and you'd be too kind to say so, and we'd both know, every morning, over tea, forever." I closed my hand over the brand. "Cassia told me once — she was drunk, which was rare, and honest, which was not — she said the day would come when the Choir would need someone willing to be unmade. She said she'd spent her whole life hoping it wouldn't be her, and her whole life being ashamed of hoping that. I'm not ashamed. I've thought about it every day since the tomb, and I'm not ashamed, because it turns out I *want* this."

Sable went very still.

"I want to be the one," I said. "Not because I'm brave. Because it's mine. The god chose me when I was nine and I've been its door for thirty years, and if the door has to come off its hinges to let the god out of that crown, then by every bone in my body, it's *my* hinges. You can't have them. I love you too much to let you have them."

The words hung there in the cold, and I realized I'd never actually said it. All this time — the fair, the book, the burning library, the dungeons, the dead mother who died so we could walk out of a palace together — I'd never once said the plain word. I'd said *you matter to me*. I'd said *stay close*. I'd said everything except the word, because mercenaries learn early that the plain words are the ones that get people killed.

"I love you," I said again, because it got easier, because it was true, because saying it felt like setting down a pack I'd carried up two hundred stairs. "That's why it has to be me. That's the whole argument. That's all of it."

* * *

Sable didn't argue. That frightened me more than any argument would have.

They were quiet for a long moment, and then they laughed — a small, wet, broken laugh — and pressed their forehead against my shoulder, right over the old scar from the Vellum Marsh contract, and stayed there.

"You're going to make me watch," they said into my coat. "You're going to stand up in front of that thing and break yourself open, and I'm going to have to stand there and watch, and the only thing I'll be allowed to do is read the counter-hymn."

"That's not nothing," I said. "The pages said the rite needs a witness who holds the true words. Without the hymn, the breaking is just a wound. With it, it's a key. You're not watching, Sable. You're the other half of it. You've been the other half since the fair."

They lifted their head. Their eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely ferocious.

"Conditions," they said.

"Conditions."

"One: you come back. Not mostly back. Not enough-back-to-walk. Back. I don't care what you have to be afterward — smaller, weaker, a farmer, a bad poet — you come back to me. Two: we find out together what's left. No hiding it. No pretending the sword arm is fine when it isn't. I've spent my career around people who lie to themselves in the margins, and I will not be married—" they caught themselves, ears going red, and plowed on, "—*partnered* to a margin-lie. Three: when this is over, you take me back to the fair, and you dance with me, and you don't get to say you've forgotten how."

"That's four conditions."

"The fourth one was free."

I laughed. It hurt, and it helped. I took their hand — the branded one, this time, pressing the cold sigil against their warm palm — and I said, "Agreed. All of it."

Above us, through two hundred steps of stone and ice, a sound began. Faint. Distant. Wrong.

Singing.

The whole city's worth of it, or something wearing the city's throat — the inverted hymn rising out of the palace like smoke from a chimney, and underneath it, a bell tolling in a pattern I didn't recognize but Sable clearly did, because they went white.

"That's the Coronation Bell," they whispered. "It hasn't rung in a hundred years. It only rings for one thing." They were already on their feet, hauling the satchel of shroud-pages up from the steps. "Vesper — they're not waiting for us. The Cardinal's crowning him. *Tomorrow.*"

I looked up the stairs, toward the sound, toward the crown, toward the bill with my name on it.

"Then we'd better not be late," I said, "to my own unmaking."
