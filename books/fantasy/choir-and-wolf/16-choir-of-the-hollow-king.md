## 16. Choir of the Hollow King

They took my boots at the gate.

I should have expected it. The Pale Guard had been conscripting musicians for a week by then — going door to door through the singers' quarter, dragging out anyone with a voice and a pulse, because the coronation of a hollow king apparently required a choir of the living. Sable had warned me. Sable had a plan for everything, and the plan had been: *don't get conscripted.*

The plan had not survived contact with a sergeant who heard me humming while I waited in the bread line.

"Name," he said, not looking up from his list.

"Amberdale. Vesper."

"Sing something."

I sang something. Badly, on purpose — a tavern song, flat and slurred. He looked up. He had the flat grey eyes of a man who had been told what to hear and had stopped hearing anything else.

"You dropped your accent on the third note. Again."

So I sang it again, properly, and he wrote my name down, and that was how I ended up in the palace of the thing that had eaten my god, wearing a borrowed surcoat of white wool, carrying nothing but my own voice and a memory I'd been trying not to think about for three days.

They took my boots because boots have soles, and soles can have things hidden in them. Knives, mostly. I hadn't had a knife in two weeks anyway, not since the dungeons, not since Cassia. But the guard didn't know that, and I let them take the boots like a man relieved to be rid of them, and I thought: *Cassia would have laughed at this. Cassia would have said, girl, you're the weapon, they can't confiscate you.*

Cassia was dead. Her diversion had bought Sable and me out of the cells and into the burning streets, and the last I'd seen of her was her sword-arm rising over a wall of pale armor, and the last thing she'd said to me was a map pressed into my hand and four words: *Finish the song, wolf.*

I was here to finish the song.

* * *

They housed the conscripted choir in the old chapel undercroft, a long low room with a vaulted ceiling and a brazier that did nothing against the cold. Forty of us, maybe fifty. Most were city singers — cathedral trained, opera trained, the kind of people who could read a line of music the way Sable read a chronicle. A few were like me: pulled off the street, conscripts of convenience, bodies to fill the back rows.

Nobody talked much. You could hear the palace above us, if you listened. All day it creaked and settled like a ship, and at night — I'd heard it before, from the streets, from the rooftops during the escape — the king sang.

The choir mistress was a small iron woman named Delyse who had clearly been singing in this palace since before the winter began and had decided, with the pragmatism of the very old, that the winter was not her problem. Her problem was that fifty voices had to be note-perfect in six days, and half of them couldn't read music.

"You." She pointed at me on the first morning. "Back row. You're a bass?"

"Whatever you need."

"Nobody is whatever I need. Bass. Stand there."

She put a sheaf of pages in front of me. The inverted hymn, written out in careful black notation, the melody crawling downward where it should have climbed, the intervals wrong in the specific, deliberate way I'd heard through the palace walls — wrong the way a face is wrong when the smile doesn't reach the eyes.

I looked at the notes and my stomach turned over.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about an oath sworn to a silent god: the god doesn't have to speak to teach you things. The Sworn of the Quiet Choir learned their faith in hymns, and the hymns were taught to children before they could read, before they could understand, sunk into the body the way water sinks into ground. I hadn't sung the true version of this melody since I was seven years old. I had spent my whole adult life in the Godless Valley, where magic couldn't follow and neither, I'd assumed, could the music.

But the music had followed. It had been waiting in my throat the whole time, patient as a god.

Delyse rapped her baton on a chair. "From the top. And if any of you sing it *correctly*—" her voice dropped, and the room went very still, "—the Guard will hear it. Everyone in this palace hears everything. Sing what's written."

We sang what was written.

* * *

The strange thing about singing a hymn inside out is that your body fights it.

I discovered this in the first hour. The inverted melody wanted to resolve downward into its wrong cadences, and every muscle in my chest and throat pulled the other way, toward the true line, the way a hand pulls back from a hot stove. I had to hold the false notes the way you hold a lie in your mouth — consciously, deliberately, with a taste like copper. Around me, the trained singers had no such trouble. They didn't know the true hymn. To them it was just music, difficult and cold and beautiful in a way that made the hair on my arms stand up.

That was the horror of it, I suppose. It *was* beautiful. The thing in the crown had taken a hymn of comfort and turned it into something gorgeous and hungry, and the city had been listening to it through the palace walls all winter, and some part of me understood why the riots hadn't stopped it, why people knelt in the freezing streets at night just to listen. You don't walk away from a voice like that. You follow it down.

"Your pitch is drifting," Delyse said, materializing at my elbow.

"Sorry."

"You're fighting the line." Her eyes were sharp and black and very, very old. "Every conscript fights the line for the first two days. Then they stop."

"What happens when they stop?"

She looked at me for a long moment. "They sleep well," she said, and moved on.

I didn't sleep well. I lay on my pallet in the undercroft and listened to the king singing above me, and I mapped the two melodies against each other in the dark — the false one on the page, the true one in my blood — and I began, slowly, to see what Cassia's maps and Sable's rescued pages had been pointing at all along.

The inverted hymn wasn't just a corruption. It was a *cage*. Every phrase of the true melody had been turned into a bar of the binding, the way you can turn a key by bending it. Which meant the shape of the true melody was still there, underneath, the way a broken bone remembers being whole. And a broken thing that remembers being whole can be set.

You just had to sing it right, at the right moment, in the right place.

The coronation. The renewal. Sable's cold furious voice in my memory: *the binding is strongest then — and weakest, if interrupted.* Strongest because the whole city would be singing along, fifty voices in this undercroft and ten thousand outside, all of them pouring their breath into the cage. Weakest because a cage that full of song has no room left for anything else.

If the true melody rose inside the false one at the moment of crowning — not replacing it, not fighting it, but *hidden inside it*, the way a river hides under ice —

I lay in the dark and my heart hammered and I thought: *I can't do this alone. I need fifty voices. I need a choir.*

I had a choir. I just had to teach it treason in six days without a single one of them noticing.

* * *

I started small. That's the only advice I'd give anyone planning heresy: start small, start deniable, start with things that could be mistakes.

A breath placed a half-beat early. A vowel opened slightly wider than written, so the note rang instead of sitting. Little sins of technique, the kind a choir mistress corrects a hundred times a season. Delyse corrected mine twice, then frowned, then stopped frowning, because here was the thing about my little sins: they made the music *better*. The false hymn was built to be sung dead, flat, obedient — a cage-note has no life in it, no overtones, nothing that escapes. When I warmed a phrase, when I let a note bloom, it didn't sound wrong. It sounded like the difference between a painted fire and a real one.

The tenor next to me — a cathedral man named Oren with a voice like dark honey and a face like a worried horse — leaned over on the third day.

"You're doing something," he whispered, during the break.

"I'm singing."

"You're singing *around* it. Like — you leave a door open in every phrase and don't walk through it." His hands moved, shaping the air. "I've sung for forty years. I know an open door when I hear one. Where does it go?"

"Somewhere warm," I said.

He looked at me for a long time. Outside, the wind was doing its work on the city; you could hear the ice groaning in the river, and somewhere above us the king's voice threaded down through the stones, singing to itself, singing to its meal. Oren's wife had been taken by the Pale Guard in the first week of the riots — everyone in the undercroft knew it, everyone knew everyone's griefs by now, we were fifty strangers welded into a congregation by fear.

"Show me the door," he said.

* * *

That's how it went, then. One singer at a time, one phrase at a time, in the language musicians have used to pass forbidden things to each other for as long as there have been forbidden things: a fingering, a breath, a placement, a way of leaning on a note that says *this one, this one matters, remember this one.* I never named it. I never sang the true melody outright — I couldn't have, not without the whole room hearing it and the Guard hearing it through the floor, and anyway that wasn't the plan. The plan was subtler. The plan was to hide the true hymn inside the false one the way a seed hides inside fruit: present in every note, invisible until the moment it splits open.

By the fifth day, half the choir was doing it without knowing what they were doing. The hymn had changed. Delyse heard it — of course she heard it, she'd been hearing everything since before I was born — and she stood at the front with her baton lowered and her eyes closed, listening to her corrupted, treasonous, gorgeous choir, and when it ended she said nothing at all.

She just looked at me. And I looked back at her. And she turned to the room and said, "Again. From the top. And this time, *mean it.*"

Whatever that meant to her, it meant what I needed it to mean to us.

* * *

The night before the coronation, I couldn't stay on my pallet.

I climbed the undercroft stair to the chapel above — conscripts were allowed in the nave during waking hours, it was the one mercy of the palace — and I stood in the dark among the pews and looked up at the place where the ceiling should have opened into a rose window. It had been walled over. Mortared shut from the inside, the stones sweating frost. Above it, faint as breath, I could hear the king singing the night-office of the inverted hymn, alone, the way it sang every night, and for the first time I listened to it not as a threat but as a voice.

And underneath the wrongness — underneath the inverted intervals and the hungry cadence — I heard it. Thin as a thread pulled through cloth. The true melody. Not sung. *Remembered.* Coming from somewhere down inside the thing wearing Aldric, from the piece of it that was still a man, still a king, still maybe a boy once who'd been taught the hymn the way I'd been taught it, sunk into the body before he could understand it.

He was in there. Under the glove, under the crown, under a century of being sung through — Aldric was in there, holding the true melody in his mouth the way a drowning man holds air, and every night he leaked a little of it out through the stones, and no one had ever heard it, because no one alive knew what they were listening for.

I pressed my hand flat against the frozen wall.

"I hear you," I said, too quiet for anything but the stone to hear. "Hold on. Tomorrow we sing you out."

The singing above me stopped.

One beat. Two. Three — long enough for my heart to forget its rhythm entirely.

Then it resumed, and this time — I would swear this on anything you care to name, on Cassia's grave, on Sable's life, on my own — this time the thread of the true melody was a little louder. A little closer to the surface. Like a man who has been holding up a ceiling for a hundred years hearing, for the first time, someone on the other side of the wall.

* * *

Sable found me at dawn. That's a whole other story — how an archivist with forged papers, a stolen surcoat, and the palace's own forgotten floor-plans got into the conscripts' chapel dressed as a minor functionary from the Office of Ceremonies, and I'll tell it someday over honey wine, but not now. What matters is that a figure in a grey clerk's robe sat down beside me on a pew at first light, and under the hood, grey eyes found mine, and for the first time in three weeks the cold in my chest went out like a lamp.

"You look terrible," Sable whispered.

"You should see the other guy. There isn't one. I'm the other guy."

"The choir's ready?"

"Half of them know what they're singing. The other half will follow Oren. And the mistress knows and doesn't care, which frightens me more than the Guard does." I kept my voice under the sound of the wind in the walled-over window. "The rite's at midday. The crowning is the third phrase of the hymn — I've marked it. That's when the binding is loudest. That's when we put the true line under it, all of us, and I walk out of the row and say the words."

Sable was quiet for a moment. When they spoke again, their voice had the careful flatness they used when they were about to say something they'd rehearsed.

"The Guard will kill you before you finish the first sentence."

"Probably."

"Vesper—"

"Sable." I turned and took their hands, both of them, cold clerk's hands, ink-stained even now, even disguised, because some things about a person can't be forged. "You read the counter-hymn from the pages. That's your part and it matters and I need you alive to do it. My part is the door. I'm the one with the brand, I'm the one the oath knows, and I'm the one it will cost — and I have spent my whole life being paid in other people's coin. Let me spend my own for once."

They didn't argue. That frightened me more than the Guard did. Sable only ever stopped arguing when they had already found a second plan, and I knew better than to ask what it was.

"Midday," they said finally, and squeezed my hands once, hard, and stood, and became a functionary again, grey and forgettable, walking toward the doors.

At the threshold they paused. Didn't turn around.

"Vesper. When you sing it — the true line, under the false one — I'll be listening for the door."

And they were gone, and the bells above the palace began to toll, and the Guard came down into the undercroft to fetch fifty voices to the crowning of the Hollow King.

I stood in the middle of the column in my borrowed boots, no knife, no magic, no oath I was still sure of keeping — and I hummed, very softly, under my breath, the first three notes of a melody a hundred years dead.

Around me, one by one, the choir began to hum it too.
