## 18. What Wears the Crown

The crown cracked and the king screamed in two voices, and then there was no king at all — just a shape standing where Aldric had knelt, a shape like smoke that had learned to want.

I had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road here. In my imaginings it was always simple: break the binding, cut the crown's hold, done. Nobody tells you what's left over when you pull a parasite out of its host. Nobody warns you that the thing itself will still be standing there, looking at you with a hundred years of appetite behind no face at all.

It was tall. That was the first wrong thing about it. Taller than any man, taller than it had been when it wore Aldric like a glove, as if shedding the body let it remember some older size. It had no face, but it had features the way a fire has features — a suggestion of eyes that were not eyes, a suggestion of a mouth that opened onto cold. The winter came off it in sheets. Where it stood, the marble of the coronation dais frosted white, and the frost spread outward in fingers, reaching for everything warm.

Behind me, the choir was still singing. My counter-song, hidden inside their inverted hymn, hung in the air of the great hall like a blade someone had left unsheathed. The thing flinched from it. Good. It could be hurt. It could be hurt, which meant it could be ended.

"Vesper." Sable's voice, from the edge of the dais, tight with fear and something fiercer underneath. They had the rescued pages clutched to their chest, the ones pulled from the queen's shroud, ink still legible by some mercy of dry stone and dead silk. "The rite worked. The binding is broken. What's left of him is — he's loose."

"I noticed," I said.

* * *

Let me tell you what I had, since it wasn't much.

I had no oath-magic. I had spent it an hour ago on the crypt steps of my own vow, spoken aloud until the brand tore free of my arm like a tooth from the jaw, and what remained in me afterward was exactly what the god had found in me before I ever swore anything: a body trained for violence, a temper that ran long and cold instead of hot, and a stubbornness my old sergeant used to call geological.

I had a knife. Ordinary steel, mercenary issue, the kind you can buy in any valley market for three coppers. It felt strange in my hand now, light as a toy. For ten years my blade had carried a blessing in it, a hum under the grip like a held breath. Now it was just sharp metal, and whatever I did next would be done the way ordinary people do things — badly, slowly, at cost.

And I had Sable. Sable Fenwick, archivist, who had never thrown a punch in their life and was currently the most dangerous person in this hall, because they were holding the counter-hymn — the true melody, the one the Silent One had taught its choir before the priest ate the song out of every throat that knew it. Sable couldn't fight. But they could read. And reading, it turned out, was a weapon too.

"It says—" Sable's voice cracked, steadied. They'd been decoding the shroud-pages all through the ceremony, lips moving, finger tracing lines while the world burned around us. "Vesper, listen. The last page. It's not a spell. It's instructions. *What was taken by song can only be returned by song.* You can't kill it. You have to unmake it. Note by note."

"Unmake it how?"

"By singing back everything it stole."

* * *

The thing moved.

Not walked — moved, the way night moves when clouds pass off the moon, everywhere at once and nowhere you can pin down. One moment it stood on the dais; the next the frost had jumped ten feet toward me and it was inside the frost, a pressure in the cold, and the temperature dropped so fast my breath cracked in my throat.

I had fought mages before. I had fought things in the Godless Valley that had no right to exist, back when the valley's magic-dead soil made even monsters come through weak and starving. But I had never fought something that was mostly hunger wearing a shape.

My training said: don't meet a charge head-on. Give ground at an angle. Make it turn. So I turned, angling along the dais steps, keeping the great pillars between us, and the cold chased me and the thing chased the cold and I put my knife into the frost-fog where its center should have been.

The blade went through like cutting smoke. No resistance. No blood. Just a sound — a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be — a voice, coming out of the wound I'd made. A woman's voice, singing a lullaby in a dialect nobody had spoken in a century.

I staggered back. "Sable—"

"It's the voices." Sable was on their feet now, pages shaking. "The chronicles said — Vesper, the priest didn't just eat the god. He fed it people. Choir members, dissenters, anyone who sang the true hymn or remembered it. Their voices are part of it. That's what it's *made* of. A hundred years of stolen throats."

The lullaby faded. Another voice rose behind it, deeper, a man's, reciting something that might have been a prayer or a confession. Then another. Then a dozen, overlapping, a crowd pressed into one mouth.

And underneath all of them, so faint I almost missed it, a melody. The true hymn. Broken into fragments, scattered across a hundred stolen singers, each one holding a piece they didn't know they had.

"Sable," I said slowly, "the counter-song. How much of it do you have?"

"All of it. The pages have the full melody. Why?"

"Because it has the pieces. We have the whole." I looked at the thing — at the priest-thing, at what had been High Priest Maldrec once, before his soul curdled into this. "We don't kill it. We sing it apart."

* * *

Here is what actually happened, and I want it recorded plainly, because later — if there is a later — people will want to make it glorious, and it was not glorious. It was ugly and slow and I was terrified the entire time.

Sable read. That was the whole plan. Sable stood at the base of the dais with the rescued pages held open against the wind of the thing's cold, and read the counter-hymn aloud, line by line, in a voice that wavered and cracked and kept going anyway. And I fought.

Not to win. Fighting to win against this thing was like fighting a river. I fought to buy seconds — seconds for Sable to reach the next line, the next phrase, the next stolen fragment. Every note Sable sang true, the thing lost a little of its hold on the matching fragment inside itself. I could see it happen. The frost would stutter. One of the inner voices would rise up, suddenly clear, singing along with Sable instead of with the hunger, and the thing's shape would ripple like water disturbed.

So my job was to keep it from stopping Sable.

It tried. Gods, it tried. It sent the cold at Sable in waves, trying to freeze the breath in their lungs, freeze the pages stiff, freeze the tears on their face into masks. I got in the way. I couldn't block cold, but I could burn its attention — charge it, make it spend its will on me, drag its focus away from the small figure with the book. I took a fist of frost across the shoulder that numbed my arm to the elbow for a full minute. I took cold in the knee that made it scream like a hinge. I learned quickly that it didn't want to touch me directly — the last of the broken oath still clung to me somehow, a ghost of warmth, and my skin burned it like a coal burns snow. Small mercy. It cost me nothing but pain, and pain I could pay.

"You're doing it," Sable gasped, between lines. "Vesper, listen — the voices — they're *harmonizing*—"

They were. As Sable worked through the hymn, more and more of the trapped voices caught the melody and held it. A child's voice, high and clear, singing a descant no adult had written. An old man humming the bass line like he'd never stopped. The woman with the lullaby, weaving her tune into the verse as if the hymn had been waiting a century for her part to come home.

The thing began to shrink.

That's the only word for it. It had been tall as two men; now it was tall as one. The frost around its feet pulled inward. Its edges, which had been hard as storm-fronts, went soft, ragged, uncertain. And through the mass of it I started to see them — faces, faint as breath on glass. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Looking out from inside their prison with expressions I wasn't prepared for.

Not rage. Not hunger anymore. Relief. The terrible, disbelieving relief of people waking from a nightmare so long they'd forgotten waking existed.

One of them looked straight at me. A young woman, maybe nineteen, hair cropped short the way choir initiates wore it. Her mouth moved. No sound reached me, but I understood her anyway, the way you understand a person across a loud room: *keep going.*

I kept going.

* * *

There was a moment, near the end, when the thing remembered it had been a man.

The stolen voices fell quiet all at once — Sable had sung them nearly free, and what was left was the core, the original, the priest's own soul, the seed the whole rot had grown from. It stood alone on the ruined dais, small now, man-sized, and for the first time it had a face. An old man's face. Kind, once, maybe, a hundred years ago, before ambition taught it arithmetic with souls.

It looked at me. And it spoke, in a voice like dry paper, and what it said was:

"She promised me I would never die."

I knew who she was. Everyone in the Sworn knew, though we weren't supposed to say it. The Silent One hadn't been devoured by accident. The prophecy pages, Cassia's testimony, the mutilated chronicles — it all pointed the same direction. The god had seen what its high priest was becoming and had gone silent rather than feed him further, and the silence had been a warning, and the warning had come a century too late.

"You were supposed to serve her," I said. My voice came out flat. I was so tired. My arm hung useless. Blood — my own, ordinary, unblessed blood — had soaked through my shirt from somewhere I couldn't remember being cut. "She trusted you with her song."

"And she left." The paper voice cracked. "When I needed her most, she went silent. She abandoned us. She abandoned *me.*" The old face twisted, and beneath the twist I saw it plain: not a monster. A man who had loved something vast and been loved back imperfectly, and had decided that imperfection was theft, and theft justified anything. "I only took what she refused to give."

"No," Sable said quietly. They had come up beside me, pages lowered, because there was nothing left in them to counter. "You took what she *was*. There's a difference. She chose silence to warn us. You chose devouring to keep her. One of those is love, Maldrec. It isn't yours."

The thing that had been the high priest looked at Sable for a long moment. Then at me. Then at the choir — my choir, the conscripted singers who had never stopped singing, who stood in their rows with frost in their hair and the true hymn still glowing in their chests like banked coals.

And it began, very softly, to sing.

The true hymn. Correctly. For the first time in a hundred years, the man who had inverted every syllable of it sang it straight, and his voice was awful and cracked and human, and the stolen voices around him rose to meet it, and the sound filled the great hall the way spring fills a valley — not loudly, but completely.

Then he came apart.

Not violently. That's the thing I need people to understand. He came apart the way fog comes apart at sunrise. The stolen voices lifted out of him one by one, each finding its own note, its own key, its own name — I heard names, in that minute, dozens of names spoken by their owners for the first time in decades, *my name is Ilsette, my name is Corvin, my name is Bren, I had a daughter, I had a garden, I liked the rain* — and each voice rose, and thinned, and went wherever released voices go, and the hall grew warmer with every one that left, measurably, physically warmer, until my frozen knee ached with returning blood and the frost on the windows wept down the glass.

The last to go was Maldrec himself. At the end he was barely a shadow, an old man's outline drawn in cold air, and just before it dissolved he turned to me and said — and I choose to believe this, I will always choose to believe it —

"Tell her I'm sorry. If she wakes. Tell her the choir kept singing."

Then nothing. Warmth. Silence — the good kind, the kind the Silent One had meant all along.

* * *

Afterward, the hall was very quiet.

The choir stood in their rows, unsure whether they were still conscripts. Somewhere beyond the walls, the Regent-Cardinal's pale guard was regrouping or fleeing — I'd deal with whichever it was in a moment, or fail to, and the world would go on either way. The false winter was dying; I could feel it in the air, a loosening, a thaw beginning somewhere far off and moving toward us like good news traveling.

I sat down on the coronation dais, which seemed appropriate, and discovered that my legs had opinions about standing that I should have consulted earlier.

Sable sat next to me. They set the rescued pages down carefully, squared their edges, and then, having preserved history adequately, put their head on my shoulder and shook. Not sobbing. Just shaking, the way you shake after carrying something heavy a long way and finally setting it down.

"We won," Sable said at last, in a small voice. "Didn't we? That was winning? Because it didn't feel like the songs say."

"It never does," I said. "The songs leave out the sitting-down-afterward part."

"A hundred voices, Vesper. Maybe more. All those people, kept in *that*, for a *century*—" Their breath hitched. "And the worst part is I'm happy. I'm sitting in a wrecked cathedral covered in frostbite and I'm *happy*, and it feels obscene."

"It's not obscene. It's the whole point. That's why he hated it so much." I looked at the empty dais, at the cracked crown lying in its own frost-melt like a broken eggshell. "He wanted a god that belonged to him. What we've got is a god that belongs to everyone, which means it belongs to no one, which means it can't be owned, which means it can't be eaten. That's not a weakness, Sable. That's the entire religion."

Sable laughed — wet, exhausted, real. "You should write that down. Someone should. Someone *will*, probably, badly, in fifty years, and I'll spend my whole career correcting them."

"You'll love it."

"I'll love it," they admitted.

I should have rested. Every part of me voted for rest. But there was a thread pulling at me still, faint as the ghost of my old brand — not pain, not command, nothing so strong as an oath ever again. More like a hand tugging a sleeve. Something in the palace below, or beneath it, or *through* it, stirring, the way something stirs in a house when the front door finally opens after a long winter.

And then the light changed.

It came up through the floor. That's the only way I can describe it — not reflected, not cast, but rising, a grey-gold glow seeping through the marble seams of the dais like dawn deciding to arrive indoors. The choir saw it. Sable saw it. The frost on the windows ran faster, and outside, distantly, I heard the city's bells begin to ring — not the alarm pattern, not the curfew pattern, but the old spring pattern, the one nobody had rung in a hundred years because nobody alive remembered what it was for, except apparently the bells did.

"What is that?" Sable whispered.

I knew. Some animal part of me that had carried an oath for ten years knew before my mind caught up, the way you know a footstep in the dark before you can name whose it is.

"That," I said, standing, legs complaining, heart doing something I refuse to call hope because hope is a professional hazard, "is the god. Waking up. And I think — Sable, I think it's *small*."

The light pulsed once, gently, like a breath. Like something newborn, somewhere beneath the ice, opening its eyes and looking — of all the souls in that hall, of all the choir and the guards and the freed dead — looking for one person in particular.

Looking for me.

I picked up my knife, sheathed it, and started toward the stairs leading down, with Sable scrambling up beside me and the whole thawing world ringing like a bell that had finally remembered its note.

"Vesper," Sable said, half-running to keep pace, "what do we do if it doesn't remember us?"

"Then we introduce ourselves," I said. "It's only polite. It's how every faith I respect got started."
