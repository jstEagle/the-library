## 20. The First Follower

The god learned to eat on its fourth day alive.

I know because I was the one who taught it. We were sitting in the ruins of the choir gallery above the palace chapel — the roof had burned through in the Cardinal's last stand, and the morning light came down in slanted gold bars through the gap — and the god, small and grey-eyed and shaped like a child of perhaps seven years, picked up a heel of bread with the gravity of someone defusing a bomb.

"It goes in the mouth," I said.

"I know," it said. "I remember mouths. I made them." It turned the bread over. "But I have never been hungry *in* one."

That stopped me for a second. A century of silence, a lifetime of worship, three weeks of war and ice and blood, and here was the thing all of it had orbited: sitting cross-legged in a burned-out gallery, holding a piece of bread like it was the most interesting object in creation.

Which, to be fair, it might have been. To the god.

"Try it," I said. "Slowly. The crust is the best part."

It bit. Chewed. Went very still, eyes widening, grey irises catching the light like frost catching dawn.

"Oh," it said softly. "Oh, that's what they were praising me for."

And I laughed — actually laughed, first time since before the crypts, maybe since before the fair, since before any of this started — until my ribs hurt and the god watched me with an expression I can only describe as delighted bewilderment, because apparently it remembered laughter from the outside but had never once heard it aimed at itself.

* * *

Let me tell you what it's like to be oathless when you've never been anything else.

The brand is gone. Where it sat on my forearm for twenty-six years — the quiet spiral of the Sworn, ink-dark, warm as banked coals whenever the Silent One paid attention, which wasn't often, but often enough — there is now only smooth skin, pale where the rest of me is weathered. Some mornings I wake up reaching for it the way you reach for a sword that isn't on the peg anymore. My hand finds nothing. My hand finds just *me*.

And here is the thing nobody tells you about losing your god's magic: you don't lose your strength. You lose your *borrowed* strength. What's left is whatever you actually built yourself, brick by brick, drill by drill, scar by scar. Turns out that's less than I had at my peak and more than I expected. Turns out I can still swing a blade for an hour before my arms shake. Turns out my body still remembers every form Mother Cassia ever beat into me, even though she's dead and her maps got us into the tomb and out of it and she never saw the spring she bought.

Turns out Vesper Amberdale, without an oath, is still somebody. I'm still deciding how I feel about that. Ask me again tomorrow.

Sable says I'm grieving. Sable says grief isn't a problem to solve; it's a thing to carry while you keep walking. Sable says a lot of things like that now, with the particular authority of someone who has read everything ever written about loss and then lost something themselves — Cassia, mostly, and the library, and a hundred years of history that burned in one night. They carry it well. Archivists are built for carrying.

Me, I keep waiting for the ache of the brand and feeling nothing where it should be, and some part of me keeps whispering that nothing is exactly what I deserve. You don't break a vow to a god, even a right vow broken for right reasons, without paying something. Maybe this emptiness is the payment. Maybe it lasts forever.

Or maybe — said another voice, quieter, the one that sounds like Sable — maybe it doesn't need to be anything. Maybe it's just room.

* * *

On the fifth day, the god asked me its first real question.

Not "what is bread" or "why does the sun feel different than I remember" or "is it normal for throats to hurt after crying," though it asked plenty of those, and I answered them all with the solemnity they deserved, because when a god asks you why onions exist you do not say "because they do" and walk away. You sit down. You explain onions.

No. On the fifth day it looked up from the fire Sable had built in the gallery hearth — we'd moved out of the palace proper, because neither of us could sleep under that roof yet, not with the real king convalescing two floors below, waking in screams some nights and apologies others — and it asked:

"Why did you stay?"

I fed a stick to the flames. "Stay where?"

"Here. With me. Everyone else who comes near does so kneeling, or bowing, or with their eyes down, or weeping, or asking for things." Its small face did something complicated. "You brought me bread. You showed me how to skip stones. You told me the river was 'being smug' and I still don't understand what that means but I liked hearing it. Why?"

"You saved my city," I said. "Sort of."

"That's a reason for gratitude. Gratitude kneels too."

"Not mine." I looked at it across the fire. Grey eyes. Child's face, mostly — but sometimes, when the light moved wrong, I'd catch something older underneath, something vast looking through a keyhole. "Look. I was bound to you before I could walk. My mother dedicated me. Her mother dedicated her. Every choice I ever made came wrapped around an oath I didn't pick. And then I broke that oath — the biggest piece of it, anyway — to set you free, and it cost—" I gestured vaguely at my own chest, at the empty place. "It cost. And now everyone in the valley wants to start worshiping you again, new temples, new choirs, the Church is already splitting into factions arguing about which of them gets to own you, and you're sitting here eating bread and asking me why I stayed."

"Yes," it said patiently. "Why?"

"Because you're the first god I've met who didn't ask."

Silence. Real silence, the kind I hadn't heard from the divine in a hundred years, and somehow it felt warmer than any answer.

Then the god smiled — a child's smile, crooked, missing nothing but knowing things — and said, "Good. Then let's begin properly." And it held out its hand across the fire, palm open, the way soldiers greet each other and gods almost never do.

"My name," it said, "was the Quiet One, and the Still Voice, and the Listener Between Songs, and eleven thousand other titles I have decided to retire. All of them. I would like to choose a smaller name. But choosing requires learning, and I have forgotten nearly everything worth learning, because the things worth learning are the small ones. Bread. Rivers. Smugness." Its hand stayed open. "So. Teach me. Not as a priest teaches. As a friend teaches. Is that a bargain you'd make, wolf-at-the-choir-door?"

I looked at that open hand for a long time.

Twenty-six years of devotion, and no one had ever asked me for friendship instead of faith. No one had ever offered me a door I could walk through both ways.

I took it. Small hand, warm, grip surprisingly strong.

"Yeah," I said, and my voice cracked somewhere in the middle and I let it. "Yeah, that's a bargain I'll make."

* * *

We fell into a strange routine, those first weeks of spring.

Mornings, I drilled — because an oathless body still needs discipline, and because the god liked to watch, sitting on the training-yard wall with its legs swinging, narrating my forms back to me in a whisper like it was remembering choreography from a hundred years ago. Sometimes it corrected me. Annoyingly, it was usually right. Turns out the god of stillness has opinions about balance.

Afternoons, we walked the thawing city. That was its favorite thing, I think — the melt. Water running in every gutter, icicles letting go of the eaves all day long, the whole capital dripping like a thawing carcass, and the god would stop at every stream and put its fingers in and go quiet with something that wasn't quite joy and wasn't quite grief but lived between them, in the country where a being remembers making winters *real* ones, seasonal ones, honest ones, and knows what it did instead.

People left offerings anyway. Couldn't help themselves. Bread on windowsills, candles in doorways, little grey ribbons tied to fences. The god always touched the offerings gently, and always gave them away again — handed the bread to whoever looked hungriest, carried the candle to a sick neighbor's window. "Worship should feed people," it told me once, matter-of-fact, like it was reporting the weather. "If it doesn't, it's just hunger wearing nice clothes." I thought of the Regent-Cardinal and his sermon of the end times and decided the god had earned that line.

Evenings belonged to Sable. That was non-negotiable, and the god respected it with the seriousness of treaty law, which — fine, yes, it had read the prophecy book by then, all of it, including the parts about me and Sable, and it had opinions, and those opinions were smug in ways I refuse to document.

Sable would come to the gallery after their days helping rebuild the archive — the royal one, rising from the ashes, and the valley one too, cataloguing what they'd saved, one armful of books and a wounded junior librarian named Pip who followed Sable around like a duckling — and the three of us would sit by the fire, and the god would ask questions, and Sable would answer them with citations, and the god would ask follow-up questions until Sable ran out of citations, which took a while but happened eventually, and watching it happen became my favorite sport.

Tonight — tonight was different. Tonight the god arrived at the fire already holding something.

It was small. A circle of braided cord, grey thread shot through with one strand of gold, plain as a soldier's kit and careful as a manuscript illumination. I knew what it was before it spoke. My stomach knew first.

"What's that?" Sable asked, though I think they knew too. Archivists recognize ritual objects. It's professional.

"A question," the god said. It sat down across from us, cross-legged, and laid the cord on the hearthstone between us like a card played face-up. "I have been thinking about oaths."

"Spare us," I said.

"No. Listen." And it was the old voice for a moment, the vast one, the one that had sung through palace walls and frozen rivers, and we listened. "An oath is a kind of love. You taught me that — you, Vesper, with your broken vow and your empty arm. I watched a hundred thousand of my sworn carry their brands, and I thought devotion was the brand. The binding. The chain that holds the love in place." It touched the cord but didn't pick it up. "I was wrong. Your love held when the chain broke. That's the lesson of your life, I think, and I have been a student of lessons longer than this valley has existed, so believe me when I grade it highly."

"Flatterer," Sable murmured.

"The Church will rebuild around me within the year," the god went on. "Choirs. Brands, if I allow it — new marks, new bindings, ten thousand people volunteering their wrists because that is what humans do with awe: they shackle it. And some of them I will accept, because some souls are happiest bound, and freedom imposed is its own cage." Its grey eyes found mine. "But I wanted one follower first. Before the choirs. Before the temples. Before any of it. One person whose devotion is a choice made with full knowledge, freely given and freely kept, unbranded, unchained — so that everything built afterward stands on that foundation instead of on fear."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the city dripped its slow spring music off a thousand eaves.

"So here is my question, Vesper Amberdale, wolf at the choir door, breaker of oaths, teacher-of-bread: will you be my first follower? Not bound. Not branded. Just — walking with me. Teaching me rivers. Correcting my theology when I get smug." A pause. A smile, small and crooked. "Is that a vow you'd make?"

And here is the thing I want to write down correctly, because Sable will ask me about this moment someday and I want my account to match theirs.

I didn't cry. I'd expected to. I'd braced for it, the way you brace for cold water. Instead what happened was simpler and stranger: everything went quiet. The same quiet that used to live in my brand, except now it wasn't coming from outside me. It was just — mine. Room. All that empty space where the oath used to live, suddenly not empty at all but *open*, like a door standing wide in warm weather.

"I'll make you a counter-offer," I said.

The god tilted its head.

"I don't follow anyone. Not anymore. Not kings, not cardinals, not prophecy books, not gods." I picked up the cord. It was lighter than it looked, warm, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and something older underneath — snowmelt, maybe, or starlight pretending to be snowmelt. "But I'll walk with you. I'll teach you the valley and the drills and which tavern keeper waters the honey wine and which one you can trust. I'll argue with you about theology whether you want me to or not. And when you forget what it's like to be small — because you will, everyone does — I'll remind you." I looped the cord over my head, where it settled against my sternum, over the place where the brand used to ache. "That's not followership. That's friendship with a long contract. Take it or leave it."

For one terrible heartbeat the god said nothing, and I thought — *gods don't take counter-offers, what were you thinking, you just haggled with divinity over the terms of your own soul—*

Then it laughed. Bright, startled, delighted, a sound with no century in it at all, and flung itself across the hearthstone to hug me with the full unschooled force of a seven-year-old body powered by the memory of mountains, and knocked me flat, and Sable was laughing too, and the cord pressed warm against my chest like a coal that had finally, after a hundred years and one long winter, caught.

"Friendship with a long contract," the god repeated into my shoulder, muffled and triumphant. "Accepted. Witnessed. Binding in perpetuity." It pulled back, grey eyes shining. "Now. First order of business, friend. There's a fair in your valley in six days, and I have never seen one, and Sable tells me there is honey wine, and I have *questions* about honey wine—"

"Hold on," I said. "Six days? How do you know about the fair?"

The god and Sable exchanged a look. A conspiratorial look. A look I would come to know very well over the following years, and learn to dread accordingly.

"Because," Sable said, grinning, pulling something from their satchel — a folded page, copied in their own neat hand, edges singed, "we're going home tomorrow. And there's something I need to show you on the road. Something about the book."

They unfolded the page. And what was written there made the fire seem suddenly very small, and the spring night very large, and the cord at my throat hum like a struck bell.

The god leaned close beside me, reading over my shoulder with a child's curiosity and something else beneath it — recognition, old and cold and patient.

"Well," it said quietly. "It seems our story isn't finished writing itself."
