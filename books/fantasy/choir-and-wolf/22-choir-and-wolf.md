## 22. Choir and Wolf

**Vesper**

The valley had a smell in spring that no other place in the world could claim, as far as Vesper was concerned — wet earth and woodsmoke and the green, impatient smell of things deciding to grow all at once. It was the smell of the Godless Valley, the smell of home, and it had never smelled so loud before. A year ago Vesper hadn't known that a valley could be *loud*. Now they knew that everything was, if you listened right.

The fair had come back with the thaw, same as it always did, and the fairground was the same chaos of canvas and frying oil and fiddles it had been the year before — the year Sable Fenwick had shown them a prophecy book for fun, the way an archivist shows anyone anything, which is to say like handing over a lit fuse and asking what could possibly go wrong.

Vesper stood at the edge of it with a pack on their back that weighed almost nothing and was, at the same time, the heaviest thing they'd ever carried.

"You're doing the face," said the pack.

"I don't have a face."

"You absolutely have a face. It's the one you make before you break a siege line. I've seen it twice now, and both times someone ended up crying." A pause, thoughtful. "Usually you."

Vesper adjusted the pack's strap with more care than the adjustment required. "You're supposed to be napping."

"I napped all winter. I'm very well rested. I'm going to be insufferable about it." The pack rustled, and a small grey eye peered out from between the flap and Vesper's shoulder — a child's eye, or something like one, wide and pale as river ice, ringed with light the way the moon is ringed on a cold night. "Is that honey wine I smell?"

"It is."

"Is it for me?"

"You're a god."

"I'm a *small* god," the eye said, with dignity. "Small gods get wine."

Vesper bought two cups from the same red-faced woman with the same cracked ladle, and if she noticed that one cup was poured slowly and carefully into a canvas pack, she had the good grace of valley folk, which is to say she noticed everything and said nothing, and only winked.

The wine was warm and thick and tasted like the year before, and the year before had tasted like the last ordinary evening of their old life. Vesper drank theirs and watched the crowd for a flash of dark hair and ink-stained fingers.

Sable was late. Sable was always late, because Sable always found one more thing in the archive, one more marginal note, one more thread to pull — and Vesper had learned, over a winter of learning many things, that you didn't rush an archivist any more than you rushed a thaw. You just stood at the edge of the field and trusted the green to come.

They came.

* * *

**Sable**

Sable had meant to be on time. Sable had, in fact, left the archive on time, which for Sable was a historic achievement worthy of its own marginal note. But the north road passed the old mill, and the old mill had a new shutter, and the new shutter had a carving on it that Sable had never seen before — a wolf and a choir of small birds, crude and charming, and beneath it a date.

Last autumn. The week they'd all been in the capital. Someone had carved them while they were gone, while they were unmaking a king, and Sable had stood in the mud for a quarter hour with frozen fingers copying the carving into a notebook because that was what Sable *did*, that was the whole architecture of Sable's soul: see a thing, write it down, keep it safe.

History was happening all the time, Sable had learned. It didn't wait for the chronicles. It happened in mill shutters and fairground fiddles and the way a mercenary looked at a cup of honey wine like it might be the last honest thing in the world.

Sable ran the rest of the way.

The fairground hit them all at once — the noise, the grease-smoke, the fiddlers tuning up for the evening dance, the children shrieking around the maypole that hadn't gone up yet, the whole bright animal mess of a valley that had survived a winter that should have killed it. And in the middle of it, exactly where Sable had known they'd be, stood Vesper Amberdale, holding two cups of honey wine and wearing the expression of a person about to do something terrifying.

Sable had catalogued that expression. It was in the notebook. *Amberdale, V. — pre-battle face. Distinguish from pre-kiss face with difficulty.*

"Vesper."

Vesper turned, and the terrifying expression broke into something so plainly glad that Sable's chest did the thing it always did, the thing no archive had ever taught them a word for.

"You're late," Vesper said.

"I found a carving."

"You found a *carving*."

"A wolf and a choir. On the mill. Someone carved us, Vesper. We're folk art. We're *vernacular material culture*." Sable took the offered cup and drank, and the honey wine was exactly as sweet and exactly as warm as the year before, and this time there was no prophecy book waiting, no fuse, no century. Just the fair, and the fiddles, and Vesper's shoulder warm against theirs. "Also I'm sorry I'm late."

"You're forgiven. You're always forgiven. It's a flaw in my character I've decided to keep."

The small god's head emerged from the pack, blinking, hair like spun frost. "Wine," it said.

"You had wine," Vesper said.

"That was *arrival* wine. This is *fair* wine. The liturgy is very clear."

"There is no liturgy. I checked. I'm the archivist."

"Then I'm founding one." The god accepted a careful splash anyway, drank it with the gravity of a sacrament, and then, in the manner of small gods everywhere, immediately fell back asleep mid-sip, its head lolling against Vesper's shoulder blade like the whole world was a bed it had been promised.

Sable watched them — the mercenary and the sleeping god — and thought about the first page of the prophecy book, the one that had started everything. *The wolf at the choir door.* The book had made it sound like doom. It had never once mentioned that the wolf would carry the choir around in a backpack and feed it honey wine.

* * *

**Vesper**

They danced badly. This was tradition now, established the year before, when Vesper — drilled in every weapon known to the free companies, fluent in the grammar of violence — had discovered that a simple valley reel could reduce them to the coordination of a startled deer. Sable had laughed so hard they'd had to sit down. Vesper had stepped on their foot. It had been, both of them later agreed, the moment.

So they danced badly again, under the string lights and the rising stars, and the fiddles played the same tune as last year, and the small god snored in the pack by the cider stall, guarded by two children who had appointed themselves its honor guard and could not be persuaded it was anything but an enchanted badger.

Vesper didn't step on Sable's foot this time. Progress. Sable noticed, of course. Sable noticed everything.

"You're not stepping on me," Sable said, suspicious, mid-turn. "Why aren't you stepping on me?"

"I've been practicing."

"You've been *practicing*?"

"With the innkeeper's daughter. She's eight. She's a brutal instructor." Vesper caught Sable's hand on the turn and held it a beat longer than the reel required. "I've been practicing a lot of things."

Sable looked at them. Sable had a way of looking that was worse than any interrogation Vesper had ever endured — patient, delighted, utterly unfooled. "Vesper Amberdale. Are you nervous?"

"No."

"Your hand is sweating."

"It's a warm evening."

"It's a *cold* evening. I'm an archivist, Vesper, I have primary sources, and your hand is sweating."

The fiddle tune ended. The next one started, slower, the kind of tune the valley saved for the last dance, when the children drooped and the old couples came out and the whole fair went soft at the edges. Around them the crowd sorted itself into pairs. The lanterns swayed.

Vesper had broken a siege line at Harrow Ford. Vesper had stood in front of the Regent-Cardinal with a brand burning on their arm and refused him to his face. Vesper had spoken their oath aloud in the sight of gods and kings and torn it out by the root, and the tearing had not killed them, and the morning after they had woken up lighter and more themselves than they had ever been.

None of it had been this frightening.

"Sable," Vesper said.

"Yes."

"I had a whole speech."

"I know. You've been rehearsing it for three weeks. The god told me. The god tells me everything, it has no concept of confidentiality, we're working on it."

"I *had* a speech," Vesper said, "about how I spent my whole life with an oath I was born into, and how I thought that was what devotion was — something carved into you before you could choose it. And then I met an archivist who couldn't dance, and I learned that you can choose a thing so completely it carves *itself*, and that's — that's better. That's the real thing. That's what the god came back to show me, I think. Not worship. Choice. Every morning, choosing." Vesper's hand was definitely sweating. Vesper no longer cared. "So I'm not asking you to swear anything. I'm asking if you'll choose it with me. Marry me. Sable. Will you—"

"Yes."

"I hadn't finished the—"

"Yes," Sable said again, already laughing, already crying, already holding Vesper's face in both ink-stained hands like a manuscript too precious to shelve. "Yes, you absolute disaster, yes, I said yes before you finished because I've known since the mill shutter, since the tomb, since the *first fair* — I catalogued it, Vesper, I have a *notebook* — yes."

The fiddle sawed on. The lanterns swayed. Somewhere by the cider stall, two children cheered, and a pack rustled, and a small grey eye opened and observed the kissing with the solemn satisfaction of a deity whose liturgy had finally been observed.

"About time," said the god, and went back to sleep.

* * *

**Sable**

Later — much later, when the fiddles were packed away and the lanterns burned down to embers and the fairground was only a field again, littered with straw and sweetness — Sable sat with their back against Vesper's shoulder on the cold grass and opened the notebook.

The god was awake again, sitting in the crook of Vesper's arm, watching the stars with the ferocious attention it gave everything, as though the sky might be a text it could learn to read if it stared hard enough. Maybe it could. Sable had stopped assuming the god couldn't do things.

"Read it to us," the god said. It loved being read to. It had learned nearly all its words that way, and its favorites were the strange ones — *vernacular*, *liturgy*, *yes*.

Sable flipped to the page from the mill shutter, the crude wolf, the choir of birds, the date. Then back, further, to the older pages — the copied lines from a prophecy book that had gone blank the day the crown broke, every word spent, every debt paid. And at the very back, a single page Sable had never shown anyone, copied out in their own hand the night before they'd left the capital. The book's last line, the one it had written as it emptied itself:

*Here ends the record of the wolf and the reader, who were given to each other twice: once by ink, and once by choice. The second time counts.*

"You know what I keep thinking," Sable said. "The book wrote us before we existed. It knew. And it still got it wrong."

Vesper shifted. "Wrong how?"

"It called me the *reader*. Like reading was the whole of me. But I didn't just read the truth this year. I burned for it. I stole it out of a fire. I rewrote it." Sable tapped the notebook. "History isn't what happened. It's what we refuse to let go of. I refuse to let go of this." A pause. "Also I refuse to let go of you, but that's less of a scholarly position."

"Is it in the notebook?"

"It's in the notebook."

The god reached up and patted the notebook with one small luminous hand, the way it patted everything it approved of — the pack, the wine cup, Vesper's scarred knuckles, the whole patient world. "Read the last line again," it said.

Sable read it. *The second time counts.*

Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and kind. In the valley below, the thaw-water ran loud in every stream, the way it would run all spring, all summer, all the ordinary miraculous years ahead. Somewhere south, a real king was learning to rule a realm that had seen him weep, and a fractured Church was learning to be small, and the Sworn of the Quiet Choir were learning, slowly, awkwardly, to be an order with no god to swear to — until one did.

But that was all south, and all tomorrow. Tonight was a field and a fairground and a notebook and a yes.

The god yawned, a vast, ungodlike yawn, and burrowed into Vesper's arm. "Tell me the story," it said. "From the beginning. The fair, and the book, and the wolf."

"You were *there*," Vesper said.

"I was *asleep* for most of it," said the god. "And besides. It's my favorite one."

So Sable opened the notebook to the first page, and Vesper put an arm around them both, and under the wheeling stars of the Godless Valley — which was not godless anymore, whatever the maps said — they began at the beginning: a mercenary with an evening off, an archivist with a book, and a prophecy that had gotten almost everything wrong except the part that mattered.

The second time counts.

It always had.
