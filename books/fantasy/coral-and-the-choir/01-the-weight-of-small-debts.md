## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The ledgers did not like to be moved, so Ines Yarrow went down to them.

That was the first thing they taught you in the Choir's counting house — not arithmetic, not calligraphy, though you learned both until your fingers ached. The first lesson was that the books had opinions. Carry a ledger up into the light and its ink would fade by evening, entries going pale as breath on glass, and no amount of re-inking would hold. Leave it where it was, in the cold vault beneath the gate, and the numbers stayed black and obedient for centuries.

So Ines worked underground, four hours a day, six days a week, alone with forty-one volumes bound in salt-stiff leather and chained to their shelves by custom rather than iron. The Choir called this the Undercroft. The clerks who were not him called it the Hole, and drew lots twice a year to decide who would be sent down there, and every drawing since he was nineteen had come up with his name, because his name always came up, because someone above had decided long ago that Ines Yarrow belonged below.

He had stopped resenting it around the time he turned twenty-five. There was a cleanliness to the work that suited him. Upstairs, the Choir traded in impossible things — relics that wept, letters from the dead, blessings sold by the ounce — and every transaction came smeared with politics, with the Provost's ambitions, with the wet fingerprints of men who wanted what the Choir kept. Down here there was only the arithmetic. A debt entered. A debt discharged. A balance carried forward. Two hundred years of small, honest numbers, and him the only living soul who read them all.

Tonight was an audit night, which meant all forty-one ledgers open at once across three trestle tables, and Ines moving among them with a lamp and a slate, confirming that the sum of everything owed still equaled the sum of everything paid.

It always did. That was the miracle nobody upstairs appreciated. The Choir's theology held that the gate below the Undercroft — the great sealed arch of green stone, older than the guild, older than the kingdom — had been shut for two centuries, ever since the country behind it drowned. Nothing crossed. Nothing answered. And yet the ledgers balanced to the copper, year after year, as if the trade continued somewhere out past the edge of the page.

Ines had a private theory about this, which he kept where he kept all his private things: nowhere. His theory was that the ledgers were simply very good at their work. That a well-kept book imposes its own order on the world, the way a straight furrow makes the plowman walk straighter. He wrote neat columns and the world, being suggestible, obliged him with neat outcomes.

At half past nine he found the entry that broke the theory.

* * *

It was in Ledger Nine, the volume covering debts of the third kind — prayers, which the Choir counted as currency the way it counted silver or salt. A prayer made is a debt incurred. A prayer answered is a debt discharged. For two hundred years the prayer columns of Ledger Nine had been a study in futility: thousands of petitions entered, none marked paid, the whole page a graveyard of wants. Fishermen asking the drowned gods for calm water. Farmers asking for rain over fields that had never needed a god to grow anything before. Children asking for mothers back.

Ines checked them the way he checked everything: line against line, tally against tally, his pen hovering but rarely falling, because correction was a last resort and a confession of earlier failure.

Line 447. Entered in the old Provost Sarel's hand, sixty years dead: *Petition of Tomas Vey, fisherman of Greywater Landing, that the sea give back his nets.* Status column, in the same ink, same decade: *Unpaid.*

Except the status column now read *Paid,* and the ink was new.

Ines stood over it for a long moment without breathing. New ink in Ledger Nine was not impossible — clerks added annotations, cross-references, marginal notes on provenance. But annotations were made in brown gall-ink, initialed, dated. This was black, the deep black of the original entries, the black that only appeared in the Undercroft, and it carried no initial and no date. It simply sat where *Unpaid* had been, occupying the same space, as if the word had been drowned and something else had surfaced in its place.

He touched the page with one finger, the way you touch a wound to learn if it's yours. The paper was dry. The ink was dry. It had been dry a while.

He turned back through Ledger Nine, line by line, faster now, his lamp swinging shadows across the shelves. Line 412: *Paid.* Line 398: *Paid.* Line 381, a widow's petition for her son's return from a sea that had taken him thirty years ago: *Paid,* in that same bottomless black, and beside it, in a margin narrow enough for only one word, another word: *Returned.*

His pen stayed in his hand the whole time and never once touched paper. You do not correct what you have not yet understood. That was the second lesson of the counting house, the one after the ledgers having opinions, and it was the lesson he was best at, because understanding was the part of the work he loved.

By midnight he had found eleven changed entries. All prayers. All old. All marked paid within — he estimated by the freshness of the black, which was a fool's method and he knew it, but it was the only method he had — the last two months.

Eleven answered prayers, and no answerer entered anywhere in the debit columns.

A debt discharged must be charged to someone. That was not theology; that was arithmetic. Every coin came from a purse. Every payment left a hole. Somewhere in the sum of the world, eleven holes had opened, and Ines Yarrow could not find them, and the imbalance was not showing up in any ledger he kept, which meant it was accumulating somewhere he could not see, at a rate he could not calculate, against a party he could not name.

The numbers were right. That was what frightened him. If the numbers had been wrong, he could have fixed them. Wrong numbers were his whole profession. Right numbers, arriving from nowhere, in ink that matched two-hundred-year-old entries — right numbers meant the ledger was keeping itself, and if the ledger was keeping itself, then Ines Yarrow was not the keeper of anything. He was a guest at his own table, reading a book that no longer needed him.

He put out his lamp at the appointed hour, one o'clock, because the Undercroft kept its own schedule and he had learned not to argue with rooms like this one. In the dark, before his eyes adjusted, he felt it again — the thing he had felt every night for two weeks and told no one about, the faint pull at the far end of the vault, past the last shelf, toward the sealed arch. Not wind. The gate was airtight; he had tested it himself with a candle flame that burned perfectly vertical for an hour. A pull, then, with no air behind it. Like standing near a drain. Like standing near a mouth.

He climbed the ninety-one steps to the counting house, locked the door behind him, and walked home through streets gone quiet and cold.

* * *

His rooms were above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, two floors of borrowed warmth that smelled always faintly of tallow. He ate standing up, bread and hard cheese and the pickled onions the chandler's wife pressed on him weekly because a man who lived alone, in her opinion, was a man slowly dying and too polite to mention it. He washed his plate. He dried it. He set it in the rack at the angle the rack demanded.

People called him careful the way they called weather mild — as a description of absence, of nothing much happening. They didn't see the architecture of it. Care was the load-bearing wall of his life. He rose at five. He audited. He copied fair drafts upstairs until the afternoon bell. He took his supper at seven, alone, and spent his evenings reconciling the day's entries in a private notebook he kept under a floorboard, because the Choir forbade clerks to remove even copies of the ledgers from the premises and Ines had decided, in the particular way he decided things, slowly and irrevocably, that a man who understood the books better than anyone alive was entitled to understand them in his own hand too.

The notebook was where he wrote the things the ledgers couldn't say. Tonight's entry began as procedure and ended as something else.

*Ledger Nine, eleven entries altered, status unpaid → paid, ink consistent with original hands, no annotator, no date. Debts discharged without recorded debtor. Balance sheet shows no corresponding deficit. Conclusion: either the ledger errs (it does not err) or the payment is real and the recipient of the charge lies outside all books I keep.*

He sat with that last sentence for a while, chewing the end of his pen, which was a habit he permitted himself exactly one hour a day.

Outside all books I keep. He turned the phrase over. It had the shape of a door.

Because here was the thing he had never said aloud, the inheritance he carried as lightly as he could manage, which was not lightly at all: the gate below the Undercroft had been built by his family. Not the Choir's founders — the Choir had come later, had found the gate standing, had built its whole trade of impossible things in the gate's long shadow and called itself keeper of what it had merely moved into. The builders had been the Yarrows. Gate-wrights, door-masons, the family whose blood was supposed to be the lock. Two hundred years ago, when the country beyond drowned and the gate was sealed, the sealing had been done by a Yarrow, and the price of the seal had emptied the family out — of members, of fortune, of history, until all that remained was Ines, an auditor with chapped hands and a room above a chandler's shop, who kept other people's accounts because his own account had been settled so thoroughly that nothing of it was left to inherit except the knowing.

He knew what the gate cost. He knew what it weighed. He had grown up on stories of it the way other children grew up on saints' lives, and he had spent his whole adult life pretending the stories were inventory records, dry and safe, numbers about stone.

Now the numbers were writing themselves, and every one of them was a prayer getting answered, and there was only one direction prayers traveled when they got answered by something on the other side of a sealed gate.

He finished the notebook entry with a line he almost didn't write, and then wrote anyway, because his whole discipline was writing the true thing:

*If the payer is the gate, then the gate has begun paying again. And if the gate pays, the gate intends to collect.*

He closed the notebook, returned it under the floorboard, and lay down on his narrow bed to sleep the way he always slept: completely, immediately, like a man falling off the edge of a well-made page.

He dreamed of the Undercroft. In the dream the ledgers were all open, all forty-one, and the black ink was rising in the status columns like water finding its level, and somewhere past the last shelf the sealed arch was breathing — slow, tidal, patient — and each breath paid another debt, and each payment was a hook set quietly in the flesh of the world, and the hooks were all set in him.

He woke at five, as always, washed, dressed, and was unlocking the counting-house door at half past six when Coral found him.

She was leaning against the doorframe in the grey light, hood back, salt still drying white in the creases of her knuckles, and she was looking at him the way she sometimes looked at him — the look he had spent four years learning not to read, because reading it would ruin both their lives. Except today her eyes weren't doing the usual thing. Today they looked frightened, and underneath the fright, urgent, and underneath that, sorry.

"Ines," she said. "There's a fisherman at Greywater Landing who prayed to a god that's been dead two hundred years." She swallowed. "And the god gave him back his nets. And when the tide came inland after — a mile inland, Ines, saltwater in the wheat fields — the god whispered names. Eleven of them." Her voice dropped. "Your name was one."

He stood very still, one hand on the key, the door half open onto the dark stairs going down.

"The ledgers," he said. "Show me which prayer was mine."
