## 2. A Prayer Answered in Salt

The prayer came in at low tide, the way the worst ones always did.

Coral was counting contraband in the back room of the Saltcellar — forty vials of drowned-land myrrh, each one worth more than the fisherman who'd carried it across the border would earn in a year — when a boy banged on the loading door and said that Tam Voss was standing waist-deep in his own kitchen, praying.

She went because Tam Voss had never once shorted her on a delivery. She went because the Saltcellar's cellar was flooding and the flood smelled like the sea, and the nearest sea was nine miles east. She went, mostly, because when a man prays to a god that has been dead for two hundred years and the god answers, someone from the Choir should be there to write it down.

She just didn't expect to be the only one.

Tam's house sat at the crook of the Fenwater where it bent toward the marshes, a low gray building with nets drying on its roof like the sails of a ship run aground. The water around it was wrong. Not flood-water — she knew flood-water, brown and chattering with debris. This was clear and green-gray and it moved the way tide moves, in long patient pulls, and when she waded the last thirty feet to his door the hem of her coat soaked up salt.

Salt. Nine miles inland.

Inside, Tam stood in his kitchen to his thighs in shining water, hands raised, eyes shut, still praying. He was a big man gone soft with age, and he looked — there was no other word for it — blessed. The water had risen around him without touching anything else. His shelves were dry. His wife's portrait on the wall was dry. Only the floor beneath him had become sea.

"Tide's come for me," he said, when he heard her splash through the door. He wasn't frightened. That was the worst part. "I asked, and it came."

"Tam." Coral kept her voice level, the voice she used on customs men. "What did you ask for?"

"For my boys." He opened his eyes. They were wet, but not from the spray. "Both lost off the Teeth three winters back. I've prayed every night since, to every god with a name left. Nothing. Nothing. Then last week old Maren told me about the gods under the water — the ones we drowned with the country — and I thought, well. What have I got to lose that I haven't already?"

"And?"

"And last night something answered." He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "Not a voice exactly. More like the feeling of a voice, if you understand. Like being spoken to from underneath. It said the boys were kept. It said they were kept the way a ledger keeps things — safe, counted, owed nothing. And it said if I wanted to see them I should let the water in."

"So you let the water in."

"I opened my door to the tide," he said simply. "It walked uphill to get here. You can't tell me it didn't want to."

The water was chest-high now. She could see fish moving in it. Small silver things, inland fish should never have seen, circling his legs like they were keeping him company. Keeping him. The word again.

"Tam," she said, "the Choir will burn this house and salt the ground it stood on, and they'll take you to the Provost, and the Provost does not pray, he *audits*. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Whatever spoke to you, whatever your boys are now — the Choir will call it a breach. They'll call *you* a breach."

"Then don't report me."

"I'm a smuggler, Tam, not a saint. My whole trade is things the Choir doesn't know about. But this—" She looked at the water. It had begun to recede, drawing back down the hill with the same unhurried patience it had arrived, taking a spoonful of Tam's kitchen floor with it, leaving the boards bleached white as bone. "This isn't a crate of myrrh. This is a door opening."

"It's an answer," Tam said. "First one I ever got. I'm not closing it."

* * *

They traveled west that night, against the flow of everything.

Coral dressed Tam in a widow's mourning grays, which was its own small cruelty since his wife had died eleven years before his sons were lost, but grief made everyone the same shape to a checkpoint clerk. She gave him a bundle of dried herring to hold in his lap so his hands would look busy and honest, and she took the river road because the river road flooded twice a year and the Choir's surveyors had stopped bothering to staff its crossings properly. Sloth, in Coral's experience, was the most reliable institution in the country.

There were three checkpoints between the Fenwater and the capital. The first waved them through; the clerk was asleep. At the second, a young enforcer with a chalk-fresh face looked at Tam's papers, looked at Tam, and asked him one question: *Have you prayed lately?*

Everyone prayed. Everyone lit candles, mumbled at shrines, crossed themselves against bad weather. What the enforcer meant was: *have you prayed to the drowned gods*, and everyone knew it, and everyone lied, and the lie had worked for two hundred years because the drowned gods had never once answered.

Tam looked at the boy and said, "No."

And Coral, watching him say it, felt the water rise in the room — not literally, though she half expected it to, half *wanted* it to, which was worse — felt it in herself, some tidal thing pulling at her from below, and understood that Tam Voss was no longer lying even when he lied. The god was in him now the way salt is in a sailor. You couldn't interrogate it out. You could only drown him or let him pass.

She let him pass. She paid the enforcer's palm with myrrh-vial money and told herself it was bribery and not something else.

At the third checkpoint the enforcers weren't sleepy and they weren't young. There were four of them, and one held a slate, and on the slate was written a list of names, and beside the list a directive fresh enough that the ink had smudged: *All persons reporting contact with submerged entities to be detained for audit of soul-ledger.*

Soul-ledger. Two months ago that phrase hadn't existed. Coral had read every document the Choir produced — reading them was how she smuggled, knowing what they feared before they feared it — and she had watched the phrase appear first in a marginal note, then in a memo, then in a directive, spreading through the bureaucracy like damp through plaster. Someone high up was afraid. Someone high up had heard something too.

The enforcers searched the cart. They found the herring. They found the widow's grays folded neatly in a sack, and for a moment — a long moment, the length of a held breath — it balanced there, Tam's secret hanging by a thread of folded cloth.

Then the eldest enforcer looked at Tam's hands. Calloused, rope-scarred, knuckles swollen with fifty years of nets. And he looked at the papers, which named a widow of sixty-one, and he frowned not at the disguise but at something else, something in Tam's face, and he said, "You've been in the water."

"No," said Tam.

"You've been in the water," the enforcer repeated, gently, the way you speak to someone you intend to hurt, "because salt dries white at the hairline and yours is white to the crown, and because you smell of deep tide, not shore tide, and because when I say the word *kept* your eyes go somewhere."

He said the word. Tam's eyes went somewhere.

Coral moved before she decided to move. Later she would reconstruct it the way you reconstruct a fall: hand into the myrrh satchel, vial out, thumb breaking the wax seal against her palm, and then the smell — the drowned-land myrrh, resinous and ancient and wrong, the smell of a country that no longer existed — filling the checkpoint booth like a door swinging open onto a drowned room. The enforcers flinched. All four of them. Even the eldest, even mid-word. Because the smell meant the thing the Choir had spent two centuries pretending was impossible: that the drowned country was not entirely drowned. That it could reach across the water and leave its scent in a smuggler's satchel, its salt in a fisherman's hair, its answer in a widower's kitchen.

"Contraband," Coral said coldly, professionally, holding up the broken vial. "Resin of the sunken coast. Class-three interdict. You'll want to log the seizure, seal the booth, file the manifests. I'll wait."

Procedure is a kind of sleepwalk. The enforcers procedure'd. They sealed the booth, they logged the seizure, they argued about whose manifest the loss came out of, and while they argued Coral walked Tam Voss out the far side of the checkpoint into a rain that had started without anyone noticing, and she did not stop walking until the booth's lantern was a smudge behind them and Tam, shaking, said her name like a question.

"You broke a vial worth more than my boat."

"I'll bill the Choir," she said. "They're paying for the war whether they know it or not."

Then, because the road was dark and long and they had nine hours of it, Tam talked. Widowers do. He talked about his boys, and the water, and the voice underneath, and Coral listened with the professional patience of a woman who has heard ten thousand confessions and absolved none of them, until near dawn, when the road climbed out of the marshes and the sky went the color of unpolished pewter, and Tam said:

"It said names."

"What?"

"The voice. Underneath. Before it told me to open the door, it said names. Like — like a ledger being read aloud. Debts being called in, or paid out, I couldn't tell which. It said my name. It said Maren's name, the old woman who told me. It said—" he rubbed his face, trying to bring it up from somewhere deeper than memory, "—it said a lot of names I didn't know. Long ones. Old ones. Names like titles. And then it said one I did know, because I'd heard it at the market, at the tax table, at the ledger desk. It said—"

He turned to her in the gray light, and his eyes were wet again, and Coral felt the morning tilt.

"—it said *Ines Yarrow*."

She kept walking. That was the discipline of her life, the whole of it: keep walking, keep your face level, keep the cargo dry. She had loved Ines Yarrow for six years across a ledger desk, in the specific cowardly way you love someone you see for eleven minutes a week, in lamplight, over columns of figures — the way he wet his pen with his ring finger instead of a cloth, the way he apologized to the paper when he made an error, as if the paper deserved courtesy. She had never said a word of it. Smugglers don't put valuables in ledgers.

"He owes the god money?" she said, and her voice came out almost steady.

"No," Tam said slowly. "That's the thing. When it said his name, it didn't sound like a debt. It sounded like — like a summons. Like the way you'd say the name of the man who built the house you live in. Like the water was saying *he's ours, he was always ours, he's coming home.*"

The road bent. Below them, past the last of the marsh, the capital lay in its bowl of smoke and bells, and somewhere in it the Choir's counting-house, and somewhere in the counting-house a desk with a lamp, and somewhere at the desk a man wetting his pen with his ring finger, writing down numbers that were right, numbers that were always right, and Coral thought: *the numbers are right, and that is what frightens him, and he doesn't yet know why.*

The god had whispered his name to a fisherman nine miles inland, in a kitchen full of tide.

She had perhaps a day. Maybe less.

"Tam," she said. "How fast can you walk?"
