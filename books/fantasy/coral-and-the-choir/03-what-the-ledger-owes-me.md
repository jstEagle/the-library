## 3. What the Ledger Owes Me

The oldest ledgers lived on the sublevel, three floors beneath the audit hall, in a room the Choir called the Deepstack and Ines privately called the throat. It was cold down there in a way that had nothing to do with the weather. The stone was older than the Choir, older than the Provosty, older than the city that had grown up around the gate like moss on a millstone. The air tasted of chalk and iron and, faintly, of the sea — though the sea was a hundred miles east and the gate above had been sealed for two hundred years.

Ines had been auditing the Deepstack for six days. He told himself it was diligence. The self-updating entries in the modern ledgers had to have a source, and sources, in his experience, were always written down somewhere. Numbers did not move on their own. Someone moved them.

He was beginning to suspect that the someone was not a someone.

The old ledgers were kept on shelves of black oak, bound in hide he chose not to examine too closely, and their spines bore no titles — only tally marks, hundreds of years of them, like the rings of a felled tree. He worked through them in chronological order, by lamplight, copying anything anomalous into his own notebook with his careful, narrow hand. Most of it was what you'd expect from the Choir's first century: tithes of grain, tithes of teeth, payments made to the gate in the currency of small griefs. A widow's wedding ring. A boy's left shoe. Names, sometimes. Just names, entered and struck through, entered and struck through, the ink going brown with age.

On the sixth night he found the ledger that had no tally marks on its spine.

It sat alone on the lowest shelf, at the level of his knees, as if it had been put there to be overlooked. The binding was not hide. It was smooth and grey and slightly warm, and when he opened it the first page was not paper but something like vellum that had been poured rather than cut. The handwriting inside was not the Choir's hand. It was older, rounder, written left to right and right to left in alternating lines, the way you'd plow a field.

He read the first entry three times before his hands stopped shaking enough to copy it.

*In the year the waters took the low country, the gate was fed its founding debt: the name of the last heir of its builders, held in trust, to be claimed when the gate wakes.*

Below it, in the same hand but newer ink — ink that was, he realized with a cold that started in his teeth and spread downward, not yet dry:

*The heir is born. Ines Yarrow. Audit-hall, third desk. He keeps the accounts well.*

His own name. His own desk. Written in a book older than the Choir, in ink that had not been dry six days ago, describing a life he had built himself, entry by entry, choice by careful choice.

He sat down on the cold floor without deciding to. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above him, through three floors of stone, he could hear the audit hall going about its evening — the shuffle of clerks, the ring of the tally-bells, the ordinary arithmetic of the world. It sounded very far away, and very thin, like a song heard through a wall.

*He keeps the accounts well.*

That was the part that made him ill. Not the name. Not even the gate. It was the accuracy. Every wrong turn he had ever taken — the apprenticeship he'd almost refused, the posting he'd requested and been denied, the transfer to the gate-office that had come through against his petition — all of it, he saw now, arranged with the same tidy competence he prided himself on. Someone had audited his life in advance and found the numbers acceptable. He had spent thirty years believing he was the most careful man in the building, and the whole time he had been a column in someone else's ledger, balanced to the penny.

He was still sitting on the floor when the summons came.

It arrived the way such things always did — a junior clerk, breathless from the stairs, holding a card of heavy cream stock with the Provost's seal in green wax. *The Provost will see the auditor Yarrow at first bell.* No reason given. No reason was ever given. But Ines had worked under the Provosty for eleven years, and he had never once been summoned at first bell, and he had never once been summoned by name. Auditors were summoned by desk number. That was the custom. That was the design.

*The heir is born. Ines Yarrow.*

He climbed the three flights with the grey-bound ledger under his coat, which was theft, which was the first thing he had ever done in his life that no one had written down in advance — or so he told himself, and did not quite believe.

* * *

He found Coral in the counting-room after midnight, checking the salt-tide manifests against the prayer-logs, which was work she was not assigned to and which she did anyway, quietly, the way she did everything that mattered. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. Her hands were stained with ink to the second knuckle. She had a way of standing at the high desks that made the whole room seem to arrange itself around her, though she was, on paper, one of the lowest-ranked clerks in the building.

She looked up when he came in, and whatever was in his face made her set down her pen.

"You've been in the Deepstack," she said.

"You know about the Deepstack?"

"I know about everything down there. It's why they keep me up here." She came around the desk. "Ines. What did you find?"

He should have lied to her. He was, he realized, a very bad liar and always had been, which was perhaps the one quality of his the ledger hadn't planned. He set the grey book on the desk between them and opened it to the page.

She read it faster than he had. He watched her lips move around his name, and he watched the color leave her face in a way that had nothing to do with fear for herself.

"Ines Yarrow," she said. "Heir of the gate-builders."

"So it says."

"So it *knows*." She put her palm flat on the page, as if she could hold the words down. "This is a feeding ledger. Do you understand? The gate doesn't just take payments. It takes *placements*. It's been writing you since before you were born — your desk, your posting, your—" She stopped. He watched her decide not to finish the sentence, and he watched her decide it badly, because her eyes went to his mouth and away again, quick as a ledger-line.

"My what?"

"Nothing." She closed the book. "Don't go."

"I haven't been anywhere."

"The Provost." She said it flatly, and he understood that of course she knew; she knew everything that moved through this building after dark. "You've been summoned. First bell. I saw the card go up an hour ago and I've been standing here trying to think of a way to stop you that wouldn't get us both written down."

"Written down where?"

"*Anywhere.*" Her voice cracked on it, just slightly, and she mastered it. "Ines, listen to me. For two hundred years the Choir has fed that gate so it would stay asleep. Small things. A ring, a shoe, a name now and then. You found the entries yourself. And now the gate is waking, and the small payments aren't holding, and the Provost has spent eleven years watching you balance your books and sharpen your pens and *keep the accounts well* —" she threw his own words back at him like a stone — "and you think first bell is about an audit?"

He thought about it. He thought about the Provost's green seal, the summons by name, the eleven years of denials and transfers that had walked him, desk by desk, floor by floor, down toward the Deepstack like a man being walked down a corridor in the dark by someone who knew where the corridor ended.

"I could refuse," he said.

"You could run."

"I'm an auditor, Coral. I don't run. I reconcile." He almost smiled. "If the Provost wants me, then the Provost has an entry that says so, and I want to read it. I have spent my whole life trusting the numbers. I'm not going to stop the day the numbers turn out to be about *me*."

"That's not courage, that's bookkeeping."

"It's the only thing I have." And then, because the night had already taken everything else from him — the design, the ordinariness, the man he'd thought he was — he said the other thing too, the thing he had never said across the ledger desk in six years of salt-tide manifests and prayer-logs. "You could come with me. To first bell. Stand in the doorway where the Provost can see you. If I'm an entry, you're a witness."

She looked at him for a long moment. The lamps in the counting-room burned very steadily. Somewhere far below them, in the throat of the building, something that was not a ledger turned a page.

"You know I can't," she said. "My vows—"

"Your vows are to the Choir."

"My vows are the only thing between me and—" She stopped again. She was very good at stopping. "First bell," she said instead. "I'll be in the corridor. Not the doorway. The corridor."

"Coral."

"The corridor, Ines." She picked up her pen. Her hand was not quite steady. "Go home. Sleep. If you can."

He couldn't. He walked home through streets that smelled of the sea a hundred miles away, and every door he passed seemed to him now like a page, and every window a column of figures, and above it all the sealed gate stood dark against the stars, patient as a creditor.

* * *

First bell found the Provost's study warm and gold-lit and full of the smell of beeswax, which Ines had not expected. He had expected stone. He had expected the throat. Instead there were carpets, and a fire, and the Provost herself — a small, neat woman with white hair and ink-stained fingers, pouring tea as if this were a social call.

"Auditor Yarrow." She did not say his name the way people said names. She said it the way he entered figures into a ledger: precisely, and with an eye toward the total. "You've been in the Deepstack."

"You know that."

"I wrote the schedule that put you there." She handed him the tea. "Sit down, heir of Yarrow. We have an accounting to settle, and you are going to want to hear it from me before you hear it from the gate."

Behind him, very softly, the study door closed — and he did not need to turn to know that the lock had not been there yesterday, because nothing in this building had ever been there by accident, least of all him.

In the corridor outside, he heard — or thought he heard, or hoped — the small, deliberate sound of Coral's boots, refusing to leave.
