## 4. The Provost's Arithmetic

Ines had rehearsed the walk a hundred times in his head, and it went differently every time. In practice it was simple: down the corridor of green lamps, past the door with no handle that only opened for the Provost's key, into the round room where the Choir kept its oldest ledger chained to a lectern shaped like a drowned man's ribcage.

He had dressed carefully. He always dressed carefully. It was the one armor he owned — good boots, ink-stained cuffs hidden under his sleeves, his hair pinned back so nothing could fall across his eyes while he worked. A man who kept accounts for impossible things could not afford to look like someone who flinched.

The Provost was waiting at the lectern, her hands folded on the open page like a woman resting them on a Bible she did not believe in. She was small, silver-haired, precise as a paper cut. She had run the Choir for thirty years, and everyone said she had never once miscounted.

"Yarrow," she said. "Sit."

There was one chair. There was always one chair. He noticed that now, the way you notice things when your blood has started telling you secrets: the room was built for interrogations disguised as meetings.

"You found the entries," she said. "The ones updating themselves."

"I flagged them. Forty-one payments received since midwinter, all marked *paid by the hand unseen*. I assumed a clerical error in the—"

"There are no clerical errors in this house." She turned the great ledger toward him. "Look at what they paid with."

He looked. Salt. Every entry, salt — measured in grains, in pounds, in tides. And beside each figure, in a hand older than any ink the Choir bought, a name he didn't recognize until he did: names of fishermen, of millers' daughters, of children. Small debts. Small payers.

"The gate eats," the Provost said quietly. "It has always eaten. Two hundred years ago, when your family sealed it, they understood something this generation of clerks forgot: a door like that doesn't close. It sleeps. And a sleeping thing must be fed, or it wakes."

Ines's pen-hand twitched against his thigh. "Fed with what?"

"Grain, at first. Livestock. Then, when the harvests thinned—" She stopped herself, as if the sentence were a debt she preferred not to state aloud. "Salt. A prayer's worth of grief, rendered down. The Choir calls it *tithing*. You will call it bookkeeping, because that is what it is. Someone must carry the numbers, Yarrow. Someone must know exactly how much we have spent keeping this country dry."

"How many?" His voice came out flat, professional, appalled. "How many people?"

"Since the sealing? Nine thousand, four hundred and six." She said it without looking up, because she had looked it up long ago, and looking again would mean feeling it. "You wanted the ledgers true. That is the truth. We keep the arithmetic exact so the rest of us can sleep."

He thought of the fisherman Coral had smuggled. He thought of the tide of salt that had come inland, answering a prayer the Choir's tithe had failed to cover. The numbers were right. The numbers had always been right. And the rightness of them sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"And me?" he said. "Why am I here?"

The Provost turned to the last page. The old ledger — the one older than the Choir itself, the one with his birth written in it in a hand like roots.

"Because the feeding is failing," she said. "The gate has begun taking payments we did not offer. Prayers answered. Tides where tides don't go. It is waking, Yarrow, and a waking gate wants what built it." Her eyes came up, finally, and they were not cruel. They were worse. They were *accounted*. "It wants its heir. You are the final entry. The payment that closes the account forever."

* * *

Coral heard it before she understood it — the way sound travels strangely through the undercroft, through stone that remembers being a harbor wall. She was two corridors away, kneeling in the smuggling passage she'd used three nights running, when the Provost's voice came through the rock thin as thread:

*"—the payment that closes the account forever."*

She knew whose account. She had known since the god whispered Ines's name to her over a drowning man's prayer, since she'd read his birth in a ledger older than her vows. She had warned him. *Don't go.* He'd gone anyway, because he was Ines, because he believed rooms with ledgers in them were honest.

Her vows hung on her like wet rope. Smuggler-priestess: the Choir had made the title an oxymoron and then punished her for living it. She had sworn obedience to the Provost, poverty to the order, silence about everything she carried through checkpoints in the false bottom of her prayer-box. For six years she had broken the small vows nightly and kept the large one — the one nobody wrote down, the one that said *you may love him but never tell him, never touch him, never cost him anything.*

She stood up in the dark passage.

Tonight the large vow broke too.

She moved fast, the way water moves when it has decided where it's going. Through the gap behind the reliquary shelf. Down the stair the novices weren't supposed to know existed. She counted doors by feel — third, second, first — and then she was outside the round room, in the corridor of green lamps, and the door with no handle was opening from the inside, and Ines was walking out between two enforcers who walked him like cargo.

She had imagined this moment for years and never once imagined it would look like this: him upright, composed, hands loose at his sides, going *willingly*. Not dragged. Escorted. A man being delivered to his own ledger line like a parcel correctly addressed.

"Ines." Her voice cracked the corridor's quiet. "Don't."

One enforcer stepped toward her; the other tightened a grip that wasn't quite a grip, because Ines Yarrow was still, officially, property of the accounting office, and damaged goods depreciate.

"They're going to feed you to it," Coral said. "Nine thousand people, and they'll make you nine thousand and seven, and call it balance."

"I know what they're going to do." He said it calmly, and that calm frightened her more than fear would have. "I've read the entries, Coral. I've read *everything*. If I refuse, they take someone else. They always take someone else. That's the whole design."

"Then let the design fail!"

"It won't fail. It will just bill someone poorer." He looked at her then — really looked, the way he never let himself across the desk — and something passed between them that neither of them would say out loud in front of the enforcers, something six years wide and one breath deep.

They took him down toward the tithe-chamber. Coral stood in the green lamplight and felt her vows dissolve around her like sugar in seawater, and what was left, when the dissolving finished, was not freedom. It was arithmetic of her own:

*If the law owns both of us, then justice will not come from the law.*

* * *

The tithe-chamber was beneath everything — below the ledgers, below the foundations, below even the memory of the harbor. A round stone throat, and at its center the gate itself: not a door anymore, just a standing seam in the world, hairline-thin, humming. When Coral pressed her ear to the outer wall an hour later, having gone down the smugglers' way no enforcer guarded because no enforcer knew it, she could hear it. Like a tuning fork struck underwater. Like a choir holding one note for two hundred years and beginning, very slightly, to waver.

Inside, they had laid Ines on the offering-stone. The Provost read the closing entries aloud — liturgy as invoice, invoice as liturgy — and the seam drank the sound and widened by a width no instrument could measure but every salt-thing in the room could feel.

And then the wavering note broke, and the gate spoke.

Not words. A pressure. A knowing. It reached up through the stone into Ines's blood and showed him — briefly, horribly, tenderly — the country behind it. Green hills going under. A capital with its bells still ringing as the water climbed the towers. His ancestors on the last hilltop, building the seam shut with their own names as mortar, paying with everything except their lives because their lives were the cheap part.

*They paid with their names,* the pressure told him. *You can pay with yours. Or we will take the rest of the world instead.*

He lay there on the cold stone and understood, finally, the shape of the trap. The Provost thought she was buying the country time. The gods thought they were collecting an heir. Both of them were right, and both of them were wrong, and the truth was an entry no ledger had space for.

The Provost gave the signal. The enforcers began the rite.

That was when the smugglers' door opened, and Coral came down into the throat of the world with a stolen knife, a false-bottomed prayer-box full of salt she'd taken off a dead fisherman, and six years of a love she was done pretending wasn't real.

She did not fight the enforcers. Fighting them was a debt she couldn't cover. She did something better: she sang. The forbidden liturgy, the one that unbinds, the one the Choir had outlawed because it lets a soul walk out of a contract. She sang it low, off-key, desperate, and the offering-stone heard it, and the stone — which remembered being a harbor wall, which remembered harbors, which remembered mercy — loosened its hold.

Ines sat up.

"What are you doing?" the Provost demanded, and then, seeing, understanding: "Sister Coral. You are spending your vows."

"Yes." She said it plainly, like a woman settling an account. "All of them. At once. Consider it a withdrawal."

The enforcers closed. The singing got louder. And somewhere above them, faint through two hundred feet of stone, the first salt tide of the new season pushed inland against a levee that had held for a century, and the levee did not hold, and everyone in that chamber would learn it by morning.

Ines took Coral's hand. Not gently — there was no time for gentle. He took it the way you take a rope thrown to a drowning man, and for once in his careful life he did not check the knots first.

"Which way?" he asked.

"Away," she said. "Downriver. Toward the coast. Toward the country that's coming back." Because that was the terrible secret she'd been carrying in her prayer-box along with everything else: the drowned land was rising. The road out of a country that no longer existed was also the road *into* it, and it was the only road left.

They went up the smugglers' stair two at a time, pursued by enforcers who moved like men reading orders they hadn't written, and behind them the Provost stood alone in the tithe-chamber beside a gate that had just been cheated of its payment, doing the only thing she knew how to do.

She opened a fresh ledger.

And wrote, in her perfect, merciless hand: *Debt outstanding. One heir. Interest accruing.*

By dawn they were out of the city, on a road that did not appear on any map the Choir sold, heading east into country where the prayers were already being answered, and the first thing Coral told him — the first true thing either of them said freely, without walls or witnesses — was this:

"I broke every vow I had to get you out. I'd break the ones I haven't taken yet."

Behind them, the water was coming.
