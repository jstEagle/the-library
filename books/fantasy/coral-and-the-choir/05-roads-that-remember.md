## 5. Roads That Remember

The road was wrong, and Coral loved it.

She'd grown up on smugglers' paths — goat tracks, salt marsh causeways, the kind of routes that existed only in the memory of people who needed them. But this was different. This road *moved*. They'd walked it for two days now, and twice it had shifted beneath them like a sleeper turning over: a bend that hadn't been there at dusk appearing by morning, a hill they'd climbed on the way out lying flat behind them when Ines looked back.

"It's remembering," Coral said, when he asked. She said it the way she read ledger entries — flat, certain, already three lines ahead. "The drowned country is pulling its old roads back up out of the water. And these roads remember where they used to go."

"Home," Ines said.

"Don't say it like it's a good thing."

He didn't answer. He'd been quiet since the city, quieter than his usual careful silence, and Coral knew the shape of what he was carrying: the Provost's arithmetic, the ledgers older than the Choir, the fact that his whole ordered life had been a column in someone else's book. She wanted to reach for him. Her vows were broken; there was nothing left to stop her but habit, and habit was the hardest debt of all to discharge.

So instead she taught him the liturgy.

It was forbidden — had been forbidden so long that most priests thought it was a fable, a story the old orders told about themselves. *The Walking Hours*, nine verses sung against the direction of travel, each one naming a road by the name it held before the drowning. The Choir burned the last written copy two hundred years ago. Coral's grandmother had taught it to her mother, who taught it to her, verse by verse, mouth to ear, the way you pass anything you want kept alive and unrecorded.

"You're a priestess," Ines said, when she finished the third verse and made him repeat it back. "You're not supposed to know this."

"I'm a smuggler who wears vestments because they open doors." She smiled without much humor. "The vestments came first, though. My family served the tide gods before the Choir bought the coast. We never stopped. We just got quiet about it."

They walked. The fourth verse named a road called Saltwife's Lament, and when Coral sang it, the path ahead straightened as if relieved to be recognized.

* * *

The village appeared on the evening of the third day, sitting in a fold of hills that no map would have admitted to. It smelled wrong before they saw it — brine and rot and something sweeter underneath, like flowers left too long in standing water.

"They've answered," Coral breathed.

Ines saw it as they came down the slope: the fields. Green, impossibly green, in a season when everything should have been brown and waiting. Wheat stood heavy-headed in rows that ran down into a valley that should have been dry ground, and at the bottom of the valley, where a stream should have trickled, there was a pool of seawater a half-mile wide, calm as a millpond, holding fish that flashed silver in the late light.

The village around it was prosperous. Fat children. Full smokehouses. Doors left unlocked.

And every door bore a mark drawn in dried salt: an eye, weeping.

"We shouldn't stop," Ines said.

"We need food, and you need sleep, and I need to see this." Coral was already walking. "This is what answered prayers look like, Ines. I've spent my whole life hearing about them. I want to count the cost while it's still itemized."

An old woman met them at the well — the only fresh water in sight, guarded like treasure. She looked at Coral's vestments and bowed. She looked at Ines and went white.

"You," she said. "You're one of theirs."

"One of whose?" Coral asked, stepping between them with the smoothness of long practice.

But the woman was looking past her, at Ines, and her cracked lips were moving through something that wasn't a greeting. It took Ines a moment to place it — the cadence of it, the rising fall of it — and then his blood went cold, because it was the liturgy. The Walking Hours. Sung backward. A welcome, or a summons, or both.

"The Tidefather marked your line," the woman said simply. "We dreamed of you before you came. He showed us the heir walking east with salt on his boots, and he told us: *when he passes, give him water, because the ones who built the door will drink last.*"

Ines's hand found Coral's sleeve. "What did he show you after that?"

The old woman's face did something terrible then — softened into grief so worn it had become almost peaceful. "Come to supper," she said. "It's easier to tell over food. Everything's easier here. That's the trouble."

* * *

The supper was abundant and the hall was warm and the villagers watched Ines eat with an attention that made his skin crawl — reverence, he realized, plain and undiluted, the look people gave relics. Coral sat close enough that their shoulders touched, and under the table her hand found his knee and stayed there, an anchor.

"After the first answered prayer," the old woman began — her name was Maren, and she was the village's priestess, though her vestments were just a salt-stiffened shawl — "it rained fish for three days. You can't imagine what that means to people who've buried two children of hunger. Then the pool came, full and sweet with catch. Then the wheat. Every prayer, answered inside a week. The Tidefather is generous."

"And the cost?" Coral asked.

Maren looked at her own hands. "The prayers don't stop being heard. That's the part nobody tells you. When the god is close, every wish is a petition. Old Tomas wished his rheumatism away and woke with hands smooth as a boy's — and couldn't feel them anymore. The miller's wife prayed her husband would love her again like he did when they were young, and he does, he loves her exactly as he did at nineteen, which is to say badly, and constantly, and he weeps all day for a girl who died forty years ago and doesn't understand why he's married to this stranger." Maren's voice didn't waver. "Little Anni prayed her brother would come home from the coast road. So he did. What was left of him. Walking. Smiling. The Tidefather keeps what the sea takes, and it gives back what it likes, and it doesn't check whether you wanted the true thing or just the shape of it."

Silence in the hall. Somewhere outside, the pool lapped gently at its new shores, patient as a creditor.

"Why are you telling us?" Ines asked quietly. "If he's generous, why warn strangers?"

Maren met his eyes. "Because he told us to. Last night, in the dream. He said: *tell the heir what I am, so that when he stands in front of me, he'll know I won't lie to him.*" She smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ines had ever seen. "That's the horror of it, child. He's honest. Every other god I've heard of lies, or hides, or makes you guess. This one tells you exactly what the price is. And people pay anyway. Because hungry is *now*, and the price is always later."

Coral's hand tightened on Ines's knee. Later, in the dark of the loft where Maren bedded them down, she said: "Now you understand what's chasing us east. Not an army. An offer."

"I understood before." His voice was low. "I grew up reading debts, Coral. I know how generosity works when it comes from something that big. It's not generosity. It's a loan."

* * *

They left before dawn, and Maren pressed a sack of bread and smoked fish into Coral's arms and refused payment with a shake of her head. At the edge of the village she caught Ines's wrist, her grip surprisingly strong.

"He'll offer you everything," she said. "Your ancestors' kingdom. Your name in stone instead of paper. And the worst of it — the trap of it — is that he'll mean it. It will all be real. Say the words, heir, and you will have it, every bit of it, forever."

"Then what's the price?"

"That everyone else pays it with you." She let go of his wrist. "Walk fast. The roads are waking faster now. By the time you reach the shore, the country won't be drowned anymore. It'll be *rising*, and it will know your blood, and doors will open for you whether you want them to or not."

They walked. Behind them the green fields glowed faintly in the pre-dawn dark, and Ines did not look back, because he had learned that much about himself already: he could refuse an argument, but he wasn't sure yet he could refuse a home.

The road climbed out of the valley and turned east, and as the sun came up it rearranged itself ahead of them with a sound like a page turning, shortening the miles, hurrying them on.

"It wants me there," Ines said.

"Everything does," Coral said. "The god. The Choir. The Provost's enforcers, who are two days behind us and gaining. The dead, who are probably already awake." She stopped walking. Turned to face him. The light was coming up gold behind her and she looked, for one unguarded moment, exhausted and fierce and young.

"So say it now," she said. "Whatever you've been not-saying since the city. Because tomorrow isn't guaranteed, Ines. Tomorrow might be underwater, or on fire, or full of enforcers, or full of gods. I broke my vows for you and I'd do it again and I am *tired* of carrying that alone."

"Coral—"

"I'm not asking you to say it back. I'm asking you to stop making me be the only one who's honest. It's bad enough being a heretic. Don't make me a fool too."

The road hummed beneath their feet, remembering. Somewhere far behind, horns — the Choir's hunting calls, thin on the wind. Somewhere far ahead, the sea, coming inland to meet them.

Ines took her hand. Held it the way he held a ledger line he'd finally decided to sign.

"When you sang the second verse yesterday," he said, "you got the note wrong. On purpose. To see if I was listening."

"I did not get it wrong—"

"You did. And I've been listening for six years, Coral. Across the desk. Through the ledgers. Every entry you ever read aloud, I heard it twice — once as numbers, once as your voice." His thumb moved across her knuckles. "I'm a careful man. Careful men don't say things they can't take back. But tomorrow isn't guaranteed, so: I love you. I have for years. There. Written down. No erasures."

For a moment she just stared at him, and then she laughed — wet-eyed, disbelieving, furious with relief — and pulled him in by the collar of his coat, and kissed him on a road that remembered being a boulevard in a capital that hadn't existed for two hundred years, while the sun rose on a country busy remembering itself.

When they broke apart, she rested her forehead against his.

"Now we run," she said. "Faster. They'll try to use you, both of them — the god and the Provost. And I have one vow left that I haven't broken yet."

"Which is?"

"The one where I promised to survive you." She took his hand again, hard, and turned them both east. "I'm keeping that one. Whatever it costs."

By midday the road had become a causeway, running out across land that was, mile by mile, turning soft and dark and tidal — and ahead of them, on the horizon, something vast was breaking the surface of the world like a whale coming up to breathe.

A city. Rising.

With its gates already open.
