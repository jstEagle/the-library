## 6. The Drowned Country's Shore

The road stopped pretending to be a road at dawn.

It had been rearranging itself for six days — straightening where they turned aside, softening into mud when they thought of hiding — and now it simply ended at a bluff above the sea, as if it had delivered them and lost interest. Below, the water was wrong. Not storm-wrong. *Level*-wrong, flat as poured pewter all the way to the horizon, and along the shoreline stood rows of white stones that Ines's eyes kept trying to file as markers until he counted them and understood they were columns.

"Those are under water," Coral said quietly. "They're supposed to be under water."

"I know what they're supposed to be." His voice came out flat too. "I've read the survey ledgers. The Choir has measured this coast every year for two hundred years. There is nothing here but sandbar and drowned masonry. That's in writing."

"And yet." Coral crouched at the bluff's edge, wind pulling her grey smuggler's coat sideways, and did the thing she always did before danger: she closed her eyes and moved her lips through three lines of forbidden liturgy, fast, like a woman checking her knives.

Ines watched her mouth instead of the sea. He had been watching that mouth for years across a ledger desk, and it still embarrassed him how much of his attention it owned. Six days on the run together and she had said *I love you* exactly once, at night, facing away from him, in the tone of someone reporting a debt they couldn't pay. He had said it back. They had not discussed it since. There hadn't been time, and there might never be time again, and somehow that made it easier to carry than if they'd had years to argue about it.

"The tide's going out," she said suddenly.

He looked. It was. Impossibly, visibly, the pewter sea was withdrawing from the shore like a held breath being let go, and as it went, it uncovered more columns, then arches between them, then whole streets laid bare and glistening, running down and away beneath the waterline in a grid so regular it could only be a city.

"That's Vell," Ines said. The name came out of him without permission, the way a sneeze does. "The capital. That's—" He stopped. He had never seen Vell. No one living had seen Vell. But he knew its street-plan the way he knew his own handwriting, because somewhere in the oldest ledger, in ink older than the Choir, the boy who would become him had once copied it out as an exercise.

Coral stood. She didn't touch him, but she angled her body so her shoulder nearly brushed his, which was its own kind of touching. "Your family built a gate here," she said. "Maybe the city remembers the family."

"The city isn't supposed to remember anything. The city is supposed to be *drowned*." He heard the crack in his own voice and hated it. "This is what the Provost was afraid of. This is what the sacrifices were for."

"Then we walk down while it's dry," Coral said, "and we find out what your blood is worth."

*

The streets were wet but solid, and the dead were shopping.

That was the detail Ines could not get past: not the architecture, though the architecture was enough to stop his heart — colonnades of white stone furred with barnacle-silk, doors taller than any door had a right to be, windows of glass so old and thick it ran green like bottle-bottoms — no, not even the smell, brine and lamp-oil and bread, bread, actual baking bread drifting from an actual bakery. It was the people. A woman arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger over eels. Two boys racing hoops down a street that had been seabed that morning. An old man sweeping his doorstep with the particular resigned rhythm of a man who sweeps it every day and will every day after.

None of them cast quite the right shadow. In the low morning light their shadows lagged a half-step behind them, like something following politely.

"Don't look at the shadows," Coral murmured. "Don't correct anyone. Don't tell them anything about the water."

"They don't know?"

"They can't afford to know. Look at the baker." She nodded with her chin. Through the bakery window a broad-armed man was handing loaves to children, laughing, flour to his elbows — and on the shelf behind him, among the jars, sat a small brass bell crusted white with salt, and below the shelf, a neat row of little clay lamps, unlit, each one labeled in careful script. Coral read the labels aloud under her breath. "*For my mother. For my daughter. For the man I wronged.*"

"Memorials," Ines said. "He lights one when—"

"When someone dies. And then forgets why they're there by supper." Her jaw tightened. "The liturgy warned me. *They rise with the country and remember nothing that would spoil the rising.* Grief doesn't keep underwater, apparently. So they just—" she made a small, helpless gesture at the whole shining street, the eels, the hoops, the bread, "—keep living it. Every day. With the lamps waiting."

A boy on a hoop swerved close, saw Ines, and stopped dead.

"You're a Yarrow," the boy said. Delighted. Certain. As if announcing rain.

"I'm sorry?"

"A Yarrow! From the Gatehouse!" The boy pointed up the long avenue ahead, toward a structure rising out of the retreating sea at the city's heart — a black ring within a white ring, vast, half-built and half-drowned, scaffolds of pale stone climbing its flanks where masons had plainly abandoned work mid-course two hundred years ago. The gate. Their gate. His. "You have the look. Everyone knows the Yarrow look. Come on, come see!" He grabbed Ines's sleeve with cold little fingers and tugged, and Ines, absurdly, let himself be tugged, because refusing felt like cruelty, and because some traitorous part of him wanted to be known.

Coral caught his other arm. "Ines."

"It's fine," he said, and wasn't sure it was.

*

The doors began opening before he reached them.

The first was a shopfront — a chandler's, ropes and lanterns stacked in salty pyramids — whose double doors swung inward as he passed, unbidden, both halves, though the chandler inside only glanced up and nodded, unsurprised, as if doors did that for everyone. The second was a garden gate. The third was a temple door heavy enough to take four men, oak banded in bronze gone green, and it opened silently, smoothly, the moment his shadow touched the threshold stone, and did not open at all for Coral when she tried it a moment later.

She stood outside it, arms crossed, looking at the door and then at him with an expression he'd last seen on her face the night she told him about her vows: the look of a woman measuring exactly how much trouble love is worth.

"Every door in this city is going to do that," she said.

"Apparently."

"Ines. Do you understand what that means? Doors don't open for visitors. Doors open for *owners*." She pushed past him into the temple — the door obligingly admitting her in his wake — and turned in the middle of the cool dim nave, where hundreds of votive lamps hung unlit from the rafters like a ceiling of sleeping stars. "This whole country sank with your family's work in its foundations. If the country is coming back, it's coming back *around* you. You're not a guest here. You're the thing it's rebuilding itself to fit."

"That's not an inheritance," he said. "That's a trap with furniture."

"Now you're learning."

But he had stopped listening, because at the far end of the nave stood a statue, and the statue was of a man holding a ledger.

Not a god. A man — youngish, tired-eyed, ink-stained fingers, the slight forward stoop of someone who has spent decades bent over a desk. Carved in white stone furred green at the hem, and at its base, a plaque in lettering Ines read without wanting to:

**YARROW THE THIRD, KEEPER OF THE LEDGERS, WHO COUNTED SO THE CITY NEED NOT FEAR.**

His hands were shaking. He put them flat against his thighs and made them stop, mostly.

"That's not possible," he said. "I'm the last. The Choir's records say the line ended. They've said so for two hundred years. It's the whole reason they needed—" He stopped. Recalculated. Said it anyway, because Coral deserved the truth and lying to a smuggler-priestess is a professional error. "Because if the line ended, there'd be no heir left to pay."

"Or," Coral said softly, walking toward the statue, "the Choir *needed* the line to have ended, so they wrote it that way, and kept the last heir on payroll counting their coins where they could watch him." She looked back at him over her shoulder. "How many years, Ines? How many years did they keep you at that desk?"

"Eleven."

"And every audit you ever flagged as impossible — the self-updating entries, the debts paying themselves — you filed it as an anomaly."

"Four hundred and six anomalies," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "I catalogued them. I numbered them. I thought I was doing my job."

"You were doing theirs."

The temple door boomed shut behind them.

Both of them spun. It hadn't been wind; there was no wind in the nave. And now, from everywhere at once — from the unlit lamps, from the wet stones underfoot, from the sea pressing patiently against every wall of the risen city — came a sound like a tide deciding to come in, deep and total and unhurried, and threaded through it, unmistakable, a voice. Many voices braided into one. Old ones. Patient ones. Ones that had been counting, too.

***YARROW,*** said the water.

Coral's hand found his and gripped hard, vows and fear and love all in the pressure of it. "Whatever they offer," she whispered, "remember the lamps. Remember the ones that never got lit."

***YARROW,*** said the water again, warmer now, almost gentle, a grandfather's voice from a house he'd never lived in. ***YOU KEPT OUR NUMBERS FAITHFULLY. COME AND BE PAID.***

At the far end of the nave, behind the statue of his ancestor, a door Ines had not noticed — tall, black-ringed, carved with the gate's own geometry — began, without his consent, without anyone's consent, to open.

And this time, he felt it in his blood.
