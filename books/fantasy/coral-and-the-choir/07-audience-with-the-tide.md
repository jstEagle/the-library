## 7. Audience with the Tide

The hall had been waiting for him.

That was Ines's first thought, and his second, and by the third he understood it wasn't a thought at all but a fact pressed into him like a seal into wax. The throne room of the risen city stood half-finished — walls of pale coral-stone rising to meet scaffolds of water that held their shape without falling — and every inch of it was angled toward the chair at the far end. Not a throne, quite. A seat carved from a single piece of drowned timber, barnacled at the base, dry as bone everywhere else. It faced the door. The door he had entered.

His blood had opened it. He hadn't asked it to.

"They know you're coming," Coral murmured beside him. She'd braided salt-grass into her hair the way her liturgy taught, the old warding pattern, though what it could ward against in here he couldn't guess. "The whole city knows. I heard them saying your name in the market square. *Yarrow.* They said it like a prayer."

"People shouldn't pray to me."

"No." She touched his wrist, brief, warm. "They shouldn't."

They walked the length of the hall alone. The dead were there — he could feel them the way you feel weather through a wall — but they kept to the galleries and the shadowed colonnades, watching with the patient hunger of an audience before the curtain rises. A woman in robes two centuries out of fashion pressed her hand to her mouth when he passed. An old man wept without sound. None of them approached. Something had told them to wait.

At the base of the dais, the tide itself rose to meet them: not water, exactly, but the memory of water, a standing column of grey-green that turned slowly on its own axis and had no reflection in the marble floor. When Ines stopped before it, the column stopped turning.

*Heir,* said the tide, in a voice like a harbor bell rung underwater. *Son of builders. Last of the nine lines.*

"I'm told you have an offer," Ines said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. Years of reading numbers aloud to men who could ruin him had taught him that much: sound certain, and the room believes the ledger is balanced even when it isn't.

*We have a kingdom.*

It spread for him then. He didn't see it so much as remember it, which was worse — the gods poured the memory directly into the blood they shared with him, and his own veins became the map. A capital of white towers above a harbor thick with masts. Terraced vineyards climbing hills that were now seabed. Nine families, nine gates, one country stitched together by contracts older than any surviving law. He smelled bread baking in a street that had been silt for two hundred years. He heard a language he'd never learned and understood every word.

"You're showing me what was," Ines said carefully. "Not what will be."

*What can be again.* The column brightened at its heart, green shading to gold. *You opened the small doors without knowing how. Your blood remembers the great one. Open it fully — not the crack your ancestors sealed, not the trickle we have squeezed through in answer to desperate prayers, but the gate as it was made to stand. And we will raise this country from the water entire. Every stone restored. Every name restored. The dead returned to their harvests, their halls, their children's children. And you—*

The tide shaped itself, briefly, into the figure of a man wearing a crown of woven current.

*—will be king of it. As your line was always meant to be. The ledger of your family ends with a debt unpaid, boy. We are offering you the means to pay it in full.*

Ines's hands were shaking. He put them behind his back, an auditor's habit, hiding the evidence.

"That's not an offer," he said. "That's an invoice."

*Is there a difference?*

"There is where I come from."

Coral made a small sound beside him — almost a laugh, almost a warning — and the tide turned its attention on her, and Ines felt the pressure of that attention like a change in depth. The gold light bent toward her.

*Priestess of the new choir,* the god said, and there was something in the voice now that hadn't been there before. Something colder. *You smuggle prayers across our water. You teach fishermen to ask us for small mercies and then help them flee when the mercies arrive. Do you think we don't keep accounts?*

"I count on it," Coral said. "Everything keeps accounts. That's the problem."

*Then hear ours. Stand aside, and be rewarded. Resist, and be recorded.*

"Recorded as what?"

*As a debt.*

She didn't flinch. Ines watched her not flinch and thought: she has been standing in front of things larger than herself her whole life, and the Choir only taught her to do it kneeling. He stepped half between her and the tide, and the dead in the galleries rustled like pages turning.

"I need to understand the terms," he said. "Fully open the gate. And in exchange?"

*A nation. A crown. Your ancestors' work completed. Your name written into history at last instead of hidden in ledgers.*

"And the living country on the other side of that gate. Mine. Coral's. The fisherman whose village flooded last week because someone prayed to you for rain."

Silence. Even the dead went still.

*The borderlands lie low,* the god said at last, and the honesty of it was the most frightening thing yet. *Water finds its level. We rise; some shores must yield. This is not cruelty. This is arithmetic.*

"Arithmetic." Ines laughed, short and joyless. "I've done arithmetic my whole life. Do you know what I learned? That every number in a ledger belongs to somebody. There's no such thing as a column that balances itself. Somebody pays. Always somebody pays, and the person holding the pen just gets to choose who."

*Your ancestors chose,* the tide said softly. *They built the gate to trade with powers beyond the world, grew rich on impossible cargoes, and when the price came due they paid it with their country instead of themselves. Sealed the door. Drowned the debt. Two hundred years of your line keeping books on other people's losses while sitting safe on the dry side of the water.* The column darkened toward deep-water blue. *We have waited patiently, heir. We have accepted crumbs. Now the account is called in, and you flinch at the terms your own blood wrote.*

The truth of it landed in him like cold water in the lungs. Because the god was right, or right enough. The Choir's ledgers, the gate-builders' ledgers, the careful columns of his own neat hand — all of it rested on a founding entry written by people who decided that their survival outweighed a nation's. His inheritance wasn't a kingdom withheld from him. It was a crime, deferred, accruing interest.

And they were offering to let him commit the same crime in reverse, with himself holding the pen.

*Think,* the god said, gentler now, which was worse. *You have spent your life invisible. Small rooms. Small sums. A name that opens no doors. We offer you a name that opens the world.*

And Ines looked down at his own hands — ink-stained, precise, hands that had never held anything heavier than a pen — and he wanted it. That was the terrible thing. He wanted it the way a starving man wants bread: completely, honestly, without shame. To walk through a white tower and have everyone know his face. To matter at the scale of harbors. To take the lonely, careful, ordered life he'd built out of other people's debts and trade it, once, for something vast.

He felt Coral watching him see it. She didn't say a word. That was her gift and he knew it — she never pulled him back by force; she just stood where the truth could find him.

* * *

They walked out along the harbor road afterward, because neither of them could bear the galleries and their patient eyes. The risen city fell away beneath them in terraces, half-restored, half-silt: a palace here, a drowned ruin there, the dead tending gardens that bloomed in seawater. Somewhere below, a child was singing a song in the old language. The child had died two hundred years ago.

"He showed you the crown," Coral said finally.

"He showed me everything."

"And?"

Ines leaned on the balustrade. Below, the sea lay flat as sheet metal, waiting.

"When the Provost offered me up as a sacrifice, I hated him for treating people as entries," he said. "For deciding the sum mattered more than the person. And that thing on the throne — it did the same arithmetic, just with bigger columns. Raise a nation, drown the borderlands. Balance achieved. And I stood there wanting to sign it." He shook his head slowly. "You know what the worst part is? It wouldn't even be a lie, what they're offering. The kingdom would be real. The bread would smell like bread. And everyone in it would owe their existence to a gate left open, and every year the water would climb a little higher on someone else's shore, and I'd sit in that chair and call it arithmetic, because that's what my family has always called it."

"Ines—"

"My whole life I thought I was poor," he said. "Small name, small rooms, small sums. Turns out I'm an heir. Turns out the estate is a lever long enough to move a sea, and every god and guild in the world wants my hand on it." He turned to look at her, and the evening light caught the salt-grass in her hair. "An inheritance is supposed to be a gift. This one's a leash. They dressed it as a crown so I wouldn't notice the collar."

"So what do we do?" she asked. Quietly. Not doubting — asking. She had a way of asking that made the answer feel like a shared decision rather than a decree, and he loved her for it, and had loved her for years across the width of a ledger desk, and had told her once on a road that remembered them, and would tell her again every day they had left if that's what the days allowed.

"The debt is real," Ines said. "I believe that. My family wrote it, the Choir fed it, the gods collected on it, and it doesn't go away because the terms are monstrous. But nobody gets to choose who pays it. Not the Provost with his sacrifices. Not the tide with its crown. And not me, trading strangers for a throne." He straightened. "There's a third entry. One nobody's written yet."

"Which is?"

"The payer chooses the currency." He was already moving, back up the terrace stairs, toward the half-built palace and the gate at its heart, walking fast enough that she had to lengthen her stride to keep pace. "My ancestors paid with a country. The Choir paid with other people's children. The gods want me to pay with yours."

"And you'll pay with—"

"Something they can't spend," Ines said, "and can't replace."

Behind them, across the flat grey water, a horn sounded — three notes, repeated, disciplined, human. Coral stopped dead on the stair and turned, and Ines watched her face do the arithmetic before he heard it himself.

"That's a Choir signal," she said. "Advance and hold. That's—" She counted again, lips moving, and went pale. "That's not a patrol. Ines. That's the full enforcer fleet. They've found the shore."

Far off, at the edge of sight, lanterns were kindling on the sea like a ledger opening its eyes.
