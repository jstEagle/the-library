## 8. Every Debt Comes Due

The bells of the risen city rang wrong.

Coral had grown up on bells — chapel bells, market bells, the Choir's own measured chimes that marked the hours of prayer and accounting alike. These were none of those. They came from under the water that still pooled in the lower streets of the half-restored capital, bronze mouths drowned for two centuries and now raised dripping into the air, and they tolled with a lag, a drag, as if sound itself had to climb out of the deep to reach them. Every stroke landed a half-beat late against her pulse.

She stood on the liturgy line she had chalked across the Plaza of Tides at dawn, alone, because alone was what was left.

Ines had gone up to the gate an hour ago and had not come back down, and she could not decide which frightened her more: that he might fail up there, or that he might succeed at something she couldn't follow him into.

Then the harbor mouth filled with lanterns, and she stopped thinking about Ines at all.

They came by sea, because the Choir always settled its accounts by the shortest route. Three long black hulls sliding through water that should have been too shallow for any keel, riding a tide that had no business existing this far north of the drowned coast. Enforcers lined the rails in their grey oilcloth coats, faces anonymous beneath their hoods, and at their prow stood the Provost's own tallymaster, holding the ledger-chain that meant writs had been signed and fees assessed and this whole city, dead and living alike, declared receivable.

Coral set her feet on the chalked line and began the forbidden liturgy.

It wasn't much to look at. Words in a language the Choir had spent two hundred years suppressing, syllables older than paper. She'd learned it in pieces, stolen from a smuggler who'd stolen it from a priestess who'd been burned for it, and she had never once said it aloud all the way through until tonight. Her voice cracked on the third phrase. She kept going.

The line of chalk began to glow like a coal under ash.

* * *

Ines heard the bells stop and knew what had replaced them: boots.

He was in the antechamber of the gate, if you could call it a chamber. The gate had no fixed shape; from below it had looked like an archway, from the causeway like a well-mouth, and here, close, it was simply a place where the world thinned, where he could see through the skin of things to water moving on the far side of everything. The gods were gathered around it the way creditors gather around a debtor's deathbed. They did not speak. They didn't need to. Their offer still hung in the air from the audience he had walked out of: *your name made great, your kingdom restored, one door opened.*

And now, behind him, the sound of the Choir coming to collect by force what he would not give freely.

"Ines." The Provost's voice carried up the stairwell, unhurried, familiar, the voice of every audit meeting of the last nine years. "Come down. You're not in trouble. You're in arithmetic."

He turned. The old man climbed the last steps with enforcers at his shoulders, and his coat was dry despite the crossing, and his eyes held the mild disappointment Ines had seen him wear over misfiled entries.

"The borderlands are flooding," Ines said. "Villages on the Sedge are underwater. That's your arithmetic?"

"That is collateral." The Provost spread his hands. "The gate must be closed properly, boy — sealed under *our* seal, with our terms, our ledger, or it opens again in fifty years and drowns three nations instead of one. Your little rebellion has forced my hand. I needed you willing. Now I only need you present."

"And Coral?"

The Provost's expression didn't change, which was answer enough.

Something in Ines went very quiet and very cold. He had spent his whole life believing in order — in the beautiful, terrible rightness of balanced columns, of debts honored, of a world where everything owed was eventually paid. He saw now that he had confused balance with justice. A ledger can balance perfectly and record a crime. The Choir had been balancing its books for two centuries on the backs of the drowned, and calling it stewardship.

"No," he said.

"Excuse me?"

"I choose neither side." His voice sounded strange to him, calm, final. "Not yours. Not theirs. Mine."

He turned back to the gate and put his hand into the thin place, and the thin place recognized his blood and opened like a hand unclenching.

Behind him the Provost snapped an order. Boots rushed forward. And then stopped — because from below, rising up the stairwell like water filling a hold, came Coral's voice, and the whole gate shuddered at the sound of it.

* * *

Coral felt the enforcers before she saw them break formation — six of them wading off the black ships' boats toward her line, cutters drawn, moving in the flat economical way of people who had done violence many times and filed it afterward.

The chalk was burning low. Her throat was raw. The liturgy had maybe a hundred more words left in it before the line failed, and the line was all that stood between the Choir and the gate, all that kept the plaza from becoming a battlefield where the dead of the risen city — gentle, confused, terribly fragile dead — would be trampled by living men with orders.

*No one will thank you,* she thought, and found the thought steadying rather than bitter. That was the secret heart of the smuggler's creed she'd traded her vows for: you do the run because it's right, not because there's a payout. Nobody thanks the ferryman. Nobody writes the ferryman into the ledger.

The first enforcer reached the line and swung.

The chalk flared. His blade stopped mid-arc as though it had struck glass, and the recoil threw him into the shallows, and Coral sang louder, tears cutting tracks through the salt spray on her face, because she knew exactly how much of herself this was costing and she paid it anyway, word after word after word.

The second and third enforcers came together. The line held. The fourth went around, along the harbor wall, outflanking her chalk entirely—

—and stopped, because Ines Yarrow stepped out of the gate's antechamber onto the top of the seaward stair, and every god in the plaza turned to look at him, and every enforcer too, and even the tide paused between strokes.

"I'm the payment," he called down, and his voice rang off the drowned bells. "Me. Not the city. Not the borderlands. Not her." He pointed at Coral, and his hand was shaking and his voice was not. "So if anyone touches her before I've finished settling this account, the account fails. Do you understand? All of you. It fails, and everyone collects nothing but water."

Silence, except for the lagging bells and the lap of a sea in the wrong place.

The tallymaster's chain clinked as he lowered it. The Provost, at the foot of the stair, wore for the first time in nine years an expression Ines couldn't read, and realized it was fear — the fear of a man watching the only asset that mattered walk toward a rival counter.

"Ines," the Provost said quietly, "whatever they promised you—"

"They didn't promise me anything I wanted," Ines said. "That's how I knew."

* * *

He came down the stairs through the parted ranks like a man walking through his own funeral, which, in a sense he was preparing, was accurate. Coral broke the liturgy just long enough to reach him, her hands finding his sleeves, gripping hard.

"You came down," she said. "You came *down.*"

"To tell them the terms changed." He was pale as the chalk. "Coral — when I go through, whatever happens at the gate —"

"Don't." Her voice broke on it. "Don't narrate it. Don't make it a goodbye. Just do it fast and let me be angry at you after."

"There won't be an after where I—"

"I know." She pressed her forehead to his, salt and lamplight and the smell of ledgers that clung to him even now. "I know exactly what you're about to spend. So listen to me once, Ines Yarrow, heir of builders, keeper of impossible accounts: ledgers forget. Paper burns. Water gets in. But I don't. Whatever you write yourself out of, I am keeping the copy. Unofficial. Off the books. Do you hear me? You will exist in one place in all the world, and it will be here—" she struck her own breastbone with a closed fist, "—and no god, no guild, no gate gets to audit that."

For a moment, in the middle of the storming city, with the flood climbing the harbor steps and the Choir regrouping on the black ships and the dead watching from their half-built windows, he smiled. It was the smile she had loved across the ledger desk for nine years, the small private one he thought nobody saw.

"Hold the line," he said. "Just a little longer."

Then he walked past her toward the gate, and the gods rose up around him like a tide deciding, and the Provost shouted something about writs and property and inheritance, and Ines Yarrow answered none of it.

Coral turned back to the harbor, knelt, and rewrote the first broken curve of chalk with fingers that would not stop shaking.

The enforcers came on anyway. Of course they did. Men with orders rarely believe the arithmetic has changed until the sum lands on them. She met the first blade with the liturgy and the second with her shoulder and the third with blood that was not entirely hers, and she sang, hoarse and furious and unthanked, while above her the thin place in the world began — slowly, at first, like a door remembering it was ever a door — to open.

And from somewhere inside it, faint under the tolling of the drowned bells, she heard the scratch of a pen beginning to write.
