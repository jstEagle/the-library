## 1. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

There is a particular arithmetic to staying alive in the Thornfell passes, and I had gotten very good at it. Better than good. Six years of it will do that—six years of weighing every risk down to the copper coin, of knowing exactly how much luck a man can spend before the mountain calls in his debts.

Take, for instance, the problem of a mule.

A mule carrying salt is worth a toll. A mule carrying salt and a false bottom of fever-bark tincture is worth a hanging. And a mule whose saddlebags also hold eleven small charms stitched into the linings—warding knots, mostly, the kind a shepherd's wife pays two coppers apiece for so her children don't cough themselves into the churchyard—is worth a fire. So you split the load. You send the mule down the western switchback with Tam Corrow, who is slow but honest, and you take the eastern path yourself with nothing on your back but flour, a hunting bow you can't actually use, and a face that says *I am the least interesting man you will meet today.*

That last part is the real cargo. Everything else is packaging.

I'd been doing this long enough that the calculations ran underneath my thoughts like water under ice. Distance to the next waystation: three hours, if the fog held. Coin in my boot: forty-one coppers, two silver bits, and a charm-token I was supposed to deliver to Widow Hesk and hadn't yet, because its knot had come loose and re-tying a warding charm on an exposed cliffside is the sort of thing that gets your fingers frozen to the thread. Risk of patrol on the low road this time of month: moderate. The king's men didn't love the mountains. They came up when they had to, grumbled like wet cats, and went back down to wherever kings kept their men warm.

That was the whole trick of Thornfell: it was too poor to be worth conquering and too steep to bother with. The empire's maps showed squiggles where we lived. Squiggles were good for business.

The morning was doing that grey thing it does up there, where the sky and the stone can't quite decide where one ends and the other begins, and I was halfway along the ledge path above Hollowmere when I felt the itch at the base of my skull that meant one of my knots had snagged something. Not danger, exactly. More like a fisherman feeling a tug on a line he'd half forgotten he set. I stopped, pretended to adjust my bootlace—it's remarkable what a man will do to his boots over the course of a day—and let my attention slide sideways into the thin, warm place where the magic lives.

The ward-charm on Tam Corrow's mule-bags had brushed against someone else's working. Fresh. Recent. Threaded through the trail-side brush like a tripwire made of attention rather than rope.

Someone was scrying the western pass.

I finished tying my bootlace with hands that wanted very much not to be shaking, and stood up, and did what I always did, which was nothing. I became a man with flour and a bad knee, and I walked on east, and I did not look toward the western pass even once, because looking is a kind of telling, and I had spent six years learning not to tell anyone anything.

* * *

The inn at Hollowmere was called the Drowned Goat, which told you most of what you needed to know about the town's relationship with optimism. It sat where two trade paths met like arguments, and it served beer that tasted of regret and smoke, and it was the closest thing I had to an office.

Berta kept it. She was sixty if she was a day, built like the doorframe she'd widened twice to fit through, and she had never once asked me what was in my bags. This was not kindness. This was professional courtesy between people who understood that questions are a currency, and some currencies devalue fast.

"Flour," I said, setting a coin on the bar.

"Bread," she said, taking it, and slid me a bowl of something brown and hot, and that was our entire transaction of words for the evening. Some friendships are built on more conversation. Ours was built on less, and it has lasted longer than most marriages I've seen.

I took my usual table—the one in the corner with sightlines to both doors and a wall at my back, because six years ago I'd learned that walls at your back are cheaper than armor—and I ate, and I listened.

You can learn everything from an inn if you shut up long enough. That night the room had a different texture to it. The drovers at the big table weren't complaining about tolls; they were talking lower, closer, the way people talk about sickness in the family. I caught fragments. *Down in the vale.* *Sealed orders.* *They took the miller's daughter at Wetherford, and her own uncle signed the witness list.*

And then a tinker fresh up from the low country said the word, and I felt it go through the room like a nail through a plank.

*"Gift-hunt,"* he said. *"Royal edict. Every gifted soul in the realm to be seized or burned, no appeal, no fine. They're calling it the Crown's Purity." He said it the way you'd say the name of a storm you could see coming over the ridge.*

Berta refilled my cup without being asked. Her hand was steady. Her eyes weren't.

I want to tell you I leapt up then. That I knew, in that moment, that my life had changed. But the truth is smaller and stupider: I did arithmetic. Because that's what I do. That's what keeps me breathing.

Item: the edict was new, proclaimed in the vale, and news travels slower than fear but faster than sense. Say a fortnight before it crawled up into the high passes in any official form. Item: patrols would start on the low roads, sweep upward. Item: my face was known in nine villages as the flour man, and unknown in all of them as anything else, which had been the point. Item: I had eleven undelivered charms, four outstanding debts owed to me, three debts I owed, and one client—

One client I could not do arithmetic about.

Because here is the flaw in every ledger I ever kept: Mistress Alba. My master. The woman who taught me the knots and the wards and the quiet ways, who taught me that magic is not thunder but thread, not conquest but mending—who taught me, above all, that the gift is a debt you pay forward or it rots in you. She was seventy-something and stubborn as a landslide, living alone in her cottage up past the Grey Stair with her goats and her grudges and her books, and she was exactly, precisely, perfectly the sort of woman the new edict existed to burn.

I sat there with my brown soup and did the cruelest calculation of my life: *if I go north tonight, hard, through the Splinter Pass, I am over the border and gone in nine days, and nobody in this room knows my true name anyway.*

And underneath it, quieter, in a voice that sounded like hers: *Emrys. You can run from hunters. You cannot run from yourself.*

I paid for my bed. I lay on it. I did not sleep.

* * *

Morning came up grey and mean, and I rose and did the thing I had decided to do, which was the only thing I could have done and called myself a man: I loaded the mule with the last of the contraband—salt below, fever-bark in the false bottom, and the eleven charms wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, plus the mended ward-token for Widow Hesk—and I pointed us not north toward the border, but west and up. Toward the Grey Stair. Toward Alba's cottage.

Tam Corrow found me at the town's edge, tightening a strap. "West?" he said. "Nothing up west but the old witch and bad weather."

"Bad weather pays better this season," I said.

He laughed, because Tam laughs at anything he doesn't understand, which is a great deal, and took his mule down the switchbacks toward the vale, and I watched him go and thought, without any evidence at all: *that's the last time I'll see Tam Corrow laughing at something.*

The climb was ugly. Fog came in off the high snowfields around midday and turned the world into wool, and I navigated by feel and by the little warmth-marks Alba had taught me to leave on stones years ago—a hedge-witch's road signs, invisible to anyone who hadn't been taught to read them. Warmth on the left-hand boulder. Faint hum in a hollow pine. Six years since I'd walked this path, and the mountain remembered me anyway, and I hated how much that comforted me.

Her cottage sat in its clearing with the afternoon light going long and amber across it, and from a hundred yards it looked exactly as it always had: stone walls, turf roof, goat-track worn in the yard, woodsmoke.

Except the smoke was wrong.

Woodsmoke rises. This hung. Low, flat, clinging to the roofline like something reluctant to leave, and it smelled—not of pine or peat—but of paper. Of burned thread. Of burnt hair.

I know the smell of a ward breaking. I've heard it described as a scream, and that's poetic nonsense; it's worse than that. It's silence. It's the sudden absence of a hum you'd stopped hearing years ago, the way you stop hearing your own heart until it stops. Alba's wards had hummed over that clearing for forty years. Every bird within a mile had nested inside their shelter without knowing why.

Now the clearing was quiet in a way clearings are not supposed to be quiet, and the door of the cottage stood open, and the door of the cottage should never stand open.

I should have run then. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, honestly: Emrys Pemberton, who survived six years by knowing exactly when to walk away, stood at the treeline with a mule and eleven illegal charms against his chest, and did not run, because the door was open and she was seventy-three and the smoke smelled like her books.

I left the mule tied and crossed the yard.

Inside, the cottage had been turned inside out—not ransacked the way thieves do it, fast and greedy, but searched the way soldiers do it, slow and thorough, shelves emptied onto floors in neat rows, drawers folded back, even the flour bin sifted. Someone had been looking for something specific and had either found it or not, and neither possibility made me feel warmer.

The hearth had been fed. Not with wood.

Books. Grimoires. Forty years of her field-notes, her remedies, her knot-ciphers, pages in her cramped left-slanting hand that I used to fall asleep reading like other boys fall asleep on maps. Burned in her own hearth, deliberately, completely—except that whoever fed the fire had done it in haste, and haste leaves edges.

I found one page, half-unburned, curled at the corner like a dead leaf, and I picked it up with fingers that shook and I read it and it meant nothing to me. It was in cipher, the old knot-code, and it referenced something called *the ninth binding*, and there was a phrase at the bottom that I'd never seen in any of her teachings, written larger than the rest, pressed hard into the paper like she'd been angry or afraid:

*It wears the crown. Do not let it finish the count.*

The rest of the page was ash.

I stood there holding a fragment of a sentence I didn't understand, in a ransacked house that smelled like my childhood, and I did the bravest and stupidest thing I have ever done, which was to walk through every room calling her name in a voice I barely recognized as mine.

No answer. Goats gone. Garden gate open. No blood, which I fixed on desperately—no blood, no body, no bones. Taken, then. Not killed. *Why would they take her?* Hunters burn the gifted; that was the whole edict, clean and simple as a knife. Unless she wasn't taken by hunters. Unless whoever searched this house wasn't wearing a uniform, and wanted what she knew more than what she was.

*It wears the crown.*

I put the scrap of page inside my shirt, next to the eleven charms, where it lay against my skin like a coal.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the ridge to the east, and through the thinning grey I saw them: five riders, maybe a mile distant, coming up the switchback trail at a pace that meant business, with the low sun flashing on something metal. Uniforms. The king's grey and black. A patrol, this far up, this fast—patrols didn't come up the Grey Stair. Patrols had never come up the Grey Stair, not once in six years, because there was nothing up here worth a day's ride.

Except now there was. Except now there was an edict, and a burned cottage, and a missing old woman, and a man standing in her yard with stolen pages against his chest and a mule's-load of felonies at his back.

Here is the last piece of the smuggler's arithmetic, the sum of six years of careful sums: I calculated the odds of walking out of that clearing unnoticed. I calculated the odds of reaching the mule, reaching the treeline, reaching the Splinter Pass, reaching the border. I got numbers. They were bad numbers. They were the worst numbers I'd ever tallied, because the fog was lifting and the ground was open and they were already close enough that I could hear tack jingling.

And I stood there in the yard of the woman who taught me everything, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the invisible life was over. That the account was closed. That whatever happened in the next ten minutes, Emrys Pemberton the flour-man, the unremarkable man, the squiggle on the empire's map—he was done. Burned in the same fire as her books.

They topped the ridge. The lead rider raised an arm and pointed, and the five of them put their horses down the slope toward me at a canter, and I did the one thing left on the ledger.

I reached into the thin, warm place—the place I'd sworn off, the place that got masters burned—and I pulled.

The first thing I did was simple, and I did it without thinking, the way you flinch: I dropped a veil over the yard, a haze-knot, cheap and crude and old as hedges, the magical equivalent of standing behind a curtain. It wouldn't fool trained seekers for more than a minute. But riders trust their eyes, and for one minute the clearing would show them nothing but fog and goats and a cold cottage, and minutes are coin to a man like me.

Then I ran for the mule, cut the tether, slapped its haunch toward the western trail—sorry, mule, nothing personal, better lost cargo than a lost life—and I went the other way, downhill, toward the scree and the river gorge, running like I hadn't run since I was a boy, with my lungs tearing and my legs burning and the veil unraveling behind me thread by thread.

I heard the shout go up behind me. I heard a horn, one long note, the sound that means *quarry sighted* in every hunt-song ever sung. I heard hooves picking down the slope after me, patient, tireless, and I understood that horses beat men on open ground, and that I had perhaps four hundred yards of scree before the gorge, and that I was going to have to spend magic like a drunkard spends his last coin, and that whatever I spent, they'd feel it. Every seeker for fifty miles would feel a working that size. I might as well have lit a bonfire on the ridge with my name nailed to it.

The smuggler's arithmetic had one line left in it, and the line said: *you are already caught, so spend it all.*

So I did.

I threw a stumble-knot backwards up the slope without looking, and heard a horse scream and a man curse. I threw a grip-spell on the scree under my own feet, the forbidden kind, the kind that uses the mountain's own weight against itself, and the whole hillside shifted and groaned and became briefly, treacherously, gloriously liquid, and I rode a small avalanche of loose stone down into the gorge while behind me two riders reined up hard at the edge of a slope that had just eaten its own footing.

I hit the river shallows, soaked, gasping, alive. On the far side the gorge wall rose dark and steep and full of the narrow cracks and ledges that only goat-herders and fools knew, and I started climbing it with hands that bled, and half-way up I looked back, just once.

They were regrouping at the gorge rim. And the lead rider had dismounted, and stood very still at the edge, head slightly tilted, in the posture of a person listening to something far away—which is exactly what a seeker does when they are tasting the residue of a working, reading it like spoor. Reading *me*.

Even at that distance, even across the wind and water, I would have sworn—I would still swear—that the rider turned and looked directly at where I clung to the rock, and that beneath the grey hat-brim, eyes found mine and held them for one long breath.

Then I pulled myself over the lip of the ledge, out of sight, and climbed into the dark of the cracks, and began the long, painful, terrified business of becoming a fugitive.

Six years of careful sums, spent in ten minutes. One page of burned cipher against my chest. An empty cottage behind me and an empty future ahead, and somewhere behind my ribs, under the blood and the panic, a sentence beating in time with my heart like a second pulse:

*It wears the crown. Do not let it finish the count.*

Whatever that meant, whoever took Alba, whatever hunted me now down every road in Thornfell—one thing was arithmetically certain.

I was no longer hiding from the world.

The world was hunting me.
