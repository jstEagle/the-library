## 2. Ash on the Wind

The mountain had a way of telling you things before they happened, if you knew how to listen. The birds had gone quiet two days ago—every thrush and chough between here and the pass, silent as stones. I'd told myself it was weather coming in. Weather was the safe explanation. Weather didn't mean anything.

Then the wind changed.

It came down off the high shoulder of Thornfell carrying something that had no business being on the wind at all: ash. Not hearth-ash, not the grey drift from a charcoal burner's kiln, which smells of oak and patience. This was different. It smelled scorched and bitter, with an undertone I recognized before I could name it—the sharp, mineral tang of burned herbs, of a drying rack's worth of yarrow and mugwort and feverfew gone to smoke all at once. Anyone else would have smelled a house fire. I smelled a life.

I smelled hers.

I stood on the trail with my pack straps digging into my shoulders and let that realization settle into me like a stone into a well. Brannagh's cottage sat three hours' walk below the ridge line, tucked into a fold of the mountain where the pines grew thick enough to hide a chimney's smoke from any eyes but mine. Three hours. I could be there by midday. I could also turn east toward the low country, take the smuggler's fork at Crag's Hollow, and be across the border into Vessany before the week turned, where no edict reached and no hunter bothered climbing.

Every instinct I had honed over six years said: go east. Every other part of me—the parts I'd tried to amputate and failed—said three hours west.

I did the arithmetic anyway, because that's what I do. That's who I've made myself. Contraband runs on numbers: weight against distance, coin against risk, the price of a charm against the price of a rope. Fleeing now cost me everything I'd built—a network of contacts from here to the coast, a reputation for reliability among people who'd hang me for less than unreliability, six years of careful, deliberate nothing. Staying cost me, at best, my freedom, and at worst—

Well. At worst was the thing I kept not thinking about. The edict had come through the valley towns a fortnight back, passed mouth to mouth like a fever: *every gifted person seized or burned.* Seized or burned. They'd even given you the choice, which was more courtesy than most kings offered. A hunt captain named Vane was sweeping the passes with thirty men and a writ signed in a hand too fine to belong to anyone honest.

Thirty men. One of me. The arithmetic there was simple enough that even grief couldn't complicate it.

But grief is a terrible accountant. It fudges the columns. It writes debts in ink that never dries.

* * *

I went west.

The trail down from the ridge was one I knew blindfolded, which was fortunate, because by the time I reached the tree line the smoke had thickened into a haze that made seeing anything clearly a kind of joke. Two days old, this burn. Maybe three. Long enough for the fire to have eaten everything soft and left only the bones of the place standing.

Brannagh's cottage had been small—one room and a loft, stone-footed, turf-roofed, the kind of dwelling that looked poor to anyone who didn't know better. To anyone who didn't know that the walls were lined with dried herbs worth more per pound than saffron, that the floorboards hid a cellar stocked with remedies half the valley depended on, that the woman inside could set a broken arm so cleanly you'd never know it had been broken. She'd spent forty years making herself look unremarkable. Poorer than poor. Harmless as a hedgewitch in a story told to children.

They'd burned her anyway.

Or—they'd burned the cottage. That distinction mattered, and I clung to it the way a drowning man clings to a spar, because when I picked my way through the blackened gate and into the yard, I found no body. No pyre-mark in the earth where a body would have been laid. Hunters who burned a gifted person in their home left a scorch-fan on the ground, a stain shaped like wings. There was nothing. Just ash, and the foundation stones, and the great iron cauldron sitting in the ruin of her hearth like a skull grinning out of a wrecked ship.

The door frame still stood. Charred, but standing, and nailed to it at eye height was a strip of parchment, curling at the edges, pinned there by someone who wanted it read.

I should have walked away right then. Reading notices nailed to doors is how invisible men stop being invisible. But my feet had already decided, and my feet have always been braver than the rest of me.

The notice was the edict itself, copied fair and posted neat: names of the seized, names of the burned, rewards offered for information. And there, halfway down, in a column of its own, a single line:

*Maren of Thornfell — taken alive. Whereabouts sealed by order of the Crown.*

Taken alive. Not burned. Not killed. Taken.

I read it four times, because the first three times my hands were shaking too hard to hold the meaning still. They hadn't executed her. Of everyone on that list—the miller's daughter with the healing touch, the ferryman who could read water, the whole sad census of people whose only crime was being born with something extra in their blood—she alone had been carried off somewhere the Crown didn't want written down. Somewhere sealed. Somewhere that required a seal, which meant somewhere important, which meant someone above a hunt captain wanted her specifically.

Why? Why keep a hedge-witch alive when you're burning everyone else?

I stood in her ruined yard turning the question over, and the question turned me, and neither of us liked where we ended up.

* * *

Inside what remained of the cottage, the ash lay knee-deep in places, drifted against the fallen beams like grey snow. I moved through it slowly, crouched, the way you move through a place where someone died—or didn't die, which can be worse—and I searched for whatever the fire had spared.

Fire takes the soft things first. Paper, cloth, hair, flesh. What it leaves is metal, stone, and bone. Her copper kettles had slumped into shapes like melted hearts. Her mortar and pestle survived, granite, useless without her hands. In the corner where her bed had been, under a collapsed rafter, I found a wooden box, scorched black but intact, its lid warped shut. I worked it open with a knife and found her good cloak pins, a handful of coins, and a lock of hair wrapped in linen that I refused to examine too closely because some griefs you carry better unwrapped.

And then, near the hearth, wedged beneath the cauldron's fallen stand where the flames had been starved of air, I found it.

A page. Single sheet, ragged vellum, browned at the edges but whole. Her handwriting—I knew it the way you know your own mother's face, every letter leaning slightly leftward, the ascenders hooked like a falcon's talons. Except this wasn't her usual hand. This was hurried. This was written fast, in the dark, maybe, or in fear, the ink blotted where she'd pressed too hard.

It was the final page of her grimoire.

I knew the book. I'd grown up inside it, practically. Brannagh's grimoire was a fat, chained thing, bound in hide, kept in a chest under her bed, and she'd shown it to me exactly once, when I was fourteen and had just run away from my apprenticeship—run from *her*, though I'd told myself I was running from the work, from the expectations, from the shape of the life she was building me into. She opened the book to show me how much I was abandoning, and I saw the last pages were blank, and she told me, with that dry humor of hers that always sounded like indifference and never was, "Those are for whoever finishes it."

She'd been writing in them ever since, I realized now. All these years, while I was down in the passes playing at being nobody, she'd been filling those final pages. Working on something. Something she wrote in haste, in secret, in a hand unlike her own—and then tore out. Tore out and hid. Because the rest of the grimoire was gone entirely, burned or carried off, but this page had been hidden under the hearth, deliberately, physically, by hands that knew hunters search ashes and also knew they search carelessly, top-down, missing what's wedged beneath cold iron.

The page held perhaps forty lines. Most of it was cipher—her old teacher's cipher, the one she'd taught me and I'd sworn I'd forgotten, and hadn't, because forgetting things you learned young is a lie children tell themselves and adults believe. But the first three lines were plain, written large, almost defiant:

*When the crown eats the king, the king must be unbound.*
*What binds may be loosed only by a living anchor.*
*The anchor pays the whole of what he holds.*

Three lines. That's all she gave me plain. Three lines that rearranged everything I thought I knew about why the hunts had started, why the edict used the word *seized* instead of simply *killed*, why one old woman in a turf-roofed cottage had been carried off alive while her neighbors went to the fire.

The crown eats the king. I'd heard whispers—smugglers hear everything, it's our trade—but whispers about a strange king were currency I'd never spent. Now I understood what the whispers had been buying. Whatever wore the throne ate magic. It hunted the gifted not out of piety or politics but appetite. And it had taken Brannagh alive because she knew how to make it stop, and it meant to use her—or wear her next, once the vessel it currently occupied wore through.

The anchor pays the whole of what he holds. I read that line and felt something in my chest go tight and cold, because I understood it too, instantly, completely, the way you understand a bill presented at your table. Unbinding the thing in the crown required someone with the gift to pour theirs out—all of it, the whole holding—into the loosening. Everything I had spent six years hiding, hoarding, rationing, denying. Everything I'd sworn off at nineteen when I ran from her cottage because I couldn't bear the size of what she wanted me to become.

She'd torn out that page and hidden it under her own hearth. Which meant she'd known. Known the edict might come, known they might take her, known that whoever came looking after would need to find it. She'd left it for me. Or for whoever came. But the cipher was *her* cipher, taught to one student only, and that student had run away six years ago and never come back until today, walking through her ashes.

She'd bet on my return. She'd bet wrong about the timing and right about the fact. I don't know which of those thoughts hurt more.

* * *

I folded the page small and put it inside my boot, against the skin, because paper in a pocket can be lost and paper in a boot has to be walked out of. Then I stood up to leave, and that was when the world stopped letting me pretend.

They came up the trail from the south. Four riders and six men on foot, grey cloaks, boar-spears, the matched boots and matched silence of professionals. A hunter's patrol, sweeping the burn site the way gulls sweep a shoreline—looking for survivors, looking for scavengers, looking for anyone drawn to the ruins by love or greed, since both kinds made useful catches. I'd watched patrols work before, from ridgelines, from the far side of valleys, always at a distance I'd chosen. Distance was my whole craft. Six years of staying unseen, and I'd gotten good at it, good enough that I'd started believing invisibility was a place you could live rather than a skill you performed.

They crested the rise ninety yards out and I was already behind the fallen chimney-stone, flat in the ash, heart going like a drum in a parade. Ninety yards. In open burnt ground with no cover between us and the tree line. If they rode past, I might slip away. If they swept wide—if one of them cast left along the stream cut, which was the sensible thing to do and therefore certain—they'd walk straight onto me.

One of them did exactly that. The sensible one. He came down the stream cut with his spear couched, scanning the bank, and his dog came with him, and dogs are the reason invisible men die. You can fool a man's eyes. You cannot fool a nose that has been bred for ten generations to find what hides.

The dog caught my scent at forty yards and went rigid, pointing, and the handler whistled his fellows, and I had perhaps twenty seconds to decide what kind of person I was going to be for the rest of my life.

Here is what six years of arithmetic had taught me: that a man who runs is chased, and a man who fights is killed, and a man who hides is found, and all three end the same way unless something breaks the pattern. I had spent six years believing the pattern could be beaten by caution alone. Caution had brought me to a burned cottage, kneeling in my teacher's ashes, with a patrol closing on me and a page in my boot that said the world was ending differently than I'd thought.

So I broke the pattern.

I did the thing I had sworn off. I used the gift—not a charm, not a hedge-trick, not the small sly magics of concealment and mending that I allowed myself because they were too minor to count. I reached down into the place I kept locked, the cellar of myself, and I took hold of the mountain.

Thornfell is old and patient and full of sleeping weight, and everything old and heavy will answer a voice that speaks correctly. I spoke correctly. I asked the slope above the stream cut—the slope the riders had just descended—to remember that it was, at bottom, loose. Scree. Shale. Ten thousand winters of frost-cracked stone pretending to be solid ground.

It remembered.

The hillside moved. Not an avalanche—nothing so dramatic, nothing that would bury men and make me a killer. Just a long, groaning shrug, a sheet of shale shifting a hand's depth downslope, enough to swallow the trail, enough to drop a wall of dust and noise between me and the treeline, enough to make eight armed professionals decide that whatever was up here wasn't worth a broken neck. The dog lost the scent in the sliding stone. The handler shouted something about the mountain being unstable. Their sergeant, a practical man, agreed, and they withdrew south to report a rockfall and a scent lost, and I went north through the trees with dust in my teeth and my ears ringing with the sound of my own name being spoken aloud to the world.

Because that's what I'd done. However carefully. However deniably—"rockfall," they'd say, "mountain shifted"—something had answered me, and something answering is visible to anyone who knows how to look, and hunters know how to look. Somewhere in a dispatch, in a ledger, in a report going south to Captain Vane herself, there was now a line: *sign of working at the Maren site. Subject unknown. Track north.*

Track north. Toward the ford. Toward the low country. Toward the road I needed and the road they'd watch.

* * *

I walked until dark, then walked past dark, because stopping felt like dying. Around midnight I made a cold camp in a hollow under a granite shelf, no fire, and sat with my back to stone and the page against my thigh and tried to think like an accountant instead of a grieving man, and discovered the two had merged into something new and worse.

The ledger looked like this. Assets: one stolen page, one forgotten cipher, one head start measured in hours. Liabilities: my face, now known; my direction, now guessed; my gift, now revealed, which meant the quiet life was not merely endangered but finished—there was no version of tomorrow in which I went back to running charms through passes and calling it a living. Debts outstanding: one teacher, taken alive, held somewhere sealed by a thing that eats magic and means to eat her knowledge next. Interest accruing: daily.

And one entry I didn't know how to classify. An asset or a liability, I couldn't say. The third line of her plain writing: *the anchor pays the whole of what he holds.*

Six years ago I ran from her because the size of what she saw in me frightened me more than the world did. Tonight I sat in the dark reading her handwriting and understanding that she had always known how it would end—that she'd written these lines years ago, torn them out, buried them under her hearth like a seed, and waited for the boy who ran to come back grown.

The invisible life was over. I understood that now with a clarity that felt less like knowledge than like a blade going in. There was no clever route, no smuggler's fork, no border far enough. The thing in the capital was eating the realm's magic one gifted soul at a time, and it had my teacher, and it had her final page's meaning, and the only person alive who could read the rest of that cipher was kneeling in the dirt wishing he were still nobody.

By dawn I was on the trail again, heading north and east together, toward the river, toward the ford at Marlebridge—the crossing every road in the mountains funnels toward, including, I knew, the hunters'.

I told myself I was choosing the ford because it was fastest. I have always been able to lie to myself fluently; it's the family gift, the one that came free.

Somewhere behind me, the ash was still falling. Somewhere ahead, the river ran grey under a white sky, and on its far bank, I would learn, a woman in a grey cloak was waiting with thirty men and a writ, and she had a face I used to know by heart.

The mountain had tried to warn me. I'd heard the birds go silent and called it weather. Some lessons you pay for twice.
