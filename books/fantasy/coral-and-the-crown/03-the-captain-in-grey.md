## 3. The Captain in Grey

The river was called the Sorrel, and like everything in Thornfell it had two names: the pretty one on maps and the true one in the mouths of people who made their living from it. Smugglers called it the Thief, because it gave with one hand — a shallow gravel ford, a blind bend, a border of pines thick enough to swallow a rider — and took with the other. Two men I'd worked with had drowned in it. One of them had been carrying my money.

I came down to the water just past dawn, when the mist still owned the banks, and I did what I always did before crossing: I counted.

This was my arithmetic, learned over six years of staying alive by being unremarkable. Horses: none, thank the quiet gods; a horse is a confession on four legs. Goods: salt pork, needles, a bolt of grey dye nobody asks questions about, and sewn into the lining of my pack's flap, three charm-stones small enough to pass for river pebbles. Coin: eleven coppers and half a silver, which sounds like poverty because it was. Witnesses: a heron, disinterested. Distance to the treeline on the far side: two hundred paces. Time since I'd last seen smoke that wasn't mine: too long.

I was, in short, a man who had reduced his life to sums so that nothing in it could surprise him. I want you to hold onto that word, *nothing*, because the river had other plans.

I was halfway across, water tugging at my knees with that patient grey insistence rivers have, when the heron left.

Herons don't leave for weather. Herons leave for reasons.

I stopped mid-ford, and the cold climbed my shins, and I did the count again with new columns. Sound: none yet, which meant whoever it was knew how to be quiet, which meant soldiers, which meant the edict, which meant Ash on the Wind, which meant Marrow Ghyll burning behind me in my memory where I would carry it forever. Sight: the far bank, pines, mist — and then the mist moved wrong. Mist moves with the wind. This moved against it, and under it, and out of it came thirty shapes in grey, unfolding from the trees like the forest deciding to stand up.

Run back? The bank behind me was open gravel, forty paces to cover, and there were archers somewhere in that grey because there are always archers somewhere in that grey. Run forward? Thirty men. Run sideways? It's a river, Emrys, my master used to say, her voice dry as old paper in my head, you can't run sideways through a river.

So I stood there, thigh-deep in the Thief, holding a pack full of contraband and dead women's secrets, while Captain Vane's writ of seizure was read aloud to me across the water.

* * *

I should say something about the voice first, because it was the voice that broke me before the face did.

"By order of the Crown," it carried over the water, clear and unhurried, the kind of voice that has never once needed to shout, "all persons bearing the unlawful gift are commanded to surrender themselves for assessment. Resistance will be met as treason."

Six years is a long time. Voices change. I told myself it wasn't hers. I told myself every man in grey cloaks sounds the same, all of them carved from the same wet wood. But my body had already recognized it — my heart was doing something stupid and arrhythmic, some dance step I hadn't practiced since I was nineteen — and my body has always been a worse liar than the rest of me.

They waded out to meet me. Four of them, then six, forming a loose crescent in the shallows while the rest held the bank. And at the center of the crescent, pushing through the last of the mist like something the mist had been trying to keep for itself, came a woman in a captain's grey, and she had a face I used to know by heart.

There's a specific cruelty in recognizing someone you loved across a stretch of killing water. You can't unsay your own heartbeat. I stood in the river feeling mine hammer out her name — *Sera, Sera, Sera* — like a prisoner rattling a cup along cell bars, and I fixed my eyes on the middle distance and thought, with the calm of a man already drowning: *don't look at her mouth, don't look at her hands, don't look at her eyes.*

I looked at her eyes.

Grey. Not cloak-grey, not river-grey. A grey with green buried in it, like moss under ice, and I had spent entire apprenticeship evenings falling into that particular color while she talked about leaving, about the coast, about anywhere, about everything except us, because we were both cowards in complementary directions.

She didn't know me.

That was my first thought, and I clung to it like flotsam. Of course she didn't. Six years, a beard I kept short for mask-fitting, a face weathered brown, hair gone from boy's-soft to something the mountain had opinions about. She'd known a lanky apprentice with ink-stained fingers who quoted herbs at dinner parties of one. She saw now a smuggler standing in a ford: lean, wary, deliberately forgettable. I have worked very hard to be forgettable. It is the only art I have ever mastered.

"Halt where you are," she said, and her voice was the same voice and not the same voice — sanded down, hardened, a blade someone had sharpened on grief, though whose grief I couldn't know. "Name?"

And here is where I tell you I did the thing I do best, the family gift, the free one. I lied.

"Pemberton," I said. "Tom Pemberton. Dyer's factor, out of Lowmarsh." My own surname, mind you — I've always thought aliases fail most often at the name, men inventing exotic strangers and then forgetting their own mothers' faces. Keep what's true, change only what must be changed. Tom even, if you please. Half a lie is sturdier than a whole one.

"Dyer's factor," she repeated, flat. She looked at my pack. "Alone? In Thornfell? Carrying what?"

"Wool sample, needles, provisions." All true. That's the trick of it; surround the lie with truths until it has nowhere to fall. "Roads are bad. I take the bad roads anyway. Pays better."

Someone behind her laughed, short and ugly. She didn't.

"Factor Pemberton." Something flickered across her face then — I want to be honest about this, because honesty about that moment costs me, and cost is the point — something flickered, some small contraction around her eyes, the look of a woman hearing an echo and unable to place the room. For one whole breath I thought: *she knows.* And I don't know which terrified me more, that she did or that I wanted her to.

Then it passed, whatever it was, smoothed away like a wrinkle under a palm, and she said, "Cross slowly. Hands visible. You'll come with us."

"I haven't crossed," I pointed out, because apparently my mouth had decided that if I was going to die, it would die pedantic.

"Then drown or cross," she said. "Those are your options."

* * *

They took my pack on the bank. A soldier went through it with the bored thoroughness of a man who has done this a hundred times and found a hundred knives and never once a reason to care. He found the needles. He found the pork. He held up the grey dye and looked at me and I shrugged, and shrugging is a language and he spoke it fluently.

He didn't find the stones. They stayed sewn in the flap, three little lies against my chest, warm as sins.

What happened next happened fast, and I've replayed it enough times since to know it was the hinge of everything, though I couldn't have told you that standing in the wet gravel of the Sorrel with my hands up.

She searched me herself.

Protocol, probably. Or suspicion — dyers' factors don't usually wade rivers at dawn looking like a man who has recently seen a cottage burn, and I must have looked like exactly that, because I had. She came close — closer than anyone had stood to me in a year — and her gloved hands went through my coat pockets with brisk impersonal efficiency, and I stared at a point above her shoulder and performed the role of an indignant tradesman, and inside I was nineteen again and standing in an herb garden making a complete fool of myself over a girl who smelled like rosemary and rain.

"You're trembling," she said. Not unkindly. Which was worse.

"Cold," I said. "River's cold."

"Mm." Her hands paused at my inner pocket — the one where I kept Marrow Ghyll's final page folded against my heart, because I am a sentimental idiot as well as a liar — and for a moment the world narrowed to her fingers and the paper beneath them and the space of one heartbeat in which my entire future balanced.

Then she withdrew her hand, took my arm above the wrist instead, and bound it. "Assessment at Corvane," she said, to me, to everyone, to no one. "Move."

Assessment. I'd heard the word before, in the way you hear a word right before you understand it's a nail being described as a fastening solution. Everyone knew what *assessment* meant when the edict came down from the capital. Some assessed were released. Most weren't heard from again. The mountain folk had a saying about it that I won't repeat here because it rhymed, and rhyming makes horror catchy.

I walked. They formed around me, grey and grey and grey, and she rode at the column's head, and I spent the first mile composing my escape.

I'm serious. That's the arithmetic again; terror just changes which column you work in. Guard spacing: eight paces, staggered. Terrain: river valley, then pine slope, then the old drover's road — chokepoints at the second mile and the fifth. My assets: three stones, a knife they hadn't found because there wasn't one, and a working knowledge of every game trail within twenty miles. My liabilities: thirty men, a captain whose voice could still reach into my chest and rearrange things, and the fact that using magic in front of witnesses was precisely the crime I'd be assessed for.

I got as far as planning the third mile when the problem arrived, and the problem was this: every plan ended with hurting her men, and some of them — I noticed this despite myself, curse my observational habits — some of them were boys. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, chafing saddle-sores and joking to hide fear. Boys like I had been, running from something, toward something, not knowing either thing properly.

And every plan also ended with her.

Because if I ran, she'd hunt me. That was her job now, written plainly in the set of her shoulders, in the way her men watched her for cues the way sailors watch weather. And I knew — I knew with a certainty that had no business surviving six years — that she was good at her job. The girl I remembered lost arguments to nobody. The woman would lose fewer.

So I walked, and I rationed my glances at her like a starving man rations bread, and I catalogued what six years had done without permission. She sat her horse like she'd been born to it, which she hadn't; she'd been afraid of horses once, and told me so at midnight, and I had promised — I distinctly remember promising — to teach her. Apparently someone else had. There was a new scar through her left eyebrow, thin and white. Her jaw was harder. Her mouth was the same, and I hated that I knew that, and hated more that knowing it felt like the only true thing left in my pockets after the search.

* * *

We made camp at dusk in a hollow below a ridge, fires low, pickets out. They gave me a share of stew, which I ate with my bound hands and what dignity remains available to a man eating stew with his wrists tied, and then she came and sat across the fire from me, alone, which captains presumably should not do with prisoners, and looked at me for a long time.

"You're not a factor," she said finally.

"Bold claim. Evidence?"

"Evidence." She almost smiled. Almost, in her case, meaning: the muscles arranged themselves and then were overruled. "Factors don't stand in freezing fords calculating arrow range. Factors don't watch the pickets. You counted us, this morning. Before you knew we'd seen you. I watched your eyes move."

Ah. Well. Caught by the one witness in thirty capable of catching me. The mountain's sense of humor is very dry.

"Everyone counts soldiers," I said. "It's a habit of the road. Healthy."

"It is," she agreed. "In factors." She fed a stick to the fire. Sparks went up and died young. "Where were you six years ago, Tom Pemberton?"

The question went through me like cold water finding the seam in a boot.

"Why?" I managed. "Does it matter?"

"Everything matters now. The edict made sure of that." Her voice was level but her thumb was moving along the edge of her gauntlet, back and forth, a small restless motion, and I recognized it, God help me, I recognized it — she did that when she was thinking hard, she did it the whole evening before she left the valley, rubbing her thumb along the rim of her cup while I sat across from her failing to say the one sentence that might have —

"South," I said. "Lowmarsh. Dye works. Nothing."

"Hm." She looked into the fire. "I knew someone from these mountains once. He'd have counted the pickets too."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a picket coughed. I sat very still, the way you do when a wound you'd bandaged years ago turns out to have been bleeding the whole time, quietly, into places you couldn't see.

"Oh?" said my voice, steady, damn it, steady. "A hunter?"

"A student. He ran off in the night without telling anyone why." She stood, brushing off her knees, and for a moment she was outlined against the firelight, and her face was unreadable, and I understood suddenly that unreadable was armor she had built, plate by plate, and I wondered who had hammered it on. "He'd be disappointed in the roads these days," she said. "If he ever came back."

She walked away into the dark between fires, and I sat there with my hands bound and three stolen stones against my chest and the last page of a dead woman's book folded where her fingers had nearly found it, and I thought about the word *assessment*, and I thought about the fact that she had touched the page and let it go, and I did not know yet whether that was mercy or something she hadn't identified yet, some suspicion still taking shape in the dark of her, patient as the river.

One thing I knew, sitting in the firelight pretending to sleep while thirty hunters breathed around me: I had spent six years learning to be invisible, and I had been very good at it, and it had all come to nothing in the space of one grey morning, because the person my invisibility was least proof against turned out to be the person who had taught me what it cost.

Tomorrow we'd reach Corvane. After Corvane, the capital, if the dispatches she read by firelight meant what I feared they meant. And somewhere ahead of both, a king I'd never seen wanted me assessed for the crime of being what I was.

I closed my eyes and listened to the Sorrel running in the dark, taking things south, giving things north.

She knew someone from these mountains.

She'd said it like a stone dropped in a well, and I was still falling after it when sleep finally took me — and the last thing I heard, in that country between waking and not, was her voice saying *he'd be disappointed in the roads these days*, and underneath it, older and warmer, the same voice at nineteen, saying: *Take me with you, Emrys. Anywhere. Just ask.*

I never asked. And now she was taking me — bound, suspected, and unrecognized, or pretending to be — to the one city in the world where a hedge-witch's apprentice goes to die.
