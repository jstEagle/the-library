## 4. Chains and Ciphers

The edict said *seized or burned*, and I had spent three days trying to decide which of those words bothered me more.

It shouldn't have bothered me at all. I'd read it aloud to my company in the yard at Vellmark, my voice steady as a drill sergeant's, and twenty-two hunters had nodded and gone to sharpen their knives. That was the job. The Crown said *gifted*, and we said *yes, sir*, and the arithmetic of the realm balanced itself on the backs of people who could light candles with a whisper.

But arithmetic is easy when the numbers stay abstract. Then I'd put my hands on Emrys Pemberton at the river ford, and the numbers had grown a face, and a scar over one eyebrow, and a way of looking at me like I was a debt he'd been expecting.

Now he rode ahead of me on a borrowed mule, wrists bound with hunter's cord, and I rode behind him with my hand nowhere near my sword, which was its own kind of confession.

"Comfortable?" I asked, because silence with him was a country I didn't know how to navigate.

"Luxurious." He didn't turn around. "I've had worse inns. The mattress is a bit lumpy and it bites."

"The mule or the cord?"

"The cord, actually. Your knots are excellent. I want you to know I've noticed."

I let that sit. Three days on the King's Road out of Thornfell, and he'd been like this the whole way—wry, unbothered, a man commenting on the weather while the weather was a hanging. Either he was the bravest prisoner I'd ever escorted or he'd already decided something about his odds that I hadn't.

I told myself I didn't care which.

I told myself that about a lot of things lately.

* * *

The trouble with escort duty, nobody warns you, is the time. A battle is over in an hour. A march gives you four days of a prisoner's profile against the morning fog, and four days is long enough for a thought to take root.

The thought, in my case, was this: *what happens to them after?*

The edict said *seized or burned*, but it didn't say what seized meant. I'd asked Captain Dreech, my commander, on the second night, casual as asking about rations. He'd looked at me over the dispatch case with those pale, patient eyes and said, "The prisoners go to the capital. The Crown handles them."

"The Crown handles them," I repeated.

"You have a question, Vane?"

"No, sir."

But I did, and it was this: in eleven years of hunting, I had never once seen a prisoner come back. Not acquitted. Not released. Not transferred, not escaped—*come back*. We delivered them to the palace gates and a clerk with grey gloves signed for them like they were crates of salted fish, and then the road home was empty, and the next hunt was already being drawn up on a map.

Eleven years. I did the sum on the third night by the fire, while Emrys slept with his bound hands tucked under his cheek like a boy, and the sum came out to forty-one. Forty-one people I had personally delivered. Forty-one names I'd written in the company ledger in my neat, upright hand.

Forty-one people who had, as far as the world was concerned, stopped existing the moment a clerk in grey gloves took possession.

I fed the fire and told myself it was the war that made records bad. The border troubles. The plague year. There were reasons. There were always reasons, and reasons were the coin the Crown paid us in, and I had been rich in reasons for eleven years.

Across the fire, Emrys opened one eye.

"You're doing sums," he said.

"I'm doing nothing."

"You've got your thinking face on. You had it when we were fourteen, too, right before you told Master Hollen his fencing drills were pointless and redesigned them. He was furious for a week. Then he used them."

"You remember that?"

"I remember everything about—" He stopped. Rolled over. "Go to sleep, Captain."

* * *

He'd known me at the ford. That was the thing I kept circling back to, like a dog worrying a bone.

He'd known me, and I hadn't known him—not until he said my name, *Sera*, with the soft r of the mountain villages we'd both grown up around, and six years fell on me like a roof collapsing.

Emrys Pemberton. The apprentice who ran. Master Ysolt's boy, the one with the hedge-magic and the crooked smile, who'd been my closest friend through the worst years of my childhood and then, one autumn morning, was simply gone—no note, no goodbye, just an empty cot and a master who wouldn't say where he'd gone and a hole in my chest shaped exactly like him.

And now I was taking him to whatever the Crown *handled*, in chains, and he was making jokes about my knots.

On the fourth day it rained, a thin grey drizzle that made the road shine like pewter, and I called a halt at a waystone shrine because even hunters have some piety left, and while my company watered the horses under the trees, I walked back to check his bindings. Procedure. That's what I told myself it was.

He was sitting under the shrine's leaning roof, watching the rain come off the stone lip in a silver thread, and when I knelt beside him to inspect the cord, he said, quietly, without moving his lips much, "Your bindings have been talking to me."

I kept my hands steady on the knots. "Prisoners who talk to their restraints usually get gagged."

"Indulge me. You wove a cipher into them."

"I wove *knots* into them."

"Hunter's cord, standard escort pattern—except you've varied the tension on the binding turns. Deliberately. It's an old signal code. My master used it for leaving messages in weaving, in basketry, in the lacing of her shutters when the tax assessors came." His voice stayed low and pleasant, the voice of a man discussing the rain. "Tight, loose, tight, crossed. It spells something, Captain. It's been spelling something against my wrists for three days, and I'd very much like to know if you meant it."

The rain came off the shrine roof. Somewhere behind me, Brannic laughed at one of Tam's jokes, and the horses stamped, and the whole ordinary machinery of an escort went on grinding.

"Read it to me," I said.

He was quiet a moment. "It says: *They do not go to trial. Do not let them take you alive to the gates. Ask the grey clerk what happened to the ones before.*"

I hadn't meant to write that. That was the worst part—I hadn't *meant* to. I'd woven the bindings at the ford with my hands moving on their own, the way they moved when I was a girl and frightened, when my mother taught me to work my worry into wool so it couldn't get into my voice. Tight, loose, crossed. My hands had known what my mouth wouldn't say.

"Coincidence," I said.

"Three days of coincidence, in perfect hunter's cipher, spelling out exactly the question you asked Dreech two nights ago loud enough for a man with hedge-sharpened hearing to catch through a canvas tent wall." He finally turned his head and looked at me, and his eyes were not wry at all. "Sera. What did you hear?"

"Nothing. I heard nothing."

"You went white as the waystone when you came out of his tent."

"I went white because the rain is cold."

"It wasn't raining two nights ago."

I stood up. My hands wanted to shake and I forbade them. Eleven years of discipline is good for exactly this—one more hour, one more day, one more mile of pretending.

"Here's what happens now," I said, loud enough to be procedure, quiet enough to be mercy. "We walk to the capital. You're my prisoner. At the gates, a clerk signs for you, and my part ends, and whatever the Crown *handles*, it handles. That's the law, and I'm the law's hands, and hands don't get to ask questions."

"And if the hands did ask?"

"Then the hands get cut off." I tightened a knot I'd already checked twice. "Stop reading my knots, Pemberton."

"Then stop writing them."

* * *

That night we made camp in the ruins of a tollhouse, walls waist-high, roof long gone, stars for rafters. I set the watch and walked the perimeter out of habit, and when I came back to the fire, Emrys was sitting up, watching me with an expression I recognized from twenty years ago—the one he wore when he'd figured out a puzzle and was deciding whether to be kind about it.

He'd never once been kind about it.

"Eleven years," he said. "You've been hunting eleven years."

"Ten and a half."

"Ten and a half years, and you've never asked what happens to them."

"I just asked."

"You asked *me*. I'm the least reliable witness in the realm; I'm motivated." He poked the fire with a stick he'd somehow gotten hold of despite the bindings, which told me my company was getting sloppy around him, or he was very good, or both. "Ask yourself instead. Forty-one names in your ledger. Write them down tonight, all of them, and then try to remember a single face from the palace gates. The clerk's face. The yard beyond the gate. Anything."

"I remember the gates."

"You remember the *outside* of the gates. That's all any of us ever see." He leaned forward, firelight moving on his face, and for a moment he wasn't a prisoner or a ghost from my childhood; he was just Emrys, who had always been able to find the exact seam in a thing and worry it open. "Sera. You wove a warning into my bindings because some part of you already knows. I'm not asking you to betray anything. I'm asking you to stop pretending the knot isn't there."

I sat down across the fire from him because my legs decided without consulting me.

"If you're wrong," I said, "and I start digging, I lose everything. My commission. My company. My—" I almost said *my name*, which would have been a lie, because my name was the one thing the Crown couldn't take; it had been bought and paid for the day they made me a captain and taught me to stop being the girl from the mountain villages. "Everything."

"And if you're right," he said, "and you don't dig?"

The fire popped. On the far side of the camp, Brannic snored in a key that could strip paint.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truest thing I'd said in eleven years, and it felt like taking off armor in winter.

He nodded slowly, and for once he didn't push. That was new. The Emrys I remembered would have pushed, would have worried the seam until the whole garment came apart. This one just looked at me across the flames with something old and careful in his face and said, "Then don't decide tonight. Decide at the gates. The gates have a way of deciding for people."

* * *

We reached the crossroads at Marrow Bridge on the sixth day, where the King's Road bent east toward the capital and the old drover's road climbed north back into Thornfell's foothills. The company halted to water the horses, and I stood at the signpost a long moment, looking north.

North was where the hunt had started. North was burned cottages and ash on the wind and a master's grimoire with a page torn out—Emrys had told me that much, one night, staring into the fire like it owed him money. North was everything I'd been sent to sweep clean.

"Captain." Tam appeared at my elbow, young and earnest and holding a dispatch case like it might hatch. "Rider came through from Vellmark. Orders for you."

I cracked the case. The seal was Dreech's. The hand was Dreech's. The words were brief and bureaucratic and they landed in my stomach like swallowed stones:

*By command of the Crown: all prisoners to be delivered intact to the Grey Clerk at the Palace postern. No stops. No visitors. No correspondence. Captain Vane to report in person upon delivery regarding the Thornfell operation. Fail not.*

*Intact.* As if I might deliver them otherwise. As if someone, somewhere, had.

*No correspondence.* Someone was worried my prisoner might write a letter. Or receive one. Or that his captain might.

I looked up. Emrys was watching me from the mule, rain-damp, bound hands loose in his lap, and he didn't ask what the orders said. He just raised his eyebrows a fraction, and I understood that he already knew, the way he'd known about the knots, the way he'd always known things about me before I knew them myself.

"Mount up," I said to the company, and my voice was steady as a drill sergeant's, and I hated it. "We ride for the capital. No stops."

We rode. The road bent east, away from the mountains, and the capital waited somewhere past the horizon like a debt coming due, and I caught myself doing sums again—the forty-one names, the grey gloves, the word *intact*—and for the first time in eleven years, the arithmetic wouldn't balance.

We were an hour past the bridge, deep in country where the road ran between old hedgerows and the light went green and dim, when Brannic's whistle cut the air—the one for trouble on the road ahead—and the hedges on both sides suddenly stood up and became people.
