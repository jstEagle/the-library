## 5. What the Road Remembers

### Sera

I had been a hunter for nine years, and in all that time I had learned one iron law of the road: the trouble never comes from the direction you're watching. You watch the treeline because trouble lives in trees. It waits behind you instead, or above you, or — as it happened on the third night out of Thornfell — inside the very prisoners you're escorting.

We were camped in a hollow below the old milestone road, six hunters and one prisoner, with the mountains finally flattening into foothills behind us and the long grey plain of the king's country opening ahead. Emrys Pemberton sat where I'd put him, hands bound in front, wrists wrapped in null-cord and prayer-ribbon, looking for all the world like a man doing sums in his head. Which, knowing him, he probably was.

I should explain about the bindings. Standard procedure for a gifted prisoner is iron at the wrists and a hood on the head. But the quartermaster's stores had run short after the first mad month of the edict — every hunter company in the realm requisitioning iron by the ton — so we'd fallen back on the old ways: braided cord soaked in salt and ash, knotted with the counter-charms my grandmother taught me before anyone thought such knowledge would ever be useful again. It held most hedge-witches fine. It had held him fine for three days.

I want to be honest here, in the privacy of my own skull, since honesty was in short supply everywhere else that year: I chose those bindings partly because they were what I had, and partly because iron looks like you expect your prisoner to be an animal. He wasn't an animal. He was — I still didn't let myself finish the sentence.

He was the boy from Aldenford who used to sit on the mill wall and name clouds for me. *That one's a fat bishop. That one's your mother-in-law.* Six years since I'd seen him, and three days of marching beside him without either of us saying it aloud. We talked instead about weather and road conditions and whether Corporal Dace snored, which he did, magnificently. Two people performing a play for an audience of five soldiers, and both of us forgetting our lines every time our eyes met longer than a heartbeat allowed.

The attack came at moonrise, which any competent commander would have predicted, because desperate people travel by moonrise and there is nothing in this world more dangerous than someone with nothing left to lose.

They came down from the rocks to the east — four of them, maybe five, moving badly, which told me everything. Fugitives don't fight like soldiers. Soldiers conserve; fugitives spend. These came spending everything, screaming names I didn't catch, fire blooming between their fingers like rotten fruit.

"Up! Up! Shields!" I was on my feet before I finished shouting, and the camp exploded into noise and orange light.

Here is what I remember of the next ninety seconds, assembled later from pieces, the way you assemble anything terrible:

Dace went down first, a bolt of green light catching him across the chest. He'd snored magnificently not ten minutes before. Brannic got his shield up in time to take a gout of flame meant for his face. The horses screamed. One of the witches — young, a girl barely older than the edict itself — stood on the rocks hurling fire with tears streaming down her face, and I understood with sick clarity that she wasn't attacking us. She was attacking the edict. We were simply the part of it within reach.

And through the middle of all of it ran Emrys Pemberton, hands still bound, putting himself directly into the killing ground.

"Stop him—" someone shouted, meaning the wrong person entirely.

What he did then, I have turned over a hundred times since, and it has never once looked like the magic the sermons describe. The sermons say the gift is a knife. What he did was closer to sewing.

A witch's fireball came arcing toward our powder stores — yes, we were carrying powder, no, I will not be taking questions about my logistical decisions — and it simply *came apart* midair, unravelling into sparks, into nothing, the way a dropped stitch unravels if you catch it right. He caught it right. He caught two more. When the girl on the rocks sent down a lance of white heat at Brannic's exposed back, Emrys threw out his tied hands and the air between them went thick as river water, the bolt slowing, bending, splashing harmlessly into the dirt.

He wasn't fighting them. He was standing between.

The last thing I saw clearly: the eldest of the attackers, a grey-stubbled man with a brand scarred across his jaw — the mark they give the ones they catch and release, the ones they've already broken once — looked at Emrys, at the bound hands working miracles, and something in his face collapsed. He shouted something in the old mountain tongue. The word, I learned much later, was *mercy*. They withdrew the way they'd come, carrying their dead, and the hollow went quiet except for burning canvas and Dace breathing wet and shallow against a saddle.

Emrys knelt in the middle of it all, wrists smoking where the null-cord had scorched him for the effort, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read. Defiance? Apology?

"Captain," he said, "I'd like to report that your prisoner attempted escape."

I crossed the distance between us before I decided to. I grabbed his collar, and every soldier in camp suddenly found something else to look at, because captains grabbing prisoners' collars is captain business.

"That's three men you saved," I said quietly. "One of them might die anyway."

"I know." His voice cracked on it. He knew exactly what it cost, did Emrys. He'd always known the price of things down to the copper.

"You could have run," I said. "They'd have taken you. You'd be free."

Something moved behind his eyes — the whole buried history of us shifting like silt in current. "Free," he repeated, tasting the word. Then, softer, almost to himself: "You always did ask the wrong questions, Sera Vane. You asked me why I didn't run. Ask me why I stayed."

I didn't have an answer. So I did what captains do. I tightened the null-cord, ordered the camp struck, and put us back on the road in the dark, because the fires would draw worse than fugitives before dawn.

But I walked beside him the whole rest of the night. And I didn't pretend, even to myself, that it was for security.

### Emrys

There is a particular arithmetic to being captured, and I had worked it out to the farthing by the second day: escape attempt, poor odds; compliance, worse odds but deferred; charm the guards, impossible, see previous entries regarding my face. What I had not accounted for — what no smuggler's ledger has a column for — was Sera Vane walking beside me in the dark asking nothing, and how that silence weighed more than the cord on my wrists.

She'd said my name. Back at the ford, when she pulled the hood off, she'd gone white as milk and said *Pemberton*, flat, like reading it off a warrant. But that night after the fight, kneeling in the burnt grass checking Dace's bandages with hands that shook only slightly, she'd said *Emrys*, just once, under her breath, and pretended she hadn't. I heard. I have spent six years training myself to hear everything while appearing to hear nothing. It is the entire trade.

So when the column stopped at dawn in a birch stand to let the wounded rest, and she posted her soldiers in a ring and sat down across the small fire from me, I decided the moment had come to stop performing.

"The big one with the red beard," I said. "Brannic. He was at the mill, wasn't he? Harvest before last?"

She looked up sharply. "How do you know that?"

"He bought charms off me. Not hexes — nothing like that. Wart-charms, mostly. A luck-knot for his brother going down the mines. He paid too much and wouldn't take change." I shrugged as best a bound man can shrug. "Smugglers remember good customers."

"And the girl on the rocks tonight. The one throwing fire and crying." Her voice was carefully level. "Do you remember her too?"

"No. But I know her." I watched the fire rather than her face, because some things are easier said to fire. "That's what the edict made, Captain. Children who learned to throw their grief. She'll be dead within a month — they all are, the loud ones. The quiet ones like me last longer, until someone sells them for the bounty." I finally looked at her. "Is that what you believe I am? Something to sell?"

"Don't." The word came out rough. "Don't do that. Don't make me the villain of a story I haven't finished reading."

"Then tell me the ending. What happens when we reach Corvale? Because I can count, Sera. Prisoners go to the palace, and nobody comes back from the palace. Not the gifted ones. Where do they go?"

For a long moment she fed sticks to the fire instead of answering. Around us the birches stood white and patient, like a jury waiting for testimony.

"I don't know," she said finally, and the honesty in it hurt worse than a lie would have. "The orders say the gifted are remanded to the Crown for assessment. Assessment. Nobody tells us what comes after. We hand them over and ride away and there's a bonus in our pay packets and nobody asks." Her jaw tightened. "I asked. Once. In the first week. Commander Roke had me scrubbing kennels for a fortnight."

"You asked," I repeated. "You actually asked."

"Someone had to." She glanced at me, and the firelight did something unfair to her face. "Six years ago you asked me to come away with you. To Thornfell. And I said the crown comes first, and you said—" She stopped.

"I said the crown doesn't love you back," I supplied. "Yes. I've regretted the delivery of that line ever since. It was true, incidentally. Just badly phrased."

Despite herself — I watched it happen, the corner of her mouth losing its war — she laughed. One short, startled sound, quickly strangled, but real. "Badly phrased. You set fire to my father's barn and you're worried about phrasing."

"That was an accident. The fire was a misjudged warming charm and the barn was structurally unsound and frankly your father should thank me for the renovation opportunity—"

"Emrys."

"What?"

"Shut up." But she was almost smiling now, and six years fell off her all at once, and for a moment she was seventeen again on the mill wall, laughing at my cloud-names, and I felt the old wound open in a new place — because it turns out grief and joy live in the same house and share a kitchen.

Then her face closed again, slower this time, like a door drawn shut by someone determined not to slam it. "The man with the brand on his jaw," she said. "Before they ran. He spoke to you."

"He apologized."

"For attacking us?"

"For making you use it. Your magic. He said — " I hesitated. Some translations feel like betrayals. "He said the gifted aren't supposed to fight each other anymore. That we did that once, generations back, and it's how the crown got its teeth. He said every time one of us burns another, the thing in the palace gets fatter."

Sera stared at me. "What thing in the palace?"

And there it was. The fork in the road. I could lie — I'm good at lying, it's most of smuggling — and stay a prisoner being escorted, safe and doomed. Or I could tell her the truth I'd been carrying since my master's cottage burned, the truth stitched into my own bones, and make her complicit.

"Ask me again," I said, "when you actually want the answer. Not the answer that lets you keep your commission. When you want the one that costs."

She held my gaze for a long, long moment. Somewhere beyond the birches, a crow called twice, and Dace coughed wetly in his blankets, and the fire settled.

"I'm starting to want expensive answers," she said quietly.

"Good," I said. "Because I think you're about to get a discount."

### Sera

We made Corvale's outskirts by the fourth evening, and I made my decision somewhere along the flats between the last hills and the first watchtowers, though I couldn't have told you the exact mile-marker where it happened. Decisions like that don't have coordinates. They have weight, and one morning you notice you've been carrying them for days.

The dispatch rider found us at dusk — a boy on a lathered horse with sealed orders from Command, the wax stamped with the royal sigil. I broke the seal expecting routing instructions.

What I got was a list. Names. Eleven names, prisoners already forwarded ahead of us over the past month, each annotated with the same single word in the same clerk's hand: *Received.* No cell assignments. No assessment dates. No disposition. Eleven human beings swallowed by the palace like stones down a well.

And beneath the list, the order that made my blood go cold and hot at once: upon arrival, the prisoner Pemberton was to be delivered not to the dungeons proper but to the *undercroft access*, where an escort would be waiting. Signed not by Roke. Signed by the King's own hand — or the stamp of it, the crowned seal pressed deep enough to dent the paper through.

Kings don't sign for individual hedge-mages. Kings sign declarations and treaties and, once in an unlucky generation, death warrants for traitors. Kings do not personally arrange the receiving hours for one smuggler with scorch marks on his wrists.

I rode back along the column to where Emrys walked between Brannic and the wagons, and I didn't dismount, because I wanted the height. I wanted to look down on him one last time as his captor, before whatever I was about to become.

"Pemberton." I held out the dispatch. "Read it."

He read it slowly, lips moving slightly — I'd noticed he did that, some old habit of checking his master's handwriting against memory — and I watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out.

"She's alive," he whispered.

"Who?"

"My master. Maren Thistlewood. The cottage — I thought she was dead, the fire, the blood — but this list. Third name." His finger trembled above the parchment. "*M. Thistlewood. Received.* She was taken to the palace, Sera. Taken, not killed. Which means—" He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and blazing all at once, and I remembered that exact look from six years ago, the night he asked me to run, and I hated how well I remembered it. "Which means they're keeping the gifted alive. For something. And the king himself wants me brought in special. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means," I said slowly, "that whatever you were going to tell me in the birch wood — the expensive answer — I need it now. All of it."

He looked at the dispatch again, at the deep-dented royal seal, and then at me, and I saw him do his arithmetic — the smuggler's arithmetic, risk weighed to the copper — and for once in his careful, hidden life, he spent everything.

"All right," he said. "But not here, and not in earshot of your soldiers. And Sera?" He smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, gallows-humor and genuine terror holding hands. "When I tell you what wears the crown of this kingdom — try to keep hold of your sword. I'm going to need it."
