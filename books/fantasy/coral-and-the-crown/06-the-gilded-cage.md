## 6. The Gilded Cage

They say you can smell a capital three days before you see it. What they don't tell you is what the smell *is*.

In my case it was tallow, river mud, and something sweeter underneath—incense, maybe, or rot dressed up in perfume. The kind of city that burns candles for its dead and calls the smoke piety. I'd been to Vael Corin exactly once before, at nineteen, holding Sera's hand on the ferry crossing with my pockets full of stolen plums and my head full of nothing better than her laugh. That boy had thought cities were just big villages with better thieves.

The man in the wagon cage knew better. Cities were arithmetic too—just with bigger numbers and worse odds.

I did the sums as we rolled down the pilgrim road. Four hunters riding escort, plus Captain Vane, minus one who'd taken fever at the last waystation. The cage was ash-wood, which meant someone had spent real money warding it, which meant someone above the captain's pay grade cared about what was inside me. And the roads were wrong. Too many patrols for an ordinary prisoner transfer, too few people on them. A capital that empties its streets when a wagon comes through is a capital that already knows what's in the wagon.

Or thinks it does.

*You're a smuggler,* I reminded myself, watching the palace rise out of the morning haze like a wedding cake left out in the rain. White stone, gold leaf, spires that caught the light and threw it back like a boast. *Smugglers survive by being worth more alive than dead.*

It should have been comforting. It wasn't. Because there are only so many reasons a man is worth more alive, and most of them end with someone opening you up to find out how you work.

* * *

They didn't take me to the dungeons.

I want that understood, because I'd spent six years imagining dungeons—the dark, the damp, the rats with ambitions. Instead they took me *down*, past the dungeons, past cells with straw and shackles bolted at civilized heights, down stairs that predated the stairs above them, into corridors where the stone had been cut by hands that believed walls should be thick enough to keep out God.

"Prisoner Pemberton," said the turnkey, a grey man with grey hands, reading from a slate as though I were a delivery of lamp oil. "By order of His Majesty, retained."

"Retained," I repeated.

"Not executed." He said it the way a man says *not raining*—a fact about weather, not mercy. "Order's signed. King's own hand."

He showed me the paper. I'm not sure why. Perhaps he enjoyed it. The seal was broken wax the color of old blood, and the signature above it was elegant, looping, confident—a king's hand, taught by tutors to look like authority.

Except I'd seen forged royal signatures before. I'd carried documents with forged royal signatures before. And this one had the particular perfection of something written by something that had studied human writing the way a crow studies a scarecrow: getting every letter right and none of the meaning.

"Comfortable," I said.

"It's the best cell in the palace," the turnkey said, without irony, because he genuinely believed it.

And here was the terrible thing: it was. A proper bed. A basin. A window—*a window*, barred, but positioned so that if I stood on the bed I could see a slice of courtyard and, beyond it, the base of the throne tower. Someone had arranged this. Someone wanted me kept alive, kept whole, and kept looking at exactly that tower.

I lay on the bed that first night and did the only sum that mattered: *Why?*

Kings didn't sign orders for hedge-witches. Kings signed edicts; underlings did the burning. An edict said *seize or burn*, and burn was cheaper. Retaining me—feeding me, housing me above the dungeon like a guest who couldn't leave—cost coin. Coin meant intent. Intent meant someone had looked at a smuggler from Thornfell and seen something worth keeping.

I fell asleep doing arithmetic and woke up doing it again, which is how I knew I was afraid. Fear, for me, had always been a form of bookkeeping.

* * *

Three days passed. Maybe four. Time moves strangely underground; the meals came at intervals that might have been regular or might have been theater.

On the third—or fourth—night, I saw the king.

I hadn't been sleeping. I'd been standing at the window doing my nightly inventory of the courtyard, which contained two guards, one cat with a proprietary air, and nothing else, when torchlight bloomed in the colonnade below. A procession. Small—not a parade, a *passing*. Six honor guards in white and gold, four priests in grey, and between them, walking slowly, a figure in a crown.

I'd never seen Aldric IV in person. Few had, anymore; the king had grown famously reclusive these last years, ruling by seal and proclamation, letting his ministers be seen so he wouldn't have to be. But I'd seen coins. I'd seen the portraits in waystation common rooms, faded and heroic. A broad man, the portraits said. A strong jaw. A father-of-the-realm face.

The man below was wearing that face the way I wore my coat—correctly, buttoned, but *put on*.

He walked like a man remembering how. Each step placed a half-beat late, corrected, placed again. His hands hung at his sides with the stillness of things hung on hooks. And when he paused at the fountain to let the priests catch up, he turned his head toward the water—and stopped. Just stopped. For the count of five, maybe six, he stood motionless while torches guttered and guards shifted and the night waited around him like a held breath.

Then his head turned back, smooth as a drawbridge, and he walked on.

I told myself: tired. Old men pause. Grief pauses. The king had lost a queen some years back, and a son before that—everyone knew the story, everyone lowered their voice about it.

But here is what I could not unsee, and what has lived behind my eyes ever since: his shadow.

The torches threw six shadows off that procession—guards, priests, all of them ordinary, all of their shadows lagging and stretching the way shadows do. The king's shadow did not lag. The king's shadow moved *first*. When he lifted his arm to bless the fountain—blessing a fountain, mind you, as though fountains had been gossiping—it was the shadow whose arm rose first, a half-heartbeat ahead of the flesh, and the flesh followed after like a man agreeing with a suggestion.

Shadows follow. That is the entire job of a shadow. Follow, copy, obey the light.

His gave orders.

I stood at the bars until the torches died and the courtyard went black, and my hands were shaking, and I noticed distantly that I wasn't cold—I was *hungry*-shaking, the deep tremor of a gift waking up after years of being sat on. My magic had gone quiet the moment they caged me, sulking, suppressed by whatever was woven into the ash-wood. Now it pressed against the inside of my ribs like a bird against glass, and it was pressing *away* from the tower. Away from the direction the king had gone.

My magic, which had never once in my life shown good sense, was terrified.

"Well," I said to the empty cell, because talking to yourself is free and no one down here grades your performance. "That's not ominous at all."

* * *

The curiosity arrived on schedule, the way it always does, right on the heels of dread like a debt collector.

Here's the problem with being built the way I'm built: fear tells me to run, but fear also tells me to *count*. And the numbers would not add up. A hollow king who walks like a puppet still signs orders. Orders that say *retain*, not *burn*. Edicts that sweep every gifted soul in the realm into cages—but why does a thing that hunts magic need cages? Burn them where they stand. Feed where you find them. Unless—

Unless it wasn't feeding yet. Unless it was *collecting*.

Unless somewhere beneath this wedding-cake palace there was a larder, and I was in it, and the elegant looping signature on my retention order was a menu notation.

I sat on the bed with my knees drawn up and made myself look at the whole ledger, ugly line by ugly line. Master Odile's cottage burned. Her grimoire missing its final page. Hunters who didn't kill me when killing me was easier. A cell with a view of the throne tower. A king whose shadow moved first.

Six years I'd survived by being small. By making myself a rounding error in other people's sums. And the whole time, the largest number in the kingdom had been counting *me*—counting all of us—herding the gifted like coin into a vault, and I had walked into the vault wearing chains and calling it luck that they hadn't killed me.

There was a knock at the door. Not the turnkey's knock—his had a rhythm, two-and-one, bored as rain. This was a single knock, soft, almost apologetic.

The door opened without waiting for my answer, which told me everything about who held the keys down here.

Captain Sera Vane stood in the corridor with a lantern held low, and she looked—gods, she looked like the last six years had been happening to her too, worn into her face in lines I wanted to trace and apologize for. She glanced down the corridor both ways, then at me, and her jaw did the thing it used to do when she was deciding whether to say a thing or swallow it.

"You're not to be executed," she said. "I checked the dispatches twice. There's no trial scheduled. No transfer. Nothing."

"I know. I read the order."

"Then you know what that means."

I did the sum out loud, because she'd always hated it when I hoarded the arithmetic. "It means someone wants me alive for reasons that have nothing to do with justice. It means I'm not a prisoner, I'm *stores*."

Her knuckles whitened on the lantern handle. "Emrys—"

"Don't." I said it gently. "Whatever you came down here to warn me about or apologize for or not apologize for—you shouldn't be seen. And I shouldn't be glad you came, and I am anyway, and we can both regret that later."

For a moment she just looked at me, and the lantern light did something unfair to her eyes. Then she set something on the floor and slid it through the bars: a heel of bread, an apple, and—wrapped in cloth, deliberately separate, as if even now she was careful about what evidence she left—a stub of chalk.

"For the wall," she said. "Counters get restless."

She turned to go, then stopped, her back to me, her voice pitched low enough that the stones barely caught it.

"The king passed my patrol yesterday. Close enough to touch." A pause, long enough that I heard her decide. "His shadow moved before he did, Emrys. I've been telling myself I imagined it for a day and a half."

"You didn't imagine it."

She nodded once, sharply, like a soldier accepting terrain she didn't want, and was gone, her footsteps fading up the stairs two at a time, quick and quiet and very much alive.

I sat back down on the bed with the apple in my hand and the chalk beside me, and I looked at the slice of tower visible through my window, black against the stars.

A king who wasn't a king. A hunt that was a harvest. A captain who had counted the same impossible sum I had and come to the same total, alone in the dark, and climbed down here anyway.

I bit into the apple. It was good. That felt like an insult, somehow.

Beneath the palace, in a cell built for something other than prisoners, I picked up the chalk and drew my first mark on the wall—not a tally of days, I decided, but a tally of questions. Room for hundreds.

Because dread was a fine survival tool, but curiosity had always been the sharper blade, and mine had just been handed a whetstone.

Somewhere above me, behind gold and white stone, something ancient wore a crown and moved its shadow first.

And somewhere in the arithmetic of this rotten, gilded place, I was going to find the number that undid it.
