## 7. The Hollow King

They came for me at dawn, or what passed for dawn down there, which was a grate in the ceiling forty feet up that turned from black to a slightly less ambitious black. Six years in the mountains had taught me to read light like a farmer reads weather. Down here, in the palace undercrofts, I'd learned instead to read the boots of my jailers. Soft-soled meant routine. Iron-shod meant someone upstairs had remembered I existed.

Iron-shod.

I stood up off the straw before they knocked, which disappointed them. Jailers hate a prisoner who ruins the drama of the door.

"Up," said the tall one, unnecessarily, because I was already up.

"Where are we going?" I asked, though I knew. You always know. It's the same way you know when the ice on a ford is about to give—there's a sound the world makes, a kind of held breath, and everything goes very quiet and very clear.

"The king will see you now," said the short one, and there it was. The sentence every gifted soul in Thornfell had been dreading since the edict came down like an avalanche with a wax seal on it.

"Does he see everyone personally?" I asked. "I'd have thought the paperwork alone—"

The tall one hit me in the stomach with a gauntleted fist, gently, the way you'd nudge a mule. It still folded me like a letter. I decided to take the hint. There's a time for wit and a time for keeping your teeth, and I've always been better at the second.

* * *

They marched me up through the palace the way you'd carry a piece of evidence, which is to say carefully and with everyone pretending not to look at you. And everyone was looking. Servants flattened themselves against walls. A chamberlain dropped an entire tray of candles and didn't even swear, which in my experience is the mark of either a saint or a man who has seen the king receive guests before.

I looked too. I couldn't help it. I'd spent six years learning every crack and seam of the Thornfell passes, and a palace is just a mountain someone built on purpose, and I read it the same way. And what I read made my skin crawl.

The place was *fed on*. I don't know how else to say it. Every hedge-witch knows the feeling of a room where magic has been spent—a hearth-charm, a birthing blessing, the small warm residue of the craft. Rooms hold it like wool holds smoke. This palace held the opposite. It was a wool coat turned inside out in a snowstorm. The stone was hungry. The tapestries looked faded not from sun but from *drinking*, if tapestries could drink. Even the light coming through the windows seemed thinner on the inside than the out, like the building had taken a cut of it at the border.

"You feel it too," said a voice beside me, and I startled, because Captain Sera Vane had materialized at my elbow in that way she had, silent as a debt coming due. She wasn't in armor. She wore the grey half-cloak of the hunt, and she looked like she hadn't slept, and she was watching me with an expression I couldn't file under anything.

"Feel what?" I said, because I'm a coward with excellent instincts.

"Never mind." She looked forward again. "When you're in there. Don't look at his shadow."

"What?"

"Emrys." My name in her mouth, after all these years, still landed like a stone in a still pond. "Don't look at his shadow."

And then the doors opened, and there wasn't any more time for advice.

* * *

The throne room of Vael Corin was the size of a church and about as forgiving. Banners of every province hung from the rafters, including, I noted with a small cold pride, the black stag of Thornfell, though ours hung lowest and looked the most faded. Courtiers lined the walls in their silks, arranged with the studied casualness of people who had come specifically to watch something happen to me.

And at the far end, on the throne of the Corantine kings, sat Aldric the Fourth, Shield of the Realm, Father of His People, and so forth.

I'd seen his face on coins. Everyone had. A square jaw, a strong nose, the kind of features sculptors like because they're easy. The man on the throne had those features. He had the crown, the scepter, the ermine, all of it, every piece in its proper place.

He was not in there.

I don't mean he was dead. Dead would have been a mercy and, frankly, less frightening. I mean the way you mean it when you say a house is empty even though the furniture's all there. His eyes were open and pointed at me, and nothing behind them pointed back. His chest rose and fell on a slow, even rhythm, like a bellows worked by someone very patient. And when he shifted his weight—just slightly, adjusting his grip on the scepter—the motion was *wrong*. Not stiff. Not sick. Wrong the way a puppet is wrong, all the movement happening at the joints and none of it in the middle, as if the man were a glove and the hand inside it had too many fingers.

I looked at his shadow, because Sera told me not to, and I have never once in my life done what I was told when the alternative was interesting.

The shadow didn't match him.

That's the only way I can put it. The king sat still, and his shadow moved. It pooled and gathered and *leaned*, the way a dog leans against a fence when it smells something on the other side. It leaned toward me. And I felt it lean. Every charm I'd ever carried, every hedge-trick in my blood, everything I'd spent six years hiding and starving and rationing down to copper-coin portions—I felt all of it turn over in me like a sleeper disturbed, and strain toward that shadow the way a moth strains at a window.

It wanted me. Not my body. Not my information. My *gift*. It wanted the magic in me the way I wanted bread after a hard pass, and it was looking at me with the whole bottomless patience of a thing that has never once gone hungry.

I made a mistake then. I flinched. I let the old smuggler's reflex take over—the one that says *be small, be dull, be nothing worth taking*—and I reached for the little dampening trick my master taught me, the one that wraps your gift in grey wool so hunters' senses slide off it.

The shadow *laughed*.

There was no sound. The king's face didn't move. But the shadow rippled, and the ripple went through the room like a bell struck under water, and every courtier shivered at once without knowing why, and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, that the dampening trick was like hiding a candle by cupping your hands around it in front of a starving man.

It hadn't noticed me despite the wool. It had noticed me *because* of it. Nothing hides from the thing that eats light except by being light.

"Emrys Pemberton," said the king.

The voice was his voice, the voice off the coins and the proclamations, and it was also not his at all, because no man's voice sounds like a well being spoken through. The courtiers bowed their heads at the sound. Somewhere in the ranks I found Sera, standing very straight, very still, her eyes fixed on a point six inches to the left of the throne. Following her own advice.

"You are a long way from your mountains," said the thing that wore the king.

I should have knelt. Protocol said kneel. Survival said kneel. But my knees had heard the well-voice too, and they'd voted to stay exactly where they were.

"Your Majesty honors me," I managed. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. Six years of haggling with smugglers over charm-tariffs will do that. "Though I confess I don't know why the crown bothers with a hedge-wizard from Thornfell. There are archmages in the Collegium. There are oracles in Senn. I make bread rise and find lost goats."

The king's mouth smiled. The smile arrived at the corners first and the eyes last, and the eyes never really committed.

"The Collegium," it said, "is a pantry we keep stocked for ceremonies. The oracles are dried up. You—" and here the shadow stretched, just a finger's width, just enough that I saw it, even if no one else did, "—you are fresh. Mountain-grown. They always are, the ones who hide. Fear seasons the gift. Like ham."

Somewhere in the gallery a woman made a small sound and was escorted out.

"I don't understand," I said, which was a lie, because I was understanding it all at once, the whole edict, the whole hunt, six years of burnings and seizures and hunters in the passes, and it all snapped into place with the horrible click of a lock I'd been picking from the wrong side. "You're not hunting the gifted because we're dangerous."

"No."

"You're hunting us because we're *food*."

The courtiers didn't react. That was the worst part. They stood there in their silks and their boredom, and either they didn't know what their king was or they'd known so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in a river. The thing on the throne regarded me with its borrowed eyes, and if it felt accused, it didn't show it. Why would it? You don't feel accused in front of the bread.

"Your master understood," it said.

The floor went out from under me, the way it does when a sentence carries a name you weren't braced for. "You've seen her."

"We have her."

The words went through me like the gauntlet had. I thought of the burned cottage, the ash on the wind, the missing final page. I had spent two weeks on the road telling myself she was dead, because dead was terrible but dead was *clean*, dead meant grief and nothing after it. Dead meant I'd failed her once, six years ago, when I ran from my apprenticeship with my tail between my legs and my gift still wet behind the ears, and the failure was finished and could be carried like a stone instead of worn like a wound.

Taken. She was taken. She was down here somewhere, in the dark under this hungry palace, and the thing that ate magic had her, and it had her because she knew something, because she'd written something on the last page of her grimoire that it wanted, and I had spent two weeks grieving a woman who was still—

"Where," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle like river ice.

"Safe," said the king, and the well-voice made the word sound like a joke only it got. "She is kept. She is *useful*. As you will be."

And here is the thing I will have to answer for, someday, to someone, maybe to myself: for one long moment, I was tempted.

Not by cruelty. Not by power. I've never wanted power; power is just weight you carry uphill. I was tempted by the oldest thing there is, the thing that keeps smugglers alive and keeps them small: the offer of *enough*. Enough food. Enough warmth. Enough of not running. Six years I had lived on the arithmetic of survival—every charm rationed, every kindness priced, every road chosen for its exits. Six years of being a rumor with a heartbeat. And here was the thing that had burned the cottages and emptied the villages, offering me, in the same breath that it confessed to holding my master, a life. A real one. A room with a door that locked from the inside.

"Serve," it said, and the shadow spread along the floor toward me like spilled ink, unhurried, certain, "and you will want for nothing. Your gift will be fed, not starved. You will see her. You will be *seen*, Emrys Pemberton. No more passes. No more hiding. No more arithmetic."

No more arithmetic.

It had read me like a ledger. It knew that was the phrase that would hook me, the one I used in my own head at three in the morning in a snowdrift, weighing my life in copper coins. And God help me, some cold practical corner of my mind actually ran the numbers. *Serve the thing, live, see her, eat real bread.* The corner of my mind that had kept me alive for six years sat there doing sums while the rest of me screamed.

And then I looked at the king.

Not at the thing wearing him. At him. At what was left of Aldric the Fourth, if anything was left. And I saw—maybe I imagined it, maybe it was the light, but I saw—his hands. They were trembling. Not the puppet's tremble, not the too-many-fingers motion. A small, human, exhausted shaking, the shaking of a man trying to hold a door shut from the inside, and the fingers of the left hand, the ones not gripping the scepter, curled once, twice, in a pattern.

Three short. One long. Three short.

It was nothing. It was a spasm. It was the twitch of a dying muscle in a body long since vacated.

It was also, and I would have bet my life on it, which was convenient, since my life was exactly what was on the table—it was also the signal my master and I had used when I was fourteen and lazy and she'd catch me letting my wards slip. Three short, one long. *Wake up. Pay attention. You are not seeing what's in front of you.*

Somebody was home.

The temptation didn't die. I want to be honest about that. The offer hung in the air like smoke, and the arithmetic kept ticking in the back of my skull, and the shadow kept leaning toward me with all the patience of a thing that had worn kings before and could wait out one hedge-wizard's pride. But the arithmetic had a new line item now, written in a dying man's curled fingers: *you are not seeing what's in front of you.* Which meant there was more to see. Which meant the board had pieces I hadn't counted. Which meant the smart play—the smuggler's play—was to take the deal, take the time, take the access, and find out what those pieces were.

So I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the passes, harder than the ford, harder than anything that came after: I smiled at it.

"I'll need to think," I said. "Your Majesty. I'm a careful man. It's how I've stayed alive."

The well-voice was silent a moment. The shadow drew back, slowly, reluctantly, the way a tide goes out, and I felt my gift settle again in my chest, bruised but mine.

"Think, then," it said. "We are generous. We have been king for a very long time. We know how to wait."

And as the guards took my arms and turned me toward the doors, the king's left hand curled once more—three short, one long—and the mouth that wasn't his said, in the well-voice, almost tenderly:

"We will show you where we keep her. When you are ready to serve."

The doors closed behind me, and the throne room's hunger faded, and I walked back down through the palace between my iron-shod escorts with my heart hammering out the same rhythm, over and over, three short, one long, three short, one long, like a knock on a door from the inside of the world.

I didn't look at Sera as they took me past. But I heard her fall into step behind me, unassigned, unexplained, and I heard her say to the guards, in the flat voice of a woman reading from regulations, "I'll take him back. The king's warrant names me his keeper."

And I thought: *she saw the hand too.* Or she saw something. Or she was simply there, the way she had always somehow been there, at the ford, at the ambush, at the worst moments of my life, arriving like weather.

They put me back in my cell. The grate overhead went dark. And in the dark I sat down on the straw and did the only arithmetic that had ever really mattered, the sum I'd been avoiding for six years:

My master was alive. The king was a door with something behind it. And somewhere in this palace was a woman I had loved and run from and been captured by, who had just claimed custody of me with a warrant I was fairly sure she'd invented.

I put my head back against the stone and started, for the first time since the edict came down, to plan.
