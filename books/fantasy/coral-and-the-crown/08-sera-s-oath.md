## 8. Sera's Oath

The order came down on the third day, written in a hand so beautiful it looked like an apology.

*By command of His Majesty: the prisoners taken in the Thornfell action are to be processed according to the Edict of Cleansing. Processing to be completed by week's end. The Crown thanks Captain Vane for her diligence and discretion.*

Processing. That was what they called it now. Not execution. Not burning. *Processing*, as if my company were a tannery and the witches were hides.

I read it four times in the officers' mess while Lieutenant Hask drank his morning tea and complained about the capital's bread. The paper was cream-colored, heavy in the hand, sealed with the royal sigil—the crown over the crossed keys—and signed at the bottom with a flourish that trailed off like smoke.

I had seen the king sign things before. Two years ago, at the autumn review, I'd stood close enough to watch him countersign a promotion order for a sergeant who'd saved three children from a flooded ford. The king had a particular way of writing: quick, impatient strokes, the letters leaning forward as though he were always about to stand up.

This signature leaned backward. Every letter of it. As though the hand that made it had been dragged across the page against its will.

"Sera." Hask lowered his voice, glancing at the door. "You've read it?"

"I've read it."

"And?" He set down his cup. "Six of them. The two from Millbrook, the ferryman's family, the old woman from the pass, the boy—"

"The boy is fourteen," I said. "He makes charms to keep milk from souring."

"He makes *unlicensed* charms. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone." Hask wasn't being cruel. That was the worst part. He was being careful, the way good soldiers are careful, because he believed the edict was law and law was the only thing standing between the realm and fire. "We knew this was coming when we took the commission. We're hunters, Sera. It's the whole word."

*I am a hunter,* I thought. *It is the whole word.*

And then I thought of Emrys Pemberton, chained in the dark beneath the palace, looking up at me through wet hair with those eyes that hadn't changed at all in six years, and I thought: *you didn't hesitate then either, did you? You just did what the warrant said.*

Except he'd run from me. I'd never run from anything in my life. I'd marched straight toward it, orders first, and now the orders wanted fourteen-year-olds burned for sour milk.

"I'll handle the processing," I said.

Hask relaxed visibly. "Good. I'll draw up the transport schedule—"

"No transport. It'll be done here. Quietly. Fewer chances for a mob, fewer chances for escape." I folded the order along its crease. "I'll need the records room for an hour."

"Records? What for?"

"Verification of capture dates. The tribunal requires them." This was true. It was also not why I needed the records room. "Go on, Hask. Draw your schedule for something else. I'll send word when it's finished."

He hesitated, then saluted and left, and I sat alone in the mess with the beautiful, terrible letter, listening to my own heartbeat and thinking about leaning signatures.

My father used to say there were two kinds of soldiers: the kind who obeyed and the kind who served. He'd been the second kind, dead twenty years now in a border skirmish nobody remembered, and he'd told me once, when I was small enough to sit on his knee and wear his helmet like a bucket: *"Sera, the day the crown asks you to do a thing your stomach won't swallow, remember that the crown isn't a man. It's a promise. If the man breaks the promise, you follow the promise, not the man."*

I had recited that to myself a thousand times on the long road from Thornfell, escorting a prisoner whose wrists I'd bound myself, telling myself that the edict was harsh but necessary, that magic was dangerous, that the burning of Millbrook had been regrettable but contained. I had held onto it the way a drowning sailor holds a spar—because letting go meant drowning.

But sparrots rot. And this morning, holding a piece of cream-colored paper with a signature that leaned the wrong way, I felt the wood come apart in my hands.

* * *

The records room was in the east wing, down a corridor that smelled of lamp oil and old vellum. A clerk named Odwin kept it—a stooped man with ink-stained cuffs and a memory like a ledger. He owed me. Last spring I'd looked away for exactly as long as it took him to smuggle his sister's family out of a quarantine district, and we had never spoken of it since.

"Captain Vane." He bowed slightly. "What can the archives provide?"

"Capture records. Thornfell action, all prisoners." I kept my voice flat, official. "Also—" and here I made the choice, the one that would burn every bridge behind me, "—the sealed dispatches. All of them. Since the edict was proclaimed."

Odwin went very still. "Those require a writ from the Privy Council."

"They require a writ from someone willing to ask questions," I said. "Are you?"

A long pause. Then Odwin turned to his shelves, moving with the deliberate calm of a man committing treason in comfortable shoes, and began pulling files.

It took him twenty minutes to assemble the stack. While he worked, I read the capture records, and that was where the first lie showed itself: dates. Emrys's capture record listed the ambush at the ford—but the date was wrong. Off by nine days. And the location was wrong too; the ford was forty miles south of where we'd actually taken him, near a village called Ashcombe.

Ashcombe. Where the cottage had burned. I'd seen the dispatch about it—a hedge-witch's dwelling, occupant unaccounted for. Occupant *taken*, more likely, though the report said otherwise. And Emrys's official record said he'd been captured at a place he'd never been, on a date that never happened.

Someone had edited history. Someone very high up wanted the trail to Ashcombe buried.

"Why?" I murmured.

"Because," said Odwin quietly, without turning around, "the sealed dispatches answer that better than I can. Captain—I'm an old man. I've kept these records for thirty years, and until this winter they were boring. Now half of them have false dates and missing pages. Whatever you find in there—" he set the last folder on the table, wax seals already broken, which meant he'd read them himself, days ago probably, "—I never gave it to you."

"Understood."

He left me alone with the folders.

The first dispatch was routine: troop allocations, supply requests, the usual bureaucratic gristle. The second was thicker, sealed with the private seal of the Lord High Inquisitor rather than the crown, and dated eleven days after the edict was proclaimed.

*To the commanders of the Hunt, eyes only: The Crown directs that prisoners of the gift be conveyed to the palace undercrofts for assessment prior to processing. Assessment is mandatory. No prisoner shall be processed—repeat, no prisoner shall be processed—without prior assessment by the Crown's assessors. Commanders are reminded that the value of a prisoner lies not in his destruction but in his delivery.*

Not in his destruction but in his delivery.

I read that line five times. It changed everything and explained nothing. If the goal was extermination, why keep them alive? If the goal was something else—something that required living, gifted prisoners delivered to the undercrofts—then the edict wasn't a cleansing at all.

It was a harvest.

The third dispatch confirmed it. Dated three weeks later, again under the Inquisitor's seal:

*Assessment yields continue to please the Crown. Note for future actions: prioritize intact captives over corpses. Subjects depleted beyond recovery are of limited use. The Crown further directs that special attention be paid to practitioners of significant power. One such subject, an elderly female practitioner formerly of Ashcombe, has been designated priority. Delivery of this subject supersedes all other operational concerns.*

An elderly female practitioner, formerly of Ashcombe.

Emrys's master. Alive, then—or she had been three weeks ago. Taken, not killed, exactly as he must have suspected. Delivered here, to the palace, to be *assessed*. To be *yielded*.

Yields. They were using harvest language. Crops language. As if people were fields.

I sat in the lamplight with the dispatches spread out before me and felt something in my chest go quiet—not the peace of certainty, but the terrible stillness of a building just before it settles. Six years of service. Two campaigns. A scar on my left arm I could tell stories about. All of it built on the belief that the crown was a promise, and the promise was worth following even when the man wearing it was cruel.

But the man wasn't cruel. The man was a *door*. I'd seen him through the bars of the transport yard—seen the way his shadow moved a half-second behind his body, like a sound arriving after its echo. Everyone said grief had bent him. Grief didn't bend shadows.

The edict wasn't policy. It was feeding.

And my company—Hask, and young Pell, and old Marta with her bad knee, and the sixty others who trusted me—my company had spent the winter dragging the harvest in.

I thought about the fourteen-year-old with the milk-charms. About the ferryman's wife, who cried the whole ride to the capital and whose only crime, per her neighbors' testimony, was that her bread always rose perfectly. About the old woman from the pass who had blessed my horse once, years ago, back before any of this, pressing her palm to its neck and saying *safe roads, soldier girl*, and how the horse had never thrown a shoe since.

*No prisoner shall be processed without prior assessment.*

Which meant that if I followed orders—if I did the thing my stomach couldn't swallow—every one of them would go down into the undercrofts first, into whatever waited down there, and be drained like wine before the fire ever touched them. And their deaths would be recorded as *processing complete*, in beautiful handwriting, with signatures that leaned the wrong way.

Unless the records said otherwise.

I looked at the blank forms stacked beside the dispatches. Processing reports. Confirmation slips. The paperwork of death, waiting to be filled in.

Odwin had said: *whatever you find in there, I never gave it to you.*

Fine. Then I never found anything. And the processing reports would say that the Thornfell prisoners had been transferred to a facility that didn't exist, on a road nobody traveled, under escort names drawn from a roster I invented on the spot. On paper, six people would die neatly and completely. In truth—

In truth I had no idea yet how to keep six people alive inside a palace that ate them. But paper was a start. Paper bought time, and time was the only currency I had left that the crown couldn't seize.

I took up the pen.

* * *

It took me until well past midnight to forge six deaths.

That sounds simple. It wasn't. A forged processing report fails not on the facts but on the texture—the little imperfections that real bureaucracy produces and clean forgery doesn't. So I made mistakes on purpose. I smudged the third entry. I misdated the second slip and corrected it in a different ink. I wrote Hask's name in the escort column and then, remembering he'd want his tea during the transfer, added a note about rations in the margin, because Hask always noted rations in margins. I let my own hand shake on the final signature—not performing grief, just letting it happen—and the result was a document so authentically weary that I almost believed it myself.

Six prisoners, processed according to the Edict. Transferred to secondary facility at [a village I invented, population ninety, famous only for its cheese]. Escort: Sergeant Dray, Corporal Fenwick, and four others whose names existed nowhere except my pen.

When it was done, I filed the originals in the cabinet where dead paperwork goes to sleep, and I sat for a long moment with my forehead resting on the cool wood, feeling lighter and heavier at once. Lighter, because six people would wake tomorrow. Heavier, because I understood what I'd just become.

A traitor. Quietly, in triplicate.

My father's voice, somewhere: *the crown isn't a man, Sera. It's a promise.* And underneath it, colder: *and you just stopped following the man.*

There was one more thing to do, and I'd been avoiding it since sundown.

* * *

The cells beneath the palace were older than the palace above them—everyone said so. The stones down there were fitted without mortar, in a pattern that predated the current dynasty, and the corridors curved in ways that made no architectural sense, as though whoever built them had been digging toward something rather than building around it.

I showed my captain's badge to the undercroft watch. They waved me through. Captains visit prisoners; that's normal; that's procedure. Nobody questions a hunter checking on her catch.

Emrys's cell was at the end of the third gallery, past a locked door that required two keys and a password that changed daily. I gave the password—*ashenford*, today's word, chosen by some clerk with a sense of humor—and descended the last stair.

He was awake. Of course he was awake. He was sitting cross-legged on the straw with his eyes closed, and when I stopped outside the bars he opened them, and in the lamplight they were the same color as the river the day we'd met at the ford—all grey-green movement, nothing settled in them.

"You're late," he said.

"I wasn't aware we had an appointment."

"We didn't. That's what made it easy to wait." He unfolded himself and stood, chains whispering. Up close, in honest light, I could see what the road hadn't shown me: he was thinner than he should be, and there was a bruised look under his eyes, the look of a man who has been doing arithmetic in the dark for days. "You've been crying."

"I have not."

"You have the same face you had at Marta's funeral, when you swore you had ash in your eye and everyone politely agreed." He wrapped his hands around the bars—not gripping, just resting, like a man leaning on a fence to talk to a neighbor. "So. Either you've come to kill me, or you've come to tell me something. You're not holding a knife, and captains don't make social calls at midnight, so."

"So," I agreed.

"So talk, Sera. Before the password changes at dawn and some other face comes down those stairs."

I should have been careful. I should have tested him, hedged, given him nothing. Instead I heard myself say: "The prisoners scheduled for processing. The others from Thornfell. Did you know them?"

Something moved behind his eyes. "Some. The ferryman's wife reads fortunes with playing cards, badly. The old woman—Wrenna—she blessed horses. Why?"

"Then they're alive tonight because of a lie I wrote." I pulled the folded copy of my forged report from inside my coat and held it up between us. "As far as the palace knows, they were transferred to a facility in the country. A facility I invented. On paper, they're dead. In truth, they're still in the holding cells upstairs, and I have maybe three days before someone checks the ledgers and notices that Sergeant Dray does not exist."

Emrys stared at the paper. Then at me. "You falsified crown records."

"I falsified crown records."

"For prisoners you were ordered to execute."

"For prisoners I was ordered to *process*." I heard my voice crack on the word and hated it. "Do you know what processing means, Emrys? Down here? Do you know what the assessments are?" I pressed the dispatches' words out of my mouth one at a time, like teeth. "*The value of a prisoner lies not in his destruction but in his delivery.* They're not killing the gifted. They're harvesting them. There's a vault somewhere under this palace full of people being drained, and my master's—your master is in it, and I found it in sealed dispatches, and I have spent the last four hours pretending to be a murderer so that six people don't join her."

Silence. The lamp flame leaned in a draft and righted itself.

"You could hang for that," he said finally.

"Yes."

"You *will* hang for that, if it unravels."

"Probably."

"Then why?" And there it was—the question under all the questions, the one he'd been carrying since the ford, maybe since the cottage fire, maybe since a night six years ago when a boy ran from an apprenticeship and a girl let him go without saying the one word that might have stopped him. "Why would you burn your whole life for strangers and a man who left you?"

I gripped the bars because my hands needed something true to hold.

"Because my father told me the crown is a promise," I said. "And tonight I read the promise's fine print, and it says *harvest*. Because Wrenna blessed my horse once and it never went lame. Because a fourteen-year-old makes charms to keep milk sweet and the crown wants him drained like a cask. Because—" and here my voice did fail me, briefly, and I let it, "—because I spent six years being loyal to a thing that eats people, and the moment I saw it plainly, staying loyal became the one order I couldn't follow. That's all. There's no grand oath in it. Just a soldier finally reading her orders."

Emrys was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough.

"The last page," he said. "Of my master's grimoire. The one that was stolen from the ashes. I think it was the unbinding ritual. I think she was taken *because* she knew it, and I think whatever wears the king means to wear her next, once she's empty enough to fit." He stepped closer to the bars, close enough that I could see the pulse in his throat. "I can't reach her from in here. I can't reach anything from in here. But you can walk these halls, and forge papers, and carry weapons, and nobody looks twice at you."

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"I heard enough. And the answer's still no—because you haven't asked me the right question yet." I leaned in, and the iron was cold against my forehead, and I didn't care. "Ask me properly, Emrys Pemberton. Ask me the way you should have asked me six years ago. Not what I'll risk for the mission. What I'll risk for *you*."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above us, a bell tolled the hour—one, deep and slow, rolling down through the bones of the palace.

He put his hands over mine on the bars. His fingers were cold and there was straw dust in his hair, and none of it mattered at all.

"Sera," he said. "Stay. Fight with me. Not for the realm, not for the prisoners, not for the crown that lied to you. For me. I'm asking badly and six years late, but I'm asking."

And I, who had faced raiders and floods and the worst winter in a generation without flinching, stood in a dungeon at one o'clock in the morning and discovered that yes was the hardest word I'd ever had to say.

"Yes," I said anyway. "Now tell me about this elder witch you mentioned—the one who supposedly knows the old passages. Because if we're doing this, we're doing it properly, and I'll need to meet her before I decide how much treason one night can hold."

His smile was tired and crooked and the same one from the riverbank, all those years ago.

"Visiting hours," he said, "are going to get interesting."
