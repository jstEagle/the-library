## 9. The Missing Page

There is a particular kind of arithmetic a prisoner learns, and it has nothing to do with copper.

I had been in the under-palace for eleven days when I finally stopped counting the hours and started counting other things instead. The number of paces my cell allowed end to end: fourteen. The number of times the guard on the night shift cleared his throat before falling asleep sitting up: three, always three. The number of days since I had seen Sera Vane's face by torchlight through these same bars: two, and each one of them had been longer than the six years that came before.

But the number that mattered—the one that kept me awake while the sleeping guard snored like a man with no conscience at all—was four.

Four words, scratched into the mortar beside my cot by some earlier tenant of this stone box. *She knew the unbinding.*

I had taken them for the ravings of a madman. Prisoners scratch all sorts of things into walls. Prayers, curses, names of people they'd wronged or loved or both. I'd read them a dozen times in my first week and dismissed them the way you dismiss a beggar's prophecy—politely, and without stopping.

Then, on the eleventh day, they rearranged themselves in my head, and I could not un-see what they became.

*She knew the unbinding.*

My master. Maren Corvane, who taught me to pull warmth from cold iron and light from dark water, who beat the arrogance out of me with a willow switch and put patience in its place. Whose cottage I had found burned to its foundations with her nowhere inside it. Whose grimoire I carried against my ribs right now, wrapped in oilcloth, because it was the last piece of her the fire hadn't eaten.

Whose final page was missing.

I sat up so fast the cot groaned. The guard snored on. And I did what I should have done at the ford, at the ambush, in every quiet hour Sera Vane had given me to think—I opened the book.

* * *

You have to understand something about Maren Corvane's grimoire before you can understand what I found, which is that the woman indexed her own paranoia. Every charm, every working, every recipe for wart-removal and weather-sense was catalogued in her cramped, slanted hand, cross-referenced twice over, because she used to say that a hedge-witch who can't find her own spells deserves whatever finds her first. Six years I'd known that book. I could navigate it blindfolded.

And it ended at page two hundred and forty-one.

Not "ended" the way books end, with a flourish and a signature. It ended the way a sentence ends when someone tears the paper mid-word. The last entry was a warding formula, complete, ordinary—and then the next leaf simply wasn't there. The stub of it remained in the binding, ragged, torn out long ago by a careful hand. Not ripped. *Removed.* By Maren herself, I'd always assumed, because Maren removed things she didn't want found, and I had assumed she'd hidden it somewhere safe, and I had assumed that someday she'd tell me where.

I had assumed a great many things about that woman. It turns out assuming is just grief wearing a practical coat.

Because here is what I knew now, here in the dark beneath the palace, with the hollow king's shadow crawling wrong across my memory: the thing on the throne eats magic. That was why the edict existed. That was why hunters swept the Thornfell passes and burned cottages and seized children who made candle flames lean toward them. Not superstition. Not piety. *Appetite.* The parasite wore the crown and reached through the edict like a man reaching through a fence to pick fruit, and every gifted soul dragged to the capital was fruit.

And the thing on the throne had signed an order—not the hunt captain's order, not the magistrate's order, but an order in the king's own hand—to keep me alive. Beneath the palace. Rather than fed to it like everyone else.

Why? I'd asked myself that question for eleven days and gotten nowhere, because I'd been asking the wrong question. The right question was not *why me*. The right question was *why does a thing that devours magic keep a hedge-mage alive in a cage instead of consuming him whole?*

The answer arrived all at once, the way terrible answers do, and it arrived wearing my master's handwriting.

It kept me alive because I might know where the page was.

No. Worse than that. It kept me alive because I might be able to *read* what the page contained, once it had the page itself. Because Maren Corvane had not been killed in that fire. She had been *taken*. Taken precisely because she was the one person in the realm who possessed the unbinding ritual—the ritual that could pry the parasite loose from its crown, from its king, from its six hundred years of borrowed life—and the thing that feared nothing else in this world feared exactly that.

It hadn't burned her cottage to destroy her. It had burned her cottage to make it look like destruction, the way you burn a letter after memorizing it. And then it had come looking for her student, because students inherit.

Gods above. Students inherit.

I pressed my forehead against the cool stone and laughed, quietly, the way you laugh when the alternative is screaming. Fourteen paces of cell, and I had spent eleven days pacing them like a man worried about his trial, when the trial had never been the point. I wasn't a prisoner. I was a *key*, locked in a box until the lockmaker needed me.

* * *

Let me tell you what guilt looks like when it finishes cooking.

For six years I had told myself a story about the night I ran from Maren's apprenticeship. In my version, I was young and frightened and the mountains were calling, and leaving was a wound I inflicted only on myself. A clean story. Selfish, maybe, but clean. Guilt with tidy edges.

The truth was uglier. The truth was that Maren had begun teaching me things in that last year that scared me—deep things, old things, things about the land's bones and the crown's shadow—and I hadn't wanted to know them. Knowing them meant obligations. Obligations meant danger. So I had run to the smuggler's passes and built myself a small invisible life of contraband and coin-counting, and I had told myself that staying unseen was wisdom rather than cowardice, and every year the story got smoother and the edges got softer until I genuinely believed it.

Meanwhile, my master stayed behind in that cottage with her paranoia and her index and her missing page, waiting for the day the crown's appetite reached Thornfell. Alone. Because her student had decided that knowing things was too expensive.

And when the day came—when the hunters came up the mountain road with their grey cloaks and their sealed dispatches—she had faced it without me. She had torn out the one page that mattered and hidden it, or sent it, or died getting it away from them, and I would never know which, because I hadn't been there. Because I'd been three valleys over, haggling over the price of smuggled brandy.

That was the guilt. Not the sharp kind, the kind that stabs and fades. The slow kind. The kind that sits down across from you at breakfast and stays for years.

But here is the thing about guilt that nobody tells you, the thing I learned on the twelfth day in a cell fourteen paces long: guilt is fuel. It burns dirty and it smokes and it stinks, but it burns. And there are worse things to burn than the knowledge that you failed someone who deserved better.

I had spent six years being invisible. Invisible men don't save anyone. Invisible men don't even try—they just calculate the odds and slip out the back of history while braver people bleed. Well. My invisibility was gone. The edict had stripped it from me, the hunters had chained it off me, the hollow king had signed it away with his stolen hand. There was no back door left to slip through.

Which meant, for the first time since I was nineteen years old, I had nothing left to lose except the people I'd already failed.

Resolve, it turns out, doesn't feel like courage. It feels like arithmetic. And my sums had finally come out even.

* * *

On the thirteenth day, Sera came to my cell again.

I heard her boots before I saw her—that measured hunter's stride, softer than the guards', deliberate—and I was on my feet before the torchlight found her face. Two days since the last visit. Two days of wondering whether she'd decide I wasn't worth the risk of visiting, whether duty had reasserted itself over whatever was cracking underneath it. I'd rehearsed a dozen openings and hated all of them.

She stopped outside the bars. Grey cloak, hood back, hair tied practically as ever. But her eyes were different. Something had happened.

"You look like hell," I said, because romance has never been my strong suit and terror makes me flippant.

"You look worse," she said. "You've been in the dark thirteen days."

"Twelve. I don't count the first one; I was unconscious."

"Thirteen." She said it with the flat certainty of a woman who had counted too, and I understood suddenly that we had both been doing the same arithmetic from opposite sides of the bars, and that this was somehow the most intimate thing that had ever passed between us.

"Why are you here, Captain?"

"Not as your captor." She glanced down the corridor, checking sightlines out of habit. "As the woman who falsified three execution records this week."

I went very still. Somewhere down the passage, the night guard snored on, oblivious, a man sleeping through the collapse of his own world.

"Sera—"

"Don't say my name like that. Not yet." Her voice was tight. "I need to tell you something, and then I need you to tell me if I'm a fool."

"I'm probably not the best judge. I'm a fool professionally."

"The dispatches." She pulled a folded paper from inside her cloak. "Sealed ones. Commander Aldric's orders come from the palace directly—from the king's privy seal. And Emrys, the prisoners aren't being executed. The ones with real gift. They're being moved. Down. To the lower vaults. There's a place under the east wing the hunters aren't cleared to enter. Prisoners go in and no records come out. No bodies. Nothing."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the cell.

"The feeding vaults," I said.

She stared at me. "What did you call them?"

"Nothing. Say the rest."

"There's more. One of the transfer orders—it's dated three weeks ago. Before the edict even went public. A prisoner transported from Thornfell province. An old woman. No name on the record, just a designation." She unfolded the paper with hands that weren't quite steady and turned it toward the torchlight. "*Asset Corvane. Retained intact.*"

The world tilted. I gripped the bars and the iron was warm under my hands, warm the way iron gets when something nearby is pulling heat out of everything around it, and I thought: *she's alive. She's down there. She's alive and they're keeping her intact the way you keep a jar intact, because what's inside is precious.*

"She's alive," I said aloud, and my voice cracked on it like ice on a pond.

"For now." Sera's jaw tightened. "Emrys, what is in those vaults? What are they doing to her?"

"Eating," I said. "Slowly. Keeping the meal fresh." And then, because she had earned it, because she had just set her neck on the block by showing me that paper: "Your king isn't a king. He's a shell. Something old lives in him, wears him, and it feeds on the gifted. The edict is a harvest. And my master—" I had to stop and start again. "My master knew how to kill it. Knew the unbinding. That's why she's down there instead of ash. It doesn't want her dead. It wants what's in her head, and it wants her body afterward. When the king wears out—"

"When the king wears out," Sera repeated, very quietly, "it needs a new one."

"A vessel that can hold it. Someone with enough gift to anchor it." I looked at her. "She's not a prisoner, Sera. She's the next crown."

Silence, except the snoring guard and the distant drip of water that every dungeon on earth seems contractually obligated to produce.

"That's why they kept you," she said slowly. "Not for your magic. For—"

"For the page. She tore the final page out of her grimoire before they took her. The unbinding ritual. It's the one thing in the world it fears, and it doesn't have it, and it thinks I know where it is." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "I don't. I've carried that book against my heart for weeks and I don't know where she hid it. But it doesn't know that."

Sera looked at me for a long moment, and I watched something shift behind her eyes—the moment when a soldier stops seeing a mission and starts seeing a war.

"Then we need to find it," she said, "before it decides to stop asking nicely."

"No," I said. "Before that, we need something else. Because there's a place under the east wing the hunters aren't cleared to enter, and there's an old woman down there who tore that page out herself, and I am done letting that woman face things alone." I met her eyes through the bars. "Sera. I need to get into the feeding vaults."

She didn't laugh. She didn't refuse. She stood there in the torchlight with a falsified execution record in her pocket and treason already in her mouth, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I had arrived at alone in the dark—except hers had one more term in it, and the term was me.

"You understand," she said finally, "that if we do this, there's no going back. Not to the company. Not to anything."

"I've been going back for six years," I said. "It's overrated."

Something almost like a smile touched her mouth and died young. "The witch-elder," she said. "The one they took last month. Maerlin. She's held in the cells below the records office—closer to us than the vaults. If anyone knows the old passages under this palace, it's her. She was court mage once, before the purge."

"Then tomorrow night—"

"Tomorrow night," she agreed. "And Emrys." She hesitated, and for a heartbeat she was nineteen again, standing on the mountain path above Maren's cottage, watching me decide whether to stay. "Whatever's down there. Whatever it did to her. You should prepare yourself."

"I know what it did to her."

"No." Her voice gentled into something I hadn't heard from her since the ford. "You know what it *wants* from her. You don't know yet what six weeks in those vaults looks like when it's finished eating. I've seen prisoners come up from the lower levels. I told myself they were mad from the dark." She folded the dispatch away. "Prepare yourself anyway."

She left. The torch guttered. And I sat back down on the cot with the grimoire against my chest and did the only thing left to do with a guilt that size: I turned it into a plan.

Fourteen paces, end to end. Tomorrow night, fifteen fewer between me and my master.

And somewhere beneath the east wing, in the dark, an old woman who had forgiven everyone but herself was waiting to teach her runaway student the last lesson she'd ever written—

if I could reach her before the crown did.
