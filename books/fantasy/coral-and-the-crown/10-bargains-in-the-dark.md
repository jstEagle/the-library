## 10. Bargains in the Dark

### Emrys

The witch-elder smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and she looked at me the way a butcher looks at a carcass that's been left out too long.

"You're the one they pulled off the river road," she said. Her voice was gravel poured slow. "The hedge-witch's boy. Maerlin's."

I hadn't heard my master's name spoken aloud since her cottage burned. It went through me like a splinter driven under the nail.

"You knew her," I said.

"I trained her." She shifted against the wall of her cell, chains clinking with the weary patience of someone who had worn them long enough to stop resenting the weight and start regarding it as furniture. "Sit down, boy. You're blocking what little light there is, and my eyes are sixty years past caring about your comfort."

Her name, when I finally learned it, was Odile Vasska, though everyone who'd ever feared her had called her the Crow of Cindermarsh. She'd been imprisoned beneath the palace for eleven years—long enough to watch three jailers die of old age, long enough that the guards had stopped checking her bindings because, as far as anyone could tell, whatever gift she'd once had had guttered out like a candle in a draught.

As far as anyone could tell. That was the phrase I kept turning over, because Sera had said it first, and Sera did not say things without reason.

She'd come to my cell two nights after the Hollow King offered me servitude. She came at the changing of the watch, when the torches burned low and the guards' attention wandered toward bread and ale. She didn't knock. Prisoners' cells don't get knocked on; they get opened, abruptly, by people who want you to know they can open them whenever they like. But this time the door swung wide and stayed wide, and Sera stood in it holding a clay lamp, and she said, "We need to talk. And not here."

*We.* I noticed the word. I was learning to notice her words the way a man learns to notice weather—the small shifts in pressure before the storm, the way certain clouds mean rain and certain silences mean everything else.

"Talk where?" I asked.

"There's an empty cistern two levels down. The pipes failed forty years ago and nobody bothered to repair what nobody uses." She set the lamp on the floor between us, careful, deliberate, like a woman laying down a weapon she intended to pick back up. "Odile Vasska is down there. Has been for years. They moved her when the old cells flooded."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you spent ten minutes in front of the king and came out looking like a man who'd seen his own funeral." Her jaw tightened. "Because I've read the sealed dispatches, Emrys. I told you that. And because if we're going to do something stupid, we should do it properly, with someone who knows how the palace is built—which none of us do, except possibly a woman who's been living inside its bones for eleven years."

*We're going to do something stupid.* I could have kissed her. Instead I said, "You realize that's treason twice over now."

"I realize I stopped counting after the first time." She picked up the lamp again. "Coming?"

The cistern was exactly as advertised: vast, dry, useless, echoing. Our voices came back to us doubled and trebled, so that every whisper sounded like a conspiracy of dozens. Odile Vasska sat on a stone shelf near the far wall, wrapped in a blanket that might once have been grey and was now the color of surrender. She watched us approach with the unhurried attention of a spider who has already decided the fly is interesting enough to keep.

"So," she said. "The captain and the hedge-boy. I wondered which of you would come first. My money was on the captain—hunters always break before witches do. Guilt is a faster solvent than fear."

"That's flattering," Sera said flatly.

"It wasn't meant to be." The old woman turned to me. "Show me your hands."

I showed her my hands. She took them in hers—stronger than I expected—and turned them over, reading the calluses, the burn scars, the faint silver tracery along my veins that only shows in people whose gift runs close to the skin.

"Hedge-work," she pronounced. "Small magic. Charms, wards, finding-things, mending. Nothing that throws fire." A pause. "Good. Fire draws attention, and attention down here gets people fed to the thing upstairs."

"The parasite."

"The *tenant*," she corrected. "It's been paying no rent for two hundred years, but it's still a tenant. The crown is its leasehold. Every king since Aldric the Fearful has been a door it props open and walks through." She released my hands. "Your master figured out how to evict it. That's why she's alive, boy, and don't look so surprised—you knew that already, or you wouldn't be standing in a dry cistern at midnight talking to an old woman in chains."

"I know the unbinding needs a page from her grimoire," I said. "A page I never saw."

"You'll need more than a page. You'll need a way out of this palace, a way back into it, and a way to survive what comes after." She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught her eyes, which were pale as frost on a window. "Which brings me to why you're both really here. You want my passages."

Sera said, "Do you have them?"

"Girl, I helped build half of them. Before your grandmother was born, I walked these undercrofts when they were a working necropolis, and I mapped every bolt-hole the masons left behind because masons always leave bolt-holes—it's professional pride." She tapped the stone shelf with one knuckle. "There are nine ways out of the undercity that the palace guard doesn't know. Two are flooded. One is full of something I don't recommend meeting. Six remain."

"And in exchange?" I asked, because nothing in this palace was free, least of all from a woman who'd survived eleven years of captivity by understanding exactly what everything cost.

"In exchange," Odile said, "you get me out of this hole before they decide I'm useful to the tenant again. I'm old, not dead, and my gift isn't gone—it's sleeping. Buried it myself when they took me, the way you bury a seed. There are things I can do awake that no one in this building suspects." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Also, I want to see it die. The tenant. Call it a professional interest. I've been watching it eat this kingdom for two centuries from the inside of a cell, and I would like very much to be present at the eviction."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Then: "The escape plan requires me to betray my own company."

It wasn't a question, but Odile answered it anyway. "Yes. Someone has to open the doors, captain, and the doors answer to hunters. Unless you've thought of another way—a hedge-boy's charms against iron locks, perhaps? I hear he's very good with knots."

"I am good with knots," I said, "but I can't charm a lock that's warded against me specifically. They keyed the restraints to my gift. Whoever designed them knew hedge-work."

"The commander knows hedge-work," Sera said quietly. "He studied under Maerlin too, years before either of us was born. He's the one who wrote the edict's enforcement protocols." She looked at me, and something in her face had gone hard and tired. "Emrys. If we do this—if I do this—there's no walking it back. Half my company will follow me. Half will follow him. And the half that follows him will be under orders to kill the half that doesn't."

"Then we make sure the halves are uneven," Odile said briskly. "In our favor. Loyalty is arithmetic, captain, same as anything else. You've served with these people for years. You know which ones flinch at burning children and which ones volunteer for it. Start there."

And that was the moment I understood what Sera had actually come to say, underneath all the logistics. She stood there in the lamplight with her company's names stacked in her head like cordwood, sorting them into the saved and the damned, and her hands were steady, and her voice was steady, and only her eyes were doing what her voice refused to—asking me whether I understood what she was about to spend.

"I understand," I said.

"You don't," she said. "But thank you for lying to me. It's the polite thing to do."

### Sera

My mother used to say that a soldier's worst day is not the day she dies. It's the day she realizes the uniform fits worse than it used to.

I thought about that in the cistern, listening to Odile Vasska count exits the way other people count coins, and I thought about it again afterward, walking back through the undercrofts alone because sending Emrys ahead of me would have looked like exactly what it was. My boots echoed. Everything down here echoes. The palace is mostly hollow—that's the joke of it, the joke nobody laughs at. A kingdom's seat, built on emptiness, ruled by something that eats the fullness out of people.

I had made lists. That's what I did instead of sleeping. Drenholt, who cried after his first execution and volunteered for the next one, because crying is easier if you decide the crying was weakness. Pell and Ossa, married six months, who would follow each other anywhere, including out. Marrec, my sergeant, twenty years of service, who once told me over bad wine that he joined the hunt because it was the only posting that came with a roof, and who had gone quieter every week since the edict. Quiet, in Marrec, was a door opening.

Eleven names I trusted. Nine I didn't. Four I genuinely couldn't guess, which was the worst category, because the ones you can't guess are the ones who surprise you in both directions.

The plan, as it stood, was simple in the way that plans built over a dry cistern at midnight are simple: they work beautifully until they meet reality. We would move Emrys out through passage four—an old grain chute beneath the east wing, disused, unwalled, opening into a tanner's yard beyond the palace grounds. From there, the marsh road south, then Thornfell, then Maerlin, assuming Maerlin was alive to be found. Odile would come with us; her "death" would be reported as natural, which even the palace clerks would believe, given her age. I would open the necessary doors during the third watch, when the rotation put two of my trusted eleven on the undercroft gate.

And then, in roughly a fortnight, Commander Hale would discover the empty cell, and the discovery would point at me like an arrow, and the company I had led for six years would be ordered to bring me in. Dead, preferably. The dispatches were clear on preference.

"You could not do it," Emrys had said, in the cistern, when I laid that part out. "You could stay clean. Let me find another way out."

"With what?" Odile had asked, before I could. "Good intentions? The boy's gift is keyed against. Mine is buried. Captain, you are the only person in this conversation who can currently walk through a door without it being opened for her. Cleanliness is a luxury of people who aren't needed."

I had hated her a little, then. I hated her correctly, the way you hate a surgeon.

Now, walking the long curve back toward the barracks, I passed the shrine alcove near the old armory—the one where hunters left tokens before a hard ride. I'd left a token there once, six years ago, before my first execution detail. A copper ring my mother gave me. I'd told myself I was asking for steadiness. What I'd actually been doing was asking permission to become the kind of person who could stand in a square and watch an old woman burn, and go home to supper.

The ring was still there. Or a ring was still there; copper greens in the dark, and mine had been bright. I didn't stop to check. Checking felt like superstition, and I had decided, somewhere between the sealed dispatches and the cistern, that I was done asking dead stone for permission.

Emrys was waiting in my office—not my office, officially; officially it was a records room with a desk in it, which is what every captain's office is. He'd let himself in with a knot-charm on the latch, which should have infuriated me and instead made me feel, absurdly, safer. Some habits of his hadn't changed. He'd always been able to make a locked thing feel like an invitation.

"You're supposed to be in your cell," I said.

"I'm supposed to be in a lot of places." He was sitting on the desk, which was also a habit, swinging one foot. In the lamplight I could see the exhaustion under his eyes, the bruised look of a man who'd spent two days being offered survival by a monster. "I wanted to say it while it was still just words. Before it's blood."

"Say what?"

"What you're doing. For me. For—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the throne, the city, the whole rotten arrangement, "—for all of it. I know what it costs. I did the arithmetic too, Sera. Eleven names. I saw your face while you counted."

"My face doesn't count out loud."

"No," he agreed. "But I've had six years to learn it anyway, and apparently the lesson stuck."

I should have sent him back to his cell. There was a version of me—the one who'd left the copper ring at the shrine—who would have sent him back, would have kept the walls up and the ledger clean and the uniform fitting. That version of me had died somewhere on the river road, or in the ambush, or the night I falsified my first record. I honestly couldn't point to the moment. Death by increments leaves no single wound to name.

"If this goes wrong," I said, "they won't just kill you. They'll feed you to it. Slowly. It likes the gifted slow—I've read the feeding schedules, Emrys. They're filed under *provisions*."

"Then we make sure it doesn't go wrong."

"That's not a plan. That's a prayer with better posture."

He laughed—quietly, because sound carried, and because he was learning the palace's acoustics faster than I'd learned them in six years. Then he slid off the desk and stood close, closer than the room required, and said, "Six years ago I ran from an apprenticeship because I was afraid of what my gift would make me. Now I'm running toward the same thing, and the difference is you're counting the cost beside me instead of across from me. I needed you to hear that before the doors start opening. Whatever happens to the eleven names, and the nine, and the four—we chose this. Both of us. Nobody dragged anybody."

"Somebody dragged somebody," I said. "You dragged me. Off a river ford, in chains, six weeks ago. I've just decided to walk the rest of the way on my own feet."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," he said, "and I include the poetry Maerlin made me memorize."

"Go back to your cell, Emrys."

"Yes, captain."

"And Emrys." He paused at the door. "Third watch, four nights from now. Don't be late. I'm only betraying my entire life once, and I'd hate to waste it."

He grinned, and for one second he was nineteen again, all elbows and certainty, and then the door closed and he was a fugitive in a palace that ate people, and I was alone with a lamp and a list of names I'd have to live with or die beside.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and did the arithmetic one more time. Eleven. It held.

### Emrys

Four nights later, the third watch began with a bell tolling wrong.

Not the hour bell—one of the inner bells, the ones that only rang for fire or succession or death in the royal line. It tolled twice, stopped, tolled again, a stutter like a heart missing beats, and every hunter in the undercroft gatehouse looked up at once.

Sera appeared at my cell door with the keys already in her hand and an expression I'd last seen on her face during the ambush on the marsh road: calm the way a drawn bow is calm.

"Change of schedule," she said. "Passage four is compromised. We're going through seven."

"Seven's the flooded one."

"Was. Odile unburied her gift yesterday." Something flickered across her face that might have been awe wearing a soldier's straight posture. "She drained the channel in an afternoon, Emrys. Eleven years she sat in that cell pretending to be finished, and she drained a river channel in an afternoon. Remind me never to lie to that woman."

Down the corridor, Odile Vasska was already waiting at the mouth of the passage, blanket abandoned, chains struck off—or rather, unlocked, because it turned out the locks had never been the obstacle; the *belief* in the locks had been, hers and everyone else's. She looked older without the blanket and infinitely more dangerous.

"Well," she said, when Sera and I reached her. "The tenant knows something's moving. That's what the bell means. It's waking the hunt." She smiled her unfriendly smile. "So we'd best be quick, children. The city above us is about to learn what its king really is—and I, for one, intend to be standing in the street when it finds out."

Behind us, far up in the palace's throat, the bell tolled again—and this time it didn't stop.
