## 11. The Breaking of the Hunt

I had rehearsed treason in my head for eleven days before I committed it, which is either a long time or no time at all depending on whether you believe conscience is a muscle or a fuse.

Mine turned out to be a fuse. Slow-burning, yes, but burning the whole while, and on the morning it finally caught, there was nothing left to do but stand back and watch my whole life go up in a very orderly, very military fashion.

It began with a roster.

Commander Aldous Reeve kept the execution ledgers himself—locked in a lacquered box he carried like a second heart—and every evening he read out the names of prisoners to be "processed" at dawn. *Processed.* He'd chosen the word carefully. Words are how men like Reeve sleep at night. Not *killed*, not *burned*, not even the honest old word, *executed*. Processed, like grain. Like ore. Like something that comes out the other end of a machine improved by the experience.

On the eleventh day, the roster had forty-one names on it. Forty-one. We had taken perhaps sixty prisoners since the edict came down from the capital, and I had told myself, in the way soldiers tell themselves things, that most of them were being held for questioning. That the questioning was real. That somewhere above my pay grade, someone was sorting the dangerous from the merely unlucky, and that the merely unlucky would walk free when the fires died down.

Forty-one names said otherwise. Forty-one names said that the sorting had already happened, and what it sorted for was ash.

I copied them. All of them. In a hand so steady it frightened me, because I knew whose steadiness it was—it was the same steadiness I'd used to write casualty reports after Skellum's Ford, the year the company lost nine good hunters and I learned that grief, done properly, looks exactly like neat penmanship.

Then I did the thing you're not supposed to do with a list of condemned people. I read it aloud to myself, alone, in the tack room behind the stables, and I let myself hear it.

*Marisol Tanner, hedge-charmer, age 61. Charges: weather-sense.*

Weather-sense. She could feel rain coming in her knees. Sixty-one years old and her crime was knowing when to bring the harvest in.

*Bren Odo, farrier's son, age 14. Charges: unlicensed charm (protective).*

Fourteen. A protective charm. The boy had stitched a sigil into his little sister's cloak hem so she wouldn't catch fever on the winter road, and for this we were going to burn him in the market square between the fishmonger's stall and the fountain where children threw copper coins for luck.

I want to tell you I stood up right then and walked to Reeve's tent and put a knife through the ledger. I want to tell you conscience works like that, clean and cinematic, one bright moment and done.

What actually happened was I sat in the straw until my legs went numb, and then I walked past his tent twice without going in, because walking in meant it started, and starting meant there was no version of the world afterward in which I got to be Captain Sera Vane of the King's Hunt anymore. Everything I had built since I was sixteen—the rank, the grey cloak, the reputation for being the only officer in the eastern companies who'd never lost a prisoner to a lynch mob—all of it lived inside that tent, and I stood outside it like a woman deciding whether to burn down her own house with herself still in it.

In the end I went in because of Emrys Pemberton. Because of what he'd said to me through the bars of his cell three nights prior, which I will get to, because it matters, and because I have discovered that the moments that break you never announce themselves as breaking points. They announce themselves as conversations.

* * *

The cells beneath the garrison were old wine vaults once, back when this town made anything besides orphans. Damp stone, iron rings, straw that hadn't been changed since the last war. Emrys lay chained in the third one, and I had been coming to him at night the way you visit a grave—not because it helps, but because not visiting feels worse.

He always heard me coming. Hedge-witch senses, he called them, though his gift was mostly bound now, leashed with hunter's iron. He'd hear my boots on the stairs and be sitting up before I reached the landing, waiting, patient as a cat that has decided the house belongs to it and everything else is furniture.

That night he said: "You've been reading the dispatches."

Not a question. I hadn't told him. I'd told no one. But he'd watched me come down those stairs for two weeks with a new weight settling onto me gram by gram, the way silt settles on a drowned thing, and he'd done the arithmetic. Smuggler's arithmetic. Risk weighed against risk, coin against coin. He'd spent six years doing sums like that in mountain passes, and apparently the skill transfers.

"I have," I admitted.

"And?"

"And they don't say what the seal says they say." I sat down across the bars from him, close enough that our knees nearly touched through the iron. "The official line is containment. Dangerous gifteds, secured for trial. But there's a sealed annex—Reeve's copy, under triple lock—that reclassifies every prisoner as *material*. Feedstock. There's a requisition schedule. There are quotas."

Emrys closed his eyes. Not in surprise. In confirmation. You could see him fitting the piece into the picture he'd been building since the palace, since the throne room, since the moment he'd looked up at the king and seen the shadow move wrong.

"The parasite," he said quietly. "It doesn't just hunt magic. It eats it. And the gifted aren't prisoners to it—they're provisions."

"Yes."

"Forty-one names."

"Forty-one names," I said, "and a delivery date."

He was silent for a long moment. Then he said the thing. The conversation-breaking thing. He didn't raise his voice; he never raised his voice; that was somehow worse. He leaned his forehead briefly against the bar, the way a man leans against a door he can't open, and he said:

"You know what happens to the ones who process them, after. When the quotas are met and the vaults are full. There's no pension for people who know where the bodies are buried. There's a pit, and the people who dug it."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I watched you do the sums in your head while I said it. I watched you arrive at the number. And the number terrified you less than the other sum—the one where you do nothing, and live, and have to be a person who did nothing for the next fifty years." He drew back and looked at me straight on. "Sera. One of those futures has you in it. Choose that one."

God help me, I laughed. A short, ugly laugh, the kind that escapes rather than expresses. "You sound like my mother."

"She sounds like she was right about you."

"My mother wanted me to be a weaver. She said soldiering would either kill me or make me cruel, and she'd accept either outcome over watching me become *indecisive*, which she considered the one unforgivable state of a human being." I wrapped my hands around the bars, an idiot gesture, intimate and useless. "She'd be proud of tonight, probably. She always did like a clean decision."

"That wasn't a compliment."

"No," I said. "It was a eulogy. For Captain Vane. She dies tonight, one way or the other. Either Reeve breaks her, or she breaks first." I stood up. "Get some sleep, smuggler. Tomorrow I'm going to need you to be the most annoying man in Thornmark."

He grinned in the dark. It was a good grin. It was the grin of a boy I'd known on the river road ten years ago, before the edicts, before the crown rotted, back when the worst thing either of us had to plan around was whether his master would notice he'd traded her rosemary for my mother's thread.

"Annoying," he promised, "is the one thing I've never had to fake."

* * *

Treason, it turns out, is mostly logistics.

You imagine it as passion—torches, speeches, a banner thrown down and stepped on. What it actually is, at least the way I did it, is a sequence of small favors called in carefully, in order, each one too small to alarm anyone until the sum of them becomes unstoppable.

First, the quartermaster. Old Hesk, twenty-two years in the company, a man who'd stopped believing in anything except his ledgers sometime during the last reign. I didn't preach at him. I showed him the requisition schedule—the one where the *material* was scheduled for transport to the capital on wagons marked GRAIN, and his own signature was forged at the bottom authorizing the transfer. Hesk stared at the forgery for a long time. Men like Hesk can survive almost any horror, but not a forged signature. It's a desecration of the only sacrament they have.

He didn't say yes. He said, "The armory inventory will show six crates of crossbow bolts missing by morning, and I will be extremely annoyed about it, and I will not look in the direction of whoever took them." Which, in quartermaster, is a declaration of war on your behalf.

Second, the sergeants. There were four I trusted, and of the four, two said yes immediately—one because of Bren Odo, whose father shod his horses and wept in his stable yard when the boy was taken, and one because of Marisol Tanner, who had set his daughter's broken arm during the flood year and charged her nothing but a story. The third sergeant said maybe, which in military terms means no with extra steps, and I left him alone. The fourth, Sergeant Corliss, said no outright, reported nothing, and asked me to tell his wife he was sorry if it came to blood. That's the thing about the company nobody writes songs about: half of loyalty is just love wearing armor, and love doesn't always point the direction the crown tells it to.

Third, and hardest: the young ones. The green cloaks, nineteen, twenty years old, who'd joined the Hunt because it was steady bread and a grey cloak earned respect in towns where their fathers had been drunks. They weren't cruel. Most of them weren't anything yet—they were waiting for the world to tell them what they were. I gathered six of them in the hay loft at dusk, and I didn't give them a speech. Speeches bounce off the young like rain off wax. I gave them the roster instead. Let them read the names themselves. Let them find the ages column and trace down it with one finger, the way you trace anything you don't want to believe.

Private Ellery Vance—nineteen herself, freckled, forever losing her helmet strap—read it twice and then handed it back with shaking hands and said, "Captain. Say it plainly. Are we burning these people tomorrow?"

And I said, plainly, "No. We are not."

And she said, "Then what are we doing?"—and I watched her understand it in real time, the whole architecture of it rising up in front of her like a building she'd been living in without ever looking at. "We're stopping it," she said finally. Not asked. Said. As if the answer had assembled itself inside her while I stood there being deliberately quiet.

Good girl. That's the whole secret of command, if anyone asks you: you don't put courage into people. You just clear away the noise long enough for them to find the courage that was already packed in their kit at birth, and then you try not to get them killed while they use it.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and cold over the market square, the kind of light that makes everything look already dead.

Reeve had the pyres stacked by first bell—three of them, oak and pitchpine, soaked in oil, arranged with the geometric precision he brought to everything, because Commander Aldous Reeve believed cruelty was acceptable but disorder was not. The prisoners had been brought out in chains at midnight and made to wait beside their own deaths for six hours, which was its own punishment, and which I understood now was not incidental. The waiting was the feedstock's tenderizing. Fear, I had learned from the sealed annexes, has a flavor the parasite preferred. They weren't burning people. They were marinating them.

I stood at the head of my company in full kit, grey cloak, polished gorget, the whole costume, and I watched Reeve take the reviewing stand with his ledger under his arm, and I felt the strangest sensation of my life, which was calm. Not peace. Calm—the flat, glassy stillness of water the instant before the rock hits it.

At the third bell, Reeve opened the ledger and began reading names.

He got through four of them.

Because at the fourth name—Bren Odo, fourteen, protective charm—I stepped forward off the line, one pace, into the space between the condemned and the fire, and I said, loudly enough for the square, for the crowd already gathering along the barricades, for the man himself:

"Captain Vane, King's Hunt, refuses this order."

Reeve's face did something complicated. Shock first—he genuinely had not imagined this, had filed me under *reliable* years ago and never revisited the file—then anger, then, horribly, a flicker of negotiation, because Reeve was a creature of systems and systems always assume the malfunction can be talked back into place. "Captain," he said, smooth as oiled leather, "you will return to your position. This is a lawful execution under sealed writ."

"It is a lawful execution under a sealed writ I have read," I said, "and the writ does not say execute. It says *deliver*. To the capital. Alive. Whatever burns today burns against the king's own paper, and everyone in this square is welcome to check my copy against the garrison seal."

There it was. The lie I'd spent three nights polishing. The writ did not, in fact, say deliver alive—the annex said material—but Reeve couldn't prove that in the square, in the moment, in front of four hundred witnesses and a crowd that had begun, visibly, audibly, to lean toward me. Mothers with children on their shoulders. The fishmonger. The priest of the low chapel, who had been forbidden to object and objected anyway with his presence alone, standing at the barricade in his plain brown robe like a nail that refused to be hammered down.

Reeve ordered my arrest. My own company—my company—was ordered to arrest me.

And this was the moment. The hinge. The half-second in which treason either becomes a mob scene or becomes a fact. Ellery Vance was nearest to me, and I saw her hand go to her shackles—standard issue, for prisoner transport—and I saw her hesitate, and I did the only thing left in the kit, which was to trust her out loud, in front of everyone.

"Ellery," I said. "The bolt crate."

She unlocked my wrists. Not hers. Mine. She walked up to her captain in front of the entire company and the entire town and she took the irons off me and put them on the ground between us like a dropped gauntlet, and the sound of them hitting the cobbles was, I swear on whatever is left to swear on, louder than the bells.

After that it happened fast, and fast is ugly.

Half the company stepped to me—Hesk's missing bolts appeared in their hands, loaded, leveled, not aimed at anyone yet, which is the most eloquent thing a drawn crossbow can say. The other half hesitated, and hesitation in a square like that curdles quickly; Sergeant Dane, the one who'd said no outright, shouted the counter-order, and for about ninety seconds there were two captains and one company and every man in grey standing in the exact spot where his whole future was being decided for him by which shout he believed.

Then Reeve made his mistake. He always was going to make a mistake; men who think cruelty is orderly always do. He ordered the archers on the south wall to loose into the crowd.

Into the crowd. Mothers. Children. The priest. Four hundred civilians behind a waist-high barricade, and Aldous Reeve, staring down a mutiny he could no longer talk his way out of, decided the solution was arrows.

Two loosed. Two. Before the rest of the wall company lowered their bows—which they did, all of them, to a man, which I will remember until I die, because those were boys I'd drilled and scolded and docked pay, and when it mattered they chose. But two arrows is enough. One took a miller's apprentice through the shoulder. The other—

The other hit Marisol Tanner.

Sixty-one years old. Weather-sense. She'd been standing at the front of the prisoners because she'd spent her whole life walking toward weather, toward trouble, toward whatever needed her knees and her knowledge, and she went down on the oil-soaked boards with an arrow through her chest, and the square went from leaning to breaking, the way a dam goes from seeping to gone.

I won't dress up what happened next. Treason, made real, is blood, and I ordered it. When Reeve's guard moved to secure the pyres—to finish what the arrows started—I gave the order I had hoped never to give: *loose.* Eleven bolts. Nine guards down, three of them dead before they finished falling, boys I'd eaten breakfast with, and I stood there in my grey cloak and watched them fall and felt the cost of conscience arrive all at once, the way a debt arrives, the whole sum, no installments, no grace period.

Conscience, it turns out, is not the voice that says *this is wrong.* Everyone has that voice. Conscience is the voice that says *wrong, and here is what it will cost, and pay anyway,* and it speaks in the exact tone of a debt collector, and it does not care that you are trembling.

Reeve himself I took alive. Barely. Dane fought like a man possessed—possessed, I thought later, by the terrible freedom of having already chosen wrong—and it took four of us and a broken crossbow stock to bring him down, and when it was done he knelt in the blood of his own guards and looked up at me with pure, uncomprehending hatred, and said the sentence I expect to hear again at his trial, and again in my dreams, for the rest of my life:

"You were the best of us. That's what makes you the worst."

* * *

By noon, the bells were ringing.

Not the garrison bells—we held those. The cathedral bells, rung by the brown-robed priest and two acolytes who'd clearly been waiting years for permission, ringing the alarm pattern, the old pattern, the one every soul in Thornmark knew from plague years and flood years: fast-slow-fast, fast-slow-fast, *the city is in danger, gather, gather.*

Except this time the danger was the crown itself, and the gathering was not to fight it but to witness it. By midday the square held two thousand people, and the market stalls had become a field hospital for the wounded of both sides, because that's what markets are for, underneath everything—keeping people alive through the bad seasons, whichever way the bad season comes.

We freed the prisoners. All of them. Forty-one names minus one: Marisol Tanner died on the boards at sunrise, and we buried her at dusk in the low chapel's ground, which technically violates six statutes and morally honors all of them. Her grave marker says *weather-sense* on it, carved by Bren Odo's father, who cried the whole time and carved beautifully anyway, because grief done properly still looks like fine work. I'm told that's a lesson. I keep forgetting to feel taught.

The company split the way I'd known it would, the way it splits in every civil wound: half stayed with Reeve's officers under Sergeant Dane, sullen and armed and waiting for orders from the capital that would make them heroes again; half came with me, thirty-one hunters in grey who were traitors now, officially, permanently, their names entered in a ledger somewhere with a price attached. Ellery Vance stood in the ranks of the defectors with her helmet strap finally buckled properly, as if she'd decided that if she was going to be hanged for a wolf she'd at least stop looking like a stray dog.

And Emrys Pemberton walked out of his cell at sundown, unchained, into a courtyard full of armed men who had burned his kind for a season, and the first thing he did—the very first—was walk down the line of defectors and thank each one of them by name. He knew their names. Of course he did. He'd memorized them through the floor of his cell, from footsteps and voices, smuggler's craft applied to prison stone, and when he reached Ellery and said "Vance—you'll do," she laughed for the first time in a week, and the sound of it did more to bind that company together than any oath I could have administered.

We had a fortress garrison to abandon, a capital to reach, a king-shaped parasite to starve, and roughly four days before word of the mutiny arrived in the throne room and the full Hunt came north for our heads.

I stood on the garrison wall that night, looking south along the dark road, and Emrys climbed up beside me—climbed, mind you, a man still weak from three weeks underground, complaining cheerfully about the ladder the entire way—and he said, "So. How does it feel? Being the most wanted woman in Thornmark?"

"Wanted," I said, testing the word. "Is that what this is."

"Best available term. The crown wants you dead. I want—" he paused, and I felt him decide, deliberately, to say it, the way you decide to step off a ledge, "—I want you. Those are different wants. Only one of them comes with a bounty."

Below us, the bells were still ringing. Fast-slow-fast. The city wouldn't stop; the priest had ordered it rung until further notice, and further notice, he said, would be a long time coming. The sound rolled out over the rooftops and down into the valleys, and somewhere out in that darkness, I knew, riders were already moving—Reeve's loyalists, Dane's messengers, the sealed dispatches flying south like arrows of their own, carrying my name and my crime to the thing that wore the king.

"They'll come," I said. "The whole Hunt. Every company that didn't break."

"Mm." He was quiet a moment. Then: "Then we'd better go collect the allies who don't carry crossbows. There's a witch-elder in the palace crypts who owes me a favor, a child in the walls who's the only person that thing can't wear, and a ritual that needs every hour we can steal." He looked at me sideways, and the grin came back, the river-road grin, the one from before. "Also, captain, I'm told you're now guilty of high treason, arson-adjacent conduct, and the theft of one very large amount of conscience. If we're doing crimes, we should do the complete set."

I laughed—really laughed, for the first time since the arrow found Marisol Tanner—and the laugh hurt, the way the first breath after a cracked rib hurts, which is to say it hurt because something underneath was trying to heal.

Fast-slow-fast, rang the bells. Gather, gather.

"All right," I said to the dark country below, to the road, to the riders already on it, to the hollow thing on its hollow throne. "Gather, then. Come see what conscience costs."

I turned south, and my company turned with me.
