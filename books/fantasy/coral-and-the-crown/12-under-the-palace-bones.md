## 12. Under the Palace Bones

The crypts beneath the palace had no name that anyone living remembered, which was the first clue they were worth knowing about.

"Everything in this city has a name," I said, crouching at the mouth of the passage while Sera held a shuttered lantern and the witch-elder Maren held, as usual, an opinion. "Streets, gutters, the rats have names. The ratcatchers name their dogs after the rats. But this—" I ran my hand along the wall, where old mortar crumbled like stale bread, "—this is just *under*."

"That's because nobody who named it wanted to be found," Maren said. She was ninety if she was a day, with hands like knotted rope and the particular patience of someone who had outlived every person who'd ever annoyed her. "Names are hooks, boy. You hang a name on a thing, you can pull it back out of the dark whenever you like. Some doors you leave unnamed so they stay shut."

"And yet here we are, opening it."

"Yes." She smiled without warmth. "I've never been much for other people's caution."

The passage behind us still echoed with the last hour's work: three dead hunters who hadn't defected fast enough, a locked gate that Wren—the youngest of Sera's turncoats—had picked with hands that shook only after the lock surrendered, and the long grey slide of stairs going down into stone that predated the palace above it. That was the thing nobody told you about capitals. They built on top of themselves, century over century, like a child stacking blocks without ever clearing the table. The palace of Aldermere sat on the bones of three older palaces, and those sat on something older still, and somewhere down there, under all that accumulated majesty, was the answer to a question I'd been carrying since the throne room.

*What* was wearing the king?

We went in.

* * *

You learn things about magic growing up in Thornfell that you can't learn from books, mostly because the people who write books have never had to hide what they are from a neighbor who'd sell them for a bounty and a pat on the head. Hedge-magic is small magic. It has to be. A charm to keep milk sweet another day. A whisper to make a horse calm on a bad bridge. A trick of shadow that turns one man into two men, briefly, when soldiers come up the pass. Small magics, cheap magics, magics that cost you a headache and a nosebleed instead of your soul.

But every hedge-witch knows the deep rules anyway, the way a farmer knows weather he's never seen. And the first deep rule is this: *magic doesn't lie*. People lie. Kings lie. Magic just *is*, and it leaves traces the way water leaves marks on stone, and if you're quiet enough and desperate enough, you can read them.

The crypts were shouting.

I felt it before we were twenty paces in—a pressure against the skin, like walking into a room where someone had been crying for a very long time. Old grief soaked into the walls. Old fear. And underneath it, threaded through everything like wire through meat, something else. Something cold and patient and *hungry*, the residue of a feeding that had gone on so long the stone itself had learned the shape of it.

Sera saw me stop. "Emrys?"

"It's here," I said. "Not the thing itself. But its... leavings. Its history. This whole place is marinated in it."

"How far?"

"Deeper. Down is where it started, or I'm a mule's uncle."

Maren snorted. "You may well be. Keep walking."

The passage widened as it descended, which was wrong—crypts narrow as they go down, that's how they're built, cheaper stone and fewer mourners—but this opened like a throat. The walls changed too. Rough-hewn blocks gave way to dressed granite, then to something smoother, darker, veined with a pale mineral I didn't recognize until I touched it and jerked my hand back.

Coral. Fossilized coral, set into the palace foundations, a hundred feet underground, fifty miles from any sea.

"Coral," Sera said flatly. "Under a mountain kingdom."

"The sea was here once," Maren said. "Before the mountains. Before the kingdoms. The world is older than its rulers, Captain, though you'd never know it from the way they talk." She lifted her chin toward the dark ahead. "Which is rather the point of tonight."

Wren carried the second lantern now, and I noticed she'd drifted close to Sera—not touching, but orbiting, the way frightened soldiers do around whoever seems least frightened. There were six of us left from the breaking of the hunt: Sera, Wren, big Tammas with his arm bound and his jaw set, the twins Olla and Breck who hadn't spoken since the bells, and me. Plus Maren, who had walked out of her cell in the undercrofts like a woman leaving a boring dinner party, and who scared me considerably more than the parasite did, though I kept that to myself.

The passage opened into a hall.

Not a crypt hall. Crypts hold coffins, niches, the tidy architecture of mourning. This was a *chamber*—vast, round, its ceiling lost in darkness above our lantern-light—and its floor was covered in writing.

I stood at the edge of it for a long moment before I understood what I was looking at, because my mind kept trying to sort the script into categories it knew: royal decrees, prayer-cycles, genealogies. It wasn't any of those. It was a single text, repeated, ring after ring after ring, spiraling inward toward the center of the floor like the year-rings of a felled tree. The same words, carved over and over, generation after generation, each ring in a different hand, some crude, some elegant, the oldest nearly worn to nothing.

"A binding," Maren said quietly. "Or the record of one. Read the innermost ring, hedge-mage. The rest are renewals."

I crossed the floor carefully, stepping between the letters as if they might wake—which, given what I could feel rising off them, they might. Sera followed. At the center, worn shallow by centuries of feet that had presumably never been meant to walk here, the first carving waited.

It took me a while. The script was Old Alderic, and my master had beaten only the rudiments of it into me before declaring my penmanship "an insult to the alphabet." But the words were simple. Simple the way a knife is simple.

*I give my death to the crown, that the crown may hold it, that I need not.*

Below it, a signature. A name.

King Aldous the First.

The founder. The man whose statue stood in every square in the capital, whose face was on the copper coins I used to count in smugglers' arithmetic, whose *Wisdom* was quoted by schoolmasters and magistrates and every pompous alderman who'd ever levied a tax.

"He was afraid," I said. My voice came out strange. "He found it—or made it, or called it, I don't know—and he was dying, and he was afraid, so he gave his death to the crown. He bound it to the crown. To the *office*. Not to himself. To the crown itself, so it would pass down, so he'd never—"

"So no king would ever have to die again," Sera finished.

"No king would ever get to," I corrected. "That's the bargain. That's always the bargain with these things. You don't buy life. You buy *not-dying*, which is a different animal entirely, and it bites."

Maren had come to stand at the edge of the inner rings, and she was reading not the center but the middle circles, the renewals, tracing them with one rope-knuckled finger without quite touching. "Look at the dates, girl. Captain. Whatever you are." Her voice had gone thin. "Aldous renews the binding in his fortieth year. His son renews it in his. And his son. Every reign, a renewal. Every renewal, the same formula—*I feed the crown, the crown holds my death.* Feed. Do you see? Even then. Even at the very beginning, it was already hungry, and already they were feeding it."

"Feeding it what?" Wren asked, from the edge of the light. She was nineteen and deserved better than this answer.

"Magic," I said. "Gifted folk. Us. The hunt isn't policy, it isn't piety, it isn't the king's fear of rivals. It's *provisions*. Six hundred years of provisions." I looked around the vast dark chamber, at the rings of handwriting closing in on the center like a trap drawing shut, and I did the arithmetic I was always doing, the smuggler's arithmetic, except this time the cargo was people. "Every gifted person seized or burned since Aldous. Every hedge-witch, every village charm-worker, every child who talked to rivers. They weren't purging corruption. They were *stocking a larder*."

The silence afterward had weight. Tammas sat down heavily on a sarcophagus lid that probably wasn't a sarcophagus lid, because nothing down here was what it pretended to be.

"My brother," Olla said. It was the first thing either twin had said since the bells. Her voice was perfectly level, which was worse than screaming. "They burned my brother in Corrow market and called it cleansing. He was eleven. He could make flowers open early. That was the whole of his gift. Flowers." She looked at me. "It ate him. All this time, it *ate him*."

"I'm sorry," I said, and it was inadequate, and we both knew it, and I said it anyway because it was true.

* * *

We should have gone deeper—I could feel the trail of the thing's history continuing downward, older strata under newer, the way coral builds on coral—but Sera called a halt at the chamber's rim, and she was right to. We'd been moving for two days on stolen sleep and thinner rations, and the bells above were still ringing their fast-slow-fast, and somewhere between us and the throne room were however many hunters hadn't turned. Conscience costs blood; I'd paid some; I wasn't eager to pay more before I had to.

So we made camp in a side alcove off the great chamber, a low vault stacked with urns that I chose not to examine closely, and Maren dozed propped against the wall like a folded tent, and I couldn't sleep at all.

I went back to the rings.

Lantern low, alone, kneeling on six centuries of confession. The renewals told a story if you read them patiently, and patience was the one skill my hiding years had sharpened to a razor. The early ones were almost reverent—kings addressing the bound thing with formal dread, *O Devourer of Deaths, O Keeper of Our Line*. By the third century the language had curdled. The kings stopped naming it. They wrote around it, the way you write around a wound. *The crown requires. The crown must be sustained. Let the crown never want.* And the lists began—right there in the stone, in the matter-of-fact hand of royal clerks. District tallies. Seizure counts. Years with unusually high numbers marked with a small symbol I eventually worked out meant *plentiful*.

Plentiful. Like herring. Like harvest.

And then, near the outer rings, recent enough that the chisel-marks were still crisp, I found the anomaly. One renewal unlike the others. Shorter. Shakier. In a hand that pressed hard enough to crack the stone, as if the writer were carving with fury or terror or both:

*It is not content with deaths. It reaches for the unborn gift. My daughter's child—*

The line broke off. Below it, in a different hand, smooth and official: *Renewal completed. Record amended per protocol.*

My daughter's child. A royal infant, mentioned once, erased immediately. I stared at that broken sentence for a long time, feeling along the edges of it the way you feel along the edges of a missing tooth. Somewhere in the last few generations, a king or queen had discovered something about the parasite that the protocols said must never be discovered—that it didn't stop at the gifted, that it reached further, hungrier, toward something in the royal blood itself—and someone had tried to say so, and the record had been *amended*.

Amended. Which meant someone else knew. Someone below the king. Someone keeping the protocols.

"You should sleep."

Sera had come up beside me without a sound, which was a hunter's habit I'd stopped resenting somewhere around the river ford and never quite gotten used to. She crouched, studying the broken line. I watched her read it twice.

"There was a child," she said.

"There was a child. And then there wasn't a mention of a child." I tapped the smooth official hand. "This clerk didn't erase a mistake, Sera. Erasure is embarrassment. This is *containment*. Somebody found out the thing in the crown wants more than magic—it wants the bloodline, maybe the bloodline most of all—and they buried the knowledge so deep even the kings' own ghosts aren't allowed to speak it."

"Why would the parasite hide its own appetite?"

"Because appetite announced becomes appetite resisted." I thought of my master, of her burned cottage, of the missing final page. "Think about it. If the realm knew its kings had been hollow vessels for six hundred years—if it knew every heir since Aldous had been worn like a glove—what happens? No. The lie has to be total. The lie is the *crown*. Take away the lie and there's no throne left to sit on."

Sera was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the flat carefulness of someone handling something armed. "I swore an oath to that throne. When I was sixteen. Hand on the crown's seal, words in my mouth, my father crying in the front row because no Vane had ever been commissioned before." She turned her lantern so the light fell full on the innermost ring, on Aldous's neat, terrified signature. "*I give my death to the crown.* He gave it. And everyone after him just... inherited the debt, and made the whole kingdom pay the interest." She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what the worst part is? I believed. Right up until the prison ledgers, right up until the sealed dispatches, some stubborn corner of me believed the edict was harsh but necessary, that the crown protected us, that the hunt kept order. I built my whole life on that belief the way this palace is built on these bones. And the bones are rotten all the way down."

"Not all the way," I said.

She looked at me.

"The bones are rotten," I said. "But bones are what's left when everything soft is stripped away. What's under the lie, under the crown, under six hundred years of feeding—it's still the realm, Sera. Still the people in it. The flower-boy in Corrow market. The nurse who hid a child in the walls, if that's what happened. The lie sits on top of something real, and the real thing is worth saving even if the crown never was." I heard myself and winced. "Listen to me. Six months ago my grand ambition was to move a shipment of fever-tonic past the toll station without paying the charm-tax. Now I'm giving speeches about the soul of a nation in a haunted basement."

"It's a good speech," Sera said. "For a haunted basement."

Above us, faint through sixty feet of stone, the bells rang again. Fast-slow-fast. Gather, gather. They'd been ringing for two days and showed no sign of tiring, and every time they rang I imagined the thing on the throne turning its borrowed head, listening, patient as mineral, certain as gravity.

Then the bells stuttered.

I felt it before I heard it—a wrongness in the pressure against my skin, the deep-water sense I'd been carrying since we entered the crypts suddenly *shifting*, like a current changing direction. The cold patient hunger threading through the stone pulled taut, pointed, all of it, every thread of six centuries of residue, pointing not downward anymore.

Pointing at us.

"Maren," I said, standing. "Maren, wake up."

She was already awake. She was standing at the mouth of the side passage, lantern raised, and her face in the light was the oldest I'd ever seen it. "It knows," she said simply. "Something we touched, something we read—the binding rings are a nervous system, boy, they run all through the deep places, and we've been walking on its nerves for hours. It felt us the way you feel a fly on your skin." She turned. "And it's not sending hunters."

The temperature in the chamber dropped. Our lanterns dimmed—not guttering, *dimming*, as if the dark itself had thickened—and from the far side of the great chamber, from a stair I would have sworn we'd already searched, came a sound I will hear until I die: a slow, wet, deliberate dragging, like something enormous pulling itself along stone on limbs that had forgotten how limbs work.

"It's coming down itself," Maren said. "Run."

* * *

We ran.

Back across the rings of confession, boots hammering the six-hundred-year-old words of kings who sold their deaths and bought a monster, lanterns swinging wild shadows up the walls. Behind us the dragging grew louder, closer, unhurried—it didn't need to hurry, the dragging-thing, it had hunted us for six hundred years and it knew exactly how fast prey tires—and ahead of us the passage we'd come by was already changing, I could feel it, the old mortar knitting itself closed like flesh over a wound.

"Wren!" Sera shouted. "The side shaft, the one you flagged!"

"On it!" Wren skidded left through a gap I'd have missed in daylight, into a crawlspace barely wide enough for Tammas, and we poured ourselves in after her, stone scraping armor, breath sawing, while behind us the dragging reached the great chamber and stopped.

Stopped. Because it didn't need to chase. From the chamber came a voice—not a sound so much as a pressure that our ears translated into voice, a voice like a landslide remembering language, and it spoke one word in Old Alderic that I knew, that every gifted child in Thornfell knows because it appears in the oldest charms, the protective ones, the ones my master taught me first:

*Mine.*

Then the corridor behind us sealed with a sound like a tomb exhaling, and the pressure receded—not gone, never gone, but withdrawn, satisfied, a fisherman leaving a hook in the water.

We collapsed in the crawlspace junction, six fugitives and a witch-elder, gasping in the dark. After a while Tammas started laughing, the shaky post-terror laugh, and Olla joined him, and even Wren, and I put my forehead against the cold stone and let the shaking happen.

It had said *mine*. About us. About me.

And it had also, in saying it, confirmed everything the rings confessed. It was down there. It had always been down there. Aldous the Wise, Aldous the Founder, Aldous whose face smiled from the copper coins—had been its first victim, and every king since had been its meal ticket, and the whole shining history of the realm was a larder ledger written in coral and bone.

"We can't go back the way we came," Sera said at last, practical as a blade, wiping grit from her face. "Where does this shaft go?"

Wren consulted her memory in the lantern light. "Up. Toward the old servant levels. And—" she hesitated, "—there's a branch. Sealed. Marked with the private cipher, the one only the king's household uses. It goes to the royal nurseries."

The nurseries. Empty for a generation, everyone said. Sealed since the last queen's child died of fever, everyone said.

Everyone said, I thought, staring at the broken sentence carved in the ring behind us. *My daughter's child—*

"Then that's the way we go," I said.

Sera looked at me, and in the lantern light I saw her arrive at the same arithmetic I'd already done, the same impossible sum, and her eyes went wide.

"You think there's someone up there," she breathed. "After all this time. You think the child survived."

"I think," I said, "that a parasite which amends its own records is a parasite with something to hide. And I think we just found out what."

Above us, faint and steady, the bells rang on. Fast-slow-fast. Gather, gather.

Somewhere in the dark of the sealed nurseries, something gathered too.
