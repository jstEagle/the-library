## 13. The Elder's Price

The witch-elder's name was Maren Ostwick, and she had been in the palace undercrofts so long that the rats had stopped considering her an intruder and started considering her a landlord.

We'd found her two days after Sera turned her company—two days of running through passages that smelled of candle smoke and older things, following a woman who claimed to have been born in these tunnels and intended, apparently, to die in them on her own schedule and no one else's. She was small, stooped, wrapped in about nine layers of wool that had once been different colors, and she looked at Emrys Pemberton, smuggler and hedge-witch, the way a fishmonger looks at a fish that has been sitting out too long.

"So," she said, settling onto a crate that groaned in protest. "You're the one with the missing page."

I was. Or I had been. My master's grimoire rode against my ribs in its oilcloth wrapping, and the hole where its final page should have been felt less like absence every day and more like a wound that hadn't decided to close.

"You know what was on it," I said.

"I know what was *not* on it," Maren said. "Which is more useful." She held up one knotted finger. "Everyone assumes the last page of an unbinding ritual contains the hardest part. The words. The gestures. The terrible secret syllable you must speak at the moment of greatest danger." Another finger. "It doesn't. The words are simple. Children could learn them. That's the trap."

Sera stood at the edge of the lamplight, arms folded, still wearing the grey of a hunt captain who had committed treason four days ago and hadn't yet found clothes to match her new life. Beside her were Tam and Odile, the two hunters who had followed her over the cliff-edge of loyalty, and Brannic, my master's other student, who had spent six years believing me dead and seemed to be taking my continued existence as a personal affront.

Maren ignored all of them. She looked at me.

"The unbinding is not a spell," she said. "It is a *transaction*. The parasite does not cling to the crown. It clings to magic itself—it grips whatever gift it finds and drinks until the vessel is empty, then moves to the next. A king. A hedge-witch. A child born with second sight in some fishing village. It has been eating this kingdom for three hundred years, and we have been calling the hunger a crown."

"And the ritual pulls it off," I said. "Like prying a leech."

"Like prying a leech that has grown roots." She leaned forward. "The words loosen the grip. But a grip needs somewhere to go when it lets go. You cannot simply cast it into the air—the moment it leaves the king, it will seize the nearest gift within reach, and I promise you, boy, there are easier meals in this room than a hollowed-out old man."

The lamp guttered. Nobody moved.

"It requires an anchor," Maren said. "A living person with the gift, standing at the center of the circle, holding the thing's attention while the words do their work. Not fighting it. Not resisting. *Offering*. You must open yourself to it completely—every thread of your magic laid out like a table set for a guest—and let it believe, for the length of the ritual, that you are the finest meal it has ever been offered. And while it feeds on you, the unbinding pries it loose from the crown, and when it tries to leap from the dying king to its new vessel—" she spread her hands, "—there must be nothing left to grip."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"And the anchor?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I expected, which I chose to interpret as courage rather than shock.

"Empty," Maren said. "Entirely. Whatever survives the feeding—and something usually does, though I make no promises about the quality of the survival—will be ordinary. No gift. No craft. No magic ever again. The well doesn't refill, boy. It's not a well anymore. It's a grave with your name on it." She paused. "And sometimes the grave fills faster than planned. Sometimes the parasite takes everything, including the pulse. The old texts give it odds. One anchor in three walks away. The others—" she shrugged, a small, terrible gesture, "—the others are the price of the word *sometimes*."

Brannic made a sound like a man swallowing his own heart. Odile said something under her breath that I didn't catch and suspected I was better off not catching.

Sera looked at me.

That was the whole event, really. She just looked at me, across the lamplight and the crates and three hundred years of parasite, and I watched her do the arithmetic—I knew her arithmetic, I'd learned it at seventeen, watching her count exits in her father's tavern—and I saw her arrive at the answer before anyone said anything aloud.

She opened her mouth.

"No," I said.

"You don't even know what I was going to—"

"You were going to say my name," I said. "Or Brannic's. Or ask if there was another way. There isn't another way. I've read the page's ghost, Sera. I've spent six years reading around that hole in the grimoire, and everything I know says Maren's right. There's one anchor required, and it has to be someone with the gift, and it has to be someone willing, because a reluctant anchor flinches, and a flinching anchor feeds the thing twice."

"There are three gifted people in this room," Sera said, and her voice had gone flat and hard, the voice she used on recruits. "Counting you."

"Counting me," I agreed. "Brannic's gift is healing—he told me once he couldn't hurt a fly with it if the fly insulted his mother. He'd be a feast and nothing else. And the third—"

"Don't," she said.

"—the third is a woman who gave up her gift years ago," I finished gently. "Didn't you? Back when you joined the hunt. They make you, don't they. The hunters. They burn it out of you or they don't take you. I've wondered for six years how you stood it."

Her jaw worked. "That's not—"

"That's why you never asked me about it," I said. "All those years ago. Why you never asked what my magic *felt* like. You'd already forgotten on purpose, and asking would have ruined the forgetting." I stood up. My knees cracked. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped with the patience of centuries. "So it can't be you. It was never going to be you. Which leaves—"

"Emrys." Brannic stepped forward, and there were tears standing in the big man's eyes, which embarrassed him visibly. "Let me—"

"You're a healer," I said. "This kingdom is going to need healers. It's going to need them desperately, for years, after what's coming. You heard Maren. The parasite has been eating the gifted for three hundred years—do you know how few of us are left? Do you know what happens to a realm full of frightened, hunted witches when the amnesty comes, if there's no one left who can mend bone and draw fever? You're not an anchor, Brannic. You're infrastructure."

"That's a horrible way to talk about a man's soul."

"It's a horrible week," I said. "We're all adjusting."

* * *

They argued with me for the better part of an hour. I want that recorded somewhere, in case history decides I was brave in a quiet, solitary way. I was not permitted to be solitary about it even slightly. Sera argued longest, and worst, meaning best—she used everything, the tactical objections first (the anchor has to hold the circle while hunters fight their way to the throne room; I'm the only one who knows the guards' rotations), then the practical ones (you're half-blind in your left eye since the vaults, you'll misjudge the circle's edge), and finally, when those ran out, the one she'd been saving, the one that wasn't an argument at all:

"Six years," she said. "You ran from me for six years, and now you're going to run from me permanently, and you expect me to stand in a doorway and hold a line while it happens."

"I expect you to survive it," I said. "That's rather the point of the whole arrangement."

"That's not the point. That's the *cost*. The point is—" She stopped herself. Her mouth did something complicated. "The point is you keep deciding things alone. You decided to run alone. You decided to come back alone. You're deciding to die alone, and you keep calling it nobility, and I keep calling it what it is, which is the same wall you built between us when we were nineteen."

Maren, from her crate, said: "She's not wrong, boy. I've known you for four days and I've watched you decide things alone twice."

"Three times," I said. "You missed the bit where I decided to trust you."

"Oh, I counted that one," Maren said. "It went on the list too."

But here is the truth, the one I circled all night and finally landed on near dawn, when the lamps burned low and everyone had run out of arguments and taken up positions of exhausted silence around the undercroft like chess pieces nobody wanted to move:

I was not sacrificing myself. I know how it looks. I know the word for it, and I know Sera will use it, and I know that part of me wants her to, because being called noble is cheaper than being called afraid.

But I sat in the dark with Maren's arithmetic for a long time, and the honest sum was this: my magic has never once made my life better. It got my master's cottage burned. It got me hunted across two provinces. It kept me alive in the passes, yes—but alive the way a rat is alive, unseen, unloved, counting copper coins in the dark and calling it a life. Every charm I ever cast to hide myself hid me from the good things too. Six years of invisibility, purchased at the price of the only person I ever—

Well.

If the parasite wants to drink me empty, let it. There's a version of this where I lose nothing but the thing that was costing me everything anyway. And there's a version where I lose all of it, pulse included, and in that version at least I'll have spent myself on something instead of hoarding myself for no one. Smuggler's arithmetic. You weigh the cargo against the road, and sometimes the road wins, and you pay, and the goods get through.

Someone has to carry it through. I'm the one holding the page-shaped hole.

* * *

Near dawn, Maren taught me the words.

They were, as promised, simple. Four lines. A child could learn them—that was the trap, she said, because the simplicity makes people think the ritual is easy, when in fact the words are the least of it. The work is in the opening. In lying down inside your own gift like a door taken off its hinges.

"Say them back," she said.

I said them back.

"Again. Slower. The third line is where anchors fail—they try to hold something back, one little corner of themselves, a reserve, a trick up the sleeve. The parasite finds the reserve instantly. It's been finding reserves for three hundred years. It will crack that corner open like an egg and pour out everything behind it, and the ritual dies with you, and the crown keeps its tenant."

"No reserves," I said.

"No reserves." She studied me with eyes gone pale as river ice. "You understand what you're agreeing to. Truly. Not the story of it. The fact."

"I'm a smuggler," I said. "I understand contracts. This one's terms are bad, but the alternative is worse, and the client is everyone."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe. Of my master, perhaps, who had presumably also negotiated with her once, long ago, over tea or brandy or both.

"She knew," Maren said quietly. "Your mistress. That's why the page was written the way it was. That's why she kept it separate. She always said the final page should only live in the hands of someone who'd already decided." She looked down at her knotted fingers. "I told her she was being dramatic. She said dramatic was the family trade."

"Was?"

Maren didn't answer. She pulled a scrap of charcoal from somewhere in her nine layers of wool and began, on the flat of a crate lid, to draw the circle. Slowly. Precisely. Lines I would need to stand inside.

I watched her hands and thought about my master's cottage, ash on the wind, and about the missing page, and about how she had been taken instead of killed because the parasite wanted what she knew—and I thought: she forgave the boy who ran. She said so, in the vaults, with almost nothing left of her voice. If she could forgive that, then surely the boy who stayed, this time, to the very end—

"Emrys."

Sera. She'd come back into the lamplight without my noticing, which was unlike me, which was perhaps the whole problem with me. She stood at the edge of the chalk lines Maren was drawing, and she looked at the circle, and then at me, and her face was doing the thing it did before she said things she couldn't take back.

"You've already decided," she said. "You decided hours ago. All that arguing was theater."

"Yes," I said. Because lying to her had a six-year head start and I was done funding it.

"When were you going to tell me?"

"I just did."

"After. You were going to tell me *after*. After the plan was set, after the bells, after it was too late to—" Her breath caught. "You were going to let me find out the way the kingdom finds out things. Too late and all at once."

I opened my mouth to defend myself and found, to my genuine surprise, that I had nothing. She was right. I'd rehearsed the confession in my head like a merchant rehearsing a price—wait until the goods are loaded, wait until the buyer's committed—and it had seemed so sensible, so *safe*, and safety was the whole disease, wasn't it. Six years of it. Staying unseen. Keeping the terrible knowledge in a locked room and handing people the furnished parts.

"Sera," I said. "There's something I have to tell you. Right now. Before another hour goes by."

She waited. The lamp flickered. Maren drew her circles and pretended, graciously, not to listen, which fooled neither of us.

"In the morning," I said, "when the others wake, I'm going to stand in front of everyone and say it plainly: the ritual needs an anchor, the anchor is me, and I've already given my word. And I need you to hear it with the others, because if I tell you alone first, you'll spend the whole day trying to find a way around it, and there isn't one, and I can't watch you look for it."

"That's cowardice dressed as kindness."

"Probably," I admitted. "I contain multitudes and most of them are flaws."

And she laughed—a short, wet, furious laugh, the kind that's grief wearing a disguise—and then she stopped laughing, and looked at me with an expression I'd only seen once before, years ago, on a night I ran, and I understood that I had about thirty seconds before she said the thing she'd been carrying since chapter one of whatever this is.

"Emrys," she said. "Before you give them your word tomorrow—before you make it official, before it's carved anywhere, before you stand in front of everyone and make it real—there's something *I* have to tell you. Something I should have said six years ago on the dock at Marrow's Reach, and didn't, because I was a coward, and then didn't again for six years, because the cowardice compounded interest."

My heart did something undignified.

"If it's an argument," I said carefully, "I've heard them all—"

"It's not an argument."

"If it's a confession of treason, we're past that—"

"It's not treason."

"If it's—" I stopped. Because she took one step closer, into the lamplight, into the unfinished chalk circle, and her hand went to the collar of her grey coat, and I remembered, suddenly, with the force of a dropped anvil, that Sera Vane had never once in her life reached for her collar unless she was about to show someone something hidden there.

"What's under the collar, Sera?" I asked.

She pulled the chain free. On the end of it, wrapped in waxed cloth gone soft with years of wearing, was a ring. Thin band. Cheap silver. The kind sold at river fairs to boys with more intention than coin.

I recognized it. Of course I recognized it. I'd bought it when I was nineteen, with smuggler's money, and lost my nerve on the dock at Marrow's Reach, and thrown it into the black water rather than carry the weight of the question I'd failed to ask.

"You went in after it," I said.

"I went in after it," she said. "Took me three dives. The current nearly had me. And then I climbed out, soaked and shaking and furious, and turned around, and you were gone." Her voice didn't break, exactly. It *bent*, the way a mast bends before it decides. "Six years, Emrys. I have carried your cowardice around my neck next to mine, and tomorrow you intend to sign away the rest of your life, and I am *done* waiting for the right moment, because there is no right moment, there is only this one."

She held the ring out across the chalk line.

"So," she said. "Ask me. Before you give them your word. Ask me *first*."
