## 14. Sera's Choice

He didn't take the ring.

I want it recorded—somewhere, anywhere, in whatever ledger the gods keep of my worst nights—that I held that ring out across the chalk line with my hand steady as a drawn blade, and Emrys Pemberton looked at it the way a man looks at a bridge he's about to burn, and then he did not reach for it.

"Sera," he said.

"Don't." My voice came out low and even, which is how you know a hunter is at her most dangerous, or her most frightened. In my experience the two share a pulse. "Don't say my name like it's an apology. You've had six years of practice saying nothing at all; don't spend your last night getting good at apologies."

"It's not—" He stopped. Rubbed the heel of his hand against his sternum, the way he does when the words are snagging on something on their way up. "It's not a proposal."

"No," I said. "A proposal would be simple. A proposal I could refuse. This is you asking me to stand in a doorway and watch you walk through it, knowing what's on the other side, and call it love." I closed my fist around the ring until I felt the band bite into my palm. Six years I'd carried it there in some form or another—in a pocket sewn shut, in a boot heel, in the hollow of my throat where words go to die. "You know what I did when you left?"

He flinched. Good. Let him flinch.

"I went back to the river," I said. "Every day for a month. I'd tell myself it was training. Current drills. Very rigorous. My sergeant thought I was devoted." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle like river ice in thaw. "I was standing where you'd stood. That's all. Standing in water up to my knees, waiting for a boy who had already decided I wasn't worth drowning for."

"That's not what happened—"

"Then *tell* me what happened, Emrys. For once in your calculating, copper-counting life, tell me the truth without an escape route built into it."

* * *

The elder had withdrawn to the far end of the crypt with the child asleep against her shoulder—the little heir, our small impossible hope, drooling on the shoulder of a woman who'd survived three purges—and so it was just the two of us in the lamplight, with the chalk line between us and six years between the chalk line and everything else.

Emrys sat down. Not gracefully. He folded onto the cold stone like his strings had been cut, and for a moment he just breathed, and I realized with a strange, distant pity that this man—who could calculate a smuggler's margin to the half-copper, who could read a cipher sewn into his own bindings, who had stared down a thing older than the crown itself and lied to its face—he did not know how to have this conversation. No one had ever taught him. I'm not sure anyone ever taught me either. We were two people fluent in silence trying to conduct a symphony.

"When I ran," he said finally, "it wasn't from you."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true, and you keep not hearing it." He looked up at me, and his eyes in the lamplight were the same brown they'd always been, the brown of river shallows over stones, and I hated him for it. Hated that time had put lines around them and hadn't changed the color. "Sera, I was nineteen. My gift was already showing through no matter how I smothered it. Charms leaking light. Wards humming when I got nervous—which was always, around you especially. And your father was a hunt warden. Your whole family, going back four generations. If anyone in that garrison town had seen what I was, do you know what would have happened? Not to me. To *you*. To everyone who'd ever let me sit at their table."

"So you protected me by vanishing."

"I protected you by making myself a story. The apprentice who ran off with a tinker's daughter, something with a shape people could gossip about instead of ask questions about." His mouth twisted. "It's the smuggler's arithmetic again. Weigh every risk. Pay every toll. And the toll that year was you."

"And you never once thought to *ask* me?" I was shaking now. I let myself shake. There was no one left to see it who mattered. "Six years, Emrys. Do you know what the arithmetic looks like from the other side of it? From the side of the person who gets *paid out* to protect? It looks like waking up every morning for a year wondering if you're dead. It looks like volunteering for hunts in Thornfell because it was the closest thing to looking for you that wouldn't get me hanged. It looks like—" I stopped, because here was the bottom of it, the coin I'd never spent. "It looks like keeping a dead man's ring. Because that's what I told myself you were. Dead. It was easier than coward. Easier than *chosen someone else over me*."

"I never chose anyone else."

"You chose a version of me that needed protecting more than she needed *you*." The words came out flat and precise and cruel, and I watched them land, and I did not take them back. "That's not love, Emrys. That's charity with better posture."

* * *

Silence, after that. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears like deep water. Somewhere above us the palace groaned in the wind, and beneath that, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the parasite's hunger hummed through the stones—we'd all learned to hear it now, the way you learn to hear a river under ice. Even the building wanted to be eaten.

When Emrys spoke again, his voice had gone quiet in a new way. Stripped. The merchant's patter, the hedge-witch's cleverness, all of it set down like tools at the end of a shift.

"You're right," he said.

"Don't make it easy on yourself—"

"*You're right.*" Louder, and the elder's head turned at the far end of the crypt, and he lowered it again but didn't soften it. "I've been doing the arithmetic wrong my whole life. I counted what I'd lose if I stayed. I never once counted what staying would have been *worth*. Do you know why I volunteered for the unbinding? Truly?"

"Because you're the anchor with the gift. Because the ritual needs one. Because you're noble and self-sacrificing and insufferable."

"Because it's tidy." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "Because if I give everything, then there's no ledger left. Nothing owed, nothing carried. No awkward years of being ordinary next to someone extraordinary. No slow mornings where you realize you gave your heart to a man who can't charm a kettle anymore and wonder if you were only in love with the magic. It's the same move as running, Sera. It's the same cowardice wearing armor. Walk out of your life cleanly rather than risk living in it badly."

The ring was still in my fist. My hand ached around it. Slowly, joint by joint, I made myself open my fingers.

"Why did you keep it?" he asked. "All these years. If I was dead to you. If I was a coward."

And there it was. The question I'd been circling since a river ford, since grey wool and a hunter's mask, since the moment I'd looked down at a captured witch and felt my whole carefully constructed life tilt on its axle.

"Because I never stopped loving you," I said, "and it was *inconvenient*."

* * *

He blinked. Of all the things I might have said, I think that was the one he had no ledger entry for.

"Inconvenient," he repeated.

"You were supposed to be dead, and instead you were inconveniently alive. You were supposed to be a stranger, and instead you were inconveniently *you*, sitting in my custody making jokes about the quality of palace chains. I had a career, Emrys. A company that trusted me. An oath I'd built my whole spine out of. And every mile we rode toward the capital, I could feel it cracking, and I knew—I *knew*—it was because of you. Because some part of me had kept a boy's ring in a boot for six years like a fool, and that part woke up the moment I saw your stupid face under the hood at the ford, and it has been making unreasonable demands ever since."

"Such as?"

"Such as treason. Such as falsifying execution orders. Such as turning my sword against my own commander last week while bells rang and good hunters died." I crouched down in front of him, finally, eye to eye, close enough to see the lamplight doubled in his eyes. "Such as standing here right now telling a man who plans to die tomorrow that I love him, which may be the single most tactically disastrous thing I have ever done, and I once charged a fortified mill with eleven men."

"Sera—"

"I'm not finished." I took his hands. They were scarred and ink-stained and trembling slightly, and they closed around mine like they'd been waiting six years for the assignment. "Here is what I never said at the river. Here is what I stitched into your bindings in cipher because I couldn't say it aloud to your sleeping face: *come back*. Not come back to me—that felt too much like asking. Just come back. Come back from wherever you'd gone. And then the edict came, and the world caught fire, and I thought: well. That's answered, then. Some questions the gods answer by burning the page."

His breath was unsteady. Good. Let him be unsteady. One of us should be allowed.

"But you're not dead," I said. "You're not burned. You're sitting here planning to hand everything over tomorrow, and I am asking you—not ordering you, I have no rank over you, I barely have rank over myself anymore—I am *asking* you to stop doing the tidy arithmetic. To consider, just for one hour of one night, the untidy option. Which is: stay. Live. Be ordinary and difficult and mine. Let the realm find another way, or let it fall, or let it surprise us. Love isn't a bargain you settle, Emrys. It's the one account you leave open forever."

* * *

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, very quietly:

"The ritual needs an anchor. There's no other way. Maerlin checked it three times. The binding is woven into the crown's gold itself—it needs a living gift poured through it to unravel, and I'm the strongest anchor we have, and the child—" his voice caught on the child, on the sleeping weight of the future against the elder's shoulder— "the child can't be asked. So it's me, or it's nothing, and nothing means the thing wears the next vessel, and the next hunt, and the next burning, forever."

"I know."

"And you're asking me to walk away anyway."

"I'm asking you to look at me while you decide." I gripped his hands harder. "Not the ledger. Me. Look at what you're spending, Emrys. Not what the realm gains—what *you* lose. What *I* lose. You taught me once that every price has two sides. So look at mine."

And he looked. Gods help him, he actually looked, and I watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes—the old habit dying hard, weighing, measuring—and then something shifted. Something broke loose. His face crumpled the way faces do when a man stops fighting and starts feeling, all at once, six years of it.

"I was going to ask you at the river," he whispered. "Before I ran. I had the ring. I'd bought it with the first money my charms ever earned—honest money, real work, warding a granary against rats. I was going to ask you, and then Master Ilenne sent word that a warden patrol was asking questions in the valley, and I sat on that riverbank doing sums, and every sum ended the same way: *if I stay, she burns with me.* So I put the ring back in my pocket, and I put the pocket in my past, and I walked north." His thumb moved across my knuckles, back and forth, like he was smoothing a crease out of six years. "I never stopped loving you either. I just got very good at pretending love was a luxury good. Out of stock. Don't ask."

"Terrible merchant," I said, and my voice broke on it, and I let it break. "Worst I ever traded with."

"Worst you ever loved, you mean."

"That too."

And then I kissed him, or he kissed me—I'll never be sure whose fault it was, and I intend to argue about it for the rest of a long, ordinary, untidy life—but it was salt and smoke and six years arriving all at once, and somewhere in the middle of it I pressed the ring into his palm and closed his fingers over it, so that for once he was holding the thing instead of running from it.

When we broke apart, foreheads touching, his breath ragged against my mouth, he said: "This doesn't change the ritual."

"No," I admitted. "It makes it worse. Infinitely worse. I've just handed you a reason to survive and you intend to spend it by dawn."

"I intend to spend everything by dawn. That's rather the point."

"Well." I pulled back far enough to look at him properly, and I felt my face settle into the expression my company used to dread—the one that meant Captain Vane had found a third option nobody had priced in. "Then here's my counteroffer, hedge-witch. Before you pour your gift into that crown tomorrow, we're getting your master back. Maerlin says the vaults under the feeding halls hold her, drained but breathing. And I say no anchor of ours goes into the fire owing a debt to the woman who taught him. Tomorrow, before the bells, before the throne room—you and me and the elder's maps. We bring Ilenne home."

Emrys stared at me. "The vaults are the most guarded place in the palace."

"Yes."

"They're directly beneath the thing itself."

"Also yes."

"That's insane."

"Mm." I stood, drawing him up with me, the ring warm between our joined hands. "You knew that when you fell in love with me. It's in the contract."

And that was when the lamps guttered—all of them at once, every flame in the crypt bending sideways toward the stairwell as though sucked by a breath—and from somewhere far above us came the sound I had been dreading since the day I first read the sealed dispatches.

Bells. Not the palace's measured quarter-hour.

War-bells. All of them. Every tower in the city, ringing at once, and beneath their bronze voice the parasite's hunger rose through the stone like floodwater.

It knew. It had always known. And the night of long bells had begun three hours early.
