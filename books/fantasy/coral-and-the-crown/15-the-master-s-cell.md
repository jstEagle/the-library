## 15. The Master's Cell

The feeding vaults lay beneath the east wing of the palace, which told me everything I needed to know about royal priorities. Not beneath the dungeons—those were for common criminals. Not beneath the armory. Beneath the *gardens*. The parasite wanted a pleasant view of the world it was eating.

The witch-elder, Maren, had drawn the way on a scrap of my maps with a stub of charcoal: down through the cistern passage, past the bone-cold archive where the palace kept its inconvenient history, then left at the door that didn't exist. "It doesn't exist," she'd said, fixing me with one milky eye, "which is why no one guards it properly. The kingdom has always been very good at not seeing what it doesn't want to see."

Sera walked beside me, sword loose in its scabbard, torch held low. Behind us came Tamsin the ferry-boy-turned-fugitive-witch, who had insisted on coming and whom I had failed to insist otherwise, mostly because he carried the rope.

"You're quiet," Sera murmured.

"I'm doing arithmetic."

"Bad kind?"

"The kind where every answer comes out to 'probably dead.'"

She almost smiled. In six years of imagined conversations—the ones I'd conducted with her memory on long mountain nights, arguing, apologizing, occasionally confessing things I'd never have said aloud—I'd forgotten how her near-smiles worked. They arrived like weather. You couldn't ask for them; you could only be standing in the right place when they broke over you.

The door that didn't exist was iron, older than the palace around it, and answered Maren's knock-pattern—a rhythm of knuckles I recognized with a jolt, because it was *Wren's* pattern, my master's, the one she used on her own cottage door when she came back from market and wanted me to open before her hands were full. The elder knew her. Of course she did. Wren Ashgrave had outlived three kings' worth of hunts by knowing people, and people who knew doors.

The iron swung inward without complaint, and cold came out to meet us—not winter cold. Cellar cold. The cold of a place where something warm had been slowly taken away.

* * *

I have tried since to describe the feeding vaults, and I've settled on this: imagine a wine cellar built by someone who had heard about cellars but never seen one, and who had been told, wrongly, that the wine was the important part rather than the drinking.

There were no racks. There were niches—hundreds of them, cut into the pale stone in rows, each one just large enough for a person to stand or kneel in, each one ringed with script so old the letters had worn into mere grooves, like riverbeds where meaning used to flow. And in the nearest dozen niches there were people.

Some were asleep. Some were beyond sleep. All of them looked the way a fire looks in its last hour—all the heat gone inward, all the color banked down to embers and ash. Their lips moved sometimes. I don't want to know what they were saying, and I already know anyway. They were counting. When your gift drains out of you, you count what's left. I'd done it myself, small charms spent in smugglers' passes, tallying the copper coins of my own soul.

Tamsin made a sound behind me and turned aside to be sick against the wall, and I let him have his privacy with it.

"There." Sera's torch found the far row. "That script—it's different."

Different was right. Where the niche-rings were worn smooth with centuries of use, one vault at the end was sealed with fresh work—new-cut characters, still sharp-edged, the chalk-marks of the mason's measuring cord still ghosted on the stone around them. New cage. Recent occupant. Recent enough that whoever had put her there expected to come back.

Expected to come back because they weren't finished taking.

My legs carried me down that aisle without consulting the rest of me, and I remember thinking, with the detached clarity that terror lends, *so this is what she meant about arithmetic*—because Wren Ashgrave had taught me sums before spells, had rapped my knuckles with the slate-edge until I understood that everything was exchangeable, that power moved through the world like water and always found its level, and now I understood at last what lesson the world had been drilling into me for six years:

Everything costs. Everything is paid for eventually. The only question is who holds the bill.

The seal on her vault yielded to Maren's pattern again—a counter-knock, an unmaking rhythm—and the stone swung open on darkness that smelled of herbs gone stale and old paper and, underneath, faint as a memory of a memory, woodsmoke. Her cottage smell. She had carried it with her, or it had clung.

"Wren?" My voice came out wrong. Small. Six years younger than the rest of me.

And from the dark, cracked as drought-earth but unmistakably hers: "Emrys Pemberton. Late as ever. Sit down before you fall down; you always did go white as milk when you were frightened, and I refuse to catch you at my age."

* * *

They had laid her on a pallet like something already being prepared for burial, though whether by their custom or mine I couldn't say. Her hair had gone fully white—when I ran, it had been silver at the temples only, distinguished, she claimed, rather than old. Her hands lay on the blanket like two fallen birds. But her eyes were the same: black, quick, missing nothing, missing *no one*, cataloguing me even as I catalogued her.

"You got tall," she said. "Pity. You were much more manageable short."

I knelt. I meant to say any number of things. What came out was: "Your cottage. I found it burned. I thought—" The sentence collapsed under its own weight.

"You thought I was ash." She said it without drama, the way she'd once announced that my warding circles slanted left and always would. "I'm harder to burn than that. Though it was a near thing. They came for the grimoire, boy, not for me—or they told themselves so. The thing on the throne sent hunters with a list, and my name was second, and the page they took was last." A dry rasp that might once have been a laugh. "First they take my book, then my liberty. If they'd wanted my dignity they'd have had to come sooner. I'd spent that years ago, teaching hedge-witches arithmetic in a country that burns us for it."

"The final page," I said. "The unbinding."

Her eyes sharpened. "You've read the rest of it, then. You finally opened the grimoire instead of using it to press flowers."

"I've read most of it. It ends mid-sentence. It ends like someone interrupted."

"It ends like someone who learned the ending gets killed for knowing it, and chose to write it down anyway, somewhere safer than paper." Her fingers twitched toward me, and I took her hand, and it weighed nothing, and I had to look at the wall for a moment until my face agreed to behave. "In here," she said, tapping my knuckles with hers, one-two-three, the old slate-edge rhythm. "Memory is the safest page. No hunter's ever confiscated one successfully."

"I can't carry that. Wren, I can barely keep straight which pass has the tollbooth—"

"You can. You'll hate it, and you'll complain, and then you'll do it, because that's who I made. Now listen." And she began.

I won't set down here the words themselves. Some of you will understand why; the rest of you are better off not understanding. But I will tell you what it was like to receive them. She gave them to me the way she'd given me everything—piecemeal, demanding, with commentary. *Breathe between the clauses, boy, magic has no patience for the winded.* *No, the accent falls there, do you want the binding to think you're asking politely?* *Again.* Again. Again. Each repetition cost her something visible; her voice thinned like ice over a stream, and still she drove through it, because Wren Ashgrave had never in her life let a student leave a lesson half-learned, and she did not intend to begin with the last one.

Somewhere in the middle of it Sera knelt down beside us, quiet as she'd been taught to be in a hundred ambushes, and stayed. She didn't speak. She just kept the torch steady so the light wouldn't waver over Wren's moving lips. A hunt captain, holding a light for a witch teaching forbidden words. I watched her do it and felt something in my chest rearrange itself permanently.

When the last clause had been repeated until it lived in me like a scar, Wren fell back against the pallet, breathing hard, satisfied the way only teachers are satisfied—hungry and full at once.

"There," she whispered. "Now you know the ending. Congratulations and condolences."

* * *

I should have left then. The plan said leave then. Every heartbeat we lingered in the vaults was a coin spent from a purse that didn't refill. Sera said as much, gently, twice.

But I sat there holding my master's weightless hand, and what came out of me instead of sense was six years overdue.

"I ran."

"Yes," said Wren.

"I saw what happened to the last apprentice who stayed. Halveric. I was fourteen and I watched them take him off the moor and I ran that same night and I never—" My throat closed. I pushed through it the way she'd taught me to push through a failing spell: badly, but forward. "I told myself it was survival. Good arithmetic. Live to fight another day. But it wasn't, Wren, it was just running, dressed up in sums. I left you alone with a cottage and a target painted on the door, and I didn't write, I didn't send word, I hid so well I disappeared even to myself—"

"Emrys."

"—and then they burned it all anyway, so what did the hiding buy? Six years of copper coins saved for a house fire. I ran and it bought *nothing*—"

"*Boy.*" Her grip tightened with what strength remained, which was more than I'd credited. Her black eyes fixed me, and for a moment she was entirely, terrifyingly herself, the woman who had caught me stealing apples at eleven and decided theft showed initiative. "Listen to me carefully, because I am going to spend strength I cannot spare to say this once.

"You ran, yes. I watched you go, and do you know what I felt? Relief." I flinched. She didn't release me. "Relief. Because Halveric's death taught you exactly what it should have: that the world is real, that fire is real, that courage without arithmetic is just suicide with better posture. A teacher who fills a student with valor and no caution is building a pyre and calling it a hero. I taught you to count, Emrys. Counting means knowing when the price is too high. You counted, six years ago, and the price *was* too high, and you lived, and everything you did in those mountains—every charm, every smuggled charm-seller's token, every small kindness sold for copper—you did alive."

"That's not—" 

"It is not absolution. Good. You don't want absolution from me; you'd never trust it, you suspicious mule. What I offer is accounting." Her voice dropped, wore thin, kept going. "I reviewed the ledger long ago. You owe me nothing for leaving. You owe me nothing for living. If anything—" and here the crack finally came, the one fissure in all that granite, "if anything, you paid ahead. Do you know what I heard, all those years, in the wind off Thornfell? Stories. A hedge-witch in the passes who wards travelers for fair prices and heals children and asks no names. I knew your hand in every tale. You thought you'd vanished, child. You were the loudest whisper in those mountains. I have never been less alone than I was these six years, listening to the legend of my own runaway boy doing small good at great distance."

The vault was very quiet. Somewhere far above, the palace went about its hollow business.

"I forgave you the night you ran," Wren said softly. "I've merely been waiting six years for you to come collect it. You always were late."

I am not going to describe what I did then. He writes this down, this future self of mine, this narrator with his wry observations and his tidy metaphors, and even he declines to record the next two minutes, because some arithmetic is private. I will say only that my master held onto me with those bird-light hands as if I were the weightless one, and that when we finally separated, she looked at Sera over my shoulder with an expression of vast, weary satisfaction, and said:

"And *this* is the hunt captain. Sit up straight, girl; I want a look at you."

* * *

What passed between Wren Ashgrave and Captain Sera Vane in the following quarter hour, I mostly witnessed from the embarrassing position of someone being discussed as if absent. Wren asked four questions. I caught fragments: *"How long have you known what you're escorting?"* ... *"And you refused them anyway, knowing the cost?"* ... *"Does he know you kept his knife all these years, or is that a surprise you've been saving?"*

"She keeps my *what*?"

"Hush," said both women, in unison, which frightened me more than the parasite had.

Then Wren's strength—which had been burning like a lamp fed on its own wax for this entire reunion—began visibly to gutter, and Sera caught my eye over her head, and mouthed, *now or never,* and I gathered my master gently toward the pallet's edge, ready to lift her, because whatever else happened, she was not staying in this place one hour longer—

"No." Her hand closed on my sleeve. "No, boy. Listen. I'm not going anywhere with you."

"The plan—"

"The plan is a young man's plan, built by a young man who still believes everyone can be saved." Her smile was terrible and fond. "Look at me honestly, Emrys. Tell me what you see. Tell me what your gift tells you, since you've gotten good enough to read a person's reserves across a room. How much do you think is left in this vessel?"

I looked. I wished I hadn't. She was a candle-end. Whatever the parasite's feeders had left her, it wasn't road-travel. It possibly wasn't sunrise.

"If I go with you, you'll slow for me," she said. "You'll slow for me, and slowing will kill the ones who *can* be saved—the child you're all whispering about, the king, half this city when the bells start. Whereas if I stay..." She shrugged, a gesture she'd made ten thousand times over slate and stewpot, utterly ordinary, utterly devastating. "If I stay, I am one drained old witch in a vault full of them, and the thing upstairs has already taken everything worth taking. My ending is written either way, child. The only question is whose story buys more with it."

"No." My voice sounded very far away. "No, we came for you, the whole point was—"

"The point," Wren said, "was the words. Which are yours now. Which I put in you the way I put everything in you: by force, with complaints, until it took." She pulled me down until her forehead rested against mine, and her skin was cool as the vault stone, and her eyes were bright as the day we met. "You were the best thing I ever taught myself to give back. Now go. Unbind the crown. Free the fool in the gold hat and the babe in the walls and every gifted soul in this rotten, beautiful kingdom. And when it takes your gift from you—and it will, boy, I've read the ritual's price, I wrote it—"

"You *wrote*—"

"Of course I wrote it. You deserve to walk in with your eyes open; you've always hated surprises." Her thumb brushed my cheekbone, once, where a callus from six years of bowstrings had hardened. "When it takes your gift, you will feel yourself become small. Ordinary. You'll grieve it like a limb. And I am telling you now, in advance, so you can hold this moment against that one: *small was always enough.* The hedges were always the point, Emrys. Not the tower. Any fool can build a tower. It takes a whole life to learn the value of a hedge."

Behind me, Sera said quietly, "Ma'am. It's time."

"I know it's time, Captain, I'm not deaf, merely dying. Go on, both of you. And Emrys—" I was three steps toward the door when her voice caught me like a hook. "The grimoire. When this is done, take it back to Thornfell. Rebuild the cottage. Teach someone. That's the inheritance; the words in your head are only the *fee*."

"Yes, Master."

"Cheeky. Go."

At the threshold I broke my own resolution and looked back. She had arranged herself upright in the niche, chin lifted, hands folded, wearing the expression she'd worn over every finished lesson: stern, satisfied, already preparing the next one. The worn script around her glowed faintly as the seal reknit, patient as erosion, and the last I saw of Wren Ashgrave was a small figure in a large stone frame, like a portrait that had decided, on reflection, to hang itself.

We did not speak in the corridors. Tamsin cried the whole way and I let him, envying him the efficiency of it.

It was only at the cistern junction, in the dark, with Sera's hand finding mine in the blackness—finding, I noticed, the way you find a thing you've mapped long ago—that the thought finally surfaced, cold and complete, the true shape of what Wren had told me without telling me:

*I wrote the ritual's price.*

Which meant she had known, all along, exactly what the unbinding would cost.

Which meant that when the parasite had come for her grimoire—for the page, for the ending—she hadn't hidden it to protect herself.

She had hidden it to protect *me* from reading it in time to change my mind.

"Sera," I said, into the dripping dark. "How fast can you get us to the throne room?"

She turned. Even blind, I could feel her reading my voice the way Wren read ledgers.

"Why?"

"Because I just realized I'm late for the only appointment in my life I can't afford to miss."
