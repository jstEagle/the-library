## 16. A Child in the Walls

The undercrofts of the palace smelled like a tomb that had given up trying to be impressive. Damp stone, old incense gone sour, and beneath it all something sweeter and worse—the faint mineral tang of the parasite's feeding vaults, which we'd left two levels above us and which I could still feel on my skin like grease that wouldn't wash off.

"Charming place," Emrys said behind me. His voice echoed wrong down here, flattened by the dark. "I've smuggled through nicer cellars. I once spent a night in a wine press. This is worse than the wine press."

"You're comparing royal crypts to a wine press."

"The wine press had better company." A pause. "Present circumstances excluded."

I didn't turn around. If I turned around, I'd look at him, and if I looked at him, I'd think about what he'd volunteered for three days ago in the witch-elder's flickering lamplight—*I'll be the anchor*—in that calm, reasonable voice he used for arithmetic and self-destruction both. Six years ago he'd run from me without a word of goodbye. Now he was planning to leave again, permanently, and he kept making jokes about wine presses as though humor were a substitute for staying alive.

So I held the lantern high and studied the wall instead.

Maera—the witch-elder, ninety-some years of spite and wisdom packed into a body like a bundle of kindling—had given us the route in a whisper: *Down past the drowned chapel, where the masons' marks change hands. The old kings built twice, Sera Vane. Once for God, once for what they actually feared.* She'd said the second palace was down here. The real one. The one with room in its bones for secrets.

The masons' marks changed where she'd promised. Above us, every stone bore the crowned chisel of the royal guild, uniform as soldiers. Here, the cuts went older, rougher, and some were marked with a symbol I didn't know—a spiral with a line struck through it.

Emrys stopped when I did. "That mark," he said slowly. "My master had it on her grimoire. On the spine, under the leather. I used to think it was just her guild sign."

"Your master who knew the unbinding ritual."

"My master who knew the unbinding ritual," he agreed quietly. "And who got taken instead of killed, because the thing upstairs doesn't waste people who know things." He touched the spiral with one finger, gentle, like touching a grave. "This isn't a hiding place, Sera. It's a legacy. Somebody's been keeping something safe down here for a long time."

* * *

We found the door an hour later, and I want to be honest about how we found it: we nearly died finding it.

The passage narrowed past the drowned chapel—we'd waded through knee-high water black as ink, and something in that water had moved with a patience I didn't care for—and opened into a round chamber floored with old mosaic, coral-and-gold patterns worn to ghosts. In the center stood a slab of granite that looked exactly like every other piece of masonry down here, except that the dust around it was disturbed. Swept. Recently swept.

Emrys crouched and ran his hand along the floor without touching the dust itself. "Someone comes and goes," he murmured. "Regularly. Look at the track—narrow feet, small stride. And there—" He pointed to a smear near the wall. "Flour. Or meal. Someone's carrying food down here."

My hand found my sword hilt out of pure habit. "A squatter?"

"In the sealed undercrofts of a paranoid palace? With flour?" He shook his head. "Whoever it is has access. Whoever it is has *purpose*."

Which is when the knife came out of the dark and pressed against my throat.

I'll give the hand holding it this much: it was steady. Small, but steady. The voice that came with it was cracked with age and iron with resolve.

"One more step toward the girl and I open you, hunter. I know your order's colors. I've buried better friends than you in worse places than this."

"Sera," Emrys said carefully, from across the chamber. "Don't."

"I'm not planning to."

The knife-wielder stepped around into my lantern light, keeping the blade at my neck the whole way, and I got my first look at her: a woman somewhere past sixty, thin as a lath, dressed in servant's grey gone greyer, with white hair pinned up in a style thirty years out of fashion and eyes that had been crying recently and recently decided to stop.

"Nurse," I guessed, because the old kings' households always had one, and because nobody else on earth holds a knife quite like a woman who has spent her life defending something smaller than herself.

Her eyes flicked to my collar, my insignia. "Captain," she spat. "They gave you a captain's braid now. When I served, hunters wore plain grey and at least had the decency to be ashamed."

"We're not with them," Emrys said. "Not anymore. Not most of us. Maera sent us."

The name landed on her like cold water. The knife trembled—one degree, no more. "Maera Thornhollow is dead. They burned her cottage in the passes."

"They took her," Emrys said. "She's alive. In the vaults. I'm going to get her back." He paused, and then he did the thing he does that isn't magic but works like it: he told the truth, plainly, with nothing hidden in his hands. "My name is Emrys Pemberton. She was my teacher. The thing wearing the king hunts the gifted to feed on them. We know about the crown. We know what it's done to every heir since Aldric the Second. And we came down here because Maera said the old builders left something in the deep palace that the parasite can't touch."

Silence. The knife stayed where it was. Somewhere far above us, muffled by ten feet of stone, a bell rang the hour, and the woman's face did something complicated—grief and fear and a fierce, exhausted hope all fighting for the same ground.

"You say you know about the heirs," she said at last. "Say it plainly. What do you know?"

"That none of them have been born right since the binding," Emrys said. "That they come out hollow, or the hollowing takes them young. That the thing needs a vessel of the bloodline and keeps the bloodline going the way a farmer keeps breeding stock." His jaw tightened. "And that the current king hasn't named an heir in twenty years, which either means the parasite has finally run the line dry—"

"—or that there was an heir it never got," the nurse finished.

The knife lowered. Slowly. Like a drawbridge coming down.

* * *

Her name was Ottoline. She'd been under-nurse to Queen Lissandra, the king's wife, dead nineteen years now in a "fall" that everyone polite had agreed not to examine too closely. She talked while she worked, working three small brass pins out of the granite slab's edge, and her voice had the flat steadiness of someone reciting a story she'd rehearsed against the day she'd finally need to tell it.

"The queen knew before anyone. Women know their own bodies, whatever the physicians say. She felt the thing in the child before it was born—or felt its absence, rather. Every babe of that line comes quiet, Captain. Too quiet. Born already half-emptied. She'd lost two before, and she told me, the night this one quickened, that she could feel the difference. That this one *fought*."

The last pin clicked free. The slab swung inward on a pivot so well-oiled it made no sound at all, revealing a low corridor lined with shelves, blankets, a cot, a child's wooden horse with one ear chewed smooth.

"She smuggled the baby out herself, four days after the birth. Told the court the child was stillborn. I watched her lie to the king's face with tears in her eyes, and the thing behind his eyes believed her, because parasites don't imagine anyone would love anything enough to fight them for it." Ottoline's mouth twisted. "She died eight months later. I've often wondered if it was grief or if she made sure of it, so the secret would die with her and live with me."

"And the child?" My voice came out strange. Smaller than I meant it to.

Ottoline stepped aside from the doorway.

"Come and see," she said. "Quietly. She startles."

* * *

I have stood in front of charging boars. I have held a bridge against forty men with a broken shield and a bad attitude. I have watched my own company execute a bound girl of fifteen because the dispatches said her gift was "agricultural but persistent," and I did not stop it fast enough, and I will carry that to whatever afterlife I've earned.

None of it prepared me for a nursery.

It wasn't much of one—a cot, a shelf of carved animals, a nightlight that burned without flame, which meant someone down here had a small, careful gift and had been using it for years to keep a child unafraid of the dark. But it was a nursery. Down in the bones of the palace, under the nose of a god-eating parasite, a loyal woman had built a nursery.

The child was awake. Of course she was; we'd made noise, however quietly. She sat up in her cot—a girl, maybe five years old, with a mop of dark hair and enormous solemn eyes—and she looked at us the way children look at strangers: with total, unnerving assessment, as if deciding whether we were worth the trouble of being afraid of.

"This is Princess Ysolde," Ottoline said, and her voice broke on the title, the first crack in all that armor. "Though she answers to Sol. And before you ask—yes. She's the true heir. Last of the line unbroken. The only one the thing upstairs cannot wear, because it tried, Captain. The night she was born, it reached for her, and she pushed it out." A wet, fierce laugh. "A day old, and it couldn't get a grip on her. The queen said she had her father's stubbornness. She had no idea how right she was."

Emrys had gone very still beside me. I knew that stillness. It was the stillness he'd had in the elder's cell when he'd understood what the unbinding would cost him, except this was different—this was the stillness of a man watching a lock he'd been picking for months suddenly swing open.

"The anchor," he said softly. "For the ritual. Maera said it needed a living anchor of the bloodline to hold the vessel steady while the parasite's torn free. I assumed—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I assumed the dying king. I offered myself because I thought the anchor had to carry the gift."

"He does," Ottoline said sharply. "Or she. The anchor must be of the blood. But Emrys Pemberton—if your ritual needs a vessel of the old line, then you are looking at the strongest vessel that line ever produced. The thing upstairs has been starving this family for six generations, and this child is what grows in the gaps when the feeding fails."

The little girl—Sol, Ysolde, heir to a throne she didn't know existed—picked up the one-eared wooden horse and held it out toward Emrys with the gravity of a queen bestowing honors.

"Horse," she said.

Emrys knelt. I watched him take the toy with both hands, watched his face do something it almost never did—lose all its cleverness, all its armor, and go simply, nakedly human.

"Hello, Sol," he said. "I'm Emrys. I think I might be here to help you go home."

She considered this. Then she pointed past him, at me, and said, with the terrifying directness of the very young:

"Why does the lady look sad?"

Because I'm looking at the future, little one, I didn't say. Because three days ago the man kneeling in front of you signed himself over to die so that a kingdom could be free, and until this moment that death bought nothing but the destruction of a monster. And now it buys you. And now I understand why he did it, and understanding makes it worse, because now I have a face to lose him for.

What I said was: "I'm not sad. I'm deciding whether to bow."

Sol frowned, unconvinced. Five years old and already better at reading people than half my company. Gods help the court when she grew into it.

* * *

We sat afterward on the mosaic floor outside the nursery while Ottoline fed the child bread and honey, and Emrys laid it out the way he lays everything out—like contraband on a table, each item weighed, each risk priced.

"So the plan changes," he said. "The unbinding needs two anchors now. Me, for the gift—I pour everything I have into the working. And her, for the bloodline—she holds the king steady, holds the vessel open, so the thing has nowhere to leap when it's torn loose. It can't jump to her. It can't jump to me, because by then there'll be nothing of me left to jump to." He said it lightly. He always said the unbearable things lightly, and I was starting to hate that more than I hated the edict.

"It also means taking a five-year-old through a burning city," I said, "on the same night we're staging a coup, with half the hunt still loyal to the commander and the other half bleeding out in the bell tower. You heard the bells tonight. The thing knows we're moving. It's flushing the city."

"It knows *something's* moving. It doesn't know what." He rubbed his eyes. Behind them, I knew, the visions were already starting—the parasite had begun reaching for him in his sleep, showing him lives he could have had if he'd just kneel. He'd told me that much and then joked about it, and I'd wanted to shake him. "Sera. Look at the alternative. If we unbind the king without an heir ready, the realm tears itself apart before dawn. Every claimant, every general, every hungry duke with a private army. We don't just need to kill the parasite. We need something to put in the empty chair the same hour. She's not a complication to the plan. She *is* the plan."

"I know." I picked at the mosaic coral under my fingers, worn smooth by centuries of knees. "I know that's true. I hate that it's true. She should be running through a garden somewhere, not being marched into a throne room as a lightning rod."

"Nobody should be anything down here," Emrys said quietly. "But she's alive, and she's strong, and somebody loved her enough to keep her that way for five years in the dark. That's not a tragedy, Sera. That's the first good news we've had since this started."

From inside the nursery came a small giggle—Sol had put honey on Ottoline's nose, and the old nurse was pretending outrage with the skill of long practice. Hope, I thought. Everyone talks about hope like it's a feeling. It isn't. It's a face. It's a child with sticky fingers and a one-eared horse, and it's an old woman who kept a secret past all reason, and it's a man with a death sentence in his pocket laughing softly at a joke he thinks I can't hear.

Hope acquires a face, and then you can't pretend anymore that any of this is abstract.

"Emrys." I said it before I could stop myself. "When this is done. After the unbinding. If it works—"

"Don't." Gentle. Final. "Don't make promises contingent on miracles. Neither of us has ever been good at those."

"I wasn't going to promise. I was going to threaten. There's a difference. Threats have consequences."

He looked at me then, really looked, and for a moment the cleverness fell away entirely and there was just Emrys, tired and mortal and mine, or as close to mine as a man gets while he's scheduled to burn himself out like a lamp.

"Threaten away, Captain," he said.

Which is when the bells began—not the hour, not the quarter, but the alarm pattern, all nine towers at once, and beneath them, rising up through the stone like water through a cracked floor, came a sound I had hoped never to hear again: the hunting horns of the Royal Order, sounding the call that meant *prey located*, and the direction they were sounding from was directly above us.

Ottoline appeared in the doorway with Sol wide-eyed in her arms. "There's another way out of these crofts," she said, all business, the knife already back in her belt. "Old servants' stair, comes up in the chapel of the drowned queen. But Captain—" her eyes were grim, "—they're not searching the undercrofts for us. Listen to the horns. Three calls, then silence. That's the pattern for a *found* prize. Someone down here has already been discovered."

Emrys went white. "Maera," he breathed. "The vaults. They're moving her. Tonight. Before we can—"

Above us, the horns sounded again, closer, and the smallest heir in the world tightened her arms around her nurse's neck and asked, in a whisper that carried anyway:

"Are the loud men coming to take my horse?"

"No, my darling," Ottoline said, and met my eyes over the child's head with a look that dared me to make her a liar. "But we're leaving now, and we're leaving fast, and Captain Vane is going to make very sure they don't."
