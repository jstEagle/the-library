## 17. The Night of Long Bells

### Emrys

The bells began at the hour when honest people are asleep and dishonest people are counting their take, and I knew before the third toll that we were too late.

There are bells for weddings. Bells for funerals. Bells for the changing of the watch, rung so faithfully that no one hears them anymore, like a heartbeat you stop noticing because it has always been there. These were none of those. These were the alarm bells of Saint Verity's tower, all nine of them, untuned and overlapping, a sound like the city itself screaming into its cupped hands.

"They know," I said.

Maren—the witch-elder, ninety years old and built like a bundle of kindling that had learned to walk out of spite—did not even slow her shuffle through the undercroft tunnel. "They've known since the moment you set foot in my cell, boy. The question was never whether it knew. The question was whether it would bother to act." She lifted her lantern higher. "It's bothering."

Above us, muffled by three floors of palace stone, something heavy went past. Then another something. Boots—too many boots, moving in that particular synchronized rhythm that means soldiers and not panic. Panic scatters. Soldiers march.

Sera caught up to me in the dark, her hand finding my sleeve without looking, the way she'd found it a hundred times on the road south. Six days ago that gesture had been a memory. Now it was just how she walked beside me.

"The hunt's mobilizing," she said. "I heard the muster horns from the barracks side. Full company. Not a patrol—a purge."

"Against us?"

"Against *everyone*." Her voice had gone flat in the way it did when she was holding something terrible very still so it couldn't move. "When they can't find the traitors, they burn the places traitors hide. And where do gifted people hide, Emrys? In the tanner's quarter. In the river slums. In the houses of every family that ever paid a hedge-witch to charm a fever out of a child." She swallowed. "Every person who ever *liked* magic is guilty tonight."

We came up out of the tunnels through the door Maren had promised us existed—a door behind a wine rack in a cellar house owned, three owners back, by a man who'd owed the elder a debt. The wine racks were still there. The door was still there. The house above it was empty, its family fled or taken, and through its shuttered windows we saw the city.

I have tried, in the years since, to describe what I saw that night, and I have never managed it without lying a little. Fire, yes—but fire is ordinary. Cities burn; it's practically their hobby. What made it wrong was the *orderliness*. The fires started in rows. The hunters moved through the streets in files of six, torching doors marked with white chalk—the mark the Crown's censors used for "gift-tainted household," which as far as I could tell meant any household where someone had once been seen buying candles from the wrong peddler. People ran ahead of the flames carrying children and pots and portraits, and the hunters let them run, because running bodies flush out hiding ones. It was not a riot. It was a harvest.

And over all of it, the bells, ringing and ringing, and beneath the bells—if you had ears like mine, ears that had spent six years listening for the hum of gift in other people—you could hear the thing underneath. The parasite. It wasn't shouting. It was *singing*, low and satisfied, a note that lived in the marrow rather than the air, and every bell in the city was accidentally harmonizing with it.

"It's feeding," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Look at the fires. Look where they're burning. It's not hunting fugitives. It's *harvesting*. Every frightened prayer, every dying witch, every scrap of fear-magic in this city—it's drinking."

Maren nodded slowly, the way a doctor nods at a wound she predicted. "A king who fears death burns his own kingdom to buy one more night. I told you what bound it here. Fear is the mortar. Tonight it's doing masonry."

Sera put her hand on my shoulder—not the road-gesture now, but something harder, an anchor thrown to a man drifting. "Then we go now," she said. "While it's busy eating."

"The throne room," I said. "The ritual. Tonight, or—" I didn't finish, because finishing meant saying *or never*, and never was standing outside those windows with a torch.

We went down through the cellar, out through a coal chute that Maren swore had carried her father's smuggling in another century, and into the streets of a city that had decided, tonight, that people like me were kindling.

I want to tell you I felt brave. What I actually felt was arithmetic. Every alley was a sum: guards plus fire minus time equals dead. I counted exits the way I used to count copper coins in Thornfell, and for the first time in six years I missed being invisible, because invisible men don't have cities burned down looking for them.

Halfway across the Tanner's Row, a hunter patrol rounded the corner ahead of us—six men in grey, torches high—and Sera moved before I could speak. She stepped out of the shadow with her captain's badge held up like a lantern, and the patrol *stopped*. Saluted. One of them, a boy who couldn't have been twenty, asked if she needed escort, and she said, "Counter-intelligence, Vane's detail, you're needed at the granary," and he *believed her*, because authority is mostly costume and confidence, and Sera wore both better than anyone I'd ever known.

She lied to her own company to save me, and then she came back into the dark and her hands were shaking, and she said, "That's the last time that trick works. They'll check the granary within the hour," and I understood that she had just spent the final coin of her old life, and had done it without hesitating, and I loved her so much it felt like a wound.

Maren pressed a hand flat against a wall as we passed, fingers spread, and murmured something in a language older than the kingdom. For one breath, the wall hummed—a ward, some grandmother-charm laid into the stone generations ago by a hedge-witch whose name nobody remembered. The parasite's song stuttered around it, like water around a stone.

"There are more of those than it knows," Maren said quietly. "Every wall in the old quarter. Grandmothers have been quietly defending this city for four hundred years while kings took the credit." She patted the stone like a horse's neck. "Hold, love. We're coming."

We came up on Palace Hill from the servants' side, through a garden where the roses had been cut back for winter and the trellises stood bare as ribs. From there we could see the whole city burning below, beautiful and obscene, and the palace above it, lit gold at every window, untouched, serene, a rich man's face above a riot.

The bells rang on. They rang for hours. Later, people would call it the Night of Long Bells, and they would say it the way you say the names of storms, and the children born the next spring would be named Bellamy and Belora, because grief does that—gets into the naming.

At the garden gate, Sera stopped. Turned to me. Took both my hands.

"When we go in," she said, "whatever happens in that room—I need you to hear me now, while it's still quiet enough to hear things. Whatever it shows you. Whatever it offers. Whatever it wears." Her grip tightened. "You come back out. That's the whole mission. Everything else is decoration."

"That's terrible tactics," I said.

"I'm aware."

"Terrible strategy. No fallback position. No extraction plan."

"Emrys."

"Yes?"

"Come back out."

I kissed her, quick and fierce, tasting smoke, and said, "Yes, Captain," and we opened the gate.

### Sera

I want it recorded—I am writing this in my head, in the grammar of reports I will never file—that I gave the order to open that gate knowing exactly what it cost.

My company was in the city. Forty-one hunters I had trained, drilled, eaten with, bled with. Half of them were down there right now, lighting the fires. Dace was down there—Dace, who had cried when his mother died, who shared his rations with the youngest recruits, who had followed me into the mountains because he believed I was a good officer. He believed in the edict. He believed the gifted were a plague and the Crown was the cure, because I had taught him to believe it, because *I* had believed it, right up until the night I read the sealed dispatches and found out what the cure was actually for.

Now his torches were part of the harvest, and I was climbing the hill to kill the harvester, and somewhere between those two facts my whole life had turned inside out like a pocket.

The palace corridors were wrong. I had walked them a hundred times in six years of guard rotations, and I knew their rhythm the way you know your own pulse. Tonight the rhythm was off. Servants scurried with packed bags. Guards stood at posts they didn't usually stand at, and their faces had the glassy look of men who had been given orders they hadn't dared read twice. And everywhere, faintly, that sound underneath the stones. I didn't have Emrys's ears, but I had spent enough nights near the king's chambers to know the feeling of being watched by something that wasn't the king.

We went up through the east stair, Maren leading, Emrys behind her, me at the rear with a drawn sword and a heart going like a drum corps. At the second landing we hit the first real resistance: four royal guardsmen, halberds down, orders to hold the stair against all comers.

I should say something about what it's like to fight men you trained beside. It's like cutting your own name out of a roster. The first one I disarmed gently—gently, because he was Ferro, who had covered my watch during the fever winter, and I broke his wrist instead of his throat and hated myself for the mercy being so small. Emrys didn't fight at all. He did something with his hands, a gesture like smoothing water, and the remaining three simply *forgot* to be interested in us—stood blinking at a wall while we walked past them. Protective magic. The only kind he'd ever been good at, the kind that had gotten him captured, the kind he was going to spend all of it tonight.

"How much do you have left?" I asked him on the third landing.

"Enough," he said.

"Liar."

"Captain," he said, with that crooked half-smile that had survived chains and cells and a burning city, "you have known for weeks that everything I say before a fight is a lie. It's how I stay calm. Let me have it."

Maren stopped at the top of the stair, before the great double doors of the antechamber, and leaned on her staff, breathing hard, and looked at the two of us with an expression I couldn't parse until later—when I understood it had been farewell. Elders know the shape of nights. She'd known since the cell.

"The vault entrance is behind the throne," she said. "The feeding vaults. If the master lives, she's there. But the binding point—the anchor-stone—is in the throne itself. The ritual must be worked *there*, in front of it, touching it. Which means, children, that you cannot sneak. You must walk in through the front door and do the impossible thing while the impossible thing watches you do it."

"Wonderful," Emrys said. "I was hoping for something understated."

The doors to the throne room were open.

Of course they were open. It wanted us to come. It had always wanted us to come; the whole hunt, the whole edict, the whole burning city was a net drawn tight around one hedge-witch from the mountains, and we had walked into it willingly because the alternative was letting it wear the world until nothing was left. Somewhere below us the bells were still ringing, and I thought: *let them ring. Let them ring until the thing that caused this is ash. Let this city's worst night be the night it got free.*

Down the length of that vast cold hall we walked, our footsteps enormous, the banners of six dead kings hanging motionless overhead, and at the far end, on a throne carved from a single block of black stone that no mason would claim, sat the king.

Or the shape of the king. By now I had seen him enough times to see through him—the stillness that was too complete, the eyes that focused a half-second after they should, the shadow that moved against the candlelight like a thing swimming upstream. He sat with his hands on the armrests the way a coat sits on a hook.

And the throne room was full. Not of courtiers. Of hunters. Two ranks of them, forty strong, my company among them, assembled in the dark beneath the dais with their torches guttering, and at their head, Commander Aldous Rennick, my superior, my mentor, the man who had pinned my captain's badge on my collar himself, holding a scroll bearing the seal I had learned to forge.

He saw me. He saw Emrys. He saw Maren. And he smiled, slow and dreadful, and unrolled the scroll.

"Sera Vane," he read aloud, into the ringing dark, "you are hereby stripped of rank and condemned for treason against the Crown. Your sentence is commuted—" and here his voice did something oily, something that didn't belong to him, I'd swear it on anything holy, "—commuted, if you kneel, and give up the witch, and swear yourself to the Crown's true service. The Crown is generous. The Crown is *hungry*. Choose."

Behind him, the thing in the king leaned forward on its throne, and every torch in the hall bent toward it at once, like grass before wind.

Emrys stepped forward, past me, into the open floor, and I watched him roll up his sleeves like a man preparing to wash dishes, and heard him say, in the wry, tired, absolutely steady voice I had fallen in love with twice:

"Your Majesty. Or whatever's wearing His Majesty. We should talk about the terms of your lease."

The bells below struck the hour again—all nine towers, all at once, long and long and long—and Emrys Pemberton walked toward the black throne with his hands open, and I drew my sword, and turned to face my own company.
