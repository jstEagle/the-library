## 18. The Unbinding

Here is what nobody tells you about walking toward a god: it is exactly like walking toward anything else, except that your knees have opinions.

The throne room of Vael Casterly was built to make a man feel small—forty feet of vaulted ceiling, pillars carved with kings who had all died wearing the same face—and I had spent my whole life avoiding rooms like this. Small rooms. Dark rooms. Rooms where you could be seen. Now here I was, crossing a floor of polished black marble in which I could see my reflection stretched out long and thin beneath my feet, like a man drowning under glass.

Behind me, steel rang against steel. Sera's voice rose over the din—"Hold the line! *Hold it!*"—and then the crash of her company meeting the loyalists at the great doors. I did not look back. Looking back is how you fall down stairs, and falling down stairs in front of an ancient parasite would have been embarrassing for both of us.

The king sat on the black throne and watched me come.

I say *the king*, but by now I knew better than to use that word honestly. What sat there had been Aldric Vael once, forty years ago, before the thing in the crown drank him down to a rind. What sat there now wore his body the way a glove wears a hand: correctly, patiently, with nothing inside. His face was kind. That was the worst part. Some old sculptor's kindness had gotten into the bones of him, and the parasite used it the way a poacher uses a henhouse—it made you want to come closer.

"Emrys Pemberton," it said, and its voice was every voice at once, a choir folded into one throat. My mother's lullabies were in there somewhere. So was Master Ysolde's. It had been eating voices off the wind for six hundred years, and it kept everything. "The hedge-witch's last student. You smell of ash and river water and a woman's stubbornness."

"Funny," I said. "You smell of old coins and something that died in a wall. We're neither of us our best selves tonight."

It laughed. The torches guttered. Somewhere behind me a man screamed, and Sera shouted something I couldn't parse, and I made the choice—the one I'd been making since Thornfell—not to look.

I stopped ten paces from the throne, on a worn circle in the marble that some mason had cut four hundred years ago and some other mason had tiled over and forgotten. Elder Maren had found it in the palace bones, drawn from memory older than memory. *They always build over the binding-stones*, she'd said. *Ashamed of them.* Under my boots, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something answered when I pressed my gift down into the stone.

Good. The stage was set. Now for the performance of a lifetime, starring a smuggler who had never wanted an audience in his life.

"You came to bargain," the parasite said.

"I came to talk terms." I held up my hands, open, empty. "You offered me survival. Servitude. A warm place at the fire while everyone I've ever known burns. Very generous. But I've read your lease, Your Majesty, and I found some problems with it."

"Its eyes"—the king's eyes—"did not blink. "Speak."

So I spoke. And while I spoke, I worked.

That's the trick of hedge-magic, the thing they never teach in towers: it lives in misdirection. A court sorcerer throws fire because he wants you watching the fire. A hedge-witch counts coins behind his teeth and slips the real working in under the patter, quiet as a cutpurse. So I talked, and my mouth was honest enough, and underneath the words my hands moved in small ways—a thumb tracing a line across a knuckle, weight shifting heel to heel, each gesture a stitch in a pattern only I could see, sewing myself to the binding-stone one thread at a time.

*Ysolde's page*. Her final words, which she'd pressed into my palm in the feeding-vaults with fingers light as paper: **What binds can be unbound, but not by force. Force feeds it. You must offer what it cannot take without letting go—you must give it everything, freely, so completely that there is no purchase left. A fist cannot close on smoke.**

She hadn't told me it would hurt. She didn't need to. Masters never do; they figure you'll find out.

"You are afraid," the parasite said. It leaned forward, and the shadows of the hall leaned with it. "Good. Fear has flavor. Six hundred years I have eaten this kingdom's gift, little witch, one gifted soul at a time, and none of them ever tasted anything but terror. You could be different. You could taste of *willingness*."

"Flattering."

"I could show you," it said softly. "Shall I show you what surrender looks like?"

And it reached into my mind.

* *

I want to describe what it did to me, and I can't, not properly, because what it did was show me my life—the life. The one where I never ran from Ysolde's cottage. The one where I stayed.

I stood in the cottage kitchen, twenty years old, flour on my hands, and Sera—Sera at nineteen, before the grey cloak, before any of it—laughed at something I'd said, and her hair was down, and outside the window the mountains went gold with evening, and I had never left, I had *never left*, and there was no edict, no hunt, no burned cottages, just years and years of mornings stretching out ahead like a road with no tolls—

"This is yours," the parasite murmured, wearing the kitchen like a mask. "This is what you bought with your running. Look how cheap it was. One small surrender. Kneel, and have it back."

In the vision, Sera kissed my floury cheek and asked what I was grinning about, and I felt my heart crack along a seam I'd caulked shut six years ago, and I thought—I will be honest, because there's no one left to lie to—I thought about kneeling. Just for a moment. Just to stay in that kitchen forever.

Then I noticed the window.

The mountains outside it were gold, yes. But they were gold the way gilding is gold—painted on, flat, no wind moving the trees, no birds, no weather. The evening never darkened. The bread never cooled. Sera's laugh repeated, note for note, the same laugh, and I understood that the parasite had no idea what joy actually sounded like. It had heard so many people scream that it thought happiness was just screaming played backward.

"No," I said.

The kitchen shattered like sugar-glass.

* *

It did not stop there. Of course it didn't. It showed me my mother alive, my father sober, the debt never called in. It showed me Thornfell green instead of hunted, the passes full of traders instead of ash. It showed me a shop of my own in some gentle town, charms legal and licensed, a sign painted with my own name—which was funny, actually, because I'd spent my whole life making sure my name appeared nowhere, and the thing didn't even know that about me. It knew hunger, not people.

Each vision hit like a wave, and between waves I stitched.

Thumb to knuckle. Weight to heel. The binding-stone sang under my feet now, low and urgent, a plucked string that wouldn't stop. Sweat ran into my eyes. Somewhere far away, steel was still ringing—Sera still holding, gods love her, gods help her—but the sounds came to me down a long tunnel, because most of my mind was occupied staying upright inside a storm of everything I'd ever wanted.

"You are *cruel* to yourself," the parasite said, almost tenderly, between visions. "All this. All this suffering, to keep a spark the size of a candleflame. I have swallowed archmages, Pemberton. Kings. I wore the first king of this realm like a coat because he was too frightened to die. Give me your little flame and I will give you a life worth having."

"There's a flaw in your arithmetic," I managed. My voice came out wrecked. I was proud of it anyway. "You keep offering me things I already decided against."

"Decisions change."

"Mine don't. Ask anyone. It's my worst quality." I wiped blood out of my eye—I'd bitten my tongue somewhere in the third vision and hadn't noticed. "My master taught me sums before she taught me spells. Every charm has a cost. Every coin has two sides. You're showing me one side of a coin that doesn't exist."

The throne room flickered. For half a heartbeat I saw it true: not the grandeur, but the rot underneath—the pillars honeycombed with hollows, the air thick with six hundred years of devoured gift, the whole gorgeous room a stomach. And in the middle of it, the king's body sagging on the throne like laundry, the thing inside him holding him up the way puppet-strings hold up a marionette, barely, fraying.

He was almost empty. The vessel was almost empty. That was why it hunted so hard now, why the pogroms burned the city below—that vast appetite, and so little left to eat.

*Almost empty*, I thought. *But not quite.*

* *

The last vision was the worst one, because it wasn't a lie.

It gave me the true future: the one where I finished the ritual. I saw myself emptied—magicless, half-blind, ordinary as a fencepost—and I saw the years after, gray and charmless, and I saw Sera grow old beside a man who could no longer warm her hands with a trick or find her in the dark with a whisper of light. I saw her carry me, eventually. I saw myself become a burden dressed up as a husband.

"That," the parasite whispered, "is also true."

And it was. That's what broke me open—not the lies, but the truth of it, the price standing there naked with its hand out. My knees buckled. I caught myself on the binding-stone with both palms, and the stone blazed cold up my arms, and I hung there, shaking, a man at the edge of his own annihilation with annihilation offering tea.

*Surrender*, said every tired bone in my body. *You've paid enough. Everyone pays enough.*

And then, from six hundred miles away and twenty years deep, from a burned cottage in the Thornfells, I heard my master's voice—her actual voice, not the parasite's counterfeit, because the counterfeit had never once called me by the name she used:

*"Emrys, boy. Count."*

Count. The first lesson. Before magic, before anything: count. Because a hedge-witch who panics loses track, and a witch who loses track kills someone. Breathe in, count four. Hold, count seven. Out, count eight.

One. Two. Three.

The visions pressed in. The kitchen. The shop. The gray years. Four. Five. Six.

*I am Emrys Pemberton*, I counted. *I run contraband and small charms through the Thornfell passes. I calculate every risk down to the copper coin. And I have already done the sums on this.*

Seven. Eight.

Because here was the sum, the one I'd carried since the vaults, the one I'd hidden even from myself because hope is contraband too: the parasite fed on magic. Fed on gift, on fear, on wanting. It gripped souls the way ivy grips a wall—by finding purchase, by finding *something to hold*. Ysolde's page had told me how to beat it and I'd been too much of a coward to say it plainly, even in my own head, until now, until there was nothing left to be cowardly about.

You don't fight it. Fighting feeds it.

You pour yourself out. Everything. Freely. Until there's nothing left to grip.

A fist cannot close on smoke.

"All right," I said aloud. My voice echoed strangely in the hollowed hall. "You win. You're right. I'm tired, and it's too much, and I surrender."

The parasite stilled. Even the battle at the doors seemed to hold its breath. When it spoke again, its voice had gone soft and slick with triumph, and under the triumph, for the first time, something that might have been hunger getting what it wanted.

"Kneel, then."

"Oh, I'm past kneeling." I spread my hands on the binding-stone and let the last of my shields fall, every wall I'd built in six years of hiding, gone in a breath, my whole self laid open like a ledger. "I'm giving."

And I poured.

* *

I don't remember it as pain. I want to say that clearly, in case anyone ever asks, in case anyone ever has to choose it. It was not pain. Pain has edges, and this had none. It was *emptying*—every charm I'd ever sewn, every secret warmth I'd banked in winter cellars, every stolen moment of light, the whole hoard of a lifetime's small magics, given up all at once and gladly, a river running out of a broken dam, and I laughed while it happened, I think, or wept, or both, because it felt like the night I ran from my apprenticeship and the morning I fell in love and every goodbye I'd ever said, all of them leaving at once.

And the parasite *drank*. Of course it drank. It seized my gift in both its ancient hands and pulled, greedy, gulping, dragging six hundred years of appetite against the flood—

—and found nothing to grip.

Because I wasn't holding anything back. Because there was no *me* in it anymore, no core, no flame tucked safe behind walls for it to seize and squeeze. I was giving it away faster than it could take, offering freely what it could only steal, and a stolen thing has handles but a given thing has none, and it began, for the very first time in six hundred years, to *slip*.

The throne room screamed.

Not the king—the *room*, the stones, the binding-circle flaring white beneath my ruined hands, and through the white I felt it thrashing, the old worm, the crown-leech, hauling itself hand over hand up the tether between me and the vessel it wore, trying to climb *into* me, trying to leap ship into the fresher body—

—and finding, in Emrys Pemberton, nothing left to hold.

Smoke, pouring through a fist. It shrieked—a sound with no throat, a sound like every devoured voice at once remembering it had been swallowed—and it let go of the king to save itself, and the old man's body dropped from the throne like a coat off a hook, and the thing that had ruled this realm for six centuries came apart in the air above the binding-stone, unravelling, unravelled, *gone*.

The white went out.

The silence after was total. Even the battle at the doors had stopped; even the bells below had, for once, nothing to say.

I was on my knees on cold marble. My hands were ordinary hands. The world looked strange—washed-out, dim at the edges, a room seen through fogged glass—and I understood distantly that this was the half-blindness, the price arriving on schedule, and that I should probably be frightened, but mostly I felt light. Terribly, wonderfully light. Like a man who has put down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Somewhere near the doors, boots were running toward me. Grey boots. Hers.

And on the steps of the dais, in the wreckage of the black throne, the old king stirred—drew a breath that was entirely, humanly his own—and turned his clouded ancient eyes on me, and tried to speak.
