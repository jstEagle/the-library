## 19. Coral and Crown

The throne room doors had been carved to look like the mouth of something swallowing, and I suppose that was fitting, because that's exactly what we were walking into.

Sera went first. Of course she did. Six years of loving her and she still hadn't learned to let me take point on anything dangerous, which was either devotion or a very specific insult about my survival instincts. Given what I was about to do with my survival instincts, I couldn't argue.

"Emrys." She stopped at the threshold, grey cloak dark with smoke from the burning city below, her sword already drawn. Behind us, through the shattered windows of the great stair, the bells were still ringing—wrong bells, cracked bells, the parasite screaming through bronze because it had finally understood what we'd come to do. "Say it again."

"You're going to hold the line."

"And you're going to—"

"Pour everything I have into an unbinding my master wrote half of and I'm finishing blind." I tried for a smile. It came out crooked, the way most of my smiles had since the feeding vaults. "You've heard it. Repeating it won't make it better."

"It makes me feel better."

"Then: I'm going to pour everything I have into an unbinding and finish it blind."

She kissed me then, quick and fierce, like a signature on a contract neither of us intended to honor—the contract being that one of us might not walk out. Then she turned to the hunters who'd followed her out of the company, maybe forty of them, ash-streaked and terrified and loyal to *her* now instead of the crown, and raised her voice in that parade-ground register that could strip paint.

"Hold this room until he's done. Whatever comes through those doors—and things will come through those doors—you hold."

* * *

The throne sat on a dais of black marble veined with coral-pink, stone quarried from some drowned reef centuries ago and hauled up here so kings could sit above the memory of the sea. The old king lay slumped in it like laundry. His eyes were open. That was somehow worse than if they hadn't been.

And around him, filling the air like heat-shimmer over summer roadstone, the thing wore his shadow wrong. I'd seen it before through bars, glimpsed it in candleflame. Now, with my gift open and reaching, I saw all of it: a vast coiled absence, older than the dynasty, threaded through the king's veins like ink through water. It had been drinking this man for sixty years. It had been drinking every gifted soul in the realm for longer than that. And it knew why we'd come.

*You,* it said, without sound, without mouth, directly into the marrow of my thoughts. The voice was patient the way glaciers are patient. *The hedge-witch's student. She wrote the words. You carry them.*

"I carry them," I said aloud, because speaking made it real, and real things are harder for monsters to unmake. I knelt beside the dais and pressed my palm flat against the coral-veined stone. Cold. Cold as a grave with pretensions. "And I'm going to use them."

*Your master said the same.* A ripple of something almost like amusement moved through the air, and somewhere below us a whole city block caught fire. *She is nearly empty now. When you fail, I will wear her next. She has such lovely deep places left.*

I thought about Maera's hands around mine in the vault, drained to translucence, pressing the final page into my grip. *Forgive the boy who ran,* she'd whispered. *Now be the man who doesn't.*

I began the ritual.

* * *

Unbinding, it turns out, is not a battle. Battles have strategies, retreats, moments where you catch your breath behind cover. Unbinding is more like holding a door shut against a flood while the flood explains, reasonably, persuasively, why you should let go.

My gift poured out of me like water from a slashed wineskin. Every charm I'd ever hidden in a smuggler's pack, every warming cantrip on cold passes, every small dishonest glamour—I fed it all into the words Maera had written and the words I'd finished, and the coral in the marble began to glow, pink as dawn, pink as the grimoire's cover, and the binding that had held the parasite to the crown for four generations began to fray.

It fought back the only way it knew how. It offered me lives.

I stood in a cottage in Thornfell with snow at the windows and Sera laughing in the doorway, flour on her hands, a child tugging my sleeve asking how charms worked. I stood in a shop in the capital with my name on the sign. I stood at Maera's hearth while she grew old slowly instead of all at once, teaching students who never had to hide. Every vision was warm. Every vision was paid for with one small price: stop. Let go. Let the words die in your throat and live the life you could have had.

"That's the trick, isn't it," I gasped, blood running from my nose, from my ears, my gift guttering like a candle in wind. "You don't break people. You just show them the exit."

*I offer no tricks. Only arithmetic.* The glacier-voice, patient. *Weigh it. One man's gift against a kingdom's peace. You have always been good at arithmetic, smuggler.*

God help me, I hesitated. For one heartbeat—one—one of the visions was so real I could smell the bread.

Then I heard Sera.

Not words. Steel—the long ringing note of her blade meeting another, the barked orders, the wet sounds the world makes when conscience gets bloody. She was holding the line. She was holding it with forty people who should have been anywhere else, and every second they bought me was a second someone was paying for in full.

Arithmetic. Fine. Here was mine: every life the parasite showed me was a life built on its continued existence. Warm cottages, laughing children, all of it mortgaged against other people's souls. I had spent six years doing risk calculations down to the copper coin. This was the largest sum I'd ever run.

I stopped counting what I'd lose and started spending.

* * *

The last of my gift went out of me like tide going out of a bay—all at once, and then in long reluctant pulls, each wave smaller than the last. I could feel the shape of my own magic leaving, the thing I'd hidden with for six years, the warmth in my chest I'd known since childhood. It didn't hurt exactly. It was worse than hurting. It was *leaving*, the way a name leaves a room when everyone stops saying it.

And the binding tore.

The parasite came off the king the way a leech comes off skin—reluctantly, furiously, with a scream that wasn't sound but pressure, shattering every window in the throne room, dropping three of Sera's hunters where they stood. It rose above the dais, vast and eyeless and starving, its worn-out vessel collapsing beneath it, and it looked for a new handhold the way any drowning thing does.

It found me.

Of course it found me. I was kneeling in the center of a circle of my own spent power, wide open, empty-handed, the nearest living thing with a drop of gift left in it. I felt it descend toward me like weather, like winter, like the moment before a fall finishes.

And then—

Nothing.

It scrabbled at me. I felt it, actually felt it, claws of pure hunger raking across the place where my gift used to live, searching for purchase, for a thread, for one warm ember to wrap itself around.

There was nothing there.

I had not held anything back. Not a spark, not a secret cantrip, not the smuggler's last copper hidden in a boot heel. Everything I had ever been able to do, I had put into the unbinding, because I had done the arithmetic honestly for once in my life. The parasite gripped at me and found a hollow man, ordinary as bread, and there was nothing in me for it to wear.

*What are you?* it shrieked, and for the first time in four generations, the glacier-voice sounded afraid.

"A smuggler," I said. My voice came out wrecked, barely there. "We know when a cargo's not worth carrying."

Sera's sword took it from behind.

Not her alone—half her surviving hunters drove their blades into it at once, steel blessed by the witch-elder's hasty blessings, and a creature that had fed on magic for centuries discovered that plain honest iron, swung by plain honest people, asks no permission and holds no gift to devour. It came apart like smoke in wind, like ash off a burned cottage, like every invisible thing I'd ever been, unraveling into nothing at all.

The silence afterward was enormous.

* * *

I don't remember falling, but I was on the floor, and Sera was there, and the coral in the marble had gone dark and ordinary, just stone again. Through the broken windows the bells had stopped. All of them. Even the wrong ones.

The old king stirred on his throne. Just a little. Enough.

Sixty years a passenger in his own body, and in his last minutes he got it back. His eyes found mine—his own eyes, human and terrified and grateful, eyes that hadn't been his own since before I was born. He tried to speak. Sera leaned close, sword still drawn, tears cutting tracks through the ash on her face.

"Thank—" the king whispered. The rest was breath.

He died human. Whatever else that room had cost, it bought him that.

Sera gathered me up off the floor, and I wanted to tell her I could stand, but the truth was I couldn't see much anymore. The world had gone soft at the edges, colors bleeding like wet ink, her face a bright blur above mine. The price list, itemized: my gift, most of my sight, probably a few years. Paid in full.

"Sera," I managed. "Did we—"

"We won," she said, and her voice broke on it, and she held me like she was never letting go, and somewhere beyond the ruined doors the city was quiet for the first time in days.

"Emrys," she whispered into my hair. "Emrys, open your eyes. There's something you need to see."

I opened them as far as they would go, which wasn't far, and through the blur I watched her lift something from behind the throne—a small bundle, wrapped in a nurse's shawl, stirring with a sound I knew from no vision the parasite had ever shown me, because it had never bothered to imagine a future it couldn't eat.

A child. Alive. Crying fit for a kingdom.
