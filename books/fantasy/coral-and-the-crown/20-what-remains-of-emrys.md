## 20. What Remains of Emrys

I have stood in a great many ruined rooms in my life. Burned cottages. A chapel that had been shelled by its own congregation. The inside of my father's tavern the night he learned what my mother's family really was. Ruin is honest work; it tells you exactly what happened and asks nothing back.

The throne room of Vaelcrest was not like that.

It was ruined *wrong*. The stone hadn't broken so much as *given up*—slabs sagging like wet bread, gold leaf on the pillars gone grey as old ash, as if color itself had been drunk out of the room over two hundred years and only now remembered how to leave. The great windows had blown inward, and the glass lay across the floor in drifts, catching the dawn light and throwing it up in little cruel sparks. Somewhere under all of it was a king. Somewhere under all of it was Emrys.

And somewhere in me was the specific, screaming quiet of a woman who has just watched the man she loves pour himself out like a jug and doesn't yet know if anything's left in him.

I got to him before I decided to move. That's the truth of it. My body went and my mind stayed standing at the door with the hunters at my back, still holding the line against Commander Aldric's loyalists, still hearing the ring of steel and the wet sounds that come after it. My knees found the rubble. My hands found his shoulders. Everything else—rank, oath, the eleven years I'd spent being exactly what the crown needed—stayed at the door and did not follow me in.

"Emrys." His name came out wrong, half-swallowed. I said it again, properly. "Emrys. Look at me."

He tried. That was the first thing I understood, and it nearly broke me: he *tried*, and his eyes wouldn't obey. They wandered past my face, past the shattered throne, fixed on some middle distance where nothing was, tracking nothing, seeing—I would learn later—a world gone soft at every edge, like a painting left out in the rain.

"I can't," he said. His voice was scraped down to the grain. "Sera. I can't see you."

"You're looking straight at me."

"I know." And then he laughed—one breath of it, cracked down the middle—and turned his face toward my voice the way a man turns toward a fire. "That's the joke, isn't it. Six years of hiding where nobody could see me, and now I can't see anybody. The mountain's got a sense of humor."

"Don't," I said. "Don't do the arithmetic thing. Not now."

"It's not arithmetic. It's inventory." His hand came up, clumsy, and found my wrist, and closed around it with a grip that had no magic left in it at all—just a smuggler's strength, just a man's hand, shaking. "Let me take stock while I still remember how. One captain. Still breathing. Good start. One grimoire—" his face crumpled, just slightly, "—one master?"

"Alive." I caught his hand before the question could finish forming. "She's alive, Emrys. Breathing. Asleep, maybe for days, but the healers say her heart's steady. She gave you everything she had left and she's *alive*."

He exhaled then, long and unsteady, and something in his chest seemed to unlock. He'd carried that guilt six years—the boy who ran from the cottage, from her, from the apprenticeship, from her love, because he thought running was the same as keeping her safe. I'd watched him carry it down the whole length of this story. Watching him set even a corner of it down was worth more to me than the crown we'd just saved.

"And the king?" he asked.

I looked over my shoulder.

* * *

The old king lay three steps below his own throne, which felt right in a way I couldn't have explained without swearing. He was small. That was the thing no portrait ever told you and no courtier dared whisper: Hollis the Fourth, the Gilded, the Long-Ruling, was a small man, and he had been made smaller by the thing that wore him, folded up inside his own body like a letter in an envelope no one opened for sixty years.

But his eyes were open. And they were *his*.

I had stood guard at audiences. I had seen those eyes a hundred times and never once seen anyone home behind them—just the deep, patient dark of the parasite looking out through a man's face, using his mouth, spending his years. Now the dark was gone. What was left was watery, frightened, ancient, and unmistakably human. A man waking in a room where everything he owned had been sold off while he slept.

"He's dying," Emrys said quietly. He couldn't see the king, but he could hear it—the rattle in the old man's breath, the way each inhale sounded borrowed. "Isn't he. The binding held him together as much as it held the creature. Take one away..."

"Take one away and both go," I said. "Yes."

"Then help me up."

"You can't walk."

"I can lean," he said, with the particular stubbornness of a man who has already spent everything and therefore cannot be billed again. "Help me up, Sera. If he's going to die human, someone should be there when he does. Someone should let him know it worked. Do you know what sixty years in your own body feels like, locked behind your own eyes? Neither do I. But I know what six years of hiding felt like, and I wouldn't wish the long version on anyone."

So I helped him up, and we crossed the ruin together—him leaning on my arm, blind, stepping carefully through the glass because I told him where it was, the two of us moving like a very bad three-legged soldier—and we knelt, both of us, in the wreckage of the gilded cage, beside the smallest king in the history of the realm.

His hand moved. It took visible effort, like a man lifting a beam. I took it. It was cold and light as a bird's.

"Captain Vane," he whispered. Of course he knew my name. The parasite had known everyone's name; apparently some of it had soaked into the shell. Or perhaps he'd simply watched me through his own eyes for years, a prisoner studying his jailers. "You refused the order."

"Yes, Majesty."

"Good girl." A ghost of humor moved across his face. "Terrible courtier. Excellent officer." His gaze slid past me, searching, and found Emrys by sound alone—or maybe by whatever faint warmth a hollowed man can feel when real magic has recently passed through a room. "And you. The hedge-witch from Thornfell."

"Hedge-mage," Emrys said. "Witch implies I charge properly."

The old king laughed. It cost him. I heard the price of it in his chest, and I saw Emrys flinch at the sound, because Emrys hears prices the way other men hear music.

"What was your name," the king whispered. "Before. When you were small. Before they taught you to hide it."

"Emrys Pemberton."

"Pemberton." He tasted it. "There were Pembertons in the valley records. Charmsmiths. Candle-blessers. Little magics." His fingers tightened on mine, or tried to. "Little magics. That's what it feared most, did you know? Not the archmages. Not the battle-craft. Those it could drink slowly, over years. But the little magics—blessings on doorways, charms against fever, luck stitched into a hem—it couldn't digest them. Kindness doesn't break down. So it ordered them burned first." His breath hitched. "Sixty years I watched it do that. In my name. With my seal. And I screamed, and no one heard, because my scream was happening somewhere the creature had already eaten."

Emrys bowed his head. "It's done," he said. "Whatever it cost—it's done. Your line isn't finished. There's a child, Majesty. A granddaughter. She survived. She's upstairs right now, asleep in a nurse's arms, and tomorrow morning she'll be crowned, and there will be nothing wearing the crown but a little girl who likes honey cakes."

I felt the change go through the old man's body before I saw it in his face. Something loosened. Something that had been clenched for sixty years, some fist deep in him that even the parasite hadn't fully pried open, and it opened now, all at once, around the shape of a child he had never been allowed to know existed.

"A granddaughter," he said.

"Elira," I said. "Named for her grandmother."

Tears ran sideways into the king's hair. He didn't wipe them. I don't think he remembered how; I think he'd forgotten crying was allowed.

"Then thank you," he whispered—to Emrys, to me, to the room, to whatever gods had finally bothered to look in. "Thank you. I can go now. I've been waiting so long to go, and it wouldn't let me, and now—" His voice thinned to thread. "Now I'm only a man. Only a tired old man. It's wonderful. You can't imagine. It's *wonderful*."

He died the way snow stops falling—not all at once, but in a slow settling, a softness arriving, until the hand in mine weighed what a hand should weigh and no more. The rattling breath went out of him between one moment and the next, and the room did not shake, and no bell rang, and the most powerful death in the history of Vaelcrest happened in total silence, in the arms of a hunt captain and a smuggler, on a floor full of glass.

Emrys felt it anyway. Blind as he was, he felt it. "He's gone," he said.

"He's gone."

"Human," Emrys said, and his voice broke on the word, and I pulled him in and let him break against my shoulder, and I did not do the arithmetic either, and I did not take inventory, and for a long time neither of us was any use to the realm at all.

* * *

They came for us eventually, of course. The realm always comes.

Bram Tullow found us first—he and the eleven hunters who'd stood the line with me against Aldric's loyalists, bloodied and singed and unable to quite believe they were still alive. Bram took one look at the dead king, one look at me kneeling in the glass with Emrys Pemberton's head on my shoulder, and did the single wisest thing I have ever seen him do: he turned around, posted two men at the doors, and told everyone else the scene was sealed pending the Protector's inspection.

Protector. That word was already loose in the palace, running ahead of me like fire down a fuse. Wren had gotten the child to safety through the undercrofts; the elder witch Maren had walked out of her cell under her own power, terrifying several medics on the way; and somewhere in the chaos of the night, someone—probably Wren, probably with Maren coaching her—had begun telling anyone with ears that Captain Sera Vane had broken the Hunt, killed the parasite, and saved the heir. None of it was entirely true. All of it was more true than the alternative stories. By dawn it would be law.

"Can you stand?" I asked Emrys, when the bells began—the good bells this time, the coronation bells being tested, their bright voices rolling over a city that smelled of smoke and was, impossibly, still standing.

"I can lean," he said again, and smiled, and it was the old smile, the ford-river smile, except that now when he aimed it at me he aimed it slightly to the left of my actual face, and my heart did something complicated and painful about it.

"Your eyes," I said. "The healers want to look at you. Maren says the gift doesn't just sit in the blood—it threads through everything, nerves, senses. You spent all of yours. Every last drop. She says some of the damage might knit back. Some might not."

"Some," he repeated, tasting the word the way the king had tasted *Pemberton*. "Huh." He was quiet a moment. Then, wry as ever, quieter than ever: "Do you know what I keep thinking about? The passes. I mapped every smuggler's road in Thornfell by feel, by starlight, by charm-light I wasn't supposed to be using. I could walk them blindfolded. I used to say that as a boast." He laughed softly. "Turns out I was just early."

"Emrys—"

"Sera." He turned toward my voice, precise as a compass needle finding north, and reached up and touched my jaw with two fingers, mapping me the way he used to map mountainsides. "Listen to me, because I can't see whether you're listening, so you'll have to tell me. I am not going to mourn this in front of you. Later, privately, into a bottle, absolutely. But not here, not today, not in front of the child who inherits a kingdom because I stopped being special." His thumb traced my cheekbone. "I bought a future with the only coin I had. I'd make the trade again before you finished asking. So don't you dare look at me like a casualty. Look at me like a man who just paid off a debt."

"That's not how grief works."

"No," he admitted. "But it's how *I* work, and you're stuck with me now, so you'd better learn the manual."

"Stuck with you," I said. "Is that a proposal, Pemberton? Because I've had proposals from dukes, and none of them phrased it as a hostage situation."

"They were dukes. They had options." His fingers curled lightly against my cheek. "I'm a blind smuggler with a dead trade, a stolen name, and one extremely dangerous woman. I have no options. I have only this. Marry me badly, Sera. Small wedding, bad wine, everyone we trust in one room, which will take four chairs."

I laughed. It came out wrecked, half sob, and I didn't care. Eleven years I had been the crown's instrument—precise, obedient, hollowed in my own quieter way—and this ridiculous, brave, half-blind man was the first thing in my entire adult life I had chosen freely, and he was asking me to keep choosing him, and the bells were ringing over a free city, and I said yes before he'd finished the sentence, which he claimed afterward was cheating.

* * *

We watched the coronation from the gallery, because Emrys refused the front row—"I unmade a throne, I'm not sitting near one"—and because I wanted the child to see ordinary faces in the crowd, not just bowing ones.

Elira, Crown of Vaelcrest, four years old, walked the length of the nave holding Wren's hand and sucking her thumb, and when the circlet was lowered onto her head she announced, clearly, to the whole assembly, that it was *itchy*, and the entire kingdom fell in love with her at once. Bram cried. Maren did not cry, but she gripped her staff hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and I saw her lips moving—some old blessing, some little magic, the kind the parasite had burned first and could never digest.

Amnesty was read aloud. Every gifted soul in the realm, pardoned. The edicts struck from the books. The Hunt dissolved, its records sealed, its commander's fate left to a tribunal I intended to attend personally.

And afterward, in the corridor outside the hall, a nurse pressed a small folded paper into Emrys's hand—a note in a spidery hand he identified instantly, blindly, by touch and by love, as his master's. He couldn't read it himself. His eyes slid off the ink like rain off slate.

"Read it to me," he said, and handed it to me, and trusted me with it.

I unfolded it. Three lines, in a hand that shook:

*"The sight may return. It may not. Either way, boy—you owe me a visit, and the cottage owes you a roof, and Thornfell owes us both a spring planting.*

*Come home. Bring her.*

*—M."*

Emrys stood very still in the coronation-day light, blind, magicless, smiling like a man who has just been handed the deed to everything.

"Well," he said. "I suppose we'd better start walking."
