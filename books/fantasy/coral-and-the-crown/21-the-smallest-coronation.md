## 21. The Smallest Coronation

### Sera

The crown was too big for her.

That was the first thing I noticed, standing in the antechamber of the old chapel with the regalia laid out on a table like instruments of surgery, and I confess my first thought—shameful, unprofessional, and entirely true—was that we could have saved ourselves a great deal of trouble by commissioning a smaller crown.

The second thing I noticed was that the child wearing it did not seem to mind.

Her name was Alenna. She was four years old, maybe five, with a cowlick at the crown of her head that no amount of royal combing had subdued, and she had spent the last three days in the undercrofts of the palace being fed honeyed bread by a witch-elder who smelled of woodsmoke and grudges. She had watched soldiers die in the corridors above her hiding place. She had watched me carry her out through smoke. And now she stood on a step stool so she could reach the anointing rail, gripping it with both hands, and asked me—in the grave, carrying whisper children use when they believe they are being very quiet—"Captain Vane, is the crown going to fall off?"

"It's pinned," I said. "I checked."

"Did you check twice?"

"Twice."

She nodded, satisfied, and turned back to face the chapel.

There were perhaps two hundred people inside. That number would have been a scandal a month ago—a coronation witnessed by two hundred souls instead of ten thousand—but after the Night of Long Bells, after the fires in the Tanner's Quarter and the bells that rang until they cracked, no one had the appetite for spectacle. The city was still smelling its own smoke. The city wanted, more than anything, to be told it could stop holding its breath.

So we gave them a small ceremony in a small chapel, and I think it may have been the only coronation in the history of this kingdom where the congregation wept more than the monarch.

Alenna could not have wept if she'd wanted to. She was too busy being solemn. Someone—I never learned who, though I have my suspicions about a certain former hedge-witch—had taught her that queens do not fidget, and she took the instruction with the ferocious seriousness of a soldier taking a last order from a dying commander. She stood through the anointing without blinking. She held the scepter, which weighed nearly as much as she did, with both hands like a farmer hauling a bucket from a deep well. When the Lord Chancellor read the proclamation—the true one, the one we had pulled from the parasite's own sealed dispatches, attesting to her blood and her survival—she listened as though she understood every word, which I suspect she did not, and which I suspect she will one day pretend she did.

And then Chancellor Ovidre said the words I had been dreading for six days, because he said them looking directly at me.

"—and Her Majesty's first act, before God and this company, is to name her Protector of the Realm: Captain Sera Vane of the Royal Hunt, who kept faith when faith was fatal."

Two hundred people turned to look at me.

I want it recorded, somewhere, in whatever ledger the gods keep of my embarrassments, that I did not want this. I had spent eleven years learning to follow orders, and then three weeks learning to break them, and the sum total of that education was a woman who trusted her own judgment about as far as she could throw a horse. A protector is a person who decides. A protector is a person the realm looks at when the next hard thing comes, and there is always a next hard thing—that much I knew down in my boots.

But there was a child on a step stool wearing a crown pinned on twice, and there were two hundred people who had buried neighbors last week, and there was a man in the third row who caught my eye at exactly the wrong moment and smiled at me the way he used to smile across a smuggler's campfire, and so what could I do? I knelt. I swore. My voice held, mostly.

Alenna dismissed me afterward with a nod so regal it nearly undid me, and then immediately ruined the effect by asking if the crown could come off now, because it was itchy.

"Five more minutes," said the Chancellor.

"That's what you said five minutes ago," said the Queen of Thornfell, and the whole chapel laughed, and I felt something in the city's chest loosen—one rib at a time, like a fist unclenching after too long a fight.

* * *

The amnesty was read on the chapel steps, because Alenna insisted the people outside should hear it too, and there were hundreds of them now, spilling into the square, drawn by word of mouth the way water finds a crack in a wall. The Chancellor read it in his dry parchment voice: every gifted soul in the realm pardoned, every hunt warrant voided, every record of the gift-registries to be burned publicly within the fortnight.

The sound the crowd made was not a cheer. I've heard cheers. Cheers are sharp and quick and gone. This was lower, longer—a sound like a building settling, like ice going out of a river. It went on so long that Alenna tugged the Chancellor's sleeve and whispered, audibly, "Are they broken?"

"No, Majesty," he said, and for once the old man's voice wavered. "I believe they're mending."

I scanned the crowd while he spoke—an old habit, and one I am in no hurry to lose—and found the faces I was looking for. Witches from the fugitive camps, gaunt and disbelieving, holding each other's sleeves as if the words might blow away. Hunters, my hunters, the forty-one who stood with me against Commander Hale's company, standing awkwardly among the people they'd been ordered to burn, nobody quite sure yet how to be forgiven or to forgive. The elder Marenna, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman reserving judgment for at least a decade.

And Emrys Pemberton, in the third row, ordinary as bread, watching a child be crowned with an expression I couldn't name until later, when I had time to sit with it.

It was relief. It was grief. It was a man watching a door close behind him and finding, to his astonishment, that the room on this side was larger than the one he'd left.

### Emrys

Here is what nobody tells you about being ordinary: it's quiet.

Not outside quiet. The square was anything but quiet—bells (new ones, the cracked ones having been replaced overnight by bell-founders working with a fervor I can only describe as patriotic panic), crowds, a Chancellor reading proclamations, a queen asking whether the crowd was broken. Inside my skull, though, for the first time since I was nine years old, there was nothing. No hum. No itch at the base of my thoughts where the gift used to live, that low constant awareness of the world's seams. Just silence, and me in it, like a man standing in a room after the furniture's been carried out.

I kept waiting to miss it. I'm still waiting. Ask me again in a year.

What I felt instead, standing in the third row between a tanner and a grandmother who kept elbowing me to see better, was something I hadn't felt since before my apprenticeship: I fit. Not the way magic made me fit—magic made everything fit too easily, made the world a lock and me a key, which sounds lovely until you learn what else has keys. This was plainer. I was a man in a crowd, watching a coronation, with sore feet and no opinions from the universe. The grandmother elbowed me again. I let her.

Sera knelt on the chapel steps and swore her oath, and I'll admit I watched her and not the queen, because I have priorities. She wore her grey captain's coat with the hunt insignia torn off—not replaced yet, just torn, the threads still frayed at the shoulder, because some things shouldn't be mended too quickly—and when she rose, the crowd made that long settling sound, and I thought: *she has no idea what she's just agreed to.* Then I thought: *yes she does. She knows exactly. She volunteered anyway.* That's the difference between us, or one of them. I calculated risks down to the copper coin for six years. She walks into rooms full of knives and starts rearranging the furniture.

The amnesty came next, and I confess I didn't listen to the words. I watched the faces. There was a woman near the front—a hedge-healer from the river villages, I'd seen her in the fugitive camps, hands like tree roots—who heard the pardon and sat down right where she stood, on the cobblestones, and put her face in her hands, and the people around her formed a little wall without being asked, so no one would stare. That was the moment the kingdom exhaled, if you want to know. Not the proclamation. The wall of strangers around a tired woman.

Afterward the square emptied slowly, the way a wound stops bleeding—reluctantly, in stages. I hung back, because hanging back is the one skill six years of smuggling left me with intact, and because I wanted to watch Sera work. She moved through the dispersing crowd like a shepherd who distrusts her own dog: directing hunters here, receiving a bow from a baker there, kneeling once to speak to a child at eye level. Protector of the Realm. She still flinched slightly whenever anyone used the title, a small twitch at the corner of her mouth, as if the words were hot.

When the square was nearly empty she found me, because of course she did. She always found me, even in the years when neither of us was looking.

"Well," she said, stopping in front of me, arms crossed. "Ordinary."

"Painfully."

"How does it feel?"

I considered the question honestly, which is more than I usually manage. "Like taking off boots you forgot you were wearing. You keep flexing your toes, surprised they're yours."

She snorted. "Poetic. Six years of contraband charm-work and your finest metaphor is footwear."

"I'm out of practice. All my good material required magic."

The joke landed the way our jokes landed now—with a half-second pause where the grief lived, and then past it. She uncrossed her arms. "The Queen wants to meet you. Formally. She's been asking."

"The Queen wants to meet *me*?"

"She asked Marenna if you were 'the one who broke the bad thing.' Marenna said yes. Alenna said—" Sera's mouth twitched, fighting the smile and losing, "—she said, 'Good. He looked tired.'"

"I *was* tired. I poured my entire soul into an unbinding ritual. Tired is the minimum available response."

"You're invited to the palace tonight. Dinner. Small one. Just the household." She paused, and the pause had weight in it, deliberate weight, the kind she deployed when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. "Emrys. The grimoire. Your master's. The Chancellery wants it—for the archives, for teaching. But it's yours by rights. Hers by rights. If you want to take it to Thornfell, rebuild the cottage, no one will stop you. No one will *follow* you." A beat. "That's an offer, not an order. I'm done with orders."

I looked at her. Grey coat, frayed shoulder, eyes that had watched me nearly die and had decided, apparently, to stand there and watch me live instead. Six years ago I ran from her because staying meant being known, and being known meant being lost. I had run out of reasons. I had run out of magic, even, which had been my last excuse.

"Thornfell," I said. "In spring. The passes are death in winter, and I've done enough dying for one reign."

Something came off her shoulders then—I saw it go, actually saw it, like snow sliding from a bough. "Spring," she agreed. "I know a captain who owes me a favor. Fast horses. Good escort."

"Expensive."

"For you? Free. Protector's privilege."

Behind her, at the chapel doors, a very small queen in a very large crown was being handed down the steps by the Chancellor, and she spotted us—spotted me—and pointed with the imperious certainty of absolute monarchy, and said, in a carrying whisper that reached every corner of the emptying square:

"*That's* the tired one. He's coming to dinner."

The grandmother beside me elbowed me in the ribs one last time, for luck, or for love, or because grandmothers answer to no crown. And I walked toward the palace—no charms in my pockets, no grimoire under my arm yet, no seams in the world humming my name—just a man, a road ahead of him, and a woman falling into step beside him like she'd been walking it all along.
