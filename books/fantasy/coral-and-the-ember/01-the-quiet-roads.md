## 1. The Quiet Roads

The ravens found Casper before dawn, the way they always did.

*Two-legs, two-legs, walking cold,* croaked the one they called Broken-Beak, hopping along a granite ledge above the switchback trail. *Walking slow. Why so slow?*

"Because I'm not alone," Casper murmured, barely moving their lips. Ahead of them, the smuggler's caravan wound down the narrow pass in a line of mules and muffled lanterns—nine men, four wagons, and enough untaxed brandy to hang every soul in it if the customs riders caught them. "Because I have to watch the road. Because some of us have jobs."

*Jobs,* Broken-Beak said, with the deep contempt only a raven can manage. *I eat eyes. What is your job?*

"Eyes," Casper said. "That's rather the point."

The raven rattled its feathers, pleased despite itself, and lifted off into the grey dark. Casper watched it go and felt the familiar ache settle back into place—the one that lived just beneath the contentment, like a stone under a bedroll.

This was the good part of their life, and they knew it. The Thornfells at first light, when the peaks went pink and the pines breathed out mist, and the whole world smelled of cold stone and juniper. The mule train jingling below. Old Marta humming off-key on the lead wagon. A purse that would be heavy by month's end, and no one asking Casper Eriksen a single question they couldn't answer.

And all of it shadowed by the thing they could never set down: the knowing.

Because Casper spoke to beasts. Had spoken to them for as long as they could remember—before they had words for it, before they understood that other children couldn't hear the barn cats complaining about the dog or the plow horses grumbling about the mud. It was as natural as breathing and twice as dangerous. The crown's priests called it an abomination. The king's edicts, read aloud in every market square, called it a crime against the natural order, punishable by fire.

So Casper had learned silence the way other people learned letters. They kept their lips still on the road. They answered the mules' complaints with a hand on a velvety nose instead of words, which satisfied the mules well enough—they mostly wanted acknowledgment anyway. They let the ravens do the scouting and never told the crew why Casper always seemed to know about the washed-out bridge two miles ahead, or the customs patrol camped in the valley, or the rockslide that had closed the high trail last week.

"We go around," they'd said that morning, tapping the map. "Landslip past the saddle."

"How d'you know?" Bram, the youngest hand, always asked. Bram asked everything.

"I don't. I guess. Ravens were making a fuss."

Half true. The ravens *had* made a fuss—but the fuss had been words, actual words, a detailed account of the slide and a complaint about the disruption to the carrion trade. You couldn't say that aloud. Not to Bram, who was decent but chatty. Not to Marta, who paid fair wages but would not burn for her guide's secrets. Not to anyone.

A life half-hidden, Casper thought, is only half-lived. Their grandmother had said something like that once, back before the edicts hardened and the fires started. She'd said it sadly, in the kitchen, while the neighbors sang hymns against the beast-speakers three doors down. Casper had been eight. They'd understood it completely by twelve, when the tinker's boy from the next village vanished, and everyone pretended not to know why.

"Casper!" Marta's voice, gravel and pipe-smoke. "What's the goat doing?"

Casper looked up. One of the wild mountain goats that haunted these slopes stood on an outcrop directly above the trail, stamping and bleating—not ordinary bleating, but the sharp triple-call that meant *danger, danger, come away.*

"What goats do," Casper said, shrugging. But they were already reading the slope, the scree, the wind direction.

*Not danger-danger,* the goat clarified, annoyed at being misread. *Man-danger. Smell of iron and dogs. Below the second bend. Many legs.*

"How many?"

*More than you have fingers. Fewer than the stars. Dogs are tired. Men are not.*

"They're coming up from the second bend," Casper called down. "Patrol. We should take the goat track east and drop into Hollow Vale."

Marta squinted at the goat, then at Casper, then at the track—a thin scar of a path that only a fool or a local would trust with wagons. "That track won't hold the wheels."

"It'll hold them better than a noose will." Casper kept their voice easy, bored even. "Goats don't spook for nothing."

It was the closest they could come to the truth, and it worked, because it always worked. Goats didn't spook for nothing. Marta swore and turned the caravan east, and twenty minutes later, from the heights of the goat track, they all watched the patrol file through the bend below—sixteen riders, black-and-gold livery, hounds straining at leads.

Black-and-gold. Not customs colors. Customs wore green.

Casper felt the small hairs rise along their arms. The king's hunters wore black-and-gold. The ones the ballads called the Ashcloaks, though nobody said that name above a whisper anymore. Customs riders chased brandy. Ashcloaks chased *people.*

"Hunters," Marta muttered, watching them pass. "Way out here? Nothing out here but goats and us."

"Maybe they like goats," someone joked, weakly.

Nobody laughed. The caravan moved on in silence, and Casper walked at the rear with their heart beating too fast, telling themselves it meant nothing. Patrols ranged where they liked. It wasn't about them. It was never about them. That was the whole architecture of their life—invisibility, layer upon layer, careful as masonry—and it had held for twenty-six years.

They told the goat thank you, later, when the wagons halted at noon in Hollow Vale. They scratched its coarse jaw and slipped it a heel of bread.

*You smell afraid,* the goat observed, chewing. *Still.*

"Professional habit."

*Liar,* said the goat comfortably, and wandered off to be right somewhere else.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow ringed with pine, fires low, watches set. And this was Casper's favorite hour, though they'd have died before admitting it: the camp settling, men gambling quietly by the coals, and the forest leaning in around the firelight, full of voices.

A fox came to the edge of the light, sleek and red, and sat down with the patience of a creature who has done business with this particular two-legs before.

*You owe me,* said the fox. It was an old argument.

"I paid you. Two eggs, midsummer."

*One egg was rotten.*

"One egg was *ripe.*"

*Rotten,* said the fox, with finality, and Casper sighed and dug out a strip of dried venison from their pack. The fox took it with surgical delicacy and sat back to eat, tail curled over its feet, and for a few minutes Casper simply sat with it, shoulder-to-shoulder almost, in the warm circle of firelight. This was the warmth. This was the thing that kept the loneliness from going septic. The animals didn't care what you were. They cared whether you were honest, whether you shared, whether you listened. By those measures Casper Eriksen was the best person in any room they'd ever entered, and no human being alive knew it.

"You're smiling," said Bram, dropping onto the log beside them with his bowl of barley. "At nothing. Again."

"At the fox."

Bram glanced at the shadows beyond the fire, saw nothing, and grinned. "You've got a way with creatures, Casper, I'll give you that. Mules love you. Even that half-dead pack pony perked up when you touched it. My gran would've said you're blessed."

*Your gran would've said more than that,* Casper thought. Aloud: "Country upbringing."

"Mm." Bram slurped his barley, then lowered his voice. "You hear what happened at Millbrook? Down in the vale?"

"No."

"Folk say a speaker was found there. Beast-tongue, like in the old stories." He said it the way you'd say *plague.* "Ashcloaks came through a fortnight back. Whole family gone. House burned. My cousin saw the smoke from the ridge." He shook his head. "Mad world. Imagine hiding a thing like that in your own house, your own blood, and never knowing till the black-and-gold come up your lane."

"Yeah," Casper said. "Imagine."

They sat a moment in the firelight, the lie sitting between them like a third man, invisible and patient. Then Bram finished his bowl and went to the dice game, and Casper stayed by the fire and let the cold in slowly, the way you let cold in when you need it to think clearly.

They had rules. Twenty-six years of rules. Never speak where ears might reach. Never look too long at an animal in company. Never, ever let anyone see you *listening*—that tilted head, that sudden stillness; the Ashcloaks trained their hounds on exactly that stillness, the smugglers said. Keep moving. Keep useful. Be the quiet one, the strange one, the one nobody bothers to learn anything about, because nobody burns a stranger.

It had worked. It had kept them alive and fed and free under open sky, and there were worse lives. Most nights they believed it.

Tonight, watching the fox melt away into the dark and feeling the old stone shift under the contentment, they believed it less.

*Lonely,* said a voice near the ground, and Casper looked down to find the camp cat—one of the half-wild mousers that traveled with the wagons—regarding them with unblinking amber eyes. Cats heard everything, even unspoken things sometimes, which was why Casper avoided cats in towns.

"I'm fine," Casper whispered.

*Cats don't ask twice,* said the cat, and left it at that, and somehow that was the worst kindness anyone had offered Casper in years.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, as always, and the ravens came back, as always, and for six hours the road was kind. Then, cresting the long ridge above Lowmark, Broken-Beak landed on a fencepost ahead of the caravan and refused to move.

*Bad,* the raven said. No jokes in it. *Bad-bad-bad. Smoke under the ground still. Cold smoke. Dead smoke.*

"Wind's wrong," Casper murmured, and stopped walking.

Ahead, down in the vale where Lowmark village sat beside its river, the air shimmered faintly grey. Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke rose. This hung, sunk into the earth itself, a haze that had soaked into the soil the way blood soaks into wool.

Marta drew rein beside them. "What is it?"

"Something's burning down there. Burned already, maybe." Casper narrowed their eyes. "Lowmark."

"Lowmark's got nothing worth burning but turnips." But Marta's hand had gone to the lens hanging round her neck, and her voice had changed, and behind them the whole caravan had gone quiet in that particular way of people who make their living outside the law and know how fast it can turn inside-out.

Down in the vale, a crow—no raven, a crow, and crows gossip terribly—flapped heavily up the slope toward Casper, exhausted, feathers smelling of char.

*Dead,* it said, before Casper could speak. *All dead or fled. Fire came at night. Men in black-and-gold. They nailed a paper to the well. Come see. Don't come see. Come see.*

Casper stood very still on the ridge, the wind pulling at their coat, and every careful layer of invisibility they had built across twenty-six years suddenly felt very thin, like cloth worn through at the elbow.

Below, the crow waited. Behind, Marta waited. Somewhere south, in a burned village, a proclamation fluttered on a well-post, and Casper already knew—knew the way you know a bone is broken—what it would say.

*Beast-speakers,* the wind seemed to carry up the slope, faint as memory. *Surrender them. Or burn with them.*
