## 2. Ash on the Wind

The wind changed two miles before they saw anything wrong.

Casper felt it first as a taste—char and old fat and something sharper underneath, like burnt hair—and then the ravens began to talk about it. *Dead fires. Dead fires. Nothing to steal.* They wheeled overhead in ragged spirals, and their voices carried that flat, disappointed note they got when a good thing had been ruined for everyone.

"Smoke ahead," Casper said quietly to Doss, the caravan master, keeping it casual, a guide's ordinary warning. "Big smoke. Not a campfire."

Doss squinted down the pass road where it bent around the shoulder of Grey Crag. "That'd be Lowmark," he said. "Village on the river. We were going to water the mules there." He chewed his toothpick a moment. "Could be a house fire."

Casper said nothing. The ravens had already told them otherwise. *All of it. All of it burning. Even the church.* But saying so out loud meant explaining how they knew, and Casper had spent twenty-six years learning not to explain.

The caravan came around the crag at midmorning, and there was Lowmark.

Or what had been Lowmark. The village sat in its river bend like a burned hand laid open on the green valley floor. Every roof was gone. Every wall was black to the height of a man, some higher, and the stone had gone white and chalky in places where the fire had burned hottest and longest. The fields around it were trampled into mud by many hooves—horses, Casper thought, shod horses, dozens of them, moving in formation. The mill was a shattered stump. The bridge still stood, which told Casper something: whoever did this had wanted the village dead but the road alive.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The mules smelled the ash and refused to go forward, ears pinned, and Doss didn't push them.

"Gods' teeth," whispered Tam, the youngest drover. "What happened here?"

Casper slid down from the lead mule and walked ahead alone, because someone had to, because that was the job, and because some part of them needed to know even as every other part screamed to turn around. The ash lay over everything like grey snow. It came up in puffs around their boots and settled on their sleeves, and each step released another ghost of that smell—burnt grain, burnt timber, burnt—

They stopped at the well.

It stood in the middle of the village square, one of the few things still upright, and someone had nailed a sheet of parchment to its posts. Four nails, one at each corner, driven with care. The paper was crisp. New. Whatever else had happened here, this had been done afterward, deliberately, by someone who wanted it read.

Casper read it twice.

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN. It has come to the attention of His Majesty that certain persons possess the abominable gift of speech with beasts, an art forbidden under ancient law and anathema to godly order. All such persons are to be surrendered to the crown's hunters within thirty days. Those who conceal a speaker, harbor a speaker, or fail to report a speaker shall be judged guilty of the same crime. Speakers taken alive will be shown mercy. Speakers found will be slain.*

Below the royal seal, in different ink, smaller: *There is no mercy.*

Casper stood very still. The words seemed to sink through their skin and settle somewhere behind the ribs, cold as the well water. *Speech with beasts.* Twenty-six years of silence, of carefulness, of never letting a word slip where a human ear could catch it—and here it was, nailed up in a burned village, describing them exactly. Naming them. Hunting them.

*You knew,* said a raven, landing on the well's iron crank. It tilted its head, one black eye fixed on Casper. *You always knew there were others. You just never thought they'd come for you.*

"I didn't know," Casper murmured, barely a breath. "I thought I was small. I thought small things don't get noticed."

*Small things get eaten,* the raven said. *Noticed is a kindness.*

Behind them, the caravan had come to a halt at the edge of the ash line, and the men were arguing in low voices. Doss wanted to push through to the river and be gone before dark. Tam wanted to turn back entirely. Old Merrick, who never said much, was looking at the proclamation from a distance, at the burned houses, at the mud churned by military horses, and his face had gone the color of tallow.

Casper heard none of it clearly. They were listening to the dog.

* * *

There was a dog in the ruins of the third house from the square.

Casper found it because it called to them—a thin, broken sound that no human could have heard, less a bark than a dying signal fire. *Here. Here. Here.* It lay beneath the collapsed corner of a roof beam, hind legs crushed, belly scorched along one side, and it had been lying there long enough that the ash had drifted over it like a blanket. It was a sheepdog, big and grey-muzzled, the kind that lives its whole life in partnership with a person. Its eyes were still bright. That was the worst part.

Casper knelt in the ash beside it. Their hands hovered over the ruin of its body without touching; there was nothing to fix, nothing to mend, and they both knew it.

"Who did this?" Casper asked softly.

The dog's answer came not in words exactly but in the way beast-speech worked—in images and pressure and smell, whole truths passed mind to mind. It gave Casper the night. Riders in black coats with gold trim, gold braid at the cuffs, gold devices on the breast. Torches lit all at once, everywhere, on a signal. Men going door to door first—not burning yet, *asking*. Asking names. Asking who lived where. A list. Someone had come with a list, and the village had answered it, and then the torches had been lit anyway.

*They took people out,* the dog said, and its mind flinched around the memory like a wound. *Some walked. Some were carried. The ones who fought, they killed right there. Then they fired the houses with everyone's things inside. Everything that smelled of us. Everything that remembered us.*

"Where did they take them?"

*North. Toward the king's road.* A pause, and then something colder, something the dog had heard shouted in the street: *The king has need of them. The king has need of all of them.*

Casper's mouth went dry. *Need.* Not prison. Not punishment. Need—as if speakers were a resource, a crop, something harvested. The word sat strangely in their chest, half-heard and half-understood, and would sit there for weeks before it bloomed into understanding.

"The proclamation says surrender and live," Casper said. "Was that true? Did anyone who went with them live?"

The dog looked at them with those bright eyes, and what it sent back was not an answer but a question of its own, the last question of a loyal animal's life: *When have the powerful ever kept a promise to the small?*

Casper stayed with it while it died. There wasn't much time—the light was failing, the caravan was arguing, every hour mattered—but some debts you pay regardless. They scratched its ears the way its person must have scratched them, and told it what Casper had told a hundred dying creatures on a hundred lonely roads: *You were good. You were loved. It wasn't your fault.* The dog held onto those words like a warm stone and let go of everything else, and when it was done, Casper covered it with what remained of its own doorstep and marked the spot with a cairn of hearthstones, three stones high, quick and rough.

Then they stood up, and the guilt arrived.

Because here was the thing Casper couldn't stop turning over, the thing that would keep them awake on the frozen passes for nights after: the dog had known them. Had *called* to them. In the last hour of its life, a beast-speaker had knelt beside it and spoken its language, and it had known, the way animals always know, that this was rare, that this was precious, that help had finally come—and the help had been able to do nothing but listen and bury it. And beyond the dog: the village. Lowmark had been full of animals, and animals talked, and if Casper had ever once broken their own rule—if they'd paused on some earlier caravan run and traded words with Lowmark's dogs and horses and crows—they might have known. Might have warned someone. Might have—

No. That was a lie and they knew it. No shepherd's warning stops riders with a royal list. But the guilt didn't care about logic. Guilt only cared that you had a gift, and the gift hadn't saved anybody.

* * *

By dusk the caravan had scattered.

It broke apart the way such things do—not with shouting but with quiet decisions made in parallel. Doss took the mules south toward the coast road, where coin was coin and nobody asked questions. Tam and Merrick split off toward Merrick's cousin's holding two valleys east. Nobody accused Casper of anything. Nobody said the word *speaker* aloud. But Casper saw the looks exchanged over the fire that wasn't lit, saw Doss studying them a beat too long, saw the way the men suddenly avoided standing downwind of them, as if the ash itself might carry evidence. Twenty-six years of invisibility, undone by a piece of parchment nailed to a well. Everyone who read that proclamation now knew what to look for. And everyone who knew Casper—even slightly, even as just the quiet guide who whistled oddly at birds—was now a potential witness, or worse, a potential informer.

"You should come south with me," Doss said, in the last grey light. He said it gruffly, looking away, which was his version of kindness. "Coast roads are fat and blind. Nobody hunts on the coast."

"They hunt everywhere now," Casper said. "That's what the proclamation means. It doesn't mean Lowmark. It means everywhere."

Doss chewed his toothpick. "Then where?"

Casper looked north, up the valley, to where the passes climbed into the white silence of the Thornfells' deep heart. Snow already lay on the high shoulders. Up there were no villages to burn, no lists, no hunters on shod horses—only cold, and hunger, and the animals, and the oldest paths, the ones no map bothered with.

"Up," Casper said. "Up is the only direction they don't look."

They parted without ceremony. Doss gave them a wheel of hard cheese and a flint, which was more than fair, and touched his cap brim, and turned his mules south, and Casper watched him until the road bent and took him. Then they stood alone in the ash-smelling dark at the edge of a murdered village, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the life they'd built was over. The careful life. The invisible life. The half-hidden life they'd told themselves was safety, was wisdom, was enough.

It had never been enough. It had just never been tested.

Casper filled their pack from the ruins—cheese, flint, a blanket pulled from a burned chest that still smelled of someone's lavender, a knife—and left the valley by the goat tracks, climbing as the stars came out. The goats found them within the hour, as goats do, a loose herd of feral billies moving along the cliff ledges, and Casper spoke with them quietly, asking after snow and forage, and the goats answered, and for a few minutes the terror eased, because the animals were still theirs. The one country no proclamation could burn.

But near midnight, high on a bare shoulder of stone with the wind cutting sideways, a voice reached them that was not a goat's.

It came down the wind from the north ridge—deep, layered, more than one throat speaking in unison, the way wolf packs sing. Casper froze. Wolves did not speak to humans. Wolves especially did not speak to humans in the deep fells where the packs had been hunted to shadows for a generation.

Yet the voice came again, closer now, threaded through the wind, and it was unmistakably addressed to them. To *them*, by name—or by what they were.

*Little speaker,* the voices sang, strange and vast and almost gentle. *You are running the wrong way. Come north. Come deeper. There is one who remembers why the fires started, and she has been waiting a very long time for someone who can carry her words.*

Casper stood in the starlight with their heart hammering, the cheese and flint heavy on their back, the burned village smoking below, and every instinct they owned said *wolves lie, wolves lure, wolves kill.*

And beneath that instinct, faint but insistent, something older said: *listen.*
