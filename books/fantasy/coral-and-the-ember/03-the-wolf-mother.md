## 3. The Wolf-Mother

Casper went north because the voices told them to, and hated themselves for it.

Three days into the deep fells the doubt had worn down to a dull stone in their chest. They climbed through pine that gave way to bare black rock, through snow that squeaked like tallow underfoot. The cheese was gone by the second day. The flint still worked, but there was nothing left to burn except wet heather and their own patience.

They spoke to what they could find. A snow-hare, trembling under a ledge: *which way is shelter?* The hare only knew fear and the smell of its own breath. A raven riding the wind above the pass: *is anything hunting me?* The raven said *cold, cold, cold* and wheeled away, uninterested in the affairs of ground-things. The mountain itself said nothing at all. Stone never did.

On the fourth day Casper stopped speaking altogether, because speech cost warmth, and warmth was running out like water from a cracked jug.

That night they curled into the hollow beneath an overhang and told themselves the truth they'd been avoiding since Lowmark: *you are going to die here.* Not dramatically. Not with hunters or wolves or fire. Just quietly, the way the mountain took everything—slowly, without malice, without even noticing.

They slept anyway. Hunger makes a strange bedfellow; it holds you close and whispers nothing.

* * *

The wolf found them at dawn.

Casper woke not to sound but to silence—the particular silence of a place where every small creature has stopped moving. They opened their eyes and she was there, ten paces off, sitting in the snow as if she'd been posed there by a sculptor with a taste for the terrible.

She was gray gone silver, silver gone white, huge in the way old things are huge—not muscle but presence. Scars laced her muzzle like a map of a country Casper never wanted to visit. One ear was torn to a ragged crescent. Her eyes were amber, and they were looking at Casper with an attention no animal had ever given them.

Every animal looked *through* Casper's words to the simple thing beneath: food, fear, direction. This one was reading them. Line by line.

"Little speaker," she said.

Casper's whole body tried to be somewhere else. There was nowhere to go. The overhang backed onto solid rock, and their legs had spent four days being eaten.

"You—" Their voice came out a ruin. They swallowed and tried again. "You speak."

"I speak." Her voice was not a voice, exactly. It arrived inside the skull the way their gift always worked, but denser, older, weighted like river stones. "I have spoken longer than your kind has had words for what I am. You are the first of your line to climb this high in sixty years, and you are nearly dead, which is a poor way to arrive."

"I'm not—"

"Do not lie to me. Lying is for creatures with something left to lose." She rose, unhurried, and Casper saw then how thin she was beneath the winter coat, how the great frame had been pared down by years the way wind pares down a tree on a ridgeline. "Eat first. Then we talk."

She turned and trotted upslope, pausing once to look back—an expectation, not a request.

Casper followed, because the alternative was the hollow and the dark and the arithmetic of starvation, and because some traitor part of them had already decided, back in the starlight three nights ago, when the voices sang out of the north.

* * *

She brought them to a kill she'd cached under rocks—a mountain goat, days old but frozen, and Casper ate raw meat by a stunted fire until their stomach stopped screaming and started merely complaining. The wolf sat watching the valley below with her ruined ear cocked, and did not eat, and Casper understood with a slow cold turn that she had killed that goat for them. That she could no longer bring down goats easily herself. That she was spending her last strength like a miser spending his final coin.

"Why?" Casper asked. Firelight moved on her scars.

"Because you are dying, and dying speakers are useless to me." No cruelty in it. Just fact, laid flat as slate. "And because I have waited a long time for one who could hear me properly, and I am not so proud as to let the waiting be wasted."

"Hear you properly? I've heard animals my whole life."

"You have heard animals," she agreed. "A sparrow's wants. A dog's griefs. Useful little noises. But I am not an animal, Casper Eriksen of the Thornfell passes." His name in her mouth made his skin prickle. "I am Vaela, last mother of the Highmoor pack, and I remember the world before your king put his crown on."

"The king's been crowned thirty years."

"Yes." Something moved behind the amber eyes—grief, or its fossil. "Thirty years exactly. You have noticed the arithmetic, perhaps. Or perhaps your kind never counts."

Casper thought of the proclamation nailed to Lowmark's well. *All beast-speakers to be surrendered or slain.* Thought of the black-and-gold riders, the ash, the dog dying in the ruins saying *crown men, crown men, fire from their hands.*

"What does the king have to do with speakers?"

Vaela turned her head and regarded him fully, and for a moment Casper felt like prey—felt the ancient machinery of her attention close around him—and then it passed, and she was only tired.

"Come to the ridge when the light is full," she said. "It is easier shown than told."

* * *

From the high ridge, on a day of brutal clarity, she showed him.

Not with words at first. She had him stand where the wind came up from the south, from the lowlands, from the distant smudge of the capital that even here you could feel—a pressure on the horizon like a bruise.

"What do you feel?"

"Cold. Wind."

"Listen past it. With the gift. What do you feel?"

Casper closed their eyes and opened the sense they usually kept shuttered, the quiet channel where animal minds murmured. And beneath the wind, beneath the birdsong and the far-off rustle of deer in the valleys, there was something else. A pull. Faint, vast, patient—the sensation of standing near an open drain in a very large room. Everything living leaned toward it, just slightly, the way grass leans from wind.

"What is that?"

"That," Vaela said, "is why your village burned. That is why the fires come every few years, closer together now, marching through the valleys like a harvest. Your king made a bargain, thirty years ago, in a war he was losing. There is a spirit beneath his city—old as the mountains, hungry as winter. He bound himself to it to win his throne's war, and the price was written into his blood and his heirs': it feeds on the gifted. Every soul born with a tongue for beasts, a hand for healing, a sight for things unseen—it drinks them. Slowly, when it can. Quickly, when it must."

Casper opened their eyes. The world looked different. The pull was still there, faint as a toothache.

"The witch-hunts—"

"Were never superstition. Your crown does not fear magic, little speaker. Your crown *eats* it. The hunters are not priests. They are drovers, herding the gifted toward the fire, and the fire carries them south to the city, and the city feeds." Her voice dropped lower, rougher. "My pack ranged the Highmoor for nine generations. We were many, and we were strong, and some of us—like me—were more than wolves. Thirty years ago there were forty of our kind in these mountains who could speak as I speak. Now I count myself, and I count the echoes of the dead. The drain has reached even here."

"And the king? Does he know?"

"He is the vessel. He knows the way a man knows a splinter has gone to the bone—he feels it every hour, and he cannot reach it." For the first time, something in her voice bent. "He bargained to save his people from a war. The ember saved them. And it has worn him ever since, a coat he cannot take off. Do not mistake this for mercy, Casper. A prisoner who guards the prison is still a jailer. But the door he locked was locked from inside him."

The wind came up the ridge and Casper shivered, though not from cold. Or not only.

"Why tell me this? Why me? I'm nobody. I guide smugglers. I hide."

"You hide beautifully. You have hidden so well that you nearly died of it." Vaela stepped closer, and her breath fogged between them. "But hiding ends today whether you choose it or not. The hunts will reach the Thornfells within two years—I have watched the pattern. There will be no passes left to hide in, no villages left unburned, no half-lived life left to live. The ember grows stronger each season. It must be unbound before it grows too strong to touch."

"Unbound." Casper laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and cracked. "By me? I talk to goats."

"You talk to *minds*. And the binding is a mind—a bargain, a word, a knot of intention wrapped around a starving god. Knots can be spoken to. Knots can be *unspoken*, by one who stands outside it and hears its shape." Her amber eyes held his. "I cannot enter the city. Wolves die at the edge of that place; the drain is strongest there, and I am old. But you can walk in where I cannot. Carry my message. Carry what I know of the binding—its making, its terms, its name. Find the king's buried self, if any of it remains, or find another way. And when it breaks, every chained gift in this realm breaks with it."

"And if I say no?"

"Then go back to your smugglers' roads and hide again, and I will return to my ridge and die, and in twenty years there will be no one left in these mountains who remembers that the world was ever otherwise." She said it without heat. That was the worst of it—the absolute absence of manipulation. Only the ledger, balanced, offered for inspection.

Casper looked south. The capital was invisible at this distance, but the pull wasn't—that faint lean in all living things, that drain. Somewhere down there, a man wore a crown like a wound. Somewhere down there were thousands like Casper, hiding, half-alive, waiting for the knock and the fire.

Somewhere behind them, in the ashes of Lowmark, a dog had died saying *crown men.*

"I'm not brave," Casper said. "I want that understood. Whatever you think you're seeing—I run. It's the one thing I'm good at."

"Good," Vaela said. "Run well, then. Run *south*, for once in your life, and run toward the thing all your instincts flee." She turned and began downslope, moving with a careful economy that betrayed how much the morning had cost her. "Rest tonight. Eat what remains. Tomorrow we begin—you have much to learn about the binding before you can carry it, and I have little time left to teach."

"How little?"

She paused. Did not turn.

"Less than the journey," she said. "Which is why we do not waste the dawn arguing."

* * *

That night, in the lee of the ridge with the fire built small, Casper lay awake listening to the old wolf breathe beside them—slow, labored, each breath a little shallower than the last, like a tide going out.

They had spent their whole life believing invisibility was safety. Four days ago the belief had burned with Lowmark. Tonight it finished dying, quietly, in the company of a creature older than the crown that hunted them both.

*I'm going to the city,* Casper thought, and the thought should have been terror, and part of it was. But beneath the terror, unwelcome and stubborn, something had shifted its weight—something that felt disgracefully like purpose.

In the morning, Vaela stood at the ridge's edge facing south, ears forward, and said:

"There is a road down from the mountains that the patrols watch. And there is a hunter coming up it—one of theirs, one of the best, and one whose heart has begun to crack. If you meet her, little speaker, do not run." Her amber eyes slid sideways, catching the light. "Not everyone in black-and-gold is what the uniform says. Remember that. It may save your life sooner than you think."
