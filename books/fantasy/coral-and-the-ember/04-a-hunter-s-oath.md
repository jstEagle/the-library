## 4. A Hunter's Oath

The cell smelled of wet stone and old blood, and the boy in it smelled of fear.

Sera Vance stood in the doorway and let him look at her. That was the first lesson of interrogation, the one they'd drilled into her at the academy until it wore grooves in her mind: let them see the black-and-gold, let them see the sword, let them do half your work for you. Fear loosened tongues faster than any iron.

She didn't feel like doing half her work for him tonight.

"Name," she said.

"T-Tomas. Tomas Reed." He was maybe sixteen. Thin as a winter branch, wrists raw from the manacles, a farmer's calluses still on his hands. They'd taken him at the Lowmark market, according to the intake sheet, for consorting with beasts. A raven, the report said. He'd fed a raven from his hand and it had spoken back, and someone had heard, and someone had always heard.

"You fed a crow in the market square."

"Raven." His voice cracked. "It told me— it told me where a lamb had got stuck, up on the fells. It talks to me. Since I was little. I never told anyone. I never *did* anything—"

"Sit down, Tomas."

He was already sitting. He sat harder, like the words had weight.

Sera pulled the second chair from the wall and turned it backward and straddled it, forearms on the rail, the way she did when she wanted a prisoner to forget she was armed. The lamp between them guttered and steadied. Somewhere above, the garrison drums changed watch.

"Tell me about the raven."

"It's just a bird. It's just—" He swallowed. "It knows things. Small things. Where the fox denned, where the ice would crack. My da said never let anyone hear it. He said the last speaker in our valley was taken when I was a baby and no one ever said where."

"Your father was right to be careful."

The boy blinked. That wasn't in the script. The script was: establish the crime, extract the names of other speakers, obtain confession, remand for transport. The transport was the part she'd stopped asking about three years ago. Prisoners went to the capital in black wagons. The wagons came back empty. No trials were ever published. No executions were ever announced. The families received sealed letters with a crown seal and no contents worth reading.

"Captain Vance." He'd heard of her, then. Everyone had. *The Hound of the Marches*, they called her in the taverns, in the tone people used for plague. "Are you going to take me to the city?"

"I'm going to ask you questions first."

"That's what they said about Elsie Marsh." His eyes were wet and furious and sixteen years old and done being afraid. "She could calm horses. They took her in the spring. Her mum still sets a bowl out for her at supper."

Sera wrote nothing on her slate. Her hand didn't move.

"Who else knows about your gift?"

"No one. My da. My da's dead now." A beat, and then, quieter, the thing he'd clearly been holding behind his teeth since the manacles went on: "If you take me, no one will know where I went either. Will they."

It wasn't a question. She didn't answer it, and her not answering was an answer, and she watched him understand it, watched something go out of his face that she'd seen go out of a hundred faces before and had never once let herself name.

*Remand for transport.* She'd written it in a fine, disciplined hand for eleven years. She had the best conviction record in the Marches. She had a commendation from the Lord Protector himself. She had, in a locked drawer in her quarters, a sealed letter with a crown seal that she had never been able to make herself open, because she already knew what wasn't in it.

"Last question," she said. "The raven. What did it call you?"

He frowned. "What?"

"Speakers get names from them. The beasts. Old habit. What did it call you?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he said, in a small voice, "It called me *little brother*."

Sera stood, put the chair back against the wall, and walked out of the cell without another word, and the boy's silence behind her was louder than any screaming she'd ever heard.

* * *

The records office in the garrison keep was hers to enter at any hour; she'd broken three smugglers in that room and two informants, and the clerks flinched when she came down the stairs. Tonight the clerks had gone to their beds, and the lamp she lit was her own, and she locked the door behind her because what she was about to do was treason.

She started with Elsie Marsh.

It took her an hour to find the intake, because it wasn't where it should have been. Eleven years of filing discipline, and the Marsh girl's transport record was a single line: *remanded, spring convoy, capital.* No arrival docket. No disposition. Every prisoner in every convoy she'd ever escorted had an arrival docket — she'd checked, idly, over the years, the way a soldier checks a strap. The capital's intake office confirmed receipt on everything. Everything but these.

She pulled the convoy manifests. Spring, three years back. Forty-one names remanded. She sent a clerk's boy down to the archive for the return ledgers, and when they came up she cross-referenced them by candlelight, name by name, and the lamp burned low and her neck ached and the number at the end of it did not change.

Forty-one remanded. Zero received.

Not just her convoys. She pulled the other Marches captains' ledgers, and then, with her heart going like a fist on a door, she sent for the regional abstracts — five years, ten years, back and back, and the pattern held like a scar: hundreds of names, speakers and suspected speakers, flowing one way down the king's road, and nothing — no trial record, no execution order, no grave registry entry, nothing — coming back. The realm's most careful bureaucracy had a hole in it the size of a generation, and everyone who touched the paperwork had signed it and saluted and gone home to supper.

Her hands were steady. That was the terrible part. She'd expected them to shake.

There was one more record she wanted, and she made herself wait until she'd put all the others back in order, because a part of her was still a soldier and the soldier said: *leave no sign.* Then she unlocked the restricted cabinet with her captain's key and took out the sealed letter she'd carried a copy of for eleven years, and she set beside it the original intake from the year her sister was taken.

Wren Vance. Age nineteen. Gift: unspecified, reported by a neighbor. Remanded, autumn convoy, capital.

Zero received.

Sera sat in the dark with the lamp out for a long time. She thought about Wren at nineteen, all elbows and bad singing, who could make a sparrow sit on her finger like it was paid to. She thought about their mother setting two bowls at supper for a year afterward, and then one, and then moving to a town where no one knew them, because the neighbors had started looking at *Sera* too, the sister who'd joined the hunters, as if the gift might be catching, as if loyalty were contagious.

She had joined the hunters because she believed the law caught monsters. She had spent eleven years learning that the law caught children, and she had personally delivered forty-one of them, and she had never once asked what was at the end of the road because the not-asking was the price of the commendation on her wall.

The ember of the lamp had gone out. She lit a candle stub, and on the back of a transport manifest she wrote in her crisp disciplined hand, the hand that had signed away forty-one lives:

*The edicts serve no law. There is no law. There is only the king, and whatever wears the king.*

She looked at the words for a while. Then she folded the manifest into her coat, next to Wren's intake record, and she went to her quarters and took out her commission — heavy paper, the crown's gold seal, the Lord Protector's signature, eleven years of her life rendered in ink — and she carried it down to the courtyard brazier.

The night air bit. The garrison slept. She stood over the coals and found, with mild surprise, that she was waiting for some feeling to stop her — duty, fear, love of the service, anything. Nothing came. The fire had been lit inside her hours ago, in a cell that smelled of wet stone, by a boy saying *it called me little brother.*

She fed the commission to the coals and watched the gold seal curl and go black.

Then she went to the cells.

* * *

Tomas Reed heard her boots coming and pressed himself against the wall, and when the key turned he stood up as tall as his shaking let him.

"Captain—"

"Listen fast." She unlocked the manacles. "There's a stable gate on the north wall, the one with the broken latch, the one I reported broken twice and was overruled on. You're going to walk out of it, go down to the river, and follow it west until you see the Thornfells. Don't take the road. Don't light a fire. Don't speak to anything with two legs."

He was rubbing his wrists, staring at her. "Why?"

"Because in the morning there'll be a report that a prisoner escaped, and the report will be true, and no one will look very hard, because the captain who lost him will be gone too." She pushed a small purse into his hand — her own coin, she realized, and found she didn't care. "There are people in those mountains who can help you. Speakers. Find them."

"You're letting me go." Not a question either. He was learning fast, this one. "Why?"

Sera thought about forty-one names and zero arrivals. She thought about a sparrow sitting on a girl's finger like it was paid to.

"Because someone has to ask what's at the end of the road," she said. "And it isn't going to be anyone who still wears this." She touched her collar, where the black-and-gold had been, where now there was only plain wool.

"Come with me," the boy said suddenly. "You know the mountains. You could—"

"I have something to do first." She stepped back and let the cell door swing. "West, Tomas. The Thornfells. Go."

He went. She listened to his bare feet fade down the corridor, and then she went the other way, to the armory, where she took back only what she'd paid for: her sword, her mail, a week's rations, and a horse that had carried her through nine years of the king's errands and owed the king nothing.

At the garrison gate the sentry saluted her, because she was Captain Vance and the hour was hers, and she saluted back, and rode out of the last post she would ever hold, and did not look at the walls.

She rode west all that night and all the next day, and on the second evening the Thornfells rose out of the haze like the backs of sleeping animals, blue and white and vast, and Sera Vance reined in on the last of the lowland hills and looked at the mountains where the king's writ ran thin and the king's wagons did not go.

Somewhere in there were the speakers. Somewhere in there was whatever the crown was afraid enough of to build a hole the size of a generation in its own records. Somewhere in there, maybe, was an answer to a question she'd carried for eleven years: *what was at the end of the road, Wren?*

She turned the horse west, and in the last light she saw — or thought she saw, high on a ridgeline against the dying sun — a great grey shape, still as stone, watching her.

A wolf. Too far to be sure. Too still to be anything else.

It watched her a long moment, and then it turned and flowed away along the ridge, north, toward the deep passes, and Sera found she was holding her breath, and made herself let it out.

*They say there's an old one in the fells,* a smuggler had told her once, in a cell not unlike Tomas's, trading the tale for a lighter sentence. *Older than the crown. They say it remembers every name.*

She rode north, following the ridgeline, following the shape that had vanished, into the mountains where the law ended and whatever came next began.
