## 5. Thieves' Gate

The mountains let Casper go the way they took them in: grudgingly, one switchback at a time.

Three weeks of high fells had taught their body new hungers. They ate pine bark and lichen and once, weeping with shame and gratitude both, the marrow Vaela cracked open for them from a frozen elk carcass. The wolf-mother moved like weather around them—there when needed, gone when not—and spoke only in that low, unhurried voice that made every word sound older than the stones.

*The city lies south,* she said on the last morning above the treeline. *Follow the river until it forgets it was ever wild. That is where they will be waiting for you. The crown's roads are its teeth.*

"I know," Casper said aloud, because speaking to her out loud had stopped feeling foolish somewhere around the second week. "I've run smugglers past Thieves' Gate since I was fourteen."

*Then you know what waits there.*

Casper did. The pass below the last ridge narrowed to a stony throat where the river cut close to the cliff, and every bandit, bounty man, and bored garrison soldier between here and the capital knew it was the one place a traveler couldn't turn aside. Smugglers paid tolls there. Fugitives paid more.

*You could go around,* Vaela said. *Four days through the icefields.*

"Four days is four days closer to the solstice." Casper pulled their cloak tight and looked down at the thread of silver water far below. "I'll go through. You keep to the heights."

*As if you could stop me.* Something that might have been amusement moved through her voice. *If you die down there, little speaker, I will eat whoever killed you. It will not bring you back, but it will be satisfying.*

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in years."

She was already moving up the slope, gray against gray, and Casper began the long descent alone.

* * *

Thieves' Gate earned its name honestly. The pass pinched between two walls of granite, and someone centuries ago had wedged an arch of black iron across the narrowest point—not a gate anymore, just a rusted skeleton of it, hung with old chains and older warnings. Toll-takers worked from a stone hut built into the cliff face, and today the hut had company.

Six men. Casper counted them from behind a boulder two hundred yards upslope, breath shallow, heart doing arithmetic. Six men in mismatched leathers, but three of them wore the black-and-gold sashes of crown hunters beneath their armor, and the others had the loose, watchful stance of hired blades. A bounty crew. Not hunting smugglers.

Hunting *speakers*.

A man was kneeling in the middle of the road with his hands laced behind his head, and beside him stood a mule loaded for travel and nothing else—no caravan, no family. A lone traveler, caught alone. One of the bounty men crouched in front of him holding something small and pale.

A bone whistle. Carved from the wing-bone of a marsh hawk, if Casper's nose was right, and hawk-bone whistles were a speaker's tool, a way to call birds without a word spoken aloud.

"You want to tell me again," the bounty man said pleasantly, "where you bought this?"

"I didn't buy it. My grandfather made it. It's just a whistle—"

"It's just a whistle." The man turned it over in his fingers. "See, my cousin works the ash-rolls in Lowmark. He says they found a whole drawer of these in the houses there. Just whistles. Just carvings. Just herbs." He smiled. "Just people, too, in the end."

Casper should have kept moving. Should have circled wide, lost a day, saved their own skin—the old rule, the rule that had kept them alive for twenty years: *be nothing, be no one, be a smudge on the road*. But the kneeling man was maybe nineteen, with straw-colored hair and a farmer's hands, and he was looking at that whistle like it was his whole life balanced in a stranger's fingers, and Casper found they had stopped walking.

*Be nothing,* whispered twenty years of habit.

But Vaela's voice was louder now: *You have spent your whole life being nothing. See what it bought the world.*

Casper whistled.

Low, under human hearing—a shape of sound meant for other ears. Two ravens sat hunched on the iron arch above the road, and at Casper's call they lifted their heads.

*Small one,* said the elder raven. *Why do you sing trouble?*

*The men below. How many blades? How many bows?*

*Five knives. One crossbow, cocked, resting on the hut roof. The shooter is cold and bored and wants his supper.*

*Can you make him want it more?*

Ravens are not brave. Ravens are clever, which is better. The elder croaked something to its companion, and a moment later both birds dropped off the arch and went screaming down the pass in a strafing wheel of wings and insults, and the crossbowman on the roof swore and swatted and fired a bolt at nothing, and in the chaos of that one wasted moment Casper came down the scree like water, fast and silent, grabbed the kneeling farmer by the collar, and said, "Run."

The farmer ran. The bounty men shouted. And then the world became very simple and very loud.

Casper got ten steps before the first knife-man cut the angle. Casper dodged left, took a backhand blow across the cheekbone that filled their vision with white sparks, and went down hard on the frozen road. Above them the bounty man raised his blade—

—and a sword came out of nowhere and took him in the throat.

Casper scrambled back against the cliff wall. The fight lasted less time than a held breath. The newcomer moved like a drill sergeant's nightmare come true: economical, brutal, utterly without flourish. A woman in a plain traveler's cloak, gray-streaked hair cropped short, a longsword in her right hand and a hunting knife already red in her left. She put down two more before the rest understood what was happening, and then the remaining bounty men made the oldest calculation in the world and ran, all except the crossbowman, who lay on the hut roof with a raven standing on his chest and an expression of profound betrayal.

Silence. River-sound. Blood steaming on the stones.

The woman turned to Casper, and Casper felt their stomach drop through the mountain, because they knew that face. Everyone in the Thornfells knew that face from woodcut proclamations and whispered warnings.

Sera Vance. The crown's hunt-captain. The woman who had burned Lowmark—or so the stories said, though the stories also said she never took prisoners, and this farmer was still breathing, still running, still alive somewhere down the road.

"You're hurt," Sera said. Her voice was flat as slate.

"So are your chances of catching me," Casper managed, and hated how young it sounded.

Sera looked at them for a long moment. Then she did something Casper would remember for the rest of their life: she reached into her coat, drew out a folded parchment bearing the royal seal, and held it up so Casper could see the wax.

"My commission," she said. "Twenty-two years of service." She struck flint to her knife and touched the flame to the parchment's corner. It caught, and she held it while it burned, watching Casper's face over the fire. "I'm told there's a wolf following you. Big. Old. Been shadowing this valley for three days."

Casper said nothing.

"She doesn't need to hide from me. Neither do you." Sera dropped the burning commission and ground it under her boot. "I burned my last village eight months ago. I've been counting the doors since. The numbers don't work, speaker. What we're taking isn't what the edicts say we're taking, and I want to know why, and every record in the capital is sealed higher than my rank can reach." She cleaned her knife on the dead man's coat, unhurried. "So. You're going to the city. I can arrest you, or I can walk with you. Choose fast. Those men will be back with friends."

*Little speaker,* said Vaela's voice, drifting down from the ridgeline like snowfall, *I have watched this woman for three days. She sleeps facing the door. She has not eaten meat in a month. And yesterday she buried a sparrow a hunter's snare had killed, and marked the grave with a stone.* A pause. *Wolves know hunters. This one has stopped being one.*

Casper looked at the burning parchment, at the blood, at the woman who had chosen—in the space of one breath—to become homeless.

"The capital," Casper said slowly. "There's something under it. In it. Feeding."

Something flickered across Sera's face—recognition, or the beginning of it. "My sister," she said. "They took her eleven years ago. Gifted. The paperwork said 'relocated for training.' There is no training facility. I've checked every ledger in three provinces." Her jaw tightened. "Say it plainly. What feeds?"

"I don't know yet. But I know someone who does."

Sera glanced up at the ridgeline, where a gray shape stood motionless against the sky, watching them with amber patience.

"Wolves don't talk," Sera said.

"No," Casper agreed. "They really do, though."

* * *

They made nine miles before dark, driving hard down the river road with the farmer sent home at the first fork and the ravens riding the wind above them like scouts. Camp was a hollow beneath a leaning birch, fire small and shielded, and for the first hour neither of them spoke. Trust, Casper was learning, is not given; it is built, plank by plank, in silence.

It was Sera who broke it.

"Lowmark," she said, staring into the flames. "You heard what happened there."

"I saw what happened there." Casper fed a stick to the fire. "Ash on everything. A proclamation nailed to the well. A dog dying in the ruins who told me who came." They looked at her. "Black-and-gold. Crown hunters. Your people said it was your company."

"It was my company." No flinch, no excuse. "And I gave the order to cordon the village, and while my cordons were up, another team went in. Not mine. Wearing our colors." Her hands curled slowly into fists. "I've spent eight months trying to find who gave that order. Every trail ends in a sealed office. Every sealed office answers to the palace." She finally turned to look at Casper fully. "You think I burned those people. So does everyone. I've stopped correcting them. It's easier than explaining that I can't prove anything."

"Do you expect me to believe that?"

"No. I expect you to verify it. That's smarter." She almost smiled; it didn't survive. "Ask your wolf. Wolves don't lie."

*She speaks true,* Vaela's voice murmured from the darkness beyond the firelight, where two amber points floated. *I have followed her scent since the passes. It smells of ash, but old ash. Grief-ash. Not fresh.*

Casper exhaled. "She vouches for you."

"That's either very reassuring or very terrifying."

"Yes," Casper said, and after a moment Sera laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut.

They traded truths the way travelers trade bread, in pieces, through the night. Casper gave her the ember: the spirit bound beneath the capital, thirty years fat on gifted souls, wearing the king like a glove. Sera gave Casper the machinery: how the harvests were dressed up as relocations, how the quotas rose every winter, how hunters who asked questions were reassigned and reassigned until they resigned or vanished. She talked about her sister—Maren, taken at sixteen, a girl who could calm horses with a touch—and her voice stayed level the entire time, which somehow made it worse.

"And you?" Sera asked near midnight. "What's your gift worth? Why haven't they taken you?"

"Because I never used it where anyone could see. Twenty years of being nobody." Casper turned a smooth stone over in their fingers. "I thought hiding was surviving. Then Lowmark burned anyway, and I learned the ember doesn't care if you're quiet. It just eats slower."

Sera nodded slowly. "My sister was quiet too."

Above the camp, on the black shoulder of the ridge, Vaela sang—one long note, low and rising, the sound wolves make when they mean it for one listener only. Casper translated without thinking:

"She says we sleep in turns. She says the road ahead smells of iron and smoke. And she says—" Casper paused, listening to something in the note that humans wouldn't catch, something old and sorrowful and certain. "She says the city is worse than we think. And that we should hurry."

Sera banked the fire and settled against her pack, sword hand free even in rest. "Nine days to the capital," she said. "Then we find out what's under it."

Casper closed their eyes and listened to the river forgetting it was wild, mile by mile, carrying them toward the place where it ended.

Somewhere south, in a gilded cage, a king was waiting to remember.
