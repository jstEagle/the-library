## 6. The Gilded Rot

The capital announced itself three days before we saw it.

The ravens told me first. They came down off the northern crags in ones and twos, and they would not go near the road. *Smoke that never stops,* one said, riding the wind above our campfire. *A city that burns in its heart every day and is not consumed.* Another, older, with a torn wing feather: *Do not go there, small speaker. The two-legs there have forgotten how to be animals. They only watch.*

"They watch," I repeated aloud, and Sera looked up from sharpening her knife.

"The ravens?"

"They say everyone in the city watches everyone else."

Sera's whetstone paused mid-stroke. "That matches what I remember."

"You've been inside before."

"I was stationed there for six years." She set the stone down. "I hunted from there. Which means I know exactly what you're about to walk into, and I want you to hear it from me before the walls do it louder." She fed another stick to the fire. "Every beggar is a paid ear. Every tavern keeper reports weekly or their license goes away. Children are taught the hand-sign for *marked* before they're taught their letters. You cannot hide your gift in Aurelholm by being quiet. Quiet is what they look for."

Vaela had stopped following us openly by then—the roads were too thick with traffic, her grey shape too memorable—but I felt her on the ridgelines at dusk and dawn, a pressure like a held breath. When Sera said *you cannot hide*, I reached out along that thread and felt Vaela's answer come back cold and simple.

*Then do not hide. Be small. Be dull. Be nothing worth a second glance.*

So we became dull. Sera cut my hair with her belt knife and burned it. She made me trade my patched mountain coat for a fishmonger's castoff slicker that stank of brine. We came down off the last spur of the Thornfells on a drizzling morning and joined the river of carts and pilgrims and peddlers flowing toward Aurelholm's south gate, and I kept my eyes down and let the city's noise wash over me like silt.

It was worse than the ravens said.

Not the size—I'd imagined size. It was the doors. Everywhere, on door lintels and gateposts and the corners of market stalls, someone had pressed a black handprint into the wood. Ash, mixed with something that made it cling through rain. I saw them within the first hundred yards and then could not stop seeing them. Dozens. Hundreds. Some old and faded to grey ghosts. Some fresh enough that the ash still glittered.

"What are they," I asked, low.

"Harvest marks." Sera didn't slow, didn't turn her head. "When the hunters take someone, they mark the household. It tells the neighbors the family is clean now—cleansed of association. And it tells anyone else who's marked that this doorway is safe to inform from." Her jaw worked. "I signed some of those orders. Early on. Before I understood what happened to the people after the word *harvested* left my mouth."

"And what happens?"

"I never asked." She said it flat and hard, the way you say a thing you've spent years refusing to say. "That's the answer you're actually asking for. I never asked, because asking meant the commission was wrong, and the commission was all I had after my sister."

We pushed through the Southmarket, under the aqueduct, past the great bronze bell that no one rang anymore—*the king silenced it himself, twenty years ago, said it disturbed his sleep*, a cart driver was telling his boy—and I felt them. That was the thing no one had warned me about. All my life I'd been alone with my gift; the closest thing to company had been goats and gulls. Now, walking a crowded street with my senses open a finger's width, I could feel them everywhere. Flickers. Embers of the same warmth I carried. A woman selling eels whose hands moved with a suggestion of serpent-speech. A boy sweeping a step who glanced at a pigeon and glanced away too fast. A whole street of dyers where the colors were impossibly vivid, and every worker there walked carefully, quietly, like people standing very still in a room with a sleeping predator.

They were hiding. All of them. Hundreds in this district alone, thousands in the city, folded small and dull just as Vaela had taught me, living under the ash-marks of their taken kin.

And something was drinking them.

I couldn't have explained how I knew. There was no sound, no scent. But walking deeper into the city I felt it the way you feel a draft under a door—a slow pull, faint and constant, tugging at every flicker of gifted warmth in Aurelholm like tide going out over a mudflat. The hidden ones felt it too. I watched an old man pause in a doorway, press his palm flat against his chest, and stand there a moment with his eyes closed, like a man listening to his own heart decide whether to keep going.

"Casper." Sera touched my elbow. "You've gone white."

"It's pulling on them." My voice came out strange. "Right now. All of them. Can't you—" I stopped. She couldn't. "It's like watching a hundred candles burn down at once."

Sera looked at the crowd, at the ash-marked lintels, at the old man still standing in his doorway with his hand over his heart. Something crossed her face that I would learn to recognize as the hunt-captain doing arithmetic she hated.

"Nine days until solstice," she said. "The eclipse falls that night. Come on. We need to get underground before dark."

* * *

The underworld of Aurelholm was not beneath it, exactly, though we did go beneath it—down through a dye-works cellar, along a smuggler's drain Sera remembered from a case seven years gone, into a cistern vault where forty lamps burned behind smoked glass. But the underworld was really a network, a nervous system of debt and favor running through the city's fat body. Sera knew its ganglia. She'd squeezed them often enough as a hunter, and apparently they remembered her grip.

The contact she trusted—or trusted most, which is a different thing—kept a counting-house front near the Tannery Ward. Mott Quill was a small, damp-eyed man with ink-stained fingers and the manner of someone perpetually apologizing for existing. He received us in a back room among bales of counterfeit wool, and when he saw Sera's face he went grey.

"Captain Vance."

"Not captain."

"No," he agreed quickly. "No, of course not. Desertion carries— well. It carries what it carries." He dabbed his forehead with a square of cloth. "You shouldn't be here. Neither of you should be here. Especially—" his damp eyes slid to me, and I understood he'd already guessed, the way rats guess about cats, "—especially your friend. There's a new tariff on unregistered gifts, did you know? Informants get silver per confirmed sighting. Silver, Captain. My own clerks would sell me for less than that."

"Then we'll be quick," Sera said. "We need three things. The palace rotation schedules. Whatever the guilds know about the solstice ceremony. And a route into the Undercroft below the Sanctum that doesn't go through any checkpoint."

Mott laughed, one dry syllable with no humor anywhere in it. "Three things. Yes. And I need a pardon and a long life and my mother back from the grave." He wrung his cloth. "I can maybe get you the first. Maybe. The second—" he hesitated, and his eyes did something furtive and frightened that made the hair rise on my neck, "—the second depends on what you think the solstice ceremony *is*."

"A coronation rite," Sera said. "Renewal of the binding. Public festival, private working. Standard practice."

"That's what the proclamations say." Mott leaned across the counter and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "But I move money for people who move money for the palace, Captain, and money tells truths mouths won't. For thirty years, on every solstice, certain accounts pay out. Payments to a chapterhouse that doesn't exist. Purchases of materials no guild will admit selling. And always, always, payments to families. Compensation. Quiet compensation, signed under seal, to households in the provinces. Do you know what the palace buys at solstice, Captain Vance?"

He let it hang. I felt the draft-under-the-door pull strengthen, as if the city itself were leaning in to listen.

"People," I said.

"Families," Mott corrected softly. "Whole bloodlines. They buy the *rights*. Some sell cheap. Some don't sell at all, and those houses get ash-marked by spring." He sat back, wiping his hands as if the words had dirtied them. "This year the payouts started early. Triple rate. Because this solstice isn't a renewal, whatever else it is. This year they're expecting a large harvest. The contracts use the phrase *final tithe*."

The lamp flames seemed to dim around the word. Or perhaps I did.

"How many?" Sera asked.

"In the city proper? Registered and tracked? Four thousand. In the realm—" he spread his ink-stained hands, helpless. "Who counts what they never wrote down? Every speaker, every seer, every hedge-witch and beast-talker and child born knowing what the crows say. Thirty years of final tithes, Captain. Your king has been eating a bloodline a season since before your sister disappeared."

I heard Sera breathe once, slowly, the way Vaela breathed before deciding whether to kill.

"The third thing," she said at last. "The Undercroft route."

"Yes, yes, I'll draw it." Mott was already pulling paper toward him, eager to be done with us, and then he paused, quill hovering. "One more thing. Free of charge, because I want you gone. You should know why the guards double at the palace on certain nights. Not ceremony nights. Odd nights. Rainy nights." His voice dropped further. "The king walks. Escapes his keepers somehow and walks out among the commons, in a plain cloak, and begs."

"Begs," Sera repeated.

"Asks strangers for help. Grabs sleeves in the Fishmarket. Weeps at people. Says things like *it's wearing me, please, find the name, speak the name*—and then his eyes change, mid-sentence, and he stands straight and thanks them kindly for their charity and walks home." Mott shuddered. "Half the city thinks he's mad. The other half thinks it's a test, to catch disloyal ears. Either way, everyone reports it, and the reports go nowhere, and the palace pays the informants extra to stop talking about it. Which, between us, is the strangest part. Why pay to silence rumors about a mad king unless the madness is true?"

Because it wasn't madness. I stood in the lamplight of that counterfeit-smelling room and thought of the pull I'd felt all day, the tide dragging at every hidden flame, and I thought of a man in a plain cloak grabbing strangers' sleeves in the rain, begging them for a word, a name, anything—and being silenced before he finished the sentence.

Thirty years. Thirty years of reaching out and having his own guards erase the memory of his reaching.

"We need to see him," I said.

Both men looked at me. Mott's face said *mad*. Sera's face said something longer and colder—an arithmetic running behind her eyes, the kind that ended in burned commissions.

"He's guarded," she said.

"So was Lowmark," I said. "So is everything. We've got nine days, Sera. Nine days before the final tithe." I looked at Mott. "Draw your route. All of it. And tell me—if the king walked into this room right now, wet and cloaked and begging—which of your clerks would report him first?"

Mott didn't answer. He didn't have to. The silence answered for him, and it sounded like a city full of ash-marked doors, and somewhere above us the ember pulled its slow tide through four thousand hidden hearts while its vessel wandered the rainy streets, grasping at sleeves, saying *please* to no one who could hear.

Mott drew the map. His hand shook.

And that was how we learned that in three days' time, if the rains held, the king would walk again—and that Sera Vance still had credentials that opened one particular door in the palace, and that the man behind that door might be reached before the ember swallowed his last lucid hour, and that nine days was both forever and no time at all.

Outside, the rain began. Somewhere in the Fishmarket, I would swear ever after, a man in a plain cloak lifted his face to it and wept.
