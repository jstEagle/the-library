## 7. What the King Remembers

The palace had a smell Sera had forgotten: beeswax and cold stone and, underneath it all, the faint char of something always burning. She stood in the servants' corridor with a tray of wine she had no intention of delivering, wearing the gray livery of a house steward, and felt thirty years collapse into the space of one breath.

Her credentials still worked. That was the obscene part. Captain Sera Vance, deserter, traitor, her face on warrants in every garrison from here to the coast—and yet the old seal ring she'd kept in her boot lining for six years opened the service door at the rear of the Ember Court as if she'd never left. The guards didn't look at stewards. They'd never looked at stewards. The crown's blindness had always been upward.

*You shouldn't be here,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had once written reports with clean margins and believed them.

*Yes I should,* answered the part that had burned her commission in a garrison brazier and watched the ash curl like a small black bird.

She moved through the corridors counting doors, matching the palace of her memory against the palace of now. More guards. More black-and-gold. And everywhere, nailed to doorframes or painted above lintels, the ash-mark—a handprint smeared in gray—marking rooms where gifted servants had once worked and no longer did. She passed a laundry where forty women should have been folding; four remained, and none of them sang. In her day they had sung constantly. She'd complained about it once, in writing. She wanted that report back so she could eat it.

The king's lucid hour was rumor even among the hunters. Once a month, sometimes less, the Ember Court would empty itself of ministers and the king would be walked in the high gallery alone—or as alone as a man ever was, with his shadow of handlers just out of earshot. Sera had dismissed the rumors as sentiment. Kings were not lucid. Kings were instruments, and this one had signed every edict she'd ever executed with a steady hand.

But Casper had said: *he begs strangers for help before the ember silences him.* And Vaela's message, carried down the ridgelines in the wolf's low voice, had said the same thing in older words: *the vessel still floats beneath the flood.*

So Sera waited in the gallery behind a screen of carved ash-wood—the irony of the wood not lost on her—with a steward's tray going warm under her hands, and when the bells rang the ninth hour she heard the shuffling approach of a small procession, and then, unbelievably, silence, and then footsteps. One set. Slow. Alone.

She looked through the fretwork and saw the king of Vhalen for the first time in twelve years.

He was smaller than his portraits. That was the first shock. The second was his eyes: they were the color of banked coals, orange bleeding outward into red at the rims, and they were moving constantly, darting, a man reading a room only he could see. His hands trembled at his sides. He wore no crown. He wore a plain wool robe belted with rope, and around his throat, half-hidden by the collar, a scarred circle of flesh like an old burn—the mark of a collar long removed, or long grown into.

He stopped at the gallery window and looked out over the city, and he wept. Quietly, without drama, the way a man weeps who has done all his weeping years ago and is only doing maintenance now.

Sera stepped out from behind the screen.

The king turned. For one heartbeat his coal-bright eyes went wide with terror, and he glanced past her shoulder toward the far door, calculating distances like a prisoner measuring a yard. Then something in his face settled, and he said, in a voice sanded down to gravel:

"You're not one of mine."

"No, Majesty."

"Then you're either a very bold assassin or a very stupid thief." He almost smiled. "I find I'm hoping for the thief. Assassins are merciful, and I've had enough mercy."

"I need to know about the binding," Sera said. "The ember. What it is. What you gave it. How it can be unmade."

The change in him was immediate and terrible. His whole body flinched as if she'd struck him, and his eyes flicked to the corners of the room, to the shadows, to the air itself—as if the walls might be listening, and perhaps they were. When he spoke again his voice dropped to a whisper scraped nearly silent.

"Say nothing of it aloud. It hears its own name. Even now—" He pressed a hand flat against his sternum, over the burn-scar. "Even now it stirs. You came at the right hour. Or the wrong one. They are often the same."

* * *

They talked in the window alcove, the king seated on the stone bench like any tired old man, Sera standing where she could see both doors. Below them the capital sprawled in winter haze, chimney smoke rising in ten thousand threads, and somewhere among those threads, she knew, were people like Casper—hidden, hunted, counted down like coins in a treasury.

"You want the truth," the king said. "Very well. I was twenty-six, and we were losing the war."

"The Border War," Sera said. "Before my time."

"After my time, too. It ended the night I made the bargain, though the histories give the credit to my generals." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "We were starving in the passes. My father dead three months, the throne barely warm under me, and the enemy holding every river crossing. And a man came to my tent—not a man. Wearing a man. He offered me victory by spring. Complete victory. He asked only one payment."

"A soul," Sera said.

"Not mine." The king's hands closed on his knees. "That's what no one understands. I refused to give mine—I was proud, I was pious, I told myself God would not count it against me if the price was other men's. The ember feeds on the gifted. On bloodlines that carry the old fire. It eats their gift, and when the gift is eaten the person remains—hollowed, dimmed, useful. Some die. Most simply... fade. Become the sort of people who never sing in laundries."

Sera thought of her sister. Of the record she'd pulled two years ago, the intake ledger from the Ash Office, the entry that read *transferred* with no destination listed. Transferred. Like grain. Like coin.

"How many?" she asked.

"I stopped counting at nine thousand." He said it without inflection, which was worse than any inflection. "It grows stronger with each one. At first it could only speak through me in dreams. Then it could move my hand while I slept—I woke with edicts signed I had no memory of writing. Now there are days—I am told there are days—when I am simply not present. I stand in council chambers and vote wars I don't remember declaring. The lucid hours grow shorter each year. This one—" he looked up at the light through the glass, measuring it like a man measuring the sun through water, "—this one may be the last long one I get before the solstice."

"The eclipse," Sera said.

"You know." Something flickered across his face—not fear. Relief. A drowning man seeing a rope thrown by someone else's hand. "Then you know what happens at the eclipse. The binding renews. It must be renewed by a living vessel, freely given, or it frays. Thirty years it has fed without renewal because I keep giving. But I am running out, Captain. There is less of me every season. At the solstice, if the bargain is renewed, it will take everything that remains—and it will have a vessel strong enough to last another century. If it is not renewed..."

"It dies?"

"It *unmakes*. All bargains end, one way or another. But it cannot be broken from outside—it must be unwoven from within, in the place where it keeps me. Someone has to enter the mindscape and reach whatever is left of the man who made the bargain." He looked at her then, directly, and his coal-rimmed eyes were wet. "I have begged strangers. Did you know that? Twice a year, in my lucid hours, they let me walk the gardens, and I beg gardeners, stable boys, visiting merchants—*help me, help me, I'll give you anything*—and the ember reaches me before they can answer, and I watch their faces go polite and confused, and I am marched inside. Do you know what that does to a man? To be the most powerful person in the realm and unable to finish a sentence?"

"Why tell me how to kill your bargain?" Sera asked. "You signed the edicts. Nine thousand people. Hunters like me burned villages on your seal."

"Yes." He didn't flinch. That, at least, she granted him. "And when I am free—if I am ever free—I will not ask for pardon. I will walk to the gates and open my wrists on the steps if the people wish it. But that is afterward. First the thing must die, and I am the only one who knows its true name, and I have been carrying this name for thirty years like a stone in my mouth, waiting for someone strong enough to carry it out."

He leaned close. His whisper was so soft she had to bend to catch it, and what he whispered was not a word in any language she knew—it was a sound, a sequence of sounds, that seemed to bend slightly wrong in the air, that made the hairs on her arms rise and the wine in the tray tremble against the glasses. Three syllables. She locked them away behind her teeth and felt them sit there, hot as coals.

"That name, spoken in the mindscape by a gifted tongue, unmakes the first bargain," he said. "Not breaks it. *Unmakes* it. As if it had never been. Every chain falls. Every hollowed soul—" he pressed his hand to his chest again, "—whatever is left of us goes free. Do you understand? Whatever is left. I do not expect there is much."

Footsteps in the corridor. Many of them, measured, unhurried—the tread of men who owned every room they entered.

The king rose in one motion, and the transformation was horrifying to watch: spine straightening, face smoothing, the trembling hands gone still, the coal-eyed wreck replaced in a breath by a monarch. Only his eyes stayed desperate, fixed on hers.

"Go," he murmured. "Now, through the screen, the stair behind it goes to the kitchens. Captain Vance." Her blood froze—he knew her, he'd known her since she stepped out, of course he had, he'd signed her commission. "You were the best hunter I ever fielded. Be better than that. Be the last one."

The doors opened. Four guards in black-and-gold, and between them a thin man in gray robes whose eyes were the same banked-coal orange as the king's, and who smiled at Sera with perfect courtesy as the procession closed around the king and drew him away, gently, the way one draws away a sleepwalker.

The robed man's gaze held hers a moment longer than courtesy required.

* * *

Sera went through the screen, down the kitchen stair, past a scullery boy who didn't look up, out through the service door her seal still opened, into the alley, into the streets, walking fast without running because running drew eyes. Only at the river gate did she let herself breathe, and only when she reached the tannery district did she let herself think.

The robed man had known. Not suspected—*known*. Which meant by nightfall the Ember Court would know a hunter had reached the king in his lucid hour, and every gate, every road, every bounty board in the city would carry her face by morning.

She found Casper where she'd left them, in the loft above the tannery, feeding scraps to Vaela's messenger crow and pretending not to talk to it. They looked up, saw her face, and went still.

"We're leaving tonight," Sera said. "All the ways out are closed after dawn."

"What happened in there?"

Sera sat down on the floorboards, back against the wall, and told them—all of it, the weeping king, the nine thousand, the begging in gardens, the name burning behind her teeth. When she finished, Casper was pale.

"He gave you the true name. To carry."

"He gave it to *you*," Sera said. "I'm just the escort. I can't speak it—only a gifted tongue can unmake the bargain, and only inside the mindscape." She looked at Casper, at this smuggler's guide who talked to ravens, who had crossed the Thornfells on foot to save people they'd never met. "Nine days. The ritual chamber is beneath the Ember Court, and after tonight we'll never get through the front door again."

Casper was quiet a moment. Then the crow on their shoulder croaked, low, and Casper cocked their head as if listening—which, Sera reminded herself, they were.

"Vaela says," they translated slowly, "that the catacombs run under the river. And that the flooded sections connect to the old temple foundations." Their eyes came up, frightened and resolute. "And that we should trust no one in the undercity. Especially anyone who offers to help."

Outside, the bells began to ring the changing of the watch, and somewhere below, faint but unmistakable, Sera heard the criers calling a new proclamation—and thought she caught, threaded through the street noise, the sound of her own name.
