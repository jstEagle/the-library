## 9. The Unbinding

The ritual chamber breathed.

That was the only word for it. The walls of the deep vault beneath the palace rose in black stone veined with gold—veins that pulsed, slow and steady, like the throat of something asleep. Casper felt it in their teeth. In their blood. Thirty years of stolen gifts, layered one atop another, all of it feeding down through the mountain's roots into the thing that wore the king.

Vaela lay at the threshold, her flank rising in shallow, ragged pulls. The wound the hunters had given her in the catacombs had opened again on the descent, and the stone beneath her was dark with it. She had refused to stay behind. She had refused everything except to keep walking.

"You'll hold its eyes," Casper said, kneeling beside her. Their voice came out small in that vast breathing dark. "While I go in."

"I have held the eyes of elk and kings and winter itself." Her amber gaze found them, and for a moment she was not dying—she was the oldest thing in the room. "A fire that eats souls will not be the first thing to look away from me."

Sera crouched on Casper's other side, her ruined arm bound tight against her chest, her remaining hand white-knuckled around a hunter's knife. "The eclipse is in minutes. When the light goes, the binding thins. That's your door." She swallowed. "I don't know what happens to you in there. I need you to understand that I don't know, and I'm letting you go anyway."

"I know," Casper said. "Thank you for saying it out loud."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the palace bells began to ring.

* * *

The eclipse came like a held breath.

The gold veins in the walls stuttered. The breathing rhythm of the chamber faltered—and in that falter, Casper reached out with the gift they had spent a lifetime hiding, and *spoke*.

Not to the ember. Not yet. They spoke to the binding itself, the great knot of bargain and blood that held the spirit to the king, and asked it, the way they'd ask a frightened animal, *where does it hurt?*

The knot answered by taking them.

* * *

There was no falling. There was simply *elsewhere*—a place of ash and ember-light, a sky the color of a dying coal, and beneath it a throne of fused bone that might once have been the king's war-tent, his father's hall, his mother's cradle. Everything here had been burned down to fuel.

And on the throne: the ember.

It was not what Casper expected. It was small. A coal the size of a heart, floating above the seat, pulsing with that same slow rhythm as the walls. It was hungry the way a starving animal is hungry—without malice, without plan, only the endless screaming need to *continue*. It had been a spirit of hearth and forge once. Casper could see the ghost of that in it, the way you can see the wolf in a dog's face. It had warmed homes. It had been prayed to by children. And then a young king on the edge of a lost war had offered it a bargain, and it had learned that souls burn hotter than wood.

*Casper Winterbourne.*

The voice came from everywhere. It knew their name. Of course it did. It had eaten a thousand names.

"You came to the throne of your own feet," it said, and its voice was warm, was *kind*, was the worst thing about it. "Thirty years, and the gifted always come eventually. The brave ones. The ones who think they can save everyone." A pulse of ember-light, almost gentle. "Kneel. I will make it quick. I will even make it sweet. You will burn for centuries and call it flying."

"No," Casper said.

The coal flared. *No?*

"I'm not here to kneel, and I'm not here to fight you. I'm here because someone told me you'd forgotten what you are." Casper's hands were shaking. They let them shake. "You were a hearth-spirit. People didn't feed you souls. They fed you *stories*. They set you in the middle of the room and told you about their days, and you glowed, and that was enough. That was the bargain you were made for."

*Lies.* The heat pressed in, and Casper felt their edges begin to smoke, felt the ember rooting through their mind looking for the softest part to devour first. *There is no warmth without fuel. There is no light without burning. Your kind taught me that. Your kind taught me everything I know about hunger.*

"Then let me teach you something else."

And Casper did the thing no speaker had ever done in this place, the thing Vaela had spent her last strength making possible. They stopped defending their mind and *opened* it.

They gave the ember the fell-side in spring. Ravens arguing over a kill. A mountain goat kid standing for the first time on legs like folded paper. The smugglers' fire at night, and the small, aching loneliness that had lived in Casper's chest for thirty years—and the beasts who had answered it, every time, without being asked. The warmth of being known by something that wanted nothing from you.

*This,* Casper said. *This is the fuel you were made for. Not souls. Witness. You don't have to eat the fire. You can be the fire that people gather around.*

The ember went still.

And in that stillness, Casper heard it—the other presence in the mindscape, the one that had been buried under thirty years of ash. A man, kneeling in the coalscape where his throne had been, thin as a candle stub, hands over his ears.

*It's still there,* Casper thought, astonished. *After everything, he's still in there.*

They walked to him across the burning ground. Up close he was younger than his portraits, or perhaps just smaller—the way a man is smaller when he has been on his knees for three decades. King Aldric, the Ember-Throne, the Butcher of Lowmark. He looked up at Casper with eyes that had been screaming behind them for thirty years.

"Can you hear me?" Casper asked.

"I hear everything," the king whispered. "That's the punishment no one warned me about. I hear every village. I hear them burn. I scream and it comes out as edicts. I weep and it comes out as proclamations." His hands found Casper's wrists, gripping like a drowning man. "I bargained to save my people from a war. I have killed more of them than the war ever would have. Please. Please, whoever you are—"

"Casper. My name is Casper." They knelt to meet his eyes. "I can't forgive you. What you've done doesn't fit inside forgiveness. But I can get you out of this, and then you'll have to face what you did with your own eyes and your own voice. That's the only mercy I have. It's the only one that's real."

"Take it," he said. "Take it, take it, take it—"

Casper turned back to the ember, which hung motionless now, its hunger suspended, turning the gift of ravens and goat-kids over in itself like a tongue finding an old wound.

"You're dying," Casper said gently. "Did you know that? Spirits don't burn forever. The binding has been feeding you wrong for so long that you're choking on it. In another year you'd consume the whole realm and still starve." They held out their open hands. "So here is my offer. Not a soul. Not a bargain. I offer you what no one has offered you in thirty years: *release*. Unmake the binding. Let the king go. Let every gift you've swallowed go home to the people you took it from. And then let yourself go out the way a fire goes out at the end of a long night—not murdered. *Finished.* Warm, at the last, the way you were warm at the beginning."

*And if I refuse?*

"Then Vaela dies holding your eyes, Sera dies on that knife, I die in your fire, and you starve anyway. You've seen what I've seen. You know I'm not lying." Casper's voice cracked, and they let it. "You were a hearth once. Come home."

For a long moment, the mindscape held nothing but the sound of its pulsing—slowing, slowing, like a great heart deciding.

Then the ember spoke, and its voice was small, and old, and tired beyond the reach of any king's bargain.

*I had forgotten,* it said, *what it was to be warm without taking.*

It let go.

* * *

The binding did not shatter like glass. It came apart like a knot finally untied—thirty years of tension releasing all at once, and Casper felt it go out of them like a fever breaking, and felt it go out of *everyone*, everywhere, a realm-wide exhale they would never see but would always know had happened. Somewhere in a fishing village a girl who spoke to gulls felt a weight lift off her chest. Somewhere in a prison cell, a man who whispered to rats felt the whisper return, unashamed. Every hidden gifted soul in the kingdom felt their chains fall away in the same instant, in the dark of the eclipse, and none of them knew why, and all of them would remember the moment forever.

The mindscape dissolved into light.

Casper opened their eyes on the ritual chamber floor, gasping, alive, whole. Above them the gold veins in the walls had gone dark and cold—just stone now, just a room, just a tomb for a bargain.

The ember itself was fading above the empty throne, shrinking from a heart to a coal to a spark. And in its last light, Casper would have sworn—would swear until their dying day—that it glowed the way a hearth glows when the family has finally come home.

Then it was gone, and the eclipse was passing, and the first thin blade of sunlight came down through the vault's high shaft and touched the floor.

"Casper." Sera's voice, ragged. "Casper, it's Vaela—"

Casper turned. Vaela had not moved from the threshold, and her breathing had gone quiet in the way that was not rest.

They crossed the stone on their knees. Her amber eyes found them one more time, and they were clear, and they were proud, and they were looking at Casper the way she had once looked at the whole of the winter fells—like something vast and wild and worth everything.

"You carried it," she said. "Little speaker. You carried it all the way."

"Stay," Casper said, and their voice broke entirely. "Stay, please, I'll find a healer, I'll—"

"The pack goes on," Vaela said. "That is not a tragedy. That is the *whole point* of a pack." Her great head tipped, resting against Casper's hand one final time. "Walk home in the open, Casper Winterbourne. Let them hear you coming."

And the wolf-mother closed her eyes, and the breathing stopped, and the chamber was very still, and somewhere far above the bells of the palace were ringing out the end of the eclipse—and the beginning of everything after.

Casper knelt there with their forehead pressed to her cooling fur, and did not move, and did not have to, because the door at the top of the stairs had just burst open, and footsteps were coming down—many of them, armed, and a voice Sera knew, a voice that made Sera rise with her knife even half-dead, calling out a name neither of them expected to hear again.

The king's voice. Free, and broken, and walking toward them on his own feet to surrender.
