## 10. Emberless

The king came down from his tower on the ninth morning after the eclipse, and Casper watched him do it.

He walked alone. No guards, no heralds, no crown. He had set it on the palace steps himself, Casper heard later, like a man leaving a hat at a door he didn't intend to knock on again. The gold caught the light and no one picked it up. It sat there through the whole of what followed, and by evening a gull was standing on it, and somehow that seemed right.

The square before the palace had filled without anyone calling it to fill. Word moved the way water moves—finding every crack, rising in every low place. Farmers who'd lost daughters to the harvest wagons. Mothers with ash-marked lintels who had spent thirty years pretending the marks were soot. Speakers who had come out of hiding for the first time since childhood, standing at the edges of the crowd with their hands shaking, waiting to learn whether the world had changed or only shifted its mask.

Casper stood among them, hood down for the first time in the capital. Sera stood at their shoulder, her empty sleeve pinned and folded with a soldier's neatness, her face gray with pain she refused to name. Vaela's blood was still under Casper's fingernails. They hadn't washed it off. They couldn't have said why.

The king reached the bottom of the steps and stopped. He was not an old man—thirty years of the ember had aged him strangely, hollowing him while leaving his eyes too young—but he looked ancient that morning, the way a burned house looks ancient the day after the fire.

"I am Aldric Marren," he said, and his voice carried without magic, just the raw force of a man who had decided to stop hiding behind everything he'd hidden behind. "And I am going to tell you what I did."

He told them. All of it. The war going badly in his twenty-fifth year, the ember-spirit rising from the old foundations beneath the city, offering victory for a binding—a tether, a feeding line, a throne sustained by something older than thrones. He told them how small the first bargain had seemed. One gifted family, surrendered quietly. Then another. Then the edicts, the hunters, the black-and-gold wagons, the ash-marks on doors where no one would ask questions twice. Thirty years of telling himself each soul was the last one the realm could spare.

"I told myself I was keeping the peace," he said. "I was keeping my own skin warm. There is no word for what I was except *thief*, and I will not dress it up."

Someone in the crowd shouted *murderer*. Someone else threw a stone. It struck his cheek and drew blood, and he did not flinch, and Casper understood then that he had been hoping for exactly that—that the people would give him back some fraction of what he'd fed away, because he could not give it back himself.

"I abdicate," he said, when the shouting wore itself out. "Not to a successor. There is no succession built on this ground. I stand down as king, and I place myself outside the crown's protection—which means outside all protection. No court. Courts are where kings go to be forgiven. You will judge me instead, all of you, however you judge me, wherever you find me." He looked out over the crowd, and his voice cracked on the last of it. "I will not run. That is the only thing left I can promise you."

Then he turned and walked into the city, unarmed and uncrowned, and the crowd parted around him the way water parts around a drowning man, and let him go.

* * *

They buried Vaela three days later, on a ridge above the tree line where the wind never quite stops talking.

Sera insisted on carrying her half the way, though one arm made it a slow, lurching labor and Casper finally took the weight from her without asking permission. The wolf's body was heavy as grief itself. Casper had wrapped her in the grey cloak Sera had worn through the catacombs, stained past saving anyway.

There were no words Casper knew for burying a wolf. So they spoke the ones they knew—the quiet speech of the fells, the one they'd used all their life in secret—and told the mountain what it was receiving. *Old mother. Teacher. The last of the north pack and the best of it.* The wind took the words and did with them what wind does.

Sera dug with her good arm until Casper made her stop and finished it alone. When the earth was closed, Sera stood over the mound for a long time, saluting with the hand she had left, which was the most honest prayer she owned.

"She held the spirit's attention," Sera said. "Nine minutes. Maybe ten. Long enough." Her jaw worked. "She knew what it would cost her when she started running down that tunnel. I saw it in her face. She ran anyway."

"She said once," Casper said, "that a life is measured by what it stands between. Not how long it lasts."

Sera looked at them. "Did she say that, or did you need her to?"

"Both," Casper said. "She'd have liked that answer."

Below them the capital lay in the valley, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, ordinary smoke, cooking smoke, the smoke of people simply living—and here and there, if you knew how to look, small bright flickers where gifted folk were testing their gifts openly for the first time. A child levitating stones in a mason's yard. An old woman trading weather-sense for bread. The chains had fallen off everyone at once, the moment the binding shattered, and the realm was still learning what its own hands could do.

"Rebuilding will take years," Sera said. "The hunters' records are a mess. Half the taken are dead; some aren't. Somebody has to find out which is which, and return the ones who can come home, and account for the ones who can't." She flexed her remaining hand. "Somebody with a guilty conscience and a working knowledge of how the machinery worked."

"You're staying, then."

"Somebody has to," she said. "And it turns out I'm bad at walking away from things. Took me forty years to notice." She glanced sideways. "You're going home."

It wasn't a question. Casper nodded.

"The Thornfells," Sera said. "Back to the passes." A pause. "Alone?"

Casper thought about it honestly, which was more than they'd ever been able to do before. "For a while. But—" They looked at the valley, at the flickers of open light. "I don't think I'll be hiding anymore. And I don't think I'll be alone. There are others up there. There always were. We just never spoke to each other—only to the beasts. That was the whole sickness of it, maybe. Speaking to everything except each other."

Sera huffed, almost a laugh. "You'll write, then. If you can find someone to carry letters up a mountain."

"There's always a smuggler," Casper said. "There's always been a smuggler."

* * *

Casper left the capital at dawn two days later, on foot, the way they'd come.

The lowlands were strange now—strange in a good way, the way a room is strange after you've opened windows shut for thirty years. People met their eyes in the road. At a waystation outside Thornmark, an innkeeper looked at Casper's weathered cloak and mountain stride and asked, plainly, "Speaker?" and when Casper said yes, the woman set an extra bowl of stew in front of them and refused payment. "My brother went into a wagon eleven years ago," she said. "If you're why they stopped coming—then eat."

Casper ate. It was the best meal of their life, and it was only stew.

The foothills came after, and then the high passes, and with the altitude came the thing Casper had been both dreading and starving for: the voices.

They found them at the first ridge. Ravens first, of course—it was always ravens—wheeling down out of a cold blue sky to land on the rocks beside the trail. *Small walker,* the eldest croaked, head cocked. *You smell of cities and burning. You smell also of the old mother. We smelled her death on the wind a moon ago. We screamed it across three valleys.*

"She's buried on the high ridge above the capital," Casper said. "Facing north."

*Good,* said the raven. *North is correct for her.* And then, softer, in the way ravens are soft, which is not very: *We mourned. We are still mourning. Come walk with us a while and tell us everything.*

So Casper walked and told them everything, and the ravens carried pieces of it ahead down the valleys, and by the second day the mountains already knew.

Mountain goats stood on impossible ledges and called their gruff greetings—*the quiet guide returns, the quiet guide, say it isn't quiet anymore*—and Casper laughed aloud on the trail, alone, for the first time in memory. A fox trotted beside them for an hour near the tree line asking sharp, delighted questions about the capital and expressing elaborate contempt for every answer. In a high meadow, wild horses came to the fence-line of their own territory and regarded Casper with deep brown patience, and one of them, an old mare, touched her nose to Casper's chest, right over the heart, and breathed there, and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of speech.

*I heard what you did,* the mare's silence said. *We all heard.*

On the fourth day Casper crossed the pass above Lowmark.

They had meant to go around. They found themselves on the trail anyway, drawn the way a tongue finds a broken tooth, and stood at the edge of the village in late afternoon light. It had been rebuilt—or was being rebuilt. New timber, bright and pale against the charred foundations that had been deliberately left standing, a few of them, as remembrance. The well was new. On the old well-stone, dragged up from the ruins and set beside the new one like a grave-marker, someone had carved fresh letters over the old proclamation's ghost:

**THEY WERE OUR NEIGHBORS.**

A woman was drawing water. She saw Casper standing there, saw the way they looked at the stone, and set down her bucket.

"You're passing through?" she asked.

"Yes," Casper said. And then, because the world had changed and they were done being careful: "I knew someone who died here. A dog. He stayed with his person until the end, and then he told me who came, and I ran, and I've felt guilty about running ever since."

The woman studied them for a long moment. "Guilt's for things you chose wrong," she said. "You chose to live. He'd have wanted that. Dogs always do." She lifted her bucket. "There's room at the inn tonight, if you want it. We're short-handed building the east wall. Pay's poor but the soup's honest."

"I'll work the wall," Casper said.

And they did—one day, then two, hauling timber and stone alongside people whose village had burned because of what Casper was, and finding, to their astonishment, that there was nothing to forgive on either side, or that forgiveness had already happened somewhere below words, in the simple fact of shared labor and shared bread. On the third morning a child of six, gap-toothed and solemn, tugged Casper's sleeve and whispered, "Mam says you talk to animals. Can you ask our goat why she kicks the fence?" Casper asked. The goat's reasons were petty and magnificent. The child laughed like bells. Something in Casper's chest, wound tight for thirty years, loosened another turn.

* * *

They reached their own valley on a morning of thin gold light, the kind the Thornfells save for autumn, when the frost comes silver and the sun comes warm and the two lie together on the grass like old friends.

Home was a smuggler's hut, abandoned, half-collapsed, that Casper had repaired in secret over seven years. Home was the smoke-vent and the drying racks and the flat stone where they'd talked with goats while the caravan slept. Home was small and cold and theirs.

Casper stood at the top of the slope and looked down at it, and did not go down right away, because the slopes were speaking.

Every hillside, every crag, every scree-run: voices. Goats and ravens, a marmot whistling news, a golden eagle circling high with a cry that needed no translation. *The speaker comes home. The speaker comes home unhidden.* Word had outrun them the whole way, passed mouth to wing to hoof across a hundred miles of stone, and the whole mountain had turned out—not to watch, but to welcome. To say: *we knew. We always knew. We kept your secret, and now you don't need one, and we are glad, we are glad, we are glad.*

Casper stood in the noise of it, the wild chorus of a mountain range saying their name, and wept without shame, and laughed without fear, and understood at last what Vaela had been trying to teach them in the frozen fells when they'd been too hungry and too frightened to hear it:

Survival was never free. It cost the wolf-mother her life. It cost Sera her sword arm. It cost a king his crown and a realm thirty years of its own children. Every freedom in the world is paid for by somebody, usually the one who least can afford it.

But standing there, on their own slope, under their own sky, named aloud by every living thing that knew them—

It was worth its price.

Casper walked down to the hut, pushed open the door, and began to build a fire.

Outside, on the flat stone, a raven landed, folded its wings, and settled in for a long stay. "Well?" it said. "Start talking. We have thirty years of questions, and winter's coming, and this time—" the bird ruffled its feathers, satisfied, "—you're going to answer every single one."

Casper smiled, put the kettle on, and pulled up a second stone to sit on.

"Ask," they said.
