## 1. The Thief Who Kept Nothing

The ferryman was dying the way old men die in Vell: slowly, stubbornly, and with a great deal of complaint about the weather.

Sable crouched in the reeds across the water from Old Bram's hut and watched the last light go amber on the river. He had been watching for two hours. That was part of the work. Anyone could take a thing from a man; the craft was in knowing which thing, and when, and how to leave the taking invisible. In Vell, where the covenant kept magic out and the Order's eyes beyond the ridgeline, a thief's only real enemy was memory—other people's, mostly, but his own worst of all.

He checked the sky again. The geese had gone south three weeks early this year, and the frost had come with them, silvering the reeds each morning a little harder than the morning before. Winter was closing the passes soon. After that, no one crossed into Vell or out of it until thaw, and Sable's trade dried up like everything else. This was his last job of the season. He wanted it done clean.

Old Bram had been a ferryman for fifty years, poling travelers across the Vellwater before the bridge went up, and he had a lifetime of crossings stored behind his eyes. Memories were the only currency worth stealing here. The valley had little gold and less use for it. But a man's recollection of his daughter's wedding, of the flood year, of the first time he'd kissed his wife under the alders—that was wealth. There were buyers downriver, past the covenant's edge, who paid well for valley memories precisely because the Order couldn't manufacture them. Magic made copies. Only living minds made originals.

Sable never kept what he stole. That was the rule, his rule, the one that kept him safe. Lift it, carry it, sell it, forget it. A thief who kept nothing could not be caught holding anything. He had built his whole life on that principle the way a mason builds on stone: the rented room with nothing personal in it, the face people described as "ordinary, I suppose," the friendships kept shallow and warm, like bathwater. Everyone in Vell knew Sable Sandoval. No one in Vell knew him. He'd worked hard to arrange it that way, and he told himself, most days, that it was the same thing as being free.

He crossed the water in Bram's own skiff, which felt like poor manners but saved time, and let himself into the hut without forcing anything. The door hadn't been locked in forty years. Inside, the room smelled of woodsmoke and river mud and something else underneath—the sweetish, faintly sour scent of a body finishing its business. Bram lay in his bed by the window, a blanket to his chin, his eyes open and fixed on the ceiling beams.

"You're the thief," Bram said. Not a question.

Sable went still in the doorway. "I don't know what you mean."

"Course you don't." The old man coughed, a wet sound, like a shovel in gravel. "Everyone says that. Sit down, boy. My hands are past working and my eyes aren't far behind, and I'll be dead before the ice is off the river. I sent for you."

"I don't do house calls."

"You do tonight." Bram turned his head with visible effort and looked at him, and his eyes were clear in a way that dying eyes sometimes are, terribly and briefly. "I've got sixty years of crossings in this skull and no one to give them to. My girl died in the fever year. My wife's been gone twenty springs. If I leave them to rot with the rest of me, they're gone forever. So I'm asking you to steal them. Properly. All of them."

"That's not—" Sable stopped. It *was* what he did. It just wasn't supposed to be asked for. "It doesn't work like charity. When I lift a memory, the person loses it. You'd die empty."

"I'd die anyway." Bram smiled, and there was river gravel in the smile too. "Better my memories cross the water one more time than drown with me. Call it a last fare. I'll pay you the usual way—you keep your cut, sell the rest. I only ask one thing." His hand came out from under the blanket, trembling, and closed on Sable's wrist with surprising strength. "Whatever you find in there. Whatever you see. You look at it twice before you sell it."

Sable should have refused. Later, much later, he would try to remember why he hadn't, and would understand that this moment—this handshake over a dying man's bed—was the exact place where his invisible life had begun to crack, though at the time it had felt like nothing at all, like a favor, like routine.

"All right," he said. "Twice."

* * *

The lifting took an hour, and it was gentle work, gentler than theft deserved. Sable sat on the stool beside the bed with his fingers light on Bram's temples and drew the memories out one crossing at a time, the way you draw thread from a spool so it doesn't tangle. He felt them pass through him: the flood year, brown water to the door lintels; a wedding under the alders, a girl laughing with flowers coming loose in her hair; the first fare Bram ever took, a peddler with a limp and a lie about the road ahead; winters, dozens of winters, ice groaning on the river like a ship changing its mind.

Bram grew quieter with each one. By midnight his breathing had gone shallow and even, and his face had smoothed out the way faces do when the weight comes off them. Near the end he gripped Sable's wrist once more.

"The garden," he whispered. "Mind the garden."

"What garden?"

But Bram's eyes had already gone somewhere else, and then they went nowhere at all, and Sable sat alone in the hut with sixty years of a stranger's life pooled inside him like water in a cupped hands.

He did what he always did. He packed up, banked the fire, left coin on the table for burial costs because he wasn't a monster, and rowed back across the river with the stolen cargo riding quiet in his head. He told himself the strange pressure behind his eyes was just volume—sixty years was more than he usually carried. He told himself he'd sort it all tomorrow, parcel it, price it, sell it downriver before the passes shut.

He told himself a lot of things that night. None of them held.

* * *

He didn't make it home. He got as far as the willow stand above the ferry landing, and there the memories shifted inside him the way cargo shifts in a boat, and one of them rose up and demanded looking at, and it was not any crossing Bram had ever made.

It was a garden.

Sable stopped walking. The willows hissed around him. He stood very still and let the image unfold, because he knew every memory in his cargo now, had handled each one like a jeweler handling stones, and this one had not been there an hour ago.

It was not Bram's. He was certain of that the way you are certain of your own name. Somewhere between the dying man's temples and the willow stand, something had slipped into the shipment—or something had been waiting in Sable all along and had chosen this night, this crowded night, to surface.

The garden was walled. High walls of grey stone gone soft with moss, and within them an impossible abundance: roses blooming out of season, fruit heavy on branches that should have been bare, grass green as a promise. Light fell through it sideways, golden, sourceless. And in the middle of the garden, among beds of coral-colored flowers he had no name for, stood a man.

The man was young. Dark-haired, lean, watchful, with the particular stillness of someone used to not being noticed—and Sable knew that stillness intimately, because he had spent thirty years perfecting it in front of every mirror in Vell.

The man was wearing his face.

Not similar. Not a resemblance. His own face, his own crooked half-smile, the small scar through his left eyebrow he'd gotten falling out of a cherry tree at nine years old—a scar no painter could have guessed, no archivist recorded, no one in the valley knew about except Sable and the tree. The man in the garden looked directly out of the memory at him, and the look was not surprised. It was patient. It was the look of someone who has been waiting a long time for tonight.

Then the image folded itself away, neat as a letter, and was gone, and Sable was standing alone among the willows with frost forming on his shoulders and his heart going like a fist on a door.

He walked the rest of the way home without deciding to. His room received him the way it always did: bare, anonymous, nothing in it that would tell a stranger anything true about him. He lit no lamp. He sat on the edge of the bed in the dark and ran the inventory of himself, the ritual he performed whenever the world tilted—name, trade, rules, exits. Sable Sandoval. Thief of memories, keeper of none. Never keep what you steal. Never be known. Never—

*Mind the garden.*

Bram had said it. Dying men said many things, but Bram had said it while looking at him, while handing him sixty years that contained no garden at all. As if the old man had known what was hidden in the thief. As if the cargo had been meant for him all along, and the ferryman's last fare was not the memories at all, but the delivery.

Sable pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. Outside, the wind had changed. It came down off the ridgeline now instead of up from the river, and it carried a smell he had never smelled in Vell in all his life, because the covenant was supposed to make it impossible:

Snow. Falling somewhere high and cold and wrong, weeks too early, and falling toward the valley.

He lowered his hands. In the dark of his careful, empty room, Sable Sandoval admitted the thing he had been refusing since the willow stand: whatever waited in that impossible garden, whoever wore his face there, it had not found him tonight by accident.

And tomorrow, before the passes closed, before the snow came down off the ridge—he was going to have to find out why.

There was an archive beneath the chapel. He'd heard the rumor for years and dismissed it, the way he dismissed everything in Vell that smelled of history. Tomorrow he would stop dismissing it.

Tomorrow he would go looking for a garden that shouldn't exist, in a book older than the valley.

He slept, eventually, badly, and dreamed of roses blooming out of season, and woke with frost on the inside of the window, spelling something almost like letters, almost like a date.
