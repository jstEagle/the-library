## 2. A Book Older Than the Valley

The chapel in Vell had no bell, because a bell would have been a promise that someone would answer it. It had a roof that leaked on the left side and a congregation of nine, most of whom came for the warmth rather than the word. Sable had attended exactly twice, both times to case the offering box.

He'd never known about the stairs.

They were behind the vestment rack, hidden by a curtain so old it had gone the color of dishwater, and they went down farther than any building in Vell had any right to be deep. The air changed halfway—colder, wetter, tasting of stone and something older than stone. Water dripped somewhere below with the patience of a thing that had been counting longer than anyone alive.

Sable stood at the top of those stairs for a long moment, the ferryman's memory folded into the locket against his chest like a coal he couldn't put down.

*You'll want the archive,* the dying man had said, or the memory of him had said it, replaying behind Sable's eyes every time he closed them. *Under the chapel. Everything true in this valley is under something else.*

Sable was not a brave man. He wanted that understood, even if only by himself. He was a careful man, a small man in the ways that mattered, a man who had spent thirty years making himself into a shape the world slid past without catching. Going down those stairs was the least careful thing he had ever done.

He went down anyway.

The water started at his ankles. The archive beneath the chapel was flooded—not ankle-deep, not knee-deep, but flooded the way a grave is filled: deliberately, completely, as if someone had decided long ago that whatever was down here should drown and had only half succeeded. Shelves rose out of black water in rows, their upper reaches furred with white mold, their lower reaches dissolved entirely. Books floated here and there, swollen fat with water, drifting like bloated fish.

And above the waterline, on the highest shelves, things had survived.

Sable waded in. His lantern threw a circle of yellow across the water, and the water gave it back doubled, so that he seemed to walk through a second drowned archive hanging upside down beneath the first. He could hear his own breathing. He could hear the drip. He could hear, very faintly, when he stopped moving, a sound he decided not to name—a soft rhythmic hush, like breathing, like turning pages.

*Everything true in this valley is under something else.*

He started with the shelves nearest the wall, where the water ran shallowest, pulling down what he could reach. Ledgers. Tax rolls from before the flood that had half-drowned the valley's history along with its low fields. A hymnal with its pages fused into one solid block. Nothing true. Nothing that made the back of his neck prickle.

Then, on the third row, at the height of his own shoulder—as if placed for a man exactly his size—he found a book bound in gray leather gone green with damp, unmarked except for a single stamped impression on the cover: a gate, open, with light coming through it.

His hands knew the weight of stolen things. This felt different. This felt like a thing that had been waiting to be lifted.

He opened it.

The first page was dated a hundred years ago, give or take—the ink browned, the hand formal, a scribe's script with little flourishes at the ends of words, the kind of writing done by someone paid by the page. *Prophecy of the Garden Revisited,* the header said, and beneath it, in smaller letters, as if the scribe had been embarrassed: *as dictated by the last keeper.*

Sable read standing in cold water up to his shins, and the cold climbed him slowly, rung by rung, as the words did their work.

Most of it was the usual prophecy mush—seasons, thrones, a garden, a winter without end. He'd heard drunk men improvise better. But then:

*And the covenant shall fail in the days of the grandson's grandson, when the frost comes early and crosses where it should not, and the Order's eaters walk unbidden through the broken door.*

Frost that crossed where it should not. Sable thought of the ferryman's hands going still. Thought of the reports from the high farms, dismissed all autumn: snow in the passes a month early, frost on windows facing *inward*, toward the valley, which no frost should do while the covenant held.

*In that hour,* the book said, *the covenant shall be reforged or lost forever, and it shall be reforged thus: by the thief with tonight's date, who keeps nothing and is kept by no one, who takes freely and gives all.*

Sable read the line again. Then again.

*The thief with tonight's date.*

It was nonsense. It was the kind of vague, slippery phrasing prophecies used so they could mean anything to anyone. Except—

Except that tonight, three nights ago, he had sat with a dying ferryman and taken the last thing the man owned. Tonight, if you counted from the night the frost first crossed inward. Tonight, if you counted the way the book seemed to insist on being counted, from some starting point a hundred years deep that ended precisely, perfectly, at him.

He turned the page. There was more, but the next leaf had been damaged—water, or fire, or a deliberate tear—and only fragments survived. *...the garden...* *...the keeper's price...* *...a soul surrendered wh—*

The rest was gone.

Sable stood in the drowned archive holding a hundred-year-old book that knew his habits, his profession, his loneliness—*keeps nothing and is kept by no one*—and felt the floor of his life tilt beneath him. Because here was the thought that arrived, unwelcome and total, like water finding a crack:

What if he had never been invisible? What if the invisibility itself had been arranged? What if his whole careful, self-made life—every choice he'd credited to his own cunning, his own caution, his own desire to be no one—had been written down a century before he was born, by a scribe with flourishes on her letters, waiting for him to walk downstairs?

He looked at the upside-down archive reflected in the black water, and for the first time in thirty years, Sable Sandoval did not know which version of himself was the reflection.

The dripping counted on. Somewhere in the dark, the hushing sound turned a page.

* * *

Wren Odile had been the archivist of Vell for eleven years, which meant she had been the archivist of a room nobody entered, guarding books nobody read, in a valley that believed history was a luxury the covenant had abolished. She had applied for the post out of the University at Corrin, expecting a quiet decade cataloguing parish records before a real appointment opened somewhere that mattered.

She had stayed because of the sound.

On her fourth night in the chapel house she had heard it—soft, rhythmic, unmistakably a page turning—from beneath the floor. She had gone down with a lamp and found the flooded archive and the drowned shelves, and she had stood in the black water listening to a book turn its own pages, and she had done the single most archivally responsible thing available to her.

She had told no one, and she had started a catalogue.

Eleven years. Four hundred and six entries. She worked from a skiff, poling between the shelf rows in the dark, recording titles by lantern-light, and once a month she descended to the deepest shelf—the one just above the waterline in the far corner, where the oldest things slept—and checked the seals.

There were seven sealed cases down there. Six had been sealed for a century. The seventh had been sealed for ninety-nine years, eleven months, and, as of this morning, four days.

She had done the arithmetic a hundred times. She had tried to make it mean nothing. Seals failed; wax crumbled; a century was simply a number. But the seventh case bore an inscription, fresh-cut relative to the others, in a hand she recognized from the Prophecy manuscript itself: *To be opened by the thief with tonight's date.*

Tonight, by her count, was the fourth day past due.

So when Wren came down her private stair that evening and found her skiff missing from its mooring line, and the water disturbed in long slow wakes, and the gray book with the open gate missing from the third row where it had sat untouched for a hundred years—she did not panic.

She took out her catalogue, turned to a fresh page, and wrote, in a hand almost as fine as the dead scribe's:

*Entry 407. The Prophecy of the Garden Revisited. Removed this evening by person or persons unknown. Condition: dry. Reader: unknown. Consequence: pending.*

Then she sat down in the skiff to wait, because whoever had taken the book had come down a stair, and stairs have two directions, and Wren Odile had waited eleven years and could wait one night more.

* * *

Sable heard the oar before he saw the lamp.

He was nearly at the vestry stair, book inside his coat against his heart beside the locket, when the light swung around the last shelf row and caught him full in the face—caught him the way he had spent thirty years ensuring no light ever would.

A woman sat in a flat-bottomed skiff, poling it with the unhurried ease of someone who had done it ten thousand times. She was perhaps fifty, gray-streaked hair pinned badly, spectacles fogged, a ledger open across her knees. She looked at him the way he imagined a customs officer looks at contraband: not angry, not surprised. Inventorying.

"You're standing in my archive," she said.

"I didn't know it belonged to anyone."

"Belong." She considered the word. "No. I keep it. Belonging is different." She poled closer, and he saw her eyes go to the shape of the book under his coat, and narrow. "Entry 406. Gray binding, gate stamp. It hasn't moved in a century. You've had it, what—an hour?"

"An hour," Sable admitted. There seemed no point lying to a woman who numbered drowned books.

"And?" she said. "Does it fit?"

He stared at her.

"The date," she said patiently. "The thief with tonight's date. I've been counting for eleven years, young man. My count says you're four days late, but counts can drift. So." She folded her hands atop the ledger, and the lantern-light steadied on her face, and Sable saw that beneath the fogged spectacles her eyes were not curious but afraid—afraid in the specific way of someone whose worst arithmetic has finally come due. "Does it fit?"

And Sable, who had lied to magistrates and lovers and his own reflection, heard himself say the truth:

"It fits."

Wren Odile nodded slowly, as if checking a final entry. Then she poled her skiff aside to clear the stair.

"Then you'd best go up," she said, "and read the rest of it. And when you're done—" her voice dropped, and the drip-counting dark seemed to lean in around her words, "—come find me. Because there's a seventh seal down here, thief, and it has your name on it. Or no name. Which may amount to the same thing."

Sable climbed the stairs two at a time, the book burning against his chest, and behind him the water went on counting, patient as ever, toward the hour when everything true in Vell would rise to the surface.
