## 4. What the Winter Eats

The frost came in the night, and by morning it had eaten the name of the baker's son.

Sable heard about it at the ferry landing, where he'd gone to return the skiff he'd borrowed—stolen, technically, though the owner was dead now and dead men don't press charges. Old Marta was there with her basket of eels, talking fast to anyone who'd listen.

"Gone," she said. "Tam's whole name. His mother says it like she's chewing on a stone. *Tam who?* She knows his face. She fed him twenty years. But ask her what she called him and there's just—" Marta made a gesture like wiping a table clean.

"Frostbite of the mind," someone said, and laughed, and nobody joined him.

Sable kept his hood up and his hands in his pockets and said nothing, because that was his craft: nothing. But underneath the stillness, something turned over. He thought of the memory he'd lifted from the dying ferryman three nights ago—the garden that shouldn't exist, his own face standing in it—and how since then the world had felt slightly wrong, like a floorboard that gives a half-inch more than it should.

Frost didn't eat names. Frost ate turnips.

He walked home the long way, past the chapel, past the flooded archive he hadn't told anyone about, and noticed the second thing.

The puddles were frozen solid, but the ice was wrong. It wasn't clear. It had writing in it—fine white filaments, branching like fern, like veins, like script. He crouched over one puddle for a long time, breath fogging, and decided the patterns almost looked like words. Almost looked like handwriting in a language he'd never learned but somehow recognized the shape of, the way you recognize a stranger's accent as being from somewhere near home.

In Vell, frost was just frost. The covenant saw to that. A hundred years ago the valley's founders had paid whatever price the covenant asked, and ever since, magic stayed beyond the ridgeline and ordinary life stayed inside it. No sorcery. no memory-eaters. No Order. Just weather, honest and dumb.

This weather was not honest or dumb.

* * *

By the third day, half the village had forgotten something.

Not big things, at first. The miller couldn't remember the tune he'd whittled to for thirty years. A boy lost the memory of learning to swim, so that he stood at the pond's edge genuinely convinced he'd drown, though his body remembered fine. Ilsa came to Sable's door with her sleeves pushed up and her jaw set the way it got when she was frightened and refusing to look frightened.

"Brann's wife forgot her wedding day," Ilsa said. "Not the marriage. The day. The whole of it, gone, and she keeps asking why everyone keeps bringing her bread."

"People bring bread when—"

"When there's been a death. Yes." Ilsa sat down without being invited, which was unlike her. "They're mourning a day that isn't dead. It's just *gone*, Sable. And here's the part I can't stop chewing on." She leaned forward. "It only takes the warm memories. The wedding days. The songs. The swimming lessons. Nobody's forgetting their griefs. Nobody's forgetting their debts."

Sable thought about that. Thought about the frost-ferns in the ice, branching, hungry.

"Winter eats first what's easiest to carry," he said. "That's not new. That's just winter."

"This isn't winter." Ilsa's voice dropped. "My family kept records, back before they were scribes for hire. Weather records. And there are stories from the last long winter—a century ago, before the covenant—about a cold that took things. Names. Faces. Whole years." She looked at him straight, and there was something in her eyes he couldn't read, some guilt-shaped thing moving behind them like a fish under ice. "The covenant was supposed to keep this out. Whatever's happening, the covenant isn't holding."

He wanted to ask her what she knew. He wanted it badly enough that his hands curled in his lap. But asking questions was how you became visible, and Sable Sandoval had spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of being furniture. So instead he said, "Then we should get ready," and started listing practical things—food, lamp oil, the passes closing early—and watched Ilsa realize he wasn't going to ask, and watched something in her face both relieve and grieve.

Later, alone, he let himself take out the stolen memory again. The ferryman's last vision: the garden under glass-bright light, hedges grown into arches, and himself—younger, unlined, unmistakably him—standing at its center with his hands open, offering something he couldn't see. Around the vision, the frost crept closer to the edges of the memory every time he held it. Eating it too. Slowly. Selectively.

It was eating toward the face.

* * *

On the fifth night, the memory-eaters crossed the ridge.

Sable was awake because he'd stopped really sleeping; he went out for wood at the hour when the dark is thickest and coldest, and that's why he saw them. Three figures coming down the high pass where no one comes down in winter, walking through snow that should have been chest-deep on them and wasn't even knee-deep. They moved without effort, without prints, the way you move in a dream. Where each one stepped, the snow dimpled inward slightly, as if the ground flinched.

They wore grey robes, and where faces should have been there was smoothness—not blankness exactly, but *unwrittenness*, skin like a page before ink. As they passed the shrine at the pass's foot—an old stone thing, moss-eaten, that every child in Vell could name—the eldest of them paused and touched it, lightly, two fingers.

The shrine forgot itself.

Sable watched it happen. The moss slid off like water. The stones' lichen-scars smoothed. And in his own head, the knowledge of the shrine flickered—he'd known that shrine his whole life, known it the way you know your own teeth—and then it was simply not there anymore. Not destroyed. *Unremembered.* He stood looking at a shape of stones and felt nothing about it, recognized nothing, and understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the air:

The covenant is already broken. It broke before the frost came. Everything since has been the sound of the door swinging shut behind us.

The memory-eaters moved on down into the valley, unhurried, patient as collectors. They weren't hunting. Not yet. They were *surveying*. You walk a field before you decide what to plant in it.

Sable did the only sensible thing. He ran home, barred his door, and sat in the dark shaking—not from fear of them, exactly, but from the collapse of the one architecture his whole life had rested on. Thirty-one years of being invisible because Vell was safe, because magic couldn't cross the ridge, because the Order was a story mothers told to make children come indoors. All of it a wall he'd never once checked, because checking meant admitting you lived behind one.

And underneath the collapse, quieter and worse: the prophecy. *The thief with tonight's date.* The garden. His own face. If the covenant was failing, then the thing the prophecy described wasn't legend. It was a schedule.

A knock at his door, soft, twice.

Ilsa slipped in before he answered, snow in her hair, and she was gray-faced. "You saw them," she said. It wasn't a question. "Everyone on the lane saw them. Half the lane has already forgotten seeing them—the frost took it while they slept. By tomorrow, Sable, no one will remember the Order came. They'll walk through the village like weather, and we'll let them, because we won't know any better."

"Unless we write it down," Sable said slowly. "Ink doesn't forget."

"Ink can be burned." She pulled something from her coat: a scrap of paper, hastily written, her handwriting slanting hard. *Three came over the pass. Grey. Faceless. They touched the shrine and the shrine forgot.* "I've made six copies. Buried five." Her voice cracked, just once. "I'm a scribe's daughter. This is the only weapon my family ever gave me."

She looked at him then with an expression he'd learn, later, to understand completely—the expression of someone carrying a secret that is about to become a betrayal, and loving you anyway, beforehand, in advance of the wound.

"Sable," she said. "There's something I have to tell you. About why the frost started when it did. About why it started after *you*—"

Outside, down the lane, a bell began to ring. Not the chapel bell. Something thinner, older, from the direction of the archive beneath the chapel—the flooded place he'd sworn he'd tell no one about, the place where a book older than the valley had named him.

The ringing didn't sound like alarm.

It sounded like an invitation.
