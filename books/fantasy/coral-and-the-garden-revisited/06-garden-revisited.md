## 6. Garden Revisited

The ferryman's memory had a smell to it, and the smell was pine and cold water and something older underneath, like stone that had been breathing for a thousand years.

Sable followed it north out of Vell, past the last farmsteads where frost had begun to eat the fences white, past the shrine where his mother used to leave bread for travelers, up into the teeth of the ridge where the covenant was supposed to keep magic out and had lately stopped trying. He walked through snow that fell upward in places. He did not let himself think about what that meant.

Three days of it. The stolen images unspooled in his head each night when he closed his eyes: a path of flat gray stones, a gate with no hinges, a garden growing wild and green in the middle of winter — impossible, impossible, the ferryman's own wonder still clinging to the images like frost on glass. The old man had carried this memory for sixty years without knowing what it was. Sable had lifted it thinking it would be coin. A dying man's last sight of something beautiful; there were collectors in the low country who paid well for beauty at the end of a life.

Instead he'd found himself standing in it. His own face, younger by nothing, older by everything, framed in flowering branches he had no name for.

Now the path of flat gray stones appeared under his boots exactly as the ferryman had seen them, and Sable's breath went short, because memory was never this accurate. Memory blurred at the edges. This was sharp as a blade laid on snow.

The gate had no hinges. It stood open anyway, the way doors do in dreams, and beyond it —

Green.

Not the stubborn, grudging green of valley pines in winter. Green like an argument against the season, vines heavy with white flowers, fruit hanging fat and ungathered, grass soft enough to sleep in. Frost stopped at the gate line as if it had hit a wall of glass. Behind him, the long winter pressed its face against the boundary and could not come in.

Sable stood in the gateway a long time, letting the warmth touch him, and hated how much he wanted it.

"You're late," said a voice.

* * *

The ghost sat on a bench beneath a tree that bore two kinds of fruit, and she looked less like a ghost than like a woman who had been sitting there so long the garden had grown around her — moss on her shoulders, ivy threaded through her gray braid, her hands folded like someone waiting for a train that comes once a century.

She watched him with eyes the color of the flooded archive's water.

"The ferryman saw you," Sable said. "Sixty years ago. Standing where I'm standing."

"I stand where anyone stands who is coming." She tilted her head. "You took his memory. Thief."

"I take things people aren't using anymore."

"And now you've brought one home." She unfolded her hands. "I am Maren. I kept this garden. Before me there was another keeper, and before her another, back to the swearing. We are the hinge, we keepers. The covenant turns on us."

"The covenant is broken," Sable said. "I've seen the winter eat memories off the vine. I've seen things cross the ridge that shouldn't be able to."

"Broken, yes. Dead, no." Maren rose, and the garden seemed to rise with her, every leaf attentive. "A broken covenant is not an ended one, thief. It is a held breath. It waits for someone to finish the sentence."

"What sentence?"

She walked, and he followed, because following was easier than admitting he'd have followed anyway. She led him down alleys of blossom to the garden's heart, where a stone basin stood full of water that reflected no sky — only darkness, and in the darkness, faint as banked coals, a pattern of light like a knot seen from very far away.

"The covenant was sworn here," Maren said. "Two realms, one promise. Magic stays beyond the ridge; the valley keeps faith below it. And the promise needed an anchor — not a word, not a stone. Words erode. Stones crack. It needed a mind. A living mind, holding the whole of the vow, every clause, every cost, every year of it, so the promise could not be forgotten even if everyone else forgot."

"A person," Sable said quietly.

"A keeper. Me, at the end. I held it for ninety years." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands did, just slightly. "Do you know what ninety years of holding looks like from inside? Every stolen thing in the world poured through one skull. Every secret the Order buried. Every name the winter ate. You hold it all or the promise fails, and you cannot set any of it down, because the moment you do—"

The basin flared. In the dark water Sable saw her — the last moment of her, a woman kneeling at this same stone, letting go, and the light going out of the knot like a lamp pinched between wet fingers.

"—the covenant breaks," Maren finished. "I died holding it. That was the design. One soul, surrendered whole. That is the price of the garden, thief. That has always been the price."

The cold came through the warm air then, finding him, finding the shape of him. *The thief with tonight's date.* Wren's voice in the archive. Ilsa's mouth saying words she'd memorized before she ever met him.

"That's why the prophecy names me."

"The prophecy doesn't name you. It describes whoever arrives. For a century, no one arrived." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing in the garden, which was saying something. "You came because you steal what people aren't using anymore. And the world has been leaving itself somewhere, waiting to be picked up."

* * *

He should have run. He knew that later, turning it over in the dark of that endless summer night the garden offered — he should have run back down the gray stones and sold the whole vision to some collector and bought a boat and gone south where winter was only weather.

But Maren showed him things instead, and he stayed.

She touched the basin and the water gave up its hoard: the valley in spring, laughing, the passes open, children swimming in the millrace with no fear of anything beyond the ridgeline. The Order's towers far away, lit and lawful and contained. A hundred years of ordinary mornings, purchased with one extraordinary suffering, renewed each generation by consent.

"This can be yours to give," Maren said. "Not your death — death is only the old way, the clumsy way. The Order will tell you they need you dead at the breaking point. They are wrong, or lying, or both. What the covenant needs is a keeper. A living anchor. Someone who holds the whole of it, willingly, forever."

"Forever," Sable said.

"Forever is just a long series of todays. I had ten thousand of them here. The flowers helped." She looked at him with those water-colored eyes. "And here is the temptation, thief, since you're honest enough to want it named: you would not suffer. Not as I suffered. The garden has learned. You would hold the promise and the promise would hold you. Warmth in winter. Bread that never runs out. Every stolen memory you've ever taken, made clean and useful, woven into the anchor instead of rotting in your skull. You would matter, Sable Sandoval. Utterly. To everyone. For all time."

"And the cost?"

"You would belong to the valley the way the river belongs to the valley. Completely." She paused. "And no one would know you. No one could. A keeper must be forgotten to function — remembered, you become a lever, and levers get pulled. The price is not your life. The price is being known."

The garden hummed around him, patient, generous, terrible.

Sable thought of Ilsa's face when she'd told him the truth — the war in it, love and betrayal sharing one expression. He thought of Wren in the flooded archive, ink-stained and certain. He thought of the ferryman dying with a garden in his head, carrying beauty he couldn't use, and Sable taking it, because that was what he did. Took what people weren't using anymore.

Maybe that was all a keeper was. The valley's last theft. Something the world had set down, waiting to be picked up.

"If I say yes," he said slowly, "when does it happen? When do I—"

The basin went dark.

All of it, at once — the coals of light snuffing out, the reflection collapsing into black water, and above the garden walls the sky shuddered like a struck bell. Frost crept across the grass in a widening ring, eating the flowers where it touched, and Maren's form thinned to smoke.

"They're coming," she said, already half-gone. "The Order. Tonight. They mean to bind you, thief, not free you — remember that when they offer you kindness. Remember—" Her voice frayed. "Remember that consent is the only coin they cannot mint."

The gate at the garden's edge began to swing shut on hinges it had never had, and beyond it, coming down the gray stones through the upward-falling snow, Sable saw lanterns. Dozens of them. And walking at their head, wrapped in a coat the color of a closed book, a figure whose face he somehow already knew he'd spend the rest of his life forgetting.

Maren's last whisper reached him like a leaf settling:

"She sold you to save her family. She's coming to unsell you. Decide, thief — decide before they arrive whether you believe her."
