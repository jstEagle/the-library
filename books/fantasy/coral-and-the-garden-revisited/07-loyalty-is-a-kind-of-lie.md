## 7. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

My family kept the prophecy for a hundred years, and we kept it the way you keep anything dangerous: in pieces, in different houses, in handwriting no one taught us to read.

I was eight when my grandmother first let me copy a fragment. She sat me at the kitchen table with a stub of pencil and a strip of paper no wider than my palm, and she dictated nine words while she stirred the pot. *The thief will come when the frost remembers.* I copied them wrong on purpose, because even then I understood that some sentences should not exist in full anywhere. She checked my work, found the error, and kissed the top of my head.

"Good girl," she said. "Now burn it."

That was my inheritance. Not the prophecy itself—anyone can write a prophecy—but the discipline of it. The cutting of truth into pieces small enough to survive. My family were scribes, which everyone assumes means keepers of books. It doesn't. It means keepers of gaps. We were the negative space around the story, and for a hundred years the negative space held.

Then Sable Sandoval walked into the ferry house where I sold passage down the Vell, and every piece of me that had been trained to lie recognized its work.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one thing I have left and I am spending it here, now, alone in a room the Order gave me because they think gratitude will make me useful. Here is the honest version: I did not fall in love with him by accident. I found him. I studied him for three weeks before I let him catch me looking. I learned his routes, his tells, the way he touched his collar when he was deciding whether to steal from someone. And then I arranged to be stealable.

He thought he chose me. Thieves always think that. But I made myself the kind of woman a man like Sable would risk everything to take—warm where he was cold, rooted where he drifted, a hearth he could imagine coming home to. I built the trap out of tenderness because tenderness was the only bait that would hold him.

And then, somewhere in the third winter, the trap closed on me instead.

That's the part I can't forgive myself for, and the part I refuse to soften: I meant it. Every night I spent in his narrow bed above the chandler's shop, every morning I watched him pretend to be nobody, I meant all of it. Loving him wasn't the lie. Loving him was the one true thing inside the lie, and I hid it there because I knew no one would ever look for the truth in the middle of a deception. Not even him. Especially not him.

So when the Order came over the ridge—when the memory-eaters crossed ground that should have burned them, proving the covenant was already broken and my family's hundred years of careful silence had bought nothing—I did what a scribe does.

I cut the truth into pieces.

* * *

They came to the ferry house at dusk, three of them, wearing grey like it was a uniform and courtesy like it was a weapon. The tall one had a face I couldn't hold in my mind; I'd look away and it would slide out of me like water through fingers. Memory-eaters don't eat memories, exactly. They eat the *having* of them. They leave you knowing something happened without being able to find it in yourself, like furniture moved in a dark room.

"We're looking for a thief," the tall one said.

"Vell is full of them."

"This one stole something that belongs to the world."

I poured tea. My hands were steady. I had practiced steadiness my whole life; it was practically the family trade. "Everything in Vell belongs to somebody who lost it."

He smiled, and the smile didn't reach whatever he used in place of eyes. Then he set a folded paper on the table between us, and I knew before I looked what it would be, because my grandmother's handwriting has been dead for twenty years and I still know it the way I know my own pulse.

A fragment. Nine words. *The thief will come when the frost remembers.*

"How," I said, and my voice came out very small.

"The chapel archive flooded forty years ago," the tall one said. "Floods move things. So do frightened people." He turned the paper so it faced me. "Your family wrote the Prophecy of the Garden Revisited, Mistress Odile. Your family broke it into pieces and scattered it. Very wise. But a broken thing can be reassembled by anyone patient enough, and we have been patient for a very long time." He leaned forward. "We know the thief's name. We know the garden is waking. What we do not know is where he is hiding, and the winter is eating this valley faster than you can imagine. Every day we wait, another village forgets its own children. You've seen it. Don't tell me you haven't."

I had. Old Marta down the lane who'd called her grandson by the wrong name for a week and then stopped calling him anything at all. The miller's boy who woke up not knowing how to read. Frost on the windows shaped like nothing, like erasure, like the world being rubbed clean from the edges inward.

"My family," I said carefully, "is in Corren. My mother. Two sisters. A nephew who just cut his first teeth."

"Yes," the tall one said, gently, the way you'd speak to someone standing on ice you intended to break. "Corren is beautiful in winter. If the winter lets it stay."

There it was. No threat spoken, none needed. Just the geography of my love laid out on the table next to my grandmother's handwriting, and me sitting between them with a teapot going cold.

I told myself I was buying time. I told myself I could give them something useless—a false direction, a name that didn't exist—and warn Sable after. I told myself the lie so smoothly that for a moment I believed it, and I realized, sitting there, that this was exactly how it must have felt to him every day of his life. The craft of it. The ease. How good lying feels in the mouth, like a key turning.

I told them about the ruined garden past the salt flats. I told them he'd go there, because the ferryman's stolen memory would pull him the way water pulls downhill. I told them the path, the landmarks, the hour he'd likely arrive.

I did not tell them I loved him. Some truths you keep even from monsters. Especially from monsters—they trade in them.

They left before dark. I sat in the empty ferry house until the lamp burned low, and I told myself the story again: buying time, warning him after, a false direction given under duress. And then I got up, and I packed a bag, and I did not set out for Corren to move my family to safety, which is what a person would do if the story were true.

I set out for the garden. To warn him. Eventually. After I'd watched. After I was sure.

That's what I told myself, walking through the falling frost with my breath freezing on my scarf. That I was going to warn him. I have since had a great deal of time, in rooms like this one, to examine that walk inch by inch, and I can tell you now what I could not admit then: I didn't know why I was going. Both stories were in me, the betrayal and the warning, braided so tight I couldn't find the seam. I loved him and I had sold him and I was running toward him and away from myself, all at once, at the speed of guilt.

* * *

I reached the garden's edge an hour past midnight, and I knew immediately that I was not the only one who had come.

The Order's lanterns were strung through the dead orchard like a festival nobody sane would attend. Grey figures stood among the black trees, patient as posts, and at the center—where the old covenant-stone should have been, where the ghost of the last keeper was said to walk—there was a structure I had only ever seen described in fragments my grandmother made me burn: a frame of white metal, jointed like a skeleton, open at the heart.

I knew what it was. God help me, I knew, because I had copied the description once, at fourteen, and burned it, and kept the burning.

A binding-frame. Not for killing. For *keeping*. The prophecy offered the Order a choice, same as it offered everyone: let the thief die and the covenant reforges itself in blood, or take the thief alive and own the anchor forever. A covenant anchored in a living mind could be steered. Tuned. The valley's magic could flow—or not flow—at the Order's discretion. They wouldn't just save the world. They'd hold its throat.

They were going to put Sable in that frame and keep him there. Not for a night. Not for a season. Permanently. A man made into a hinge, conscious, willing or not, forever—and they'd call it mercy, because the alternative was death, and because most of the valley would never know enough to grieve.

I stood behind a fallen wall with frost climbing my boots and watched them check the frame's joints, and I understood that I had done this. My directions. My hours. My grandmother's discipline turned inside out—not keeping the truth safe but delivering it, gift-wrapped, to the people who would use it to build a cage.

Somewhere beyond the lanterns, Sable was walking toward us out of the dark, following a dead man's memory, trusting the night because the night was all he'd ever trusted. And I was crouched in the ruins of the place where his fate was written, holding knowledge that could save him, and no one in the world knew I was here. No one would ever know which side I'd chosen unless I made them know. Unless I made it loud.

I thought about my nephew, who just cut his first teeth. I thought about my mother's kitchen. I thought about the tall one's smile sliding off his face like water.

Then I thought about Sable's hands, quick and careful, the night he showed me how he palmed a memory without breaking it—the gentleness of a profession that should have made him cruel and hadn't. I thought: *he never lied to me. Not once. He lied to everyone else in the world, and he told me the truth, and I repaid it with a map.*

The nearest lantern was ten paces away. The nearest grey figure, twelve. Between me and Sable lay the whole machinery of the Order, awake and waiting, and the only weapons I had were a scribe's training and a liar's nerve.

I stood up.

"Frost remembers," I said, loudly, in my grandmother's cadence, the cadence every Odile for five generations had used to carry the prophecy's pieces—and every grey head in the orchard turned toward the sound. "But so do I. And I'm done forgetting on command."

The tall one stepped out of the dark, smiling his water-smile. "Mistress Odile. Come to watch?"

"No," I said, and started walking toward the heart of the garden, toward the frame, toward the man I had betrayed. "Come to rewrite."

Behind me, far off across the flats, something answered—a light in the ruin, moving, coming closer. He was already here.
