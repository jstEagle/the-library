## 10. The Man With No Yesterday

The frost came out of the ground in the first week of Thawmonth, and with it came the smell of green things deciding to live.

Ilsa stood at her window above the chandler's shop and watched the valley of Vell wake up. Snowmelt ran silver down the gutters. The chapel bell rang wrong-footed and cheerful, the way it always did when Wren Odile was the one pulling the rope. Somewhere below, a child shouted that the river was moving, *the river is moving*, as if water were a miracle instead of a habit.

A year ago it had been a miracle. A year ago the river had been a slab of grey silence, and the frost had been eating names out of people's heads like moths in wool.

Now there was bread on the windowsills and mud on the roads and an old woman on the corner selling early crocuses she swore she hadn't planted, because no one had planted them; they'd simply come up overnight in rows too neat for accident, along the lane where nobody walked but one man.

Ilsa pulled on her coat and went out to be forgotten again.

* * *

He was in the garden by the time she reached the ridge path, because he was always in the garden by then, sleeves rolled, hands dark to the wrist with soil. The ruined walls of the old covenant-garden had come back together over the year — not rebuilt, exactly. Remembered into place, stone by stone, whenever his fingers touched them. He didn't seem to know he did it. Or maybe he did, now, and had stopped minding.

"Morning," he said, without turning around.

"Morning."

"You're late. Crocuses opened at dawn. They wanted an audience."

"I'm never late. You're just always early." She stepped through the gap where the gate had been — the gate was back too, iron and lichen-eaten, though it never quite latched, as if it remembered being broken and couldn't commit to whole. "You could come down to the village, you know. There's a market today. Wren's selling duplicates from the archive. Actual paper books. People are fighting over them."

"I don't think so." He said it lightly, the way you decline an invitation to somewhere you've already been in a dream. He straightened, wiped his hands on his thighs, and looked at her.

That was the part no one warned her about. Everyone in Vell had agreed, gently, collectively, not to remember him — the covenant took what it needed, and what it needed was a man who existed nowhere in anyone's mind. But Ilsa had made herself a different bargain. She remembered everything. Every stolen memory he'd poured back into the world had passed through her hands like water through a sieve, and some of it had stayed in the cloth. She remembered the ferryman's last crossing. She remembered the flooded archive and the way Sable's face went white over a century-old page. She remembered the longest night, the High Keeper's voice like a closing door, and Sable saying *no — a third way* — and then holding him while he unmade himself, name first.

She remembered all of it. And every morning he looked at her with the polite, open curiosity of a stranger meeting someone new, and every morning she chose to walk through the gate anyway.

"Do I know you?" he asked. He asked it most mornings. Not suspiciously. Almost hopefully.

"No," she said, as she always said. "But you will."

* * *

They worked. That was the shape their days had found: she weeded, he pruned, they talked about nothing, or about everything except the thing. He told her about the roses that had come up black along the north wall and how he thought they might be a mistake. He told her the bees had started following him from bed to garden like a debt collection. He told her, once, haltingly, that he dreamed of a ferry, and a dying man's hand on his wrist, and a face in the water that was his own — and that he woke from those dreams feeling *robbed*, which made no sense, because he'd never had anything taken.

"You had everything taken," she said, before she could stop herself.

He looked at her. Spring light moved through the new leaves between them.

"That's a strange thing to say to a stranger," he said, not unkindly.

"It is," she agreed. "I say strange things. It's why I keep coming back."

He laughed. It was Sable's laugh. That was the cruelty and the mercy of it — the covenant had taken his memories, his name, the whole architecture of himself, but it hadn't touched the grain of him. He still moved like a thief, quiet even in open ground. He still watched exits without meaning to. When she dropped a trowel he caught it before it hit the earth, fast and easy, hands that had spent a lifetime lifting things that weren't theirs now giving things back.

Wren Odile had a theory about that. Wren had many theories, and after a year of freedom from the Order's cells she delivered them the way other people sneezed. *The covenant didn't erase him,* she'd said, hunched over her desk in the re-flooded-and-then-re-drained archive, ink to the elbows. *It anchored him. You can't anchor something by emptying it. You empty it so it can hold the valley instead. Everything he was got poured out to make room for everything else.* Then she'd looked up, and her sharp archivist's face had gone briefly, unbearably gentle. *He's not gone, Ilsa. He's load-bearing.*

Load-bearing. Ilsa turned the word over every day like a coin she couldn't spend.

* * *

At midday they sat on the low wall and ate bread and hard cheese, and the valley lay below them doing its ordinary resurrection. Smoke from chimneys. The ferry running again — a new ferryman, a broad cheerful woman who waved up at the garden every time she crossed, because everyone waved at the garden now. Nobody knew why. It was simply the thing you did, the way you tipped your hat to the chapel. The garden was Vell's good luck, its quiet guardian, its *something*. Children left flowers at the gate. Farmers left bread. No one could have said who they were leaving it for.

"He doesn't know," Ilsa had said to Wren once, furious with grief. "He saved all of us and he doesn't even know there's a *he* to do the saving."

"No," Wren had said. "And that's the price working exactly as designed. He consented to it. That was the whole point — the Order wanted to bind him into a tool, the ghost wanted a corpse, and he said *no, I'll do it awake, I'll do it willing, I'll do it as a gift.*" She'd rubbed her eyes. "You can grieve it, Ilsa. You should. But don't you dare pity him. Pity would be another kind of theft."

So Ilsa didn't pity him. She visited. She brought news like offerings: the passes open, the Order's remaining memory-eaters withdrawn beyond the ridge under some new accord Wren was brokering, the crocuses, the black roses, the child born in Frostmonth — *Frostmonth*, safe and screaming and named after nobody's prophecy — with a birthmark shaped like a key.

Today he ate his bread and watched the valley and said, "I feel like I'm waiting for something."

Her heart did the thing it always did, the drop, the flare.

"What kind of thing?"

"I don't know. Like there's a door in me and someone's supposed to knock." He smiled, embarrassed. "Gardener's superstition. Ignore me."

"The gate doesn't latch," she said. "Maybe that's it. Maybe you're waiting for someone to finally close it."

"Would you want it closed?"

She looked at him. His face in the spring light, patient, open, entirely unfamiliar and entirely known.

"No," she said. "I like it the way it is."

* * *

She stayed until the light went long and gold, and then she walked down the ridge path with the day's small ache settled in her chest where it lived now, permanent as a splinter she'd decided to keep. At the bend where the path dropped toward the village she stopped, because she always stopped, and looked back.

He was standing at the gate. Watching her go. He did that sometimes — she'd caught him twice before and pretended she hadn't. Just watching, with an expression she had no right to recognize and recognized anyway: the look of a man trying to memorize something he'd been told he wasn't allowed to keep.

She raised her hand. He raised his.

Tomorrow she would come back and introduce herself again. Tomorrow he would ask if they knew each other, and she would say *no, but you will*, and they would weed the south bed, and she would tell him about the market, and he would laugh Sable's laugh, and none of it would accumulate, none of it would build, because he could not hold a single yesterday and she refused to stop carrying all of hers.

It was not the love they'd had. It was not the lie they'd had either — her family's scribes' blood, the prophecy's demand, the deliberate finding of him. This was the third thing, the thing he'd chosen on the longest night: a covenant kept willingly, daily, without reward or recognition. She had simply joined it.

*Ilsa,* she thought at him across the distance, the way she did every evening, a prayer with no addressee. *Your name was Sable Sandoval. You stole memories and gave them all back. You were born in Vell and you died on the longest night and you got up the next morning anyway. I loved you before, and I love you now, and tomorrow I'll tell you so, and you'll smile like it's the first time.*

Because sometimes — three times now, in a year, and she kept count the way misers keep coins — he said it first. Out of nowhere, mid-weed, mid-sentence, he would look at her with sudden startled tenderness and say *I love you*, and then go pink and apologize, because what a strange thing to say to a stranger.

Three times. She was almost sure it meant something. She was almost sure the man underneath the anchor surfaced sometimes, in dreams, in the space between one breath and the next, reaching for the only thing the covenant couldn't carry for him.

She was halfway down the path when the bell in the village began to ring — not the chapel's cheerful wrong-footed peal but the old alarm bell, the one that hadn't sounded since the winter, deep and urgent, rolling up the valley.

Ilsa turned.

Up at the garden, the man with no yesterday was standing very still at the gate. Beyond him, past the ridge, past the passes that should have been closed by law and covenant both, the sky above the Order's country had begun to glow — a thin, cold, silver line, like frost climbing a window.

And he was looking at it, hand pressed flat against the unlatched gate, with an expression she knew better than his own face.

Recognition.
