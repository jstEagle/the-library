## 3. Ledgers and Lies

The counting-house smelled of tallow and cold iron, the way it always did after dark, when the day-clerks had gone home to their suppers and the lamps burned low over the great sloped desks like votive flames in a temple no god visited twice.

Jude Wren climbed out of the stairwell with her heart still hammering the courier's name against her ribs and found the evening shift already assembled: three junior factors bent over their columns, a boy whose whole job was trimming wicks, and old Maren at the verification desk, squinting at a bottled storm through smoked glass to confirm it hadn't leaked. Ordinary work. Ordinary night. The Guild's machinery grinding on, indifferent, exactly as she needed it to be.

She took her seat at the reconciliation desk—the one nearest the archive wall, chosen years ago for precisely this kind of moment, though she'd never imagined needing it—and opened the ledger in front of her as though the last hour had been nothing but ink.

Her hands were not steady. She made them steady by force of will, the way she'd once made them steady around a sword hilt while men screamed, and the similarity of those two acts frightened her more than the blood on her cuffs.

*Winterbourne.* The word sat behind her teeth like a stone she couldn't swallow or spit out.

The courier was dead. That much was certain; she had felt the exact moment his pulse stopped under her fingers, that small stuttering halt she knew from the war, from holding too many men as they went. Dead men could not be interrogated. But dead men left questions, and questions in the Meridian Guild did not stay buried long. A body at the salt door would be reported by the door-watch within the hour. A body meant an inquiry. An inquiry meant Corvane.

She needed somewhere the map could sleep until morning. Somewhere no one would look, because no one ever looked there.

Jude rose, crossed to the archive wall, and drew down the ledger of unrecoverable debts.

It was a slim volume, bound in grey calf, and it was the most boring object in the building—which is to say, the safest. Every trade house kept one. When a debtor died without heirs, when a cargo sank beyond salvage, when a contract simply evaporated into the fog of bad luck, the loss went into this book, was mourned for precisely the length of time it took to write the entry, and was then forgotten forever. No auditor audited losses. Losses were the price of doing business in impossible things. To pore over them was to admit sentiment, and sentiment, in the Meridian Guild, was a greater scandal than theft.

She slid the wax-sealed roll between pages two hundred and eleven and two hundred and twelve—an entry concerning nine barrels of bottled year that had spoiled in transit, their vintage gone sour, the debtor drowned—pressed the ledger flat, and shelved it back in its place between *Unfulfilled Contracts, D–H* and *Collateral, Perishable*. The spine didn't even look different. Nothing looked different. That was the whole art of it, she thought, and hated how easily the thought came, how the old reflexes woke in her hands like dogs hearing their names.

Ten years of balancing other people's impossible trades, and it turned out she had been practicing all along for the only entry that mattered: the one she was about to lie her way through.

* * *

Master Corvane came down the archive stairs at the ninth bell, and the room changed temperature.

He did it without raising his voice, which was the thing about him. He was a narrow man, silver at the temples, dressed in the dove-grey of the audit office with a chain of office so fine it might have been mistaken for decoration rather than authority. He moved through the counting-house the way a current moves through water—you felt him before you saw him, a pressure against the skin—and every clerk in the room sat a little straighter without quite knowing why.

"Factor Wren." His voice was warm. It was always warm. That was the trap of Master Corvane: he made you feel singled out, favored, seen, right up until you understood that being seen by him was the same as being weighed. "A word, if you please."

"Of course, Master Auditor."

He led her not to his office but to the inquiry table near the verification desk, where the light was good and the witnesses were many, and Jude understood both choices at once. He wanted her comfortable enough to talk and observed enough to be careful. He was very good at this. She had watched him dismantle three factors' careers over miscounted lots of dragonbone, smiling gently the entire time.

"A man died at the salt door this evening," Corvane said. "Just before closing bell. You were the last one out."

"I heard the commotion from the stairwell." True. "I didn't see anything." Also true, if you counted the dying man himself as something other than a thing seen—a distinction so fine it whistled.

"No?" Corvane folded his hands on the table. They were elegant hands, ink-stained at the nails, the hands of a man who had never lifted anything heavier than a pen because he'd never needed to. "The door-watch says otherwise. Says a factor was kneeling over the body when they came round the corner. Dark hair, grey coat, Guild issue." He smiled apologetically. "That describes half our staff, I'll grant. But it also describes you."

Jude let the silence sit for exactly as long as honest confusion would sit before it curdled into evasion. Two breaths. She had learned timing in another life, in another war, and it appeared the skill transferred.

"I knelt," she said. "Yes. I'm sorry—I should have said so at once. He was face-down and I thought—" She shook her head, and let a little genuine horror show through, which was not difficult; the horror was real, just aimed at a different target than Corvane supposed. "I thought he might be one of ours. I turned him partway over. There was so much blood. I've never—" She stopped. Swallowed. Let the sentence die the way a civilian's sentence dies, squeamish and unfinished.

Corvane watched her do it, and she felt the watching like a lamp held close to paper.

"You've never seen a dead man before," he said. Not quite a question.

"Not like that. Not up close." This was the deepest cut of the lie, and it went in smooth as a blade through water. Ten years ago she had knelt over more bodies than she could number, had closed the eyes of friends with thumbs still wet with their blood, had learned the exact weight a human soul leaves behind when it goes. And now she stood in dove-grey lamplight and confessed to innocence she had buried a decade past, and the confession cost her nothing but a small, cold piece of herself she suspected she'd never get back.

"And he said nothing to you? Before the watch arrived?"

"He was unconscious. Or—" She frowned, honestly, searching memory she actually possessed. "Delirious. He said something when I first came upon him, but it was just... sounds. Dying men say things. Water, I think. Or mother. I couldn't tell you."

*Winterbourne.*

The name rang in her skull like a struck coin, and she kept her face still through sheer discipline, through the old training that said *your face is a ledger too, keep it balanced*, and she realized, with a lurch, that this was what the oath had really been teaching her all along—not silence, but performance. Her mentor had made her a liar the day she made her a survivor. She owed Vessa that truth, at least, wherever Vessa's ghost was keeping its accounts.

Corvane nodded slowly. Then he asked the question she had been waiting for, the one she'd have asked in his place, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards even as her voice stayed level.

"The courier carried a sealed consignment. Standard practice—everything that crosses our salt door is logged, even the things we don't open. But tonight's log shows no entry." He tilted his head, birdlike, pleasant. "A man dies carrying Guild freight, and the freight vanishes between the door and the watch's arrival. You can see my difficulty, Factor Wren."

"I can." She met his eyes. Grey eyes, patient as arithmetic. "But I can't help you. I touched nothing. I ran for the watch—or I came inside for help, I hardly remember which, my hands were shaking." She held them up, and they were shaking now, genuinely, because some part of her body had decided to stop lying on her behalf and start telling the truth, and the truth was that she was terrified. "Whatever he carried, it left with whoever cut him. Check the harbor road. Check the tide-gates. I was inside before the bell finished ringing."

Another silence. Corvane studied her the way he studied a suspect ledger—not reading the numbers, reading the spaces between them, hunting for the column that didn't sum.

And then he smiled, warm as banked coals, and the interview was over.

"Well," he said, rising. "Robbery, most likely. The lower streets breed knife-men the way cellars breed damp. The watch will drag the harbor by morning and we'll have our answer." He paused at the edge of the lamplight, and turned back, and for one unguarded instant the warmth drained out of his face entirely, leaving something older and drier underneath, something that looked at her the way an appraiser looks at a stone that might be glass and might be diamond. "You're a careful woman, Factor Wren. Your columns always balance. In ten years I don't believe I've ever caught you in an error."

"Thank you, Master Auditor."

"Mm." The smile returned, seamless as a shut door. "Careful people interest me. Errors are simple—they're just mistakes. But perfect balance..." He tapped the table once with one ink-stained finger. "Perfect balance takes *work*. Goodnight, Factor Wren."

He climbed the stairs to his office, and the counting-house exhaled around her, and Jude discovered she had been holding her breath since the ninth bell.

* * *

She worked for two hours after that because working was the only alternative to thinking, and thinking, just now, was a country she could not afford to enter. She reconciled a shipment of bottled north wind against its manifest. She initialed a dispute over a borrowed year that a merchant in Vail wanted returned early, with interest, though how one paid interest on a year of someone's life was a question the Guild answered with arithmetic and never with conscience. The ledgers were soothing. Numbers did not lie, or rather—they lied beautifully, on command, in perfect formation, and no one ever bled for it.

But under the numbers, the other ledger kept writing itself.

*I lied to Master Corvane.* Not fudged a figure, not rounded a loss, not committed any of the small venal sins the Guild forgave before breakfast. Lied. Looked a dangerous man in the eye and handed him a false world, whole and furnished, and walked away with him believing it.

And the terrible thing—the thing she turned over and over like a coin with two identical faces—was how little it had cost. One lie, and the machinery of her decade-long disguise had come back to life beneath her hands, oiled and eager, all those muscles she'd built at Ashfall Bridge and never allowed herself to use since. She had spent ten years telling herself she was a clerk who had once been something else. Tonight she'd learned the truth ran the other direction: she was a swordspeaker who had gotten very, very good at clerking, and the swordspeaker was still in there, waiting, patient as rust.

Vessa's voice, from ten years and a lifetime away: *They'll ask you questions, little bird. Lie with your whole body or don't lie at all.*

Jude put down her pen and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations.

Across the room, Maren was packing up her verification lenses. The wick-boy snuffed lamps one by one, walking the shadows closer to her desk with each pinch of darkness. Closing time. Which meant the archive, which meant the grey calf-bound ledger, which meant she had to decide—now, in the next thirty seconds—whether to leave the map where it slept or move it somewhere safer, somewhere personal, somewhere—

The map. She had put it away without examining it. Hadn't dared. Wax-sealed, blood-flecked at one corner where the courier's hand had gripped it, and she had shelved it blind, like a factor filing a debt, because looking at it would make it real and she was not yet ready for it to be real.

But it was real. A man had died whispering her true name. Corvane had stood over her weighing her like merchandise. The map existed whether she looked at it or not, and tomorrow the watch would drag the harbor and find nothing, and Corvane—who did not believe in nothing, who believed only in unbalanced columns—would come back down those stairs with better questions.

She waited until the wick-boy's footsteps faded, until Maren's key turned in the street door, until the counting-house belonged to her and the dark and the soft tick of the master clock. Then she crossed to the archive wall, took down the ledger of unrecoverable debts, and carried it to her desk, to the last burning lamp, to the truth.

The seal broke cleanly under her thumb.

The parchment inside was old—older than the Guild, older than the war, older maybe than the city—and it was heavier than paper had any right to be, dense as lead leaf, cool as riverstone. She unrolled it across her desk and weighted the corners with inkwells, and the lamplight pooled on it, and she looked at what a man had died to bring her.

At first glance: nothing. Blank vellum, faintly yellowed, marked only at its center by a single drawn symbol—a rose, rendered in a few economical strokes, its stem a spiral of thorns.

Then the ink moved.

Not smudged. Not shifted by the draft from the window. *Moved*—deliberately, slowly, the way a sleeper turns over—with lines crawling outward from the rose like roots seeking water, branching, forking, resolving into coastlines she almost recognized and channels she didn't, a whole country drawing itself out of blankness while she watched. Rivers she knew from Guild shipping charts appeared and then kept going, running inland past their own sources, into territory no chart in the Meridian archives had ever bothered to record, because no one traded there, because nothing there could be bought or sold or survived.

The Drowned Marches. The sunken country. The place the war had ended and everything else had begun.

And at the center of it all, where the routes converged like veins toward a heart, the thorned rose pulsed—once, twice, slow as a sleeping pulse—and with each pulse Jude felt an answering pull low in her chest, in the place behind her sternum where the sword-speech lived, coiled and dormant and silent for ten years.

It hummed.

Not loudly. Barely at all. A single note, felt more than heard, like the memory of a bell, and the parchment hummed back, and for one suspended moment Jude Winterbourne stood alone in a dark counting-house holding a conversation she had sworn never to have again.

The lamp guttered. The ink settled. The map lay still, complete, waiting—looking, she thought with a dread that felt absurdly like relief, exactly like a thing that had finally been delivered.

She should burn it. She knew that with the clarity of a balanced column. Burn it, report the robbery, grieve the courier, go on being Jude Wren until Jude Wren was all that was left of her.

Instead she rolled the map up with hands that had stopped pretending to shake, hid it again among the dead debts, and sat down in the dark to listen to her own heart argue with itself.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a bell rang the hour, and beneath it—so faint she told herself she imagined it—the city's ten thousand sleeping blades gave one small, collective shiver in their sheaths.
