## 5. What the Oath Cost

Ten years is a long time to carry a bridge.

Jude carried Ashfall Bridge the way other people carried scars—under her skin, aching when the weather turned. She had not thought of it in months. She had trained herself not to think of it at all, the way she had trained herself to keep her voice flat and her hands still and the sword-speech folded up small beneath her ribs like a letter she would never send.

But tonight, with the map hidden in a ledger of debts and the ink still crawling behind her eyelids whenever she closed them, the memory came for her anyway. It always did on nights like this—nights when something old stirred in the world and knocked against the walls she'd built.

She sat alone in her narrow room above the counting-house, one candle burning, and let it come. That was the discipline of it. Not forgetting. Letting it come, and surviving it, and putting it back.

* * *

The bridge had been older than the war. It crossed the River Sere where the water ran white over broken granite, and its arches were carved with names that predated every kingdom that claimed them. The swordspeakers had chosen it for their last stand because bridges could only be held from two ends, and there were only nine of them left to hold anything at all.

Nine. Jude had spent ten years teaching herself to say that number without flinching, and she still hadn't managed it.

She had been twenty-two, and she remembered the morning as a series of small, stupid details: the way her bootlace had frayed and she'd knotted it twice; the taste of hard bread and river-damp; Maren Voss standing at the center of the span with her grey braid coming loose, sharpening a sword that didn't need sharpening because it was speech, not steel, that made a swordspeaker's blade bite.

"You're doing the thing," Maren had said, without looking up.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you count us." Maren's whetstone whispered along the edge. "I can hear you counting. You do it with your eyes. Left to right, like reading a ledger."

"I wasn't—"

"Jude." Maren looked up then, and her eyes were the pale grey of weather coming. "Don't count. Counting is how you start saying goodbye."

But Jude had counted anyway. She was the youngest, and the others treated her like a ledger-keeper who'd wandered onto the field—which, in fairness, was half true. Serah the Tall, who could speak a shield out of thin air and laughed too loudly. The twins, Aldous and Bram, who fought in unison so perfect it looked rehearsed even though they swore it wasn't. Old Hesk, whose voice had gone to gravel from forty years of speaking iron. Ivetta, who was dying already, slow, of something in her lungs, and had come anyway. And Maren. Maren last and first, Maren at the center, because that was where the anchor stood.

The enemy came at noon.

They came the way the Order of the Grafted Hand always came: not as soldiers but as a harvest. Their ranks were wrong—too smooth, too silent, men moving like cuttings taken from a single plant, and above them flew their banner, a tree with roots for a crown. Jude had seen that banner in dreams ever since. In the dreams it was closer.

The first hour was noise. The second hour was work. Swordspeech was not shouting; it was precision, a grammar spoken through steel, and Jude had been good at it—better than good, though no one had ever told her so except Maren, once, quietly, in a way that had frightened her more than any compliment should have. She spoke her blade into patterns that sang, and the singing cut, and men fell off the bridge into the white water and did not come up.

By the third hour they were four. By the fourth, three.

Jude did not remember killing her first man. She remembered the fourth, because he was young and he hesitated, and hesitation was a mercy she repaid by making it quick. She remembered thinking, absurdly, *I will have to write this down*, because her whole life she had believed that things written down were things survived, and she had not yet learned that some things could not be survived by being recorded. Some things could only be survived by being buried.

Serah fell at dusk. Jude heard the exact moment the shield-speech stopped—the air itself seemed to sag—and then Serah was gone from the left end of the line and the enemy poured through the gap like water finding a crack, and Aldous and Bram died together, which was the only mercy that day, because neither twin ever had to learn what the world sounded like without the other.

That left Maren and Jude and the middle of the bridge, and the whole weight of the Grafted Hand coming on like a tide that had decided, personally, to drown them.

"Jude." Maren's voice, and it was calm, and the calm was the most terrifying sound of the entire war. "Listen to me now. Not fight. Listen."

"I can hold—"

"You can't. Neither can I. It's done, child. It's been done since noon and we were never holding this bridge—we were *spending* it." Maren caught her by the collar, steel-gauntleted fingers, and pulled her close, and up close Jude could see that Maren was bleeding from somewhere under her armor, had been for hours, had known. "There is a thing worse than losing a battle. There is losing everything the battle was for. Do you understand me? The Order doesn't want the bridge. They want what we are. They want the speech itself, grown into their own stock, and if they take a living swordspeaker tonight they get exactly that."

"So we die," Jude said. Her voice came out very small. "We die and that's the plan."

"We die, or you live wrongly." Maren smiled, and it was the worst smile Jude had ever seen, because it was gentle. "Those are the options. I've thought about this longer than you have."

* * *

What happened next, Jude had replayed ten thousand times, and in ten thousand replays she had tried to find the moment she could have refused, and in ten thousand replays there wasn't one—or rather, there was, but refusing would have meant Maren dying for nothing, and grief is a coward's arithmetic. You always pay.

Maren drove her back step by step to the far end of the bridge, fighting as she went—not to win, Jude understood now, but to spend, to make each yard cost the Order so much that they would break before they broke the last two swordspeakers. At the bridge's end there was a shrine, some older god's alcove cut into the cliff, and Maren shoved her into it and put her own body between Jude and the world.

"Maren, please—"

"Swear it." Maren took Jude's sword-hand in both of hers, and pressed the flat of the blade against her own throat, which was either a threat or an offering or both. "Swear silence. Swear you'll bury the speech so deep no one digs it out—not the Order, not a friend, not yourself. Swear you'll be ordinary. Ledgers, Jude. Be a woman of ledgers. Live somewhere numbers are honest and blades are just metal, and never, never speak again."

"They'll kill you."

"They're going to kill me regardless. The oath doesn't change that. The oath changes what my death *buys*." Maren's grip tightened. "If they find nothing here—if the last swordspeaker is a clerk with calloused hands and a quiet mouth—then the harvest fails. Ten years, twenty, fifty, however long it takes them to stop looking. You starve the garden by hiding the seed. Do you hear me? You hide the seed."

"I don't want to hide. I want to stand—"

"Yes." For a moment Maren's composure cracked, and underneath it was something so raw and so tired that Jude felt it in her own chest like a wound opening. "Yes, I know. Standing is all any of us ever wanted. But wanting is how they grow things in us, Jude. The Order doesn't conquer people. It *cultivates* them. It finds the want and plants in it." She leaned her forehead against Jude's, and her blood was on Jude's cheek, warm as speech. "So swear against your want. Swear the hardest oath there is: the one against yourself."

And Jude—who was twenty-two, and bleeding, and had counted nine and then seven and then three and then was about to count one—swore.

She swore it in the old grammar, the deep grammar, the words that go into the bone and stay. She felt the oath take root in her the way the speech itself had once taken root, a second planting, a hedge around a garden. And the moment it settled, the sword-speech didn't leave her—it couldn't leave her, it was grown into her—but it went *quiet*, muffled, folded down beneath layers of sworn silence like a fire banked under ash. Still there. Always there. Humming under her skin on bad nights, humming under her skin on good ones too, if she was honest, which she tried not to be.

Then Maren stepped out of the alcove, straightened her shoulders, and walked back onto the bridge to meet the Grafted Hand alone, and her last words over her shoulder were not grand at all. They were:

"Keep honest books, girl. It's the only immortality worth the price."

Jude watched her die. Of course she watched. Maren fought the way she had lived, precisely, spending herself yard by yard, and the Order's cuttings came on and came on, and at the end Maren Voss stood at the center of Ashfall Bridge—the anchor's place, held to the last—and the white water roared below, and the banner with the rooted crown came down the span like night coming down a corridor.

Jude did not remember leaving the alcove. She remembered walking. She remembered walking for a long time, downhill, away from the river, with her sword-hand curled around nothing, and somewhere in the walking she became a woman who balanced ledgers, because a woman balancing ledgers was the only person left alive that Maren's death had purchased, and she would not waste the purchase.

* * *

The candle had burned down to a stub. Jude surfaced from the memory the way a swimmer surfaces—too fast, gasping, the present slamming down around her.

Her room. Her desk. Her ledgers, squared and true. Through the window, the harbor bells rang the late watch, and somewhere below, the Guild's enforcers were asking questions about a dead courier, and Master Corvane was asking questions about her answers, and under her breastbone the old oath hummed against the new trouble like two tuning forks struck near each other.

Ten years of silence. Ten years of being nobody, of rounding numbers and keeping her voice flat and letting the gift lie fallow under the ash of the vow. She had told herself it was penance. She had told herself it was loyalty to the dead. She had built a whole architecture of reasons, and the architecture had held, mostly, right up until a bleeding man had gasped *Winterbourne* at the salt door—a name no living person should have known—and pressed a map against her palms that breathed.

Because here was the thing she had never said aloud, not even to Maren's ghost, not even in the privacy of her own skull: hiding had not starved the hunger. It had fed it. Every year of the quiet had made the speech louder in its prison, the way a held note grows in the ear. And now something was calling it—a map drawn in crawling ink, a rose of thorns blooming where no rose should be—and the oath she had sworn at nineteen-hundred-hours on a broken bridge was straining against the truth she carried like a seam about to split.

Maren had bought her survival with silence. But Maren had also said: *you starve the garden by hiding the seed.* And Jude had hidden the seed for ten years, and the seed had not starved. It had waited. Things grown in the dark grow toward whatever light finally finds them.

She looked at the ledger where the map lay hidden, and the candle guttered, and beneath her ribs the old words stirred like a sleeper turning over.

Tomorrow Corvane would ask his questions again. Tomorrow the walls would be a little closer. And tomorrow night, when the counting-house slept, Jude Winterbourne was going to unroll a dead man's map and see what her mentor had died to keep buried—because the oath had been made to protect the world from the Garden, and she was beginning to fear she had sworn it facing the wrong direction.

The candle went out. In the dark, faint as breath, the sword-speech hummed—and for the first time in ten years, Jude hummed back.
