## 7. Debts Called In

The summons came the way all of Corvane's summons came: not by messenger, not by note, but by silence.

Jude had learned to read the counting-house the way sailors read weather. The clerks' pens scratched in their usual rhythm through the morning; the apprentices hauled their ledgers up and down the stair in their usual shuffling procession; the great brass scales in the weighing room clinked their usual small music as factors weighed out storm-glass and bottled year and the powdered teeth of things no one could name. Everything was usual. That was what was wrong.

Because at half past ten, the scratching stopped. All of it. Every pen on every floor, at once, for the length of three heartbeats—and then resumed, as though nothing had happened, as though a hundred clerks had not simultaneously remembered some instruction they had all been given long ago and hoped never to use. *When the house goes quiet, someone is being weighed.*

She had been at Meridian nine years and had heard that silence four times. Twice it had ended with a factor escorted out by enforcers, his accounts seized, his name struck from the rolls so thoroughly that within a month his own mother claimed never to have had a son. Once it had ended with nothing at all—just a junior clerk weeping in the stairwell because he'd been called before Corvane over a miscounted crate of phoenix ash and had come back gray-faced but employed. And once—

Once it had ended with a body on the salt door's step, and everyone pretending not to see it until the tide took it away.

At noon, an apprentice she'd never seen before appeared at her desk. He was perhaps fourteen, with ink to his elbows and the flat, careful expression of a child raised in a house where children learn early not to ask questions. He did not say "Master Corvane requests your presence." He said only: *"The auditor will see you now."*

*Now.* Not at your convenience. Not after the afternoon reconciliation. Now, in the middle of the trading day, when the counting-house was fullest and most eyes were busy elsewhere—which meant, of course, that no eyes would be free to witness whatever happened next.

"Tell him I'll come directly," Jude said, and the boy nodded once and vanished, and she sat for another moment with her hand flat on the ledger she'd been balancing—the ledger of debts, the one with the map sewn into its spine like a splinter of bone in flesh—and made herself breathe.

The map was there. She checked without checking, the way a woman checks a hidden knife: not looking, just letting her fingers rest against the binding's swollen edge, confirming the weight, the wrongness, the secret shape of it beneath the calfskin. Still there. Still hers to carry. Still the thing that was going to kill her if she carried it badly.

Then she stood, smoothed her clerk's coat, and walked toward the auditor's office the way condemned debtors walk: slowly enough to seem composed, quickly enough to seem unafraid.

* * *

Corvane's office occupied the tower's best corner, high above the harbor, with windows on two sides so that light came at him from every angle and left him nowhere to hide—and nowhere for anyone else to hide either. It was a room designed, Jude had always thought, like one of the Guild's own instruments: a scale, a lens, a vice. Everything in it was arranged to make the person sitting across from Master Corvane feel measured.

He was measuring her already. She felt it before she crossed the threshold—that faint pressure, like the moment before a storm when the air itself seems to lean against your skin. Her sword-speech woke under it, humming along her forearms the way it always did when something old and hungry was near, and she crushed it down, down, down into the marrow where it lived, where it had lived for ten years, where it whispered to her at night like a second heartbeat she was forbidden to acknowledge.

"Master Kavanagh," Corvane said, and gestured to the chair.

It was a good chair. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing that frightened her. When Corvane meant to dismiss someone, he gave them a stool—low, backless, uncomfortable, the furniture of a petitioner. When he meant to destroy them, he gave them nothing at all; he simply stood them in the middle of the floor while he worked around them. But this chair was deep and leather and angled toward his desk rather than across from it, which was the geometry of confidence, of a man who wanted you comfortable because comfort made people honest, and honesty was the raw material of everything he did.

She sat. The leather creaked. Somewhere below, the harbor bells rang the quarter-hour, and the sound came through the glass thickened by salt, softened, almost kind.

"Do you know why you're here?" Corvane asked.

He was not looking at her. He was looking at a document on his desk—a single sheet, laid square to the desk's edge with the precision of a man who squared his papers the way other men squared their accounts. His voice was warm. It was always warm. That was the terrible thing about Corvane; warmth was his native language, and he spoke it fluently in every register from genuine kindness to the specific honeyed tone he used on people he intended to ruin, and after nine years Jude still could not always tell the difference until it was too late.

"I imagine it concerns the courier," she said. "The man who died at the salt door."

"Mm." He turned the sheet around and slid it across the desk toward her. "Read this."

It was an incident report. She knew the form—she had filed dozens herself, the standard Guild record of irregular events, the bureaucratic skeleton on which the Guild hung its muscle of consequence. This one was dated yesterday. *Death of unidentified male, found at south service entrance, cause consistent with blade-wound.* Ordinary so far. Ordinary as dust.

Then she reached the inventory.

*Goods recovered from the deceased's possession:* the line began, and listed, in the neat clerical hand she recognized as belonging to Enforcer Doss—who had been one of the first through the salt door last night, whose lantern she had seen bobbing in the fog as she pressed the courier's map against her stomach and lied with her whole body about knowing nothing:

*A wax-sealed scroll case, empty.*
*A map of the coastal road between Meridian and the Marches, water-damaged, torn at the eastern margin.*
*A purse containing eleven silver marks and one coin of unknown provenance, gold, bearing the sigil of a thorned rose.*

And then, at the bottom, in a different hand—not Doss's blunt strokes but something finer, more deliberate, the handwriting of a man who wrote the way he spoke, beautifully and with intent:

*Note: recovered goods do not correspond to items logged at intake. The seal-case arrived empty. No map matching the above description has entered the Guild's possession. Disposition of the missing item is under inquiry.*

Jude set the paper down. Her hands were steady. She had trained them to be steady through nine years of audits and inventories and the slow daily discipline of being nobody, and they obeyed her even now, even with her pulse hammering so hard against the chair's arm that she could feel the wood vibrating.

"This says the map wasn't recovered," she said carefully.

"It does." Corvane's smile was small and pleasant and arrived like a knife wrapped in cloth. "Which is interesting, don't you think? Because the report also says the courier was robbed. Robbery is the finding—it's how the death has been logged, it's what goes to the harbormaster, it's what keeps the street quiet. A dead courier is bad for trade. A murdered courier carrying Guild property is worse. So we call it robbery, we log the loss, and the world turns on." He leaned back, and the light from the two windows caught him full in the face, and for a moment his pleasantness looked almost like innocence. "But a robbery requires stolen goods. And stolen goods require a thief. And the goods in question—" he tapped the report once, lightly, "—are described in detail that appears nowhere in any statement you gave last night."

The room tilted. Not literally—rooms didn't tilt—but inside her chest something shifted its weight, the way ballast shifts in a hull, and for a moment she understood viscerally, physically, what drowning men feel in the last seconds before the water closes: not panic, exactly, but a horrible clarity about how much time remained.

"You told me," Corvane said, and his voice stayed gentle, stayed warm, stayed the voice of a man discussing a discrepancy in a spice shipment rather than the voice of a man closing a trap, "that the courier said nothing. That he died before he could speak. That you saw no goods, received nothing, observed nothing unusual beyond a dying man and a closed door. You were quite thorough. I remember admiring the thoroughness."

"He was dying," Jude said. "There wasn't time for inventory."

"No," Corvane agreed readily. "Of course not. Dying men are famously poor witnesses. And yet." He turned his hand palm-up, an open gesture, the gesture of a merchant offering fair terms. "Someone described that map. Described the seal-case. Described the coin—the rose sigil, which, incidentally, is a sigil our own archivists had to look up, because it hasn't circulated since the war. Someone knew what that courier carried before anyone opened anything. Someone described the *contents*, Master Kavanagh. Contents are very difficult to describe from outside a sealed case."

He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; he treated silence the way other men treated interest, letting it accrue, letting it compound, until the person across from him was paying on a debt they hadn't known they'd taken out.

Jude made herself look at the window instead of at him. Below, the harbor lay bright and indifferent, barges crawling with cargo, gulls hanging in the wind like scraps of thrown paper. Somewhere out there was the world where she had planned to be nobody forever, and it looked exactly as unreachable as it had always been.

"Who reported the description?" she asked.

"That would be telling," Corvane said pleasantly. "The Guild protects its sources. You know that better than most—you've benefited from it often enough."

*You've benefited from it often enough.* The words went through her like a fishhook going through soft flesh, catching on something she hadn't shown anyone. Because it was true. Nine years of benefiting. Nine years of the Guild's discretion wrapping around her like a coat too large, hiding the shape of what she was. She had thought of that discretion as hers, as a wall she'd built, and here was Corvane gently reminding her that walls in the Meridian Guild belonged to the house, not to the people who leaned against them.

"The question isn't who," Corvane continued, "the question is what. What was taken. Where it went. Whether it will be returned." He folded his hands. "I want to be clear with you, Master Kavanagh, because clarity is a courtesy I extend even in unpleasant matters. You are not accused of anything. There is no charge. There is an inquiry, and inquiries at Meridian have a way of resolving themselves once the relevant parties stop treating them as accusations and start treating them as arithmetic. Something entered this building's orbit yesterday and failed to arrive at its destination. The Guild does not care, particularly, about moral questions. The Guild cares about balance. An unbalanced entry is like an unpaid debt: it accrues. And I am, as you know, the house's collector."

He said it all without menace. That was the masterpiece of it. Any other man, delivering these words, would have needed threats, needed fury, needed the theatrical cruelty of a villain in a ballad. Corvane needed none of it. He simply laid out the terms the way he'd lay out terms for a loan, and let her understand, in the privacy of her own skull, that the collateral was her life.

"If the item were returned," Jude said, testing the ice ahead of her boot, hearing the crack underneath, "the inquiry would close?"

"It would be resolved," Corvane corrected, with the pedant's precision that was somehow worse than vagueness. "Resolved favorably, I should think. The Guild forgives losses that restore themselves. It is thefts that persist which become crimes." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "And disappearances, of course. Disappearances are the worst entries of all. They cannot be forgiven because they cannot be found. They simply remain, year after year, a red figure in the margin, waiting."

He was giving her until tomorrow. Maybe until tonight. He was doing it kindly, and the kindness was the cruelest thing in the room.

* * *

She walked back down the tower stairs with the report's words lodged in her like shrapnel, and she made herself pass through the counting-floor at a normal pace, made herself stop at her desk, made herself pick up her pen and enter three figures in the storm-glass account with a hand that did not shake, because shaking hands were how the caught were identified, and she was not caught yet.

But she could hear it now, the thing she had refused to hear last night when the enforcers came with their lanterns and she lied to them smoothly, completely, with the fluency of a woman who had practiced being unreadable for a decade: the walls had not merely closed. They had *described* her. Someone had stood in that fog at the salt door and watched her take the case, or watched her afterward, or watched her lie, and had gone to Corvane and given him not suspicion but *inventory*—the rose-sigiled coin, the torn eastern margin, the water damage. Details. Specifics. The currency of certainty.

Sefa Ilvane's face rose behind her eyes, unbidden. The factor with the pale stare who had stood at the edge of the crowd last night, watching, saying nothing, her attention fixed on Jude with the particular quality of a cat watching a hole in a wainscot. Jude had told herself it meant nothing. Factors watched everything; it was the occupational disease of people paid to notice discrepancies. But now the noticing had a shape, and the shape fit Sefa's stillness the way a key fits the lock it was cut for.

Or it didn't. Or it was Doss. Or it was the apprentice boy. Or it was Corvane himself, who had known about the courier before the courier ever reached the salt door, who had perhaps sent him, who had perhaps killed him at one remove, who had perhaps constructed this entire theater—death, report, summons—as a machine for finding out what Jude would do with a map to the Garden. She turned the possibilities over like coins bitten for counterfeit, and every one of them rang true, and that was the trap's real architecture: not a single accusation but a hall of mirrors, each reflecting a version of her guilt, so that whichever way she moved she moved toward one of them.

The map lay against her ribs, sewn into the spine of the debt-ledger, and it was warm. Parchment shouldn't be warm. She had told herself yesterday that it was her own body heat trapped in the binding, a mundane explanation, a clerk's explanation, and she had believed it the way you believe things you need to believe. But it was warm again now, warmer than the room, warmer than her, and beneath the warmth, faint as a pulse felt through a bandage, she could feel it *humming*—and her gift, buried ten years deep, hummed back.

*They know,* the resonance said, in the wordless way such things said things. *They know, and they are patient, and patience is a blade too.*

She finished the storm-glass entry. She blotted it. She squared the ledger with the desk's edge, because Corvane squared his papers and because small disciplines were the raft she had built her survival on, and then she sat very still in the noise of a hundred pens and listened to the day spend itself toward evening.

Tomorrow, Corvane would expect an answer. Tonight, the tide would turn, and the lower city would fill with dark water, and the tidewalks—the drowned streets, the old roads beneath the harbor's skin—would be passable to anyone desperate enough and foolish enough and gifted enough to walk them.

She had sworn, ten years ago, on a bridge full of ash and dying men, that she would never speak again. Never speak, never sign, never let the syllables of power cross her lips for any reason, for any price, unto death.

Tonight she was going to find out what her oath was worth against a locked gate, a rising tide, and a road out of the city that began underwater.

She picked up her pen one last time and wrote, in the margin of the debt-ledger, in the tiny cipher she used for notes no auditor was meant to read, a single character: a rose, drawn in six strokes, thorned.

Then she closed the book over it, and waited for the bell.
