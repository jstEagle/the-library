## 8. Running the Tidewalks

The closing bell rang at the ninth hour, as it had every night for ten years, and Jude Winterbourne rose from her desk with the map pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She had dressed that morning with care she hadn't understood until now: plain grey wool, no jewelry, hair bound back under a clerk's cap so ordinary it made her face forgettable. She had chosen the ledger to carry—the debts of minor factors, dull enough that no auditor would miss it and thick enough to hide a folded parchment against its spine. She had even, without quite admitting why, left her window unlatched.

Now all those small decisions assembled themselves into an escape, and she walked out of the counting-house the way she walked out every evening—shoulders down, eyes on the floor, one more tired clerk going home to bread and bed.

The Guild's front hall was marble and echoes. Portraits of dead masters watched her from their gilt frames, men who had traded in bottled storms and borrowed years and never once, as far as she knew, in anything true. She passed under them. She did not run. Running was a confession, and Corvane would be listening for confessions the way other men listened for music.

*Walk*, she told herself. *You have walked this hall nine thousand times.*

But her body knew better than her pride. Her pulse was a drum in her throat, and under her skin, in the place where the gift lived coiled and quiet, something had begun to hum.

It had been humming since the courier died against the salt door. It had grown louder when she lied to Corvane. And tonight, with the map breathing against her breastbone and the Guild's shadow falling long across everything she loved about her small invented life, it sang.

The tide was coming in.

---

Saltgate was built on pilings over a drowned harbor, and twice a day the sea took the lower city back. The tidewalks were the streets beneath the wealthy quarter's stairways—narrow stone lanes where the water ran ankle-deep at low tide and chest-deep at high, lit by lamp-posts that stood in the flood like drowned sentries. The poor who lived there kept boats moored to their doorposts and measured the hours by the water on their thresholds.

Jude went down the cliff-stair two steps at a time, cap pulled low, and stepped off the last stair into cold black water that soaked her to the knee.

*This is madness*, said the voice that had kept her alive for ten years. *Take a coach. Take a ferry. Take any road a normal woman takes.*

But Corvane owned the coaches and the ferries, or knew who did. The moment the courier died with her name on his lips, every ordinary road in Saltgate became a corridor in someone else's house. The tidewalks were the only roads that belonged to no one—and the only ones that required her to go where she had sworn never to go again: into the old country of her own power.

She waded north along Tallow Lane. The water rose past her calves, then her knees. Lamps guttered in brackets above her head; their light lay broken on the current in long ribbons of gold. Somewhere behind her, up the hill, a whistle blew—Guild enforcers' signal, three short notes. Then again. Not yet her name in their mouths, perhaps. But close.

Her hand found the gate before she was ready for it.

---

It stood where Tallow Lane narrowed between two warehouses—a barred iron gate, older than the Guild, put up in some forgotten century to keep the tide from running wild through the merchants' cellars. It was locked every dusk by the wardens and opened at dawn, and everyone in the lower city knew you waited for dawn, because the gate did not open for bribes or begging or crowbars. The lock was salt-welded shut most winters. Beyond it lay the long tidewalk called the Serpent, which ran half a mile through open water to the old ferry arches, and from there out of Saltgate entirely.

Jude gripped the bars. Water shoved against her thighs, patient and enormous. Behind her, faint but distinct, boots on the cliff-stair. More than one pair.

There was no key. There was no time. There was only the thing she had carried since Ashfall Bridge, the syllables sitting under her tongue like stones she had swallowed whole.

*I swore*, she thought.

*You swore never to make war*, answered another voice, older, worn smooth as riverglass—her mentor's voice, ten years dead and still the loudest in her skull. *I never said a word about gates.*

She almost laughed. That was exactly the sort of distinction Maren had loved, the lawyer's knife-edge the old woman had walked all her life and finally bled out on. Jude closed her eyes. The gift rose in her like breath before speech, familiar as her own name and terrible as it had always been—not fire, not lightning, nothing so honest. Just *weight*. A pressure in the world, leaning toward her, waiting to be told what shape to take.

She had not spoken in ten years. Her first word came out cracked and rusted and very small.

"*Open.*"

The gate obeyed.

Not with a crash. With a sigh—an iron groan, a soft explosion of rust, the lock turning as if a hand she couldn't see had found the key it had been missing for a hundred years. The bars swung inward on water-swollen hinges, and the way lay dark and open before her, the Serpent stretching out into the flooded dark.

Exhilaration hit her like the tide itself.

Ten years of being ordinary. Ten years of ledgers and ink-stained fingers and pretending her blood was just blood. Ten years of flinching from kitchen knives because they hummed to her, of biting her tongue against doors that stuck and storms that raged, and all of it dissolved in one word—one *word*—and the world leaned forward to do what she asked.

She laughed aloud, alone, thigh-deep in black water, and the sound startled gulls off the warehouse roof. She felt sixteen again. She felt like the girl on the bridge before the dying started. She felt—

*Loud*, she realized. *You are being loud, and gates do not open themselves.*

The exhilaration curdled even as she pushed through the gateway and hauled it shut behind her. The lock re-seized with a click that sounded, in the silence after, obscenely final. Whoever followed would find a locked gate and know that it had not been locked ten seconds ago. Doors remembered hands. Iron remembered voices.

And somewhere behind her, someone might have seen.

---

She ran the Serpent.

It was not a road for running—slick stones under moving water, lamp-lit in patches and blind-dark between—but fear lent her grace. Water broke around her knees. Her lungs burned with salt air. Twice she slipped and caught herself on a mooring post, and both times she kept moving, because behind her, distant now but steady, came the slap of boots in shallows. They had reached the gate. She heard a man swear at it, heard iron rattle, heard the long pause of someone doing arithmetic: *locked gate, open woman, impossible sum.*

Then a voice, carrying flat across the water: "Gate's sealed. She's gone to ground in the warehouses. Sweep the row."

They hadn't seen her. Relief made her stupid for exactly three more steps, which was how far she got before her foot found the gap in the Serpent's stones.

She went down hard. The map case struck the stones; the ledger flew from under her arm and slapped into the current, pages fanning white in the lamplight. She lunged for it, plunged shoulder-deep, came up gasping with the book clutched in both fists and her cap gone, her hair streaming loose and black down her back, and the water closing over the spot where she'd fallen like a mouth.

She dragged herself onto the wider landing by the ferry arches, coughing brine, shaking, alive. Above her the arches rose like whale ribs. Below her the tide ticked upward against the stones, unhurried. She had perhaps an hour before the Serpent drowned entirely and the only way out was the ferry—and the ferryman answered to whoever paid.

From the shadows under the nearest arch, someone began, slowly, to clap.

Jede spun, wet hair whipping, one hand rising empty and useless.

A woman detached herself from the dark with the ease of someone who had been standing comfortably for a long time. Guild factor's coat, good boots dry above the flood-line, dark hair pinned with brutal practicality. Sefa Ilvane. Third desk from the window in the factor's gallery. The one whose columns always balanced, whose silences in the counting-house always seemed to Jude less like emptiness and more like aim.

"Il—" Jude began.

"Sefa." The woman smiled without warmth. "Though I suppose we're past desk names, aren't we, *Winterbourne*."

The surname landed like a blow. Jude's back found the arch's cold stone. "I don't know what you—"

"Third lie you've told this week." Sefa's voice stayed level, conversational, the tone of a factor reading figures aloud. "You lied to Corvane on Tuesday about the courier. Your pen stopped for half a beat before the word 'never saw him,' the same half-beat it stopped just now. You did it in the March-quarter audit too, over the storm-bottle shipment. You've done it every time you've lied for ten years, which is nearly never, which is what makes it visible." She tilted her head. "People who lie often lie smoothly. You're out of practice. You were honest for a very long decade, and then a man bled out on your salt door, and now you can't stop."

"You saw the gate," Jude said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"I saw the gate." Sefa glanced out at the black water, at the distant lamps of the swept warehouses where enforcers were kicking open doors belonging to no one. "Iron that hasn't moved since the war, opening for a clerk with ink on her cuffs. I grew up in a village that the swordspeakers passed through in the last year of the fighting. I know what it looks like when the world does what a person says." Her eyes came back to Jude, and they were not cruel—that was somehow worse. They were *accounting*. "So. You're worth more than I thought, and Corvane wants your head, and you're standing on a drowning road with one exit that I control."

"The ferryman?"

"Works for me tonight." Sefa stepped closer, water swirling around her boots, and held out one hand—not for a weapon, not for an arrest. Open. Waiting. "Here's the shape of it, swordspeaker. You hold a map I want. I know passages you need. Corvane will find us both by morning unless we're already gone. You can refuse me and drown honestly, or you can take my hand, and we'll spend the rest of this disagreeing about who is using whom."

The tide surged against the landing, cold through Jude's soaked wool. Under the arches, a lantern kindled—the ferryman, readying his pole. Behind them both, across the drowned city, a whistle blew three notes, then three more, then a fourth note that Jude had never heard used before. A name, going out into the dark. Hers.

Sefa's hand stayed open between them, steady as a signature on a contract.

Jude looked at it—at the offered alliance, at the trap she could see the edges of and couldn't see past—and understood, with the strange clarity that comes of cold water and colder fear, that she had no third choice at all.

She took the hand.

* * *

The ferry slid out from under the arches into open channel, and Saltgate burned its lamps behind them like a ledger of everything she owed.
