## 9. An Offer in the Rain

The rainmarket opened an hour before dawn, as it always did, because the people who sold stolen things preferred the cover of weather to the cover of night. Sefa Ilvane had been working the stalls since she was nine years old, back when her job had been holding lanterns and keeping her mouth shut, and she knew its rhythms the way a gambler knew a marked deck: which awnings leaked, which vendors watered their wine, which corners of the flooded lower city turned honest men into liars and liars into philosophers.

She also knew, with the same bone-deep certainty, that Jude Winterbourne was coming here.

Sefa had watched her leave the Guild two nights past—had seen her slip out through the salt door with a ledger-shaped bundle under her coat, had seen what happened at the tidewalk gate that no one else had been positioned to see. Sefa had been following the auditor's quiet instructions for three days, tracking the courier's last hours through the city's veins, and she had arrived at the salt door just late enough to miss the dying man and just early enough to watch Winterbourne speak a locked gate into obedience.

Ten years Sefa had spent learning to read people the way other people read ledgers. She knew every tell the counting-house bred: the clerk who licked his thumb before falsifying a column, the factor who laughed too loudly when asked about his debts, the master who steepled his fingers when he was about to ruin someone. And she knew that when Jude lied to Corvane—and Sefa had stood close enough in that corridor to hear the lie, smooth and rehearsed and almost perfect—her left hand curled slightly, fingers pressing against her palm as if holding something down.

Jude held something down. That much was obvious. The question—the question that had kept Sefa awake through two rain-soaked nights on a rooftop above the tidewalks—was *what*, and *why it mattered enough to kill a courier over*, and most importantly, *what it would be worth*.

Because Sefa did not intend to hand Corvane anything. Not yet. Possibly not ever.

She took up her position beneath the eave of a dried-fish stall whose owner owed her a favor and a debt of silence both, and she waited.

* * *

The rainmarket in full spate was a cathedral of canvas and noise. Water ran in channels down the middle of the lane, black with lamp-oil and fish scales, and the vendors shouted over it—oyster-women crying their beds, a knife-grinder ringing steel against stone, a boy selling candles that supposedly burned underwater. Lanterns hung from every awning pole, their light smeared long and gold across the wet cobbles, so that the whole market seemed to float on a shallow sea of broken light.

Sefa saw Jude enter from the north stairs.

It was impossible not to. Everyone else in the market moved like they belonged to it—shoulders hunched, eyes flicking, hands tucked away—while Winterbourne walked through the crowd like a woman reading a document, upright and careful and entirely wrong for the place. Her hood was up but her posture said *Guild* louder than any badge. She stopped at the candle-boy's stall, then the map-seller's, then lingered too long at a tinker's cart where a battered compass swung on a string, and Sefa understood: she wasn't shopping. She was checking whether she'd been followed. Amateurs checked once. Professionals checked twice. Jude was checking three times, and doing it badly, because fear makes even clever people stupid.

Sefa let her finish. Let her buy nothing. Let her turn toward the oyster-stalls at the market's drowned heart, where the channels widened and the crowd thickened and the lanterns grew scarce—because everyone who wanted privacy came here eventually, believing that darkness was the same thing as safety.

Then Sefa moved.

She didn't follow directly. She cut through the fish-stalls, past the ice-blocks sweating under sacking, and came around behind Jude through the gap between a chandler's cart and the wall, stepping into the narrow lane where the channel ran deepest and no one lingered unless they had business worth drowning for.

"Master Winterbourne."

Jude spun. Fast—faster than any clerk should move—and for one naked instant her hand went to her hip where no sword hung, and Sefa felt the hair rise on her arms, because that motion had been drilled into the woman by someone who had expected her to die needing it. But then the moment closed over like water, and Jude was a clerk again, pale and composed and lying already.

"You're mistaken," she said. "I'm no one's master."

"No," Sefa agreed. "You're the Guild's second-journeyman of accounts, third floor, west range. You balance the storm-bottle contracts. You have excellent penmanship and no vices anyone has ever proven." She tilted her head, letting the lantern-light from the mouth of the lane catch her face, letting Jude see exactly who stood there. "And you are Master Corvane's errand-runner this week, whether you know it or not."

Recognition moved across Jude's face like a shadow under ice. "You're the factor. Ilvane. Marches trade."

"Sefa Ilvane." She inclined her head, mock-formal, rain ticking off the awning above them. "You've read my filings."

"I've audited your filings." Something dry flickered through the fear. "Your numbers round too generously."

"My numbers are *honest*. There's a difference." Sefa stepped closer, boots finding the stones between the channel-water. Up close, Jude smelled of ink and cold iron and something older underneath, something like struck flint. "You want to know why I'm here instead of in Corvane's office reporting you. I'll tell you, since you're clearly going to stand there all night pretending you don't care."

"I'm going back to my rooms," Jude said, and turned.

Sefa let her take exactly one step. "The courier's name was Tam Vosk."

Jude stopped.

"He ran the Marches route for eleven years," Sefa continued quietly. "I knew him. He carried my letters home when I was a war-child waiting on word that never came. And three days ago he bled out against your salt door carrying a map that Corvane's enforcers are now describing in detail to the Guild council—a map they could only describe if someone told them what was on it, and the only person who saw it is standing in front of me pretending she's going back to her rooms." She paused. "You're not going back to your rooms, Winterbourne. Your rooms were searched this afternoon while you were filing. Nothing was found, which means either you hid it well or you're carrying it, and your coat is hanging wrong on the left side. So we can keep doing this dance until the tide comes in, or you can stop lying, because—" and here she smiled, thin and sharp, "—your left hand curls when you lie. It's doing it right now."

For a long moment the only sounds were the market roaring beyond the lane and the rain drumming the canvas overhead, and Jude Winterbourne stood very still, and Sefa watched her decide. Watched her weigh the odds the way she weighed a column of figures, hunting the error. Watched her understand, finally, that the game had changed—that this wasn't Corvane's trap, that Sefa was offering something no one had offered her in ten years.

"The map breathes," Jude said at last.

Sefa blinked. "What?"

"It unrolls itself. The ink moves. It redraws the route depending on—" Jude stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if she'd said too much. Then, deliberately, she went on, quieter: "Depending on things I don't understand. And Corvane wants it. Which means whatever's at the end of it, the Guild masters mean to harvest. They've already logged the courier's death as robbery. They've invented goods that were stolen. They're building a legal fiction around a dead man so they can hunt whoever carries his cargo." Her voice hardened. "That's not auditing. That's laundering."

"Now you're speaking my language." Sefa leaned against the chandler's cart, arms crossed, rain gathering in the folds of her oilskin. "Here's what I know, and I'll give it to you free because free things build trust, and trust is the only currency in this market that doesn't get counterfeited. One: Corvane isn't Guild. Oh, he wears the chain and signs the ledgers, but I've traced his requisitions. He's moving coin through accounts that predate the Guild charter. Old money. *War* money. Two: the Hollow Men have been contracted. Three of them arrived by packet-boat Tuesday; two more came overland yesterday. You don't send five nameless killers after a runaway clerk. You send them after a swordspeaker's map." She let that sit. "Three: I know the Drowned Marches better than any living factor, because I grew up in them, in the mud, eating what the marsh gave and burying what the war gave. If you're going where I think you're going, you'll die in the first week without me."

"And if I'm not going anywhere?"

"Then you'll die in the second week anyway, because Corvane doesn't leave loose threads, and you've been pulling on one since the salt door." Sefa straightened. "So here is my offer, and I'll only make it once. Partnership. Not service—I'm not your hired guide and you're not my employer. Partnership. You hold the map. I hold the route, the contacts, the ferry-tolls, the places to sleep where the Hollow Men won't look. Both of us hold a hatred of Corvane, which is as good a foundation as any. We split whatever's at the end of this—knowledge, salvage, leverage, I don't care which. And neither of us betrays the other, because betrayal requires believing the other side will lose, and I don't believe you'll lose. I think you might be the most dangerous thing the Guild has employed in ten years, and I'd rather be beside that than in front of it."

Jude stared at her. Rain ran off the edge of her hood and fell between them, silver in the dark.

"Why?" The word came out raw. "Why not sell me? You could walk to Corvane tonight and be made senior factor by morning. You'd own half the Marches routes within the year. Don't tell me you're sentimental about Tam Vosk—you reported factors for less than what I've done."

"I could," Sefa conceded. "And then I'd spend the rest of my life knowing Corvane owned me the way he owns everything he touches—by knowing what I'd do for advancement. No." She shook her head slowly. "I've been a war-child and a warehouse-rat and a factor, and the one lesson all three taught me is that men like Corvane don't pay their debts, they *transfer* them. Sell you to him, and the debt becomes mine forever. Stand beside you, and maybe—maybe—we burn the ledger instead." She spread her hands. "Besides. I want something bigger than survival, Winterbourne. I want to know what's at the end of that map. I want to see the place the war came from. Call it curiosity. Call it greed. Call it the only honest thing about me."

Above them, the rain intensified, hammering the canvas roofs until the whole market sounded like applause. Somewhere a vendor cursed and lashed down his awning. The channel at their feet rose an inch, black water licking at their boot-soles.

Jude was silent a long time. When she spoke, her voice was low and stripped of everything clerical.

"If I say yes, you should know what I am."

"I know what you are." Sefa held her gaze. "I watched a locked gate open at your word. Whatever you call it—sword-speech, oath-work, the old gift—I know it exists, I know the Guild pays fortunes for rumors of it, and I know that Corvane's masters would flay you alive for it. Which is precisely why I'm not telling him." She extended her hand, palm up, the old market gesture: *nothing hidden, nothing held*. "Do we have terms?"

Jude looked at the hand. At Sefa. Back at the hand. Sefa could nearly hear the arithmetic running behind those grey eyes—the odds, the exits, the cost of trusting a stranger in a rainmarket at midnight. She could see the exact moment the calculation failed, when Jude realized there was no column left to check, that the only entry remaining was faith, and how much it must cost a woman who had spent ten years hiding to write that entry at all.

Jude took her hand.

Her grip was stronger than expected, callused in strange places—not a clerk's calluses—and her left hand, Sefa noted, stayed perfectly flat and still.

"One condition," Jude said. "We don't speak of what I am again until the Marches force us to. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Sefa shook once, firm, and released. "Then listen, because we leave tonight and the tide won't wait. There's a smuggler's stair off the oyster-flats, below the north wall—Corvane's watchers don't cover it because it smells too bad to be useful. I have a boat cached there and a cousin who owes me silence for two days' sail downcoast. From there, the Drowned Marches." She pulled her hood lower and glanced out at the market, at the lanterns guttering in the rising wind. "One more thing. The Hollow Men don't sleep, and they were seen asking after a woman with ink stains at the south gates an hour ago. So whatever courage you've been rationing, Winterbourne—spend it now."

Jude drew a breath that shuddered only slightly at the edges. When she exhaled, she was steady.

"Lead the way, then, factor."

They moved together into the rain, two shadows among many, threading between stalls as the market roared on around them, oblivious. Behind them the lanterns burned low; ahead, down the drowned lanes, the oyster-flats waited with their stink and their secrets and their smuggler's stair.

And somewhere in the dark of the city, five nameless men were already walking.
