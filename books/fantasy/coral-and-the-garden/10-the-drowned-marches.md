## 10. The Drowned Marches

The road ended where the world had drowned, and the Marches began the way a wound begins—gradually, then all at once.

Jude felt it before she saw it: a change in the quality of silence, as if the air itself had been holding its breath for ten years and had not yet been told it could exhale. The hard-packed road behind them gave way to mud the color of old bruises, and the mud gave way to shallow water stitched with reeds, and beyond the reeds, under a sky the grey of unpolished pewter, the sunken country opened out before them like a held secret.

Sefa stopped at the last dry stone and looked at Jude with an expression that was almost apologetic.

"I should have said something sooner," Sefa said. "About what it looks like."

"You said marshes. You said the war was fought here." Jude adjusted the strap of her pack, the map—folded now inside its ledger of debts—a small hard weight against her ribs. "You didn't say it would feel like this."

"Like what?"

"Like walking into a room where someone died," Jude said quietly, "and no one has moved anything since."

Sefa said nothing to that, which was itself an answer. She stepped off the stone and into the shallows, and Jude followed.

The water was warm in a way water should not be—blood-warm, almost—and it clung to their boots as though reluctant to let them pass. Reeds grew in ranks taller than a mounted man, whispering against each other with a sound like paper turning, like pages being read somewhere just out of sight. The light came down flat and sourceless, shadowless, so that distances became impossible to judge; a heron standing fifty yards away looked near enough to touch, and a dead tree ten feet off might have been a mile inland. Everything existed at once and nowhere. It was a country that had been taken out of time and set aside, unfinished.

And then they found the first soldier.

He stood in the shallows perhaps thirty paces from the path—or what passed for a path—and he was stone. Not buried, not sunk: standing, upright, whole, his boots planted in the silt where his boots had planted themselves on the last morning of the war. His face was turned toward some enemy long since scattered or dissolved, and his mouth was open mid-shout, and his sword was raised above his head, arrested at the top of its arc, the blade furred with rust the color of dried blood. Moss had colonized his shoulders. A bird's nest sat in the crook of his raised elbow, long abandoned. Water ran slowly, endlessly, off the edge of his blade into the marsh, drop by drop by drop, and had been running, Jude understood, since before she was born.

She stopped walking without deciding to.

"He won't hurt you," Sefa said, from several steps ahead. "None of them will. They can't."

"I know that."

"You don't sound like you know that."

Jude made herself move closer. Made herself look up at the stilled face, the young jaw, the tendons of the throat carved in permanent strain. He had been someone's son. Someone had probably carved his name into the hilt of that sword—the way soldiers did, the way they had always done, because a name on a blade was a prayer that you mattered—and the carving would be there still, under the rust, legible to anyone patient enough to scrape away ten years of weather. But there was no one left patient enough. There was only Jude, and the whisper.

It started low. She thought at first it was the reeds.

Then she thought it was the blood in her own ears.

Then she knew better, because the whisper was coming from the sword.

*—and hold, and hold, and hold the line until the—*

The words were not words exactly. They were the shape of words, the residue of a command spoken so many times, with such desperate conviction, that it had soaked into the steel the way ink soaks into parchment, and now, in the presence of someone who could hear such things, it seeped back out. Jude's skin prickled from wrist to shoulder. The gift she had starved for a decade stirred under her sternum like an animal smelling home, and for one vertiginous moment she almost answered—almost spoke back to the dead blade, *you are released, you are done, rest*—and had to clamp her teeth against it.

"Jude." Sefa's voice, sharp. "Your face. What is it?"

"Nothing." The lie came automatically, smoothly, the way lies had come ever since the salt door. "The mist. Let's keep moving."

But Sefa was watching her with those factor's eyes that weighed and appraised everything, and Jude walked past the soldier without looking back, and behind her, faint as breath, the sword went on whispering its last order to no one.

* * *

They made camp that night on the roof of a drowned farmhouse.

Only the ridge of it broke the water—a line of tiles furred green, a chimney leaning like a drunk—and the space between chimney and ridge was just large enough for two bedrolls and a small fire fed with driftwood and dried reed-tubers that smoked acrid and blue. Below them, somewhere under ten feet of black water, lay a hearth, a table, perhaps chairs. Perhaps a family had lived here once, farming the rich soil of a country that was not yet a battlefield. Jude tried not to think about whether they had gotten out.

"The water rose after," Sefa said. She was sitting with her knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, staring at the dark horizon where the marsh met the sky with no seam between. "Not during. That's the part nobody tells you. The war itself only lasted three years. Then the swordspeakers broke each other, and the ground broke too—all those oaths, all that power spent into the earth at once—and the rivers changed their minds about where to go. Whole provinces went under in a decade. My grandmother used to say the land drowned itself rather than keep feeding them." She glanced sideways at Jude. "Feeding *you*. People like you."

"People like me," Jude agreed evenly.

"Don't take offense. I'm only saying what everyone says."

"And does everyone say why the soldiers are still standing?"

That earned her a pause. Sefa unfolded slightly, interested despite herself. "You've heard about them before the Marches?"

"Everyone's heard of the petrified regiments. Pilgrims talk. Smugglers talk more." Jude fed a reed-tuber to the fire. "But hearing of a thing isn't understanding it. Petrification is a known art—battle-mages used it to preserve prisoners, and there are villages in the south where a whole raiding party stands in the well-field as a warning. Those men can be chiseled down and carted off as curiosities. These—" she nodded at the darkness, where she knew, without seeing, that more soldiers waited, ranks of them, "—these aren't statues. Statues don't hum."

"Hum."

"To anyone who can hear it." Jude looked into the fire rather than at Sefa. "Steel remembers. Every smith knows it—that's why they quench blades in special water, sing to them at the forge, give them names. A sword holds the shape of every hand that's gripped it and every word shouted over it. Most steel forgets quickly; it's soft metal, in that sense. But these blades—" her voice dropped, almost unwillingly, "—these were forged for oath-war. Spoken into. Bound to their wielders with vows on both sides, steel promising flesh and flesh promising steel. And when the wielders died mid-command, mid-swing, the vows never got to finish. An unfinished vow doesn't end, Sefa. It waits. It goes on being true forever, like a debt with no date of repayment written on it."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the reeds, water dripped from a petrified blade, patient as a clock.

"So they're still fighting," Sefa said slowly. "In there. In the metal."

"They're still obeying. I don't know if it's the same thing." Jude pulled her coat tighter. "Sleep. We cross the open flats tomorrow, and I'd rather do it with you awake and sharp."

Sefa lay down, but Jude, wrapped in her cloak by the dying fire, kept watch over a country of the obedient dead, and did not sleep at all.

* * *

The flats began at first light, if the grey paling of the east could be called light, and they were worse than the reed-beds, because on the flats there was nowhere to look away to.

The regiments stood in the shallows in formation.

Not scattered, not random: ranked. File upon file, company upon company, hundreds of soldiers petrified in the act of advance, their faces lifted toward a horizon that now held nothing but more marsh, more soldiers, more waiting. Some carried shields up. Some crouched behind them. One entire rank had been caught kneeling, reloading weapons that no longer needed loading, and the front line of the opposing army stood barely forty paces off—two armies frozen in the instant before contact, separated forever by a gap no charge would ever close. Between them the water lay mirror-flat, so that each army faced its own reflection, and Jude could not decide which was the ghost and which was the man.

She walked through them because the map said to walk through them. The route drawn in crawling ink led straight down the old line of advance, through the very center of the unmade battle, and Jude suspected the map chose its routes with a cruelty that was not accidental.

The whispering grew.

It came from everywhere now—from the raised blades and the lowered ones, from scabbards and spear-points and the rusted heads of halberds—layer upon layer of unfinished commands, spoken orders, dying vows, all of it overlapping into a low continuous murmur like a congregation reciting from different books. And because Jude could not stop her ears, she caught fragments:

*—for the crown, for the crown, until the walls or my—*

*—I swear this steel to your service while breath—*

*—come back to me, come back, I'll hold the bridge until you—*

That last one nearly stopped her feet. She recognized the cadence. It was the cadence of her own order, the swordspeakers' formulae, the binding-speech she had learned at fifteen and sworn herself silent over at twenty. Someone had bound a common soldier's blade with swordspeech—improvised, desperate, half-trained—and it had worked, and the working had outlived everyone it was meant to protect. *I'll hold the bridge until you.* Until whom? Until what? The bridge in question was likely forty feet under the water to her left. The *you* was ash. Only the promise went on, faithful and idiot and eternal, holding a bridge that was no longer there.

Her eyes burned. She kept walking.

"Jude." Sefa's voice, low, careful. She had fallen into step beside her. "You're crying."

"It's the wind."

"There's no wind."

"Then it's the mist." Jude wiped her face with the back of her glove, angry at herself, at the flat grey light, at the dead who would not finish their sentences. "Do you know what it's like, hearing a promise that can't ever be kept? Ten thousand of them? This whole country is one long unpaid debt, and I can hear every line of it, and there is nothing—" her voice cracked and she hated that most of all, "—nothing I can do for any of them. Not one. Not even say *it's over.*"

"Why not?" Sefa asked. Simply. Terribly. "If you can hear them, couldn't you—"

"No." Too fast, too loud. Jude swallowed. "No. Whatever I am or am not, speaking to these blades wouldn't free them. It would wake them. And waking things that were put down on purpose is how the last war started. So we walk. Quietly. Quickly. And I keep my ears shut, and my mouth shut, and we get to the Garden, and we burn whatever Corvane wants so badly, and none of this ever has to matter again."

Sefa studied her for a long moment, the flat light making her face unreadable.

"That's the second time you've almost told me what you are," she said. "I'm keeping count."

"I know," Jude said, and walked on.

* * *

They lost the path at noon.

Or the path lost itself—the map, sensing nothing wrong, continued cheerfully east across a stretch of flats that ended abruptly in a channel of deep black water, thirty feet across, too wide to jump, too cold to swim in any season Jude could imagine. Sefa cursed creatively and thoroughly, testing the bank's edge, finding only soft mud that swallowed her boot to the ankle.

"Ferry crossing," she said grimly. "Was a ferry crossing. Rope-and-plank, back when there was a here and a there. Nothing now."

"The map disagrees."

"The map's ink crawls around and rearranges itself at night. Forgive it a little optimism." Sefa straightened, wiping her hands, and her gaze snagged on something downstream. "Huh."

"Huh what?"

"There's a crossing marker. Old Guild post." She started along the bank, and Jude followed, and fifty yards on, half-drowned in reeds, they found it: a rotted post bearing the Meridian Guild's sigil, worn nearly smooth, with a rusted chain running from its base into the channel. And moored to the chain, riding low but intact, was a flat-bottomed ferry barge, its planks silvered with age, its pole still shipped upright in the stern as if the ferryman had stepped off a moment ago and meant to return.

"It's been ten years," Jude said. "Twenty. The wood should be soup."

"It should." Sefa's voice had gone flat and wary, the factor's voice, the one that smelled a bad contract three signatures away. "Look at the waterline. No rot below either. Someone's maintained this. Or—" she paused.

"Or the Marches maintain it," Jude finished quietly. "A crossing kept ready. A route kept open. Sefa, the whole war ran on logistics. Oath-armies moved on ferries and fords and supply posts, and every one of those was bound with speech the same as the blades. Maybe the vows haven't finished here either. Maybe the ferry is still waiting to be needed."

They looked at each other across the barge.

"We could go around," Sefa offered, without conviction.

"By the map, going around adds nine days through the deep fens." Jude stepped aboard. The planks took her weight without complaint, solid and sure, and the barge barely swayed—as if it were glad, as if it had been standing at attention for years and finally, *finally*, someone had arrived with somewhere to be. She pushed off with the pole, and the bottom, instead of dragging in mud, slid clean, as though the channel had been swept for them.

Halfway across, the whispering changed.

All around the boat, rising from the water on both sides, the whispering gathered itself, focused, and spoke—one voice made of many, rust and river and old iron, addressing her directly for the first time since they'd entered the Marches. Not a fragment. Not a broken order.

A question.

*Bearer of the living word. Are you come to pay us, or to collect?*

The pole shook in Jude's hands. Beside her, Sefa had gone rigid, staring at her, at the water, at her again—but Jude kept her eyes on the channel ahead, kept poling, kept the barge moving, because stopping felt like the most dangerous thing in the world.

"Neither," she said aloud, to the water, to the dead. "We're only passing through."

The silence that followed had weight, and shape, and attention.

*For now,* the Marches whispered, from every sunken blade on both banks at once, *the debt sleeps. But you carry the word, and the word wakes what it touches. Mind the third ford. The ones who stand there remember hands.*

The whispering sank back into its endless murmur, and the far bank arrived, and Jude grounded the pole and did not look down at the black water where, beneath the surface, she could feel them—hundreds of blades, pointed upward, listening.

Sefa waited until they were a mile gone before she spoke.

"The third ford," she said. "What stands there?"

Jude watched the reeds parting ahead of them, and thought of a rank of soldiers she'd seen that morning—kneeling, patient, their rusted swords resting across their stone thighs, closer together than any other formation, as if they had been placed rather than caught. As if they had been *left.*

"A guard," she said. "Somebody posted a guard, and nobody ever relieved it."
