## 11. Whispers in Rust

The Marches did not want them there. Jude had understood that from the first mile, the way one understands a closed door—not with reason but with the flat certainty of the body. The causeway they followed was old imperial road, drowned and dredged and drowned again, its paving stones furred green where they broke the surface of the marsh. On either side the water lay brown-gold and still as poured honey, thick with silt, holding the sky in a smeared mirror. Nothing moved in it. Not fish, not birds, not even the wind, which seemed to arrive at the reeds already tired.

And standing in the water—standing everywhere—were the soldiers.

Jude had known, of course. Everyone knew. The petrified legions were the war's most famous corpse, carved into ballads and cautionary tales for children: the armies of the Accord and the Thorn Covenant, caught mid-charge by whatever the swordspeakers had loosed at Ashfall Bridge, turned to grey stone from boot to brow. Ten thousand figures, it was said. Twenty. No one had ever finished counting. They stood in the shallows in ranks that had not quite dissolved after ten years of water and frost, some leaning, some kneeling, some frozen with an arm flung out toward an enemy who was also stone and also reaching.

Knowing about them was different from walking between them.

They came close to the causeway here, close enough that Jude could see the individual faces—the softening of stone where rain had worn a cheek smooth, the lichen blooming green across a pauldron like moss on a fallen branch. One young soldier stood barely three feet away at the road's edge, his mouth open around a shout no one would ever finish hearing, his spear couched beneath his arm. His eyes had weathered into blankness. Jude made herself look anyway. She owed them that much, she thought. She had helped end them—or failed to save them; after ten years she still could not decide which sentence was true—and the least she could do was count their faces among her debts.

"You keep looking at them like you know them," Sefa said behind her.

"I don't."

"You look like you *owe* them."

Jude kept walking. "Everyone owes the dead something. That's what makes us living."

Sefa made a sound low in her throat—not agreement, exactly. She walked the way she did everything, economically, her factor's boots chosen for city cobbles now sucking at the mud with every step, her expression arranged into the particular boredom she wore like armor. But Jude had spent two days learning her tells, the way Sefa had once learned hers. The boredom was a performance. Beneath it, Sefa's eyes moved constantly: counting soldiers, marking sightlines, noting which stretches of causeway would take their weight and which would fold them into the water. A war-child's habits. They never slept.

The humming started an hour past midday.

At first Jude thought it was her own blood. It had been doing this since the map—a low resonance under the skin, a tuning fork struck somewhere behind the sternum—but here in the Marches it deepened, gathered harmonics, became almost a chord. She stopped on the causeway without deciding to stop.

Sefa stopped too. "What is it?"

"Nothing." The lie came automatically, and tasted worse than usual, because the truth was rising in her throat like water in a well. "We should make the ferry crossing before dark. The ferryman—"

"The ferryman can wait." Sefa stepped closer, and her voice dropped into something stripped of performance. "Jude. The stones are singing."

She was right. That was the horror of it. It wasn't only Jude's blood—it was audible, faint but real, a vibration in the air like the hum of a struck bell heard through a wall. And it was coming from the soldiers. From the spears couched under stone arms, the swords half-drawn in grey fists, the blades rusted mid-swing that the ballads always mentioned, because rusted iron sang differently than stone, a thinner note, a colder one.

"They're responding to me," Jude said, before she could stop herself, and then heard what she'd said, and closed her eyes.

"*Responding.*"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Let me—" But there was no way to arrange this, no ledger entry that would balance. Ten years of careful arithmetic and it all came due on a flooded road in the middle of a dead country. "My name isn't just Winterbourne," she said. "It's the last one. There used to be others. Swordspeakers. We didn't carry swords. We *spoke* them. We spoke metal awake."

The hum rose. She felt it answer her confession, felt the nearest blades lean toward her voice the way flowers lean toward light, and something in her chest cracked along a seam she'd sealed at nineteen years old.

Sefa had gone very still. Stiller than the soldiers. "You're one of them," she said. "You're a swordspeaker. You're the *last* swordspeaker, and you've been balancing accounts for Corvane for ten years."

"Someone had to keep the ledgers honest."

"That's your defense?" For the first time since the salt door, Sefa laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "Gods. All those lies you told so smoothly. I thought I'd found a clever fraud. I found a *relic*."

The word went through her like a spear. Relic. Thing left over. And perhaps that was fair, but—

The hum broke into harmony.

Later, Jude would try to reconstruct the exact moment it went wrong, and she never fully could. She remembered the sun going behind cloud, though the clouds here were permanent residents. She remembered the taste of copper at the back of her tongue. She remembered thinking, with strange clarity: *they can smell it. My blood. They've been waiting ten years for someone whose blood still remembers how to answer.*

The young soldier with the couched spear did not move. Stone does not move. But the spear in his arms shivered, and a flake of rust fell from the blade like dried blood, and the hum climbed an octave, and every sword within earshot—that was the phrase her mind supplied, *within earshot*, as if the weapons had ears—every sword began to pull.

Not the bodies. Just the metal. Blades strained against stone grips with a grinding groan, iron fighting to free itself from the hands that had held it for a decade, and the sound was terrible, a chorus of hinges and grief, and Jude understood with cold, complete knowledge: they weren't attacking. They were *answering*. Her gift had woken in her blood and broadcast itself across the marsh like a shout in a valley, and everything forged and forgotten was turning toward the sound.

"Sefa," she said. "*Run.*"

Sefa ran. She was quick—war-child quick, low and fast along the causeway—but the causeway was narrow and slick, and the nearest blade tore free.

That was the image that would stay with Jude afterward: not the violence, but the tearing. A rusted longsword wrenching itself out of a stone fist with a shriek like a nail pulled from wood, taking fingers of petrified flesh with it, and then moving through the air with the drunken, hungry grace of something that had been asleep far too long. It did not fly so much as *lurch*, dragging itself by its own weight, and it went for the loudest thing in the marsh besides Jude.

It went for Sefa.

"No—" Jude spun, reached for the words, and found her own voice locked. Ten years of silence is not broken cleanly. The speech was there, coiled under her skin, but the oath sat on top of it like a stone lid, and prying the lid off hurt more than she'd imagined any pain could. She got out the first word—"*Still*"—and the lurching sword stuttered, dragged, fought her.

A second blade came free. Then a third. Down the line of the ranks, iron was waking all along the shore, a wave of grinding and shrieking rolling outward from where she stood, and the hum in her chest was no longer hers alone; it was the whole drowned army humming through her ribs, and they wanted, they *wanted*, ten years of stillness and here at last was blood that spoke their language—

The first sword caught Sefa across the shoulder.

Not the edge—she twisted fast enough, war-child fast, and took the flat, the pommel, the full ugly weight of rusted iron swung by nothing, and it knocked her off the causeway into the shallow water. She went down hard. Came up coughing, one hand pressed to her collarbone, her eyes wide and fixed not on the sword but on Jude, and in that look there was no fear of the weapon at all.

There was fear of *her*.

That was the monstrous thing. Not the blades. Jude had grown up with blades; she knew their appetites. The monstrous thing was Sefa's face—her ally, her watcher, her uneasy partner in theft and flight—looking at her the way people looked at the war. Looking at her like a natural disaster wearing a woman's coat.

"*Kneel,*" Jude said, and the oath-lid shattered, and the speech came out of her whole.

She hadn't spoken a true command in ten years. The word left her and the marsh *obeyed*. Every freed blade dropped—not fell, *dropped*, driven down into the mud point-first like a field of grim harvest, a dozen swords planting themselves upright in the shallows, quivering. The hum collapsed into silence so sudden her ears rang with it. The second wave, the blades still straining in stone fists, sagged back against their petrified owners and went quiet, rust settling, the chord dying note by note until there was nothing but water dripping and Sefa breathing hard in the shallows and Jude on her knees on the causeway without remembering how she'd gotten there.

Her nose was bleeding. She wiped it and looked at her hand, red-brown in the grey light. Old habit. Blood price, her mentor used to call it. Speech drawn from the deep places always drew something else up with it.

Sefa hauled herself onto the causeway. She did it slowly, watching Jude the entire time, and when she stood she did not come closer. Water streamed off her coat. Her hand was still clamped over her collarbone, and beneath it Jude could see the bruise already flowering, dark as spilled ink.

"So," Sefa said.

"So."

"You're the thing my village fed to the Garden." Her voice was level. That frightened Jude more than shouting would have. Level voices were what came *after* the decision. "You're what the war was actually about. And you let me walk beside you for two days believing you were a runaway bookkeeper."

"I *am* a runaway bookkeeper."

"You're the last ember of a fire that burned my childhood down." Sefa's eyes flicked to the planted swords standing in the mud, twelve of them, upright and silent, and Jude saw her cataloguing each one, calculating angles, distances, whether stone fists could be trusted to hold their iron now. "Do you know what I was going to do? When we got to the Garden? I was going to sell the map and disappear. That was the whole plan. Simple. Clean. Now—" She laughed again, and this time it had a crack in it. "Now I'm standing next to the map *and* the treasure, and I'm bleeding, and I don't know which one of you is more dangerous."

"The sword hit you. Not me."

"The sword answered *you*. Don't split hairs with me, Winterbourne. I grew up splitting hairs with people who could kill me." She flexed the shoulder, winced, kept her eyes on Jude. "How long? How long have you been able to do that?"

"Since I was six."

"And you hid it for—what, fifteen years? Sixteen?"

"Ten since the oath. Sixteen since the first word." Jude got to her feet. Her legs shook. The speech had taken more than blood; it had taken something structural, some beam inside her, and she felt the lack like a missing tooth, the tongue going again and again to the gap. "There were four hundred of us at Ashfall Bridge. There were eleven alive at sunset. My mentor bought my life with a vow of silence, and I kept it, because keeping it was the only part of her I had left. Until the courier. Until the map." She looked down the drowned road, toward the distant shape of the ferry landing, a black tooth against the grey horizon. "Until you."

"Flattering."

"It wasn't a compliment. It was an accusation. I should have told you at the salt door. I should have told you in the rainmarket." She met Sefa's eyes and made herself hold them. "I'm telling you now because you deserve to decide what to do with it. Sell me to Corvane. Leave me on this road. Whatever you choose, choose it knowing."

The marsh held its breath. Somewhere out in the ranks, water dripped from a stone elbow. The planted swords stood in their row like a fence built by grief.

Sefa was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Jude counted her own heartbeats and lost track twice. When Sefa finally spoke, her voice had changed—stripped of the factor's polish, of the predator's patience, down to something rawer and younger, a girl's voice from a burned village.

"My brother thought the swordspeakers were angels," she said. "He used to draw them on the walls. Little stick figures with shining swords. He died in the second harvest, when the Guild came through collecting for Corvane's ledgers." She touched her bruised collarbone again, absently, testing it. "If I turn you in, I sell the last angel to the man who killed him. If I don't, I'm allied with a power that just proved it can wake every blade between here and the sea." A short breath, almost a laugh. "You're right. It's monstrous. Both options are monstrous. Welcome to my whole life."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a *not yet*. There's a difference, and you'll learn it." She started walking, favoring the left side, and jerked her head for Jude to follow. "Ferry landing's two miles. We cross before dark or we camp in the open with those things listening to your heartbeat all night. And Jude—" She didn't turn around. "From now on, anything your blood decides to do, I hear about it *first*. I've been lied to by institutions my entire life. I won't take it from one person too."

"It wasn't—"

"It was. Walk."

Jude walked. Behind them, the twelve planted swords stood in the shallows, and as they drew away she felt them—faintly, the way one feels a hand resting on one's shoulder after the hand has gone—felt them *watching*. Not hostile anymore. Worse. Hopeful. They had tasted her voice, and they would wait for it, rusting patiently in the mud, the way the whole drowned country waited, ten thousand stone soldiers holding their unfinished shouts, and every rusted blade among them dreaming of being spoken awake.

At the bend in the causeway, the ferry landing came into view at last—and with it, a figure. A lantern hung from a crooked pole, unlit despite the grey afternoon, and beneath it sat a flat-bottomed boat and a tall shape wrapped in oilcloth who had, Jude realized with a sinking certainty, been waiting far longer than two travelers could possibly have warranted.

The ferryman raised his head. Even at this distance, she heard him smile.

"Toll," he called across the water, in a voice like rope tightening, "is paid in memories. Decide what you'll surrender, ladies. The boat leaves with the dark, and it does not wait, and it does not return."
