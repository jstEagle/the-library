## 12. The Ferryman's Toll

The river appeared where no river should be.

Sefa had crossed the Drowned Marches twice in her life—once as a child carried in the bottom of a grain cart, once as a woman with Guild coin and a hired guide who turned back at the petrified soldiers and would not go further, oath or no oath. Neither time had she seen water like this. It lay across their path without warning, black and utterly still, a mile wide or ten, impossible to say. No wind touched it. The reeds along the shore stood bent toward it, all in one direction, like a crowd leaning in to listen.

At its edge waited a boat.

It was a small thing, flat-bottomed, tarred black, with a lantern hanging from a curved pole at the stern. The lantern's flame burned the wrong color—a deep bruised violet, casting shadows that fell the wrong way, toward the light instead of away from it. And at the tiller stood the ferryman.

He was not what the old stories promised. No hooded skeleton, no rotting ghoul of the marsh-folk's warnings. He was simply a man, grey-cloaked, unremarkable, with the patient face of someone who has done one job for a very long time and has stopped wondering whether he is good at it. Only his eyes were wrong: they were the color of the water, and when Sefa met them she felt, briefly, every memory she owned laid out on a table, being counted.

She looked away first.

"That's him," Jude said quietly. She stood at Sefa's shoulder with her hand resting—not idly, Sefa noted—on the hilt of the sword that had followed them out of the soldier-field. The blade rode at her hip now as if it had always been there, though three days ago it had been rust in a dead man's fist. "The Toll."

"You know this from your order." It was not quite a question. Since the whispers in the rust, since the moment Jude had spoken down a field of animate swords with her voice alone, Sefa had been recalibrating everything she thought she knew about the quiet auditor with ink-stained fingers. She did it methodically, the way she did everything: new facts, new columns, new sums.

"I know it from stories my mentor told," Jude said. "Whether they're true—" She looked at the violet lantern. "We're about to find out."

Sefa walked toward the boat before her courage could develop an opinion. That was the trick of it, she had learned early. Never let fear finish its sentence.

The ferryman watched them come. When they were ten paces off he raised one hand—not a greeting, a pause—and spoke. His voice was dry as paper pulled from a drowned man's pocket.

"One crossing. Two passengers. Two fares." He turned his wrong-colored eyes on each of them in turn. "I don't take coin here. Coin is only ever a stand-in for wanting. I take the want itself. I take memory."

"We know," Sefa said.

"Do you?" Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the place a smile had been sanded away. "Most who reach me know the rule but not the price. The rule is easy: pay a memory. The price is that you don't choose which one."

* * *

Sefa made herself breathe slowly.

"Not chosen by whom?" Jude asked. Her voice was level. Sefa recognized the levelness for what it was—the same trained flatness Jude used over ledgers, over lies, over everything. A wall built out of professional calm, mortared so long ago the builder had probably forgotten it was there.

"By the river," said the ferryman. "I am only its hands. It takes what weighs most. What you have carried longest. What you would miss so badly that missing it has become part of you." He gestured at the boat with his chin. "That is the fare. Not any memory. *The* memory. And you will not know what is gone until it is gone. You will feel the hole it leaves. You will spend the rest of your life learning its shape."

Silence stretched over the still water. Somewhere far out, something moved beneath the surface—not a fish; too slow, too large, moving with the deliberation of a thing that was listening.

"And if we refuse?" Sefa asked.

"Then you walk around," the ferryman said. "The Marches will take... some time. Some years. Perhaps some of you." He shrugged, a small economical motion. "Everyone pays eventually. I merely offer a discount for directness."

Sefa laughed before she could stop herself—one short bark that startled a heron out of the reeds, if it was a heron; it flew wrong too. The ferryman regarded her without offense.

"You find this amusing, factor of the Meridian Guild."

"I find it familiar," Sefa said. "A toll dressed as necessity. A gatekeeper who claims he doesn't set the rules, he only enforces them." She stepped closer to the water's edge. "Every institution I've ever worked for was run by men saying exactly that."

"Perhaps," the ferryman said, "your institutions learned it from places like this." He held out his hand, palm up, an open invitation. "Board when you are ready. Or turn back. The Garden does not move."

*The Garden does not move.* But we do, Sefa thought. We move constantly, we run and bargain and bleed, and at the end of every road there is another toll, and everyone pretends the toll-taker is not also the road-maker.

She thought about Corvane. She thought about the courier dying against the salt door with a name in his mouth and wax under his fingernails. She thought about her brother, which was dangerous, because thinking about him meant the river might hear.

She stopped thinking about him immediately, deliberately, slamming the door—but it was already too late, she realized. The water had rippled very slightly, all across its surface, in a pattern too regular to be wind. The river had heard. The river was counting.

* * *

They argued in the reeds, low-voiced, while the ferryman waited with the patience of driftwood.

"It could take anything," Jude said. "Your languages. Your names. Your knowledge of the Marches—we need that."

"It takes one memory. The heaviest one. And I know what mine is." Sefa heard her own voice go flat and precise, the Guild-negotiator's voice, the one she used when the number on the table mattered too much to show it. "There's really only one candidate. So in effect I do get to choose. I'm choosing to hand it over rather than have it ripped out mid-crossing."

"Sefa—"

"Don't." She said it sharply enough that Jude actually closed her mouth. Ten days of travel together had taught Sefa the exact weight of Jude Winterbourne's silences, and how rare it was to make one happen. "You don't get to counsel sacrifice. Not today. Today you're carrying something just as heavy, and we both know it."

Jude's jaw tightened. She looked out at the black water, and Sefa watched her do the same arithmetic she was doing herself: what do you own that you could not bear to lose, and isn't the answer always the same terrible thing?

"My mentor," Jude said finally, so quietly Sefa almost lost it to the reeds. "Master Ellery Voss. At Ashfall Bridge she—" Jude stopped. Started again. "She bought my life with an oath. Made me swear silence. Then she stayed behind on the bridge and I ran, and I have spent ten years unable to remember her face without pain, because the last time I saw it she was turning away from me." Her thumb moved along the pommel of the borrowed sword, back and forth, a small unconscious rhythm. "If the river takes that... I don't know what I'll be without it. The grief is ugly. But it's mine. It's the only honest thing I kept from that day."

"Yes," Sefa said. "Exactly that."

They looked at each other, and for a moment the transactional wariness that had governed their alliance thinned to something else—two women standing at the edge of a black river, each about to amputate a piece of herself to keep walking.

"The Hollow Men are two days behind us," Sefa said. "Corvane is perhaps three. Those are the only numbers that matter now."

Jude exhaled through her nose. "Board the boat, factor."

* * *

The boat did not rock. That was the worst of it, Sefa thought as they pushed off—or rather, as the ferryman merely lifted one finger and the boat left the shore without pole or sail or oar. There was nothing to hold onto, no motion to brace against, no evidence at all that they were moving except that the far shore, invisible before, began—slowly, grudgingly—to exist.

The water beside the hull was clear here, in the deep channel. Clear and impossibly deep. Sefa looked down once and saw lights far below, drifting, thousands of them, like a city seen from the air at night. Memories. All the fares ever paid, sinking and settling and glowing. She saw shapes among them: a wedding dance, a face laughing, a burning house, hands braiding hair. Whole lives paid out one recollection at a time to cross a river that went nowhere anyone wanted to stay.

"Don't look long," the ferryman said from the tiller. "Some passengers see their own, floating up, and try to buy them back. There is no buying back."

"What happens to them?" Jude asked. Her voice was careful. "The memories. Do you keep them? Use them?"

"They sink," the ferryman said. "They become the river. Every river remembers everything that crossed it; that is why old rivers are dangerous and young ones are not. Yours will join the rest. It will not suffer. It will not even notice. That is the mercy of being a memory—you exist only when remembered."

Sefa stared at him. "And the people who paid? We're supposed to just—continue? Missing a load-bearing wall?"

"You'll be surprised," the ferryman said, with the first real expression she'd seen on him—a flicker of something like pity, quickly troweled over. "People continue after worse amputations. They build rooms around the hole. Sometimes they build very beautiful rooms." He adjusted the tiller a fraction, though the boat needed no steering. "Though I will tell you what few ask. The river does not take the whole memory cleanly. It takes the center of it. The image, the sound, the name. The edges survive. You will remember that there was love. You will remember that there was loss. You will remember that you used to know exactly what they looked like. You will simply never again see the face."

"That's monstrous," Jude said.

"That's grief," said the ferryman. "I only perform it faster."

* * *

Midway, the river called first claim.

Sefa felt it before she understood it—a softening at the back of her skull, a warmth like sunlight on a winter afternoon, and then Tam was there.

Not summoned. Simply *present*, the way he always was, underneath everything: her younger brother, eight years old and furious about it, standing in the doorway of their house in a village that fed the Garden's first harvest, holding the wooden sword he'd carved himself because his sister was going to be a swordspeaker and somebody in the family ought to have one ready. His hair was crooked where he'd cut it himself. His front teeth had a gap. He said, the way he always said it, grinning: *"When you get your sword, Sef, promise you'll come back for me. Promise you won't leave me in the fields."*

She had promised. She had left anyway, at fourteen, in the bottom of a grain cart, because the recruiters came once a generation and did not wait, and she told herself she would return with money, with power, with a way out for both of them—

—and then the Garden took the village's tithe, and there was no Tam to return to, and there never had been again, and she had carried that doorway, that grin, that gap-toothed promise through twenty years of ledgers and factors' tables and careful, predatory patience, and it weighed, oh, it weighed—

"No," she said aloud, and grabbed for it, and of course there was nothing to grab; you cannot hold a memory in your hands, that is the entire nature of a memory. The warmth at the back of her skull was gentle, terribly gentle, the gentleness of a tide taking sand.

*"Promise you'll come back for me."*

"I'm sorry," Sefa said, into the empty air of the middle of the river, to nobody, to everybody. "Tam. I'm sorry. I came back for the map instead."

And it was gone.

She knew it immediately the way you know a tooth is missing—with the tongue of the mind probing a sudden absence. The doorway remained. She could describe the doorway, the crooked lintel, the smell of the house. She could even describe the boy, factually: eight years old, wooden sword, gap in his teeth. But the face—

The face was a white space shaped like a face. She reached for it and found smooth nothing, and understood with a horror that arrived late, like thunder after lightning, exactly what the ferryman had meant. She would spend the rest of her life knowing there had been a boy who loved her, and never again seeing him love her.

Something wet hit her hand. She was crying, she discovered, distantly, as if reporting on someone else. She did not wipe her eyes. Let the auditor watch. Let the swordspeaker see precisely what kind of creature she had allied with, and what it cost, and that she paid it anyway, sitting upright, without begging.

The ferryman watched her with his river-colored eyes and said nothing, and gave her, from somewhere inside his grey cloak, a square of clean cloth. It was the single kindest thing anyone had done for her in years, and she hated him for it.

* * *

They took Jude second.

Sefa saw it happen from across the boat's width: Jude went rigid, her breath catching on a snag, and her lips moved around a word—*no*—that she was too disciplined to say, because she had agreed to this, because she had done the arithmetic, because she was Jude Winterbourne who kept her oaths even the stupid ones, especially the stupid ones.

Sefa watched ten years fall off her companion's shoulders, and wished she could look away, and didn't.

Whatever surfaced in Jude—Ellery Voss, the bridge, the turning-away—it held her for four heartbeats, five, six, her knuckles white on the gunwale, and then the river took it with the same gentle tide, and Jude gasped like a swimmer coming up, and sat there staring at her own hands.

"She had grey eyes," Jude whispered. It was not to Sefa. It was not to anyone. "I know she had grey eyes. I've told myself she had grey eyes for ten years. I can't—I know it the way you know a fact in a ledger, and I can't *see* it."

Her face crumpled and reassembled itself, crumpled and reassembled, a war between the woman and the mask, fought in seconds. Sefa watched her lose and win simultaneously.

"I remember the oath," Jude said, steadier now, feeling along the edges the ferryman had described. "I remember every word she made me swear. I remember her voice. I remember that she turned away from me on the bridge and I ran. But her face is—" She made a small, terrible sound that was almost a laugh. "It's like a portrait someone hung backwards."

"Now you understand the fare," the ferryman said.

The far shore was close. When had that happened? The black water was thinning to brown, reeds rising ahead, and beyond them, faint as a rumor, the first dark suggestion of hedgerow—Thornwall, though neither of them knew it yet.

Sefa wiped her face with the ferryman's cloth and folded it and handed it back, and he took it, and for a moment their fingers were near, human to whatever he was.

"What did it take from you?" she asked him. "To make you the hands of the river? Was it worth knowing the price?"

He considered the question honestly, she would give him that. He looked out over his remembering water for a long moment.

"I don't know," he said. "I paid so long ago that the hole is the shape of my whole life. Perhaps I was someone's brother too." The boat touched the shore. He lifted his finger, and they were still. "Go. Follow the hedge until it opens for you or refuses you. And factor—"

Sefa paused, one foot on the mud.

"The river tells me things. It is gossiping water, and it holds everything ever surrendered to it." His river-eyes were fixed on her, unreadable. "Two days behind you, men without names have reached the petrified soldiers. And one of the soldiers has begun to whisper to them about a woman who speaks to swords."

Behind her, she heard Jude go very still.

"The Garden does not move," the ferryman said, picking up his violet lantern as the boat began, gently, to slide backward into the dark. "But everything else does. Walk quickly."
