## 13. Corvane's Hounds

The counting-house of the Meridian Guild kept its true hours in the dark. By day it was all brass and arithmetic, the clatter of abacuses, the polite violence of interest compounded. But after the closing bell, when the factors went home to their suppers and their small dishonesties, Master Corvane descended to the Vault of Accounts and did his real work by lamplight.

He liked this hour. He liked the way the ledgers breathed.

Not literally—Corvane had no patience for the romantic superstitions that clung to impossible trade like barnacles. The ledgers breathed in the sense that any living system breathes: they took in debts and exhaled obligations, and somewhere in the long intestines of the Guild's accounts, everything a person owed eventually came due. Corvane had spent thirty years learning the rhythm of that breathing. He could put his ear to a column of figures and hear a man's future failing like a distant heart.

Tonight he heard something else. Tonight he heard a name spoken at the salt door, and names were currency too—the oldest kind, and the most dangerous.

*Winterbourne.*

The courier had died with the word half out of his mouth, and the word had traveled exactly as far as it needed to: into the ears of two enforcers, one of whom had sold it to Corvane for the price of his daughter's debt forgiven, and from Corvane's ears down into the ledger where such things were recorded in ink that only appeared under certain conditions. The enforcer thought he had been paid generously. He would think so right up until the moment Corvane decided otherwise. That was the nature of payment in the Meridian: nothing was ever fully paid; it was only deferred, with interest accruing in the dark.

Corvane turned the page of the death record and read it again, though he knew it by heart.

*Male courier, unregistered. Cause of death: three knife wounds, professional work. Goods stolen: one map, wax-sealed, provenance unknown.*

The entry was a lie, and it was his lie, and it was beautiful. He had written *stolen* because stolen goods could be pursued, recovered, and their thief punished. A gift freely given would have required an entirely different set of procedures—procedures involving questions about who gives maps to counting-house clerks, and why, and what else clerks might be given. Corvane did not intend to answer questions. He intended to close them.

He dipped his pen. The nib scratched against the vellum like a small animal trying to get out of a sack.

Below the death record, in the margin where auditors were permitted to make private annotations, he wrote:

*Item: one map, active. Item: one clerk, Jude Kestrel-alias-Winterbourne, compromised. Item: retrieval authorized. Witnesses: none retained.*

The lamplight guttered. Somewhere above him, through six floors of stone and cedar, the city went on making noise—carts, gulls, a drunk singing something about the war that everyone pretended not to remember. Corvane listened to the drunk for a moment with an expression that might have been mistaken for nostalgia by someone who did not know him. It was not nostalgia. It was inventory. He was counting how many people in the city still remembered the war well enough to recognize what was coming, and the number pleased him. It was very small. Small numbers were easy numbers.

* * *

They came to him at midnight, as they always did, though there was no reason they should have known the hour. The Hollow Men did not keep appointments. They simply arrived when they were needed, the way weather arrives, or rot.

There were five of them, and they came up the Vault stairs without sound, which was the first thing people always noticed about them—not their faces, not their gray uniforms without insignia, but the silence. Men make noise when they walk. They shift their weight, clear their throats, announce themselves to the world in a hundred small ways because they are afraid the world will forget them. The Hollow Men made no noise because the world had already forgotten them, thoroughly and surgically, and they knew it the way an amputee knows a missing limb: constantly, and without being able to do anything about it.

Their names had been taken from them. Not erased—Corvane was careful about the distinction, and the distinction mattered. Erasure left gaps, and gaps left grief, and grief left questions. What the Guild's surgeons did was finer work than erasure. They had gone into each man's memory with instruments Corvane preferred not to think about and removed the name the way you might remove a splinter: cleanly, leaving the flesh around it whole. Each Hollow Man remembered his mother's face. He remembered his childhood home, his first fight, the taste of every meal he'd ever loved. He remembered everything except the one word that connected all those things to a self. Ask him who he was and he would open his mouth and find nothing there—not pain, precisely, but a smoothness, a place where something had grown around an absence until the absence became a kind of organ.

It was, Corvane considered, the most honest condition a servant could have. A man with a name wanted things. He wanted revenge, or redemption, or to go home. A man without a name wanted only what he was told to want, and wanted it with the whole of himself, because there was nothing else in him to want it instead.

"Sit," Corvane said, though there were no chairs. The Hollow Men did not sit. They stood in their loose semicircle, five gray shapes at the edge of the lamplight, and waited with the patience of furniture.

He let them wait. This too was procedure. Obedience, like debt, appreciated with time.

"The Guild has suffered a theft," he said. His voice was mild, almost apologetic, the voice of a man discussing a spilled drink. "A document of some value has been taken from these premises by a person in our employ. You will retrieve it."

He described the map without describing it. This was deliberate. He gave them its dimensions, its weight, the particular smell of its wax seal—which he had sampled from the courier's corpse, pressing the broken seal to his tongue like a communicant—and the way the parchment behaved when rolled: reluctantly, as if it would rather be flat. He did not tell them what the map showed. The Hollow Men did not need to know what they carried. Knowledge was weight, and weight slowed men down, and besides, Corvane had learned long ago that the surest way to keep a secret was to distribute it in pieces that meant nothing until assembled. He held the assembling part. He would always hold the assembling part.

"The thief," he continued, "is a woman. She will have left the city by the east roads or the tidewalks—she is cleverer than her position suggests, which I confess surprised me." He allowed himself the smallest pause here, the ghost of a smile. Surprise was not an emotion he indulged often, and he resented it even when he did. "You will find her. You will take the document. And you will leave no witnesses."

One of the five shifted—a fractional movement, a settling of shoulders—and Corvane marked it. They were not supposed to shift. He catalogued the man's position in the semicircle, the direction of the movement, the faint creak of his harness. Third from the left. He would watch that one. Even hollowed men wore down, like coins passed hand to hand, their faces smoothing away. Sometimes what remained underneath was nothing at all. Sometimes it was worse than nothing.

"No witnesses," the third-from-the-left repeated. His voice was flat as slate. It was not a question, but it was nearly one, and Corvane heard the nearness of it the way he heard everything: as an account beginning to run.

"You understand the term," Corvane said pleasantly. "Anyone who has seen the document, spoken with the thief, or assisted her in flight. Anyone who might describe your faces to the council, should the council ever grow curious about Guild operations—which it will not, if you are thorough." He folded his hands on the ledger before him. "I am aware that the phrase 'no witnesses' admits of interpretation. Some of my predecessors interpreted it narrowly. Kill the witnesses, leave the bystanders. I interpret it broadly. If a woman sells your quarry bread at a market stall, she has witnessed. If a child points in the direction you rode, that child has witnessed. Do you understand me?"

"We understand," said the one at the far right, who seemed to speak for the others, or perhaps simply spoke first because the others no longer cared about the order of things.

"Good." Corvane opened a drawer and drew out a purse—not of coin but of tokens, small lead discs stamped with the Guild's meridian line. "Payment on completion. Half now, as is customary, though I hardly know why we maintain the custom with men who cannot spend money on pleasure." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow crueler. "Buy provisions. Buy horses. Buy silence, where silence can be bought—it usually can, in this city, at reasonable rates."

The purse changed hands without changing the air.

"One more thing." Corvane rose from his chair for the first time, and the lamplight caught his face, and if any of the Hollow Men had still possessed the capacity to be unsettled, the sight might have unsettled them. There was nothing remarkable about Corvane's face—it was a bureaucrat's face, soft-jawed, spectacled, built for rooms rather than battlefields—but his eyes had the quality of eyes that had read too many final entries. People said the war had ended ten years ago. Corvane knew better. Wars did not end. They were merely balanced, carried forward, entered into the great ledger as assets awaiting reactivation. He had spent ten years keeping that asset on the books while lesser men called it closed. "The woman may have help. If anyone travels with her—if anyone shelters her—they fall under the same provision. No witnesses. Am I understood?"

"Yes, Master Auditor."

"Then go. East roads first. If she is clever, she will take the tidewalks, and the tidewalks flood at second bell tonight, which will slow her whether she knows it or not." He sat back down, already turning to other pages, dismissing them the way one dismisses rain. Then, as they reached the stair: "Gentlemen."

They stopped. Five gray backs, motionless.

"When you find her," Corvane said, without looking up, "you may find that she says things. Words that do strange work. If she speaks to you—if she says anything at all—you are to stop your ears or cut out your hearing, whichever is faster, and finish the task in silence. Am I understood?"

A pause. The longest pause the Hollow Men had ever offered anyone, nearly two full seconds.

"She talks?" asked third-from-the-left.

"All women talk," Corvane said mildly. "This one's words happen to be weapons. Go."

* * *

After they had gone—he listened until even the stairs forgot their footsteps—Corvane remained alone in the Vault with his lamps and his ledgers, and for a while he did nothing at all except listen to the building settle around him.

Then he unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a sword hilt without a blade. It was old work, pre-war, the grip wrapped in leather gone dark as dried blood. He unwrapped it and held it under the lamp and turned it slowly, and from the empty space where steel should have been there came a sound—not a ringing, not quite a hum, but the memory of a hum, the way a struck bell remembers itself in the metal long after the air goes quiet.

He had paid eleven fortunes for this hilt, over nine years, in transactions scattered across four countries so that no single ledger ever showed the shape of the purchase. The sellers had all believed they were selling scrap. One of them, a fence in Vhal Marenne, had laughed at him—"a hilt with no blade, master auditor, what use is that?"—and Corvane had smiled and paid him double and noted his address for later. Use was not the point. Resonance was the point. When the Garden woke—if it woke—the hilt would sing, and Corvane would hear it before anyone, and he would know that his decade of patient accounting had finally matured.

He had never held a sword. He had never wanted to. Swords were for the people history used up and wrote off as losses; Corvane had watched the swordspeakers burn at Ashfall Bridge and understood, with the clarity of a man reading a balance sheet mid-audit, that the ones holding the blades were never the ones holding the power. Power lived upstream. Power lived in the ledgers, in the supply lines, in the quiet rooms where someone decided what a thing was worth. The swordspeakers had been magnificent and bankrupt, spending themselves like coin, and when they ran out, the world called it peace.

Peace, Corvane thought, setting the hilt back in its cloth. The longest-running fraud in history. An entry on the books marked *closed* that no auditor had ever properly verified.

And now a dying courier had pressed a map into the hands of a clerk with a dead woman's surname, and the fraud was in danger of being audited from the other direction—from below, from the roots, from whatever still grew in the Drowned Marches where the world's first weapon had been planted like a seed.

He should have felt anger. A lesser administrator would have. But Corvane had outlived anger the way he had outlived most things, and what filled him instead was something colder and more durable: the satisfaction of a man whose forecast proves correct. He had suspected Winterbourne blood in the Guild's ranks for years—an old name does not stay buried unless someone keeps burying it, and burial costs money, and money leaves traces. He had never acted on the suspicion. Acting early was wasteful. Better to let the debt ripen until it could be collected all at once, principal and interest together.

Now the interest included a map, a burned oath, and—though the reports were thin on this point—possibly a witness named Sefa Ilvane, a factor with a talent for noticing things and a history that made her either a liability or an instrument. Corvane had not yet decided which. He had entered her in the ledger under both columns, with a note to reconcile the entries at his convenience.

He blew out two of the three lamps and sat in the reduced light, doing what he did best: nothing visible.

Above him, the city slept. Below the city, the tide moved through the drowned streets on its ancient schedule. Far to the east, past the salt flats and the petrified armies, something in the Marches waited under water and thorn, patient as an unpaid debt. And somewhere between here and there, a woman who had spent ten years pretending to be ordinary was running through the dark with a piece of parchment that wanted to be flat, and five gray men were already saddling horses they had bought with lead tokens, riding east with their ears open and their names gone.

Corvane opened a fresh ledger to its first clean page. At the top, in his small precise hand, he wrote a single line:

*Account reopened.*

Underneath it, after a moment's consideration, he added the date, and then—one more line, smaller, almost tender, the closest thing to prayer a man like him could produce:

*Let all balances come due.*

He closed the book. The lamp gutted once more and steadied. In the Vault of Accounts, in the dark that smelled of vellum and cold iron, the Meridian Guild began, quietly, to breathe in.
