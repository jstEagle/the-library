## 14. The Inn at Halfway

The inn announced itself an hour before they reached it—a squat stone fist raised against the flat gray sky of the Marches' edge, its walls scarred with old scorch-marks and newer repairs, its single tower flying a flag so faded Jude could not tell whether it had once been green or blue. Halfway, the sign said, in letters burned rather than painted: *The Inn at Halfway. All Roads Stop Here.*

"Charming," Sefa said.

"It means what it says." Jude shifted the strap of her pack, where the map rode wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, warm as a sleeping animal. "Halfway between the Marches and anywhere worth going. The garrison towns used to send couriers here. It's fortified because everything on this road was fortified once."

"Once," Sefa repeated, and looked out at the drowned fields beyond the road—paddies gone to reed and rust, a harrow standing in six inches of stagnant water like the ribcage of something that had lain down to die—and did not finish the thought.

They had been walking for three days since the ferryman. Jude still caught herself reaching for the memory of her mentor's face the way a tongue worries the socket of a lost tooth. She knew she had paid it; she could feel the smooth-edged absence where it had been. What remained was a shape without features: a voice like dry leaves, hands scarred from sword-grip, a name she now knew only as *my mentor*, the way you know a word in a language you no longer speak. She had wept the first night, quietly, facing away from Sefa, and Sefa had pretended to sleep with the rigid stillness of someone who was listening very hard indeed. Neither of them had mentioned it since. That, too, was a kind of ledger entry.

The inn's gate was oak banded with iron, and the man who opened it was broad as the door itself, with a beard going to iron-gray and eyes that catalogued them in one pass: two women, armed, mud to the knee, one favoring her left ankle. He did not ask their business. Places like this survived the war by not asking.

"Two beds if you have them," Jude said. "And food. Whatever the pot is, we'll take it."

"There's stew. There's beer. There's water if you're simple." He stepped aside. "Common room or yard?"

"Common room."

He grunted, which might have meant anything, and led them through a passage built to be defended by three men and a boiling cauldron into a courtyard that smelled of horse, woodsmoke, and rain about to arrive. The common room beyond was low-ceilinged and dim, lit by lamps behind horn panes, half full of carters and drovers who looked up, assessed, and looked down again with the practiced indifference of people who understood that attention was a debt and curiosity collected interest.

Jude chose a table in the corner with a wall at her back and a view of both doors. Sefa sat opposite, close enough that their knees nearly touched beneath the table, and when the pot-girl came, Sefa ordered for both of them before Jude could speak—stew, bread, beer, and a room, coins counted out with a factor's economy. It was the first time she had touched her own money since the tidewalks. Guild scrip left a trail; coin did not.

"You're paying," Jude said, when the girl had gone. "That's new."

"I'm investing." Sefa pulled off her gloves finger by finger. Her hands were chapped from the Marches' brackish water, and there was a scratch along one knuckle from the thornwall country they'd skirted that morning. "You hold the map. I hold the way through. But neither of us holds dinner, and I find I think better fed."

"That's the most honest thing you've said since the rainmarket."

"Don't get used to it." But there was no edge on it. Three days of shared danger had worn the edge down to something almost like ease, and the strangeness of that sat in Jude's chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The stew was mutton and barley and something rooty Jude didn't recognize, and it was the best thing she had ever tasted, which said more about the last week than the cook. They ate in silence for a while, the good silence of exhaustion, and then Sefa pushed her bowl away, tore a piece of bread in half, and held one half out to Jude without looking at her.

"My village is forty miles south of here," Sefa said. "Or it was. You'd call it nothing. A cluster of houses around a mill, terraces cut into the hillside, a shrine to some river god nobody remembered the name of anymore. The soil was wrong for grain—too wet, too strange—but things grew there that grew nowhere else. My mother said the ground had been fed too well once, and never forgot the taste."

Jude kept her hands still around her beer. "Fed how?"

Sefa turned the bread over in her fingers, not eating it. "I used to ask that. I stopped asking when I was nine, because my father boxed my ears for it, and my mother cried, and I learned that some questions are doors you don't open in a house like ours." She looked up. "You know what the Garden was. You were made there—or near enough. Do you know what fed it?"

"The oaths," Jude said slowly. "The tree drinks oaths. I've seen it."

"Oaths go up the chain. Power flows down. And power has to come from somewhere." Sefa's voice was level, the levelness of a woman reciting figures she had long since memorized. "There were twelve villages in the Garden's shadow. Not servants—the records, such as they were, called them *irrigation communities*. The soil drank what ran off from the terraces above. Everything the Garden pruned away. Every oath broken to make a swordspeaker, every name burned off a Hollow Man, every—" she paused, and Jude watched her choose precision over flinching— "every piece of a person that the making required. It all went somewhere. It went downhill. Into our fields."

The common room noise seemed to recede. Jude heard the fire tick, a carter laugh at something, rain beginning against the shutters.

"What grew?" she asked, though she already dreaded the answer.

"Wheat with grains like drops of blood, that cooked into bread that would keep for ten years. Apples that glowed faintly in the dark. Children born with green eyes in a village where no one else had them." Sefa smiled, and it was the worst smile Jude had ever seen on her, because it was genuine. "We ate well. That was the bargain, though no one alive ever signed it. We were fed by the Garden's leavings, and every few years the Garden's people came down the hill with carts, and they took—not crops. They took *people*. Volunteers first. Then not volunteers. My grandmother went in the second cart. My aunt in the fourth. They came back sometimes, weeks later, walking up from the Marches, and they'd sit by our fire and not speak, and their shadows moved wrong, and within a year they were dead or gone or hollow. My mother said they'd been *pruned*." She finally bit into the bread, chewed, swallowed. "I was six when I watched my uncle come back and try to dig up the shrine floor with his bare hands, saying there was a door under it, saying he could hear watering."

"Sefa—"

"I'm not finished." She wasn't cruel about it. She was simply a factor closing an account. "When the war ended and the swordspeakers broke, the harvests stopped. Within a generation the village was starving on soil that had forgotten how to be ordinary. Half of us left. The rest stayed and got poorer and stranger. I left at fourteen with a peddler's caravan and a head for numbers, and I swore I'd never be downstream of anyone again." She brushed crumbs from her fingers, one at a time. "Then I joined the Guild, which trades in impossible things, and spent fifteen years working my way toward the ledgers where impossible things are priced. Because I wanted to know who had signed for my village. Who had set the price. Whose hand was on the pen when my grandmother became an entry."

"And?"

"And it was Corvane's order. Old Corvane, young Corvane—it doesn't matter. His seal is on the irrigation charters. The Guild didn't create the arrangement, Jude. It *inherited* it. Bought it at auction after the war, along with everything else the old enemy order couldn't carry away." She met Jude's eyes. "So when I say I don't trust him, understand that I am not being tactical. I am being filial."

The rain thickened against the shutters. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged loose and was latched again. Jude found that her hands had gone cold around the beer she hadn't drunk.

"You could have told me this three days ago."

"Could I? What would you have done?" Sefa leaned forward. "You'd have pitied me. Pity makes people stupid and generous, and I needed you sharp and selfish. Now you know I'm not following you for the Guild, or for gold, or even for the map. I'm following you because the thing at the end of your map is the thing that ate my family, and I want to watch it burn, and if it can't burn I want to own it." She sat back. "That's the whole of my ledger. Read it however you like."

Jude read it. She thought of Ashfall Bridge, of the oath pressed into her mouth like a gag, of ten years of small gray days balancing other people's impossible debts while the sword-speech hummed under her skin like a caged bird. She had thought herself the war's orphan. Sitting across from Sefa Ilvane, she understood she had merely been its employee.

"My mentor made me swear silence," she heard herself say. "At Ashfall. In exchange for my life. I've hated the oath for ten years and obeyed it for ten years, and I told myself obedience was honor." She turned the mug in a slow circle. "But I think part of it was simpler. If I never spoke, I never had to learn what my gift cost other people. Your village paid for my training, Sefa. Downhill. In blood-wheat and hollowed uncles. I drank the beer and balanced the books and called myself innocent because I hadn't signed anything."

"Yes," Sefa said. "That's exactly what innocence looks like from downstream."

It should have been a wound. Instead it landed like a key turning, and Jude felt something in her chest unlock that had been locked so long she'd mistaken the lock for the shape of her heart.

"Thank you," she said.

Sefa blinked. For a moment the factor's armor slipped and something younger showed through—something that had stood in a starving village watching wrong-shadowed uncles, waiting for someone upstream to say those exact words. Then the armor came back down, but slower than usual, and she looked away first.

"Eat your bread," she said roughly. "It's getting wet."

* * *

They took a room with two beds and a window overlooking the yard, and Jude slept the way soldiers sleep—shallow, armed, surfacing at every sound. Once she woke to see Sefa sitting up in the dark, cross-legged, awake and silent, looking at the window as if answers were written on the rain. Their eyes met. Sefa lay back down. Neither spoke. There was a fragile intimacy in it, the intimacy of two women who had shown each other their worst accounts and found the other still willing to extend credit, and it frightened Jude more than the Hollow Men did, because things you fear can be fought, and things you need can only be lost.

Dusk came early under the rain.

Jude woke to a silence with a texture to it. The common room below had gone quiet—not empty-quiet, but held-breath quiet, the quiet of a room full of people all pretending not to listen. She was out of bed with her knife drawn before her mind fully arrived, and saw Sefa already at the window, motionless.

"Horses," Sefa breathed. "Four. No—five. They came in from the north road, slow, no lanterns."

Jude joined her at the glass. Below, in the yard, five riders dismounted in the failing light. They moved wrong—too smoothly, too identically, each man stepping down and standing and turning in the same rhythm, like figures in a music box. Rain ran off their hooded cloaks. None of them checked the horses properly. None of them spoke. When the stable boy approached, one of them simply looked at him, and the boy stopped as if he'd walked into a wall, and backed away, and ran.

"Hollow Men," Jude whispered. Her mouth had gone dry. "Five of them. Corvane must have pushed them hard to catch us this fast."

"They're not coming to the front door." Sefa's eyes tracked the yard. Four of the figures were moving toward the inn's rear wall, spreading out with terrible unhurried patience, cutting off the back gate, the drainage culvert, the stable lane. The fifth stood in the center of the yard, face lifted to the rain, and even through the warped glass Jude felt its attention pass over the building like a hand over a ledger, reading.

"They know we're here."

"They always knew. They're not tracking the map, Jude. They're tracking *you*. Corvane said retrieve the map and leave no witnesses, and the cheapest way to do both is to follow the witness." Sefa stepped back from the window, and when Jude turned, the factor was already pulling her boots on, her face pale but steady, her hands quick and sure. "Back stairs or roof?"

"How do you know there's a back stair?"

"Because there's always a back stair, and because I bribed the pot-girl twenty minutes after we arrived." Sefa slung her pack. "Old habits. Villages downstream learn to count the exits."

Below, in the yard, the fifth Hollow Man lowered his face from the rain and began, without hurry, to walk toward the inn's front door.

Jude's hand found the hilt of her plain soldier's sword—not a speaker's blade, just steel—and the sword-speech rose under her skin for the first time in days, humming against her ribs like something waking. She thought of the dead blades in the marshes. She thought of the oath. She thought of Sefa's grandmother in the second cart, and her own mentor's face, which she could no longer picture, and which had been spent buying her exactly this kind of night.

"Roof," she said. "And Sefa—if it comes to speaking, whatever you hear me say, don't repeat it. Words like mine stick."

"Noted," Sefa said, and opened the door onto the dark.
