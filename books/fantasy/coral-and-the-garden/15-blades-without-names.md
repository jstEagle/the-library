## 15. Blades Without Names

The first sign was the horses—or rather, the absence of them.

Jude had been awake for an hour before dawn, lying on the narrow pallet in the room she shared with Sefa at the Inn at Halfway, listening to the sounds a fortified inn made when it slept: the settling of old timber, the mutter of the peat fire in the common room below, the wind worrying the thatch. She knew the stable yard held six horses because she had counted them when they arrived, and she could hear each one breathing if she let herself listen the way she never let herself listen—the deep way, the way that reached past walls and skin and into the humming marrow of things.

Now there were only four breaths in the yard.

Two horses gone. And no creak of the gate to say they'd left.

She sat up in the dark. Across the room, Sefa was already awake—Jude could tell by the quality of her stillness, the difference between sleep and a body pretending at it. Sefa had been a war-child; she did not sleep so much as wait for morning with her eyes closed.

"They're here," Jude said quietly.

"I know." Sefa's voice came flat from the darkness. "The dogs stopped barking a quarter hour ago. Dogs don't stop barking. They get eaten or they get paid."

Jude rose and dressed without light, fingers finding buckles by memory. Her pack was already packed—it had been packed since the rainmarket, since the moment she'd understood that running would become her whole life for a while. But they couldn't run yet. The tidewalks were behind them, the Marches ahead, and between the inn and the marsh road lay a mile of open fen crossed by two causeways, both of them visible from the upper windows. In daylight you might cross unseen among the carts. In the grey hour before dawn, two women moving fast against the grain of the land would be as legible as ink on wet paper.

"We go out the back," Sefa said, "through the kitchen, over the midden wall, and take the drainage cut south. It's flooded to the thigh but it bends away from the causeways."

"You've been here before."

"I told you. My village fed the Garden's first harvest. Everything within a hundred miles of the Marches is a place I've been before, one way or another." A pause. "Hollow Men don't ride through the night unless Corvane wants something badly. He wants us badly."

"He wants the map."

"He wants what the map opens." Sefa stood, and even in the dark Jude felt the change in her, the factor's smoothness burning away to reveal something older and harder underneath. "Get your knife. Not for fighting. For cutting the map out of your coat if it comes to that. Better burned than read."

* * *

They came down the back stair into the smell of yesterday's bread and tallow. The kitchen was cold, its hearth raked to embers, and the innkeep's boy slept in a cot by the door with his mouth open. Jude stepped around him gently. Whatever happened next, she wanted no dead children on her ledger.

The midden wall was shoulder-high and slick with frost. Sefa went over it first, silent as spilled water, and Jude followed less gracefully, catching her palm on the coping and feeling the sting of a split callus. Below lay the drainage cut, a black ribbon of standing water steaming faintly where the fen's warmth met the night's cold. They waded in. The cold climbed Jude's body like a verdict—knees, thighs, waist—and she set her teeth against it and kept moving, one hand on the cut's stone lip, boots finding the silted bottom step by step.

Behind them, above them, the inn sat dark and unsuspecting. Jude thought of the innkeep's wife who had given them barley soup and not asked their names, and hoped the Hollow Men would be content to search and leave. The Hollow Men were not cruel, exactly. Cruelty required wanting something from you. They only required.

They were fifty yards along the cut when the door above the midden wall opened and lantern-light spilled down like something poured.

"Stop," said a voice that had no texture to it. No anger, no command's bite. Just the word, laid down like a coin on a counter. "The Guild requires the document. You are not required."

Four of them on the wall, black shapes against the lantern-glow, and two more dropping soundlessly into the cut behind—Jude heard the water part and knew the arithmetic was done. Six. She and Sefa had knives, a map, and forty yards of flooded ditch.

Sefa's hand found hers in the dark and squeezed once. *Run.*

They ran. Water exploded around their thighs, the cut bending south as promised, and behind them the Hollow Men came on with the terrible economy of things that do not tire, do not fear, do not hurry. Jude's lungs burned. The fen opened around them—black water, black reeds, a sky just beginning to bruise grey at the eastern edge—and somewhere in that greying east, miles off across the drowned country, she felt it again: the pull she had been refusing to feel since the salt door. The Garden, drawing her like a hook draws a fish.

A Hollow Man stepped out of the reeds ahead of them.

He had simply been standing there, in water to his knees, waiting, and now he raised a hand—not a weapon, just a hand, an open palm, the gesture of a toll-keeper—and said, "The Guild requires—"

Sefa hit him like a landslide.

Later, Jude would understand that everything Sefa had ever been was in that moment: the war-child, the factor who counted other people's weaknesses the way she counted coin, the woman who had watched her village be harvested and had decided, somewhere in the years after, that she would never again be the one who waited. She struck the Hollow Man at the knee joint with her shoulder and drove him backward into the water and was on top of him with her knife drawn before Jude had finished flinching.

But the others were closing. Jude turned and saw them coming through the reeds—four shapes, unhurried, faces pale ovals without expression, and she understood with a clarity that felt like falling that knives would not save them, that running would not save them, that the only thing in all the drowned world that could save them now was the thing she had spent ten years burying under ledgers and lies and the small gray virtue of being nobody.

Her oath said: *You will not speak the blade-tongue while you live, nor teach it, nor let it be known in you.*

Her oath had bought her life at Ashfall Bridge with her mentor's blood still cooling on the stones. Every quiet year at the Meridian Guild had been a payment on that debt. She had told herself the silence was the oath's spirit—that a gift unused was a gift unbroken, that hiding was a kind of keeping faith.

But the oath's letter said *never*, and the letter had always known this moment was coming. Oaths are written by people who have seen the future's weather.

The Hollow Man nearest her drew a short sword.

And Jude looked past him—to the thing she had seen on the march and refused to see, the thing every traveler through the Drowned Marches learned to look away from. Fifty yards off, where the drainage cut met the old military road, a petrified soldier stood in the shallows, mid-stride, mid-war, his body gone to gray stone ten years ago on the day the swordspeakers broke. His sword was still in his hand. Rusted, yes—but rust is only iron remembering it was ore, and iron remembers everything.

Jude spoke.

Not aloud, not entirely. The sword-speech was never wholly sound; it was sound the way a wave is water, the visible crest of something moving far beneath. She shaped the old words—the ones her mentor had drilled into her at Ashfall until they lived in her jaw like teeth—and aimed them at the dead man's blade, and said:

*Rise. Your watch is not ended. Rise and stand for me.*

The world answered.

It began as a hum in her own bones, the gift waking after ten years of feigned death like a limb full of pins and needles, painful and ecstatic at once. Then the petrified soldier's sword shuddered. Rust sloughed from it in red clouds that bloomed and unraveled in the black water. The blade brightened—not polished, but *remembered*, the metal recalling some older morning when it had been forged and named and loved—and the soldier's stone fingers, impossibly, creaked open.

The sword came free.

It flew. There was no gentler word for it. It crossed the fifty yards of fen in a shriek of displaced air and took the nearest Hollow Man's weapon from his hand—not cutting him, turning him, spinning him into the reeds with the flat—and then it hung in the air before Jude, dripping, trembling, singing a note too low for ears and too high for comfort.

The remaining Hollow Men stopped.

For the first time since she had known them, they hesitated. They had no names, and so perhaps they had no words for what they saw, but their bodies remembered what their minds had been surgically relieved of: everyone who had fought in the last decade of the war knew what a living blade in the air meant. It meant a swordspeaker. It meant the end of ordinary arithmetic.

"Go," Jude said to Sefa, and her voice came out strange to her, resonant, doubled, as if someone spoke with her. "South, along the road. I'm right behind you."

"Jude—"

"*Go.*"

Sefa went. Jude heard her crashing through the reeds and did not watch her go, because the sword had drifted to her side now, close enough to touch, and touching it was like putting her hand into a river of other people's memories. Its last wielder had been called Aldric. He had been afraid of water. He had written letters he never sent. He had died at Ashfall Bridge believing the swordspeakers would win, and his sword had held that belief for ten years like a coal holding heat, waiting.

*I know,* Jude told it silently. *I was there too.*

The Hollow Men came anyway. Of course they came. Fear required a self to feel it, and theirs had been taken out with the names. The first swung at her with his recovered short sword and her dead soldier's blade met it with a clang that rang across the fen like a bell in a burning town, and then it was not a duel but a lesson—the sword-speech pouring through her now, ten dammed-up years breaking all at once, words of deflection and binding and *fall* shaping themselves faster than thought. She drove the second Hollow Man into the water. She bound the third's ankles in a loop of force older than any guild. The fourth she disarmed and the fourth's sword joined hers in the air, and for one vertiginous moment Jude Winterbourne stood in a drowning fen with two blades wheeling around her like gulls and power running through her veins like spring flood, and she felt—

She felt *wonderful*.

That was the guilt of it. Not the broken oath, not yet—the oath's reckoning would come later, quietly, in the shape of everything that followed. What gutted her, in the moment, was how good it was. Ten years of pretending to be a woman who balanced accounts, and the truth was that she had been built for this, grown for it, cultivated like a crop in a garden she was only beginning to imagine, and every cell of her sang *yes* as the blades danced. She laughed. She actually laughed, there in the killing ground, and the sound frightened her more than the Hollow Men had.

Then one of them, the one Sefa had put down in the reeds, rose behind her with a knife.

The hound-sword—she would think of it that way later, the way you think of a dog that chose you—struck without her calling it. It came around in a flat arc and took the knife and two of the man's fingers, and he folded into the water without a sound, and the suddenness of it, the *eagerness* of it, killed Jude's laughter in her throat.

*Enough,* she told the blades. *Enough. Down.*

They came down. Both of them, hovering to rest in the shallows like tired birds, and the hum faded, and the fen was suddenly very quiet, and Jude stood panting in water to her waist with frost forming on her sleeves and four Hollow Men groaning or bleeding or simply sitting in the reeds, defeated, nameless, looking at her with faces that could not wonder but whose bodies wondered anyway.

One of them spoke. The one whose sword she had taken first. He held his ruined hand against his chest and looked at the dead soldier's blade floating at Jude's hip, and he said, in that textureless voice, with something almost like the ghost of a question in it:

"That sword knows you."

"It doesn't," Jude said.

But even as she said it, the blade drifted closer to her, nudging her elbow through the wet wool of her coat, the way a horse noses the hand that fed it. And she felt what it carried now that the battle-hum had faded: not just Aldric's memories but a new thread, fresh-spun, spun in the last five minutes. *Her.* The fen, the frost, the doubled voice saying *rise*. The shape of her jaw as she spoke the words. The taste of her name in the old tongue—Winterbourne, winter-born, spoken over steel.

The sword had been made by a smith, owned by a soldier, petrified by a war. Now it had been spoken awake by a swordspeaker, and in the grammar of blades that made her its maker, and what a blade's maker says, the blade keeps.

"It does," the Hollow Man said, without inflection, and somehow it was the most terrible thing anyone had said to her in ten years.

* * *

She caught up with Sefa a mile south, where the drainage cut gave out onto the old military road and the first true light of morning was coming up over the Drowned Marches, gilding the thousands of petrified soldiers who stood in the marshes on either side, mid-swing, forever. Sefa was soaked to the ribs and bleeding from a graze along her forearm she hadn't mentioned and probably wouldn't. She looked at Jude—at the sword riding at her hip without a scabbard, hovering a half-inch clear of her coat, faithful as a shadow—and Jude watched her arrive at the understanding she had already arrived at in the reeds.

"You're one of them," Sefa said. "A swordspeaker. You've been one the whole time."

"Yes."

"At the Guild. Balancing ledgers. With *that* under your skin."

"With that under my skin, yes." Jude's voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "I swore an oath. There was a bridge called Ashfall, and an order that died there, and I was the last one off it, and the price of walking away was never speaking again. So I didn't speak. For ten years I didn't speak."

"And today?"

Today. Jude looked down at the sword. In the growing light she could see the rust returning slowly to it, the old defeat creeping back in at the edges, but it pressed itself against her hip like something seeking warmth, and she could not make herself push it away.

"Today men without names came to kill us for a map," she said, "and I decided the oath's letter could break before we did." She started walking south, toward the Garden, toward whatever was waiting there, and the sword floated up and followed at her shoulder like a hound that has forgiven the long day's hunger. "Come on. We've lost the night. Corvane will have riders on the marsh road by noon."

Sefa fell into step beside her, and for a long time neither of them spoke, which was its own kind of honesty. Then, without looking over:

"The sword," Sefa said. "It's watching you."

"I know."

"No—I mean it's *watching* you. The way a dog watches. The way something watches the thing it belongs to." She paused. "What happens, Jude, when a thing like that decides it belongs to you? What happens when it wants something?"

Jude had no answer. The sword hummed softly at her shoulder, carrying a dead man's memories and a living woman's voice, and ahead of them the Marches opened wide and drowned and whispering, and somewhere in their drowned heart a garden of thorns was waiting to learn her name.
