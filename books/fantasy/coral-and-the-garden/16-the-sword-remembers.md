## 16. The Sword Remembers

It was waiting for her when she woke.

Jude knew it before she opened her eyes—knew it the way one knows a hand resting on the blanket beside her, the way she had once known her mentor's breathing in the next bedroll at Ashfall. A hum, low and patient, sitting just at the edge of hearing. Not the wild shriek of the Hollow Men's fight, not the hungry chorus of the Marches' rusted dead. Something quieter. Something that had *followed*.

She sat up in the grey light of the abandoned watchtower where they'd made camp, and there it was: the soldier's sword, driven point-down into the packed earth three feet from her bedroll, standing perfectly upright in soil that should have refused it.

"You're supposed to be rust," she told it.

The hum deepened, almost pleased.

Behind her, Sefa stirred. "Is that—" She stopped, propped herself on one elbow, and stared at the blade with the flat, assessing look Jude was beginning to recognize as the factor's version of fear. "That's the sword you raised at the inn."

"It was supposed to stay raised long enough to kill four men with no names." Jude rose, joints stiff from sleeping on stone, and circled the thing warily. Up close it looked worse than it had in the torchlight—the blade pitted, the guard wrapped in leather gone to black lace, a maker's mark worn nearly smooth. But the steel itself had changed. Where she had spoken life into it, the metal had brightened, as if some inner fire had been coaxed back into iron that had spent forty years learning how to be dirt.

"And now?" Sefa asked.

"Now it won't leave."

*Won't leave.* The words came out of her mouth and she heard how they sounded—not like a description of an object but of a creature. Of a dog that had chosen its person. She reached out, hesitated, then laid two fingers against the flat of the blade.

The whisper came immediately, the way a cat presses into a hand.

Not words. Not yet. Fragments. Rain on a bridge. A woman laughing with her mouth full of bread. The smell of cedar oil and hot metal. A name, spoken over and over in training yards until it wore grooves into a young mind: *Vessa. Vessa. Vessa.*

Jude snatched her fingers back.

"What did it show you?" Sefa had come to stand beside her, arms crossed, close enough to be warm and far enough to be strategic. Jude had noticed that about her by now—Sefa measured every distance like a merchant measuring cloth, and never gave an inch she couldn't account for.

"Memories." Jude flexed her fingers, trying to shake off the residue of someone else's rain. "Its last wielder's. A woman named Vessa."

"The petrified soldiers in the Marches were men, mostly. I saw them."

"This one wasn't in the Marches. It was carried out. Carried somewhere it could be dropped—at Halfway, maybe, or on the road—and forgotten." Jude looked at the sword again, and the looking hurt in a way she hadn't anticipated. Because she had done this. She had spoken a dead thing awake, and whatever it was now, it was partly *hers*, the way a child is partly its parents' and entirely its own. "When I broke my oath at the inn, I didn't just wake the steel. I woke everything the steel remembered."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. In the tower's broken window behind her, the sky was going from ash-grey to the pale gold that meant the Drowned Marches would soon be steaming, and their day of wading would begin. "Does it talk?"

"Not in words. Not properly. It shows me things. And sometimes—" Jude pressed her lips together. She had decided something in the night, lying awake listening to the hum, and deciding to say it aloud was harder than she'd expected. "Sometimes it answers questions. Like a hound that understands more than it should."

"A hound." Sefa's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "You've made yourself a dog out of a dead woman's sword, Winterbourne. The Guild would pay a fortune for that trick. Corvane would burn a city for it."

"I know." That was precisely what frightened her.

* * *

They walked north through the morning, following the map's inked road toward Thornwall, and the sword came with them.

It did not walk—it had no legs—but it moved. When they broke camp it pulled itself free of the earth with a soft grinding sigh and drifted after Jude at knee height, point trailing, close enough that its shadow kept pace with hers. When she stopped, it stopped. When she turned, it turned. Twice it darted ahead around a bend in the drowned road, and twice it returned, having found nothing worth reporting except, apparently, the pleasure of scouting ahead for her.

Sefa watched all of this with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to.

"Can other people see it?" she asked, low, as they waded a stretch where the road had sunk to mid-calf depth and the water lay brown and still as old tea.

"Anyone can see it. But it only listens to me." Jude lifted her hem over the waterline out of habit rather than hope; her boots had been wet since the Tidewalks. "Which is the problem. If Corvane's people catch sight of a floating sword, they'll know exactly what they're chasing."

"They already know what they're chasing. They've known since the courier died." Sefa said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "The question is whether they know you can do *this*. There's a difference between hunting a thief carrying a map and hunting the last swordspeaker alive."

"There are no last swordspeakers alive. That's the entire point of me."

"Mm." Sefa stepped over a submerged fence post furred with green weed. "And yet."

Jude let it go. Arguing with Sefa Ilvane about what she was felt like arguing with a ledger about what it contained. The numbers were the numbers.

But the sword had opinions about the silence. As they climbed out of the flooded stretch onto higher ground—a levee road crowned with wind-bent alders—the hum against her hip sharpened, insistent, and the fragments came again, unbidden:

*A forge under a hill. Bellows breathing like lungs. Vessa's hands, scarred across both palms, wrapping a new grip in leather while an old man says: a sword is a promise you make to strangers. Vessa laughing. Vessa afraid. Vessa running through snow toward a bridge that is already burning—*

Jude stumbled.

Sefa caught her elbow—actually caught it, grip firm, no calculation in it for once—and held her upright until the vision passed. "What is it? What does it want?"

"It doesn't want anything. It's just—" Jude steadied herself against an alder trunk, breathing. "It remembers dying. Not its own. Hers. Vessa died at Ashfall Bridge. This sword was there when the order fell. And now it's walking next to the only other person who was there and lived, and it keeps—" Her voice caught on something sharp. "It keeps showing me her hands. Over and over. Like it thinks if it shows me enough times I'll be able to tell it why."

Sefa released her elbow slowly. For a long moment the only sound was water dripping from their hems and the wind combing the alder leaves.

"My brother used to follow me like that," Sefa said at last. "After our village burned. Not because he understood what had happened. Because I was the shape his grief fit inside." She started walking again. "I'm telling you this so you understand I know what I'm looking at. That thing isn't loyal to you, Winterbourne. It's loyal to what you remind it of. That's worse, and it's also better, depending on the day."

Jude watched the sword drift ahead through the alder shade, patient as a held breath, and thought about the oath she had sworn at seventeen with smoke in her mouth: *never speak, never teach, never name yourself, and live.* She had thought the oath was a wall. Now she understood it was a door she'd bricked over with herself on the wrong side.

And the sword—her sword, God help her, the one she'd made—had come along and started pulling bricks loose one at a time, showing her each one. *Here is a forge. Here are scarred hands. Here is the bridge. Here is what you buried.*

Being known by something she had made was not at all like being known by a person. A person might choose to stop knowing you. The sword simply knew, the way iron knows fire, and waited to see what she would become.

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a tollhouse, its roof half fallen, its toll-board still hanging by one chain and swinging even in windless air, creaking out the memory of a price nobody paid anymore. Sefa took first watch without being asked, settling into the doorway with her knife drawn across her knees, watching the dark road south for Hollow Men.

Jude sat cross-legged by the cold hearth with the sword laid across her lap, and for the first time since raising it, she touched it deliberately.

The flood came gentler this time, as if the blade had learned her limits. Images unspooled slow and deliberate, offered rather than forced:

*A cache. Underground. Vessa younger, hair braided tight for travel, descending stone steps cut into a hillside behind a waterfall gone dry. A door of black oak banded in bronze, and on the door, a sign in the old speech—the speech Jude had been born to and sworn away—glowing faintly like banked coals. Vessa pressing her palm to it. The door opening on a chamber lined with racks, and on the racks, swords. Dozens. Hundreds. Sleeping blades in oiled linen, humming faintly, all of them, like a choir holding one note forever.*

*Vessa's voice, speaking to someone behind her—a girl's voice answering, young, frightened:* "Why are we hiding them? Why don't we use them?"

*Vessa's answer, tired beyond her years:* "Because a weapon you hide is a secret. A weapon you use is a war. We hid them so there would never have to be another war. Promise me you'll remember where they sleep."

*And the girl—Jude knew, with a certainty that dropped through her stomach like a stone through water, knew the way you know your own reflection—that girl was her. Ten years old. Before Ashfall. Before the oath. Before everything.*

The vision closed. Jude sat shaking in the ruined tollhouse with a dead woman's sword in her lap and a location burning behind her eyes: the dry waterfall, the hillside, the black door. She could feel the knowledge settling into her like a splinter working toward the bone. The cache was real. She had *seen* it—had stood in it, in Vessa's memory, small and ten years old and asking the right question and getting the only honest answer anyone ever gave her.

A weapon you hide is a secret. A weapon you use is a war.

And now she was walking toward the Garden with a living sword at her knee and a hidden armory mapped in her blood, and Corvane wanted the Garden, and the Guild wanted the Garden, and somewhere behind them four nameless men had died and more would come, and every road led back to the same fork: hide the weapons, or use them.

"You've gone white," Sefa said from the doorway, not turning around. "What did it show you?"

Jude considered lying. It would have been easy—she'd gotten good at it, these past weeks, good enough that Master Corvane himself had almost believed her. But the sword hummed against her thighs, warm as a sleeping animal, and it had shown her its whole heart without being asked, and lying to Sefa while touching it felt like a kind of theft.

"It showed me where the order hid its swords," Jude said. "All of them. Hundreds of blades, sleeping. Enough to arm a war."

Sefa turned around then. In the starlight her face was unreadable, but her voice, when it came, was very quiet. "And it showed you this *now*. Two days before Thornwall. One day, probably, before Corvane reaches the Garden." She paused. "Winterbourne. Do you think that's a coincidence?"

"No." Jude looked down at the blade. The maker's mark caught the starlight—she saw it clearly for the first time, and her breath went out of her. Not a forge-mark. A garden-mark: a rose of thorns, tiny, stamped into the tang. The same sigil the map had bloomed into ink. "This sword wasn't made in any forge. It was grown here. In the Garden. Which means Vessa brought it *out* of the Garden—which means Vessa knew the Garden, knew the cache, knew the hiding places—and she put all of it into the steel before she died at Ashfall."

"So the sword isn't following you because you raised it."

"No." Jude's mouth was dry. "It's following me because it was always meant to find its way home. And I'm the homecoming it chose."

Outside, far to the south, too far to be thunder, something answered: a single deep note, like a bell struck underwater, rolling up through the ground and into the soles of Jude's boots. The sword leapt from her lap and hovered at her shoulder, point aimed south, humming hard.

Every blade in the world, Corvane had said once, in a ledger-note she'd been meant to file and forget. Every blade remembers its root.

The Garden was calling its children home. And it had just learned that one of them was already on the road.
