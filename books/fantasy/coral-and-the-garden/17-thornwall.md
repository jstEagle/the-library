## 17. Thornwall

Sefa Ilvane had crossed borders before—smuggled through customs at Meridian's salt-gates with contraband stitched into her coat lining, walked the demilitarized causeways of the Ashfall provinces with forged papers and a smile that made clerks forget to look. Borders, in her experience, were just doors that hadn't yet learned what they were worth. You found the price. You paid it. You passed.

Thornwall was not a border. Thornwall was a question, and Sefa suspected it did not accept payment.

They had seen it since midmorning: a dark green line drawn across the horizon of the Drowned Marches, so perfectly straight it looked ruled onto the world by a careful hand. As they approached, the line grew into a wall—a hedge, if the word could stretch that far. Brambles forty feet tall, woven dense as masonry, their thorns long as knives and pale as bone. It ran farther than Sefa could see in either direction, vanishing east and west into the marsh haze. No gate broke its face. No road tunneled beneath. Yet the map in Jude's satchel showed the route passing straight through, as though whoever had drawn it had never doubted the wall would let them by.

The reanimated sword rode at Jude's hip like a hound at heel, humming faintly whenever the wind shifted. Sefa still wasn't used to it. She'd grown up around weapons; she knew how a sword should behave, which was to say: not at all. This one watched things. She'd caught it more than once angling its blade toward her when she stood behind Jude, and she chose—deliberately, the way she chose most things—not to take offense.

"Tell me again what we know about this," she said, stopping an arm's length from the nearest bramble.

"Nothing," Jude said. "That's what we know."

"Wonderful. I do love walking into the unknown unarmed and underprovisioned."

"You're armed." Jude nodded at the knife at Sefa's belt.

"It's a fruit knife."

"It's held your life together for thirty years," Jude said. "I've seen you use it on rope, on bread, on a man in Halfway who looked at you wrong."

"He was looking at *you* wrong," Sefa said. "And he apologized. That doesn't count."

Jude almost smiled. It was a small thing, the way Jude's smiles always were—a crack of light under a door quickly shut—but Sefa had learned to catch them, the way she'd once learned to catch the flicker in a debtor's eye that meant he was about to lie about his assets. Ten days in the Marches, sleeping in ruined ferry-stations and eating smoked eel and hardtack, and she had catalogued Jude Winterbourne's tells like entries in a ledger. The near-smile meant trust. The silence before answering a direct question meant memory. The way her left hand drifted toward her hip when something hummed meant the old gift was awake and unhappy about it.

Sefa had built her whole life on knowing what people wanted before they said it. It was how you survived a war childhood: read the soldiers' hunger before they reached for it, read the factor's boredom before he burned your village's petition, read the room, always, faster than the room could turn on you.

Which was why Thornwall unsettled her more than she let show. Because she couldn't read it. The hedge had no face, no mood, no want she could find purchase on. It simply waited, green and patient, older than patience.

* * *

The brambles moved first.

It was subtle—the way a change in current is subtle until you're drowning. One moment the wall stood inert; the next, a passage had opened where there hadn't been one, thorns curling back from a corridor barely wide enough for two, walled in living green so high it swallowed the sky. The air that breathed out of it smelled of loam and roses and something underneath, something older and sweeter that Sefa's hindbrain flagged as *wrong* the way a ledger balances too neatly—too clean to be true.

"It wants us to go in," Jude said.

"Obviously."

"That should worry us more than it does."

"I'm worried plenty," Sefa said. "I'm just also cold, tired, and out of roads. Whatever's behind this wall is better than standing in front of it arguing." She drew her fruit knife anyway—an old habit, mostly for comfort—and stepped past Jude into the green.

The corridor closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting.

Inside, the light changed. Not darker, exactly—*filtered*, as if the sun had been asked to speak more quietly. The bramble walls pressed close but did not touch them, and here and there among the thorns hung small white flowers, open like watching eyes. The path sloped gently downward, then up, winding without any logic Sefa could map, and after a hundred paces she realized she no longer knew which direction was north, or whether north still mattered in here.

The sword at Jude's hip had gone silent. Completely silent. Even its hum had stopped, as though it were holding its breath.

Then the flowers began to whisper.

Not words. Not yet. Just a sound like pages turning somewhere deep in the hedge, like a great many voices rehearsing a single sentence they hadn't decided on. Sefa felt it against her skin, a pressure like the moment before a storm breaks, and she understood—with the flat certainty of a woman who has survived things she shouldn't have—that the wall was reading them.

"Don't fight it," Jude said quietly. She had stopped walking. Her eyes had gone distant, unfocused, fixed on some middle distance that wasn't there. "Whatever it shows you—it's not real. It's a test. Let it look, and keep walking."

"How do you know?"

"Because the Garden grew this wall, and the Garden was built on wanting." Jude's voice had a strange evenness, the tone of someone reciting a lesson learned painfully. "Everything here runs on desire. It's the water. It's the soil. Of course the door asks what you thirst for before it lets you drink."

"And if I don't answer?"

The whispering rose, soft and amused, like wind through ten thousand leaves. Sefa had the distinct impression of being laughed at, gently, by something very old.

"You'll answer," Jude said. "Everyone answers."

* * *

It took Sefa in the third turning.

There was no transition—that was the frightening part. One step she was walking beside Jude through the green corridor; the next, the corridor was gone and she was standing on a balcony.

She knew the balcony. That was the obscene thing about it. She knew the iron railing worn smooth on the inner curve where a hand rested, knew the view of the Meridian harbor spread below in morning gold, knew the sound of the counting-bell rolling up from the Guild quarter. She had stood on that balcony exactly once, nine years ago, delivering documents to Master Aldous Vane, and she had allowed herself—one breath, no more—to imagine the view belonging to her.

In the vision, it did.

Below her, the Guild's courtyard was full. Not clerks and factors—crowds. Hundreds of them, thousands, gathered in the gray light of dawn to watch the changing of power. She could hear the murmur roll up to her like tide. And when the doors of the great hall opened and the council emerged, they were not coming to meet her. They were coming to *bow*. Old Corvane's silver tongue was silent forever—she saw that in the way the masters flanked empty air where he should have been—and the woman they turned to, the woman whose name the crowd was learning to pronounce, wore the auditor's chain of office over a plain merchant's coat, because she had never needed finery to be obeyed.

*Sefa Ilvane,* the crowd murmured, and the sound of her own name in a thousand mouths was warm as wine.

She looked down at her hands in the vision and they were steady. That was the detail that undid her. In every dream she'd ever permitted herself, her hands shook at moments like these. Here they didn't. Here she had wanted nothing for so long that wanting had finally died of starvation, and what remained was pure, uncluttered command. She signed something—she couldn't see what, only feel the weight of the seal in her grip—and the crowd roared, and the city beyond the harbor walls glittered with a prosperity she had authored, and somewhere in the vision's deep background, so faint she might have imagined it, a village that had burned when she was eight years old stood rebuilt, whitewashed, whole, its fields green, its people alive and calling her benefactor, calling her—

"Sefa."

Jude's voice. Thin, as if through cloth.

"Sefa. Walk. Don't answer it."

But the vision wasn't finished. It leaned closer, intimate now, showing her the private hours of that imagined reign: the ledgers balanced to the last copper, honest as glass; the Hollow Men disbanded and given back their names; the archives opened; the war dead counted, named, mourned publicly, properly, for the first time. A world put right by a woman who knew exactly what power cost because she had paid for hers in blood and memory and a brother's face surrendered to a ferryman.

And that was the trap's true tooth, Sefa realized dimly, watching herself be wise and good and absolute. The vision wasn't offering her tyranny. It was offering her *justice*—her justice, calibrated to the milligram, administered by someone who would never abuse it because she had already been every kind of victim. All she had to do was stay. All she had to do was stop walking. All she had to do was want it enough to stand still on a balcony that didn't exist and rule a crowd made of thorn-flowers.

Her feet were very still. When had her feet become still?

"*Walk,*" Jude said, sharper now, and something in the vision flickered—annoyed, almost, at the interruption—and Sefa felt the balcony begin to thin like smoke deciding whether to be a dream or a fire.

She thought of her brother. Not the memory she'd paid the ferryman—those were gone, scooped out clean, leaving only the shape of an absence. She thought instead of the fact of him, the bare truth she'd kept when everything else went: that she had loved someone once, wholly, before the world taught her arithmetic. The vision offered to rebuild even that, she realized. Given time, it would grow her a new brother from bramble and wish, smiling and hollow, and she would rule beside a puppet made of her own grief.

"No," Sefa said aloud, to the wall, to herself. "I don't take loans from creditors I can't see."

And she lifted her foot and walked.

* * *

The balcony tore away like wet paper.

She came back to herself mid-stride in the green corridor, heart slamming, hand white-knuckled on the fruit knife, and Jude was gripping her elbow—when had Jude taken her elbow?—with both hands, steering her forward.

"There," Jude said. "Keep going. It lets go slowly. Don't look at the flowers."

"What did it give you?" Sefa managed. Her voice sounded scraped.

Jude was quiet for four steps. Five. Six.

"A ledger," she said at last.

"A ledger."

"An empty one. Clean pages. A desk by a window with the sea in it." Jude's jaw worked, as if the words tasted odd. "No seal. No order. No oath. No name on the cover, even. Just—" she exhaled— "quiet. Balance. Nothing owed to anyone, nothing owing. I sat there in the vision for what felt like years and nobody came to the desk. Nobody wanted anything from me. I just... added sums that came out even, and the light moved across the floor, and that was all."

Sefa stared at her. "That's your deepest want? Peace and arithmetic?"

"Is that funny?"

"It's the saddest thing I've ever heard," Sefa said, "and I once heard a man auction his own shadow."

Jude's mouth twitched—that near-smile again, briefer than usual. "What did it offer you?"

"The world," Sefa said. "On a plate, with my seal on it."

"And?"

"And I declined the loan." She rolled her shoulders, working the last of the vision's honey out of her muscles. "Though I'll confess it was well-drawn. Whoever tends this hedge knows collateral when it sees it. It didn't offer me gold or thrones. It offered me the version of me I'd have been if the war hadn't happened. That's the only currency I can't counterfeit."

Ahead, the corridor brightened. The whispering in the leaves had changed character—no longer hungry, no longer curious. Satisfied. A toll collected, entered, filed away somewhere in the green dark. The brambles ahead began to draw apart, thorn by slow thorn, opening like hands.

They stepped through together into grey afternoon light and the smell of open marsh, and behind them Thornwall sealed itself seamless, as if no passage had ever existed, as if two women had not just been weighed inside it and found—barely, conditionally—acceptable.

Sefa turned back once. The wall stood silent, flowers closing one by one like eyes returning to sleep.

"It let us through," she said. "That worries me."

"Why?"

"Because I've spent my life among institutions," Sefa said, "and the dangerous ones are never the ones that bar the door. They're the ones that check your papers, smile, and wave you toward whatever's waiting." She nodded down the slope, where the marsh fell away into a long green valley that no map of the Marches had ever shown her—a valley terraced in ancient stone, overgrown, vast, and unmistakably shaped by intention.

At its heart, half-lost in the haze, something enormous caught the low sun.

Jude's hand had gone to her hip. The sword was humming again—low, urgent, and Sefa could have sworn, watching the blade tremble in its sheath, that it was not warning them.

It was greeting the Garden like a dog greeting home.

"They're expecting us," Jude said quietly.

"Yes," Sefa agreed, and started down the slope. "Let's try very hard not to be what they're expecting."
