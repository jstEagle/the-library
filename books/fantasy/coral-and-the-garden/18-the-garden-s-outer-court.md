## 18. The Garden's Outer Court

They smelled it before they saw it.

For three days the Drowned Marches had given them nothing but rot and rust—the sweet-rot of drowned peat, the iron stink of petrified soldiers standing in the shallows with their swords half-swung, frozen in the last second of a war that had ended without them. Jude had learned to breathe through her mouth. She had learned to sleep with the hound-sword wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, because it whispered otherwise, and its whispers had grown less like memories and more like longing.

But on the morning of the fourth day, the marsh changed its mind.

The water cleared. Not clean—nothing in the Marches was clean—but *clearer*, as if the land itself were tidying up for company. Reeds gave way to rushes, rushes to something Jude had no name for: pale grass that grew in perfect concentric rings, each blade the same height, each ring a hand-span wider than the last. It looked mowed. It looked *tended*. And nothing in a drowned country tended anything.

Sefa stopped at the edge of the first ring and would not step onto it.

"That's not wild," she said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when she was doing sums she didn't like. "Wild doesn't grow in circles."

"No," Jude said. "It doesn't."

The hound-sword, still bound and buried, hummed against her hip. She felt it through the leather, through the oilcloth, through the layers of denial she had spent ten years weaving between herself and that particular sensation—a low, resonant *yes*, like a tuning fork struck against stone. The sword knew where it was. Whatever this place was, the sword had been made with knowledge of it, or by it, or for it, and the difference no longer seemed to matter.

They walked the rings inward for most of the morning. The ground rose by degrees, so gradually that Jude didn't notice the water leaving them until she looked back and saw the Marches spread out below like a gray-green sea, petrified soldiers scattered across it the way flotsam scatters after a wreck. The air warmed. The light changed—softer, gold-filtered, as though it were passing through leaves that weren't there yet.

Then the hedge began.

* * *

It rose out of the earth without transition, which was the first impossible thing about it. One moment there was grass; the next there was a wall of green forty feet high, dense as masonry, running in a long unbroken curve to the left and right until both ends vanished into haze. It was not Thornwall—that had been a week behind them, a snarling, grasping thing that tested them with visions and had let them pass only because it liked what it saw in them, or perhaps because it despised it. This was different. This hedge was *combed*. Its face was flat as a planed board. Somewhere in its depths birds were singing in patterns too regular to be natural, four notes, four notes, four notes, like a bell counting hours.

"Someone keeps this," Sefa said quietly.

"Someone kept this," Jude corrected. "Ten years. Maybe longer. The war ended and the world forgot this place existed." She touched the hedge with two fingers. It was warm. Growing things in the Marches were cold, always, drinking from chilled water—but this was warm as skin. "I don't think anyone's kept it. I think it keeps itself."

She heard Sefa swallow.

There was a gate. Of course there was a gate—it announced itself the way everything here did, with a terrible courtesy, an archway cut into the living hedge and framed in white wood gone silver with age. No door. Just the arch, and beyond it a tunnel of green, and beyond *that*, faintly, a scent that stopped Jude's breath.

Roses.

Not the drowned-rot smell of the Marches. Not the iron and peat. Roses—old roses, heavy and dark, the kind that grew outside her grandmother's house in a village that no longer appeared on maps, the kind that meant *someone lives here, someone loves this soil*. The scent reached through the arch like a hand extended in greeting, and every instinct Jude had honed across a decade of ledgers told her the same thing her grandmother's kitchen had taught her when she was small enough to hide under the table:

Never walk into a room just because it smells welcome. Find out who baked the bread.

But they had come four hundred miles for this. They had paid a ferryman Jude's memory of her mentor's face. They had fought rusted swords awake and run from Hollow Men and slept in ditches, and the map pressed into her hands by a dying courier had one destination on it, drawn in ink that breathed, marked by a rose of thorns. There was no version of this story in which she turned around at the door.

Jude went first. It was her map. It was her name the courier had died gasping. And if something inside wanted a swordspeaker—if the humming under her skin, quiet for ten years, had been leading her here the whole time like a fishhook set deep—then it should be her the hook caught.

* * *

The outer court opened around them all at once, the way a cathedral opens when you step through the wrong small door expecting a closet.

Terraces. Seven of them, Jude counted, rising in broad shallow steps toward a hill that must have been the spine of some drowned ridge, each terrace wide enough to hold a market square, each edged in that same combed hedge, each planted—she could not think how else to describe it—with rows. Orchards gone feral but feral *in formation*, trees that had once been pruned into shapes and now sprawled, their escaped branches still reaching along the old lines like rivers remembering their banks. Beds of herbs run to seed. Trellises collapsed into green heaps. A fountain at the center of the lowest terrace, dry for decades, its basin cracked, its central figure a woman holding up her hands—and from the woman's open palms, water had once poured upward. Jude stood looking at that for a long time. Water that rises. Speech that makes steel listen. The same trick, done twice.

"It's huge," Sefa said, and her voice echoed slightly, which it should not have done in open air. "Bigger than the Guild hall. Bigger than the war-camp at Ashfall. Who *built* this?"

"Gardeners," Jude said. "That's the word the inscription used. I grew power; it grew me." She had carried those words since the crypt, since the Gardener's grave, since the upright coffin and the final command carved into the stone: *Burn what I planted.* "This wasn't built, Sefa. It was cultivated. You don't build swordspeech. You grow it. Row by row. Student by student. That's what this place was—a field where people were the crop."

Sefa was silent for a moment. Then, very softly: "My village was a field too."

Jude didn't answer. There was nothing to answer with. The Garden had fed on villages the way its orchards fed on rain, and Sefa had been born in the furrows of that harvest, and no apology Jude could speak would reach back twenty years and unmake it. She only walked beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched now and then, and let the silence do what words couldn't.

They climbed the first terrace. That was when Jude noticed the topiary.

* * *

At intervals along the hedge-lines, spaced with a regularity that made her spine prickle, stood figures cut from yew. Ten feet tall. Fifteen. Green and dense and patient. At a distance, in the flat gold light, they might have been sculpture—herons, hounds, helmed soldiers, abstract spirals, the usual vocabulary of a great estate's grounds. Jude had seen topiary before, in the gardens of wealthy merchants whose accounts she audited, and she knew what it was: plant shaped by human patience into human whimsy.

These were not whimsy.

As they climbed, the figures resolved. A heron became a sentry, its neck a spear-shaft, one leg raised mid-stride. A spiral became a coiled serpent with a mouth of bare white wood, and the mouth was open. A soldier became a soldier—helm, cuirass, a great two-handed blade rendered in living wood held point-down before it in the old guard stance, the stance Jude's own mentor had drilled into her before she was old enough to hold a real sword. Point-down was the peace posture. Point-down meant *I will not draw unless commanded.*

"They're bound," Jude said. Her mouth had gone dry. "Old commands. The gardeners spoke them into the yew the way we spoke into steel, and the yew is still listening. Still obeying. Nobody ever came here to release them."

"From what?"

"From guarding."

Sefa stopped walking. Jude watched her look down the terraces—at thirty figures, fifty, more than either of them could count, spread across seven levels of a hillside, every one of them holding a posture of watchfulness, every one of them aimed, she realized with a slow cold turn of her stomach, *inward*. Not facing the approaches. Not facing the Marches or the road or any enemy that might come from outside.

Facing the center. Facing whatever lay at the heart of the Garden, up the hill, past the seventh terrace.

They weren't guards against intrusion. They were guards against *emergence*.

"Sefa," Jude said carefully. "What does it mean when you post your soldiers facing in?"

Sefa's jaw worked. She was a factor; she thought in flows and balances, in what moved where and who profited. Jude watched her arrive at the answer the way one watches someone count coins and find the purse short.

"It means the thing they're containing was worth more than anything outside," Sefa said. "Or more dangerous than anything outside." A pause. "Or both."

"Yes."

"And we're walking toward it."

"We've been walking toward it since a courier bled out against the salt door," Jude said. "Since before that. Since Ashfall Bridge, maybe. Since the oath." She flexed her right hand, the one that remembered shapes of speech her mouth had been forbidden to make for a decade. Under her skin, faint as a pulse in a sleeping limb, the gift hummed. It had been humming since the rings of grass. It was humming *louder* here, and the hum had a direction. It pulled uphill. "The map wants me to go up. My—" she almost said *the gift*, then couldn't lie even to herself in this place— "*I* want to go up. Both of those facts frighten me equally."

* * *

They crossed the dry fountain's terrace, and that was where the golems woke.

Jude felt it before she saw it—a pressure change, a shift in the quality of the silence, the way a room feels when someone enters it behind you. She turned. Fifty paces back, at the head of the stair they'd just climbed, the soldier-topiary had moved.

It stood differently now. Before, it had been a shape; now it was a *stance*, weight settled, blade still point-down but gripped, the yew fingers visibly—impossibly—wrapped around the wooden hilt. Its helm had no eyes, only a shadowed slot, and in the slot Jude sensed attention the way you sense a cat watching you from a dark shelf: not seen, but absolutely felt.

Then, from somewhere in the hedge behind it, a voice.

It was not a voice like a person's. It was a voice like wind through a keyhole, like the singing of the hedge-birds slowed down and given grammar, and it spoke in the old cadence—the cadence of command, the speech-form Jude had learned at nine years old and sworn away at nineteen. Her body knew it before her mind translated it. Her spine straightened involuntarily. Her hand twitched toward a sword she wasn't carrying.

*"Who walks the outer court?"*

Jude froze. Every instinct of concealment, every skill of a decade spent being nobody, screamed at her to say *travelers, lost pilgrims, nothing, no one.* But the question hadn't been asked in the common tongue. It had been asked in swordspeech, and swordspeech did not accept evasion. Swordspeech demanded true answers the way scales demanded true weights. She had spent her life balancing other people's false accounts; she would not balance her own here, not with the old law listening.

She answered in kind. Her voice came out rusty, cracked, half a whisper, and it hurt her throat like drawing a blade after years in a sheath—but it came.

"Jude Winterbourne. Last sworn of the order. Come under a dead courier's seal, seeking the heart of the Garden."

The silence afterward lasted eleven heartbeats. She counted. She couldn't help counting; she was an auditor even now, even terrified, even here.

Then the soldier-golem inclined its head—not a bow, nothing so gracious, more the minimal acknowledgment of a sentry recognizing a countersign—and stepped aside. And down the line, terrace by terrace, figure by figure, in a ripple she could see but barely believe, the topiary turned. Not toward them. *Alongside* them. Each guardian rotated on roots that must have torn free of decades of soil and re-anchored, aligning itself to the path of their climb, forming an honor guard—or an escort—or a procession of warders marching two prisoners up the hill.

Sefa grabbed Jude's arm. "What did you tell it?"

"The truth."

"Why?"

"Because it wouldn't have accepted anything else." Jude's voice shook. "That's the thing about the old speech, Sefa. It can't lie and it can't be lied to. Which means somewhere in this garden, there's a record of exactly who I am and why I've come, spoken in my own voice, binding as a signature." She laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and high. "I've kept honest books my whole career because I couldn't keep an honest mouth. Here, the mouth has to be honest too. Everything here is a ledger."

"Then read the entry above us," Sefa said, and pointed uphill.

Above the seventh terrace, crowning the hill, stood the ruin of a great house—or the bones of one, walls of pale stone roofless against the sky, windows empty, ivy pouring through them like water filling a hull. And rising through the broken roofline, taller than any wall, taller than anything living had a right to be, Jude could see the crown of a tree. Just the crown. A cloud of dark foliage like a thunderhead anchored to the earth, vast and still.

The source. The root-vault's tenant. The thing Corvane would graft himself onto. The thing the tree's oldest command would one day turn sunward. She didn't know any of that yet—not in words, not in certainty—but she knew it the way she'd known the rings of grass were tended: by the hum in her blood, which had stopped pulling and started *singing*.

And beneath the singing, underneath it, woven through it like a thread through cloth, she heard something else. Faint. Patient. Familiar.

A voice she had paid a ferryman to forget, speaking in a tone she had sworn never to use again, saying a word she had not said aloud in ten years.

*Welcome home.*

Jude Winterbourne stood in the outer court of the Garden where swordspeakers were made and unmade, escorted by soldiers grown from yew, expected—had been expected, perhaps, since before the courier died, since before the map was drawn, since before the oath—and the word *home* sat on her tongue like a coin she didn't remember earning.

She took the next step uphill anyway. Behind her, in perfect unison, the topiary took theirs.
