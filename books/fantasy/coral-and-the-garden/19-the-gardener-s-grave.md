## 19. The Gardener's Grave

The terraces climbed in slow switchbacks toward the heart of the Garden, and with every step the yew soldiers fell away, one by one, peeling off like an honor guard dismissed at the edge of a king's private grounds. Jude watched the last of them stop at a low archway of white stone, take up a position of perfect stillness, and become—so far as any eye could tell—a hedge again.

"They don't follow past here," Sefa said quietly. She had her knife out. She'd had it out since the outer court, and her knuckles were pale around the grip. "You notice that? They escorted us. Not captured us. There's a difference, and I don't like the difference."

"I noticed."

"You're very calm for a woman walking into a trap that's been growing for a hundred years."

"I'm very tired," Jude said, "and I've been in traps before. They're all the same after the first one. The only question is whether the trapper wants you dead or wants something else, and if it's something else, you can still bargain."

Sefa made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "You'd have made a terrible factor. Factors panic properly. It keeps the ledgers balanced."

They passed under the arch. Beyond it the path widened into a long avenue lined with what Jude first took for statues—figures of weathered stone in the robes of a dozen vanished orders, their faces worn smooth as river pebbles. Then she saw the third one's hands, and stopped.

The hands were folded. Not carved that way. Folded, the way a living person folds them, one thumb over the other, and the stone of the wrists had cracked along the lines of old tendons. These had not been sculptors' models. These had been people, once, who had come to the Garden and stood at the edge of its heart and chosen—by oath, by grief, by exhaustion—to stop. To let the Garden keep them the way it kept everything: slowly, patiently, in living stone.

"They came here to die," Sefa said. Her voice had gone flat, the way it went flat when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "And it took its time about it."

"Or they came here to stay," Jude said. "There's a difference too."

"Is there? You'd know better than me."

Jude didn't answer. She walked on, and the avenue of the standing dead gave way to open ground, and the ground gave way to a sunken court of black flagstone where, at the center, a low barrow rose like a knuckle of earth—unmarked, unadorned, its turf cropped short by some gardener's discipline that had outlived the gardeners.

The barrow had a door.

That was the wrongness of it, Jude thought later. Graves don't have doors. Graves have stones, slabs, sealed lids—things that say *finished*. This had a door of green bronze, thin as a sheet, set into the barrow's face, and it was not locked. It was not even latched. It stood open a hand's width, as if someone had gone in recently and expected to come back out.

Or as if someone had never left at all.

* * *

Inside, the air was dry and smelled of cedar and old iron, and it was warm. Warm, in a hillside crypt beneath a drowned country. Jude's skin prickled with the wrongness of it, the way it pricked when a ledger's columns refused to sum—when the numbers were individually correct and collectively impossible.

The passage went down only a few steps and opened into a round chamber, and the chamber was not a tomb.

It was a root cellar. Of course it was a root cellar; the Garden was nothing if not consistent. But the roots here were not the pale, questing things she had glimpsed beneath the terraces. These roots had been *invited*. They grew along the walls in deliberate patterns, braided into shelves and frames and a single chair, grown around a standing figure at the chamber's center the way ivy grows around a trellis—reverently, without constriction, as if the wood itself understood it was in the presence of its maker.

The figure was upright. Of course it was upright. Jude had known it would be, in the way you know a word before you finish reading it. The Garden's maker had not been laid down like the dead. He had been planted.

He was mummified rather than skeletal—dried to the color of walnut, his robes fused with the bark that embraced him, his face turned up toward a ceiling of woven roots through which a single shaft of true daylight fell, filtered green, like light at the bottom of a pond. His hands were raised, palms open, in a gesture that might have been blessing or might have been surrender. Jude had seen that gesture before. She had seen it on the petrified soldiers in the Marches, the ones caught mid-swing, the ones whose swords still whispered. It was the gesture people made in the last instant, when the thing they had made turned to face them.

At his feet, grown flush with the floor so that no blade could pry it loose, was a plate of the same green bronze as the door. Words were cut into it, and the words had been inlaid with something dark that had never tarnished.

Jude knelt. The sword-speech under her skin—the hum she had starved for ten years, the hum that had come back to her in the Marches like a dog coming home—went silent. Not quiet. *Silent*, the way a room goes silent when someone important enters it.

The inscription was in the old formal script, the script of oaths, the script she had learned at Ashfall Bridge from a dying woman's mouth. It was short. It was the shortest thing she had ever read in that script, and that was its horror; oaths run long, but this was not an oath. This was a confession, and confessions, in Jude's experience, were always short because the truth has no clauses.

*I grew power; it grew me. Burn what I planted.*

* * *

Sefa read it over her shoulder. Jude felt her breath, felt her go still.

"That's it?" Sefa said at last. "That's the whole— he built this. He built *all of this*. The soldiers, the swords, the—" she gestured at the braided walls, the standing dead above, the whole sunken empire of green and iron. "And his last words are six words long?"

"Seven," Jude said. "Count again."

Sefa counted. "Burn what I planted," she said. "That's four."

"The first part. *I grew power; it grew me.*" Jude's voice came out strange, distant, as if someone else were speaking through the shape of her mouth. "Three. Seven words total. He spent a hundred years of craft and a war's worth of blood, and he left seven words."

"What do they mean? *It grew me*—what does that mean?"

Jude looked up at the mummified face. The eyes had dried to pale beads, but the sockets were angled upward, toward the light-shaft, toward the surface, toward the Garden. Not toward the roots. Toward the thing he had made, the way a man looks at a debt he can no longer pay.

"It means the ledger ran both directions," Jude said. "He thought he was the gardener. He thought the power was a crop—something you sow, tend, harvest, control. And somewhere along the way the rows reversed. The power started cultivating *him*. His choices. His oaths. Maybe his thoughts. Maybe by the end he couldn't tell which of his wants were his." She stood. Her knees ached. She was thirty-four years old and her knees ached like her mentor's had. "And when he finally saw it—when he finally read his own accounts honestly—he found the only entry that could balance the book."

"Burn it," Sefa said slowly.

"Burn it."

"But he didn't." Sefa's voice sharpened. "The Garden's still here. The tree—whatever's down there, whatever the map leads to—it's still alive. He wrote *burn what I planted* and then he planted himself in the middle of it like a fence post. That's not a confession, that's a coward's epitaph. He wanted someone else to do it."

"Yes," Jude said. "Exactly that."

"And you're not angry?"

Jude was angry. That was the trouble. She was angry in a way that had no floor to it, a vertigo-anger, because she had spent ten years believing the story her mentor had died to give her: that the swordspeakers had been broken at Ashfall Bridge, that the war had been ended by sacrifice, that silence was the price of peace. A clean story. A story with columns that summed.

And here, in a root cellar, was the entry that didn't fit. The maker of the power had known, at the end, that the power could not be kept, only ended. He had said so in seven words and then left the ending to whoever came after—because he could not do it himself, because the thing he had grown had grown him too well, had rooted in him past the point where his hands could hold the torch.

*It grew me.*

Jude thought of the hum under her skin. She thought of how it had felt in the Marches, speaking the rusted swords down—how *right* it had felt, how the power had fit her like a hand fits a glove, how she had told herself she was using it and had not once asked whether it was using her. She thought of the ferryman's toll, the memory she had paid, and how the tree below—whatever the tree was—had spoken to the courier in her mentor's voice, or so the dying man's ravings suggested, or so the map's crawling ink implied. The power knew what people loved. The power knew how to make keeping it feel like virtue.

That was how it grew you. Not with chains. With *want*.

* * *

She walked the chamber slowly, reading the roots the way she read ledgers. There were other inscriptions, smaller, grown over—fragments of the maker's later hand, scratched into bark with a knife rather than cut in bronze. She brushed the moss from one and found only a date, and a word: *still listening*. Another: *it forgave me, which was worse*. A third, near the floor, so worn she had to trace it with her fingers: *I taught it to speak so it could learn to want. I did not think about what it would want.*

The courier, Jude thought. The bleeding man at the salt door with her true surname in his mouth. He had come from here. He had read these walls, or ones like them, and he had understood what Jude was only now beginning to understand: that the Garden was not a ruin. It was a *crop*, still growing, still waiting for harvest—and the Guild's masters, with their bottled storms and borrowed years, had finally found a field worth the reaping. The map hadn't been a treasure map. It had been a warning label, and the courier had died carrying it because a warning, to be worth anything, has to reach someone who can read it.

Someone who could read it. Someone with the hum under her skin. Someone whose name was Winterbourne.

He hadn't been delivering the map to the Guild at all. He had been delivering it *past* the Guild, to the one person in Meridian who would know what the words meant and what they cost—and he had used her surname because it was the one key that would make her listen, and he had spent his last breath on it because he knew they would come for the map, and they had, and they were coming still. Corvane was not behind them. Corvane was *ahead* of them, or would be, because Corvane read ledgers too, and Corvane had been auditing the impossible trades long enough to know what a field of power was worth.

"He didn't bury himself here to hide the message," Jude said aloud. "He buried himself here to *sign* it. A confession isn't valid without a signature. He made himself the seal."

Sefa stared at her. "You're saying a dead man planned this."

"I'm saying a dead man did the only honest accounting of his life, and then waited a hundred years for someone to audit it." Jude turned back to the bronze plate. The seven words. The raised hands. The upturned face. *I grew power; it grew me.* She thought of her oath, sworn on a bridge at nineteen, and wondered— for the first time, with a cold, clean, terrible clarity—whose want the oath had been serving. Her mentor's? The order's? Or the power's own, wearing the shape of sacrifice, teaching her to bury the gift so deep it could only ever be dug up by someone who came looking?

*Burn what I planted.*

The words sat in her like a seed.

Above them, faint through the woven ceiling, came a sound Jude had not heard in ten years and had hoped never to hear again: the far-off, harmonized ring of many blades being drawn at once. Not from the Marches. From the road. From the direction of the world.

Sefa's head came up. "Tell me that's the wind."

Jude put her hand flat against the bronze plate, against the seven words, and the hum under her skin woke and answered—not with words, but with something worse. With *recognition*.

"It's not the wind," she said. "Someone's brought swords to a garden."
