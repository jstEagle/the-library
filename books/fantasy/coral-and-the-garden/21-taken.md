## 21. Taken

Sefa Ilvane had spent her whole life learning the difference between a promise and a trap, and she had learned it young, in a village that no longer appeared on any map worth printing. Promises were what people made when they wanted something. Traps were what they built when they already had you.

She was thinking about this now, standing in the outer court of the Garden with bramble-shadow falling across her shoulders like a hand she hadn't invited, watching the topiary golems come out of their green sleep.

They did not move the way living things moved. There was no hesitation in them, no weight-shift, no tell. One moment the great yew shapes flanking the western terrace were only shapes—hedges pruned by some long-dead gardener into the forms of kneeling soldiers—and the next they were unfolding, branches cracking like ship's timbers in a storm, roots tearing free of soil with a sound like a hundred small bones breaking at once.

Jude had gone still in the way Sefa had learned to read as danger. Not fear-stillness. Calculation-stillness. Her eyes tracked the nearest golem's arms—arms that had been hawthorn and ivy an hour ago and were now something harder, wood grown dense as ironwood around a core of old command—and her lips parted slightly, the way they always did when sword-speech rose in her.

"Stand back," Jude said quietly. "I can speak these."

"You can speak *some* things," Sefa said. She kept her voice level, because panic was a currency and she refused to spend hers. "These aren't swords, Winterbourne. These are older. Look at them."

Jude looked. Sefa watched her look—the particular narrowing, the way her gaze went from surface to structure, reading the golems the way Sefa herself read a bill of lading, hunting for the clause underneath the courtesy.

"They're bound," Jude said at last. "Bound to commands older than anything I was taught."

"Older than your order," Sefa agreed. "Older than the war. The Garden doesn't take orders from swordspeakers. It takes orders from whoever planted it." She nodded toward the terraces climbing away above them, green and dark and patient. "And whoever planted it has been dead a very long time. Which means the commands don't change. They just wait."

The first golem reached them before Jude finished answering. It did not run; running was for creatures with fear. It simply advanced, each stride covering ground no hedge should have been able to cover, its yew fingers opening like the pages of a book.

Jude spoke anyway. Sefa heard it—a single syllable, low, shaped more like a root-word than a word, the kind of sound that made the air feel heavier after it left your mouth. The golem slowed. Its leading arm drooped, leaves shivering, and for one heartbeat Sefa thought—

Then the second syllable died in Jude's throat. Sefa saw her face change, saw the calculation collapse into something colder: recognition. Whatever law held these things had heard her voice and found it wanting—not wrong, just *junior*. A newer oath trying to override an older one, and the older one, as always, winning.

"It won't hold," Jude said, and there was fury in it, and under the fury something that looked almost like relief, though Sefa couldn't have said why anyone would be relieved. "Whatever binds them predates my kind. I can slow them. I can't stop them."

"Then stop trying," Sefa said. "Save your voice. You'll want it later."

She didn't know yet how right she was.

* * *

The golems did not hurt them. That was the strangest part, the part Sefa would turn over later like a coin worn smooth by handling. The yew hands closed on their arms—gentle, absurdly gentle, a grip calibrated to restrain rather than crush—and lifted them, and carried them, and the whole time the smell of crushed green filled Sefa's nose until she thought she would be sick from it.

They were carried up the terraces. Past fountains choked with moss. Past rows of beds where plants grew in patterns too regular to be wild and too strange to be ornamental—Sefa recognized none of them, and she had grown up in a country where every child learned which weeds fed you and which ones killed you. These were neither. These were crops. Someone had farmed this place, and what they had farmed was not food.

"They're taking us to the house," Jude said from beside her, her voice tight with the effort of not struggling. Struggling against a grip like that would cost skin. Sefa had already tested it with one wrist and gotten a warning pressure in return, firm as a manacle, careful as a nurse. "Or whatever passes for a house here."

"There," Sefa said.

At the top of the final terrace stood a ruin that had never quite finished being grand. Walls of pale stone gone green at the joints, windows empty or glazed with grime, a roof half-collapsed into a courtyard where a tree grew through the floor of what might once have been a ballroom. And in front of it, arranged on the steps with a precision that made Sefa's stomach drop, stood men.

Hollow Men. She knew them on sight now—had seen them at the inn, had counted them in the dark, had memorized the particular wrongness of how they stood. Too evenly weighted. Too still. Men whose names had been cut out of them moved differently than other men; grief and vanity and fear all pulled at a body in small ways, and these bodies felt none of it. Twelve of them on the steps. More in the courtyard behind. And at their center, on the top step, in traveling clothes too fine for the Marches and boots too clean for the road—

Corvane.

He looked rested. That was the detail Sefa hated most. They had crossed the Drowned Marches, paid the ferryman in memories, fought Hollow Men at a fortified inn, slept in ditches, and Corvane had arrived looking like a man who had taken a coach and stopped at comfortable houses along the way. Of course he had. He'd known the route. He'd probably known it before they did.

"Well," Corvane said, and his voice was warm, courteous, pitched to carry. "The Guild thanks you both for your service."

The golems set them down on the gravel. Sefa got her feet under her slowly, cataloguing: Jude to her left, three paces, unharmed, breathing controlled. Twelve crossbows among the Hollow Men, all leveled, all steady. The golems withdrawing a few paces but not leaving—standing at the edges of the courtyard like furniture that happened to be armed. No path out that wasn't covered.

"Your service," Corvane repeated, when neither of them answered. "You recovered the map. You opened the way. Two factors of the Meridian Guild, working independently, converging on the same objective—do you know how rare that is? How *pleasing*? Most men spend their whole careers avoiding exactly the work that matters."

"You logged the courier's death as robbery," Jude said. Her voice was flat. Sefa had heard that flatness before, in the counting-house, and knew it for what it was: the sound of someone holding themselves together with arithmetic. "You sent killers after us. And now you thank us."

"I did what the situation required," Corvane said, with the mild tone of a man discussing freight rates. "As did you. As did our friend Mistress Ilvane, who has been lying to me since Salt Door day, and doing it rather well, I'll admit. I nearly believed her report about the courier. Nearly." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was designed to be a good smile. "But we'll come to accounts later. First, the Garden."

He turned and gestured at the ruin behind him, and Sefa understood suddenly, completely, with the cold clarity of a woman who has just found the hidden clause in her own contract, that Corvane had not followed them.

Corvane had *led* them.

The map had come from his side of the ledger. The courier had been his—or had been carrying his goods, or had been dying inconveniently somewhere his plans required a corpse. Every step of their journey had been a step down a corridor he had built, and now they stood at the end of it, in the room he had furnished, and the door was closing.

"You knew," Sefa said.

"I suspected," Corvane corrected. "There's a difference. Suspicion is cheaper and covers more ground. But yes—I knew the map would find its way to the one person alive who could read what the Garden actually is. Swordspeech leaves traces, Mistress Ilvane. Resonance. The Guild's ledgers record a payment made in pre-war currency; the Guild's ledgers record a courier who knew a forbidden surname; the Guild's ledgers, if one reads them carefully enough, record everything." He spread his hands. "I am an auditor. Reading is what I do."

"And what do you want?" Jude asked.

"What I have always wanted." Corvane's smile deepened by a degree. "What's below."

* * *

They took Jude first.

Sefa watched it happen and could do nothing, and the nothing was the worst thing she had ever done. Four Hollow Men crossed the gravel, took Jude's arms, took the knife from her belt, took even the cord at her wrist that she used to tie back her hair—one of them folded it and put it in his pocket, which told Sefa something about orders received, about thoroughness, about a mind that thought in inventories. Corvane's mind.

Jude didn't fight. Sefa had expected fighting. Instead Jude turned her head and looked at Sefa directly, across twelve crossbows and twenty feet of gravel, and what passed between them in that look was not a plea and not a farewell. It was an instruction. Sefa had spent years reading people professionally, and she read it clearly:

*Stay whole. Stay useful. Wait.*

Then they walked her toward the ruin, through doors that had hung open for a century, and the dark inside took her, and the courtyard was quieter by exactly one person.

Corvane stayed. He dismissed most of the Hollow Men inward after her—all but four—and then he came and stood near Sefa, close enough that she could smell the ink on him, good ink, expensive ink, the ink of a man who signed things.

"Mistress Ilvane," he said. "We should discuss your account."

"My account is simple," Sefa said. "You want me dead or silent, and I'd prefer otherwise."

"Dead, actually, is wasteful. Silence is cheap. But you've misread the ledger again." He studied her with the patience of a man who enjoyed studying things. "You are a factor of the Meridian Guild with a documented talent for survival and an undocumented talent for observation. You crossed the Drowned Marches with the last swordspeaker in the world and arrived intact. Do you know what that makes you, in my valuation?"

"Expensive," Sefa said.

"Precise." He inclined his head, genuinely pleased, and Sefa felt the old cold pleasure of being seen accurately by a dangerous man, and hated herself for feeling it. "So let us skip past the part where you pretend to be a loyal employee and I pretend to believe you. You bargained with the ferryman. You navigated Thornwall. You are still standing. I find myself curious what else you know."

Sefa weighed it. This was the moment—she knew it was the moment, had known since the golems closed their green hands around her wrists—that the theme of her whole crooked life came due. Loyalty or truth. The Guild had bought her loyalty at fourteen with bread and a bunk and the first kind words she'd ever been paid for, and she had repaid it with fifteen years of competent silence. But loyalty to what? To masters who logged murders as robberies? To an auditor who had just thanked her for services while arranging her disposal?

Truth, then. Truth was the only asset she'd ever owned outright.

"The irrigation," Sefa said.

Corvane went very still. It was the first genuine reaction she'd gotten from him, and she treasured it, banked it, marked it in her mental ledger as *leverage, confirmed*.

"Say more."

"The Garden isn't dead," Sefa said. "Overgrown, yes. Abandoned, yes. But power doesn't rot like crops do—it pools. It drains downhill. Everything on this hillside—the terraces, the beds, the channels, those plants nobody can name—is a system. Water was never the point. The water was the *carrier*. Whoever built this place figured out how to make power flow like water, and they irrigated with it, and everything that grows here has been drinking it for a hundred years." She paused, letting him lean in, and he leaned. "Which means the tree below isn't the source. It's the cistern. And whoever controls the channels controls what fills it."

Corvane's eyes narrowed. "You've seen the channels."

"I've walked beside them since the outer court. You haven't. You've been following a map drawn by a dead man, Master Corvane, and maps show roads, not systems." She let that sit. Let him feel the specific humiliation of a man who prided himself on reading everything discovering a page he hadn't read. "My life for what I know. All of it. The channels, the gates, the flow-rates, the failure points. Everything I observed between the thornwall and this courtyard."

"That's a large price for information you gathered for free."

"Nothing was free," Sefa said. "I paid for every mile of it. In memories, in blood, in a partner I just watched get carried into the dark." She held his gaze. "Do we have terms?"

Corvane considered her for a long moment. Around them the Hollow Men stood in their nameless patience, and the golems stood in their older patience, and somewhere below the courtyard, in a vault full of roots and oaths, Jude Winterbourne was learning what the Garden wanted from her.

"We have terms," Corvane said. "Provisionally. You will walk the channels with me tomorrow and demonstrate this knowledge. If it proves true, you live, and your account with the Guild will reflect... flexibility. If it proves false—" He shrugged elegantly. "Then the Guild's loss is a rounding error."

"Tomorrow," Sefa said. "Not tonight?"

"Tonight," Corvane said, turning toward the ruin, "I have an appointment below."

He paused at the threshold, and the last light off the terraces caught his face, and for one unguarded instant Sefa saw past the auditor's calm to the thing underneath—the hunger, the old hunger, the hunger of a man who had spent decades keeping books for powers that never once put his name in them.

"She's worth ten of you," Corvane said, almost gently. "Perhaps a hundred. Try not to need her back."

Then the door swallowed him, and Sefa stood alone in the courtyard with four Hollow Men and the smell of crushed green, and the evening came down over the Garden like a lid.

She sat on the steps because sitting was allowed and standing all night was not strategy, just suffering. Above her the ruined house held its broken silhouette against the stars. Below her, under stone and root and a century of accumulated silence, the tree drank its oaths, and Jude Winterbourne was somewhere in the dark of it, and Sefa had traded the only truth she owned to buy time she didn't know how to spend.

*Wait*, the look had said. *Stay whole. Stay useful.*

She pressed her palms flat against the cold stone steps, feeling for vibration, for hum, for any sign of what moved beneath her. Nothing. The Garden kept its own counsel.

But she had seen the channels. She had counted the sluice-gates. And somewhere in her memory, filed with the same care she gave to forged bills of lading, was the detail Corvane would not think to ask about until it was far too late:

Every irrigation system ever built had a flood-gate. And every flood-gate had a weak point. And Sefa Ilvane, who had spent her whole life learning the difference between promises and traps, had already found theirs.

She just needed to reach it before morning.
