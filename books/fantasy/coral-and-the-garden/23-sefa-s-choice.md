## 23. Sefa's Choice

The anchor-chains hung from the trellis like the ribs of some enormous drowned animal, and Sefa Ilvane had been counting them since dawn.

Seven chains, each thick as her wrist, each studded with iron collars that bit into the living wood of the Garden's central tree. The Hollow Men had strung them in the night while she slept in the guest-house Corvane had assigned her—a room with clean sheets and a locked window, which told her everything she needed to know about how much he trusted her. The chains ran down into the root-vault below, where they had taken Jude, and at their tops they terminated in great brass anchors driven into the trellis itself, humming faintly with a power that made Sefa's teeth ache when she stood too close.

She had made her bargain in this same courtyard, twelve hours ago. She could still feel the shape of the words in her mouth, smooth as coins: *I know the irrigation of this place. I know where the old channels run, where the power pools, where it can be tapped without waking what sleeps beneath. Give me my life and a factor's commission, and I will draw you a map worth more than Jude Winterbourne.*

Corvane had smiled his ledger-smile, the one that balanced every column before it was spoken, and had said, "Of course, Mistress Ilvane. I have always valued a practical woman."

And she had believed—no, that wasn't honest, and honesty was the whole point of the exercise now, wasn't it? She hadn't believed him. She had simply preferred the bargain to the alternative, the way a drowning merchant prefers any rope to none, without inspecting where the other end is tied.

Now she stood at the edge of the outer court, in the grey light before true morning, and counted chains.

*Seven.* One for each of the first swordspeakers, if the ferryman's toll had taught her anything—if the memory she'd paid for her brother's face had bought anything at all besides an ache with no name attached to it. Seven roots for seven oaths. And somewhere below, at the bottom of all that drinking dark, the tree was already beginning to feed on Jude. She could tell because the chains had begun to sweat. Beads of moisture gathered on the iron links and fell, one by one, into the moss, and where they fell the moss grew faster than moss should grow, green spreading in little rings like ripples in a pond.

Sefa had spent her whole life learning to read ledgers. This was a ledger too. *Debit: one swordspeaker. Credit: one war.*

* * *

She had been a war-child. That was the phrase the relief camps used, the ones who fed you if you didn't ask questions about your parents. War-children got extra bread on founding days and nothing at all on the anniversary of Ashfall Bridge, because the matrons said grief was a debt the young shouldn't be made to carry. Sefa had carried it anyway. She had carried it through her apprenticeship to a salt-factor, through her letters of recommendation, through the long climb to a desk in the Meridian Guild where impossible things changed hands behind columns of respectable numbers. She had carried it so long she had stopped noticing its weight, the way you stop noticing a stone sewn into the hem of your coat.

Then she had seen Jude lie.

It was a small thing, the kind of thing no one else in the counting-house would ever have marked. Master Corvane asked about the courier, and Jude said, "He came to the wrong door. I gave him directions to the infirmary," and as she said it, her left hand moved—just slightly, just the index finger, tracing a small circle against her thigh. A tell. A child's tell, the kind children make when they've practiced a lie in front of a mirror until the words are smooth but the body hasn't been consulted. Sefa knew it because she had done the same thing herself at eight years old, swearing to the camp matron that she hadn't stolen the bread, her finger drawing its little guilty orbit against her skirt.

A swordspeaker who lied like a child. Who hid something so large it hummed under her skin, and hid it so badly, after ten years of practice, because she had never once had anyone to practice it *for*. Because hiding was not the same as being dishonest, and Jude Winterbourne was perhaps the most honest liar Sefa had ever watched.

That was the moment, though she hadn't known it then. That was the moment the stone shifted in her hem and she felt it again for the first time in twenty years.

* * *

The Hollow Men were changing the watch.

Sefa had learned their rhythm over two days: four on the trellis platform, three patrolling the colonnade, the rest sleeping in the barracks tent by the gate. They changed watch at first bell, and during the change there was a span—she had timed it, because timing things was what she did, intervals and interest and the compounding of small advantages—there was a span of perhaps forty heartbeats when the trellis platform held only two of them, and both of those two were occupied with the handover of tokens, touching palm to palm in their nameless salute.

Forty heartbeats. She had unshipped cargoes worth more than a city block in less.

The problem was not the Hollow Men. The problem was the chains themselves. Each anchor was set into the trellis with a locking-pin of black iron, and the pins were bound with speech—not swordspeech, but the older grammar underneath it, the language the Garden itself spoke, the one the topiary golems obeyed. She couldn't cut the pins. She suspected no blade in the world could cut them; they were grown, not forged, and growing things only yielded to commands.

But she had spent yesterday afternoon drawing Corvane his map of the irrigation channels, and in doing so she had been forced to actually read the Garden—the way you can skim a contract for years and then, the day you're called upon to explain it aloud, finally understand what you signed. The channels of power ran everywhere in this place, but they ran *downhill*. Everything here drained toward the vault, toward the tree, the way wealth drains toward a capital. The chains weren't holding the tree down. They were funnels. Every drop of swordspeech that Jude bled, every scrap of oath the tree drank, would rise up these seven channels and pool in the anchors, ready for Corvane's grafting rite—and Sefa had seen the rite's diagram in his papers, had seen the way the anchors' brass throats converged on a single collar meant for a single throat.

So she didn't need to break the chains. She needed to *spill* them. Break the seals on the anchors and let the gathered power run out into the moss, wasted, useless, dissipated into ground that had been drinking power for a thousand years and could drink forever. It wouldn't stop Corvane. But it would slow him. It would cost him days, maybe a week, and a week was a currency too, and right now it was the only coin she had.

Forty heartbeats per anchor. Seven anchors. Two hundred and eighty heartbeats, if nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever went wrong only once.

* * *

She waited for first bell.

The Guild's bells reached even here, faint across the Marches, a sound like memory of a city. When it came, thin and bronze and far away, the Hollow Men on the platform turned to each other and began the token-handover, and Sefa moved.

She had dressed for it: dark wool, no jewelry, hair bound back with cord the way she'd bound it hauling cargo on the salt-docks at fourteen. She crossed the open court in the shadow of the colonnade, counting her own heartbeats now, and came up the service stair to the trellis platform with her back against the lattice of dead vines, and the two Hollow Men never turned, because Hollow Men do not look at what does not concern their orders, and their orders concerned the chains, not the shadows.

The first anchor was warm.

That unsettled her more than she expected. Brass should be cold in the pre-dawn damp, but this metal held a heat like a living thing, like pressing your palm to the flank of a horse. The locking-pin sat in its socket at the anchor's crown, black iron veined with silver, and around its base the old grammar curled in characters that seemed to move when she looked away and hold still when she looked directly at them, the way fish hold still in clear water.

There was a seal across the pin. Wax, or something like wax—pale, faintly luminous, smelling of roses and rot.

Sefa took out her factor's knife. It was a good knife, Guild-stamped, the tool of her trade for fifteen years, the one she'd used to cut sample threads from silk bolts and pry lids from crates of bottled storms. She set its point against the seal's edge, whispered the only word of command she knew—stolen from listening at Jude's door two nights ago, a small word, a hinge-word, the word for *open*—and pushed.

The seal split with a sound like a sigh.

Power came out of the anchor the way water comes out of a breached cistern—not a spray but a *pressure*, a rush of invisible weight that knocked her back a step and set every hair on her arms standing. The moss below the platform flushed emerald, then gold, then a deep bruised violet, blooming and dying and blooming again in fast-forward seasons. Somewhere far below, she felt rather than heard something vast turn over in its sleep.

One.

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them with detached professional interest, the way she'd once looked at a ledger that wouldn't balance, and made herself breathe, and went to the second anchor.

Two. Three. Four.

By the fourth, the Hollow Men had finished their handover and the platform should have held four guards again, but the world had gone strange around the bleeding anchors—light bent near them, footsteps slowed, and the patrol that came up the stair walked as if wading through honey, their blank faces turning toward the spilled radiance with something that might, in men who still had faces, have been wonder. Sefa worked in the pocket of distorted time, sweating, her breath ragged, the hinge-word hoarse in her throat.

Five.

The fifth anchor fought her. Its seal had been renewed recently—she could see the wax was younger, brighter—and when her knife touched it, the seal *pushed back*, a pressure against her mind like a hand flat against her forehead, and into that pressure poured an image: herself, seated at a high desk, a master's chain of office on her shoulders, factors rising and bowing as she entered, the whole Guild arranged around her like a garden arranged around its keeper—

She laughed. Actually laughed, breathless and ugly, kneeling on the trellis platform with blood beginning to seep through her sleeve where the lattice thorns had opened her forearm without her noticing.

"I've seen that vision before," she told the anchor. "Thornwall showed me the same one. You'll have to offer better than my own ambition. I've priced it." She leaned her weight onto the knife. "I know exactly what it costs."

The seal broke.

Six.

On the sixth, the distortion collapsed. Time snapped back like a released cable, and the patrol was suddenly *there*, three paces away, and the nearest Hollow Man drew his blade with the smooth economy of a man who has forgotten everything except how to use one.

Sefa did the arithmetic she was best at. Three guards, one knife, six anchors broken, one remaining. The equation had only one solution, and she had known it since dawn, and knowing it had made the morning strangely calm.

She threw herself at the seventh anchor instead of away from them.

The blade took her across the shoulder-blade as she reached it—a long, burning line, shallow enough that she knew in the instant of its landing that she would live, deep enough that her arm went half-numb and the knife nearly left her fingers. She caught it two-handed. The last seal was old, ancient, the original binding laid down by whoever had built this place, and it did not push visions at her. It simply held, patient as bedrock.

Behind her, boots. The particular silence of men without names closing in.

"Jude," Sefa said, not loudly, to no one, to the tree, to the woman drinking darkness below. "You stubborn, honest liar. If you die down there after everything, I'm filing a formal complaint."

She spoke the hinge-word and drove the knife in with all her weight and all her years and all the stone she had carried in her hem since she was eight years old.

The seventh seal burst like a struck bell.

The release lifted her off her feet. For one suspended heartbeat she hung in a fountain of light—gold and green and the deep rose-color of the thorned map, all the colors of the Garden pouring out of the broken anchor and running wild across the platform, into the moss, down through the trellis, everywhere except where Corvane wanted it. The Hollow Men staggered. One dropped his sword and pressed both hands to his temples as if remembering something hurt. In the ringing aftermath Sefa heard—clearly, unmistakably, from the man whose blade had opened her back—one word, spoken in a cracked and wondering voice:

"...Brac?"

Then the butt of a spear came down and the world went white, and then it went dark, and the last thing Sefa Ilvane thought, as her cheek found the moss and the moss drank the blood out of her wound with terrible gentle efficiency, was a purely professional assessment:

*Six of seven. He'll lose three days. Maybe four.*

*Worth it.*

* * *

She woke to hands binding her wrists, and kept her eyes closed, and listened.

"—the factor betrayed the terms," someone was saying, a hollow voice, a voice like a room with the furniture removed. "The auditor will determine payment."

"She saved us trouble," said another voice, and this one was different—rougher, uncertain, a voice with cracks in it where a name might grow back. "The anchors were going to fail anyway. Ask him. Ask—" A pause. "Ask me why I said my own name just now."

Silence. Then the first voice, colder: "The auditor is coming. Bind her properly. Leave her for judgment."

Sefa opened her eyes.

Above her, the trellis arched against a brightening sky, six anchors weeping light into the grass, the seventh shattered, its brass split open like a seedpod. Below her, through the platform's gaps, the earth went down and down toward the vault where a tree drank oaths and a swordspeaker waited in the dark, not yet knowing what Sefa had bought for her, or what it had cost.

Judgment was coming. So, she thought, watching the far gate where dust was rising along the road from the Marches, was Corvane.

And somewhere below, if the tree's trembling in the soil was any indication, something had just begun to wake up.
