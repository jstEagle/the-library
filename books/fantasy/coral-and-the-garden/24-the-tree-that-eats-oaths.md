## 24. The Tree That Eats Oaths

The dark below the Garden was not empty.

Jude had learned that within the first hour of her captivity. The root-vault was a chamber of packed earth and pale stone, lit by nothing she could name—no torch, no lamp, only a faint green luminescence that seeped from the walls themselves, as though the soil remembered sunlight and gave it back grudgingly. The roots came through everywhere. They crossed the ceiling like the ribs of some buried leviathan, threaded the floor beneath her bare feet, curled along the walls in slow, muscular coils. And they were warm. That was the worst of it. She had expected roots to be cold, damp, dead things. These were warm as a sleeping animal, and when she pressed her palm against one, she felt a pulse.

Slow. Patient. Older than the war. Older than the oaths.

They had taken her boots, her belt, the little knife sewn into her sleeve—the Guild habit of assuming everyone was armed, which in Jude's case had been true for ten years in ways no auditor ever suspected. They left her the dress, plain grey wool, and her hair loose because there was no one to braid it. She sat with her back against the stone and counted her breathing the way Maren had taught her, four counts in, seven held, eight out, until fear became something she could carry instead of something that carried her.

Maren. Even thinking the name hurt now, in this place, and she did not know why until much later.

* * *

Time moved strangely underground. There was no bell, no tide, no shift-change of light through windows. Jude slept, or drifted near sleep, and woke not knowing whether an hour or a day had passed. Her stomach told her it had been long enough to matter. No food came. No water either, and yet her throat was never raw, her lips never cracked. The air itself was thick enough to drink, sweet with the smell of turned earth and roses and, beneath both, something coppery that she refused to name.

On what she judged to be the second day—or the third, or the first—the roots began to move toward her.

Not quickly. Nothing in this place was quick. But over hours, so gradually she might have missed it if she had been doing anything other than watching, the nearest root detached itself from the wall and crept across the floor, the way ivy would cross a fence if you watched it for a season. It stopped a hand's width from her bare foot. Its tip split, delicately, like a bud deciding, and from the split rose a single flower.

A rose. White, thornless, perfect, glowing faintly with the same green-gold light as the walls.

Jude did not touch it. She had read enough ledgers to know that nothing offered freely in a place like this was free.

"I know what you are," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small and human in all that ancient dark, and she was glad of it. "You're the source. The first root. Whatever Corvane wants to graft himself onto—you're it."

The flower trembled. Not in wind; there was no wind. It trembled the way a throat trembles before speech.

And then the vault spoke to her in Maren's voice.

* * *

"Jude."

She was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, heart slamming, hands half-curled around a sword that wasn't there and hadn't been for ten years. The voice came from everywhere—from the roots, the walls, the warm pulsing dark—and it was *her*, exactly her, the low even tone, the Ashfall accent worn smooth by years of command, the slight dryness at the end of sentences that meant Maren Kell was amused and trying not to show it.

"You're dead," Jude said. "I watched them bury you under the bridge stones. I carried the stone myself."

"Did you?" The voice was gentle. Terribly gentle. "Or did you carry the grief, and let the stone stand in for me? Grief is heavy too. Easier to lift, in some ways. It fits the hands."

"Stop it." Jude's voice cracked on the second word and she hated herself for it. "Stop using her voice. You've drunk enough oaths to know how we sound, all of us, every swordspeaker who ever fed you—but you don't get to wear her."

Silence. The flower swayed once, twice, like a head considering.

Then, in a voice that was still Maren's but quieter now, almost apologetic: "It is the only voice you would listen to. I have tried others. I have tried your own. You flinch from yourself worse than from the dead."

"That's not an answer."

"No," the tree agreed. "It is an apology. Take it or leave it. I have waited a long time, Jude Winterbourne. I can wait while you decide what you will permit me to sound like."

She should have sat back down. She should have retreated behind the counting-house wall she'd built inside herself, the ledger-face, the flat polite voice that said *the accounts balance, Master Corvane, within acceptable variance*. Instead she slid down the stone until she was sitting again, knees drawn up, facing the white rose and the dark it grew from.

"What do you want?"

"To be tended." So simple it was obscene. "I am the first oath ever spoken into living wood. Do you know what an oath is, child? Truly? Your order thought it was a promise. A promise is a thing between people. An oath is a thing between a soul and the world—it cuts a channel, and power runs down the channel like rain off a roof. Every swordspeaker who ever swore fed me. Every vow of silence, every vow of service, every death-oath sworn over a broken blade. I drank them all. I grew. And everything I grew, I grew *from*—your order's courage, your order's cruelty, your order's ten thousand small surrenders. I am made of what you gave away."

"And Corvane wants to drink from you."

"Corvane wants to *be* me." Something moved in the dark, vast and slow—a root shifting its weight, the earth groaning. "He thinks I am a well. He thinks he will graft his branch onto my trunk and draw power forever, reignite his pretty war, write his name large enough that history cannot lose it again. He is wrong, but he is not stupid, and he has Hollow Men and iron chains and time. You are here because he needs a speaker to open me. Roots grown from oaths only open to oaths. His are stolen. Yours were given."

"So use me and be done with it."

"I intend to," said Maren's voice, mild as morning. "But not the way he intends. I am offering you a different graft, Jude. Mine."

* * *

The flower opened wider. From its heart spilled light—not the dim green of the walls but a clean gold, and in the gold Jude saw things, or remembered them, or was shown them; the distinction had stopped mattering.

She saw the counting-house as it had been the day she arrived, nineteen years old, ash still in the seams of her knuckles, signing her name as *Jude Wren* for the first time and feeling the lie settle over her like a coat that almost fit. She saw the ledger of the bottled storm, the borrowed year, the debt paid in a currency dead since Ashfall—and she saw, behind each entry, the truth the Guild had never recorded: where the storm had been taken from, whose year it had been, who had starved when that coin went out of circulation. She saw her own decade laid out like an account book, every column balanced, every line item a small burial.

"I can give it back," the tree said. "All of it. Hear me fully before you refuse, because refusal out of pride is just another surrender, and you have made enough of those for one life."

The light sharpened.

"Your gift. Not the thin thread you kept alive under your skin like a smuggled coal—I mean the whole of it. The speech that moves iron. At Ashfall you spoke three swords at once and the bridge itself leaned to hear you. You were the strongest of your generation and you buried it under bookkeeping. I can raise it. Whole. Stronger than it was, because I have grown since, and everything I have grown is yours to speak through."

"I don't want—"

"Your name." Relentless, gentle, precise. "Winterbourne. Spoken openly, in daylight, in the city where you hid it. Your order restored—not the broken remnant, not the survivors' guilt-club, but the order as it was meant to be, refounded with you at its root. Students. Apprentices. Children who hum when they hold kitchen knives and don't know why, gathered somewhere safe and taught properly, taught by *you*. And Maren—" the voice softened, and Jude's eyes burned, "—Maren's last hour, unmade. I drank that oath too, the death-oath she swore over you, the bargain that bought your silence with your survival. I remember it perfectly. Oaths are the only thing I keep perfectly. Give yourself to me, and I will give you back the memory of her face without the flinch. You will grieve her cleanly, the way the living grieve, instead of carrying her like contraband for the rest of your days."

The vault was very quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled by earth and stone, Jude thought she heard the groan of iron—chains, golems, something—but it was distant, dreamlike, easy to set aside. Everything was easy to set aside. That was the horror of the offer. It fit her wounds the way the name *Wren* had fit her shoulders. Almost perfectly. Warmly. Like it had been measured for her.

Because it had. The tree had been drinking oaths for a thousand years. It knew exactly what a surrendered person hungered for.

* * *

"What does it cost?" Jude asked. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "There's always a cost. I keep ledgers. Nothing trades at fair value, especially not from something that calls itself generous."

"Wisdom," said the tree. "Very well. The price is this: you become my root-keeper. My hands. My voice in the world above. You will tend me as the Gardener tended me, and the Gardener's successor after him, and so on back to the first grave in the crypt upstairs. You will live long—longer than Sefa Ilvane, longer than anyone you might come to love, though I notice you do not let yourself love, so perhaps that cost is theoretical. You will stay below. The sun will visit you as rumor visits prisoners. And every oath spoken anywhere in the world will pass through you on its way to me, and you will feel all of them. All of them, Jude. Every promise made in bad faith. Every wedding vow already rotting. Every soldier's oath the night before a battle that will kill him. You will drink what I drink. That is what a root-keeper is. Not a master of the power. A *root* of it. Held. Fed. Never free."

"And if I refuse?"

"Corvane opens me anyway, clumsily, with stolen words and iron, and the war comes back wearing Guild colors. Or you die in this room and the world above decides its own fate without you, which it has been doing for ten years rather well, all things considered." A pause, and then, in Maren's dry tone: "I am not threatening you. I am simply honest, which I understand is rare in your profession lately."

Jude laughed. It came out cracked and wet and startled her more than the voice had. "You've been reading my ledgers."

"I have been reading *you*. It is the same discipline."

She looked at the white rose for a long time. The gold light had gentled, waiting, patient as interest accruing. And Jude Winterbourne, who had spent ten years balancing books full of impossible things, finally made herself look directly at the entry she had avoided her whole adult life:

She wanted it. Gods help her, she wanted it. She wanted the gift back like a phantom limb wanted itching scratched. She wanted to say *Winterbourne* out loud in the sun. She wanted Maren's face without the flinch more than she wanted breath. The tree hadn't invented these hungers. It had merely read her accurately—which was, she understood suddenly, the most dangerous thing anything could ever do.

"You know why I'll say no," she said quietly.

"Tell me anyway. I collect reasons."

"Because Maren didn't buy my survival so I could be planted. She bought it so I could *live*—and living isn't having what you lost back. Living is carrying what you lost and building anyway. You're offering me restoration. Restoration is just a prettier word for going backward." She stood. Her legs shook and she let them. "And because I've seen what happens to things that eat oaths. They grow enormous and hungry and alone in the dark, waiting centuries for someone desperate enough to keep them company. I've audited enough lonely fortunes to recognize one."

The silence stretched. Then, softly, in a voice that was no longer Maren's at all but something older, wearier, almost sad:

"The Gardener said nearly the same thing, at the end. *I grew power; it grew me.* He wrote it on his own grave so no one would forget." The rose closed, slowly, petal by petal. "You may yet prove as wise as a dead man, Jude Winterbourne. Or you may change your mind when the iron comes. They always think they won't. The desperate ones always think they won't."

"Then we'll find out together."

"No," said the tree, and the warmth drained out of the vault like a tide going out. "We will find out separately. Listen."

Far above—through fathoms of earth, through terraces and topiary and the crypt of the upright dead—came a sound Jude knew in her bones before her ears understood it: the shriek of anchor-chains snapping, one after another, and men shouting names they were only just remembering they had.

The roots around her shuddered. The green light flickered.

And in the dark of the root-vault, the first oath in the world began, very softly, to scream.
