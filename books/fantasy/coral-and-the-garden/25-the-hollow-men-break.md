## 25. The Hollow Men Break

Sefa had counted prisoners before. In the war camps of her childhood she had learned to tally men the way merchants tallied stock—by weight, by stillness, by the particular quality of their silence—and so when the Hollow Men were dragged into the Garden's outer court, bound with their own anchor-chains and dumped among the toppled topiary like sacks of wet grain, she did what she had always done.

She counted.

Nine. Nine Hollow Men, faceless in the way that was worse than masked: faces present, features whole, and yet somehow *unclaimed*, as though no one had ever bothered to sign them. Their armor was Guild-gray, unmarked by crest or campaign. Their eyes moved without settling on anything. When they breathed, they breathed in unison, a habit drilled into bodies that had forgotten everything else.

Sefa stood over them in the green half-light beneath the trellis arches, her shoulder wound bound in strips torn from her own shirt, and felt something old and cold turn over in her chest.

She knew what these men were. She had been nearly one of them.

* * *

The village of Ilvane had fed the Garden's first harvest—that was the story she'd told Jude at the inn at Halfway, and it was true, but it was not the whole truth. The whole truth was smaller and uglier. The whole truth was that after the harvesters came through, taking oaths and memories and the strong young blood of anyone who could hold a blade, the enemy order had needed somewhere to put the ones who were left. Children who watched. Children who remembered too much.

They hadn't killed them. Killing was waste.

They had *emptied* them.

Sefa had been seven. She remembered the room—the white room, the smell of vinegar and cut grass, the man with kind hands who had told her, gently, that forgetting would be a mercy. She remembered fighting him. She remembered losing. And then—

Then there was a gap, years wide, and when she came out the other side of it she was eleven years old and standing in a Guild counting-house with no name she trusted and a talent for numbers that felt like someone else's inheritance.

The Hollow Men had been made the same way. Older, harder, more thoroughly scraped—but the same. She could see it now, looking down at them, the way you can see the watermark in paper only when you tilt it against the light. Underneath the blankness there was paper. There had always been paper.

And paper held ink, if you knew how to write.

* * *

Jude was below. That was the shape of everything now: Jude below, in the root-vault, in Corvane's hands; the tree drinking oaths somewhere under Sefa's feet; the whole Garden groaning like a ship in weather since the grafting rite had begun. Every few minutes a tremor ran up through the terraces, and somewhere in the hedges thorns split their own stems to bloom black flowers out of season. The air tasted of sap and iron.

Sefa crouched beside the nearest prisoner. He didn't flinch. That was the horror of them—they didn't flinch at anything, because flinching required a self worth protecting.

"You can hear me," she said. Not a question. "I know you can. Your ears work. It's only the rest of you they took."

Nothing. His breathing kept its drill-time rhythm.

"I'm going to tell you a thing," she went on, keeping her voice low and even, the voice she used on frightened factors and stubborn debtors. "A thing about how they make you. I know because they tried it on me first, when I was small enough to fit in a harvest crate. They don't cut the name out. Do you understand? They can't. A name isn't stored anywhere they can reach. What they do is they teach you to stop answering to it. They starve the answer until it dies of quiet."

His eyes flickered. Barely. A fish turning under ice.

"There," Sefa said softly. "There you are."

* * *

She worked along the row of them, kneeling in the wet grass, speaking to each in turn—not the same words, never the same words, because a name that could be triggered by a phrase was just another chain, and she refused to build chains. Instead she gave each one a different door.

To the second: *"You had a mother once. Someone taught you to tie your boots. Whose hands?"*

To the third: *"Before the Guild bought you, you were paid in something. Not coin. What did you love enough to fight for, before they made fighting your only weather?"*

To the fourth, a big man with a scarred jaw: *"Rain. You like rain. Why do you like rain? Nobody likes rain without a reason."*

Most of them gave her nothing. Their breathing stayed metronome-steady, and their eyes slid off her questions like rain off waxed canvas, and Sefa felt the cold turn over in her chest again, because she recognized that sliding. She had done it herself, for years, whenever anyone asked about Ilvane. The mind protecting its wound by pretending there was no flesh beneath.

But the fifth one—the small one, wiry, with a crooked nose that had clearly been broken and badly set by someone who cared whether it healed straight—flinched.

Actually flinched. A full-body twitch, like a horse bothered by flies.

Sefa moved closer, ignoring the tremor in her wounded shoulder. "That hurt," she observed. "Good. Pain means there's someone home to feel it."

"Stop," he said. His voice came out rusted, unused, a hinge in a house long shut up. "Talking. Stop."

"Why?"

"Because—" His jaw worked. Sweat beaded at his hairline despite the cool green shade. "Because it doesn't belong to me."

"What doesn't?"

"The—" He swallowed. His chained hands turned over, palms up, an unconscious gesture of supplication that no Hollow Man should have been capable of. "The wanting. To answer. We're not supposed to want things. Wanting is how they find the cracks."

Sefa sat back on her heels and let the silence stretch, and in the silence she heard, distantly, the tree humming under the earth—a sound like a choir heard through a wall, like every oath ever spoken into steel all sounding at once. The Garden was waking. Whatever Corvane was doing below, he was doing it faster now.

"What's your name?" she asked quietly.

He shook his head. But his lips moved, shaping something, refusing sound.

"Say it," she said. "Not for me. For you. A name is the one thing they can't confiscate if you keep saying it. Believe me. I've been holding mine in my teeth for twenty years."

* * *

He wept before he spoke. Silently, horribly, the way men weep who have forgotten how—it came out of him in shudders without tears at first, and then with tears, great ugly ones, and Sefa knelt there in the ruined garden of a dead god's estate and put her hand on his shoulder and let him, because someone had once failed to do that for her.

"Brac," he said at last. Just the word. Just the weight of it leaving him.

"Brac," she repeated.

And the world changed—or rather, he changed, and the world merely noticed. It was not dramatic. There was no light, no thunder. What happened was that his spine straightened. His breathing fell out of unison with the other eight. His eyes, which had been drifting like a man reading nothing, fixed on her face and *stayed*, and behind them something kindled that had been banked coals for God knew how many years.

"They told us we volunteered," he said. His voice was stronger now, finding its floors. "After. They told us we chose the hollowing. That it was an honor. That men who remember too much are dangerous to the people who pay them." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, a laugh with nothing funny anywhere near it. "I had a farm. Did you know that? Up in the Terraces above Vail. Apples. My wife pressed cider every autumn and the whole valley smelled like—"

He stopped. His face did something complicated.

"It's gone," he said. "Her face is gone. But the smell isn't. They couldn't take the smell."

"No," Sefa said. "They never can. That's the flaw in their method. They take the ledger entries and leave the margins." She gripped his shoulder, hard, through the Guild-gray armor. "Brac. Listen to me. Corvane is below with your mistress's enemy, grafting himself onto a tree that eats oaths, and when he finishes, every sword in the world wakes up and the war starts again, and villages like yours—like mine—become harvest fields. Again. You were made into a weapon to protect that future. I'm asking you to be a weapon to end it."

"I'm chained."

"I know a woman," Sefa said, "who speaks to locks."

* * *

It was not quite that simple, but it was nearly that simple, which frightened Sefa more than complexity would have. Because Brac, once turned, did not need convincing—he needed only direction, and the moment she gave it, he became terrifyingly efficient. He called to his fellows by their drill-numbers, then caught himself, and did something better: he said his own name aloud, over and over, deliberately, the way you'd strike flint.

"Brac. Brac. Brac."

And the fourth one—the big man, the one who liked rain—broke. Then the seventh. The second fought it, actually fought it, thrashing against his chains and moaning *no, no, we chose this, we chose this,* until Brac leaned close and said, "We chose nothing. I remember the white room. Don't you remember the white room?"—and the man went still, and then he wept, and then he was a person again, or near enough.

Five of nine. Five names recovered in the space of an hour, in a garden that was convulsing around them, while somewhere below the tree drank deeper and deeper.

Three remained hollow. Truly hollow. Sefa knelt before the last of these—a young one, barely older than the girl she herself had been in the white room—and spoke every door she knew, and got nothing back, and finally sat in the grass and admitted defeat to that one face, which regarded her with the mild patience of furniture.

"We'll bring them anyway," Brac said behind her. He had gotten his hands free—Jude's trick with the lock had traveled, passed mouth to mouth among the waking men, a piece of swordspeech borrowed secondhand, and Sefa noted with a strange pride how eagerly they'd learned it. "Maybe the names come back. Maybe they don't. Either way they walk out of here as witnesses, not instruments."

"And Corvane?" Sefa asked, standing. Her shoulder screamed. She ignored it. "What does he do when his hounds come home with teeth?"

Brac looked at her, and in his face she saw the whole terrible arithmetic of the hollowed man restored: grief and rage and purpose, all of it arriving at once, none of it sorted yet.

"He'll do what handlers always do," Brac said. "He'll call us by our numbers. And for the first time in years, nobody will answer."

* * *

They armed themselves from the fallen—Guild steel, anchor-chains repurposed as binding line, a pair of short blades that hummed faintly and uneasily in the Garden's charged air, as if they sensed the argument going on beneath the ground about whether swords ought to sleep or wake. Sefa took one and hated the feel of it and took it anyway.

Above the terraces, the sky had gone strange. Green-gold, storm-lit without clouds, the color of the inside of a leaf. Every hedge was blooming black. The topiary golems had stopped moving entirely, frozen mid-stride like the petrified soldiers in the Marches, as though the Garden itself were holding its breath.

And under it all, the humming rose. Sefa felt it in her fillings, in the scar tissue of her shoulder, in the memory of the white room. Somewhere below, Corvane was becoming something that had never existed before, and the world's every edge was sharpening in sympathy.

"Which way down?" Brac asked.

Sefa closed her eyes and listened—not with her ears, but with the part of her that had survived the harvesters, the part that had spent twenty years reading ledgers for the lie hidden in honest-looking columns. Power had a sound. Corruption had a sound. And directly ahead, past the frozen golems, past the trellis where Jude had lost her sword and won a stranger victory, a stairway spiraled down into the dark between the roots, and from it came a chorus of stolen voices arguing with themselves in Corvane's single throat.

"That way," she said.

Brac shouldered his chain. Around him, the five named men and three unnamed ones formed up without being ordered—soldiers choosing, for the first time in their hollow lives, their own formation—and Sefa Ilvane, factor of the Meridian Guild, war-child of Ilvane village, keeper of twenty years of held breath, drew the humming blade and started down.

Behind her, in the grass of the outer court, the last hollow man sat unmoving among his brothers' abandoned chains.

But his lips were moving.

Shaping something.

Almost a name.
