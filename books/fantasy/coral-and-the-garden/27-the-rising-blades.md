## 27. The Rising Blades

The first thing Jude felt was not sound but pressure—a hum in the stone itself, rising through the floor of her cell like water climbing a wick. It came up through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her ankles, and settled behind her teeth with a taste like iron and rain.

She had been counting. That was the habit of a decade: when the dark pressed close, she counted. She had counted the drips that seeped through the vault's ceiling—forty-one between the last torch-gutter and the last—and she had counted the roots that had grown through the mortar since Corvane's men had thrown her down here, pale and blind as grubs, drinking the damp. She had counted the hours by the rhythm of the tree above, which breathed. She was almost certain it breathed.

Then the count broke.

The hum swelled, and every root in the walls shivered at once, and somewhere far above—through six feet of earth and stone and the tangled heartwood of the oath-tree—the world rang like a struck bell.

Jude stood. Her chains were old Garden-work, grown rather than forged, and they had been quiet for two days, sullen, half-asleep. Now they woke. The links tightened against her wrists—not to strangle, she understood dimly, but to *listen*—and the metal began, very softly, to sing.

"Oh no," she whispered. "Oh, you shouldn't."

But they should, and they did. Somewhere above her, Corvane was beginning his graft. She could feel it the way a sailor feels a storm over the horizon before the clouds show: a wrongness in the air, a pulling, a great mouth opening beneath the world. And the tree—the tree that ate oaths, the tree grown from the first swordspeaker's grave—was answering him. Or answering *through* him. Every blade within a hundred miles had felt its master's voice call, and every blade was waking up.

She heard them come.

Not footsteps. A rustle, a whisper, a dry sliding scrape—hundreds of small sounds braided into one long hiss, like wind through wheat, like scales, like pages turning in a ledger no one was reading anymore. They came down the stairwell that led to her cell. They came through cracks in the stone too thin for any living thing. They came up through the earth itself, drawn along the roots, and they filled the corridor outside her door with a cold silver light that flickered like moonlight on moving water.

Swords. Dozens of them. Then more than dozens. Blades without hands to hold them, drifting up the stairwell in a slow spiral, point-down, hilt-up, humming their hunger. Old blades and older ones—leaf-shaped things green with age, cavalry sabers still wearing their scabbards like shrouds, a great two-hander whose fuller was crusted black with something that had never been cleaned out. They moved the way fish move in a flooded cellar: aimless, patient, waiting for the water to tell them where to go.

And the water was telling them. Jude could hear it now, faintly, in the chorus of their hum—a single word repeated over and over in a voice made of many voices, a voice she recognized with a lurch of sickness.

Corvane's voice. Half of it his own; the rest borrowed, stolen, grafted already into his throat. *Come. Come. Come.*

They were coming to be taken up. To be wielded. To be given hands again after ten years of rust and silence, and they did not care whose hands.

Jude pressed herself back against the wall. The chains sang louder, tugging at her wrists, and she realized with a cold drop in her stomach that they were singing *to* the blades outside. Calling to them. Telling them exactly where the last swordspeaker in the world was chained.

"No," she said, and put everything she had left into the word—not the gift, just her voice, just a woman's voice cracking in a dark room. "No. Quiet. Be *quiet*."

The chains stilled. For a heartbeat the corridor went silent too, all those drifting blades hanging motionless in the silver light, listening.

Then the door of her cell burst inward, and the hound-sword came through it like a dog through a gate.

It had followed her since the inn at Halfway—since she had spoken it back from death, a dead soldier's blade reanimated by her blood and her broken oath, loyal as a hound and nearly as dangerous. She had lost it in the golems' ambush at the outer court; she had assumed it rusted or captured or wandering. But here it was, flying to her across the dark the way it always flew, and it took one look—one *sense*, whatever looking meant to a thing like it—at the chains on her wrists, and it cut them.

The blow was precise and utterly indifferent to her comfort. The chain parted an inch from her skin; the shock of it numbed her hand to the elbow. The second stroke took the ankle-chain, the third the wall-anchor, and then the sword hovered in front of her face, quivering, its edge shedding a fine dust of light, and in the back of her mind where its voice lived she felt it press against her like a large animal shoving its head under her hand: *found you found you found you found you.*

"I know," Jude said. Her eyes were burning. She would think about why later. "I know. Good boy. Stay close."

The corridor was filling. The drifting blades had resumed their slow spiral downward, and now they packed the passage wall to wall, a river of steel flowing toward her cell, toward the call, toward the only living swordspeaker in reach. Their hum had changed pitch. It was no longer a summons. It was a question, asked in a thousand rusty voices at once:

*Are you the hands? Are you the hands? Are you the hands?*

Jude looked at them—all that dead war come flooding home—and felt the old grief rise in her throat like bile. This was what Ashfall Bridge had been, she thought. This exact moment, scaled up: ten thousand blades asking ten thousand dying women the same question, and no answer anywhere except blood.

"Not me," she said. "I'm not your hands. I'm not anyone's hands ever again."

They kept coming anyway. Want does not listen.

So she did the only thing left. She stepped out of her ruined cell into the river of steel, raised her voice the way her mentor had taught her on a bridge ten years and a lifetime ago—low, level, pitched to carry through metal the way a bell's note carries through water—and she spoke.

"*Down.*"

The word hit the corridor like a dropped anchor. Every blade in sight slammed point-first into the stone floor and froze there, quivering, planted like a forest of grey saplings. The hum dropped to a sullen mutter. Dust sifted down through the silver light.

Jude stood in the middle of it, shaking, her breath smoking in the cold. The hound-sword hovered at her shoulder, and she felt its fierce, simple pride, and underneath it her own terror, because the word had cost her something. Speaking to that many blades at once, against the pull of the tree and the graft and Corvane's stolen chorus—it was like hauling a net full of live eels. Something in her chest had torn a little. She could feel it bleeding.

"Up," she told the hound-sword. "We're going up."

* * *

The stairwell was a battle fought entirely in silence and rust.

The blades she had commanded stayed down—but the stairwell went up through levels she had not spoken to, and those levels were awake. On the first landing, three sabers came off a rack of petrified bone and met her in the dark, and the hound-sword intercepted them with a shriek of steel on steel that Jude felt in her fillings. It fought the way it had died: stubbornly, joylessly, with a soldier's economy. It drove two of the sabers into the wall and held them there while Jude ran past, and then it tore free and caught up, leaving them pinned and howling silently behind her.

On the second landing, the roots got clever.

They had been drinking oaths for centuries, those roots, and they had learned what oaths tasted like. As Jude climbed, they came through the walls around her—not grabbing, not striking, but *offering*: pale tendrils curling toward her wrists and ankles, shaping themselves, horribly, into the suggestion of a chain. An invitation. *Bind yourself again,* the shape said. *Be safe. Be small. We will hold you the way you were held, gently, forever.*

"Get away from me," Jude snarled, and the hound-sword sheared through them, and the severed ends bled a sap that smelled like a burned letter.

By the third landing she was running flat-out, boots hammering ancient stone, the vault's cold giving way to a warmer, greener dark that smelled of loam and flowers rotting sweetly somewhere below. The whole Garden was convulsing around her now. Through the walls she could hear the topiary golems—those patient hedge-things bound to commands older than the Guild—groaning awake in their courts, tearing themselves loose from their beds, shearing their own branches into edges. Above her, muffled by earth and turf, came the sounds of the surface: shouts, the crack of splitting hedge, the deep bell-note of the grafting rite rolling out over everything like weather.

And everywhere, everywhere, the swords. Rising out of the ground itself, out of the graves of the Marches beyond the walls, out of cellars and wells and the mud of the Drowned Marshes for fifty miles around—every blade in the world that remembered being wielded was waking, and the nearest ones were coming *here*, and the Garden was going to war with itself: golems against roots, roots against blades, blades against anything that moved.

Chaos as consequence. Jude thought of the courier dying against the salt door with her true name in his mouth, and understood, finally, what he had been trying to say. Not *run*. Not *hide*. He had been trying to say: *it's already starting. Someone has to be there when it does.*

She burst out of the stairwell into the night.

* * *

The outer court of the Garden was unrecognizable.

Terraces that had lain sleeping for a decade were torn open. Hedges had uprooted themselves and were walking—or rather, lurching, dragging their root-balls behind them like wounded men dragging their legs, shearing their own limbs into blades as they came. Golems fought golems in the moonlight, bound by contradictory commands, tearing each other into mulch with the terrible patience of things that cannot tire. And through it all drifted the swords—hundreds of them now, rising out of the earth in glittering spirals, streaming toward the center of the Garden, toward the trellis, toward Corvane and the tree and the grafting rite that called them home.

The sky was wrong. There should have been stars. Instead there was a glow rising from the heart of the estate, gold-green and pulsing, and with every pulse Jude felt the tear in her chest widen, felt the tree's voice push harder against the inside of her skull: *come, come, come, daughter, come home, I have your name, I have your order, I have everything the oath took—*

"Liar," Jude panted, and ran.

The hound-sword flew cover above her, a bright ugly streak of violence, batting aside stray blades that dove at her from the dark. Once, a golem loomed out of the smoke directly in her path—a hedge-thing twelve feet tall, shaped like a soldier, its face a smooth blank of clipped yew—and for one frozen moment it looked down at her and she looked up at it, and she felt the old command stirring inside it, older than Corvane, older than the Guild: *guard the Garden. Keep the swordspeakers.*

Keep the swordspeakers. Not kill them. Keep them.

Its branch-arm rose. Jude spoke one word—"Stay"—and the golem stopped mid-stroke, trembling, two laws grinding against each other in its wooden heart, and she slipped between its legs while it stood there tearing itself apart with indecision.

She was halfway across the third terrace when the Hollow Men found her.

There were six of them, coming down the terrace steps in formation, nameless and smooth-faced, their armor dull as lead. They carried Guild steel—forged, not grown, the only blades in the Garden that were not singing—and they moved with the horrible unity of men who had nothing left inside them to disagree about. Corvane must have left them at the trellis as a screen. Now the rite's convulsions had shaken them loose, and they had one order left that still functioned: the map, the witness, no survivors.

The hound-sword screamed down to meet them and was beaten back—three of them together, shields locked, Guild drill against a dead man's ghost. Jude skidded to a halt on the wet grass. Behind her, the river of rising blades. Ahead, the wall of nameless men. Above, the gold-green pulse, faster now, feverish, the whole Garden groaning toward some climax she could feel in her teeth.

Her chest was bleeding somewhere she couldn't touch. Her voice was going hoarse. She had spent ten years keeping this gift locked in a box, and in one night she had spent it like a drunkard spends a fortune, and she had maybe—she counted, because counting was the habit, counting was the ledger, counting was hers—maybe four words left in her before the well ran dry.

Four words. Six Hollow Men. One trellis.

The hound-sword regrouped at her shoulder, battered, its edge nicked, its hum gone ragged. In the back of her mind it pressed its question against her, the same question the whole risen Garden was asking, the same question Ashfall Bridge had asked and she had answered with silence:

*Are you the hands?*

Jude Winterbourne looked at the nameless men advancing through the smoke, and at the burning heart of the Garden beyond them, and she thought of Sefa somewhere in that smoke—captured, bargaining, or dead—and of the courier who had died with her name in his mouth, and of her mentor on the bridge saying *swear it, swear silence, and live*—and she understood that the oath had never actually been about silence at all. It had been about this. About choosing, with a clear head and a dry well, what was worth the last of your words.

She chose.

"Three words," she told the hound-sword quietly. "Make them count."

And she lifted her voice—hoarse, cracked, the last clear coin in a spent purse—and gave the night the oldest command a swordspeaker knows, the first one taught, the one carved into the lintel of every training hall the war had burned:

"*To me.*"

Every blade within earshot turned in the air.

Hundreds of them. The river of rising steel reversed itself with a sound like the sea changing its mind, and came down the terraces in a glittering flood, past the fighting golems, over the heads of the Hollow Men—who, to their eternal credit as soldiers, did not break, but raised their shields, because they had forgotten that shields were invented for arrows and stones, and had never once in their surgically emptied lives imagined a rain of swords.

Jude walked forward through what happened next, and did not look at most of it.

When the noise ended, the terrace steps were empty, and the hound-sword hovered at her side trailing someone else's blood, and the way to the trellis lay open through the smoke—up the long central stair, toward the gold-green pulse, toward the tree, toward Corvane, who even now was rising to meet her, his silhouette framed against the rite's feverish light, his voice coming down the stairwell in a chorus of a hundred stolen throats:

"You're late, Auditor Winterbourne. The accounts are open. Come and settle them."

Jude started up the stairs, her torn chest burning, her spent voice a rasp, the hound-sword humming grimly at her shoulder—and behind her, in the dark of the ravaged court, one of the fallen Hollow Men stirred, and reached, and did not stay down.
