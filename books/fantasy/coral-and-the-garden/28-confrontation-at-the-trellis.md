## 28. Confrontation at the Trellis

The vault spat her out at the top of the world.

Jude came up through a seam in the Garden's terraces, hands bleeding from the climb, the hound-sword still humming against her back like an animal pressed close for warmth. Behind and below her, the root cellar was a throat full of light—the tree had begun to shine through its own bark, sap gone luminous, every root drinking deep on the feast Corvane was pouring into it. The whole hillside shuddered like a sleeper turning over.

Above her, cut into the crown of the Garden's highest terrace, stood the trellis.

She had seen it once before, ten years ago, in the last hour before Ashfall Bridge—described in her mentor's voice, drawn in charcoal on the back of a supply manifest because he would not say its name aloud. *They grow them there,* he'd said. *Not swordspeakers. The speech itself. On a frame of iron and bone, the way you grow grapes.* He had crossed it out afterward so hard the paper tore.

It was worse than his drawing. The trellis rose three men high, a lattice of black iron grown through—not built with, but *grown through*—with pale woody stems as thick as her wrist. Along its arms hung grafts: dozens of them, each a knuckle of scarred wood where a living branch had been spliced onto the trellis's central vine. Some were old, grey and dormant. Some were fresh, weeping amber sap that caught the burning light and threw it back in slow drops.

And at the center, lashed to the vine with chains of green wood that had grown around him as surely as any smith's iron, hung Corvane.

He was half-grafted.

Jude had imagined this moment on the long road—in the Marches, at the inn, in the dark hours when sleep refused her—and in every imagining Corvane had been himself: silver-tongued, immaculate, the auditor's calm wrapped around something rotten. She had prepared herself for his voice, his reasonableness, the terrible patience of a man reading aloud from a ledger in which your death was already a balanced entry.

She had not prepared herself for what the graft was doing to him.

His right arm had gone wooden to the shoulder. Not dead wood—*living* wood, grain swirling where muscle should be, bark fine as silk along the forearm, fingers lengthened and split into twigs that budded as she watched. The graft climbed his neck in veins of pale cambium, and where it crossed his throat, his voice—

His voice was wrong.

"You're late," said Corvane, in four voices at once.

Jude stopped at the edge of the terrace. The hound-sword slid free of her back without being asked; it knew. It had known since the vault, since the flood of awakened steel below, that they were walking toward the place it had been made to fear.

"Master Corvane," she said, and was surprised at how steady her own voice came out. One voice. Her voice. That was going to matter, she understood suddenly. That was going to be the whole of it. "You should stop. Whatever stage this is—there are stages past which it stops being you."

"It stopped being me years ago," Corvane said—or rather, the voices said, braided together, a choir speaking in imperfect unison so that each word arrived slightly frayed at the edges. "That was rather the point. Do you know whose voices these are, Winterbourne? No. Of course not. You were a child. You burned nothing. You only *signed*."

The grafts on the trellis stirred. Each knuckle of scarred wood flexed, and from each one came a whisper, faint as wind through a keyhole—a dozen, fifty, a hundred voices layered under Corvane's, and now that she listened, now that her gift reached toward them unbidden, Jude could feel what they were.

Swordspeeches. Every graft on the trellis held one. The Garden had cultivated them here for centuries—grown the speech itself like fruit, harvested it, grafted it, stored it—and Corvane had woken all of them at once and begun to take them in.

"The Guild traded in impossible things," Corvane said. "Bottled storms. Borrowed years. A city's luck by the pound. And do you know what I learned at my desk, balancing those entries? Impossible things are only difficult things that someone was willing to pay enough for. The war ended because the swordspeakers died, not because anyone wanted peace. There is demand, Winterbourne. There has always been demand. I am merely restoring supply."

Below them, distantly, the Hollow Men were shouting—Brac's voice rising above the others, and another voice answering, Sefa's, hoarse and furious—and the sound of chains parting like green wood splitting. Jude let herself hear it for exactly one breath. Sefa was alive. Sefa was fighting. Whatever happened on this terrace, that had already bought her everything.

She drew the hound-sword.

It came alive in her grip the way it always did, eager, remembering, whispering fragments of its dead wielder up through the tang and into her palm—*a bridge, rain, a woman with grey eyes saying hold, hold, hold*—and Jude spoke to it the way she had spoken to the petrified swords in the Marches, softly, in the old cadence:

"Not yet. Wait."

And the sword waited, trembling.

Corvane's remaining hand opened. Something rose out of the trellis behind him—a blade, but not one blade. It assembled itself as it came, iron vines stripping their own thorns and forging them midair into an edge, the graft-wood flowing into a hilt, and when it settled into Corvane's grip it was a sword the way the trellis was a tree: technically, monstrously, made of borrowed parts. A hundred speeches hummed in its steel. When it moved, it did not ring. It *chattered*—all those voices at once, trying to speak through metal.

"I had hoped," Corvane said, "to offer you a position. Root-keeper. Second voice. The tree likes you—it told me so, in her voice, the mentor's voice, didn't it? It knows what wounds to sell through." He smiled, and the smile split along lines of new bark. "But you've come up swinging, and honestly, Winterbourne, I find I prefer it. Negotiation is so much simpler after the other party is exhausted."

He came off the trellis fast—faster than a man should move with half a tree growing out of him, because it wasn't only half a tree anymore, it was half a *harvest*, a hundred stolen gifts feeding his limbs.

Jude met him.

The first exchange nearly took her arm off. The hound-sword rang against the chatter-blade and the sound was wrong, a chord instead of a note, dissonance that went through her teeth and into the roots of her gift and shook things loose there—memories, the oath, Ashfall Bridge, her mentor's mouth shaping the word *silence*. Corvane fought like an auditor: economically, precisely, no motion wasted, every parry converting directly into a counter. But underneath the economy there was the chorus, and the chorus was *greedy*. Each time their blades touched, she felt the chatter-blade reaching for the hound-sword's speech, trying to graft it, drink it, add its dead soldier's memories to Corvane's collection.

"It remembers you," Corvane said, delighted, terrible. "The dog. Oh, that's *lovely*, that's exactly the kind of entry the old order never bothered to keep. A sword that loves. Do you know how rare that is? How *valuable*?"

"It isn't mine to sell," Jude said, and turned his thrust with a twist that cost her the ground she'd gained, boots skidding in loam.

They fought down the length of the terrace, past topiary golems frozen mid-stride with their ancient commands half-spoken, past beds of roses gone feral and thorned, and the Garden watched them fight the way a house watches guests who have begun breaking furniture—with the patient, offended attention of something that will still be here when they're gone. The light was worsening. The tree below was shining brighter, and the brightness bled up through the soil, so that their shadows swung and doubled and the whole terrace seemed underwater.

Jude was losing.

She knew it the way she knew balances—by the arithmetic. Corvane had a hundred gifts and she had one and a borrowed sword. He did not tire; the grafts drank from the trellis with every heartbeat. She had been climbing since midnight, bleeding since the vault, and her left arm had gone numb where a thorn-vine had opened it during the ascent. He drove her back step by step, unhurried now, savoring, the chorus murmuring commentary in a dozen dead voices—*press her, press her, the little oath-keeper, the last of them, the last, the last*—

Her heel struck the trellis's base.

One of the grafts, low on the iron, brushed her shoulder. And Jude felt it *reach*.

It was like the ferryman's toll happening in reverse—a memory tugged out of her, not taken but *tasted*, the graft drinking a sip of her through the skin: rain on Ashfall Bridge, her mentor's hand heavy on her hair, the oath leaving her mouth like blood. She cried out and wrenched away, and the graft sighed after her, wanting more, and Corvane laughed—one voice laughing inside a hundred others, and the hundred others laughing too, badly, like instruments imitating a man.

"Do you feel it?" he said. "Do you understand yet? This is what the Garden was always for. Not soldiers. Not war. *Collection.* Every gift ever sworn, filed, and held. Your mentor died keeping you out of this lattice. Give me one reason his sacrifice should survive the quarter."

Jude looked at him—at the wooden arm budding in the torchlight, at the throat where the cambium pulsed with each of his words, at the man-shaped ledger into which a hundred souls had been entered and none of them balanced—and she understood, finally, what he had actually purchased.

Nothing. He had purchased nothing. He had *borrowed*.

Every graft was a debt. Every voice in him was owed to someone—to the tree that grew them, to the dead who'd first spoken them, to the Garden's oldest command, the one written into its roots before there were auditors or orders or wars. The Gardener's inscription rose in her memory: *I grew power; it grew me.*

Power grows toward whatever feeds it. That is all power knows how to do.

And somewhere beneath the grafting, beneath Corvane's cleverness and the Hollow Men and the ledgers and the war, the tree was still a plant. Still obeying the oldest instruction in the world, the one buried deeper than any oath, the first command any gardener speaks to anything—

Jude dropped the hound-sword.

It fell from her hand deliberately, ringing on the terrace stones, and Corvane's eyes flared with triumph, and the chatter-blade rose for the killing stroke, and in the half-second that remained she did not reach for a weapon, or an angle, or a trick. She reached past all of it, past her fear and her fury and her grief, down into the gift itself, and she spoke—not to Corvane, not to the blade, not even to the tree, but to the trellis, to the whole rooted architecture of stolen power, in the oldest words she knew:

"*Grow toward the sun.*"

The world changed pitch.

It was not loud. That was the terrible thing about it—how quiet it was. A single sentence in the first cadence, the gardener's cadence, older than oaths and orders and the war both, and every living thing on the terrace heard it the way roots hear water.

The trellis *turned*.

Not all at once—wood does not hurry—but with the irresistible patience of a thing doing what it was made for. The great vine shifted in its iron cradle with a groan of bending metal. The grafts flexed, all of them, a hundred knuckles of scarred wood opening toward the burning light pouring up from the tree below—and then past it, upward, toward the true sun just greying the east, and as they turned they *pulled*, and everything they had drunk came with them.

Corvane screamed.

It was a hundred screams in one, because that was what he was now, and the grafts were taking back what he had borrowed. The wooden arm lengthened, spread, flattened against the trellis and *rooted*—she saw the moment it happened, saw his own flesh accept the instruction, saw bark close over his fingers like water closing over a stone. The chatter-blade fell from a hand that was no longer entirely a hand, and hit the stones, and lay there whispering in a hundred fading voices, going quiet voice by voice as the trellis reclaimed each one.

"No—" Corvane's own voice, alone now, stripped of the choir, thin and human and horrified. "No, you can't—you don't understand what I've *built*—"

"It isn't yours," Jude said. "It never was. You wrote yourself into a ledger you don't control. I'm sorry, Master Corvane. The entry doesn't balance."

The green chains around his chest tightened—not cruelly; the Garden was never cruel, only total—and drew him back against the vine, and the grafts closed over him the way a hedge closes over a fence post. She watched his face while it happened. She made herself watch. There was fury in it, and then bargaining, and then, in the last moments before the bark reached his jaw, something worse than either: relief. The look of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he had forgotten it was heavy.

Then he was only a shape in the trellis. A graft among grafts. His eyes stayed open, and stayed human, and stayed fixed on her.

The terrace was silent except for the creak of turning wood and, far below, the dwindling sounds of battle—the Hollow Men's anchor-chains parting, Brac's voice calling names into the smoke.

Jude stood very still until her legs agreed to hold her. Then she knelt and picked up the hound-sword, which pressed against her palm like a dog leaning on its person, and looked at the chatter-blade lying spent on the stones, and at the trellis, and at the eyes in the wood.

She had won. She had won by dropping her sword, and the victory sat strangely in her chest—heavier than any blow she had landed in ten years of hiding. Force had failed in under a hundred heartbeats. Surrender had done in one word what a decade of oaths could not.

Somewhere below, the Garden groaned, and the light from the root cellar began to change—from the bright gold of feasting to something redder, angrier, as the tree understood that its harvest was being called home.

The eyes in the trellis blinked. Slowly. Deliberately. And though no sound came out, Jude read his lips as easily as an entry in a ledger, and what Corvane mouthed, with the last of his human breath, was:

*Ask me why.*
