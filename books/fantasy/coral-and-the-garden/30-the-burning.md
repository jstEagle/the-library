## 30. The Burning

The fire started small, because all true things do.

Jude knelt at the edge of the root-vault with a lantern in one hand and a flint in the other, and she did not strike them together. Not yet. The tree filled the chamber the way a voice fills a throat—everywhere at once, from the inside—and its roots ran up through the soil like veins through a wrist, pale and thick and drinking. She could feel it drinking even now. Even after Corvane's fall. Even with the grafting trellis shattered above and his chorus of stolen voices scattered into silence, the tree went on pulling oaths out of the dark earth as patiently as it had for a thousand years.

*You could stop,* it said to her, in her mentor's voice, in her own voice, in voices she had no names for. *You could simply stop.*

"I know," Jude said aloud. Her voice sounded very small in so large a dark. "That's what frightens me."

She thought of Ashfall Bridge. She thought of ten years of ledgers, of columns of numbers that added up because she made them add up, of the humming under her skin that she had starved and starved and starved until it became less a gift than a scar that ached when rain was coming. She thought of the courier dying against the salt door with her name—her true name—in his mouth, spending his last breath on a stranger because someone had to carry the truth past the walls of the world.

She struck the flint.

The sparks caught on oil-soaked rags she'd gathered from the gardener's stores, and flame stood up along the nearest root like a soldier coming to attention. The tree did not scream. That was the worst of it. It only sighed, long and green and deep, the way a forest sighs when wind moves through it, and the fire took hold and began to climb.

Jude climbed too.

* * *

Above, the Garden was already at war with itself.

The topiary golems had been bound by old commands, and old commands were unraveling with every root that burned. Some stood motionless in the terraced walks, yew shapes of hounds and herons and helmeted men, waiting for orders that would never come again. Others had torn themselves loose of their beds and wandered, dragging roots behind them like entrails, toppling into the reflecting pools with sounds like drowning giants. The air smelled of cut green and hot resin and something older underneath—the smell of a library burning, if libraries grew instead of being built.

Sefa was where Jude had left her, propped against the low wall of the outer court with Brac standing over her and three other Hollow Men—no, not hollow anymore, not since Sefa had spoken their names back into them—arrayed around the courtyard like a garrison of one and a half souls. Her shoulder was bound, badly, with strips of Corvane's fine gray coat. Her face was gray beneath the brown, but her eyes found Jude the moment Jude came up the stair, sharp as an auditor finding a misfiled entry.

"You're alive," Sefa said. "I had it down as fifty-fifty."

"The tree didn't want me dead." Jude crossed the court, stepping over a fallen golem's arm, and crouched beside her. "It wanted me rooted."

"And?"

"And I set it on fire."

Something moved across Sefa's face—not surprise; Sefa did not waste surprise—but something adjacent to it. Respect, maybe, or grief wearing respect's clothes. "Corvane spent thirty years building toward this place," she said. "And you burned it in an afternoon."

"He spent thirty years wanting to matter." Jude looked out over the terraces, at the smoke beginning to thread up between the hedges, thin white ribbons against a bruised evening sky. "I just wanted it to end."

Brac shifted at the wall. He was a big man gone soft-eyed since he'd remembered his name, and he watched the rising smoke the way a man watches a funeral pyre for someone he never liked but cannot quite hate. "The swords," he said. His voice was rough, unpracticed; he was still learning to use it for anything but obedience. "In the cities. Did they stop?"

Jude reached inward, to the place where the humming lived.

It was still there. That was the thing she had not let herself think about in the vault, striking the flint, watching the fire climb: the gift had not died with Corvane's rite. The tree had poured itself into the grafting, and the grafting had broken, and all that power had gone somewhere—into the air, into the ground, into every blade in the world that had risen singing from its sheath and then, confused, lain back down. And some fraction of it, Jude knew, had pooled in her. The last vessel standing. The last oath-bound throat the old words still fit.

"It's quiet now," she said. It wasn't a lie. The blades had stilled. But the humming under her skin was louder than it had been in ten years, a full choir where there had been one thin note, and they both knew what she wasn't saying.

Sefa knew. Sefa always knew.

"You felt it," Sefa said quietly. "When you spoke the tree's command. *Grow toward the sun.*" She said the words carefully, the way one handles a borrowed knife. "You didn't lose anything doing it. You gained."

Jude didn't answer. In the middle distance, a hedge-row caught, and flame walked along it in a bright line, and the heat came across the terrace like a hand laid flat against her face.

* * *

They should have run then. Later, walking the ash-roads, Jude would understand that everything that happened next happened because none of them ran—because the fire was slow and the Garden was vast and the night was warm, and because the tree had one more thing to say.

It came for her at the heart of the outer court, where the first rose had been planted a thousand years ago and where, even now, a single bush bloomed black-thorned and stubborn among the spreading smoke. The voice arrived not through her ears but through the humming, rising up her spine like water finding a level, and it wore her mentor's voice one last time because the tree was not cruel, exactly—it was efficient, and efficiency meant using whatever key opened the lock.

*Jude.*

She was alone at the wall. Sefa had gone with Brac to see to the horses; the others were dousing embers along the eastern walk, keeping their escape road clear. The firelight made the whole court flicker like a page turning.

"I'm here," Jude said. "Though I don't know why I'm humoring you."

*Because you want to hear it.* A pause, and then, gently, terribly: *One gift remains. Mine. Freely given, or freely refused—I am past compelling anyone. You have seen to that.*

The black rose bush shivered though there was no wind. Its petals opened, one by one, and each petal held a small light, and the lights arranged themselves into a shape Jude recognized: a door. A threshold. The kind of choice that is only offered once.

*Take root,* the tree said. *Here, in my ashes, or in any soil you choose—you carry me now, all of me, every word I ever grew. Take root and live centuries. Speak and the world will speak back. Rebuild your order. Raise swordspeakers who are not bound by silence, not broken by war, not taught to hide. You saw what Corvane wanted to make of me. Make something better. Or—*

The petals closed.

*—walk away. Stay human. Stay brief. Let the gift die in the burning with everything else, and be Jude Winterbourne, auditor, for forty more years, and die in a bed with honest ledgers on the shelf beside her. There is no wrong answer. That is the last lesson I have to teach, and I learned it too late to use it myself: power that must be taken corrupts. Power that is offered can only be answered.*

The fire roared somewhere below, and the whole terrace trembled, and Jude put her hand flat against the warm stone and tried to feel the size of the thing being asked of her.

Centuries. She tried to imagine centuries. Watching everyone she knew grow old and fold into the ground while she stayed green. Watching kingdoms rise on the memory of a war she'd ended, and remembering it better than anyone alive, and being unable to die of the remembering. She thought of the girl she had been at Ashfall Bridge, seventeen and screaming, holding her mentor while the life went out of him, swearing silence because silence was the only coin left in her purse. She thought of ten years of pretending the humming was nothing. She thought of how good it had felt—one moment, one terrible golden moment—to speak a gate open and feel the world obey.

And she thought of the ledger. Her ledger. The empty one she'd seen in Thornwall's vision, the deepest want the brambles had pulled out of her: not power, not restoration, not her order raised again in marble and gold. Just a clean page. An account that balanced. A life whose sums she could defend.

"No," Jude said.

The word came out steady, and that surprised her. She had expected it to cost more.

"I won't take root. I won't rebuild it. Not gentler, not wiser, not better." She stood, and the heat pressed at her back, and she turned to face the burning heart of the Garden with her chin up the way she imagined her mentor would have wanted, though he'd have told her to stop posturing and mind her footing. "You grew power, and it grew you, and your maker knew it before either of you did—that's why he wrote *burn what I planted* on his own grave. I'm not going to spend centuries being cleverer than everyone about a thing that ate a thousand years of gardeners. I'm going to be a woman. For as long as that lasts."

*Even,* the tree said, and its voice was fading now, going thin and green and far away, *if the gift dies with me? Even if you stand in the ash tomorrow and reach for the words and find nothing there? Ever?*

"Yes."

*Then take this,* it said, *the only inheritance worth leaving: the knowing of what you refused.*

The black rose bush bloomed one final time—all its petals at once, all its little lights—and the light went up like a flock of birds released, streaming into the smoke, into the sky, into every sword in every city in the world, and Jude felt the humming under her skin swell to a crescendo and then, softly, like a singer finishing a phrase, begin to fade.

Not gone. Fading. Like a bell after it's been struck, ringing down and down toward silence, and she understood with perfect clarity that by morning it would be gone entirely, and that this was the price, and that she had named it herself, and that naming it did not make it hurt less.

She sat down on the warm stone and let herself cry—not the way she'd cried at Ashfall Bridge, seventeen and broken, but the way you cry at a funeral for someone you loved who suffered a long time and is finally, finally done.

* * *

Sefa found her there when the eastern sky had gone the color of iron.

"You're sitting in a burning garden," Sefa said, "alone, in the dark, crying. I want it noted that I considered several explanations before settling on the obvious one."

"She offered me everything." Jude wiped her face with the heel of her hand. Her voice came out scraped raw. "Everything the oath took. Everything the war took. Centuries of it."

"And you said no."

"I said no."

Sefa lowered herself onto the wall beside her, wincing at her bound shoulder, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below them the Garden burned in earnest now, terraces collapsing inward, the great hedges going up like green thunder, and the heat on their faces was enormous and almost gentle, the way a forge is gentle to the thing being forged.

"Corvane would have said yes," Sefa said at last. "To the tree. To all of it."

"I know."

"That's the difference, isn't it. Not strength. Not goodness, even. Just—" she searched for the word, and found it, because Sefa always found the word— "*appetite*. He was hungry, and you weren't. Not for that."

Jude looked at her hands. They were ordinary hands, ink-stained at the fingertips, a burn blister rising on the left palm from the lantern. By tomorrow they would be only that. Only a counting-house clerk's hands, forever.

"There's something else," she said. "By morning, I'll be—"

"Don't." Sefa's voice cracked like a whip, and then softened, which was rarer and more frightening. "Don't tell me like it's a confession. Tell me like it's a fact we're putting in the ledger. *Item: the gift dies with the Garden. Item: Jude Winterbourne walks out human.* Signed, witnessed, done." She paused. "Because that's what you chose. And I watched you choose it. And in thirty years of reading people for the Guild, it's the only time I've ever seen anyone refuse the big prize and mean it."

The fire sighed. Somewhere in the collapsing vaults, the tree's last roots gave way with a sound like a ship breaking up, and a column of sparks went up through the broken roof of the world and scattered east on the wind, over the Marches, toward home.

"We should go," Jude said. Her voice was steadier than she felt. "Before the fire takes the causeway."

"In a moment." Sefa was watching the blaze with an expression Jude had never seen on her—not the auditor's cool assessment, not the war-child's flat watchfulness, but something young and unguarded, a girl looking at the thing that had eaten her village finally, finally burning. "My brother used to say fires like this sound like the sea," she said quietly. "He was seven. He was wrong about most things, but he was right about that."

Jude listened. Under the roar and crackle, under the groan of falling stone, there it was: a long rhythmic breathing, wave after wave after wave, the sound of something vast letting go.

She stood, and held out her hand, and Sefa took it, and together they turned their backs on the Garden of swordspeech, on the thousand years of grown power, on the gift that was even now draining out of Jude's blood like tide off a sandbar—leaving her, strand by strand, only herself.

Behind them, the last of the black roses opened in the heat, and burned, and were beautiful, and were gone.

Ahead, down the long causeway through the smoking Marches, the road home waited—forty leagues of ash and petrified soldiers and a wounded friend leaning on her arm, and Jude Winterbourne, for the first time in ten years, entirely, irrevocably, ordinary.

She took the first step anyway.

The humming sang once, faintly, like a farewell—and then the dawn came up gray over the Drowned Marches, and the fire behind them spoke its long sea-sound, and the two women walked out of the burning Garden into whatever the world would be without it.
