## 32. The Long Road Home

The Marches smelled different on the way back.

Sefa noticed it first at the edge of the sunken causeway, where the water went from brown to the color of old glass. Coming in, the whole country had reeked of rust and rot — the iron stink of ten thousand dead swords, the sweet-rot of peat eating through leather and bone. Now there was something else under it. Rain-washed stone. Green things starting where green things had no business being.

She didn't trust it. She had been a war-child; she knew that quiet was not the same as peace. Quiet was what came before.

But Brac walked beside her with his hands loose at his sides — actually loose, not held in that coiled readiness the Hollow Men wore like a second skin — and behind them, eleven more of the nameless walked in something that was almost formation but not quite, because formations were for soldiers and these men were trying, haltingly, to be something else. A few of them had started saying their names aloud each morning when they woke. Brac had begun it, standing up out of the wet grass and saying *Brac* to the grey sky like a man testing whether ice would hold. Now they all did it. Some of the names were short and ugly. One man's name turned out to be Pellow, and he wept for an hour the first time he said it, and after that he said it constantly, to everyone, until Sefa wanted to strangle him, and then one evening she caught herself smiling when he did, and decided strangling could wait.

*Pellow,* she thought now, watching him pick his way along the causeway stones. *His name is Pellow.*

It still felt like a kind of magic, that. Cheaper than swordspeech. More durable.

"You're doing it again," Brac said.

"Doing what."

"Counting us." He nodded at her eyes, which had been moving down the line without her permission. "You count like a factor. Left to right. You'll get a total soon."

"I always get a total," Sefa said. "Twelve of you. Two of us. One mule who hates all of us equally. That's the sum of everything I have left in the world, and half of it is a mule."

"The mule carried your pack over the ferry," Brac said gravely. "I'd count the mule as an asset."

The ferryman had taken memories from them both — Sefa's brother, Jude's mentor — and Sefa had spent three days afterward reaching for Tam's face and finding only the shape where it had been, like a hand pressed into sand after the tide. She'd told herself she would remember the important things. His laugh, which had been too loud. The way he'd cheated at cards and been terrible at hiding it. She remembered those. It was his face that was gone, his actual face, and some mornings she woke furious about it and some mornings she understood that this was the toll, that grief with no face attached was just weight, and weight could be carried.

Jude hadn't spoken about hers. Not once. But Sefa had watched her at the inn fire, watching her own hands as if checking whether someone else's fingers had been left there instead.

* * *

They passed the first of the crumbling soldiers near midday.

Sefa heard it before she saw it: a sound like a long exhale, mineral and soft, and then a section of the marsh mist shifted and she saw a figure that had stood petrified since the war lean forward — slowly, so slowly, a fall that had taken a decade to begin — and come apart. Stone flaked from stone. The raised sword dropped first, breaking into rust-red pieces on the water, and then the shoulders, the helm, the chest with its carved relief of a screaming mouth. By the time their column reached the place, there was nothing left but a mound of reddish gravel with sedge already growing through it, and the water lapping at it gently, the way water does, indifferent and patient both.

The Hollow Men stopped. All twelve of them, in a line, looking at the mound.

"We fought here," one of them said. He was called Aldous; his name had come back two days ago and he still pronounced it carefully, like a foreign word. "Didn't we? Some of this ground. I don't — I can't—"

"They cut the battles out with the rest," Brac said quietly. "Names first. Then faces. Then the reasons. The reasons go last, because the reasons are the biggest piece."

"I know I bled here."

"You know you bled here," Brac agreed. "That's yours. They couldn't take what was in the body. Only what was in the ledgers."

Sefa looked at the mound a moment longer. Ten years ago, riding the supply roads with her village's tithe-cart, she had passed soldiers like this one by the hundreds — men and women turned to standing stone mid-swing, the war's last argument frozen in salt-marsh. Her village had fed the Garden's first harvest; she had grown up knowing, in the wordless way children know things, that the statues were not monuments. They were crops that had failed. Or maybe crops that had succeeded, depending on whose ledger you read.

And now they were falling apart. Peacefully. The word kept coming back to her — peacefully — because there was no other word for the way they crumbled. No violence in it. The Garden was dead, burned down to its root-vault by a woman with honest ledgers and a gift she'd given away, and every sword the Garden had ever grown was letting go at its own pace, like a fist unclenching.

"Move," Sefa said, but softly, and the column moved.

* * *

They made camp that night on a rise above the drowned orchard — rows of petrified apple trees standing knee-deep in black water, their branches heavy with stone fruit that had never rotted because it had never ripened. The Hollow Men built fires with the competence of men who had done nothing else for years, and Pellow produced a string of dried fish from somewhere and began distributing it with the solemnity of a priest, saying everyone's name as he handed over each fish. *Brac. Aldous. Pellow. Maren.* The woman called Maren took her fish and said *thank you, Pellow,* and he glowed like a struck match.

Sefa sat apart, as she usually did, sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen. It was how she thought. Jude had teased her about it exactly once, early on, before either of them trusted the other, and Sefa had told her that a knife was the only conversation partner that never lied, and Jude had gone quiet in that particular way she had — the way of a woman hearing an echo of something she used to believe.

Footsteps in the grass. She didn't look up. There were only fourteen people in the world whose footsteps she needed to know, and she knew all of them.

"He counts them too," Sefa said. "Brac. Every night. You've seen it."

"I've seen it." Jude settled onto the grass beside her, cross-legged, forearms on knees. In the firelight her face was thinner than it had been in the counting-house, a lifetime ago. There was grey coming into her hair that Sefa suspected hadn't been there before the vault. "He counts them the way you'd audit a strongroom. Making sure nothing's missing."

"Nothing is missing. That's what frightens him."

"Yes." Jude was quiet a moment. "He told me. This afternoon. That he keeps waiting for Corvane to have left something in them — a hook, a command, a ledger-entry that comes due — and every morning it isn't there, and every morning he doesn't quite believe it."

"And do you believe it?"

"I'm learning to." Jude turned a small stick over in her fingers, snapped it, tossed the halves away. "It's strange. I spent ten years believing that anything given freely has a price hidden in it. The Guild taught me that. My oaths taught me that. And now—" she gestured, vaguely, at the fires, the men, the crumbling orchard, the whole ruined country letting itself down gently into rest, "—now I keep finding things that were just... given. And I don't know where to file them."

Sefa snorted. "In the impossible column."

"There isn't an impossible column anymore. We voted."

"There's always an impossible column. You just write it honestly now." Sefa set the knife down. Below them, Pellow had gotten Maren to laugh at something — actually laugh, a rough unused sound — and several of the others had turned toward it the way cold hands turn toward a fire. "Look at them. A month ago they were weapons with legs. Now they're going to arrive in the city expecting to be people, and the city is going to take one look at twelve Hollow Men walking up the Salt Road and start building barricades."

"Then we arrive with them," Jude said. "All the way to the gates. Let them see who walks escort."

"That's not strategy. That's a statement."

"I've had enough strategy to last me," Jude said. "Corvane had strategy. Look where his statement got him."

Sefa didn't answer right away. She found she was turning something over — not the knife this time, an older thing. She had bargained with Corvane. She had offered the Garden's secrets for her life, and then she had cut the anchor-chains anyway and let the bargain burn, and she had been wounded for it, and captured, and she still could not say — even to herself, even in the privacy of her own skull where no auditor could read it — whether she had chosen truth or simply chosen Jude. The difference mattered. She was a factor. Factors are supposed to know what they're trading and why.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked. "The speech. You gave it up in the fire. Do you reach for it?"

Jude was silent long enough that Sefa looked over. Firelight moved on her face.

"Every day," Jude said at last. "Usually in the morning. I wake up and there's a silence under my skin where the humming used to be, and for about four breaths I grieve it like a limb. And then—" she smiled, small and crooked, "—and then I get up, and I'm only a woman, and nobody in the world can make me do a war. I've decided the trade was fair. Ask me again some winter."

"I'll ask again every winter," Sefa said. "Consider it entered in the ledger."

* * *

Two days later they crossed the field where Jude had woken the dead sword.

Sefa felt the change before she understood it. The Hollow Men slowed, and Brac stopped altogether, staring at a stretch of marsh grass that grew taller and greener than anywhere else for a hundred paces around — a scar of fertility, a wound healing too well. At the center of it lay a sword.

Or rather, a sword-shaped drift of rust. When Jude knelt and brushed the grass aside, it came apart at her touch, flakes of red drifting on the wind like the world's smallest funeral. Whatever had animated it — whatever fragment of its last wielder had ridden it out of the Garden and across the Marches like a loyal hound — had gone home when the Garden burned. There was nothing left to bury. Jude buried the rust anyway, scooping a hollow in the soft ground with her hands, and the Hollow Men stood around her in a rough ring, and Pellow said all their names aloud, one by one, like a roll call for a soldier nobody else remembered.

Afterward Jude washed her hands in a pool and didn't speak for hours, and Sefa rode the mule alongside her and let the silence be, because she was learning — slowly, against her own grain — that some accounts should be allowed to close themselves.

* * *

On the ninth day they reached the last of the petrified companies, and this was the one Sefa had been dreading.

She had seen them on the way in: a full rank of thirty soldiers, stone-still on the crest of a flooded levee, frozen in the instant before a charge. Their captain stood at the center with her arm flung forward, mouth open on a shout that had lasted ten years. Sefa had looked at them on the way south and thought: *that's what we're marching toward. That's what waiting at the end of every oath looks like.*

Now, as the column climbed the levee, the captain was already falling.

Not violently. She leaned into her own gesture, the outflung arm sinking like a branch heavy with fruit, and as the stone shed away from her Sefa saw — everyone saw — that beneath the stone the woman was only salt and dust, and the dust was lifting on the marsh wind in fine bright spirals, catching the morning light. The shout finally ended. It sounded almost like a sigh.

The Hollow Men halted and stood at attention, unbidden, all twelve of them, an honor guard for a woman who had died before most of them were born. Brac saluted. After a moment, awkwardly, Aldous did too. Then Maren. Then Pellow, fish-string and all.

Sefa found herself thinking of her brother again — of the shape where his face had been — and of her village, which had fed the Garden's first harvest and been paid in ash, and of the fact that she had spent her whole life assuming the world ran on ledgers because ledgers were cruel and she preferred a cruelty she could read. But here was a cruelty ending. Here were thirty soldiers going home as dust on a wind, unpaid, unmourned, unharnessed, and it looked — she searched for the word and found it and distrusted it and used it anyway —

It looked like forgiveness. Given by no one. Asked of no one. Just the war, at last, putting itself down.

"Factor Ilvane." Brac's voice, at her shoulder. "You're crying."

"I'm auditing," Sefa said. "It's very similar."

He didn't smile, because Brac rarely smiled, but the corner of his mouth moved, and that from Brac was worth a purse of silver.

* * *

They came out of the Marches on the twelfth day, onto hard road and rising ground, and the city appeared in the late afternoon haze the way it always did — a grey animal crouched at the river mouth, smoke rising off its back like heat off a hide. Somewhere in there was the Meridian Guild, its counting-house, its salt door, its council chambers where masters who had never bled for a single entry in their books were about to learn what honesty cost.

Somewhere in there, too, was a girl with a humming kitchen knife, though none of them knew that yet.

The Hollow Men drew together at the sight of the walls. Sefa watched them do it — watched twelve men and women who had spent years as instruments remember, all at once, that cities had opinions about instruments. Pellow's hand went to his empty belt. Maren stepped closer to Aldous without seeming to notice she'd done it.

"They'll ask what we are," Brac said.

"They'll ask," Sefa agreed. "And you'll tell them the truth, because you've got one now, and it's heavier than any lie you ever carried." She mounted the mule — the asset, the indispensable, the one creature in the party who had never once pretended to be anything but what it was — and looked down the line of them: the freed, the nameless-no-longer, the unlikely escort, and ahead of them on foot, walking with a swordspeaker's straight back and an honest ledger's light step, Jude Winterbourne, last of her order and first of something new.

"Well then," Sefa said. "Column of march. We've a Guild to reckon, and I intend to present the bill personally."

They started down the Salt Road toward the gates, fourteen travelers and one mule, and behind them the Marches went on crumbling gently into peace, and ahead of them — though Sefa could not yet have said how she knew — something was already waiting: a rumor running ahead of them through the factors' halls, a whisper of chains cut and a garden burned, gathering names and numbers the way rumors do.

By the time they reached the walls, the Guild would already know they were coming.
