## 33. The Guild Reckons

The city smelled of salt and woodsmoke when Jude came home, and she stood a long while at the top of the Tidewalk stairs just breathing it in, as if the air itself were a ledger she needed to reconcile before she could go further.

She had left this place as a fugitive with a stolen map pressed against her ribs. She returned carrying Sefa on her back for the last three miles of road, because Sefa's wound had opened again at the Marches' edge and she had refused—flatly, furiously, in the voice of a woman who had bargained with ferry-men and out-thought an auditor—to be carried like luggage until her legs gave out beneath her. Behind them, at a respectful distance that was not quite respect, walked Brac and eleven other Hollow Men who were no longer hollow, each of them blinking at the world like men waking from a long dream they could not fully remember.

Jude's gift was gone. She had felt it leave in the burning, a warmth drawn out through her palms like thread from a spool, and what remained was only Jude Winterbourne: tired, singed, forty-one years old, with ink stains that would never again hum.

It was, she discovered over the long road home, enough. It was more than enough. But enough did not mean finished.

* * *

They came to the Salt Door at closing bell, because there was a symmetry in that which Jude refused to resist. The same door against which a dying courier had collapsed and pressed a wax-sealed map into her hands. The door where her invisibility had ended. She stood before it now with the master seal of the Meridian Guild in a pouch at her hip, heavy as a headstone, and knocked.

The porter who answered was a boy she didn't know—young, nervous, with flour still on his sleeves from some errand—and he looked at the three ragged travelers and the twelve armed ones behind them and said, "The Guild is closed."

"Then open it," Jude said, "because I'm bringing it back."

He did not understand. Of course he didn't. But old Marta Voss, who kept the ground-floor counting room and had once taught Jude how to spot a forged bill of lading by the weight of its wax alone, came shuffling down the corridor behind him, squinting, and then stopped as though she'd walked into glass.

"Winterbourne," she said.

"Marta."

"They logged you dead." Marta's voice was flat with shock, but her eyes were already moving—counting, always counting—the escort, the wounds, the seal-pouch. "Robbery, on the tidewalks. Master Corvane signed the entry himself."

"I know," Jude said. "I've read his handwriting. All of it. Every fraudulent line of ten years of it." She held up the pouch. "And I have the seal to prove whose desk those entries came off of. Wake the council, Marta. Tell them the auditor's ledger has come due."

Marta looked at her for a long moment—at the burn scars on her hands, the road dust, the woman named Ilvane swaying on her feet beside her—and something old and stubborn moved behind the bookkeeper's eyes, the same stubbornness that had kept honest columns in a dishonest house for thirty years.

"The council meets at dawn whether I wake them or not," Marta said. "But I'll wake them anyway. And Winterbourne—" her voice caught, and steadied, "—it's good to see your name walking around where names can see it."

* * *

The council chamber of the Meridian Guild sat above the great trading floor, a long room of dark cedar and darker windows, where seven masters sat behind a crescent table worn smooth by generations of elbows. In the old days—before, when Jude had been nothing but a competent junior accountant with steady hands—they had seemed to her like gods of arithmetic, remote and absolute. Now they looked like what they were: frightened administrators in good wool, and one of them, Master Ellery Dune, was already talking before the doors had finished opening.

"This proceeding is irregular," Dune said. "There is no scheduled hearing. There is no quorum petition filed. There is no—" He saw the seal-pouch and stopped talking so abruptly that the silence had texture.

Jude set the pouch on the crescent table and drew out the master seal: black iron, the size of a fist, stamped with the Meridian's sign—a wave crossed by a balance-beam. It was the seal that appeared on every charter, every writ of trade, every order the Guild had issued for two hundred years. It was supposed to be in Corvane's keeping, and Corvane's keeping had turned out to mean chained inside a grafting trellis in a burning garden, clutched by a man who had never been a Guildsman at all.

"Do you know what this is?" Jude asked.

"It is the property of this council," said Master Ondrell, a thin man with a voice like dry paper, "and its presence outside Guild custody constitutes—"

"Its presence *inside* Guild custody constituted treason for ten years," Jude said. "Shall we discuss custody?"

She let the silence sit. Around the crescent, six faces performed six different calculations. The seventh face belonged to Mistress Halvard, eldest of the masters, a woman who had signed Jude's apprenticeship papers a decade ago without ever learning why the girl flinched at the word *oath*, and Halvard was watching Jude with the particular stillness of someone deciding whether to bet the house.

"You were declared dead," Halvard said at last. "By the auditor. On evidence he produced himself. And you are here, alive, holding the seal he lost. Start at the beginning, factor. You have until I finish this tea."

So Jude started at the beginning, or near enough—the courier at the Salt Door, the map, the flight through the tidewalks—and then she went past the beginning into the middle, where the story got worse: the Hollow Men, the Garden, the root-vault, the tree that drank oaths, and Corvane standing among the roots with his sleeves rolled back like a surgeon, reciting from memory rites no living Guildsman should have known.

"He was never ours," she told them. "Or rather—we were always his. The Guild wasn't corrupted recently. It was founded wrong, or captured early; his papers suggest the rot goes back past any of our grandparents. He used your charters, your ledgers, your honest factors' honest work as cover for a single long transaction: the acquisition of the Garden and everything grown there. Swordspeech, harvested under Guild seal. A war, financed by your counting-house."

"That's impossible," said Ondrell.

"Yes," said Sefa Ilvane, from her chair by the wall, where she sat gray-faced with her wound bound in fresh linen. "That was rather his favorite kind of trade."

A few of the masters almost smiled. Not Dune. Dune was looking at the seal the way a man looks at a snake he'd thought was a belt.

"And your proof?" Dune asked. "You bring us a story. Stories are cheap. Where is the documentation?"

Jude had been waiting for that question the way a swordspeaker waits for the ring of steel. She unbuckled the second satchel—the heavy one, the one Brac had carried across three hundred miles of Marches without ever asking what was in it—and began setting documents on the crescent table, one by one, each squared precisely to the table's edge because some habits outlive their reasons.

Corvane's private ledger, recovered from his quarters at Halfway Inn, where he'd kept rooms under a false charter number. Inside it: payments to the Hollow Men brokers, dating back nine years. Payments to ferrymen of the Marches. A recurring entry, quarterly, labeled only *Irrigation*, in sums that mapped exactly onto the disappearance of Guild assets the council had been mourning for a decade as misfortune.

His correspondence, sealed with the old enemy order's cipher—which Master Halvard recognized on sight, going pale, because she had seen that cipher last as a girl, on the proclamations that preceded Ashfall Bridge.

And finally, thinnest and most damning: the audit trail of the courier's death. The robbery-log Corvane had filed, describing goods Jude had never reported—described in detail no one could have known unless he had known them first. His own hand. His own fraud, entered into the record like a confession he'd been too arrogant to read aloud.

"There," Jude said, tapping the robbery-log. "That page convicted me in absentia. That page resurrects me. Read it against my testimony and tell me which of us the ink supports."

Dune read. They all read. The chamber filled with the small sounds of paper and breathing and, somewhere below, the great trading floor waking up—factors arriving, bells, the murmur of a house beginning its day without knowing yet that its foundations had shifted.

Master Ondrell put down Corvane's ledger and said, carefully, "Assuming we accept this—assuming the council accepts that its own auditor was an agent of the old enemy—what is it you propose? That you be reinstated? Compensated? Honored?"

"No," Jude said.

Because that was the trap, and she had watched it close on better people than Ondrell. Power offered to the wounded looks like justice and behaves like inheritance. She had burned a garden rather than take root in it. She was not going to water a smaller one here.

"I propose the ledgers be opened," she said. "All of them. Every charter, every sealed account, every entry older than anyone can personally vouch for—audited by factors, not masters. I propose the trade in impossible things end: no bottled storms, no borrowed years, no purchases whose price cannot be written honestly in a column. The Guild trades in difficult things from tomorrow onward, or it doesn't trade at all. And I propose that the seal—" she touched the black iron, cold under her fingers, "—go somewhere it cannot be quietly picked up again. Held in trust. Split, if the law allows. A seal that one man can carry is a seal that one man will eventually steal."

"And who enforces all this?" Dune asked, and there it was, the real question, the one under every question: *who holds power when the powerful fall?*

"You do," Jude said. "That's the horror of it. That's always been the horror of it. You had the authority to check him any day in the last ten years and none of you wanted to look, because looking might cost you comfort, and comfort is the interest paid on unexamined accounts." She let that land. She was not gentle about it; gentleness had bought the Garden ten quiet centuries and ended in ash. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm the least trustworthy person in this room—I lied to a silver-tongued auditor in these very halls and enjoyed getting away with it. I'm asking you to build a system that doesn't require trusting anyone. That's all an honest ledger ever was."

* * *

The masters resisted. Of course they did.

Ondrell argued procedure for an hour—that the seal must return to council custody pending investigation, that investigation meant masters, that masters investigating themselves was somehow the natural order of things. Dune raised the specter of market panic: if word spread that the Guild had harbored an enemy agent, counterparties would flee, contracts would collapse, the whole edifice of trust would—

"Word's already spreading," said a voice from the doorway.

Jude turned, and there stood Perrin Ashe, third-factor of the salt contracts, a broad calm man she'd shared a counting desk with for four years and who had once covered for her absence during the courier incident without ever asking why. He was not alone. Behind him crowded the morning shift of the trading floor—two dozen factors, clerks, porters, still in their street clothes, still smelling of the fog outside—and the crowd was not orderly. Crowds that arrive together and stand together are never merely crowds.

"How?" Jude asked.

Perrin's mouth twitched. "Sefa Ilvane sent a letter ahead from Halfway. Before you even crossed the Marches. She wrote to every factor she could name, telling them Corvane was a fraud, the seal was coming home, and the ledgers were going to be opened—and asking us to be standing in this room when they were."

Jude looked at Sefa. Sefa, gray and bandaged, examined her fingernails with the studied indifference of a woman who had absolutely planned all of it.

"You could have told me," Jude said.

"You'd have argued about the wording," Sefa said.

"What does the letter say, exactly?" demanded Master Ondrell, and Perrin Ashe recited it from memory, which told Jude something about how many times he'd read it:

"*To the factors of the Meridian Guild: I am a war-child. My village fed the Garden's first harvest, and I spent my life believing power was a thing done to people like me by people above me. I have seen the bottom of this Guild's deepest account, and the entry reads:* **they knew**. *Your masters did not stop Corvane because stopping him required looking, and looking was expensive. Tomorrow, Jude Winterbourne returns with his seal and his crimes in writing. Be present when she presents them. Not to make history—to witness it. Witnessing is the only office in this Guild that was never corrupt.*"

Silence, after that. The particular silence of a room recalculating everything.

"Rumor of her deeds," Halvard murmured, half to herself, and Jude realized the old woman had heard the phrase too—had heard it traveling ahead of them for weeks, mutating through market towns and ferry crossings: the woman who broke the anchor-chains, the factor who freed the Hollow Men by speaking their names back into them. Sefa had become, in the mouths of strangers, something between a saint and a storm. And saints and storms both draw crowds.

"The factors do not consent," Perrin said now, formally, loudly, in the cadence of a man invoking an old rule most had forgotten existed. "Under charter law, article nine—the factors' veto—an auditor may be removed by joint petition of the factors when his accounts fail inspection, and no seal may pass between custodians without a witnessed count. We are witnesses. Count it."

And they counted it. Right there, before the council, Perrin and two senior clerks verified the seal's markings, weighed it, matched its impression against the charter archive, and entered the whole proceeding into the day-book in front of everyone—honest columns, public ink, the first fully witnessed transfer of power the Meridian Guild had performed in living memory. Jude watched the numbers go down and felt something in her chest loosen that had been tight since a bleeding man died against the Salt Door saying her true surname. This was what she had fought for. Not the fire. Not the tree's temptation. *This*: a page that anyone could read, that read the same to everyone, that could not be grafted onto, whispered to, or grown fat in the dark.

The masters folded—not all at once, but the way bad positions fold in any negotiation, the weakest argument first. Ondrell abstained. Dune, cornered by article nine and twenty witnesses, voted yes with the enthusiasm of a man salvaging his seat. Halvard voted yes with actual conviction, and afterward took Jude's burned hands in hers and said, quietly, "Your mentor would be proud, if pride weren't against her religion," and Jude had to pretend for a moment to study the charter archive so no one would see her eyes.

By noon it was done. Corvane's accounts frozen. His name struck from the rolls—not erased; *struck*, entered in red in the strike-ledger, visible forever, because hiding a crime was how the last crime happened. The seal split by charter-law provision into three wards, to be held by the factors' elected committee, the council, and the archive, none of which could open a writ without the other two. And the factors, voting as a body, appointed an interim auditor to oversee the reopening of every sealed ledger in the house.

They appointed Jude Winterbourne.

"I don't want it," she said, when Perrin raised her name.

"Good," said Sefa, from her chair. "Take it anyway. The last person who wanted that desk was Corvane. Let's try giving it to someone who'll hold it like a hot iron."

"I'm not a Guildsman anymore. I was declared dead."

"Then we'll un-declare you," Perrin said, and grinned, and the grin was echoed around the room by two dozen tired, hopeful, ordinary faces—people who balanced books, who caught errors, who had spent years doing honest work inside dishonest walls and had never once been asked what they thought. "Article nine requires an auditor confirmed by factor vote. You have it. Fifty-one to nothing. Even Ondrell's clerk voted, and she's terrified of you."

Jude looked at the desk in question—Corvane's old desk, being carried in even now by four porters who handled it like something dead—and felt the old ache, the one from chapter one of her life, the ache of a gifted life spent pretending to be ordinary. Except it had changed key. She was ordinary now, truly ordinary, no humming under her skin, no dead swords dreaming of her blood. And ordinary, she understood at last, was not a wound. It was a qualification. An auditor's whole virtue was that she could be audited.

"All right," she said. "One condition. Every entry I make gets countersigned. By Sefa, or Marta, or Perrin—if I'm ever the only witness to my own work, fire me on the spot."

"Counter-signed by committee," Perrin agreed solemnly. "Welcome back from the dead, Auditor Winterbourne. Your first task awaits."

"What task?"

He nodded toward the window, down toward the trading floor, where a commotion was rising—the particular rising murmur of many voices arriving at once, questions stacking on questions. Through the glass Jude could see people gathering in Meridian Square beyond the Guild steps: scholars with satchels, survivors with scarves pulled low, a knot of gray-cloaked figures from the Marches relief societies, and at the center of it all, being handed up the steps by a trembling junior clerk, a document so thick it took two arms to carry.

Sefa followed Jude's gaze and went briefly, beautifully pale.

"That's my filing," Sefa said. "The Garden's true history. I sent it to the archive yesterday with instructions to register it publicly." She swallowed. "I may have underestimated how many people would want to read it."

Below, someone began hammering a notice to the Guild's outer doors, and the square roared like a sea coming in, and Jude Winterbourne—who had once hidden a world-ending gift under a junior accountant's collar—picked up her first pen as auditor of an honest house, dipped it, and reached for a fresh page.
