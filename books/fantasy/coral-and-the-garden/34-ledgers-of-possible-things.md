## 34. Ledgers of Possible Things

The Meridian Guild reopened on a morning that smelled of salt and fresh-cut pine.

Sefa Ilvane stood in the counting-house doorway before the doors were even unbarred, watching carpenters fit new shutters over windows that had been shattered during the reckoning—shattered, some said, by Corvane's Hollow Men when the council came for them; others swore the Guild's own factors had broken them in their hurry to be first at the evidence. Both stories were true enough. Sefa had stopped preferring one truth to another somewhere in the Drowned Marches, kneeling in anchor-chain grease with blood running into her boot, choosing which side of her own ledger she wanted to die on.

The doors themselves had survived. Old black oak, older iron, salt-scoured by two centuries of harbor wind. The council had voted to replace them—a new Guild deserved new doors, Master Ondrey said—and the vote had failed by an embarrassing margin, because no factor in the room could name what the old doors had ever done wrong.

Sefa approved. She was learning to think like an auditor, and an auditor's first question was never *is it beautiful* or *is it new*. It was *what did it actually do*.

She stepped inside. Her boots rang on the floorboards, on boards she had walked every working day for eleven years, back when the Guild's ledgers held bottled storms and borrowed years and a payment once made in a currency that hadn't existed since the war—the entry that had started all of this, though Sefa hadn't known it then. She'd only known that Jude Winterbourne's pen had paused over that line for exactly three seconds too long, and that a woman who balanced impossible accounts without blinking did not pause unless the line meant something.

Predatory patience, Jude called it now, half laughing, half not, whenever they argued about those early weeks. Sefa preferred *professional diligence*. Neither of them believed it.

The counting-house had been emptied and scrubbed. The great wall of pigeonholes where the impossible trades had been filed stood bare, its little drawers pulled out and stacked in the yard like coffins awaiting assignment. Someone had chalked numbers on each stack. Sefa recognized the hand—small, precise, the numerals formed in the old swordspeaker fashion with the crossbar struck through—and felt the now-familiar tightening in her chest that was equal parts irritation and something she refused, even privately, to call tenderness.

Jude had been at work since before dawn. Of course she had. The woman treated penance like a harvest: you rose early, you worked until your hands ached, and you did not speak of what you were paying for.

* * *

By midmorning the factors began to arrive.

They came in ones and twos at first, cautious as gulls around a fish cart, then in a steady stream once word spread that the doors were open and no one had been turned away. Sefa watched them from the auditor's dais—Jude's dais now, though Jude herself sat at a plain desk beside it rather than upon it, having declared that chairs with any height advantage were "the first step toward becoming Corvane" and would not be tolerated.

Master Ondrey came early, as he always did, silver-haired and shrewd, his coat brushed but his cuffs frayed in the way of a man whose fortune had been tied up in trades he now suspected he'd never understood. He stopped at the threshold and looked around the stripped room with the expression of a man visiting his own tomb.

"It's smaller," he said.

"It's honest-sized," Sefa said. "You'll adjust."

Ondrey's mouth twitched. He had voted for her appointment as head factor—not because he liked her; she was fairly sure no one on the council liked her—but because she had come back from the Marches with proof, testimony, the master seal itself pressed into her palm by a dying man who had wanted to matter in a ledger that had forgotten him. Sefa carried that seal still, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her document case. She had thought about melting it down. Instead she kept it as auditors kept everything: as evidence.

"The rules," Ondrey said, following her to the intake counter. "The ones Mistress Kavanagh posted. I've read them. I have concerns."

"Everyone has concerns. It's why we built the concern ledger."

"You're joking."

"I am entirely serious." Sefa slid the book across the counter to him. It was a plain thing, cheap paper, bound in undyed leather. On the first page, in Jude's precise hand: *Concerns of the Factors of the Meridian Guild, to be reviewed monthly and answered in writing.* Beneath it, already, four entries. "Entry one: Master Pell objects to the prohibition on trading in memories. Entry two: someone anonymous objects to the prohibition on trading in memories, in handwriting suspiciously similar to Master Pell's. Entry three: a request that the Guild resume selling weather, on the grounds that 'difficult' is undefined. Entry four: a complaint that the complaint process is itself a form of impossible trade, since no one believes complaints will be answered."

"And will they?"

"Every one. In writing. With our names signed." Sefa tapped the page. "That's the whole revolution, Ondrey. It fits in a book."

He stared at her for a long moment, this man who had spent thirty years profiting from a system that sold bottled lightning to drought-struck provinces and called it commerce. Then he took out his own pen, leaned over the concern ledger, and added a fifth line: *Concern: I do not know what my own past trades were worth. Concern: I may not want to.*

Sefa read it, said nothing, and filed him under *promising*.

* * *

The new rules hung framed above the intake counter, copied out fair by Jude's hand because the council's scribe had quit rather than write anything that might later be used against him:

*The Meridian Guild trades in difficult things, not impossible ones.*

*A difficult thing can be earned, learned, built, bargained for, or waited for. An impossible thing can only be taken.*

*Every entry will show its cost. Every cost will include the human one.*

*No trade shall require a person to give up their memory, their name, their oath, or their self. If a client cannot say what a thing cost them, the Guild will not sell it to them.*

*All ledgers are open to audit by any factor, any season, without notice.*

*The Guild keeps accounts it can defend.*

That last line had caused the shouting match. Sefa had been in the room for it—she was in the room for most things now, having discovered that the fastest way to stop being the woman who almost betrayed everyone was to be the woman who was inconveniently present at every meeting. One of the southern masters, a fat comfortable man named Hallow, had risen purple-faced and demanded to know who would decide what accounts could be "defended," and whether the Guild was to become a schoolroom.

And Jude—who rarely raised her voice, who had a way of going very quiet and very still that Sefa recognized now as the swordspeaker's discipline worn down to a civilian habit—had said, "I will decide. I'm the auditor. That's the desk's whole purpose. And if I ever defend an account that shouldn't be defended, then any factor in this room may open my books, read my reasoning, and call me a liar to my face in front of the council. That's the difference between us and Corvane. He made himself the only reader of his own ledger. I'm making myself the most-read."

Hallow had sat down. Most of them sat down, eventually. Power that invited scrutiny turned out to be a strange, uncomfortable thing to argue against.

Sefa had thought about that speech for days afterward. She had spent her life believing that knowledge was leverage—that the value of knowing a thing lived in how few others knew it. Her whole career at the Guild had been built on noticing what others missed and hoarding it. Watching Jude do the opposite—watching her take the most dangerous knowledge in the world and nail it to a wall where anyone could read it—had rearranged something in Sefa that she was still, weeks later, occasionally catching herself touching like a bruise.

*Truth as the only durable foundation.* She'd written that in her private notes after the reckoning. She was beginning, cautiously, to believe it.

* * *

At noon, the first clients came.

Not the old clients—the princes and archmagisters and merchant-kings who had once bought years of their lives back and storms in bottles. Those men had sent letters, furious and frightened, demanding to know where they would now acquire the things the Guild had promised them, and the council's answer, drafted by Jude and delivered under seal, ran to a single sentence: *Perhaps learn to wait for rain.*

The new clients were different. A cooper whose daughter had gone deaf from fever, asking—not demanding, asking, with his cap wrung in his hands—whether the Guild knew anyone who taught finger-speech. A widow with her husband's unfinished ship's model and a request for a craftsman who owed the family a debt and might finish it. Two brothers who had inherited a field that flooded every spring and wanted, not a miracle, but an engineer, and were willing to pay in labor at the Guild's docks for three summers to earn the introduction.

Difficult things. Earnable things. Sefa took their entries herself, writing each one out in full, costs listed, names signed by both parties, and found—to her genuine surprise—that she liked the work. There was a pleasure in it she had never gotten from brokering a bottled storm: the clean click of a transaction that left both parties more whole than it found them. Impossible trades had always felt, to her, like pickpocketing the future. This was more like planting.

In the afternoon lull she climbed the stairs to the scriptorium, where the public archive was taking shape.

This had been Sefa's project, claimed in the first week with the ferocity of a woman staking territory. The Garden's true history—everything they had seen, everything the Gardener's grave inscription confessed, everything the freed Hollow Men could remember of their stolen service—transcribed, cross-referenced, and shelved where any citizen could read it. The first impossible thing recorded honestly. Scholars had begun to arrive within days: thin, intense people with ink-stained fingers and the particular hunger of those who had suspected the official histories were lies and finally held proof. Survivors too. War-children grown old, like her, coming to stand in front of the shelf and touch the spines and say nothing at all.

She paused at the archive door. Inside, a young scholar was copying the account of Thornwall aloud to a companion, stumbling over the description of the visions, and an old woman Sefa didn't know was listening with tears running silently down her face.

Sefa withdrew without being seen. Some entries needed privacy more than witnesses.

On the landing, she nearly collided with Brac.

He still moved like a Hollow Man—too quiet, weight always centered, hands loose and ready—but the other habits were fading week by week. He'd taken to wearing colors. Today it was a scarf the orange of marigolds, which clashed magnificently with his gray scarred face and which Sefa suspected a woman at the docks had chosen for him. He carried a crate of ledgers against his chest like a shield.

"Ilveane." He nodded. His voice had gotten rougher as he'd gotten more human, as though names wore grooves into the throat. "Auditor wants these in the west annex. Says the fire-risk shelves need re-sorting."

"Then carry them to the fire-risk shelves, Brac."

"She also says—" he shifted the crate, awkward, "—says to tell you the third box has your name in it. The Marches accounts. She wants your corrections before it goes to the archive."

My corrections. As if the Garden's history belonged to both of them. As if the woman who burned it and the woman who'd have sold it were equally responsible for telling it true.

Which, Sefa reflected, descending the stairs behind Brac's slow careful load, was probably exactly what Jude believed. Penance shared was still penance. But it was lighter, and it got done faster, and there were evenings afterward when two women sat in the counting-house with cold tea and argued about decimal conventions until the lamps burned out, and those evenings were the closest thing either of them had to forgiveness.

* * *

She found Jude at dusk, at the auditor's desk that wasn't on a dais, working through the day's entries by lamplight.

The counting-house had emptied. Through the open windows the harbor breathed its evening breath—rigging clinking, gulls quarreling, the far-off bell of the tide-bell tolling the turn. Sefa set the corrected Marches accounts on the desk and stood a moment watching Jude read, because there was a thing she had decided to say and saying it required looking at the woman first.

Jude Winterbourne looked smaller than she had in the Marches. Smaller and steadier, the way a tree looks steadier after the storm takes its dead branches. Her gift was gone—burned in the Garden with everything else, traded away in the only choice Sefa had ever seen her make without hesitation—and sometimes, watching Jude reach for a jar on a high shelf, Sefa caught the ghost of a flinch: the body remembering a strength the soul had given back. But her hands on the ledger were sure. Whatever else the fire had taken, it had left her exactly herself.

"The third box needs your corrections," Sefa said. "Brac told me. I haven't read them yet."

"They're not corrections so much as confessions." Jude didn't look up. "There's a section on the ferryman's toll. What you paid. What I paid." Her pen paused. "I wrote down that you gave a memory of your brother. I wrote that I gave the memory of my mentor's face. But I don't—I can't verify the shape of what you lost. Only you know what that memory weighed. So the entry has a space in it. Your space. To fill or leave empty, whichever serves you."

Sefa looked at her for a long time.

A year ago—gods, less than a year—she would have taken that space and filled it with something useful. Something leveraged. A detail to hold, a softness to exploit. Predatory patience. She had built a whole self out of knowing things other people didn't, and here was Jude Winterbourne handing her the pen and turning the ledger around and saying *your space, your truth, your choice*, and meaning it.

"He played the fiddle badly," Sefa said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "My brother. That's the memory the ferryman took. Not his face—I kept his face, thank all the drowned gods, I fought for his face—but the sound of him playing. Badly. Every winter solstice, out of tune, and our mother covering her ears and laughing anyway." She pulled out the chair across from Jude and sat. "I want the record to say what it cost. That's the point of the new rules, isn't it? Costs included."

"That's the whole point." Jude wrote it down, verbatim, unhurried, and signed beneath it. Then she slid the ledger across. "Read it back. Check my work."

"No one checks an auditor's work."

"Everyone checks mine. It's rule six." A flicker of the old dry humor. "I put it there on purpose. Keep me honest, Ilvane. You're better at spotting lies than anyone I've ever met—including me, especially me. If I ever start defending an account that shouldn't be defended, I want you to be the one who catches it. Consider it your permanent commission."

Sefa took the ledger. Read the entry. Found it exact, and found, in the smallness of that exactness, something that loosened a knot she'd carried since a rainmarket in a city she'd been ready to betray everyone in.

"Penance and hope," she said quietly. "That's what this room is now. Do you know that?"

"I know it's a counting-house with good light and bad chairs."

"Sefa." She almost never used her own name aloud; it still felt like spending coin. But she said it now, and let it stand. "I mean it. I helped build the old Guild. I profited from it. I'd have sold that map for less than Corvane offered, if the price had been right, and we both know it. Rebuilding this place—it's the only honest entry I'll ever get to add to my own ledger. Let me keep adding to it."

Jude regarded her across the lamp. Outside, the tide-bell finished its tolling; the harbor settled; somewhere below in the yard, the night-porter began his rounds, keys jingling.

"Then come to the desk tomorrow," Jude said. "There's a backlog of concerns, Pell is filing fraudulent objections again, and I need someone at intake who knows what a lie looks like when it signs its own name." She capped her ink. "We're building something that might last. It'll need more than two pairs of hands."

They closed the ledgers together, stacked them square, and banked the lamp—and it was as they reached the doors, as Sefa's hand touched the old black oak, that the sound came.

Faint. From the direction of the harbor road. A high, thin note, like a blade drawn slowly across stone—or like a kitchen knife humming on a shelf, singing a song it had no reason to know.

Jude went absolutely still.

"You hear it too," Sefa said. Not a question.

"Yes," Jude Winterbourne said, and her voice held wonder and dread in equal measure, and her hand was already reaching for the bar. "And I think it's getting closer."
