## 35. The Sword's Farewell

The autumn came early to Meridian, the way it always did, sliding in off the salt water and turning the counting-house windows to grey glass by mid-afternoon. Jude had learned to mark the seasons by the ledgers now instead of by the light. Autumn was when the shipping factors closed their accounts against the storm months. Winter was when the debt books fattened. Spring, if she was honest with herself, was the only season she still loved—spring, when the girl Tamsin practiced her speech-scales in the yard and the sound of it drifted up through the open shutters like birdsong that knew your name.

But this was autumn, and there was work to be done, and the sword would not stop humming.

She felt it before she heard it—that faint vibration in the bones of her forearm where her gift used to live, the ghost of a sensation she had not truly felt since the Garden burned. Except this was no ghost. The hound-sword lay wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom drawer of her desk, beneath the sealed records of Corvane's fraud and the master seal itself, and it hummed the way a dog hums when it presses its head against a sleeping child: patient, loyal, asking nothing but presence.

It had been quiet for most of the summer. It had followed her home from the Marches like something ashamed of its own survival, riding in a cart among the honest cargo, whispering only occasionally—fragments of its last wielder's memories surfacing like bodies in floodwater. *The bridge at Ashfall. Rain on hot iron. A woman laughing, telling him he gripped too tight.* Jude had listened the way one listens to a dying man's stories, with reverence and dread in equal measure, because every fragment reminded her what she had made: a dead soldier's sword, spoken back from rust, bound to her by a word she had sworn never to speak again.

And now, as the days shortened, the whispers changed.

They grew quieter. Slower. Like footsteps losing their certainty. And last night, sitting alone over the final audit of the year, Jude had understood what the sword was trying to tell her, because it stopped whispering memories entirely and spoke in plain words for the first time since the vault.

*It is finished,* the sword had said. Its voice was the voice of its last wielder, a man named—she still didn't know his name—a man who had died at Ashfall Bridge holding a line that could not be held. *You buried me once. Bury me again. Properly, this time.*

* * *

She took it out at dawn, when the counting-house was empty and the tide was low enough to show the bones of the old harbor wall. She unwrapped the oilcloth on her desk and looked at what ten months of carrying had done to it.

The blade had been beautiful once, or so the fragments of memory insisted—the wielder had polished it every morning, a vanity he'd confessed to no one but the steel. Now it was mottled, grey-and-brown bloom spreading from the tip toward the tang, rust advancing the way it had begun to before she'd ever woken it. She had held the decay back with her speech for months without admitting to herself that she was doing it. Every night, half-asleep, some small surviving corner of her had murmured *hold, hold, hold* into the metal, and the metal had obeyed, because obedience was all it had ever known.

Now she let go. She sat with her hands flat on either side of the blade and simply—stopped. And the rust moved in like the tide coming back to a shore that had been defended too long. She watched it climb. It did not hurt, exactly. It felt like watching an old wound finally allowed to scar.

"You know," she said aloud, because the office was empty and she had long since stopped being embarrassed about talking to it, "most people would have asked me sooner."

*I did not want to leave you unguarded,* the sword said. Its voice was thinner now, fraying at the edges like cloth worn through. *There were Hollow Men in the world. There was Corvane. There was a war that wanted restarting.*

"There isn't anymore."

*Because you ended it.* A pause, and in the pause she heard the memory-surface: rain, iron, the bridge, a woman's laugh. *That is what we were for. All of us. Not the killing. The ending.*

Jude's throat tightened. She had spent a decade believing her oaths had been a cage, and another decade learning they had been a promise, and somewhere between the two she had lost the ability to tell grief from gratitude. The tree in the Garden had offered her everything back—her order, her name, her gift—and she had chosen ash instead, chosen to stay human, chosen to lose the thing that made her *her* so that no one else could ever be unmade the way swordspeakers had been unmade. Some nights she regretted it. Most nights she slept well. She had decided that this unevenness, this ragged arithmetic of loss and rest, was what peace actually sounded like when it was earned rather than given.

"Where will you go?" she asked. "When I speak you down. Do you—go anywhere?"

The sword considered. She felt it considering, the way she'd once felt the bottled storm shifting in its glass.

*Into the same silence you live in,* it said. *We will be neighbors.*

* * *

Sefa found her at the harbor wall an hour past dawn, because Sefa always found her, because Sefa had built a career out of knowing where things were and had lately turned that talent toward the single purpose of keeping Jude from disappearing into herself. She came down the worn steps with two paper cones of roasted chestnuts and no expression at all, which from Sefa was a whole paragraph of concern.

"You've got the look," Sefa said, handing her a cone. "The ledger-look. Like you're auditing something that can't be counted."

"I'm burying a friend today."

Sefa glanced at the bundle under Jude's arm—the oilcloth, the shape of it unmistakable to anyone who had stood beside her in the vault while awakened swords screamed out of the walls. Something moved behind her eyes, quick and private. Sefa had paid a ferryman a memory of her brother once; she knew better than anyone alive what it cost to carry the dead and what it cost to set them down.

"The courier," Sefa said quietly. "You're doing them together."

"I promised myself I would. Ten years ago I couldn't even learn his name. I wasn't allowed to ask." Jude looked out at the water, grey-green, patient, smelling of salt and rope and cold. "But names matter. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? Corvane hollowed men by taking their names. Brac came back when he remembered his. The Guild lied for a century because it kept everything anonymous—goods, debts, people." She turned the chestnut cone in her hands without eating any of it. "So I went looking. After we got back. Through the Guild's oldest war-era rolls, the ones nobody has opened since the reckoning."

"And?"

Jude reached into her coat and drew out a folded slip of paper, soft at the creases from being handled too many times. "Dannel Ferric. Third Company, oath-bound, listed fallen at Ashfall Bridge. Survived by no one." Her voice caught on the last word, and she let it. "Survived by no one," she repeated, steadier. "Except a courier's guild apprentice he sponsored, years before the war, who grew up to carry maps through occupied country and die against a salt door with my true name in his mouth. The Guild's records connect them. Dannel Ferric paid the apprenticeship fees. He never met the boy—he was already dead—but the money was his, left in trust, and the trust was administered by..." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "By my order. By the very order I thought had ended with me. My mentor signed the disbursement. She knew where his charity went. She probably knew the boy would find me someday, if the war ever threatened to come back. She built a thread from his grave to my door and trusted me to pull it."

Sefa was silent for a moment. Then: "Your mentor is still teaching you. From wherever mentors go."

"She always was insufferable that way."

* * *

They buried him outside the city walls, in the small cemetery on the headland where the Guild had lately begun keeping a plot for those who died in its service honestly—which was to say, a plot that had been empty until the reckoning, until Jude's reforms, until now. Dannel Ferric would be its first tenant, posthumously enrolled in a Guild that had traded fairly at last, though he'd never known it existed. There was a stonecutter in the lower city who owed Sefa a favor, and the stone would say:

**DANNEL FERRIC**
*Swordspeaker of the Third Company*
*He held. He gave. He sent a map.*
*Survived by the woman he never met.*

Jude had argued about the last line. Sefa had won, the way Sefa won arguments—not by force but by refusing to be embarrassed on anyone else's behalf.

The hole was dug by afternoon. Brac came, and three of the other freed Hollow Men, standing awkwardly in their plain clothes like men who had not yet learned what their shoulders were for now that no one aimed them. Tamsin came too, solemn in her good coat, clutching a sprig of rowan because Jude had told her that swordspeakers' burials once included a living branch—"something that keeps growing after the wood is cut"—and the girl had taken this with the terrible earnestness of the young and refused to leave it behind.

Jude knelt at the edge of the pit with the sword across her knees, unwrapped for the last time. The rust had reached the tang overnight. There was very little left to hold back, and less reason to.

"Dannel," she said. "I'm told that's your name. I carried your sword for ten months and your charity for ten years, and I never thanked either properly." She laid her palm flat against the pitted blade. "So: thank you. For the map. For the boy. For holding the bridge long enough for a coward to run and live and grow old enough to do one right thing with her life."

*Not a coward,* the sword said, in a voice barely above the sound of wind in dry grass. *A keeper. You kept it all. The oaths. The ledgers. Even me.*

"Will it hurt you?" Tamsin asked softly, from behind her. "Being unmade."

"It's already mostly rust, little one. I'm only agreeing with what the weather decided." Jude looked up at her student—at the girl who hummed kitchen knives without knowing why, who was learning, lesson by lesson, what the gift cost and how to keep honest records of it. "Remember this part. Power isn't only in the speaking. Sometimes it's in the stopping."

Then she spoke.

Not loudly. Not in the old battle-tongue that had cracked stone and woke the dead. Just a word—the word for *rest*, the word for *enough*, the word her own mentor had whispered over her at Ashfall Bridge when the world was burning and survival was the price of silence. She felt the syllables leave her like blood leaving a opened vein, felt the last of her gift rise up through her throat and pour into the steel one final time, and the sword drank it the way it had drunk everything she'd ever offered: completely, gratefully, without question.

The rust bloomed. It climbed the blade like ivy given permission, brown flowering into orange, orange into the deep red-black of old blood, and flakes fell away from the edges and sifted down into the pit like the first snow of a winter that would never end. The humming faded. The warmth faded. What remained in her hands was only iron—cold, ordinary, mortal iron, the kind any smith could forge and any farmer could buy.

*Thank you,* said the silence where the sword had been. Or perhaps it was only her own heart, closing a door gently.

Jude laid it in the earth beside the folded map—what remained of the map, the wax seal broken, the ink long since gone still and ordinary—and covered them both with the first shovelful herself. Brac took the second. Tamsin, kneeling carefully at the edge, placed the rowan sprig on top of the soil and patted it down with both hands, and Sefa put her hand briefly on Jude's shoulder, hard, once, which was Sefa's way of saying everything.

They stood a while as the light went gold and then grey. The sea below the headland kept its own accounts, endless and indifferent, and the wind coming off it smelled of salt, and faintly—though Jude might have imagined it—of roses.

* * *

She walked back alone as dusk came on, taking the long way along the tidewalks, past the flooded lower streets where she'd once run for her life with a dead man's map against her ribs. The city was lighting its lamps. Somewhere a factor was arguing with a clerk; somewhere a ship was coming in with cargo that would need honest weighing; somewhere Tamsin was probably practicing her scales when she ought to be asleep.

At the mouth of Salt Door Lane, she stopped.

The door was new—oak, banded in iron, hung straight on its hinges—and painted on it, at her own instruction, in letters any passerby could read, were the words: **MERIDIAN GUILD — LEDGERS OPEN TO PUBLIC INSPECTION.** Below them, smaller: **NO DEBT IS FINAL THAT WAS NOT FAIRLY MADE.**

Ten years ago she had pressed herself against a version of this door while a dying man gasped her name. Tonight she put her hand flat against the oak and felt nothing humming underneath—not her gift, not the old war, not the tree's long roots drinking oaths somewhere far away in drowned country. Just wood. Just a city going about its evening. Just the enormous, fragile, ordinary machinery of a world that would keep turning precisely because she had chosen, twice now, to give up the power that could have forced it to turn her way.

Her forearm ached where the gift had lived. It would ache, she suspected, for the rest of her life—the way a missing tooth aches, the way an amputated limb reports weather. She had made her peace with the ache. It was, she had come to believe, the truest record she kept: written not in ink but in nerve, proof that something precious had been here and been surrendered on purpose.

She pushed open the door and went inside, into lamplight and the smell of paper, where tomorrow's entries waited to be made honestly.

And it was there, on her desk, that she found it: an envelope of cheap grey paper, no seal, no signature, delivered sometime in the hours she'd been standing at a graveside on the headland. Inside was a single sheet, and on the sheet, in a child's careful, uncertain hand—

*Dear Madam Auditor. I hope this finds you. My knife started singing last week and my mother says I should throw it in the river but I can't make it stop and Daed says singing knives are bad luck but also he says maybe someone in the city would know. Please write back. Yours respectfully, Wren Callow, age nine, Millbrook.*

Jude read it twice. Then she sat down slowly in her chair, the letter trembling slightly in fingers that no longer held any power at all, and began, in her best auditor's hand, to compose a reply.
