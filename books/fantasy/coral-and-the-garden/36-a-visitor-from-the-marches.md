## 36. A Visitor from the Marches

The girl came in with the spring tides, which was fitting, Jude thought later, because everything that had ever mattered to her had arrived on the smell of salt and the sound of water against stone.

It was a Tuesday, if such a thing could be said to matter. The counting-house was quiet in the way that quiet had become a kind of luxury—Sefa at the far desk arguing with a factor from the salt-quarries about the true weight of a delivered cargo, the girl's predecessor in the apprenticeship yard practicing her letters, the afternoon light coming through the high windows in long amber bars that turned the ledger-dust to drifting gold. Jude sat at the auditor's desk, which had once been Corvane's, and which she had never fully managed to think of as hers, and balanced the week's accounts of difficult things: three tons of Drowned Marches peat, a contract of safe passage through the Thornwall passes, a widow's pension paid in the coin of a country that no longer existed, honored because the Guild had said it would be.

Honest ledgers. She had fought a war, in her way, for honest ledgers, and most days the phrase still felt too small for what it had cost.

The bell above the salt door rang—the plain bell, not the merchant's bell, which meant someone had walked in off the street without an appointment, which meant Sefa would be irritated, which meant Jude usually handled these herself.

The girl was perhaps eleven, maybe twelve, small for her age in the way of children who had not been fed regularly until recently. She wore a traveler's coat cut down from an adult's, patched at both elbows, and she stood in the doorway of the counting-house floor with her fists clenched at her sides as if she had rehearsed a speech on the walk over and was now discovering that the room had not read the script.

"I need to sell something," she said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles were white. "The man at the door said you buy strange things."

"We buy difficult things," Jude said. "Strange is free. Strange you can have just for asking."

The girl frowned at that, working it over, deciding whether it was a joke and whether jokes were safe. Jude knew the calculation. She had made it herself, once, standing in a doorway not so different from this one, ten years and one lifetime ago.

"What is it you're selling?"

The girl reached into her coat and drew out a kitchen knife.

It was, Jude saw in the first instant, a very ordinary knife. Wooden handle worn smooth, blade of cheap iron, the kind sold in market stalls in every coastal town from here to the Thornwall for a few coppers. It had been sharpened many times by someone who knew what they were doing and had been used for nothing grander than onions and bread and, once, from a stain near the tang, a birthday cake's icing.

And it was humming.

Jude heard it the way she had once heard swords speak—not with her ears, not anymore, that sense had gone into the fire with the Garden—but with the older, deeper thing underneath, the thing the gift had left behind when it died, the way a field remembers a harvest. The knife was singing a low, wavering note, a child's note, uncertain and off-key, the sound of a blade that did not know it was a blade, that had never been taught what blades were for.

The girl was holding it by the blade, careful of the edge, handle offered outward, the way the door-man had presumably taught her. She had not noticed the sound. Or she had noticed it and thought it was the wind, or the sea, or her own blood in her ears.

"When did it start?" Jude asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "The humming. When did it start?"

The girl's eyes went wide—not with fear, Jude saw, but with the particular relief of someone who has been told for weeks that they are imagining a thing and has just heard a stranger name it.

"So you can hear it." The words tumbled out of her. "Mam says it's my head. The tinker said it's the metal, bad batch, sings when the weather turns. But it doesn't sing when the weather turns, it sings when—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "It sings when I'm scared. And when I'm angry. And once when my brother fell in the harbor and I was pulling him out, it sang so loud I couldn't hear the water."

*It sings when I'm scared.*

Jude set down her pen. She was aware of the room around her—the factor's voice trailing off as Sefa, too, had gone still, the girl in the yard still scratching at her letters, the amber light, the dust—and aware of it the way one is aware of the surface of deep water.

"What's your name?" Jude asked.

"Tamsin. Tamsin Reeve. We're from Marsh's End, up the coast. My mam says the Marches took our farm when I was a baby, so we came to the coast, but—" Tamsin stopped herself, the way children do when they realize they are telling their whole life to a stranger. She held out the knife again, more insistently. "Are you going to buy it or not? I need two silvers. For my brother's foot. The bone-setter wants two silvers and Mam says we can't, and the knife's the only thing we own that anyone would want, because—" her voice dropped, went fierce and embarrassed at once, "—because it's wrong. It's a wrong knife. Nobody in Marsh's End will touch it anymore."

Jude did not take the knife. Not yet. Taking it was a decision, and she knew—she had known since the girl walked in, since the first wavering note reached the place under her hearing—what kind of decision it was.

"Where did the knife come from, Tamsin?"

"It was my gran's. She died in the winter." Tamsin's chin came up, defiant, daring Jude to make something sentimental of it. "It's just a kitchen knife. It's not worth two silvers. I know that. But you buy strange things."

*Difficult,* Jude thought. *We buy difficult things. And this, child, is the most difficult thing to walk through that door in a year.*

Because she could feel it now, clearly, the way she had once felt a whole armory at Ashfall Bridge: the knife was not wrong. The knife was *listening*. It had been made of ordinary iron by an ordinary smith, and somewhere in its long dull life it had been held, over and over, by someone whose blood remembered the Garden—Tamsin's gran, or her gran before her, some woman out of the Drowned Marches who had never spoken a word of swordspeech in her life and had passed down not the gift but the *ground* of the gift, the seedbed, the soil—and the iron had drunk it the way the tree had drunk oaths, slowly, in kitchen-light, over bread and onions and a birthday cake.

The gift was not dead. The gift had scattered. It had gone to ground in the way that fire goes to ground in a peat marsh, invisible, patient, surviving in a hundred small hearths along a hundred drowned coastlines, in kitchen knives and hinge-irons and the nails of old boats, waiting.

*Burn what I planted,* the Gardener had written. She had burned it. She had walked out of the Garden human, and she had told herself the war's potential had died in the smoke behind her.

The smoke had apparently drifted.

"Jude." Sefa's voice, low, from the far desk. She had crossed half the floor without Jude noticing, which was its own kind of warning; Sefa Ilvane did not move quietly for anyone. She stood now with her arms folded, looking at the girl with an expression Jude had learned to read over a year of ledgers and arguments: not softness—Sefa did not do softness—but recognition. A war-child looking at a March-child. "The knife. Is it what I think it is?"

"It's a kitchen knife."

"You know what I mean."

"I know what it means." Jude rose from the auditor's desk. "There's a difference. I'm deciding how big it is."

* * *

She took Tamsin into the small room behind the archive, the one with the good chairs and the window that looked out on the yard, and she sat the girl down and put two silvers on the table between them, and then she did not touch the knife for a long moment.

"Two silvers for your brother's foot," Jude said. "That's yours whether you sell me the knife or not. Consider it the Guild's standing bounty on honest answers. I have some questions, and I pay for true ones."

Tamsin eyed the coins, then Jude, then the coins again, with the shrewdness of a child who had learned that gifts from strangers have edges. "What kind of questions?"

"Where your gran was born. Whether her mother ever talked about the war. Whether anyone in Marsh's End ever said your family was *odd*—lucky with animals, or never bitten by the ferry-dogs, or able to find things that were lost." Jude paused. "Whether the knife ever sang before this winter."

Tamsin's frown deepened, and then, slowly, the defiance in her face changed into something more careful, more frightened, and much more honest.

"It sang before," she whispered. "Gran used to say it was talking to her. We thought she was—she was old, and she'd say the knife missed the war, and we laughed, because it was just a knife, and she'd get angry, and say, *it's not just anything, it remembers being needed.*" The girl's hands were flat on the table now, on either side of the blade, as if guarding it. "She talked to it. Every night. Before she died she made me promise to keep it sharp. She said—" Tamsin's voice cracked, and she pushed through it the way she had pushed through everything, jaw set, eyes bright, "—she said, *it'll need you someday, and you'll need it, and that's how it works.*"

The room was very quiet. Through the window, faintly, came the sound of the girl in the yard reciting her letters, and the sea, and the bell-buoy beyond the harbor wall.

*It remembers being needed.*

Jude thought of the hound-sword, the dead soldier's blade that had followed her out of the Garden's war, loyal as a dog, whispering fragments of a wielder long dead, until she had spoken it back to rust and buried it with the courier whose name she had learned too late. She thought of the tree in the root-vault, grown from the first swordspeaker, drinking oaths, offering her back everything she had lost if only she would take root and become its keeper. She thought of the rose in the Marches, thornless, blooming from the ash, which she had visited in the autumn and left uncut and told no one but Sefa about.

Power's residue. Harmless, beautiful, watched.

She had watched the rose. She had not thought to watch the world.

"How many of these are there?" Sefa asked from the doorway. She had not sat down. She was looking at the knife with the expression of a factor assessing cargo, which was how Sefa looked at everything that frightened her. "Kitchen knives. Hinge-irons. Boat-nails. How many humming things are scattered up and down this coast, Jude, and how long before someone who isn't us finds one?"

"Someone already has," Jude said quietly. "The girl found one. The tinker called it a bad batch of metal. Mam called it a sickness of the head." She turned to Tamsin. "Who else knows it sings? Outside your family?"

Tamsin shrugged, then stopped, and Jude watched the answer arrive in the girl's face before she spoke it.

"The bone-setter's man came by last week," Tamsin said slowly. "About the foot. He heard it. He picked it up off the table and his face went—" she searched for the word, "—*hungry*. Like when the gulls find a fish. He asked where we got it. He asked if there were more. Gran's other knife burned in the fire, I told him, which was a lie, there wasn't another knife, but he didn't know that." Her chin came up again, and this time there was fear under the defiance, a child's fear, cold and specific. "He gave me his card. He said if I ever found another one, the Guild would pay. He said he was *from* the Guild."

The silence that followed had weight.

Sefa said a word in the old March dialect that their new Guild rules technically fined people for, and then, "Which office? Which man?"

"He didn't say an office. He had a card." Tamsin dug in her coat and produced it: good stock, expensive stock, the kind of card no honest bone-setter's man carried. Jude took it and turned it over.

There was no name. There was no office. There was only the Guild's new seal—the seal she herself had commissioned, the seal of the open ledger—and beneath it, stamped small, a single word:

*HARVEST.*

Jude sat with the card in her hand and felt the year turn over beneath her like a boat in a swell. She had rebuilt the Guild on honest ledgers. She had opened the archives, filed the Garden's true history where any scholar could read it, taught a child her letters in the yard, signed her own full name to every account she kept. She had believed—she had *needed* to believe—that the war's potential had died in the smoke, that the worst of it was buried with the hound-sword and the courier and the man who had only wanted to matter in a ledger that had forgotten him.

And someone was out there using her seal, her open archives, her honest Guild, as a net to trawl the coast for humming iron and the children who could hear it.

"Keep the knife," Jude said to Tamsin. Her voice was steady. She was proud of that, again, and this time the pride had iron in it. "Keep the two silvers. And now I'm going to ask you the hardest question, and I'll pay a full silver more for the true answer, and the true answer is the only one I'll accept. Would you like to learn why it sings?"

Tamsin looked at her—the way, Jude thought, she herself had once looked at a dying mentor on a broken bridge, at the moment when a life splits into before and after.

"I'd like to know why everyone lies about it," Tamsin said.

Jude laughed—a short, startled sound, the first laugh the room had heard in hours—and pushed the third silver across the table.

* * *

She stood in the yard at dusk, after Tamsin had been fed in the kitchen and installed by the fire with her brother's name and her mother's address written into the Guild's visitor book in a careful, unpracticed hand. Sefa came out to stand beside her, arms folded against the sea wind, and for a while neither of them spoke. In the amber light the yard looked almost peaceful: the practice posts, the chalk letters on the flagstones, the girl in the window of the kitchen laughing at something the cook had said.

"You're going to teach her," Sefa said. It was not a question.

"I'm going to answer her questions. There's a difference."

"You said that about the knife."

"I say a lot of things about differences. It's most of my job." Jude turned the bone-setter's man's card over in her fingers one last time, then held it to the lantern flame and let it burn down to her fingertips before dropping it on the flagstones and grinding it out with her heel. "Someone is harvesting, Sefa. Using our seal. Which means either the seal was copied, or someone inside these walls is selling our name by the pound. And either way, there's a child in my kitchen who can hear iron sing, and a dead woman's promise in her pocket—*it'll need you someday*—and a coast full of gran's-knives and boat-nails that I burned a Garden to set free, and it turns out you can't burn a thing that lives in people."

Sefa was quiet a moment. The wind moved her hair; she did not bother with it. "Secrets were the danger," she said at last, quoting Jude back to herself, the way she did when she had lost an argument but not surrendered it. "That's what you told me. So tell me the truth now. Are you afraid?"

Jude looked out past the harbor wall, where the first stars were coming up over the black line of the Marches, the drowned country where petrified soldiers were crumbling peacefully into the marsh, where a thornless rose bloomed uncut in the ash. The past refusing to be buried. The gift gone to ground and growing back in kitchen knives, in eleven-year-old girls, in whatever hungry hands were already reaching for it with her own Guild's seal stamped on the reach.

"Yes," she said. "But I've been afraid before, and the ledgers balanced anyway."

She turned back toward the counting-house, toward the light and the child at the fire, and found that her decision had made itself somewhere in the last hour without consulting her, the way the true ones always did.

"First thing tomorrow," she said, "I want every factor on the coast asked one question: who else has come asking after humming iron? And Sefa—"

"I know. I'll write it myself." Sefa was already moving toward the door, then paused, hand on the frame, and looked back with something that on a softer face would have been a smile. "And the girl?"

Jude looked through the window at Tamsin Reeve, laughing by the fire with a wrong, wonderful knife wrapped in cloth beside her, and felt the whole weight of the old oath and the old fire and the old grief settle into something almost like purpose.

"The girl," Jude said, "starts her lessons Monday."

But even as she said it, even as the warmth of it settled, her eyes went back to the burned card on the flagstones, to the small stamped word already fading in her memory into something larger and colder: *Harvest.* Someone out there had moved first. Someone out there had a list, and a seal, and a head start—and the first name on that list, Jude was suddenly, coldly certain, had not been Tamsin Reeve's at all.

It had been hers.
