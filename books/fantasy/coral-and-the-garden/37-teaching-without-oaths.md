## 37. Teaching Without Oaths

The girl came back on a Tuesday.

Jude had not expected her to—the way one does not expect rain after a long drought, or a letter from the dead—but there she was at the salt door at closing bell, standing exactly where a dying courier had once stood with his blood soaking into the stones, and the symmetry of it sat in Jude's chest like a swallowed coin.

Her name was Wren. She was perhaps fourteen, with ink-stained fingers and a kitchen knife wrapped in three layers of linen and tied with butcher's twine, held out in front of her like something that might bite. Which, Jude supposed, it might.

"It hums louder when I'm scared," Wren said. Her voice was steady but her knuckles were white around the bundle. "It's been humming for six days. My mother wanted me to throw it in the harbor. I couldn't."

"You couldn't," Jude repeated. Not *wouldn't*. The distinction mattered. She stepped back and opened the door wider. "Come in before the watch sees you. They've been told to report anything strange near the Guild, and you, girl, are currently the strangest thing in Meridian."

The counting-house had changed since Corvane. Jude had taken the auditor's desk—a promotion she had never asked for and could not, in honesty, refuse—and the great ledger room smelled different now: less of sealing wax and secrets, more of lamp oil and honest sweat. The factors worked with their doors open. It had taken months to convince them that open doors were not an invitation to theft but an inoculation against it.

Wren walked through the room the way all children did, wide-eyed, counting the brass lamps, the marble counters, the enormous wall of pigeonholed ledgers stretching floor to ceiling. But her eyes kept returning to the bundle in her own hands. And beneath the linen, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, Jude could feel the knife singing.

She felt it in her teeth. In the old hollow places where her gift used to live.

That was the cruelty of it. Ten years of silence, and then the Garden, and then the burning—and she had come out of that fire human, emptied, ordinary in the way she had spent a decade pretending to be. She should have been relieved. Some days she almost was. But the gift had left its shape behind, the way a removed tooth leaves a socket the tongue cannot stop exploring, and now every humming blade in the city found that hollow and rang against it like a bell against a tomb.

*You gave it up,* she reminded herself, climbing the stairs to her office. *You chose this.*

The reminder did not help. It never did.

* * *

In the office, with the door shut and the lamp turned high, Jude unwrapped the knife.

It was nothing. A cheap thing, carbon steel gone grey at the edge, a handle of river birch worn smooth by someone else's hand—it had belonged, Wren explained in a rush, to her grandmother, who had died last spring, who had never spoken of any gift, who had used it only for onions. But the steel remembered. When Jude closed her fingers around the handle, the humming resolved into something almost like language, the way surf resolves into individual waves if you listen long enough. Fragments. A woman's hands dicing roots. A winter kitchen. A fear so old and small it had fossilized into habit: *hide it, hide it, hide it.*

Three generations of hiding, Jude thought. The oath had ended at Ashfall Bridge, but its echo had traveled down bloodlines like a stain through water, teaching grandmothers to teach granddaughters that the humming must never be named.

"Well?" Wren said. She had pulled a chair close without asking, and her eyes were fixed on Jude's hands. "Can you make it stop?"

"I can't." Jude set the knife on the desk between them, still wrapped, because some part of her instinct insisted that bare steel in a Guild office was a confession. "No one can make it stop. That's the first lesson, if you want lessons. The humming isn't a flaw in the knife or in you. It's the knife remembering what people are, and wanting to be useful. It goes quiet when it's used honestly and loud when it's hidden. Like a conscience."

"My grandmother hid hers her whole life."

"And how was her life?"

Wren's jaw tightened. "Small," she said finally. "She never left the district. She flinched at soldiers. She made me promise—" the girl stopped, and something moved behind her face that was too old for fourteen, too familiar, and Jude recognized it because she had worn the same expression in a mirror for ten years. "She made me promise never to tell anyone about the humming. She died making me promise."

The lamp hissed. Downstairs, a factor laughed at something, the sound muffled by the floorboards, ordinary as bread.

"Wren." Jude leaned forward. "I'm going to tell you something, and then you're going to decide, and whatever you decide, you'll walk out of here tonight free and no one will follow you. Do you understand? This is not a recruitment. This is not an order. There is no oath waiting at the end of it."

"That's what everyone says right before the oath."

Jude laughed—a short, surprised sound—and Wren looked startled, then pleased, as though she'd won something.

"Yes," Jude said. "Exactly that. So believe nothing until you've seen the end of it. Here is what I'll tell you: I was a swordspeaker. Not a kitchen-hum, not a whisper in cutlery. The real thing. I could speak to steel and steel would answer. I fought at Ashfall Bridge, and when my side broke, my mentor bought my life with a vow of silence. I spent ten years pretending to be a bookkeeper while everything I was slept under my skin like a knife in linen." She turned her palms up, empty, human. "Then I burned the place where gifts like mine were grown. I came out of that fire without mine. And I am telling you plainly: I do not know if I would do it again. That is how complicated it is. Anyone who tells you it's simple is selling something."

Wren stared at her. Then, slowly, she reached across the desk and unwound the twine herself, folding back the linen layer by layer, until the knife lay bare on the wood.

It sang. Even Jude, giftless, could nearly hear it—or perhaps she was only remembering the hearing—and Wren's shoulders dropped an inch, the way a person relaxes when a secret finally leaves their body.

"It stopped hurting," Wren whispered. "Being near you. Why did it stop hurting?"

Because your hollow answers mine, Jude thought, and did not say it. Because two silences can recognize each other across a room. Instead she said: "Because you're not hiding it anymore. That's most of everything, girl. Most of the pain is the hiding."

* * *

Sefa was waiting in Jude's rooms that night, which meant she had let herself in with the spare key she had copied without permission, which meant she had news, which meant the wine was already poured.

"You took the girl upstairs," Sefa said, without preamble. She stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the lamplit traffic of the tidewalks below, and her reflection in the dark glass watched Jude instead. "Corvane's old informants would have paid a month's wages for that sight. The last swordspeaker, alone in a locked room with an awakened blade and a child."

"The last swordspeaker is dead, Sefa. I told you that. I buried her in the Marches along with everything else."

"The gift isn't dead." Sefa turned. She had a scar now, from the anchor-chains, a pale seam along her jaw that she wore like a receipt for goods delivered. "You told me yourself. Your visitor proves it. The Garden burned, but the seeds were already scattered—through bloodlines, through kitchens, through every grandmother who ever hid a humming knife. You didn't kill it. You just made it orphaned."

"Sefa—"

"No. You'll hear me." Sefa crossed the room and stood close, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for things that frightened her, which was a very short list and Jude knew all of it. "I grew up in a village that fed that Garden its first harvest. I have spent my whole life learning what happens to children who become *useful* to power. Corvane wanted to graft himself onto it. Before him, the Gardener grew it like a crop. Before *him*, gods know. And now you want to take a fourteen-year-old whose grandmother taught her to be afraid, and you want to train her. Train her, Jude. In a Guild full of merchants who would sell their mothers for margin, sitting on top of the only instruction manual for weapons that think."

"She's not a weapon."

"Neither were you." Sefa's eyes were very dark and very steady. "And Ashfall Bridge happened anyway."

The words landed where they were meant to. Jude sat down slowly on the edge of the bed, and for a moment the room was only lamplight and the distant slap of harbor water against pilings.

"I know," she said quietly. "You think I haven't lain awake doing exactly that arithmetic? Every night since she knocked on the salt door. I know what I am. I know what I was. If I teach her nothing, someone else finds her eventually—someone worse than me, someone with less grief and more ambition. The humming doesn't stay hidden forever, Sefa. Hers has been screaming for six days. The whole eastern district could probably hear it if they knew what to listen for."

"So teach her to hide it. Teach her your trick. Ten years of ledgers—that worked well enough."

"For me?" Jude looked up, and let Sefa see all of it, unarmored: the decade of smallness, the flinch at soldiers, the gift sleeping under her skin like a splinter working toward the heart. "You just finished telling me what my life was. Small. Afraid. Half a person wearing the other half as a disguise. You want me to hand that life to a child and call it safety? I called it survival, and it was, and it cost exactly as much as the war did, only paid out slowly, in installments, over ten years, so I could pretend it wasn't costing anything at all."

Sefa said nothing. But she sat down beside her on the bed, which from Sefa was a whole argument conceded.

"There's another way," Jude said. "There has to be. Not silence and not service. Something we haven't tried yet, because everyone who understood the gift was either trying to own it or bury it. Nobody ever just—" she gestured vaguely, helplessly, "—taught it. Like letters. Like sums. With the costs written next to the benefits, in the same hand, on the same page."

"Honest ledgers," Sefa said. Her mouth curved, wry and reluctant. "You're going to solve a thousand years of blood with bookkeeping."

"I solved the Guild with bookkeeping."

"You solved the Guild because the factors were already tired of being robbed." Sefa sighed, long and slow, and rubbed the scar on her jaw the way she did when she was surrendering to something. "All right. All right. But hear my terms, auditor, since apparently we're having terms again. She lives here, in the Guild, where I can watch who comes near her. She learns the history first—all of it, Ashfall and the harvests and the Gardener's grave—before she learns a single word of speech. And if the council tries to claim her, if any master sees an asset where I see a girl, we burn the whole arrangement and disappear her so thoroughly even the Marches forget her. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Jude exhaled, and realized she had been holding that breath for days. "You're more protective than I am."

"I'm more honest about it than you are. You've been protective since the moment she walked in. You just call it something else so it doesn't scare you."

* * *

The next morning, Jude found Wren in the counting-house yard behind the salt door, where the factors took their smoke and their arguments. The girl stood alone in the middle of the cobbles with her grandmother's knife held out at arm's length, and she was—Jude slowed, then stopped—she was *listening*. Eyes shut, head tilted, the morning fog beading in her hair. The knife hummed, and Wren's lips moved faintly, shaping sounds that weren't quite words, the way a child shapes sounds before grammar arrives.

"What are you doing?" Jude asked.

Wren's eyes flew open. For a heartbeat she looked guilty—the old reflex, the grandmother's reflex—and then, deliberately, she straightened her shoulders and did not hide the knife.

"It got louder when you came outside," she said. "Like it knows you. So I was asking it why."

"What did it say?"

"Nothing yet." Wren's chin lifted, defiant, hopeful, terrified. "But it was listening back. Wasn't it? That's how it starts?"

Jude looked at the girl—at the ink stains, the white knuckles, the stubborn spine, the whole unbearably familiar shape of her—and felt the future arrive, quiet as fog off the harbor, carrying with it every danger Sefa had named and several she hadn't. Somewhere in the city, other knives were humming. Other granddaughters lying awake, listening. The Garden was ash, but its seeds had already scattered, and they would sprout whether anyone tended them or not. That was the nature of growing things.

*Yes,* Jude thought. *That's how it starts.*

"Go get your coat," she said aloud. "And bring paper. From today, everything we learn gets written down—what the speech costs, what it gives, every mistake I ever made, starting with the oath itself. Honest records. That's the whole curriculum, girl. The rest is practice."

Wren ran for the door, and the knife sang in her hand like something waking.

Behind Jude, from the shadow of the doorway, Sefa spoke without turning her head. "A rider came in from the Marches an hour ago. Scholars, at the border stations—dozens of them, asking after the Garden's archive. Someone filed the true history in the public registry." A pause. "They're asking for the Guild by name, Jude. They'll be at our doors within the week."

Jude watched Wren disappear up the stairs, humming under her breath in time with a blade that had waited three generations to be spoken to.

"Then we'd better have honest answers ready," she said, "before the world starts checking our work."
