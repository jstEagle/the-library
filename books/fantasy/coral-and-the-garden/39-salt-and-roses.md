## 39. Salt and Roses

The counting-house smelled of ink and brine at dusk, and Jude had learned, over the long slow year, to love both equally.

She sat at Corvane's old desk—her desk now, though she had never had it renamed; names, she had decided, should be earned rather than seized—and worked by the last of the light coming through windows propped open against the evening heat. The sea came in with it: salt, weed, the tar-and-fish stink of the harbor at low tide, and beneath all of it, faint as memory, the sweetness of roses that did not grow anywhere in the city. She had stopped trying to find their source. Some things you carried rather than found.

Below her, in the cobbled yard between the counting-house and the old salt store, Tamsin was practicing.

Jude could hear her without listening—the particular hush that fell over sound when speech was being done properly, as though the air itself leaned in. The girl stood among the delivery crates with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back with a strip of red cloth that had once, Jude suspected, been a ribbon from a much finer life, holding a kitchen knife in both hands the way other children held kittens.

"Again," Tamsin said to herself, and then, to the knife, in a voice pitched low and careful as a child crossing ice: "*Be still.*"

From the yard came a small bright ringing, like a bell struck underwater.

*Be still,* and the blade obeyed. It was the first scale every swordspeaker learned—or every sword-speaker had learned, back when there were enough of them to have scales, exercises, gradations, a whole music of command. *Be still. Be sharp. Be true.* Simple words carrying impossible weight, because the weight was never in the words. It was in what you were willing to be answerable for after you said them.

Tamsin was seven months into her apprenticeship and could do three things reliably. *Be still.* *Come here,* spoken to iron within arm's reach. And once, memorably, catastrophically, *open*, which had unlatched every drawer, door, and padlock on the ground floor of the Meridian Guild simultaneously, including the strongroom, including the privy doors, including—Sefa still brought this up at least weekly—a crate of imported glassware that no one had wanted opened quite that fast.

Jude set down her pen and listened to the yard.

"*Be still.*" Ring. "*Be still.*" Ring. A pause, longer. Then, quieter, testing the edge of something new: "*Remember—*"

The ringing stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a held breath stops.

Jude was out of her chair before she knew she'd moved, taking the stairs two at a time, and she told herself it was only habit, only caution, only the ordinary vigilance of a woman who kept honest books—but her heart was going hard against her ribs, because she had said those exact words exactly that way once, ten years ago, on a bridge full of dying men, to a sword she had loved.

In the yard, the knife lay flat on the cobbles where Tamsin had dropped it. The girl stood over it, white-faced, hands pressed to her mouth.

"It talked back," Tamsin whispered when she saw Jude. "It's not supposed to talk back. You said they don't talk unless—" Her eyes went enormous. "Unless they remember someone."

Jude picked up the knife. It was warm. Kitchen steel, cheap, stamped with some cutler's mark out of the eastern isles, and underneath the warmth it hummed—not the dead rust-whisper of the Marches, nothing so broken, but a young sound, an eager one. A sound like a dog that has just understood its own name.

"It's not remembering someone," Jude said. "It's remembering you."

"But I've never—it's just a knife—"

"Every gift starts somewhere," Jude said. "Mine started with a fire poker." She turned the blade over, watching the last light run along the edge. "What did you say?"

"*Remember me.*"

"And what did it say back?"

Tamsin's chin trembled, and Jude realized with a lurch that the girl was not frightened so much as overwhelmed—standing in the first moment of being known by something that was not a person, which is a kind of loneliness and a kind of company all at once, and no child should meet it alone in a yard at dusk. No one should. That had been the whole lesson of everything, and she had almost missed it again.

"What," Jude said gently, "did it say."

"It said—" Tamsin swallowed. "It said, *I'm here.*"

Jude closed her hand around the knife's grip and felt the hum settle against her palm like a cat leaning into a known touch. Somewhere behind her ribs, in the place where her own gift had lived and burned and gone, something ached. Not grief, exactly. Not anymore. Grief had been the first year. This was more like the feeling of hearing a language you used to speak fluently, spoken now by someone younger, badly, beautifully, with your old accent.

"Well," she said. "Then you'd better learn how to answer."

* * *

"You're coddling her."

"I'm teaching her."

"You're teaching her the way you wish someone had taught you, which is admirable and also completely unworkable, because you are not seven." Sefa Ilvane came through the connecting door from the factors' hall with a ledger under one arm and the expression of a woman who had spent the afternoon losing arguments to arithmetic. She dropped into the chair across the desk without asking, which was another thing about this year: people sat in Jude's office without asking, and instead of feeling like trespass it felt like weather.

"The Guild charter," Sefa continued, flipping the ledger open, "specifies apprenticeships of five years minimum. Five. With examinations. I checked—I wrote half the revised charter myself, I know what it says."

"She's not a factor apprentice. She's—"

"A swordspeaker. Yes. Which means she needs structure, supervision, and a curriculum, not dusk sessions in a yard where anyone can wander past and hear her saying *be still* to a paring knife." Sefa tapped the ledger. "Which brings me to the actual reason I climbed two flights of stairs to annoy you. We have a problem with the archive requests. Again."

"The scholars?"

"The scholars are fine. Scholars queue, scholars pay fees, scholars leave. It's the others." Sefa turned the ledger around so Jude could read it. The entry was in Sefa's precise, upright hand, each figure ruled twice. "Three visitors this month who asked not about the Garden's history but about its location. Two who asked about survivors. One who asked—" Sefa's jaw tightened— "whether the tree could regrow from root-cuttings. He tipped the archivist a silver mark to look away while he copied a page."

Jude read the entry twice. The numbers were clean. The implication was not.

"Did he get the page?"

"He got a copy with the location references blotted out. He doesn't know that yet. He thinks he stole something." Sefa allowed herself the smallest smile, the one she saved for victories that cost nothing. "Brac followed him to the ferry dock. If he takes ship east, we'll know who he writes to. If he takes ship south—" She shrugged. "South is the Marches. There's nothing left down there but ash and honest mud."

"There's the rose," Jude said quietly.

The room went still in the way rooms did when the rose was mentioned, which was often lately, and always the same way—as though the word itself were a door everyone had agreed not to open and yet kept finding themselves standing in front of.

"That thing is a curiosity," Sefa said finally. "A flower growing out of a burned field. The botanists can't classify it, the priests argue about it, and every month some pilgrim comes back claiming it cured their cough or made them dream true. It's a rose, Jude. It's not a warning."

"It grew where power died," Jude said. "Nothing else grows there. The soldiers won't even crumble near it—they stand cleaner, if anything, like it's holding them up." She thought of her visit in the spring: the gray marsh, the ranks of petrified men mid-swing, rusted forever toward blows they would never land, and at the center of all that silence a single thornless rose, red as a struck match, turning very slowly on its stem to face whoever came. Watching. Harmless. Watching. "I left it uncut."

"You left it uncut," Sefa repeated, in the tone she used for entries that didn't balance.

"Some things you watch," Jude said. "You don't have to own them to keep them."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her close the ledger and soften by a degree—which, from Sefa, was a full surrender. "Fine. Watch your rose. But put guards on the archive, formalize the girl's training, and write it all down properly, because I refuse to spend another quarter explaining to the council why our ledgers contain phrases like *unclassifiable botanical anomaly, see personal notes of Auditor Kavanagh*. Personal notes are not records, Jude. Records are how we stay honest."

"Records are how we stay honest," Jude agreed. "That's practically your motto."

"It's our motto. I just say it louder." Sefa stood, gathered her ledger, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame—another thing about this year, these small hesitations that had grown where suspicion used to live. "The girl said *remember me* today and the knife answered. Brac told me. He hears things, the yard carries sound." Her voice was carefully neutral. "What will you teach her first? Really first. Not the scales."

Jude looked out the window. Below, Tamsin had retrieved her knife and was walking the yard's perimeter alone, deliberately, touching each iron hinge and nail and gate-ring as she passed, murmuring to it, introducing herself. Making friends with the world's metal one piece at a time.

"Honesty," Jude said. "Same as everyone else. Just earlier."

Sefa snorted softly and went out, and the stairwell took her footsteps down and away, and the counting-house settled into its evening quiet.

* * *

Dusk thickened. The clerks had gone home in twos and threes, calling goodnights through the open windows, and the lamps of the harbor district began to bloom along the tidewalks below—amber, green, the occasional fish-oil flare of the market boats coming in late with the tide. Sea air moved through the counting-house in slow draughts, lifting the corners of loose papers, carrying up the sounds of the city settling: cart wheels, a drunk singing somewhere off-key and content, gulls arguing over harbor leavings.

Jude lit one lamp and let the rest of the room go dark around its circle.

On the desk before her lay the ledger she had started a year ago, on the road home, in an inn at Halfway with Sefa fevered beside her and the smoke of the Garden still visible on the southern horizon. It was not a Guild ledger. It belonged to no institution, answered to no council, and would never be filed in any archive. It was bound in plain gray calfskin, unadorned, the kind of book a merchant's daughter might use for household accounts, and it contained one entry per day for three hundred and sixty-four days.

She turned back through it now, reading her own year.

Early pages, written in a hand that shook: *Day 3. Sefa's fever broke. She asked if I regretted it. I said ask me in a year.* Later pages, steadier: *Day 41. First council session. Three masters walked out. The factors stayed. The factors always stay—that is the whole difference between them and the masters, and I was a fool to take a decade to learn it.* Pages from winter, terse, practical: *Day 118. Audited the storm-in-a-bottle trade. Closed it. The buyer wept. The seller did not. Draw your own conclusions about which of them ever really owned it.*

And pages like this one, from spring: *Day 203. Tamsin arrived. Knife humming. No idea why. Everyone assumed I would turn her away or bind her to silence. As if those were the only two tools I owned.*

Her pen hovered over the fresh page.

*Day 365.*

What did you write, at the end of a year, about the burning of a garden and the rebuilding of a guild and the slow unclenching of a fist you hadn't known you'd been making for ten years? What did you write about a girl in a yard learning to say *be still*, and a knife that answered *I'm here*, and a rival who had become the loudest conscience in the building, and a mercenary called Brac who now guarded the archive door and flinched, still, whenever anyone said his name kindly?

Jude dipped her pen. Wrote:

*Day 365. The ledgers balance. All of them. The Guild trades in difficult things and turns away impossible ones, and the difference, it turns out, was never in the goods. It was in whether we admitted what we were doing. Tamsin spoke her first true word today and the iron answered her, and she was not afraid, or rather she was afraid and did it anyway, which is the same thing done properly. I signed my name below without shortening it.*

She paused. Looked at the window, at the dark water beyond the roofs, at the far south where the horizon held nothing visible but held everything regardless: ash, mud, petrified men, a rose.

*I am not what I was,* she wrote. *I am not sorry.*

Then, beneath, in the space she had left blank on every page of this ledger since the first day, waiting to understand what belonged there:

*Final entry of the year. The Garden is ash. The war is ash. The gift survives in small hands, scattered, uncounted—and it will be taught honestly or not at all, starting here. I keep this record so that someday, if the world asks what we bought with all that burning, the answer will be written down: we bought the right to stop pretending.*

She signed it.

*Jude Winterbourne, Auditor of the Meridian Guild. Last of the oath-bound swordspeakers — and the first to break the oath by speaking freely.*

Her full name, unshortened, in her own hand, in a ledger she could defend line by line. The ink dried slowly in the sea-damp air, and she watched it dry without impatience. Ten years she had hidden the name like contraband. Now it was simply a fact on a page, and facts, honestly kept, needed no defending.

Down in the yard, faintly, Tamsin's voice rose one more time—small, clear, unhurried: "*Goodnight,*" spoken to the gate hinges, to the nails, to the knives sleeping in the kitchen. And from the iron, in a chorus too soft for anyone but a swordspeaker to catch, the answering hum, like a house full of small creatures settling down.

Jude smiled, blew out the lamp, and stood for a moment in the dark with the salt wind moving past her and the roses-that-were-not-there sweetening the air.

Tomorrow there would be council squabbles and archive guards to appoint and a curriculum to design and Sefa to argue with, gloriously, at length. Tomorrow there would be the quarterly report, and the letter from the eastern scholars, and the question she had been deferring all month about what, exactly, to tell the girl about Ashfall Bridge.

But tonight—

Tonight, far to the south, in a drowned country where nothing should grow, a thornless rose was turning slowly on its stem in the starlight, keeping its patient watch over ash and rust and the peaceful crumbling of old wars. Jude could not have said how she knew this. She only knew it the way she knew the tide would come in, the way she had once known what a blade wanted before it spoke.

She only knew that tomorrow morning, there would be a visitor at the salt door.

There was always, now, a visitor at the salt door.
