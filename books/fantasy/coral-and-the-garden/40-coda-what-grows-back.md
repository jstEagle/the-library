## 40. Coda: What Grows Back

The road into the Drowned Marches had changed in a year, and Jude noticed every change the way a woman notices grey hairs—each one proof that time had kept moving whether she watched it or not.

The ferry at Halfway now took coin.

That was the first miracle, and she still marveled at it every visit. The ferryman's barge sat lower in the black water than she remembered, weighted with cargo instead of memories: sacks of salt, bolts of cloth, crates of honest trade goods bound for the settlements creeping back into the drowned country's edges. The ferryman himself—a new man, or perhaps the old one grown a face again, she'd never been certain—held out a broad palm for her fare and asked for three coppers without ceremony, without hunger, without that terrible patient look of a thing that fed on what people could least afford to lose.

Jude paid him like anyone. She paid him gladly. Three coppers for a crossing felt like the cheapest thing she had ever bought, and she had once traded in bottled storms.

"You come through often," the ferryman said as he poled them across the flooded plain. Not an accusation. Just an observation, the way men on water mark regulars by their weather-worn boots.

"Once a season," she said. "I keep accounts."

He nodded as if that explained everything, and perhaps it did. In the year since the Garden burned, everyone had learned to explain themselves through trade. It was safer than explaining themselves through power.

The far bank came up out of the mist, and beyond it the land rose in slow terraces of ash-grey earth where green was beginning its long argument with ruin. Jude stepped off the barge, adjusted the strap of her pack, and began the walk she had made eleven times before.

* * *

The petrified soldiers were going home.

She passed the first of them an hour in, standing where he had stood for decades, sword rusted mid-swing, face frozen in the ancient fury of a battle no one alive remembered starting. Except that now his fury was softening. The stone of him flaked at the edges. His raised arm had settled by degrees, the way a mountain settles, until the sword hung almost at his side rather than raised to strike. Moss grew in the seams of his armor, green threading through grey, and small pale flowers—nothing magical, just ordinary marshflowers doing what ordinary things do—had rooted in the crack between his breastplate and his shoulder.

By the end of the day's walk she counted dozens of them changed. Some had crumbled entirely, leaving heaps of mineral dust shaped vaguely like kneeling men, already colonized by reeds and rushes. Others stood half-finished, as if time itself had paused mid-sculpture to reconsider its work. One young soldier—she thought of him as young; there was something unfinished about the jaw beneath the stone—had fallen gently sideways into the water and lay there with his open hand cupping a small bright pool where tadpoles swam.

Sefa would have made a joke. *At least they're finally lying down on the job.* But Sefa hadn't come this trip. Someone had to mind the counting-house, and Sefa guarded that place now the way she had once guarded her own secrets—with her whole scarred heart—and so Jude walked alone, which was fitting. This was a walk that belonged to being alone.

She stopped at the fallen soldier and crouched beside him. Ten years ago, in another life, she could have touched his sword and known his name, his last thought, the color of the sky over his childhood. Now she touched nothing. She only looked, and let him be a stranger, and found that strangers deserved dignity too.

"Rest well," she said, in her own voice, her only voice, plain as ledger ink. "Whoever you were."

The tadpoles scattered at her shadow and regathered. She walked on.

* * *

She reached Thornwall at dusk.

Or what had been Thornwall. The great hedge of living brambles that had once tested intruders with visions of their deepest want had died with the Garden, and what remained was a black rampart of withered thorns taller than the city walls of Meridian, brittle as old bone, rustling when the wind moved through it with a sound like ten thousand dry whispers all trying at once to remember what they used to want.

Jude made camp in its shadow. She built a small fire, ate bread and hard cheese and a pear from her own orchard—the Guild kept an orchard now; she had insisted; growing things that simply grew, without ambition, seemed like a healthy discipline for an institution that had spent a century trading in impossibilities—and watched the last light fail against the dead hedge.

Once, this wall had shown her an empty ledger and peace, and she had wondered for years afterward whether that was truly her deepest want or merely the want she could bear to look at. Now, warm at a small fire with no gift humming under her skin and no oath pressing on her tongue, she suspected they were the same thing, and that she had simply become honest enough to know it.

She slept without dreams, mostly. In the morning she cut an opening in the bramble wall with her knife—it cut easily now, dead wood, asking nothing of her blood—and went through.

* * *

The Garden was a graveyard wearing a meadow's clothes, and every season it wore the disguise better.

The terraces still descended in their vast steps toward the hidden vault below, but grass had taken the paths, and wildflowers had taken the beds where power had once been cultivated in rows like crops. The topiary golems were gone entirely—not toppled, not broken, but *gone*, dissolved the way a held breath dissolves when the word releasing it is finally spoken. Jude liked to think they had simply been allowed to finish becoming shrubs. Everything here was finishing, these days. Becoming what it should have been all along.

She walked the outer court slowly, as she always did, letting the place register her the way a ledger registers an entry. The burned tree's root-vault lay sealed beneath a slab of living rock that Brac and four of the freed Hollow Men had rolled into place with ropes and sweat and no speech at all except the ordinary kind, the kind two men use to count a lift together—*ready, and, down.* They had refused payment. Brac had said, in his slow way, "Some jobs you do because you're the one who remembers your name."

The courier's grave was there too, near the sealed vault, marked with a simple stone. *Maren Ostrell, Courier, Third Bell. She carried what she was given.* Jude had learned the name from a Guild shipping manifest nine months after the burial—one line in one ledger, filed correctly, cross-referenced, signed. That was how everything precious revealed itself eventually: as a line in a ledger, if you kept honest ones. Maren Ostrell had pressed a map into Jude's hands and died saying her true surname aloud, and all these months later Jude still did not know why Maren had chosen her, whether chance or some dying cunning or the old network of swordspeaker sympathies reaching up out of history one last time to tap a Winterbourne on the shoulder. She had stopped needing to know. Some debts were best honored rather than audited.

She laid a sprig of rosemary on the stone. She always did.

Then she climbed the long terraces to the heart of it, where the ground was still black with the burning, and found what she had come to find.

* * *

It bloomed from a seam in the ash, alone, exactly where the great tree had drunk its last draught of oaths.

One rose. Thornless. Its stem was pale green and unarmored, naked against the char, as if it had decided that defenses were for a different kind of life. Its petals were the deep coral-red of the flower that had marked the map—that crawling inked bloom of thorns that had started everything—and it turned on its stem, very slowly, to face her when she came near.

Jude had watched it for a full year now. Once a season, eleven visits, and each time she came prepared for it to be different. Larger. Hungrier. Surrounded by seedlings. Whispering in her mentor's stolen voice, offering restoration, offering everything the oath had taken and the burning had finished taking.

Each time it was only a rose.

It had not spread. Not one seedling, not one runner, not one shoot anywhere in the acres of ash. Botanists from the southern colleges had begged cuttings—they wrote letters now, formal petitions through proper channels, because the Guild's archive held the Garden's true history and scholars had begun to arrive like pilgrims, hungry for knowledge that had nearly ended the world—and every petition sat unanswered in a drawer in Jude's desk. She was not ready to decide what this flower was owed. Perhaps she never would be. Deciding slowly, she had learned, was also a form of care.

She knelt in the ash beside it. Her knees left prints in the grey, and she noticed—as she noticed every visit—that the prints filled in behind her within moments, ash sifting level, the ground smoothing itself with a patience that might have been wind and might have been memory settling. The residue of power, the scholars called it, in their careful papers. Harmless, they believed. Beautiful, they agreed. Watched, she required.

"Hullo," she said.

The rose turned its coral face toward her voice. It had no voice of its own. That was the first thing she had confirmed, that first raw week after the burning when she had come expecting grief to have a sound and found only silence where the humming used to live. The tree had spoken with her mentor's voice. The swords had whispered in rust and blood. The rose said nothing at all. Whatever it was—last seed of the source, final gesture of a power that had eaten oaths and makers both—it had spent its voice in the burning, and what remained was only beauty, doing the one thing beauty does, which is exist where you can see it and ask nothing.

She had tested it, that first year. She was not ashamed of that; she was proud of it. She had brought instruments. She had brought a scholar with steady hands and no gift and nothing to lose, and let him take measurements of light and soil and the strange warmth that pooled around the stem on cold mornings. She had even, once, brought a knife, and held it near the stem, and waited to feel the old resonance rise in her blood like a tide.

Nothing. The rose held still. The knife stayed a knife. Under her skin there was only the ordinary weather of an ordinary body, and she had wept then, kneeling in the ash with a blade in her hand, wept for the gift and the war and the mentor whose face the ferryman had taken and whose voice the tree had stolen, and the rose had simply turned its face toward her weeping and gone on being beautiful, which was somehow the most forgiving thing she had ever witnessed.

That was the day she understood what it was. Not a remnant. A remainder. Everything the Garden had been—every stolen oath, every grafted voice, every soldier petrified mid-swing and every courier dying against a salt door—had passed through the fire of her choice and condensed into this: one flower, harmless, thornless, turning toward whatever came near. Power's residue. Proof that the strength had existed, and proof that it had ended, both at once, in a single bloom you could cover with two hands.

Hope, she thought, tempered by vigilance. That was the whole lesson, and the rose taught it without a word, and she had written it into no ledger because some entries belonged only to the person who made them.

"I'm leaving you uncut," she told it, as she always told it. "Same as last time. Same as next time, probably, though I'll bring the question again, because I promised the council I'd review it every season, and I keep my promises now. All of them. Even the small ones." She smiled at herself—at the sound of an auditor reciting review schedules to a flower—and the smile felt earned. "The girl asked about you, you know. Wren. She wanted to come along this trip, and Sefa said no, too soon, and Wren argued—I think she gets that from me, gods help us—and finally I told her the truth. I told her there's a flower growing where the worst thing in the world used to live, and when she's older, and steadier in her scales of speech, she can come see it and tell me what she thinks it means. And she said—" Jude laughed softly, remembering. "She said, *'It means things grow back different.'* Out of the mouths of apprentices. She's twelve and she's righter than the whole southern college of botany."

The rose turned, slowly, following some warmth or attention only it perceived. A bee had found it—impossible, surely, this deep in the Marches, miles from any hive—and yet there it was, working the coral petals with industry and complete indifference to history. The bee didn't know what had burned here. The bee knew there was nectar. Jude found that she loved the bee with a fierceness that surprised her: this small ordinary worker, crossing the ruined world on its own errands, finding sweetness in the exact spot where the sweetness had once cost everything.

Maybe that was the whole of it. Maybe that was what grew back—not the power, never the power, she would make sure of that with audits and seasons and her plain unmagical vigilance for the rest of her life—but the *ordinary*, returning to fill the space the extraordinary had vacated. Bees. Marshflowers in cracked breastplates. Soldiers crumbling gently into soil that would feed reeds that would feed birds. A ferry that took coppers. A Guild that traded in difficult things honestly arrived at. A girl with a humming kitchen knife learning, in a yard by the sea, that a gift was a thing you could hold up to daylight and study instead of bury.

She rose. Her knees ached, forty-one years old and honest about it. She brushed ash from her coat and stood a moment longer in the wide grey silence, looking down at the single bloom turning patiently on its stem.

"One more season," she said. "Watched. Untouched. We'll see what you decide to be."

She did not kneel again, and she did not touch it, and she turned her back on it with the confidence of a woman who had checked the locks of something that no longer needed locking—but checked anyway, because hope without vigilance was just the old hunger wearing flowers.

Behind her, in the ash, the rose went on blooming. Before her, the long road home led through a country quietly forgetting how to be haunted, past soldiers lying down at last, toward a counting-house at dusk where a lamp would be lit and accounts would need keeping and someone would argue with her over the price of salt, and she would love every minute of the argument, because it was hers, and ordinary, and true.

Jude Winterbourne walked out of the Drowned Marches for the twelfth time, carrying nothing, leaving the rose uncut, and the road took her home.
