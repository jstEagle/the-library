## 1. The Valley That Forgets Magic

The dying woman's last words were about plums.

Rosalind Okonkwo sat at the bedside with a rib bone braced against her knee and her finest chisel in her hand, waiting for Maren Cottle to get there — to the words that mattered, the ones worth the weight of bone. But Maren kept circling back to the plum tree behind her mother's house, the one that had come down in a storm forty years ago, and how the fruit had rotted on the grass because no one had time to pick it all, and how she had thought about that waste every day since, every single day, as though the plums had been a debt she owed the world.

"Say it plain, then," Rosalind said gently. "What do you want carved?"

Maren's eyes, clouded as river glass, found her. "That I was here. That I loved my sister more than I ever told her, and she knew it anyway, but I want it written. I want it in something that lasts."

Rosalind nodded and set the chisel's edge to the bone. "Then that's what it will say."

The words came out of her the way they always did — not written, not planned, but *found*, as if they had been waiting inside the bone all along and she was only uncovering them. Her hands knew the work. Her grandmother had taught her the angles, the way to hold the bone against the grain so it would not split, the trick of warming it first in your palms so the chisel moved like a boat through still water. *Bone remembers being alive*, her grandmother used to say. *Be kind to it, and it will be kind to you.*

*She loved her sister more than she could say, and the sister knew it anyway, but she wanted it written. She wanted it in something that lasts.*

Rosalind carved it in the old script, the one that predated the valley, the one that looked less like letters than like the tracks birds leave on fresh snow. When she finished, she blew the bone dust from the grooves and held the rib up to the lamplight. It caught the flame and held it, a small warm glow moving inside the bone like a swallowed star.

Maren made a sound — not quite a word. Her hand, papery and light, closed around Rosalind's wrist.

"He'll come for it," she whispered. "My sister's boy. Tell him — tell him it's for him."

"It will be in his hands before the week is out," Rosalind said. "I promise you."

Maren Cottle died an hour before dawn, quietly, in the way of people who have finally finished saying what they needed to say. Rosalind sat with her until the light came up gray over the hills, because she had learned long ago that the dead deserve a witness, and because in Hollowmere there was no one else to sit with them.

Outside, the valley was doing what it always did at first light: forgetting.

That was the truth of Hollowmere, the truth Rosalind had known so long it had worn smooth as a river stone. Magic could not cross the boundary stones. Whatever came in — a charm, a curse, a whisper of the far country's power — shed itself at the ring of standing stones like a coat left at a door. The valley was a pocket of plainness in a world that crackled elsewhere. Wheat grew here because wheat grew anywhere. People died here because people died everywhere. But nothing *worked* here. No charms on the doorways, no blessings on the wells, no hexes on the unfaithful.

And no voices from the dead.

That was the part Rosalind had never told anyone, not fully, not even her grandmother, who had perhaps guessed. Beyond the stones, her gift was not a gift at all but a kind of hearing — the dead spoke constantly, endlessly, in every language and none, a tide of voices that had nearly drowned her the one time she had stood at the boundary as a girl and reached her hand past the stones. Her grandmother had pulled her back by the collar, white-faced, and had never spoken of it again.

But here, inside the valley, the dead were silent. What Rosalind had was only the carving. The words came to her, and she put them into bone, and the bone held them — that was all. A craft. A trade. Something a sensible woman in a sensible valley could do with her hands.

She wrapped Maren's rib in linen, tucked it into her satchel, and stepped out into a morning that smelled of woodsmoke and turned earth.

* * *

Hollowmere in autumn was a place that seemed designed by someone who loved the idea of valleys more than valleys themselves. The hills came down close and green on either side, stitched with dry-stone walls older than anyone's grandmother. The river ran shallow and quick over brown stones. The village itself was a single long street of slate-roofed houses, a mill, a tavern called the Drowned Bell, and — at the far end, where the street gave up and became a footpath — the boundary stones, gray and lichen-eaten, standing in their ancient ring like patient teeth.

Rosalind's workshop sat between the mill and the church, in a building that had been a bone-carver's workshop for longer than the church had stood. The villagers were practical about this. They needed what she did. Every family in the valley would, sooner or later, climb her steps with a dying relative or a fresh grief and ask her to make the words last. They were grateful in the way people are grateful for the undertaker and the midwife — warmly, and from a distance.

They did not invite her to things.

It was not cruelty. Rosalind had spent thirty years learning the difference, and she had gotten very good at it, the way you get good at reading weather. It was that her work touched the one thing everyone in the valley preferred not to think about, and so she had become a kind of standing stone herself — useful, permanent, and slightly wrong to stand too near. She was forty-one years old and had lived her whole life in Hollowmere, and there were people in the village who could not tell you her first name, only *the bone-carver*.

She carried this the way she carried her satchel — without complaint, without even much thought, the strap worn smooth where it crossed her shoulder. Loneliness, she had found, was like that. It did not crush you. It just fit, eventually, the way an old coat fits, and you stopped noticing the weight of it, and one day you caught your reflection in a shop window and saw how it hung on you and thought: *ah. So that's what I've been wearing.*

The morning's errands took her down the long street. She delivered Maren's rib to the sister's boy — a broad, red-eyed man named Tam, who turned the bone over in his huge hands and wept without embarrassment, which Rosalind appreciated — and then she bought bread, and then she was passing the Drowned Bell when the tavern-keeper's wife, Bess Harrow, came out onto the step with the expression of a woman who has been lying in wait.

"There you are." Bess planted herself in Rosalind's path. She was a small, round, unstoppable woman, and she had been trying to arrange Rosalind's life for eleven years, ever since Rosalind's mother died. "Thursday. You're still coming Thursday."

"I said I'd think about Thursday."

"You thought about it all of last week, which means yes." Bess handed her a loaf she had not asked for, which was Bess's way of closing negotiations. "His name is Theo Vance. A cartographer. Passing through, doing some survey work for the county — he'll be in the valley a month, maybe two. He asked Berran at the mill if there was anywhere decent to eat, and I said the tavern, and I said — casually, mind — that there were interesting people in Hollowmere."

Rosalind looked at her. "You said me."

"I said interesting people, and then he asked, and I may have mentioned a bone-carver." Bess's chin came up, defiant. "You're forty-one, Rosalind Okonkwo. You carve dead people's last words into bone and you talk to no one but dead people's relatives, and the last time you so much as had supper with a man who wasn't family was—"

"I remember when it was."

"Then Thursday. Seven o'clock. He's got kind eyes, Berran says. And hands—" Bess paused, savoring it. "Berran says he draws while he talks. Little maps on the margins of everything. Napkins, table edges. A man who can't stop drawing the world."

Rosalind stood in the street with the unwanted loaf in her arms and felt the old coat of her loneliness shift on her shoulders — not fall away, nothing so dramatic, but shift, the way a coat shifts when someone takes your arm.

"Thursday," she said at last. "But if it's terrible, Bess, I'm carving your name into something unflattering."

Bess laughed all the way back inside.

* * *

Thursday came gray and cold, with a wind off the hills that smelled like the first argument of winter. Rosalind spent the afternoon unable to work — three times she set a bone in her bench-vise and three times took it out again, because her hands would not steady and the words would not come. She had carved last words for two hundred people. She could not think of a first sentence.

In the end she wore the dark green dress, the one that made her look less like a woman who worked alone with dead men's bones, and she walked down the street at seven o'clock exactly, having decided that arriving early would look eager and arriving late would look like she'd had to be persuaded, which she had, but there was no need to advertise it.

Theo Vance was already at the corner table, and he was drawing.

He had a map spread out before him — an actual survey map, inked and precise — but his pencil was not on it. It was on the margin of a leather notebook, moving in quick sure strokes, and what he was drawing was the view from the window: the mill, the river, the hills going dark. He drew the way other men breathed, without deciding to. He was perhaps thirty-five, lean, with ink stains on his fingers and a face that seemed built for being interested in things.

He looked up, saw her, and did the thing that decided her, though she would not understand until later why it mattered so much: he stopped drawing. He closed the notebook, gave her his whole attention, and said, "You must be the interesting people. I'm Theo. Theo Vance. Bess said seven, and I got here at six out of nerves, so you've caught me mid-ambush of the view."

"Rosalind." She sat. "Bess said you draw."

"Bess," he said gravely, "has been extremely thorough. She told me you carve bone, that you've lived here all your life, that you're the most interesting person in the valley, and that I should on no account mention the first three things, which of course means I've now mentioned all three."

Despite herself — despite thirty years of practice at keeping the world at the length of a chisel — Rosalind laughed.

The dinner went the way dinners almost never went. He asked her about her work and actually listened to the answer, leaning in, asking the questions no one asked: *What do the words feel like when they come? Do you choose them, or do they choose you? What happens to a last word after it's carved — does it stay warm?* No one had ever asked if the bone stayed warm. She told him it did, a little, for a while, and he wrote that down, and she found she did not mind.

In return he told her about maps — how a map was a lie you agreed to believe, how every mapmaker's true work was deciding what to leave out, how he'd once surveyed a coastline for a duke and been ordered to move a river two miles east because the duke's grandfather had owned land there. "The country doesn't exist," he said. "The map exists. I've started to feel guilty about it, which is how I ended up here, mapping a valley nobody's ever bothered to map because there's nothing here worth mapping."

"There's nothing here worth mapping," Rosalind agreed. "That's the whole point of it."

"Is it?" He was looking at her with that interested face, and she felt the strange, terrifying sensation of being *seen* — not the bone-carver, not the standing stone, but the woman inside the coat. "I don't know. There's a boundary ring at the end of your street that no one in the village will walk past after dark. There's a church with no bell. There's a workshop older than the church. From where I sit, this valley is the most interesting unmapped thing in the county."

He turned his notebook around and showed her what he'd drawn while they talked — she hadn't even noticed — and it was her. Not flattered, not idealized: her, in the green dress, hands resting on the table, with an expression she did not recognize as her own and which looked, unbearably, like hope.

She should have said something. She opened her mouth to say something.

And that was when she saw what he had drawn on the page beneath — the page he'd covered earlier, while waiting, while she was still walking down the street toward him.

It was a building. A palace, drawn in the same quick sure strokes: towers, terraces, a great gate like a mouth, and above it all a crown worked into the stonework — a crown that was not a crown at all but something *hollow*, a ring with nothing inside it, drawn with a precision that made her chest go cold, because she knew it.

She had carved it. Two winters ago, a dying peddler who had come from far over the hills had gripped her hands and raved about a palace he'd seen in his youth, and she had carved his terror into a skull's crown bone, and the palace in her memory matched this one line for line — a place that did not exist on any map, a place Theo Vance could not possibly have seen.

"Theo," she said slowly. "Where did you draw that?"

He looked down at the page, and the interest in his face curdled into something else — confusion, then a kind of dawning unease, as though he were a man realizing the ground beneath him had been drawn too.

"I don't know," he said. "I've never seen it before in my life."
