## 2. A Date with Theo Vance

Rosalind spent forty minutes deciding what to wear, which was thirty-nine minutes longer than she had spent on any decision that week, including the one about whether to accept Mrs. Halloway's dying wish to be carved into the lintel of her own front door.

She settled on the green dress. Not because it was beautiful — it was old, and the hem had been mended twice with thread that didn't quite match — but because it was the only thing she owned that made her look like a woman who went on dates, rather than a woman who sat alone in a workshop smelling of bone dust and tallow, talking to people who were no longer strictly people.

"You'll do," she told the mirror.

The mirror said nothing. In Hollowmere, even the mirrors kept their opinions to themselves.

* * *

The Lamplight Tavern smelled of woodsmoke and spilled cider, and it was half full of the living, which was as crowded as Rosalind liked anything to be. She arrived early, chose the corner table where the candle burned steadiest, and arranged her hands on the table like a woman who had practiced being at ease and was still rehearsing.

He was late by four minutes. She counted. Then the door swung open and a man came in shaking rain off his coat, and she knew him immediately, though they'd never met — not by sight, but by the way he looked at the room. He looked at it the way she imagined a man looks at an unexplored coastline: hungry, measuring, already drawing it in his head.

"Rosalind?" He crossed to her table with an apologetic smile. "Theo Vance. I'm sorry — the bridge road flooded, and I stopped to sketch the water level, which is a deeply unromantic thing to admit on arrival."

"It's fine." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. She cleared her throat. "You sketch floods?"

"I sketch everything." He sat down across from her, unwinding his scarf. Up close he was younger than his voice suggested — maybe thirty, with ink stains on his fingers that no amount of scrubbing would ever fully lift, and a small scar through one eyebrow like a cartographer's mark on a face. "My mother says I drew maps before I could write. Apparently my first word was *north*."

"What was your second?"

"*Farther north.*"

And to her own considerable surprise, Rosalind laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate hinge in winter, but it came out.

* * *

The trouble with dates, Rosalind had decided years ago, was that they required you to be interesting, and she had spent so long being useful that she'd forgotten how to be anything else. When people in Hollowmere learned what she did, they did one of two things: they leaned away, politely, the way one leans from a butcher's block, or they leaned in too close, wanting their grandmother's last words preserved, wanting their own futures read out of marrow.

Theo Vance did neither.

"A bone-carver," he said, when she finally admitted it, turning his cup slowly on its saucer. "That's — all right, I have questions, and I promise none of them are rude, but I can't guarantee the order."

"Ask."

"Do you carve before death or after?"

"After. Always after." She traced the rim of her cup. "A person's last words belong to them while they're alive. I'm just — the postman. I take what they leave behind and put it somewhere it won't rot."

"Where does it go?"

"Into the bone. Whatever bone they choose, or their family chooses, or—" she shrugged, "—whatever's nearest, if it's urgent. The words sink in. They stay legible for as long as the bone holds together. Centuries, sometimes."

"And you can read them back?"

"I can hear them." She said it carefully, watching his face. This was the part where most people's eyes flickered — toward the door, toward superstition, toward pity. "Not the way you're thinking. It's not voices in my head. It's more like... standing in a room where someone has just finished speaking. The words are still in the air. Bone holds the air longer than flesh does."

"That's the most beautiful description of anything I've ever heard," Theo said, "and I once interviewed a woman who claimed to have mapped the underside of a cloud."

"Did you believe her?"

"I believed her map was gorgeous. That's usually enough."

He was doing something dangerous, this man. He was making her want to talk. Not perform, not deflect, not manage the conversation the way she managed everything else — but actually talk, about the work, about the valley, about the strange gray loneliness of hearing the dead murmur in every keepsake box in Hollowmere while the living kept their distance.

"So why maps?" she asked, to steady herself. "Why does a man dedicate his life to drawing places he's never been?"

Theo's grin turned sheepish. "Because I've never been anywhere either. Isn't that absurd? A cartographer who's never left the valley. But there are books — old ones, in the archive — travel accounts, fragments of atlases from before the boundary stones went up. I redraw them. Correct them. Sometimes I invent the parts that are missing." He hesitated, then pulled a battered leather folio from inside his coat. "Can I show you something? It's either going to impress you or make you think I'm mad, and honestly I've lost the ability to tell which."

"Those are usually the same thing," Rosalind said, and he laughed, and opened the folio.

* * *

His sketches were extraordinary.

Not accurate — she had no way to judge accuracy — but *alive*, in a way that made her chest ache. Coastlines drawn with confident, sweeping strokes. Mountain ranges shaded so finely they seemed to cast real shadows on the page. Cities rendered in tiny obsessive detail, down to individual rooftops, individual windows, a single figure crossing a bridge that existed nowhere except in Theo Vance's imagination.

Except.

Rosalind turned a page, and another, and then stopped so abruptly that the candle guttered.

It was a palace. Drawn in his quick sure hand, labeled in his cramped script: *the king's seat — guesswork.* Seven towers, each crowned with a spire like a needle. A great hall whose roof was shaped like a ribcage, arching over a courtyard. And below it, half-suggested in light pencil, a staircase descending into darkness — into cellars, into the earth beneath the building, drawn as though the palace had roots.

"Rosalind?" Theo's voice seemed to come from far away. "You've gone white. Is it bad? It's bad. I knew the perspective on the west tower was—"

"Where did you see this?"

"Nowhere. That's what I told you. I invented it." He frowned. "Well. Mostly. There was a fragment in an old atlas, maybe six lines describing a palace of seven towers, and I filled in the rest. Why? What's wrong?"

Rosalind reached into the satchel she carried everywhere — the satchel that held her tools, her wax, her strips of oiled cloth — and drew out a piece of bone wrapped in linen. She unwrapped it with the care of a woman handling a sleeping animal.

It was a rib bone, long and yellowed, older than anyone in the tavern. It had come to her three winters ago, left anonymously at her door with no note, which had never happened before and hadn't happened since. Carved into its surface in a hand finer than her own was a picture: a palace of seven towers, a ribcage roof, and beneath it, descending stairs. And around the border, in letters so small she needed firelight and patience to read them, a phrase repeated over and over like a prayer or a warning:

*THE CROWN IS HOLLOW. THE CROWN IS HOLLOW. THE CROWN IS HOLLOW.*

Theo stared at it for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed — quieter, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing his weight.

"That's my palace."

"It's your palace."

"But I've never—"

"I know." She set the bone on the table between them, and the candlelight moved across the carvings, and for a moment — she would swear this later, swear it to herself in the dark — the shadows in the etched stairwell seemed deeper than shadows should be. "I know exactly what you're going to say, because I've said it to myself for three years. Coincidence. Common architectural styles. Two people imagining the same impossible building." She looked up at him. "But I don't believe in coincidence anymore, Mr. Vance. I carve last words for a living. Do you know what the dead almost never say? *What a coincidence.*"

He didn't laugh. She watched him decide not to laugh, watched him take her seriously instead, and something in her chest that had been clenched for years loosened by a single degree.

"May I?" He reached for the bone, then paused, hand hovering. "Is it — is it safe to touch?"

"It's just bone."

"It doesn't feel like just bone." But he touched it anyway, fingertips tracing the seven towers, and Rosalind watched his face and saw something she recognized from her own mirror: the particular fear of a person discovering that the world is larger and stranger than advertised.

"There's more," he said slowly. "There's a book. In the archive — I catalogued it last month, it's been sitting uncatalogued for decades, nobody wanted to touch it because the binding is..." He shook his head. "It's dated 1897. It's a book of prophecies, or it claims to be, and I remember thinking it was nonsense, charming nonsense, the kind of thing you catalogue and forget. But I keep thinking — I could have sworn—" He stopped himself, laughing at himself, embarrassed. "Listen to me. You tell me a story about a haunted rib and suddenly I'm chasing omens."

"What's the book called?"

"It doesn't have a title. Just a date on the flyleaf and a name — the compiler's name, some archivist nobody's ever heard of." He drained his cider like a man fortifying himself. "I could show you. Tomorrow. Or—" he glanced at the window, where the rain had thickened against the glass, "—or tonight, if you're not sick of me yet. The archive's two streets away. I have a key. It's probably the least romantic second location in the history of courtship."

Rosalind looked at the bone. At his sketchbook. At this stranger with ink-stained fingers who had drawn, without knowing it, the same nightmare she'd been carrying for three years.

Every instinct she had honed in Hollowmere said: stay by the fire. Stay where the dead are quiet and the living are predictable and nothing ever crosses the boundary stones, in either direction.

But underneath that, quieter, older, something in her said: *go.* The same voice that had made her unwrap the anonymous bone a hundred times. The same voice that had kept her listening when everyone else stopped.

"All right," she said. "Tonight. But I'm holding you to the cider first. If we're going to chase omens, I refuse to do it sober."

* * *

They talked for another hour, and it was the strangest hour of Rosalind Okonkwo's adult life, because it felt easy. He asked about her work and didn't flinch. He told her about the atlas fragments, the coastlines of countries no Hollowmere child had ever heard named aloud, and she found herself leaning forward, forgetting to guard her face. Once he reached across the table to turn the rib bone toward the light and his hand brushed hers, and neither of them moved away quickly, and both of them pretended not to notice, which is its own kind of honesty.

Once — only once — she caught him looking at her with an expression she couldn't name. Not desire exactly, or not only. Recognition, maybe. As if he'd sketched her somewhere too, in some other book, in some other life, and was only now comparing the drawing to the original.

"What?" she asked.

"Nothing." He smiled and shook his head. "I was going to say you have a laugh like a door opening. But that sounds like a line, and I promised myself I wouldn't use lines tonight."

"Did you practice not using lines?"

"Extensively. I wrote several down and then burned them."

"Good ones?"

"The worst ones. One involved your eyes and a compass rose. I want you to know I suffered."

And Rosalind laughed again — the rusty hinge, swinging easier now — and thought, with a kind of terror and a kind of hope braided so tightly together she couldn't tell them apart: *oh no. Oh no, I like him.*

That was the danger of it. Not the bone, not the prophecy, not the palace with its hollow crown. The danger was this: that after years of talking only to the dead, she had met one living person who listened back, and already she could feel the shape of the hole he would leave if he went. Being known was a door, and doors swing both ways.

She was still thinking that — still turning her cup, still smiling despite herself — when Theo glanced toward the window and went very still.

"Rosalind," he said. "Do you hear that?"

She listened. Rain on glass. The murmur of the tavern. And beneath it, faint as breath on the far side of a wall, the dead of Hollowmere — the patient, murmuring chorus she had heard all her life, soft as sifted flour —

— and they were not murmuring.

They were saying his name.

"Theo Vance," whispered the bones in her satchel, the keepsakes on the mantels of houses she'd never entered, the old buried ribs under the chapel floor, every scrap of carved bone in the valley, rising together like a tide. "Theo Vance. Theo Vance."

Outside, beyond the lamplight, past the edge of town where the boundary stones stood in their ancient circle, something in the night had begun to pull.
