## 3. The Book That Waited a Century

The archive beneath the Hollowmere town hall smelled of dust and cold iron, and Theo Vance loved it the way some men loved churches.

He had come in an hour early for their date—deliberately, shamelessly early—because Rosalind had mentioned, over the phone, in that dry careful voice of hers, that the valley kept records going back four centuries, and because he was a cartographer and therefore constitutionally incapable of walking past an archive without going in. Maps were his trade. Old maps were his weakness. And Hollowmere, perched at the bottom of its green bowl of hills, ringed by those strange grey boundary stones that every mapmaker in the country had argued about for two hundred years—the valley was practically made of things worth mapping wrong.

That was the thing about Hollowmere that had hooked him from the moment his bus crested the ridge: it didn't appear correctly on any map. He'd checked. National survey, ordnance sheets, atlases from three countries—in every one, the valley was a smudge, a misprint, a place where roads politely declined to connect. Cartographers called it the Hollowmere Error. There was a paper about it. Theo had read the paper twice on the ride down, delighted by the phrase *persistent geodetic anomaly*, and had privately resolved to be the man who finally mapped the place properly.

Now, standing in the archive with a lantern-style lamp humming above a reading table, he understood why no one had managed it. The valley did not want to be known. It sat inside itself like a held breath.

"Closing's at eight," said the archivist, a stooped woman named Mrs. Aldersey who had regarded him with the flat suspicion people in small places reserved for outsiders, then softened by exactly one degree when he'd shown her his compass and called her catalogue card system "elegant." She pointed at a door behind a curtain. "Old ledgers through there. Don't smoke, don't eat, don't take anything past the threshold."

"The threshold of the archive?"

"Of the building," she said, and did not elaborate, which was somehow worse.

She shuffled off toward her desk upstairs, and Theo was alone with four hundred years of paper.

He worked methodically, because that was the only way he knew how to do anything. Survey records first, then parish rolls, then the fat leather-bound registers with brass clasps gone green with age. He filled pages of his sketchbook with transcriptions—field boundaries, stone placements, the odd habit Hollowmere's surveyors had of drawing the boundary stones twice, once as they appeared and once, in a smaller hand, as if seen from the other side. He found a map from 1821 where someone had inked a note across the valley's southern rim: *Here the needle turns. Do not trust the hill.* He copied that too, grinning.

It was nearly seven when he pulled out the book that ruined everything.

It was shelved wrong—that was what caught his eye first. Every other ledger stood spine-out, tidy as soldiers. This one lay flat, wrapped in a linen cloth gone the colour of old tea, tucked at the back of the lowest shelf where you'd have to kneel to find it. Someone had hidden it. Or someone had put it somewhere only the right person would bother to kneel.

He drew it out. The cloth came away in a puff of dust that smelled faintly of something floral gone to rot—dried flowers, maybe, pressed into the folds a long time ago.

The cover was black leather tooled with a pattern he couldn't quite resolve into a shape. Every time he looked directly at it, the tooling seemed to rearrange, the way a word stops looking like a word if you stare at it too long. He tilted the lamp. In the low amber light, the pattern resolved for half a second into something like a crown, drawn hollow—a circle of points with nothing inside them—and then slid apart again.

The title page read, in faded copperplate:

*Prophecies Concerning the Valley and the World Beyond. Compiled from the accounts of the dead, set down by the hand of M. Okonkwo. Anno 1897.*

Okonkwo. Theo's breath caught pleasantly. Rosalind's surname. A grandmother? A great-great-something? He filed it away as a gift for conversation—*hey, your family name is in your own archive*—and turned the page.

The paper was good stock, heavy, ragged at the edges, and completely blank.

Page after page of it. Blank, blank, blank, with occasional headers in that same copperplate—*On the Boundary Stones*, *On the Keeping of Names*, *On the Crown That Is Not Worn But Wears*—above emptiness. Theo frowned. Had the ink faded? But the headers hadn't. He rubbed a page between finger and thumb; the paper had the faintest texture, like something had been written there once and scraped away, or like writing waiting just under the surface, shy of him.

He was reaching for the lamp to angle it harder when the page under his hand darkened.

Not gradually. All at once, like water flooding through blotting paper, ink bloomed across the spread in looping, confident script—as though the words had been there all along and had simply decided, now, kneeling cartographer, lamplight, seventh day of the month, to become visible.

Theo went very still. His professional instinct said *trick of light, wet paper, fatigue*. His other instinct, older and quieter, the one that had told him to kneel at the back of the shelf, said *read it.*

He read it.

*On the seventh day of this month, in the year the compiler cannot number but the dead call Now, a man comes down from the ridge road on the afternoon coach. He carries instruments for measuring the world and finds it does not wish to be measured. He is a maker of maps. His name is Vance.*

Theo's mouth went dry. He looked at the header of the room, at the curtain, at the door. The archive was silent except for the hum of the lamp. He looked back down.

*His name is Vance,* the page repeated, patiently, as if he might have missed it. *Theodoric Elias Vance, called Theo, who draws coastlines for money and borders for love. On this night he sits below the town hall with the book that waited a century for his hands.*

"This isn't funny," Theo said aloud, and his voice sounded thin and stupid in the stone room. A prank. Some local with a sense of humour and a UV pen. But the ink was not modern; even he could see that, the brown-black bloom of oak gall, the letterforms of a trained nineteenth-century hand. And it was still writing. As he watched, new lines surfaced mid-page, the pen-strokes appearing stroke by stroke though no pen touched them, and he realized with a lurch that the words were describing the lamp, the table, the exact posture of his shoulders.

*Below the town hall, on this same night, the carver's daughter takes her supper with him. Her name is Okonkwo also. They will speak of maps. She will begin, for the first time in six years, to hope.*

Rosalind. The ink knew Rosalind. Knew about the date—tonight, the little restaurant on the square, seven-thirty, he was meant to be there in twenty minutes. A cold thread walked down his spine, and yet he could not stop reading, could not stop turning pages that now obligingly filled themselves, hunting for the part that explained how a book from 1897 knew the reservation he'd made on Tuesday.

He found the part. It was near the end of the book, on a page headed *On the Crown That Is Not Worn But Wears*, and the handwriting changed here—tighter, faster, the loops sharpened as if written against time.

*A warning, set down for whoever comes after. The dead have shown me a king beyond the stones whose body is not his own. Something old wears him the way a glove is worn, and calls itself the crown, and the kingdom believes the crown rules. The dead call it the Hollow King. They are afraid to name it further, and I have learned not to press the dead when they are afraid.*

Then, lower on the page, in a hand that shook:

*And the dead have shown me the man from the ridge road, the mapmaker, standing in the Hollow King's hall. The book has waited one hundred years to say this, so I will say it plainly, and beg forgiveness of whoever reads it:*

*Tonight, the seventh day of this month, the boundary will take Theo Vance.*

*There is no version of this night in which it does not.*

Theo stood up so fast the chair screeched. His heart was slamming. He looked around the archive as if someone might step out from between the shelves—Mrs. Aldersey, a colleague, anyone—and explain. Because this was insane. Books didn't write themselves. Prophecies weren't real; he drew isobars for a living. He had a table booked at Marlow's at seven-thirty and he was wearing his good jacket and he'd rehearsed three opening jokes in front of the guesthouse mirror, and no century-old ledger in a basement got to—

*You are reading this within one hour of your crossing,* said the next line, surfacing gently, almost kindly. *I am sorry. I watched you kneel at the shelf in the seeing-pool and I hoped you would not come, knowing it would make no difference. Run if you like. It has never helped.*

"I'm leaving," Theo announced to the empty room, to the book, to the dead woman whose handwriting it was, "and I'm going to go sit across from a very guarded woman who carves bone for a living, and none of this"—he jabbed a finger at the page—"is going to happen."

The book turned its own page.

He would think about that later—about the sound of it, the soft papery sigh of a hundred-year-old leaf lifting without a hand—and about how he did not run. Even then, even with dread sitting on his chest like a stone, some stubborn scholarly part of him wanted to know how the sentence ended. That was the flaw in him, probably. Maps were all about wanting to know what was past the edge of the sheet.

The final page bore only a few lines, and these were written slowly, deliberately, each letter formed with terrible care:

*If you are he: remember the stones stand in a circle, but they are not the circle. The door is where the river goes quiet.*

*And know this, so that you do not go thinking you were taken by accident. You were asked for. By name. The crown has been reading our letters to the dead for a hundred years, waiting for a mapmaker, because a mapmaker can find the thing it lost.*

*It lost its name, Theo Vance. It will want yours.*

The lamp flickered.

The hum of it bent downward, a note sliding out of tune, and the shadows in the archive leaned all at once to one side, the way grass leans before wind, though no wind moved in the sealed stone room. Cold crawled up through the floor, through the soles of his shoes, into his shins—not the cold of a cellar but a directional cold, a pulling cold, coming from everywhere and arriving nowhere, and Theo understood with animal clarity that the book had not been a warning.

It had been a countdown.

"No," he said. "No, no—" He grabbed the book, meaning to shove it back onto the shelf, meaning to throw it, he wasn't sure, and the moment his fingers closed on the black leather the pull became a current. His jacket snapped against his body like fabric in a gale that existed only for him. Papers lifted off tables throughout the archive, a slow snowfall of four centuries whirling around him, and somewhere upstairs Mrs. Aldersey's chair scraped and she shouted something down the stairwell that he couldn't hear over the sound—which was worse than any noise, which was a silence with a shape, the sound of the river outside going quiet.

*The door is where the river goes quiet.*

He thought, absurdly, of Rosalind. Of the half hour they'd spoken on the phone, her voice dry and careful, a woman handling conversation the way she presumably handled a chisel—one slip and the material remembers forever. He thought: *she was starting to hope.* The book had said so. Six years, the book had said, and tonight she'd been starting to hope, and he was going to miss the date.

That was the thought that broke him open—not terror, not the whirling papers, but the plain human awfulness of standing someone up.

He tried to let go of the book. He couldn't. His hands had stopped taking instructions. The cold had him by the bones now, and he felt the boundary of the valley the way you feel a threshold underfoot even blindfolded—a line drawn through the world, and himself being dragged across it backwards, his heels carving two furrows in the dust of the archive floor, the last thing he saw the lamp swinging on its cord, the last thing he heard Mrs. Aldersey's voice cracking into a scream for help that reached him from very far away, from the wrong side of a line no living thing should cross.

The black book stayed behind, falling shut on the floor with a sound like a mouth closing.

Above it, the air healed over smoothly, and the dust settled, and the papers came down soft as snow.

On the reading table, forgotten, Theo Vance's sketchbook lay open to the page he'd been working on before he knelt at the shelf: a quick pencil study of the view from the ridge road, the green bowl of the valley, the grey stones in their patient circle. Under it, in his neat cartographer's hand, he had written the time and date, as he always did, as a matter of professional habit.

7:12 p.m., the seventh of the month.

In twenty minutes, Rosalind Okonkwo would arrive at Marlow's, order a glass of wine to steady her hands, and wait.

And far beyond the stones, past the line where the river forgot how to make sound, in a hall lit by candles the colour of old teeth, something that wore a king and called itself a crown felt a familiar tug at the edge of its awareness, and smiled with a mouth it had not been born with, and said, to no one, in a voice like an empty room:

"At last. The mapmaker."
