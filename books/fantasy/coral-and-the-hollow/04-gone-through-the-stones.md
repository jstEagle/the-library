## 4. Gone Through the Stones

The tavern was called the Drowned Bell, though no one living remembered why, and Rosalind had long ago stopped asking the dead about it. They only said *the water came up and the water went down* in voices like silt settling, which explained nothing and made her sad every time.

She was laughing. That was the strange thing she would remember afterward — that she had been laughing, actually laughing, at something Theo Vance had said about the cartographer who'd mapped his own hometown so badly he'd put the church in the river, when Theo reached for his ale and knocked it with his elbow and caught it without looking, without even breaking his sentence, and something about the smallness of it, the ordinary grace of a man saving a drink mid-story, had undone her completely.

"—so he redrew the whole map," Theo was saying, "and titled it *Here Be Nothing*, and submitted it to the guild anyway. They gave him an award. A *posthumous* award, because by then he'd died of embarrassment—"

"You're lying."

"I am absolutely lying." He grinned. He had flour still on his cuff from the bakery next door, where he'd bought two honey rolls before dinner and then been too nervous to offer them until dessert, and now they sat between them on the table going stale, and somehow that was the most endearing thing she'd seen in years. "But it's a good lie. Maps are mostly good lies, if you're honest about it. Every flat map of a round world is a polite fiction. The trick is knowing which lies you've told."

Rosalind turned her cup slowly on the scarred wood. Around them the tavern did its evening work — dice clattering, someone tuning a fiddle badly, the fire popping in the great hearth. And underneath it all, faint as breath on a winter window, the dead of Hollowmere murmured their endless half-sentences. She heard them the way you hear rain: not listening, just aware.

Tonight they were quieter than usual. Tonight they seemed to be *waiting*, which was nonsense, because the dead didn't wait for anything. Waiting was a living vice.

"You went quiet," Theo said gently.

"I do that."

"I noticed. You go quiet like other people clear their throats. Like you're checking whether it's safe to say the next thing." He didn't say it as an accusation. He said it the way he'd drawn the coastline of Hollowmere earlier, on a napkin, freehand, from memory after one afternoon's surveying — carefully, and getting it right.

Her first instinct was to deflect. Her second instinct, surprising her, was to tell the truth.

"My work is with the dead," she said. "Bone-carving. Last words, mostly. People bring me a rib or a knuckle bone from someone they loved, and I carve what that person needed said before they died and couldn't. It's..." She searched for the word and hated all of them. "It means I spend my days inside other people's griefs. And my nights alone in a house full of finished sentences. So I'm out of practice at—" she gestured vaguely at the table, at him, at herself, "—unfinished ones."

Theo was quiet for a moment. Then: "My mother died when I was nineteen. I never got her last words. She was unconscious for three days, and I kept waiting for her to wake up and say something worth keeping, and she didn't, and then there wasn't any more waiting to do." He looked at his hands. "I think what you do might be the most important work in this valley. Maybe anywhere."

Something in Rosalind's chest cracked along an old seam.

"Theo—"

"Sorry. Too much. I do that on dates, apparently. Emotional cartography. I map the deep water before anyone's agreed to sail it."

"No," she said. "It's not too much. It's—" *Exactly right. Exactly the thing no one has said to me in eleven years.* "I was going to say thank you."

He smiled, and outside, far off toward the valley's edge, thunder rolled — except there were no clouds tonight, and the boundary stones didn't make weather, and Rosalind felt the sound in her teeth rather than her ears.

The dead had gone silent.

All of them. All at once. For the first time in her life, the murmur beneath the world simply *stopped*, and the silence was so total, so wrong, that her hand flew to the carving knife at her belt before she knew she'd moved.

"Rosalind?" Theo leaned forward. "What is it?"

"I don't—" 

The candles guttered. Not from wind; the windows were shut. The flames bent sideways, all of them, all through the tavern, bending toward the door that led out toward the eastern road, toward the stones, as if the light itself were being pulled somewhere.

And Theo — Theo set down his ale very carefully, the way a man does when the floor has begun to tilt, and said, "That's odd. I feel like I'm being *read*. Like a book someone's opening to check a page."

"What page?" Her voice came out flat and cold and calm, the voice she used over dying people, and she would be grateful for it later.

"I don't know. Something about a crown." He frowned, pressing his palm flat against his sternum. "There's a phrase stuck in my head. *The hollow crown.* Why do I know that phrase? I've never—"

The air between them split.

There was no better word for it. The space an arm's length away tore like wet paper, and through the tear Rosalind saw — for less than a heartbeat — a sky the color of old ash, and towers black against it, and something vast turning its attention toward them the way an eye turns —

— and Theo Vance was gone.

Not fallen. Not dragged across the floor. *Gone*, mid-blink, his chair still rocking, his napkin map fluttering down onto the boards where his boots had been. The tear sealed itself with a sound like a held note ending, and the candles stood up straight again, and the tavern roared on around them, dice and fiddle and fire, because no one else had seen. No one else ever saw anything.

Rosalind sat perfectly still for the length of three heartbeats.

Then the dead came back.

They came back *loud*.

A hundred voices, five hundred, all of them speaking at once and all of them saying some version of the same terrible sentence, tumbling over each other in their haste, the dead of Hollowmere who had spent decades murmuring their unfinished business suddenly united:

*He crossed. He crossed. The living man crossed. Through the stones, past the stones, where nothing living goes — he crossed, carver, he crossed—*

Rosalind stood up so fast her chair went over backward.

*Where?* she demanded, inside her skull, inside whatever place her hearing lived. *Which stone? Which direction?*

*East,* they wailed. *The old boundary. The tall one with the lightning-scar. But carver — carver, listen — he didn't cross like a traveler crosses a door. He crossed like a thing being* ***summoned.*** *Something on the other side knew his name.*

* * *

She ran.

She ran through streets that had known her whole life, past neighbors who called her name and got no answer, up the eastern road with her lungs burning and her braid coming loose, until the trees thinned and the land rose and there it stood: the great boundary stone, taller than two men, seamed with the lightning-scar of some storm a century dead.

The air around it shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmers above a forge — the visible edge of Hollowmere's oldest mercy. Magic could not cross into the valley. Magic could not cross *out*. Whatever gifts the founders' bloodlines carried — and Okonkwo blood carried one of the oldest — slept here, muffled, wrapped in wool, kept small. Rosalind had always thought of the boundary as a wall.

Standing before it now, she understood it was also a cage, and that something outside had just reached through the bars and taken a man.

The grass at the stone's base was flattened in a perfect circle, as though something enormous had pressed down and lifted away. In the center of the circle lay a leather sketchbook.

She knew it instantly. She'd watched him draw in it at the table — the palace, the napkin coastlines, the little marginal sketches of gulls he claimed he'd invented because he'd never actually seen one. His name was stamped on the cover in gold leaf gone soft with handling: *T. VANCE, GUILD OF CARTOGRAPHERS.*

She picked it up with hands that shook.

The last drawn page was the palace.

Not a fantasy. Not a fancy. Every line of it was confident, observed, *measured* — the kind of drawing that came from standing in front of a thing and refusing to leave until you understood it. Spires like needles. Walls that climbed a cliff above a gray sea. Windows arranged in ranks, and in the highest tower, a single window drawn slightly larger than the others, and beside it, in Theo's neat cartographer's hand, a notation she would carry to her grave:

*Throne room faces east. Light hits the throne at dawn. Whoever sits there is lit like a saint and shadowed like a liar.*

Beneath the palace, smaller, almost hidden in the margin, he'd sketched a figure standing before the gates — a woman, tools hanging at her belt, a blade of pale material in her hand. Bone-white. A carving knife.

And beneath the figure, underlined twice, her own name:

*Rosalind Okonkwo.*

She sank down onto the cold grass inside the scorched circle, the sketchbook open in her lap, and let the guilt arrive, because it was going to arrive regardless and she had learned long ago that grief deferred collected interest.

*You knew.* The thought came with teeth. *You recognized the palace from the carved bone — the one old Marta brought you, her grandmother's last words, carved sixty years ago by a carver whose hands shook — you recognized those spires and you said nothing, because saying something meant explaining how you knew, meant showing him the bone, meant letting him see the strangeness of you on the one night you wanted to be ordinary.*

*And the prophecy — if there is a prophecy, if something out there has been waiting a hundred years for a cartographer named Theo Vance to walk into your valley and sit across a table from you — then you weren't just a woman on a date. You were bait. You were the hook, and you never even felt the line.*

The dead gathered around her. She couldn't see them — she never could, here in the valley, where her gift was muffled to whispers — but she felt them the way you feel a crowd at your back: old farmers, drowned children, Marta's grandmother, generations of Hollowmere's finished sentences, all of them pressing close, all of them trying to comfort her in the only language they had left.

*Not your fault, carver.*

*Not your fault.*

*You couldn't have known.*

"But I can know now," she said aloud, and her voice cracked the night open. "That's the difference. I couldn't have known *before*. I can know everything, starting now."

She looked down at the last page again. At the woman with the carving knife, drawn in his sure, quick lines. At her name in his handwriting — the first time anyone had ever written her name like it belonged in a story.

"He drew me there," she whispered. "Before any of this happened, he drew me standing at those gates. What does that mean? What does that *mean*?"

The dead went quiet in the particular way they did when they knew something and were afraid to say it. Then, reluctantly, in fragments, in the voices of the oldest among them:

*It means the book was right.*

*What book?*

Silence. Longer this time. Then, from Marta's grandmother, in a whisper dry as moth wings:

*The archive keeps a book that waited a hundred years, carver. The archivist's grandfather sealed it. It names things that haven't happened yet. It named him.*

Rosalind closed the sketchbook and held it against her chest, over her heart, as if she could keep it beating for him by proxy.

The boundary stone shimmered beside her, patient as it had been for three hundred years. Magic could not cross it. But she had heard the dead say something else tonight, hadn't she? Back in the tavern, in the flood of their terror — buried in the wailing, a single thread of stranger knowledge, older than the stones themselves:

*The dead may cross. The dead have always crossed. Only the living are kept.*

Only the living are kept.

Rosalind rose to her feet. She wiped her face once, efficiently, the way she did before sitting with a dying client, and slung the sketchbook across her back beside her roll of carving tools.

"Then the dead will carry my letters," she said to the stone, to the ash-colored dark beyond it, to whatever king or crown or hunger had reached into her valley and stolen the first man in eleven years to make her laugh. "And I will follow every one."

Behind her, the dead of Hollowmere began, softly, to sing.
