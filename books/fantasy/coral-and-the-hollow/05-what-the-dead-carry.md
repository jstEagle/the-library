## 5. What the Dead Carry

The tavern had not emptied so much as evacuated. Chairs stood where Theo's collapse had knocked them, and a ring of pale faces lingered at the edges of the lamplight, unwilling to come closer, unwilling to leave. Someone had righted the table but not bothered with the spilled wine, which had gone sticky and dark across the wood like something shed rather than spilled.

Rosalind sat where he had sat.

His sketchbook lay in her lap. She had not opened it again since the tavern keeper pressed it into her hands — *it was his, miss, he'd have wanted you to have it* — because she already knew what was on the last page, and knowing it once felt like the limit of what she could survive knowing twice. The palace with its impossible towers. Her own name beneath it, in his quick sure hand: *Rosalind.*

She turned pages anyway. That was the thing about grief, she thought; it made you do exactly what you could not afford to do. Here were maps of Hollowmere drawn better than any official survey — he'd sketched the mill race, the crooked lane behind the chandler's, the boundary stones themselves, rendered with an accuracy that should have been impossible for a man who'd lived here three weeks. Here was her profile, done from memory, done *quickly*, the way you drew a thing you were afraid of forgetting. Here, halfway through, the drawings changed character. Palaces. Harbors. A bridge over a gorge that no one had built, in a country no one had named, drawn with the same certainty as the lane behind the chandler's.

Places he'd never been. Places that had come to him the way the dead came to her.

"You saw it too," she said to the empty chair opposite. "Whatever it is. You saw it before I did."

The tavern's dead murmured their agreement from the corners, the way they always did when she spoke aloud to no one living — old Marta who'd died of fever forty years back, still fussing about the damp; the drowned boy from the mill race who never said anything at all, just hummed. Hollowmere's dead were quiet things, faded things. The valley wore them smooth, the same way it wore everything else. Magic couldn't cross the boundary stones, and neither, mostly, could anything sharp enough to matter.

But Theo had crossed. Something had reached into the middle of a sentence — *I think we should talk about the book, Rosalind, I think it's* — and taken him, mid-word, through stone that had not let so much as a curse slip out in two hundred years.

She closed the sketchbook and pressed both palms flat against its cover, as if she could hold him inside it by pressure alone.

"Tell me how," she said to the dead.

And the dead, for once, went silent — which was itself an answer. They knew something. They were afraid of it.

* * *

Her workshop smelled of tallow and bone dust and the particular mineral sweetness of fresh-cut rib. Rosalind lit every lamp she owned, then the candles besides, until the little room glowed like the inside of a lantern, because tonight she did not want shadows. Shadows were where whispers lived, and she needed to think clearly, the way she hadn't thought clearly since the boundary took him.

She laid her tools out on the cloth in their proper order. Gravers, fine as needles. The hooked knife for splitting. The burnisher. Her hands knew this ritual the way they knew prayer, and the ritual steadied her, and she hated how much she needed steadying.

For eleven years she had carved messages from the dead into bone. It was a small magic, a valley magic, the only kind Hollowmere allowed — and even it was diminished here, muffled, like hearing a conversation through a wall. In the valley, the dead spoke to her faintly, briefly, in fragments that faded within days of dying unless she caught them and fixed them in bone. A last word for a widow. A debt confessed. A name for a grave that had gone unmarked. She had carved hundreds of such messages. She had never once carved one *from herself*. The dead carried words for the living; that was the whole of the arrangement, the entire shape of her gift. Nobody had ever asked her what happened if the carver herself had something to say.

Nobody had ever been taken through the stones before, either.

She picked up the rib — a deer's rib, long and pale and gently curved, cured for months and ready — and set it on the leather pad. Then she sat with her hands in her lap and did not carve, because first she had to understand the thing the dead would not tell her straight.

Think it through, Rosalind. You are good at thinking through.

Magic could not cross the boundary. That was bedrock. The stones had held for two centuries; the valley was a hole in the world's fabric, a place where power simply *ended*, and everything she'd ever been told agreed that nothing of the uncanny passed either way. And yet something had pulled a living man through the stones tonight as easily as a hand pulls a thread.

Unless the stones weren't broken. Unless the stones had never been what everyone believed.

She thought of old Mother Cass, the bone-carver before her, dead now twenty years, whose teachings Rosalind had inherited secondhand and half-scoffed-at. *Bone remembers, girl. Bone is the one thing that goes everywhere.* She had thought it a carver's superstition, the kind of thing you said over apprentices to make the work feel holy.

But the dead crossed the boundary constantly. Every soul that died in Hollowmere passed out of the valley into whatever waited beyond — that was theology even the godless accepted, because the graves emptied of whispers within the year. The dead left. The dead *always* left. And they left carrying whatever she had carved into their keeping-bones, their messages delivered — or so the recipients claimed, in letters that arrived from distant cities in her clients' families' hands — to the living outside.

Rosalind went very still.

Eleven years of letters. Eleven years of replies from beyond the valley — *your mother's message reached us; thank you; the carving was received* — and she had never once wondered how the bones traveled. She had assumed couriers, family pilgrimages, the ordinary post. But the keepers who collected the carved bones from her door were always strangers. Always recently bereaved. Always *gone by morning*.

They were not couriers. They were the newly dead, walking out of their own wake, doing one last kindness before the valley's fade took them. Carrying bone. Carrying *message*.

Bone remembered, and bone went everywhere, and the dead could carry it anywhere — because the dead were not magic. The dead were just people, finished. The boundary stopped power, but it did not stop *persons*. It had never stopped persons.

It had stopped persons going *in*.

That was the shape of it. That was why nothing came into Hollowmere — no sorcery, no prophecy, no whisper from the wide world's vast and noisy supernatural ocean. The valley was sealed against ingress. Egress was another matter. Things could leave. Things had always been able to leave.

Something outside had found a gap in the seal and pulled Theo through it.

Which meant — and here her hands began to shake, so she gripped the edge of the bench — which meant the road back might exist. Narrow. Wrong-way. Probably fatal. But a man had been pulled out through a door meant to stay shut, and doors that can be forced open from one side can sometimes be forced open from the other.

Not with people. She understood that much already, felt it in the flat negative silence of the dead whenever she circled near the question. Whatever had taken Theo had used the one gap; there would be no second pulling, no counter-abduction. She could not send anyone after him. She could not go herself, not yet, not blind.

But a message. A message was small. A message was bone, and bone remembered, and bone went everywhere.

"Right," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange and strong in the candlelight. "Then we write a letter."

* * *

Choosing the carrier broke her heart, and she had known it would, and she did it anyway.

There were three new deaths in Hollowmere that week. Old Tam Wicker, who'd died Tuesday in his sleep at eighty-one, full of years and soup — but Tam had been deaf for a decade and stubborn for six, and he'd never once carried a message without complaining. The miller's youngest, a child taken by winter fever, whom Rosalind would sooner carve her own hands off than burden. And then there was Joss.

Joss Perrin, twenty-six, thrown from his horse on the north road four days ago. Rosalind had carved his last words for his mother just yesterday: *tell her the horse wasn't hers to blame.* He'd been a courier by trade. The fast-post rider between Hollowmere and the lowland towns, the young man who'd carried her own outgoing parcels a hundred times, who'd tipped his cap at her and called her *miss* and once, memorably, raced a thunderstorm up the valley road and won.

He stood now in the corner of her workshop, translucent as breath on glass, watching her with the patient puzzlement of the recently dead.

"You know what I'm asking," she said.

He looked down at himself. Looked at the rib on the bench. Back up. When he spoke, his voice had the odd doubled quality the new dead had — his own voice, and under it, faintly, the sound of wind moving through an empty house.

"It's past the stones, miss."

"I know."

"I don't go past the stones." Not refusing. Frightened. "Nobody does. You finish here and then you — go on. Wherever it is we go on to. Nobody comes back to say."

"I know," Rosalind said again, softer. "And I'm asking anyway. One delivery, Joss. Your last one. There's a man on the other side of the boundary who is alive and alone and doesn't know anyone is coming for him. You're the fastest rider this valley ever produced." She tried for his grin, his living grin, the one from the storm-race. "Storm's on your left, Joss. Race it."

The dead boy — the dead *man*, he'd been a man, twenty-six years of a man — was quiet for a long time. Around them the older ghosts of Hollowmere drifted close to listen, Marta and the others, drawn by the novelty of being addressed directly, and their attention felt like candle-flame warmth.

"What do I carry?" Joss asked at last.

"A letter."

"To someone dead?"

"To someone alive," Rosalind said. "In a country where the dead are apparently put to work. Which means you'll be able to find him, I think. The dead notice things there. The dead will know where a stranger who fell out of the sky has been taken." She paused. "You may find the dead there aren't free, Joss. If they're not — be careful. Come home if you can. Don't come home if coming home costs more than the letter's worth."

"And what's the letter worth, miss?"

Everything, she almost said. Instead she picked up the graver.

* * *

Carving her own words was nothing like carving the dead's.

When she worked a dead person's message, the words came *through* her — she was a doorway, a tool, a pair of steady hands for voices that could no longer hold pens. She barely chose anything. The choosing was the hard part now. Every phrase she considered was wrong. *Stay safe* was absurd. *Help is coming* was a promise she had no right to make. *I'll find you* presumed a road she hadn't proven existed. *Who are you really?* was true but useless and cruel besides, because she knew who he was; she'd known by the second hour of the date, known in the way you know rain is coming, known and been terrified and hopeful anyway, and he'd been taken before she could say any of it.

So she carved the truth in its plainest form, the way she'd want it received if she were the one alone in a hostile country:

*I know you were taken. It was not your fault and not mine, whatever the book says. I am coming. I don't yet know the road, but I know it exists, because you were pulled through a door and doors open in both directions or they are not doors. Hold on. Learn the ground. You're a mapmaker — map it. Trust the dead if they speak kindly and trust no crown. — R.*

Thirty-one words. It took her two hours. She carved each letter shallow and exact, the way Mother Cass had taught her — *shallow cuts read longest, girl; deep cuts crack* — and when she finished, she ran her thumb over the grooves and felt the words the way the dead must feel them: as texture, as truth fixed in material, as something that could not be unsaid.

Then she wrapped the rib in wool, bound it with cord, and held it out.

"For the man Vance," she said. "Theo Vance. Cartographer. Taken tonight through the north stones. Find him. Give it into his living hands — his *living* hands, Joss, no one else's. Then you're done. Free and clear."

Joss took the bundle. His fingers closed around it and, wonder of wonders, the wool did not fall through him; the dead of Hollowmere could touch what they were given to carry, always had been able to, and she had never once thought about what that implied until tonight.

He looked at her with his young face and his old eyes.

"He matters to you," Joss observed.

"He matters to me."

"More than the valley?"

The question landed like a dropped stone. Outside, Hollowmere lay quiet under its stars — the safe valley, the sealed valley, the place that had kept her all her life the way a jar keeps a moth. Everything she loved was here: her workshop, her tools, her dead, her routines worn soft as old cloth. Beyond the stones lay a kingdom she knew only from a murdered king's memories and a cartographer's impossible sketches, full of crowns and parasites and things that harvested people like wheat.

"Ask me again when I've brought him home," she said.

Joss grinned — the storm-race grin, brief and bright — and walked through her wall.

She watched him go from the window, fading as he moved, growing fainter against the dark fields, until the night swallowed him somewhere near the standing stones and she could see him no longer. The valley's other dead gathered around her in the workshop, silent, companionable. Marta fussed about the cold. The drowned boy hummed.

Rosalind sat down among them, exhausted, hollowed out, and strangely, terribly awake.

Grief, she understood now, was just love with nowhere to go. So she had given hers somewhere to go — thirty-one words, riding out of the valley on the shoulders of a dead courier, aimed at a man she'd known for one evening and would cross the end of the world for.

Now all she had to do was find the road herself.

She was reaching for her coat to walk the boundary line by lantern-light when every ghost in the room went still at once — and old Marta, faded Marta, forty-years-dead Marta, raised her head as if listening to something far away, and said in a voice like ash falling:

*"Girl. The bones are singing from the north. Something dead is coming back through the stones — and it's calling your name."*
