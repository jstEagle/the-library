## 6. The Kingdom of Ash and Salt

Theo woke to the taste of salt.

Not sea salt — something older, mineral, the taste of a mouthful of ground bone. It coated his teeth and sat heavy on his tongue, and for a long, disoriented moment he could not remember why he would be lying on his back staring at a sky the color of tarnished pewter.

The sky was wrong.

He knew skies. He had spent eleven years drawing them — the flat gray of London in November, the bruised violet before desert rain, the pale washed blue over the Alps that he'd once spent three days trying to mix from ink and water and patience. This was none of those. This was a sky that had been *used*, scoured of color the way a well-worn coin is scoured of its face, and beneath it the light fell flat and sourceless, casting shadows that pointed in no particular direction.

He sat up. His sketchbook was still clutched against his chest, both arms wrapped around it like a man hugging a life preserver, and his fingers ached from gripping it. The last thing he remembered — Rosalind's face across the tavern table, her mouth opening around the first syllable of whatever she'd been about to say about the palace drawing — came back to him in a rush, and with it the memory of the *pull*, that terrible gentle tug behind his sternum, as if the world had reached into him and simply lifted.

"Rosalind," he said aloud. His voice cracked the silence and nothing answered it.

He got to his feet on a hillside of ash-gray grass that crumbled like old paper when he touched it. Below him, in a shallow valley threaded by a river the color of weak tea, lay a town.

It was not a large town — perhaps two hundred rooftops, most of them slate or thatch, arranged along the river's bend with the organic sprawl of any settlement that had grown rather than been planned. But even from this distance, even with the flat light flattening every detail, Theo could see what was wrong with it. There were no chimneys smoking. No laundry on lines. No movement at all except for a column of figures moving through the central square with the slow, synchronized gait of people walking in their sleep.

His cartographer's mind did what it always did when he was frightened: it started working. Elevation maybe two hundred feet above the valley floor. River flowing north-northwest, which meant — if this world had the decency to share a compass with his own — that larger water lay that way. Defensible terrain to the south. A road, faded almost to nothing, running east toward hills that looked like they had been sketched by someone who had run out of confidence halfway through.

And everywhere, everywhere, the smell of ash and salt.

He was still cataloguing — because cataloguing was how he kept panic at arm's length, because a map was a way of saying *I understand you* to a place that wanted to kill him — when he saw the soldiers.

There were six of them, riding up the road toward the town, and their armor told him more in ten seconds than an hour of observation might have. It was beautiful work, filigreed steel chased with patterns that hurt to look at directly, the kind of armor worn by men who wanted to be seen from very far away. But it was *dirty* — not battle-dirty but neglect-dirty, the grime of things maintained by rote rather than care. And the men inside it rode like puppets whose puppeteer had grown bored: spines too straight, hands too loose on the reins, faces empty as unsent letters.

They were herding people. That took Theo another moment to parse, because the townspeople weren't fleeing. They were lining up. In the square below, one by one, the villagers stepped forward to where a soldier dismounted and held out a small object — a lantern? a bowl? — and each villager leaned over it and *exhaled*, and then walked away slower than they'd come, shoulders lower, eyes dimmer, until by the time the line reached its tenth person the ones who had already passed were standing perfectly still among the living like furniture that had learned to breathe.

Harvesting. The word arrived in Theo's mind uninvited and complete, the way words sometimes did when he read old maps — *here be dragons*, and you understood the dragons without being told.

He should have run. He understood that later, looking back, with the terrible clarity hindsight grants. He should have melted into the grass and gone to ground and spent a week learning this place before letting it see his face. Instead he stood on the ridgeline like an idiot, sketchbook open, pencil already moving, because some deep-buried part of him believed that anything drawn was anything survived, and so he stood there mapping the harvest of a village's souls while a seventh soldier — the one he hadn't seen, riding wide through the grass — came up behind him and said:

"You're a long way from anywhere, mapmaker."

* * *

The soldier's name was Brann, though he offered it the way a man offers a coat he no longer expects to get back. He was younger than his voice, twenty-five at most, with the same too-straight spine as the others but something flickering behind his eyes that the villagers' eyes had lost — fear, Theo thought. Living, unharvested fear.

"You know what I am?" Theo said carefully. They were walking down toward the town now, Brann's hand resting on Theo's shoulder with a grip that was courteous the way a manacle is courteous. "A mapmaker?"

"Everyone knows what the mapmaker is." Brann said it without inflection, reciting. "The prophecy says the mapmaker comes through the stones on the night of the crossing, bearing the book that waits."

"The book that—" Theo's stomach dropped. "What book?"

Brann glanced at him then, and for half a second the fear behind his eyes sharpened into something almost like pity. "You don't have it."

"I don't— I don't know what book. I don't know where I am. I was having dinner." The absurdity of it made his voice climb. "I was having *dinner*. There was a woman, and a tavern, and I drew her a palace, and then—"

He stopped, because Brann had stopped, and because the soldier was looking at him with an expression that had shifted from pity to something far more dangerous: interest.

"You drew a palace," Brann repeated slowly.

"A palace. Yes. From imagination. Or — I thought it was imagination."

"Describe it."

"I don't see how that's—"

"*Describe it.*"

So Theo described it, because the hand on his shoulder had tightened and because there are moments when a man understands that obedience is the cheaper currency. "Black stone. Nine towers, but the ninth is shorter than the others, cut off, like it broke and they never rebuilt it. A courtyard with a tree in it that has no leaves but isn't dead. Gates of coral — white coral, or bone, I couldn't tell. Stairs inside that go down farther than the building is tall."

Brann had gone very still. Around them the ash-grass whispered against itself with no wind to explain it.

"That's the Hollow Court," Brann said quietly. "That's the king's house. No one outside the court has seen it in sixty years. No one *draws* it." He looked at Theo the way men look at weather that has turned strange. "The prophecy says the mapmaker will draw what he has never seen. Come on. You're expected."

"Expected," Theo said. The word tasted of salt. "By whom?"

Brann didn't answer. Ahead of them the town waited in its flat light, and the harvested villagers stood motionless in the square, and Theo walked toward all of it knowing, with the certainty of a man reading contour lines before a flood, that he had crossed a boundary that did not open twice.

* * *

They held him in a house near the square — a good house, by the look of it, though its owners were nowhere visible, and there was a fine layer of undisturbed dust on the stairs that suggested they had not left recently so much as *ceased*. Brann posted himself outside the door and told him, through it, that a rider had gone ahead to the Court, that the king would send for him, and that he should rest.

Rest. Theo sat on a bed that smelled of someone else's life and tried to think.

The problem — the thing his mind kept snagging on, the way a pen snags on grit in the paper — was *expected*. Being lost was frightening but comprehensible; lost was a condition with procedures, lost was something a man worked his way out of. But this place had known he was coming. A book a hundred years old had written down his date with Rosalind Okonkwo as casually as a stationmaster writes a departure. And now soldiers who had never seen him greeted him by trade and said *you're expected* with the flat certainty of people reading off a schedule.

He opened his sketchbook with hands that were not quite steady and flipped to the last page. The palace again — black stone, nine towers, the broken ninth. And beneath it, in his own handwriting that he had no memory of writing, small and precise:

*Rosalind Okonkwo.*

He stared at it for a long time. Then, because his hands needed work and his mind needed a task, he began to draw the town.

This was his ritual, his oldest one. When the world went wrong, he mapped it, because a map forced honesty: here is what I can see, here is what I can infer, here is what I do not know and must mark as such. He drew the river's course and the road's decay and the square with its silent standing villagers, and as he drew, details surfaced that his first glance had missed. The houses were not abandoned. They were *occupied* — he could see the faint signs, a curtain twitching, a shadow behind glass — but no one came out unless the soldiers called them. Doors stayed shut. Shutters closed at the sound of hooves. A whole town performing the animal logic of prey.

And the harvested ones — the still ones — were not indoors. They stood in doorways and gardens and along the lane, wherever the harvest had happened to leave them, and they did not eat, did not sleep, did not seek shelter when the flat light dimmed toward evening. They just stood, and their faces wore expressions Theo recognized from his grandmother's sickroom, from the last day of her life: the expression of people who had once contained something vast and specific and were now only the shape of it.

Toward dusk, one of them moved.

She was an old woman, standing at the gate of the house next door, and she turned her head — slowly, with the creaking deliberateness of a weathervane — until her eyes found the window where Theo sat. And although she was forty feet away and the glass between them was clouded with salt-film, Theo felt certain, with a certainty that raised every hair on his arms, that she was looking directly at him.

Her lips moved. No sound came out. But Theo's gift for pattern — the cartographer's curse, seeing systems everywhere — assembled the shapes into words anyway, and the words were:

*You're the one from beyond the stones.*

Then her head swung back to its original position, and she was furniture again.

Theo set down his pencil. His hands, he noticed distantly, were shaking badly enough to ruin linework, which had never once happened in eleven years of professional draftsmanship, including the afternoon he'd been caught in a rockslide in the passes above Vell.

"They're waiting on you," Brann said through the door, and there was something in the young soldier's voice that hadn't been there before — something almost apologetic. "Rider came back. The Court sends a carriage."

"A carriage," Theo repeated.

"For the mapmaker." A pause. "I'd sit with your back straight, if I were you. The king notices posture."

Footsteps, hoofbeats, the creak of an axle. Through the window Theo watched the carriage come down the ruined road — black lacquer, drawn by four horses whose coats were the same ash-gray as the grass, driven by a figure whose face was turned away — and he understood, sitting there in a dead stranger's house with a sketchbook full of a town's quiet horror, that he had been wrong about the nature of his situation in one fundamental way.

He had thought he was a man lost in a hostile country.

He was not. He was a man summoned. Somewhere in a palace of black stone with a broken ninth tower, something ancient and patient had read a book written a century ago, had noted the date, and had sent a carriage — and everything in Theo that had ever studied old maps, that had ever traced the borders of vanished kingdoms, knew exactly what became of things that were summoned and failed to please.

He gathered his pencils. He squared his sketchbook. He straightened his back.

The door opened, and the carriage waited, and the kingdom of ash and salt received him like a guest it had always intended to have.

* * *

The road to the Court took three days, and Theo spent them drawing, because it was that or screaming.

He drew from the carriage window as the country unrolled: fields planted with crops that grew in perfect geometric rows and were never harvested. Villages that shrank as they traveled inward, their populations thinning, their still-ones multiplying, until near the end the roadside stood lined with harvested villagers at fifty-foot intervals like fence posts, facing the road, watching the carriage pass with eyes that tracked. He drew the mountains ringing the capital, and noticed — his pencil hesitating, then confirming — that they were symmetrical. Perfectly, impossibly symmetrical, mirrored around the palace's axis, the way terrain looks in dreams and in lies.

On the second night, Brann sat with him while he ate bread that tasted of nothing at all.

"Why does it want a mapmaker?" Theo asked. "Your king."

Brann was quiet for long enough that Theo thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "The kingdom doesn't match the maps anymore. Hasn't for years. Roads go places they shouldn't. Rivers run backward in the east. Soldiers get lost between here and the coast — lost, in our own country, following our own charts." He picked at the bread without eating it. "Sixty years ago there was a surveyor who could draw true maps. Wherever he drew was real, whether it matched the land or not. The land obeyed him." His voice dropped. "They say the king has been looking for another one ever since. Or looking for that one again."

"What happened to the surveyor?"

"He was called to the Court," Brann said. "No one saw him after."

Outside, the flat light was failing, and along the roadside the still-ones turned their heads in unison to follow the carriage, forty faces, fifty, a congregation of the emptied, and Theo bent low over his sketchbook and drew them all, every face, because he had decided something in the dark of the third night of travel and the decision required evidence.

If he was going to be devoured by whatever waited in the black palace, then he was going to do what he had always done with the unmapped and the murderous: he was going to chart it. Every tower, every guard rotation, every corridor glimpsed through a doorway. Because maps outlive their makers — that was the whole faith of his profession, the quiet religion of every man who had ever died with his boots in a pass and his notes in a satchel — and somewhere beyond the boundary stones, in a valley where magic could not follow, a woman named Rosalind Okonkwo was presumably staring at the chair where he had vanished mid-sentence.

*Come find me,* he thought, pressing the pencil hard enough to groove the page. *When you do, I'll leave you the way.*

* * *

The Hollow Court announced itself a day before they reached it, rising out of the mirrored mountains like a fact that had been argued into the landscape: nine towers of black stone, the ninth broken short, gates of pale coral that gleamed like bone in the sourceless light. Theo did not draw it. His hands would not obey. Some part of him understood, with an animal clarity older than cartography, that drawing a thing in this country was a kind of claim, and he did not want any part of himself bound to those walls.

Servants met the carriage — living servants, hollow-eyed but breathing — and led him through corridors where the candles burned without flickering, and at last, at the end of a hall hung with portraits whose faces had all been scraped smooth, two great doors of carved bone swung inward, and a herald with a voice like dry parchment announced:

"The mapmaker. Come through the stones, as was foretold."

The throne room stretched away under vaulted darkness. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks, applauding — slow, rhythmic, perfectly synchronized applause with no enthusiasm in it whatsoever, the sound of a machine built to mimic welcome. And at the far end, ascending the dais steps with unhurried grace, stood a tall figure in silver and black.

King Aldric was handsome in the way of portraits rather than men: composed, symmetrical, every feature correct. He smiled as he descended, and the smile was warm and practiced and reached every muscle of his face.

But his eyes, as they settled on Theo across the length of that hall, were like rooms from which the furniture had been removed — spacious, lit, and utterly vacant — and when he spoke, his voice carried easily, kindly, to every corner of the silence:

"At last. We have waited such a long time for you, mapmaker."

Theo bowed, because his knees chose that moment to make the decision for him, and as his head went down he heard the synchronized applause continue, and continue, and continue — and understood that it had never stopped since he entered, that it would not stop until commanded, and that the command, like everything else in this kingdom, belonged to whatever was wearing the crown.

Somewhere in the ranks of courtiers, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, Theo heard — or dreamed he heard — a whisper with no mouth behind it:

*She is coming.*
