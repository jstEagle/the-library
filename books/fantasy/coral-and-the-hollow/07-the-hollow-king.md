## 7. The Hollow King

They dressed him for the audience in a room with no windows.

Two servants, silent as furniture, laid out clothes that were almost right — a courtier's coat of grey wool cut in a fashion forty years out of date, a shirt with too many buttons, boots that fit as if they'd been measured from someone else's feet and adjusted by guesswork. Theo stood on a low stool while they worked around him like tide around a rock, pinning here, tugging there, and he understood, with the particular clarity of a man watching his own funeral arrangements, that they weren't dressing him.

They were presenting him.

"Am I allowed to know what happens next?" he asked.

The elder servant — a woman with a face like folded paper and eyes that never quite landed on him — paused with her hands full of his collar. For a moment something moved behind her expression, quick as a fish under ice. Then it was gone.

"The King receives those the Court names," she said. "You have been named."

"Named what?"

She finished the collar and stepped back, considering her work the way Rosalind considered a fresh-carved bone — no. Not like that. He was doing that constantly now, reaching for comparisons and finding her at the bottom of every one. Three days in this kingdom of ash and salt, and his mind kept circling back to a tavern table, a woman who touched bones like other people touched letters, the exact angle of her smile when he'd said something stupid about contour lines. He held onto those details the way a drowning man holds driftwood: not because they floated, but because letting go felt worse.

"You've been named *expected*," the servant said, and there it was again, that fish under ice. Fear, he thought. Real fear, worn smooth by repetition until it barely registered as an emotion anymore. "That is rarer than you would believe, sir. Most people who are brought to the palace were not expected by anyone."

*Most people.* He let that go unexamined, because examining it would require thinking about the villages he'd passed through on the road — the ones where doors stood open and meals sat half-eaten and nobody answered when the soldiers called out friendly greetings. Or the other kind of village, the one near the salt flats, where everyone had been present but somehow absent, smiling at nothing, their children reciting the harvest dates of years they couldn't possibly remember.

The soldiers had called those places *productive.*

They led him through corridors that made his cartographer's heart ache. That was the thing nobody had warned him about — that a palace could lie architecturally. The halls bent where they should have run straight. Staircases descended into rooms higher than the floors above them. Windows on the north face showed morning light while windows on the south showed dusk. He counted paces obsessively, mapped turns in his head, built and rebuilt the floor plan three times, and each version contradicted the last, not because his memory failed but because the palace itself refused to hold still. It wasn't badly built. It was *argued with.* Every corridor looked like a compromise between what the architect intended and what something else had insisted upon.

And threaded through all of it, faint as wire under wallpaper, ran a sound he still hadn't learned to stop hearing: whispering. Hundreds of voices, maybe thousands, murmuring behind the walls, under the floors, in the spaces between the stones. Not words. Not yet. Just the pressure of language, like standing outside a locked room where a conversation never ends.

The dead, Sefa had called them once, before deserting — no. Before vanishing. The soldier who'd found him in the borderlands had used the word casually, the way you'd say *weather*, and then had gone pale and stopped talking altogether.

The throne room doors were bone-white wood carved so densely with figures that Theo's eye couldn't find a single inch of plain surface. He leaned close as they passed — habit, curiosity, the mapmaker's compulsion to read any surface that might be a document — and realized the figures were people. Thousands of them, generations of them, all facing inward toward some center point the carving didn't show. All kneeling.

All with their faces smoothed blank.

* * *

King Aldric sat on a throne of black stone at the far end of a hall built for ten thousand mourners.

Theo had prepared himself for a monster. He'd spent the walk down the aisle rehearsing it: the parasite-king of the prophecy book, *the hollow crown*, whatever wore Aldric's body would surely look wrong, move wrong, be wrong in some way he could name and therefore survive. He had imagined too many teeth, too many joints, eyes like coin slots. You prepare for the wolf and you can face the wolf.

What he got was a tired man in his fifties with a well-made crown sitting slightly askew, as though its owner kept forgetting to straighten it.

Aldric had a broad, honest face — the kind farmers trusted, the kind painted onto coins to reassure markets. His hands rested open on the throne's arms, palms up, a posture of welcome so natural it looked rehearsed by someone who had studied welcome but never felt it. And when his gaze found Theo across the length of the hall, he smiled.

It was a good smile. Warm, crinkling at the corners, deployed with perfect timing as Theo reached the base of the dais.

And it was entirely disconnected from his eyes.

That was the horror, Theo thought later, and thought again every night after. Not that the king's eyes were cruel or cold or strange. They were *empty*. They were rooms in a house where the furniture had been sold off room by room, and what remained was space — clean, swept, available for occupancy. The smile happened below them like weather happening over an abandoned field. Two different men's expressions stitched onto one skull, and only the stitching visible if you knew to look.

"Mapmaker," Aldric said. His voice was pleasant, mid-register, the voice of a man asking about your family. "You've come a long way."

"Apparently," Theo managed. His knees wanted to bend; protocol, presumably, required bending. He made them hold. Some stubborn cartographic instinct insisted that you did not kneel to terrain you hadn't surveyed yet. "Your Majesty honors me."

"I do." The king tilted his head, and for half a second the smile stayed exactly where it was while everything underneath it recalculated. "Though I confess I'm curious how you came. The roads between here and the valley have been closed for longer than you've been alive. Longer than I've been alive, if the ledgers are honest, and the ledgers are always honest." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Aren't they?"

"I wouldn't know, Your Majesty. Where I'm from, we don't keep many ledgers." True, and true in the way that mattered: Hollowmere kept almost no records at all. Magic couldn't cross the boundary stones, and neither, apparently, could paperwork. "We're not much of anywhere. That's why I map other places."

"Mm." Aldric rose. Up close, moving, the wrongness got worse, because his body moved beautifully — fluid, graceful, a king's carriage — and his eyes tracked nothing at all. They fixed on Theo's face and stayed fixed, unblinking a beat too long, like a portrait pretending to be a window. "Come. Walk with me. Thrones are for audiences, and this isn't one. This is a conversation between two men who understand documents."

He swept toward a side door, and the courtiers filling the hall — dozens of them, silent, arranged in patterns too regular to be accidental — bowed in a single motion, like wheat before wind. Theo followed the king. Behind him, he heard the great doors begin to close, and the whispering in the walls swelled briefly, the way water rises around a sinking hull.

* * *

The king's study should not have existed where it existed.

Theo knew this with certainty, because he'd mapped this wing of the palace four times in four attempts and the study appeared in none of his versions. It sat behind a door disguised as wall paneling, and inside, it was the first honest room he'd seen in the whole building: warm fire, cluttered desk, books stacked in genuinely disordered towers, a window overlooking a garden where real birds made real noise. Someone had lived here. Someone had loved books and bad organization and the view of flowering trees.

Someone whose chair the current occupant now settled into like a man trying on a coat that almost fit.

"Wine?" Aldric offered, already pouring two glasses without waiting for an answer. "It's from the southern terraces. The vines predate me. Possibly they predate the kingdom. Everything here predates everything; you get used to it." He handed a glass across. "You don't drink?"

Theo realized he was holding the glass without drinking, and made himself take a sip. It was excellent wine. That was somehow the worst detail yet — that the monster had impeccable taste.

"Aldric," the king said, settling back. "Since we're being informal. Or rather — since the man whose name that is has little use for formality at present. Shall I explain what you're looking at, Mapmaker? You strike me as a person who dislikes not knowing things. Your eyes have been measuring my palace since you walked into it. Corridors, distances, sight lines. You've redrawn this wing four times in your head and gotten four different answers." The smile again, brief, warm, horrifying. "I admire it. Curiosity is such a durable quality. So few things survive curation."

"What is this?" Theo kept his voice level. Mostly. "Why am I here? What do you want?"

"*Want* is a strong word. Let us say: anticipate." Aldric turned his glass slowly, watching the light move through it, and for a moment his attention seemed to go somewhere else entirely — inward, or further than inward. When it returned, it returned sharp. "A hundred and twenty-seven years ago, a book was written. In your valley, which is a very inconvenient place, because it is the one place in the world where my reach thins to nothing. The book was written by a seer named Maren Voss, who died comfortably and was buried quietly and left behind one volume of prophecies that the valley, in its wisdom, filed away and forgot." He looked up. "You found it. Tuesday last. Third shelf, behind the tax rolls of a village that hasn't existed since before the valley had a name."

The fire crackled. Somewhere beyond the window, a bird sang, and Theo thought absurdly: *even the birds here might be listening.*

"How do you know that?"

"Because the dead told me. Because the dead tell me everything that happens within reach of anything I've ever touched, and my reach is considerable, and growing." Aldric said it mildly, the way another man might describe a hobby. "But the valley is dark to me. The dead who die there stay theirs, and the living who live there are blind spots, and I have spent a long time being patient about blind spots. And then, three days ago, a man walked out of that darkness carrying the exact shape of a prophecy written before his grandfather was born." He set down the glass. "You walked out of the darkness, Mapmaker. Do you understand what a novelty that is? I haven't been surprised in sixty years. You surprised me twice. Once by existing. Once by being *named*."

"I don't know what the book says."

"No," Aldric agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Because you were removed from the archive before you finished reading it, which was necessary, and regrettable, and I want you to know the timing was not my choice. The boundary took you. The valley protects its own secrets with more violence than I ever could." He spread his hands — the open-palmed gesture again, welcome performed by memory. "Which brings us to the matter at hand. The book still exists. In the archive. On its shelf. And I cannot see it, and I cannot send anyone to fetch it, because everything I send into the valley comes back empty — or doesn't come back. But you, Mapmaker. You came *out* of it. Living, whole, and apparently returnable."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "You want me to go back."

"I want to offer you passage home," Aldric corrected gently. "There's a difference, and the difference matters to me. I don't compel. Compulsion is vulgar. I offer. Consider: a guide to the boundary, papers that will open every gate between here and the stones, and safe conduct past every soldier who currently believes you belong to the Court. In exchange, you retrieve one book. Read it, even — I'm told curiosity is durable. Bring it back to me, and walk home to your life. To your—" the pause was surgical, "—your evening plans."

The word *plans* landed like a hand on the back of Theo's neck. The date. The tavern. Rosalind laughing into her cup, saying *you drew a palace I've only ever seen in a dead woman's last words, Theo, how is that possible.* He had told the king none of this. He had told no one in this kingdom any of this.

"The dead told you," Theo said quietly.

"The dead tell me everything," Aldric agreed. "Including, incidentally, that a bone-carver in your valley has begun sending messages through corpses. Crude work. Sentimental. But resourceful." The empty eyes watched him with terrible patience. "So let us not pretend, you and I, that your heart isn't already halfway home. I'm offering to reunite you with it. All I ask is paper."

"And if I refuse?"

The smile remained. It was, Theo understood suddenly, the most frightening thing about the man: the smile never negotiated. Everything else in that face could shift, calculate, threaten, plead — but the smile stayed fixed like a signature, like something applied at manufacture.

"Then you remain my guest," Aldric said warmly, "for as long as guests remain interesting. And Mapmaker — I mean this as a compliment — you may be interesting for a very long time."

He rose, signaling an end to hospitality, and crossed to the window. Below, in the garden, servants moved among the flowerbeds, and Theo noticed — couldn't help noticing, the way you can't help looking at a wound — that their movements repeated. Exactly. The same turn, the same pause, the same reach toward the same rosebush, over and over, like figures trapped in a loop of film.

"They're resting," Aldric said, without turning around. "Between duties. It's restful, what I offer them. No grief. No fear. No forgetting the ones they love, because they can't forget anything at all." He glanced back, and the stranger's smile bloomed one more time across the borrowed face. "Think about my offer. Sleep on it. You'll find the dead will make sure you sleep deeply — they're very attentive that way."

At the door, Theo stopped. One question, the one that had been clawing at him since the throne room, since the blank-faced carvings, since the looping gardeners.

"The man," he said. "Aldric. The actual man. Is he still in there?"

For the first time, the smile flickered — not vanished, never vanished, but dimmed, like a lamp behind smoked glass. And in the emptied rooms of the king's eyes, something looked back at Theo that was old, and patient, and terribly, terribly hungry to be asked exactly that.

"He's resting," the King said.

The door closed behind him, and in the silence of the corridor, beneath the floor, beneath the walls, the dead began — softly, in hundreds of stolen voices, in perfect unison — to sing him a lullaby he had never heard, in a language he didn't know, about a book on a third shelf behind forgotten tax rolls, waiting in the dark of a valley that magic could not enter.

And neither, he realized with a chill that started in his spine and went all the way down, could he — unless he found the way the prophecy had pushed him out.

Unless he found what the valley wanted with him in the first place.
