## 9. The Bone-Carver's Exile

The boundary stones did not look like much. That was the first cruelty of it.

Rosalind had lived her whole life within sight of them — seven leaning pillars of grey granite ringed with lichen, standing in a broken crescent at the mouth of the valley like old men who had forgotten what they were guarding. Children in Hollowmere used to dare each other to touch them. The stones never did anything. They just stood there, humming that particular silence she'd never been able to name until now: the silence of a held breath.

She stood before the gap between the third and fourth stone with her pack on her back and Theo's sketchbook pressed against her ribs, and for a long moment she could not make her legs obey.

Beyond the stones the land went on. She had always known that, in the abstract way you know the sea is full of fish. Hills rolled up into grey-green distances, and somewhere out there was a kingdom of ash and salt where a king wore another creature's smile, and somewhere out there Theo Vance was either alive or he wasn't, and every hour she spent not moving was an hour she would have to answer for to whatever part of herself kept score.

"Go," whispered the dead of Hollowmere, all around her, faint as breath on glass. In the valley their voices were muffled, wrapped in wool, a murmur through walls. "Go, carver. Go."

"I'm going," she said aloud, because talking to the dead was the only conversation she was good at, and because her voice steadied something in her chest. "I'm going. Stop pushing."

They didn't stop pushing. The dead of Hollowmere had waited three days while she sharpened her tools, packed bone blanks, argued with the elders who told her the valley was a mercy and leaving it was a wound. They were done waiting. She felt their insistence like hands between her shoulder blades.

Rosalind Okonkwo took one step forward, then another, and crossed the line where Hollowmere ended and everything else began.

* * *

It was like being born.

That was the only word for it, and she hated the word, but there it was. One moment the world was the world; the next, sound crashed into her from every direction at once, and she went down onto her knees on wet grass with her hands over her ears, though her ears had nothing to do with it. It was not sound. It was *pressure*. It was a hundred thousand voices pouring through her skull as if someone had pulled a cork out of her and the ocean had decided to move in.

In Hollowmere, the dead came to her thin and distant, one at a time, polite as callers at a door. A dying woman's last words, carried up through the marrow. Her grandmother's warnings. Little fragments, little favors. She had thought that was the gift. She had thought that was *her*.

Outside the stones, she understood that the valley hadn't been muting the dead. It had been muting *her*. What flooded back now was the whole of it, unfiltered, and the whole of it was unbearable.

Dead voices everywhere. Under the grass. In the stones themselves, older than language. In the air over the road, where travelers had died across centuries and left their last words hanging like frost. They weren't speaking to her — most of them didn't even know she was there — they simply *were*, the way living people breathe without thinking, and she could hear all of it, all the time, layered and tangled and screaming in places and weeping in others and in one spot twenty feet to her left laughing, endlessly, horribly, some man repeating his own last joke into eternity.

"*Stop.*"

They didn't. Of course they didn't. You couldn't ask a river to stop being water.

She crawled toward the third boundary stone and pressed her forehead against the granite, and found a sliver of quiet there, the way you find shade under a single tree. The stone hummed its held-breath silence against her skin. She clung to it. Her stomach heaved and produced nothing; she hadn't eaten since morning. Tears ran down her face freely and she let them, because no one was watching except the dead, and the dead had seen everything humans did and judged none of it.

*This is what I am,* she thought, and the thought arrived whole and terrible. *This is what I am outside the valley. Not a woman who hears the dead sometimes. A woman made of ears.*

How had anyone with this gift ever survived? How had her mother — who had the hearing too, faintly, before the stones — how had any of them chosen to stay? No. Not chosen. Been *kept*. The elders said the valley protected them from the world. She had never asked what the world was being protected from. Or what the valley was protecting itself from having walking around inside it.

A voice cut through the roar, and this one was alive.

"Don't move."

Rosalind lifted her head from the stone. Twenty paces down the slope, half-hidden by gorse, stood a woman in a soldier's coat with the insignia torn off, holding a crossbow that was aimed at Rosalind's heart with the steady economy of long practice. Short-cropped hair, brown skin gone weathered, a scar splitting one eyebrow. Her eyes moved — coat, pack, hands, face — reading Rosalind the way Rosalind read bones.

"You're crying," the woman said. "Kneeling at a boundary stone. Crying." Her aim didn't waver. "And I watched you come through from the valley side, which nobody does, which *nobody* does, so either you're mad or—"

"Or," Rosalind said. Her voice came out shredded. She swallowed and tried again. "Or I'm what you think I am."

"What am I thinking?"

"You tell me. You're the one with the bow."

Something flickered across the soldier's face — not quite amusement, not quite pain. "Bone-carver," she said quietly. "Out loud. Gods help me." And then, louder, harder: "Get up slowly. Hands where I can see them."

Rosalind got up slowly. The dead roared in around her the moment she left the stone's shadow and she staggered, caught herself, gritted her teeth against the flood. The soldier watched her sway and her eyes narrowed — not with confusion. With recognition.

"That's the listening-sickness," she said. "New-crossed. My grandmother talked about it. Said the old heretics heard the dead so loud they'd put awls through their own ears trying to make it stop." The crossbow dipped an inch. "You're really one of them."

"I carve messages," Rosalind said. "For grieving families. Last words into bone. That's all. That's all it ever was, back home."

"And here it isn't."

"Here I don't know what it is yet."

The soldier studied her for a long moment. Then, abruptly, she lowered the crossbow all the way and slung it across her back, and the suddenness of it made Rosalind more nervous than the aiming had.

"Sefa," the woman said. "Formerly of the Fourth Ashen Guard. Formerly meaning as of nineteen days ago, when I stopped being anything the crown would admit to." She jerked her chin at the boundary stones. "You're Rosalind Okonkwo. The mapmaker's letter named you."

Every sound in Rosalind's body stopped, even the dead, for one impossible second.

"Theo's *what*?"

* * *

Sefa led her off the road to a shepherd's hut with a collapsed roof, half-swallowed by gorse, and built a small fire in the corner that still had ceiling. She moved like someone who had learned to be invisible in open country: low, quick, always facing the wind. While she worked, Rosalind sat with her back to the wall and her hands pressed flat against her thighs and tried to be a person instead of an ear.

It was getting worse, or she was getting better at drowning. Hard to say. The dead of this kingdom spoke differently than the dead of Hollowmere — that was the thing she couldn't stop noticing, the thing crawling under her skin. Hollowmere's dead were finished. At peace, mostly, or at least *ended*, their words worn smooth like river stones. These dead were raw. Recent. Hundreds of them, thousands, all along the road and in the fields and in the ground under the fire, and they all repeated the same shapes of sentence: *they came at dawn, they took his name, she forgot her children, don't sign the ledger, don't sign the ledger, don't sign—*

Sign what ledger? Whose name?

"Drink," Sefa said, pressing a tin cup into her hands. Willow bark tea, bitter and hot. Rosalind drank it too fast and burned her tongue and didn't care.

"Theo," she said. "You said a letter. He sent a letter?"

"Through the courier-dead, if you can believe it. I still half don't." Sefa crouched across the fire, and in its light her scar looked fresh, though it was clearly years old. "Three days ago a dead man walked into the camp of the Fourth Ashen Guard, regular as you please, and handed a corporal a rib bone with writing carved into it, and then stood there rotting politely until the captain ordered him shot apart. Which they did. Which did nothing. He picked himself up, handed the bone to the corporal again, and pointed at me."

"Why you?"

"Because I flinched." Sefa's mouth twisted. "Everyone else saw a corpse doing tricks. I saw a message. I grew up on stories about bone-carvers, carver — my gran was from the salt marshes, before the purges, and she told me things the church burned people for knowing. So I took the bone. And two days later the captain announced we were marching on Thornhollow village to do harvest-work, and I decided nineteen days of desertion might as well become a habit."

"Harvest-work," Rosalind repeated. The phrase landed wrong in her ear, like a word in a language almost hers. Around her the dead murmured: *the ledger, the ledger, they took his name.* "What does that mean, harvest?"

Sefa looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Rosalind's face made her decide something.

"It means soldiers go into a village and the king's readers set up a ledger, and one by one the villagers kneel and the readers take memories out of them. Big ones. Names, faces, loves. They write down what they take and burn the rest, and afterward the villagers are — lighter. Compliant. Smiling." Her jaw worked. "The crown calls it tithing. The king calls it pruning. The priests say the Hollow King is merciful, that he only takes what we can spare." She laughed, short and awful. "He takes what we can't."

The fire cracked. Somewhere beyond the collapsed roof, a night bird called, and underneath the bird, underneath the wind, underneath everything, the dead went on whispering their ledger.

"Here." Sefa reached into her coat and drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, and passed it across the fire.

Rosalind knew what it was before she unwrapped it. She knew it the way she knew her own hands.

A rib bone. Human, or near enough. Carved along its curve in her own style — no, in *her actual hand*, her spacing, her stroke-lengths, the little hooked terminals she used without thinking. Because it *was* her hand. Her first letter. The one she had given to the courier in the tavern yard four days ago, pressing the warm bone into a dead man's grip and telling him: *find the cartographer, find the man who crossed the stones.*

She read it by firelight, her own words coming back to her:

*I am coming. Do not sign anything. Do not give them your name. Hold on, Theo. — R.*

"He got it," she said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "He got it and he sent—" She stopped. Looked up. "You said a letter. His letter. Where is it?"

Sefa's face did something complicated.

"That's the hard part, carver."

"Where. Is. It."

"The bone reached me. His reply didn't come by the dead." She fed a stick to the fire, not looking at Rosalind. "His reply came by the king's post. A sealed letter, delivered to the Fourth Guard's captain, ordering that any deserters carrying correspondence from the palace archive be taken alive. It described you. It described your tools. It was signed by the Royal Cartographer's office — *Vance, T., Acting.*"

The tea turned to iron in Rosalind's stomach.

"They're using him," she said. "They've got him carving for them."

"Or he's playing a game with whatever's nearest to play with." Sefa's eyes came up, sharp and level. "I don't know him. I know the seal on the letter was genuine and the handwriting was his and the order was to capture you alive, which means somebody wearing his name wants you found. Maybe it's him, buying time, keeping you out of the harvest queues by making you a prize instead of a tithe. Maybe it's the thing that wears the king, using his hand the way it uses his face." She shrugged. "Either way, carver, here's what I know. The dead carried your bone three hundred miles in four days because you asked nicely. Nothing else in this kingdom obeys anybody anymore. And the prophecy my gran used to whisper says that when the carver crosses the water, the hollow crown cracks."

Rosalind stared into the fire. Around her, patient as tide, the dead murmured their ledger, their stolen names, their unfinished griefs — thousands of them, deafening, endless, and for the first time since she'd crossed the stones she listened *into* it instead of against it, the way she'd listen into a dying woman's chest for the true word beneath the rattle.

There. Under everything. A thread of repetition, far away, coming from the direction of the capital — not a villager's voice, not a soldier's. A scholar's cadence, clipped and precise, reciting coordinates over and over into the dark like a man hiding a message in plain song.

*North-northwest. The false maps lie. Follow the river that isn't on the page. North-northwest.*

Theo's voice. Or what was left of it, carried by whatever dead were near enough to hear a man think aloud in his cell.

Rosalind closed her hand around the rib bone until the edges bit her palm.

"Sefa," she said. "How fast can you march?"
