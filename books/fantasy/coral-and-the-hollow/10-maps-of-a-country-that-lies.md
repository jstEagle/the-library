## 10. Maps of a Country That Lies

The fire was small and mean, the kind of fire that had learned to be ashamed of itself.

Rosalind sat beside it in the ruin of a shepherd's hut two days beyond the boundary stones, her hands still trembling from what lay outside. She had thought Hollowmere's silence was quiet. She had been wrong. Out here, the dead were not whispers at the edge of sleep — they were a tide. Every mile she walked, they pressed against her like moths against glass: soldiers in ditches, farmers under their own orchards, children whose names no one had spoken aloud in years. By the second night she had learned to walk with her jaw clenched and her mind shut like a door, because if she opened it even a crack, hundreds of voices poured through.

*You're loud,* Sefa had said that morning, watching Rosalind stagger. *I can hear them leaking out of you. We'll die of it.*

So now they sat in silence, or near silence, while Sefa cleaned her sword with a rag gone gray from use. She was a compact woman, all angles and economy, with a deserter's cropped hair and a soldier's habit of sitting facing the door. Her uniform had been picked apart stitch by stitch; only the boots remained, too good to burn.

"You haven't asked," Sefa said, without looking up.

"Asked what?"

"Anything. Where we're going. Why I'm helping you. What I did." The rag paused on the blade. "Deserters don't help strangers for free."

"I assumed you'd tell me when you wanted to."

Sefa looked up then, and something moved behind her eyes — surprise, quickly killed. "In Hollowmere, is that how people are?"

"No," Rosalind said. "In Hollowmere people ask constantly and listen never. I just got tired of both sides of it." She turned the rib bone over in her fingers — the courier's rib, given freely before he died, *take this where I can't go*. It was blank still. She hadn't known what to carve yet.

Sefa grunted, which seemed to be her version of respect.

Outside, the wind moved through a country that smelled of ash and salt, as its name promised. Somewhere far off, a bell rang once — not a village bell; Rosalind had learned those. This one came from the direction of the palace, deep and slow, like something counting.

"The harvest bells," Sefa said, following her gaze. "They ring when the king takes memories from a town. You hear them every few days now. Used to be once a season."

Rosalind's stomach turned. "And no one stops him."

"Stop him." Sefa laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "People here don't stop the king. They memorize the routes his soldiers take so they can be somewhere else. They teach their children which stories are safe to tell. My mother made me recite boring things before bed — the price of turnips, my uncle's bad knee — so if I was ever harvested, the takers would get nothing worth having." She sheathed the sword. "That's what resistance looks like here. A whole kingdom learning to be dull."

Rosalind looked down at her hands. Carver's hands, her grandmother had called them, though her grandmother had also called them cursed. In Hollowmere, magic pooled around the boundary stones and refused to come in, and so her gift had been a candle flame cupped behind glass — real, but small. Here, she could feel it like a second heartbeat.

"Sefa. The maps you showed me. The ones your unit used."

"What about them?"

"They're wrong."

Sefa went very still. It was the stillness of a woman deciding whether the person across the fire was a fool or a threat. "They're the king's own surveyors' work."

"I know. That's why they're wrong." Rosalind set down the bone and reached for the map Sefa had let her copy — charcoal on cloth, the road to the capital, the palace district, the river. "Look at the river. On your map it bends east around the hill at Vessany. But the dead along that road keep telling me about drowning in a river that bends west. Three separate dead, three different decades. Rivers don't move that far in fifty years, but roads do, and someone redrew this."

"You're reading geography off corpses."

"I'm reading *truth* off corpses. There's a difference." She met Sefa's eyes. "Your maps aren't badly drawn. They're *lied*. Deliberately. And the lie is maintained somehow — I can feel it when I touch the cloth. Like a hand pressed flat over the truth."

Sefa was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a woman stepping onto ice. "There's a word among the old soldiers. *Map-sick*, they call it. You navigate by the king's maps and you end up in places that shouldn't exist. Roads that loop back. Bridges over dry valleys. Men have starved three miles from a town because every road out of it pointed away." She rubbed her face. "We were told it was curse-fog, old battle magic. Everyone believed it, because believing it meant the maps weren't lies, just wounded. A lie you can forgive easier than treason."

"It's not fog. It's craft. Someone rewrites the country on paper, and the paper argues with the ground until the ground gives up." Rosalind felt cold settle into her. "Which means the palace can't be reached by any honest road, because there isn't an honest road left. Except—" She stopped.

"Except?"

"Theo." His name caught in her chest like a fishbone. "He drew places he'd never been. Perfectly. He told me cartographers have instincts — that after enough years you feel when a line is wrong, the way a musician hears a false note. If anyone can read these maps and find the true shape underneath the lie, it's him." She stared into the fire. "If he's alive. If he's in the palace."

"He's in the palace," Sefa said quietly. "The court took a mapmaker the day after the boundary pulled him through. Word travels down even to deserters. They gave him rooms and ink and called him a guest." Her mouth twisted. "This kingdom doesn't have guests."

Rosalind closed her eyes. *A guest.* Caged and thanked for it. She thought of his hands sketching the palace he'd never seen, laughing at himself, saying *I dream in coastlines, Roz, it's a terrible way to live.* The memory hurt in the specific way that only good memories can hurt when you don't know if you'll make more.

"Then I need to send him another letter," she said. "One that tells him how to be found."

* * *

Theo had learned that the parasite-king's court kept excellent time and terrible secrets, and that the two were related: everything happened exactly on schedule so that nothing could happen off the record.

He stood now at the window of his gilded rooms, watching the evening bells, pretending to refine a survey of the eastern provinces. The survey was a fiction. The real work lay beneath it — pages of true observations hidden inside false ones, the way he'd once hidden love notes inside homework as a boy. Every window in the palace gave him a bearing. Every corridor walk gave him a pace count. He was building the palace in his head, floor by floor, the way a man builds a raft from the wreckage of his ship.

*A cartographer's instinct,* he'd told the smiling king. *Give me height and I'll give you truth.*

It was the most dangerous sentence he'd ever spoken, because it was true, and because the thing wearing Aldric had leaned forward with interest and said, *Truth. How novel.*

A sound in the corner of the room made him turn.

At first he thought it was dust moving wrong. Then it resolved: a figure standing where no figure should be, gray as ash, translucent at the edges, wearing the rotted livery of a royal courier who had clearly died decades ago. Theo's whole body went cold. He knew what the dead were here. He had heard them in the walls at night, murmuring reports to their master — the entire kingdom's dead bent into a single listening ear.

But this one wasn't looking at the walls. It was looking at *him*, holding out one gray hand.

On the palm lay a small white curve. Bone. Carved.

Theo crossed the room before he could think better of it and took it. The courier dissolved like smoke remembering it was smoke.

The carving was shallow, hurried, letters cut by someone working fast with poor tools. But he would have known that hand anywhere — the slight rightward lean, the way her R's kicked. He had watched those hands for one perfect evening over bad wine and better conversation.

*Roz.*

He read it twice, three times, pressing the bone to his forehead like something holy.

*I am outside the city. The maps lie; trust no drawn road. I will reach the Feast of Hollow Lights. Find the man inside the king — his name is half-erased but not gone. Keep yourself whole. Do not trade anything for passage home. Nothing. I am coming. — R*

She was coming. Two days past the boundary, alone, into a kingdom that ate memories — and coming anyway. Theo sat down on the floor because standing seemed suddenly ambitious, and laughed once, wetly, at the absurdity of it: rescued by correspondence. Guided home by a woman who carved promises into the ribs of the willing dead.

Then he read the letter again, properly, and the relief curdled.

*Do not trade anything.* She knew. Which meant she knew what the king had offered him — which meant either she guessed, or the dead talked, or both. And the rest of the letter was worse, in its way. *Find the man inside the king. His name is half-erased but not gone.* She was asking him to go deeper. To get closer to the thing that had already offered to buy him and had begun, he suspected, to shop.

He thought of the archive below the east wing — the room he'd found by accident, full of records with holes in them, names scraped out like scars. He had copied one name onto a scrap tucked inside his boot. Aldric's true name, or most of it. *Half-erased but not gone.*

The bells finished counting. Below, torches flared along the processional road: preparations for the Feast of Hollow Lights, six days away. Six days to become indispensable to a monster, to find a cellar he wasn't sure existed, to keep his own mind intact while something ancient tried it on like a coat.

Theo took out his pencil and began, carefully, to draw — a false map for the king's eyes, and threaded invisibly through its lines, a true one. A message in bearings and elevations that only one kind of person in the world could read.

*Hold on, Roz. I'll leave you directions. I always do.*

* * *

"She sent him *bone*?" Sefa stared at the rib Rosalind held up, its message already fading as the carving settled back into smoothness — spent, delivered, done. "Through the boundary. Through the king's own dead-network. And it arrived?"

"The dead carry what they're given. Even enslaved ones, apparently, if the giver asks politely and pays in grief." Rosalind set the spent bone down. "His first reply hasn't come, but the courier returned empty-handed, which means empty-pawed, which means—"

"Which means you're guessing," Sefa said. But she said it while feeding the fire, which was different from saying it while reaching for a sword.

Rosalind studied her across the flames. Two days of shared road, shared watch, shared silence. Sefa had killed a wolf on the first night without waking her, had given her the drier bedroll without comment, had asked precisely zero questions about the bone-carving except one: *does it hurt them?* And when Rosalind said no, the dead give freely, Sefa had nodded like a woman settling a debt.

"Why are you helping me?" Rosalind asked now. "Really. Not the answer you rehearsed."

Sefa didn't answer for a long time. Wind worried the ruined roof above them. Then, quietly, as if setting down something heavy:

"When I was twelve, my grandmother died. And that night she stood at the foot of my bed — not a ghost story, actually there, gray and tired — and she said, *They've taken me, little bird. The king's men hold the dead here like birds in cages, and the king listens through us. Don't ever say anything true where a body is buried.*" Sefa's jaw worked. "I grew up watching my words around graves. Watching my words everywhere. And then they made me a harvester, and I watched my brother forget our mother's face, and I ran." She looked up. "You talk to the dead like they're people. You *asked* that courier, I saw you — asked, thanked him. No one in this kingdom has treated the dead like people since the hollow crown came. So that's why. Not you. Them. Someone owes them courtesy, and you're the first person I've met who pays it."

Rosalind found she couldn't speak for a moment. Loneliness, she thought, worn so long it feels like skin — and then someone says a thing like that, and you remember the skin was always thin.

"We'll need more than courtesy," she managed finally. "The feast is in six days. The palace is a liar's maze. And the only person who can read the lie is locked inside it."

"So we get a message in to him, and he reads us a way in."

"He already may have." Rosalind touched her collarbone, where a faint ache had lived since dawn — the place her gift registered messages traveling toward her. Or perhaps she only hoped it did. "The dead say a courier walks the wall-road tonight carrying something small and white for the carver at the shepherd's ruin."

Sefa raised an eyebrow. "Convenient."

"The dead are rarely convenient. They're usually *urgent*." Rosalind stood, brushing ash from her knees, and felt the tide of voices press close — hundreds of the enslaved, and threading among them, faint but unmistakable, one voice that leaned right when it laughed.

Not his voice. A dead man carrying his words. But it was enough. It was everything.

She lifted the new rib bone — blank, waiting — and her carving knife together, and began to write the plan that would either save a kingdom or bury two strangers and a mapmaker under it:

*Tell him the feast. Tell him the cellar. Tell him I keep my promises, carved or otherwise.*

Above the hills, the palace lit its hollow lights, one by one, like eyes opening.
