## 12. Letters Across the Dark

### Rosalind

The dead did not sleep, which meant neither, properly, did she.

Rosalind sat in the ruined chapel outside Sefa's camp with a rib bone the length of her forearm resting on her knee, a burin in her hand, and no idea how to say what needed saying. She had carved last words for forty years. Last words were easy — they came pre-broken, pre-honest, wrung out of people who had run out of time to lie. But this was not a last word. This was the opposite: a first word, sent into the dark to a living man, and she kept striking through her own beginnings.

*Dear Theo* — too much like a letter to a stranger.

*I found your sketchbook* — too much like an accusation.

*I'm coming* — true, and useless without everything that followed.

Sefa stood watch at the chapel door, arms crossed against the cold, pretending not to listen. There was nothing to hear. The carving made no sound but a whisper of steel on bone.

The courier waited on the altar slab where the parishioners had once laid their offerings. His name had been Merrick; he had been a soldier of the hollow king's army for eleven years, and then he had been a deserter for six days, and then he had been dead for one. The parasite's dead walked when it called them, but a bone-carver could call them louder, and what Rosalind had learned in three weeks of crossing this gray country was that the dead here were not empty. They were *held*. Held things remember being held, and given the smallest opening they strained toward anything that felt like release.

Merrick strained toward her now. She could feel him at the edge of her hearing, the way you feel weather coming — a pressure, a patience.

She looked down at the rib and began again, small letters, because space was finite and so was time:

*T. — The valley I come from forgets magic. I thought it was a mercy. It was only quiet. Out here I can hear all of them, every soul this thing has ever taken, and some nights I want to tear my own ears off and other nights I think: if I can hear them, I can free them.*

She stopped. Struck none of it through. It was more than she'd meant to say, and exactly enough.

*Sefa is taking me along the salt road to the city. We'll be there by the Feast of Hollow Lights — she says every soul in the palace will be drunk on borrowed light, even the thing in the king. Get Aldric to me. Any cellar, any crypt, any room with stone walls and no windows. Bring the man, not the smile he wears. I need his hands flat on my table and I need four minutes and I need no one screaming.*

*Four minutes. That's how long the carving takes once I know the name. I have the name. Don't ask how. Ask me when we're both somewhere the dead aren't listening.*

*A last thing. Your map of Hollowmere — the one you drew from memory on our date, the one you said you'd finish someday. I keep thinking about the way you drew the river wrong on purpose. You said, "A map should tell the truth about the land or the truth about the mapmaker, and the second one lasts longer." I didn't understand you then. I understand you now. Stay strange, Theo Vance. Stay the kind of man who lies beautifully to maps.*

*R.*

She read it over twice. Then, because the bone held more, she added the thing she had circled for an hour without landing:

*P.S. In the tavern, before the stones took you, you were telling me about a coastline. I never heard the end of it. Finish it for me when I see you. I'll know if you invent it.*

That was a lie, and she knew it — she wouldn't know, couldn't know — but she wanted him to believe she would. She wanted him carrying that sentence like a coin in his pocket, something to turn over in the dark.

She set the rib on the altar slab beside Merrick's folded hands.

"Take it to the palace," she said aloud, and let the hearing rise in her like floodwater. "There's a man there who draws maps. He'll be the one the walls are watching. Give it to him and you're done — do you understand? Done. Nothing holds you after."

The dead man's chest rose, fell. His eyes opened on nothing. He took the rib in both hands, gently, the way the living take a sleeping child, and stood.

At the door, he paused. The voice that came out of him was dry as pages turning, and it was not entirely his own — some of it was Merrick, some of it was the chorus behind him, and some of it, faintly, was older than all of them.

*"Carver,"* it said. *"It knows you're writing. It reads what we carry."*

Rosalind went cold. "Then it knows I'm coming."

*"Yes,"* said the dead. *"But it does not know which feast. It thinks you are two travelers and a grudge. Lie to us, carver. We will carry the lie gladly. It's the only gift left we can give."*

She smiled, and it hurt her face. "All right," she said. "Here's the lie: tell it I've turned back. Tell it the carver lost her nerve at the salt marshes and went home to her valley."

Merrick's ruined mouth curved. *"Gladly,"* he said, and walked out into the dark, and the dark closed over him like water.

Sefa appeared at her shoulder. "Well?"

"It reads the letters." Rosalind flexed her stiff fingers around the burin. "So from tonight, we write it fiction."

### Theo

The letters arrived the way everything arrived in that palace: quietly, and with someone else's hands.

Theo had learned the couriers' schedule by accident — by *map*, really, mapping the palace the way he mapped everything now, because the falsified charts in the archive were useless and his own hand-drawn plan of corridors and stairwells was the only honest geography in the kingdom. He had noticed that twice a week, at the change of the night watch, a servant died. Not murdered. *Spent.* The parasite wore its servants of air the way it wore the king, and when one wore through, it dropped them wherever they stood and called up another.

The fourth spent servant had fallen in the gallery outside his guest chambers, and as the light left her, she had pressed something into his hand: a rib bone, carved over in fine, fierce lettering.

He had read it eleven times. He had slept holding it, which was either madness or the most sane act of his life.

Now, five nights later, a second courier came — a boy this time, maybe fifteen, wearing the gray livery of the palace dead, standing in Theo's doorway at the hour when the corridor lamps guttered. The boy held out a fresh-carved bone and spoke in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

*"She says: changed route, arriving Feast of Hollow Lights, find the king, bring him to stone and silence. She says also:"* and here the boy hesitated, and for a moment his own face surfaced through the borrowed voice, young and scared and human — *"she says, stay strange."*

Theo laughed. It came out cracked. He hadn't laughed in weeks and it sounded like a hinge that needed oiling.

"Tell her—" He stopped. Told to a dead boy, relayed through whatever network the parasite wove, every word a coin dropped into the enemy's pocket. He crossed to his desk, pulled out paper he'd smuggled from the archive, and wrote quickly, folding the page before the ink dried. "Give her this. Only her. Can you do that?"

*"The dead don't read,"* the boy said. Then, honestly: *"We're read. But she taught us a trick. Fold it small and we carry it inside the bone of our own arm. It can't see what's written in us. It never learned to look inward."*

"Then put this inside your arm," Theo said, "and thank you."

The boy took the page. For a heartbeat he looked at Theo with his own eyes. "You're the mapmaker," he said, in his real voice, barely a whisper. "From the prophecy. My mother used to say the prophecy wrong. She said it promised a *kingdom unmapped*, not a kingdom *unmade*. She said that was hope." He tucked the paper against his forearm, and the skin closed over it like water closing over a stone, and the borrowed stillness came back down over his face like a visor, and he was gone.

Theo sat down at his desk and put his head in his hands.

Five weeks in this place. Five weeks of smiling at courtiers whose smiles weren't theirs, of eating food that tasted of nothing, of lying awake while the thing in the king's body spoke to him through the walls in a voice like honey poured over gravel. *Mapmaker,* it liked to call him. *My cartographer of the unwritten places. Tell me again about your little valley where nothing follows.* It thought it was seducing him. It thought flattery was a solvent. And every night it peeled away a little more of him — not memories, not yet, it was saving those, it had told him so with the tenderness of a connoisseur — but the *edges*. The taste of rain. The smell of his father's workshop. The name of his childhood street. Small things, gone quiet, like rooms sealed off in a house.

He still had her. That was the strange arithmetic of it: the parasite had eaten the periphery of his life and left the center untouched, and the center was a tavern in Hollowmere, lamplight, a woman with bone dust on her knuckles laughing at his terrible joke about contour lines. Every night he rebuilt her from the ground up, deliberately, like a man rehearsing a speech: her name, her face, the exact pitch of her laugh, the way she'd gone still when he'd sketched the palace — *how do you know this room, Theo, why do you know this room* — and he'd said *I don't, I've never left home*, and they'd both known he was lying and neither of them had minded.

He took out the first letter again. Read the postscript. *Finish the coastline for me when I see you. I'll know if you invented it.*

He got out paper and began, right there, at midnight, to write her the ending of the story he'd never finished — the coastline, the harbor town, the lighthouse built on coral. Because whether or not she'd know, *he* would know, and he was beginning to understand that in this kingdom the only property worth defending was the truth you kept about yourself.

### Rosalind

The reply came back folded inside a dead girl's wrist, and Rosalind read it by candlelight in the salt marshes while Sefa counted rounds and swore at her boots.

His handwriting was beautiful and slightly hurried, a cartographer's hand, all clean lines leaning forward like they had somewhere to be.

*R. — The coastline, as promised: past the harbor town of Vel, the cliffs go white and the sea goes green and there's a lighthouse on a coral shelf that the locals swear grows a foot a year, which is impossible, which is why I believe it. The keeper is a woman who has never once looked at the sea. She looks inland. When I asked her why, she said, "I already know what's out there. It's what walks toward me I have to watch." I woke up this morning and realized I've been the keeper for five weeks. Watching inland. Waiting for you to walk toward me.*

*Feast of Hollow Lights. Understood. The king dines in public that night — the thing likes an audience — but it sends him to the crypt below the east chapel afterward to "rest," which means it takes the body off and stores it. If you can reach the crypt, you'll have him alone for the length of the feast's final toast. That's your four minutes and more. Sefa will know the east chapel. Everyone avoids it. The dead there are oldest and the thing doesn't waste surveillance on what it considers furniture.*

*One warning, and I need you to take it seriously: it suspects me. Tonight at supper it asked me, idly, what I remembered about the tavern. I told it a version with a different woman and a different town and it seemed satisfied, but satisfaction isn't belief. If it decides to eat my memories rather than trade them, I won't be able to tell you who I am when you find me. So carve something into the crypt wall before the feast — anything, your initials, a date, a line only the two of us would understand — and if I look at you that night without recognition, show me the wall. Bones remember longer than brains. You taught me that. Well. You'll teach me that.*

*Yours across the dark,*
*T.*

P.S. *The keeper of the lighthouse is wrong, by the way. It's not what walks toward her she has to watch. It's what she becomes while waiting. Tell Sefa the boots fail because she's walking south with a northward heart. Maps are honest. People are the liars.*

Rosalind read the P.S. three times. On the third reading she laughed out loud, alone in a marsh at midnight, and the sound startled a heron up out of the reeds, and somewhere in the vast listening dark of the kingdom a parasite wearing a king cocked its borrowed head.

"He writes back," she told Sefa later, folding the letter away. "He jokes. It's eating him piece by piece and he *jokes*."

"Is that good?"

"It's the best sign there is." She looked east, toward the city, toward the smudge of light on the horizon where the palace burned its endless hollow festival glow. "People stop joking right before they break. As long as he's funny, he's fighting."

Sefa said nothing for a moment. Then: "The east chapel. I know it. I harvested there." Her jaw worked. "Twice."

"I know."

"You knew?"

"The dead told me. They also told me you wept while you did it, and that you buried your brother's memories under a rowan tree instead of delivering them, and that the parasite has been looking for that rowan tree for six years." Rosalind met her eyes. "You're not my sin, Sefa. You're my way in."

Sefa turned away fast, but not fast enough to hide her face breaking open, and after a while she said, hoarse: "The feast is in nine days. The salt road takes seven if the checkpoints are asleep. They won't be asleep."

"Then we walk the road that isn't on any map."

"There is no road that isn't on any map."

"There will be," Rosalind said, "in nine days. He'll draw it for us. He's very good at roads that don't exist — he drew mine first."

### Theo

Nine days before the feast, the parasite summoned him to the throne room at an hour when the court was empty, and Theo walked the long corridor knowing, the way animals know weather, that something had changed.

The king sat slumped on the throne like laundry. Up close, always, the wrongness was worse: the beauty of the body and the vacancy behind it, the smile that arrived a half-second late like a translation. Its eyes found Theo across sixty feet of marble.

*"Mapmaker."* The voice filled the room without effort. *"Come stand in the light. I want to look at you."*

Theo came. Standing in the light was the job. You stood in the light and you lied with your whole posture.

*"Nine days until my feast,"* it said. *"Do you know what we celebrate?"*

"Hollow Lights. The old victory."

*"The old harvest,"* it corrected, gently. *"Long ago a carver tried to unmake me with a name. A true name, cut into a king's bone. Do you know what happened to her?"*

"No," Theo said, and his mouth had gone dry, because he did know — he'd read it in the archive, in the margin of a burned ledger, in a hand that shook: *the carver was taken; the carver was worn.*

*"She became part of the chorus,"* the parasite said. *"And do you know what the chorus sings to me lately, mapmaker? It sings about bones. Carved bones, traveling. Letters. Someone is writing to someone in my kingdom, and I do not tolerate mail."* It leaned forward, and the king's handsome face hung slack beneath it, and for one terrible instant Theo saw the man underneath — Aldric, deep down, watching through his own eyes from somewhere far away, mouthing a single word over and over: *soon. soon. soon.*

*"So here is my offer, renewed and improved,"* the parasite said pleasantly. *"Bring me the writer, and I will not merely return your memories of her. I will return them enlarged. Every detail sharper. I am generous with what I love, and I love memory the way sailors love the sea."*

"I don't know any writer," Theo said.

*"Of course you don't."* The smile widened, late and lovely and empty. *"But your handwriting has changed these past weeks, mapmaker. It leans forward now. Like it has somewhere to be."*

Theo bowed. Turned. Walked the sixty feet of marble with his spine straight and his heart going like a drum, and behind him the throne room was silent, which was worse than laughter, silence was always worse.

That night he wrote his shortest letter, and gave it to the boy with the keyhole voice, and watched it disappear into the bone of the boy's arm.

*She knows. Change nothing. Nine days. — T.*

And underneath, small, the thing he had not dared to put in any letter before:

*When this is done, if I am still me — dinner. Somewhere with bad wine and good light. I owe you the end of a story about a lighthouse.*

Three nights later the answer came back, folded in a dead girl's wrist, and it was four words long, and he read it by the light of the hollow festival lamps being hung in their thousands across the city, each one burning pale as bone:

*Bad wine. Good light. Come.*

He laughed again — the cracked-hinge laugh — and folded the letter into his boot, over his heart's nearest neighbor, and began counting down the nine days like a man counting steps to a door he could not yet see.

On the eighth day, the parasite canceled the feast's final toast.

On the ninth, it doubled the guard on the east chapel.

And on the morning of the Feast of Hollow Lights itself, a deserting soldier walked through the palace's servant gate carrying two lanterns, a bundle of carving tools wrapped in oilcloth, and a face the dead had taught her to borrow — while somewhere above her, in a tower room, a mapmaker sharpened a pencil and told himself, one more time, the shape of her laugh.
