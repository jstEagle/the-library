## 13. The Feast of Hollow Lights

The invitation arrived on a plate of black glass, carried by a servant whose eyes never quite settled on anything — not the doorframe, not Theo's face, not the candle flames guttering along the corridor. The script was gold leaf pressed into the glass itself: *His Majesty requests the honor of the mapmaker's presence at the Feast of Hollow Lights.*

*Requests.* As though there were a version of this evening where Theo declined and simply went on breathing.

He turned the plate over in his hands. It was warm, though nothing had touched it but the servant's gloved fingers, and beneath the warmth he felt something faintly rhythmic, like a pulse trapped in the glass. He set it down quickly.

"You'll want the blue coat," said Vessin, the chamberlain who had been assigned to him — or assigned *to watch* him; in this palace the two professions seemed to share a wardrobe. "Silver is for the royal household. Blue says honored guest. Green would say prisoner who has been forgiven, which you are not."

"And what does no coat at all say?"

Vessin smiled with genuine pleasure, as if Theo had finally asked an intelligent question. "That you have already been hollowed. They'd seat you near the king."

Theo dressed in the blue coat. He had learned, in the three weeks since his arrival, that survival here was mostly a matter of costume — wearing the right expression the way one wore the right colors, keeping one's face a map with nothing marked upon it. His sketchbook stayed hidden beneath a floorboard in his rooms, wrapped in oilcloth, because the pages that mattered were not the ones showing trade routes and river fords. The pages that mattered held Aldric's true name, copied letter by careful letter from the half-erased archive folio, disguised among sketches of bridges.

A name was a weapon. He carried it the way other men carried knives — close to the body, checked hourly, never spoken aloud.

Tonight, though, the name felt heavier than usual. Tonight he would be seated within twenty feet of the thing that wore the king, and it had begun asking questions.

---

The great hall of the palace had been built by people who believed that awe was a form of governance. Columns of veined marble rose into darkness where chandeliers hung like frozen explosions, each one holding hundreds of candles, each flame burning a color slightly wrong — too white, too steady, flames that did not flicker when doors opened. Theo counted them out of habit. Forty-one chandeliers. Four thousand candles, give or take. Not one of them moved.

The courtiers moved instead. They flowed across the floor in silks of ash-grey and salt-white — the kingdom's colors, Ash and Salt, worn like a reminder of who owned everything — and their laughter came in waves that broke exactly when the music swelled and receded exactly when it softened, so precisely that Theo began to suspect the orchestra was following the room rather than the reverse.

"Mapmaker." A woman in dove-grey took his arm without asking. Lady Isbet, he thought, or Iseult; her face kept sliding out of his memory the way faces did here, and he suspected that was not an accident either. "You must tell me — is it true you come from beyond the boundary stones? From the valley where magic cannot go?"

"It's true I come from somewhere quiet," Theo said.

"How dreadful." She said it with perfect sincerity. Around them, four hundred people dined on courses Theo could not identify — meat that tasted of nothing until salt was added, wine that tasted of salt, bread that tasted of almost — and every dish arrived under silver covers that were lifted in unison, as if the food itself required ceremony to be permitted flavor.

He ate little. He watched much.

The king sat at the high table on a throne of black coral — actual coral, bone-pale and branching, and Theo's stomach turned when he saw it, because Rosalind had once told him, over a second glass of wine neither of them had needed, that coral was bone that had learned to live underwater. *Everything alive remembers being stone,* she'd said. He had thought it was poetry then. Now it looked like architecture.

King Aldric — the parasite wearing King Aldric — raised a hand, and four thousand candles dimmed in unison.

"Hollow Lights," the king said, and his voice filled the hall without effort, the way water fills a basin. "Once a year we remember what we owe. Once a year we remember what we are given." He smiled, and the smile belonged to a stranger; Theo had seen the real Aldric's face in old portraits in the archive, and the difference was in the eyes, always the eyes. Portraits showed a man with crow's feet carved deep from laughing. The man on the throne had those same creases, preserved like fossil record, around eyes that had never once used them. "This year, we are given much. We are given unity. We are given obedience, freely offered. And we are given—" the pause was surgical, timed to the candlelight, "—a guest."

Every head turned toward Theo.

He had prepared for this. Three weeks of preparation, of rehearsing blandness, of making himself a map with nothing marked. He stood, inclined his head, and said, "Your Majesty honors me."

"I do." The king gestured, gracious as summer. "A cartographer. A maker of maps. What a useful thing a mapmaker is." He let the word *useful* sit in the air like a coin on a table. "Tell the court, mapmaker — what do you find when you map a country?"

Theo heard the trap. It was elegant, almost beautiful, the way a snare is beautiful if you don't step in it. Say *borders* and he admitted the kingdom's maps were worth mapping. Say *lies* and he announced he knew about the falsification. Say *truth* and he flattered a regime built on erasure.

"A country," Theo said slowly, "is whatever its rivers decide. Maps just follow the water."

Laughter rolled through the hall, warm and approving, and the king laughed too — a full-bodied, kingly laugh that reached his mouth and stopped at the border of his eyes. "Follow the water," the king repeated, delighted. "Yes. We shall all follow the water." He raised his cup. "To our guest."

Four hundred cups rose. Four hundred voices said *to our guest.* And Theo, drinking, understood with cold clarity that he had just been measured, weighed, and found — not wanting, exactly. Found *interesting.*

Interesting was worse than suspicious. Suspicious things got watched. Interesting things got taken apart.

---

The feast went on. Courses came and went. Theo danced twice, both times with women whose names slid off him like rain off glass, and between dances he drifted, mapping the hall the way he mapped everything now — exits first, then sightlines, then the rhythm of the guards' rotation. Six guards at the main doors, changing every quarter hour. Two at the servants' entrance. One, unexpectedly, beside a plain door behind the musicians' gallery that led down, judging by the draft, into the cellars.

Into the cellars, where a hollowed man lay in the dark, begging to be allowed to die.

Rosalind was coming. Her letters arrived through the dead — folded scraps of vellum that appeared in his rooms with no explanation, written in a hand that slanted left and pressed hard enough to emboss the page. *Hold on. I'm coming. Don't let it learn your face.* The last one had included a drawing: a small, fierce sketch of a woman with carving tools at her belt, done from memory by someone who had never seen her, described by someone who had. Sefa, presumably. The deserter. The dead courier had whispered her name before dissolving into the wall like smoke finding a crack.

The Feast of Hollow Lights. That was the plan. The whole court gathered, the king distracted by ceremony, Sefa opening a servant's door while the palace's attention burned on the hall above. Tomorrow night, Rosalind would cross the falsified maps with Sefa as her guide, and Theo would bring Aldric — the real one, the broken original rotting in a cellar — to meet her, and she would carve the parasite's name out of the world.

It was a good plan. It was even, in its way, a beautiful plan.

It assumed they had time.

The music stopped mid-phrase.

Not faded. Stopped, the way a neck stops. Four thousand candles flared white, and the entire hall turned — smoothly, simultaneously, four hundred heads rotating with a synchronization that made Theo's skin crawl — toward a man standing near the eastern columns.

Theo knew him vaguely. Lord Merrick? Master of the Granaries, grey-bearded, heavyset, the kind of courtier who had survived forty years of this reign by being furniture. He was holding a cup. His face was the color of tallow.

"Your Majesty," Merrick said, and his voice cracked in the silence like ice on a pond. "I — forgive me — I only meant to say that the harvest quotas, the southern quotas, my lord, the numbers do not — a man cannot reap what was never sown, and the villages in the south, they've forgotten how to plant, they've forgotten their own—"

He stopped. Because the king had raised one finger.

And the hall waited, and Theo waited, and some animal part of his brain screamed at him to look away, to study his cup, to be furniture — but he couldn't. He watched the way you watch a carriage accident: helplessly, completely.

"There it is," the king said gently. "Do you hear it, my lords? A doubt. Small as a seed. But seeds grow." He descended from the dais, unhurried, his footsteps silent on stone that should have rung. The court parted before him like water before a keel. "Lord Merrick. Forty years of faithful service. Do you know what I remember most about your service?"

Merrick's lips moved. No sound came out.

"Nothing," the king said, warmly. "I remember nothing, because there is nothing to remember. You have been the most forgettable of my servants, and tonight, at last, you will be rewarded for it."

He placed his palm flat against Merrick's forehead.

What happened next happened in absolute silence, which was the worst part. Theo had expected screaming. There was none. Merrick's eyes widened — not in pain, Theo realized, but in *recognition*, the way a man's eyes widen when he walks into his own house and finds a stranger sitting in his chair. Then the widening reversed. The eyes narrowed. The lines of his face smoothed. Theo watched sixty years of living drain out of a human face like water out of a pulled plug — the grief-lines first, then the laugh-lines, then the particular set of the jaw that comes from a lifetime of specific decisions, until what stood in Merrick's clothes was a tall, blank, breathing surface.

The court applauded.

They applauded *immediately*, without hesitation, four hundred pairs of hands coming together in a wave of sound that crashed against the walls, and Theo's hands rose with them because not rising was death, and he clapped, and clapped, and hated every single motion of his own traitorous palms. Beside him, Lady Isbet-or-Iseult dabbed at her eyes. "Beautiful," she whispered. "Every year, more beautiful."

The hollowed Merrick bowed to the king. Then he walked — steadily, obediently — to the edge of the hall and took up a post beside the door, where he stood at attention like a new soldier, empty-eyed, waiting for orders. A courtier made into a lamp-post. A man made into a socket.

"And so," the king said, turning back to his guests, spreading his arms, the picture of paternal warmth, "we remember what we are given. Sit. Eat. The night is long, and full of light."

The music resumed mid-phrase, exactly where it had stopped, as if the pause had been cut out of time with scissors.

---

Theo did not remember leaving the hall. He found himself afterward in a colonnade overlooking the inner gardens, where frost had silvered the hedges and the fountains ran with water that steamed faintly, unnaturally warm. His hands were shaking. He looked at them shaking and thought, distantly: *that's data too.*

Because here was what he now knew, assembled with a cartographer's cold precision:

One. The demonstration had been scheduled. Merrick's outburst had been too perfectly timed, his protest too conveniently doomed — a man does not survive forty years at a hollowing court and then blurt out grain statistics at the feast unless the script allows it. The parasite had wanted the court to see a hollowing tonight. Which meant the hollowing wasn't the message.

Two. The message was the guest. *We are given a guest.* The king had put Theo's usefulness on display in front of four hundred witnesses, had weighed him publicly, and then — hours later, after the wine, when defenses were soft — had shown everyone exactly what happened to things the crown found inconvenient. It was a sentence with Theo's name implied in every clause.

Three. The dead had told Rosalind the parasite's name. He didn't know how he knew this — he knew it the way he knew the weight of his own sketchbook, some certainty sunk below memory — but the letters had grown shorter lately, more urgent, and the last one had contained a single line he'd read forty times: *We have it. The true name. Keep him alive until I arrive.*

*Keep him alive.* Aldric. The man in the cellar, who begged to die.

And four — the coldest entry in the ledger — the parasite had begun asking him questions. Casual ones, over chess, three days ago: *Tell me, mapmaker, what lies beyond your valley? Are there others like you? Who taught you to draw?* Each question harmless alone. Together, a net. It was searching his history for the seam, the place where his story had been stitched together hastily, the false border on the map of himself.

It suspected him. It had perhaps always suspected him. Prophecies did not summon men across boundaries by accident; the book had named him a century before his birth, and the thing wearing the king had read that book, and had let him live anyway, and had fed him and clothed him and seated him at its feasts — because a prophecy fulfilled under supervision was a prophecy defanged.

Which meant the plan's timetable was already wrong. Rosalind would come tomorrow night, through Sefa's door, expecting a court drunk on festival and a king distracted by ritual. But the parasite had just spent this evening demonstrating, to Theo specifically, that it could unmake a man in front of four hundred applauding witnesses in less time than it takes to drink a toast.

Tomorrow night might already belong to it.

Behind him, in the colonnade's shadow, he heard footsteps — light, deliberate, the tread of someone who had learned to walk quietly in a palace that listened. Sefa emerged from an archway in servant's grey, hood back, her scarred jaw tight.

"You saw," she said. Not a question.

"I saw."

"Then you understand. It's not waiting for the feast to end." She glanced back toward the hall, where the applause had started again over something — another course, another toast, another small annihilation Theo would never know about. "It's waiting for *you*. My contact in the kitchens says the king ordered your chambers moved this afternoon. Closer to the royal wing. 'For the guest's comfort.'"

Comfort. The word sat in Theo's stomach like a swallowed stone.

"When does she arrive?" Sefa asked.

"Tomorrow. Midnight. Through the scullery door."

Sefa closed her eyes briefly, the way a gambler closes them before turning a card she already knows is bad. "Then we move tonight. Before they move you. Because once you're behind the royal wing's doors, mapmaker—"

From the hall, muffled by distance and stone, the orchestra struck up again — bright, relentless, celebratory — and beneath it, so faint he might have imagined it, Theo heard the sound of four hundred people applauding something that had not yet happened.

"—there won't be a door left to open," Sefa finished.

Theo looked down at his shaking hands, then stilled them. Somewhere beyond the mountains, past the falsified maps and the boundary stones, a woman with carving tools was walking toward him through the dark, carrying a name sharp enough to kill a king.

They would just have to make sure there was still a king left to kill.

"Tonight, then," Theo said. "Show me the door."
