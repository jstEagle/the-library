## 14. Sefa's Price

The fire had burned down to coals by the time Sefa finally spoke, and Rosalind did not rush her. That was the thing about bone-carvers, Sefa was learning — they knew how to wait. They spent their lives listening to the dead, and the dead were never in a hurry to be understood.

"I had a brother," Sefa said, to the fire rather than to Rosalind. "Tam. He was older by two years, which he never let me forget. He used to say I'd been born angry and just hadn't grown out of it yet." A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished. "He wasn't wrong."

The safehouse was an abandoned mill at the edge of a village whose name had been scraped from every signpost — one of the parasite's little erasures, tidy as a fingernail paring. The wheel had long since rotted; the millrace ran dry and silver in the moonlight. Somewhere beyond the hills, the palace of Ash and Salt glowed faintly against the dark like a coal that refused to die.

"He joined the King's Harvest when we were nineteen," Sefa went on. "Everyone did, or pretended to want to. It was that or the salt mines. Tam said it was honest work." She laughed, short and bitter. "Harvesting memory. Honest work. Like being a gravedigger is honest because somebody has to do it."

Rosalind sat cross-legged on the far side of the coals, her carving tools laid out on a square of cloth before her — awls and burins and a small curved knife Sefa tried not to look at too often. The woman had crossed the boundary of Hollowmere four days ago with nothing but those tools and a rib bone full of someone else's words, and she hadn't complained once, not even when the flood of the kingdom's dead hit her at the stones. Sefa had watched her stagger under it, both hands pressed over her ears, blood threading from one nostril — and then she'd straightened up and asked, in a voice gone hoarse, *which way to the road.*

"You don't have to tell me," Rosalind said now.

"Yeah, I do." Sefa turned her hands over, looked at the calluses there. Soldier's hands, harvester's hands. "Because tomorrow I'm going to open a door into the palace for you, and you should know exactly what kind of person is opening it."

* * *

The Harvest barracks stood outside the town of Vell, three days' march south, and Sefa had been proud of her uniform in a way that shamed her now. Grey wool, black braid, a badge of polished vertebrae worn at the collar. She had believed the stories they told in training: that memory-harvesting was mercy, that the villagers who gave up their recollections lived lighter afterward, unburdened. That the Crown took only what could be spared.

She had believed it right up until the morning Sergeant Ovist handed her the harvest slate with a name inked on it in the Crown's red.

*Tam Okoro-Vane. Relation: sibling.*

"They wouldn't," she'd said. Ovist hadn't even looked up from his ledger. "It's a loyalty test," he'd said. "Family first. Everyone does it eventually. You'll feel better after."

That was the part Sefa couldn't stop saying, all these months later, to anyone who would listen and most who wouldn't: *you'll feel better after.* As if the harvesting left the harvester clean. As if the memories didn't go somewhere. She'd learned since — from Rosalind, from the dead who whispered through the valley-woman's carved bones — exactly where they went. Into the hollow king's endless hunger. Fed to the parasite wearing Aldric's body the way a man feeds coins into a machine that never pays out.

"I stood in our mother's kitchen for an hour before he came down," Sefa said. Her voice had gone flat, the way it did when she talked about this; Rosalind had noticed she kept it level the way a soldier keeps a wall standing — not because it's strong, but because if it falls everything behind it comes down too. "He was making tea. He always made tea in the mornings, terrible tea, too strong. And I had the slate in my hand and the needle in its case and he turned around and smiled at me and said, 'You're up early.'"

Rosalind's hands had gone still over her tools. Outside, the dry millrace murmured over its stones.

"I told him it was an exercise. A drill. I told him to hold still, it would take a minute." Sefa's jaw worked. "And here's the thing — here's the thing I need you to understand, carver — he *believed* me. Because I was his sister. Because why would I lie? So he sat down and he held out his arm for the needle like I was going to draw blood for a physic, and he kept talking the whole time. About how he'd found work for me at the mill in Vell, so I could transfer out of the Harvest. About how he'd met someone. A girl who baked bread. He was going to ask me to stand at his wedding."

The coals ticked and settled.

"And then it was done, and he looked at me — and I will see that look until I die, carver. Not anger. Anger I could have carried. It was worse than that. He looked at me the way you look at a stranger in your house, and he said, 'I'm sorry — do I know you?'"

Sefa stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the water and the wind through the broken slats of the mill.

"I walked him back inside. I told our mother he'd had a fit, a sickness. Then I walked out of the barracks gate and kept walking, and I've been walking since. Eight months." She finally raised her eyes to Rosalind's. "Deserters get caught, they're harvested themselves. All of it, everything, down to their mother's face. So you should know: everything I am is sitting here talking to you, and tomorrow I'm handing it to the Crown on a platter."

* * *

Rosalind was quiet a while. When she spoke, her voice was gentle, but Sefa noticed she didn't offer comfort, not exactly. Bone-carvers, she was learning, dealt in truer currency than that.

"The dead tell me things about you," Rosalind said.

Sefa flinched. "Then they've got no manners."

"They say a deserter has been leaving bread on the steps of the almshouse in Vell for eight months. They say a grey-uniformed woman pulled three children out of a flooded well near Saltmarsh and left before anyone could thank her. They say—" Rosalind paused. "They say the harvested remember nothing, but the ones who loved them do. And Tam's girl — the baker — leaves a loaf on a windowsill every week for a man who doesn't know why she does it. And the dead say a woman in a deserting uniform watches that window from across the street, some nights, and cries."

"Stop it."

"She doesn't know you saved him from anything worse," Rosalind said quietly. "If you'd refused, they'd have sent another harvester. One who wouldn't have stayed an hour. One who wouldn't have let him finish his tea."

"I let him finish his tea," Sefa repeated, and her voice finally cracked down the middle like old plaster. "Gods. I let him have his *tea.*"

"Yes," Rosalind said. "And you've punished yourself for eight months. And tomorrow you can keep punishing yourself somewhere far away where it's safe. Or—" she picked up the rib bone from her cloth, the one bearing Theo's last letter, its carved lines catching the coal-light, "—you can spend yourself on something. There's a difference. The dead taught me that much. A life spent is worth more than a life preserved."

Sefa stared at the bone. At the cartographer's careful hand, letters scratched shallow and urgent: *the feast is in six days. she suspects me. come through the servants' postern on the north side, below the kitchens. I'll get the king to you if I can.*

"He says she suspects him," Rosalind said. "The parasite. Which means he has days, maybe hours. And I can't walk into that palace through the front gate."

"No," Sefa said. "You can't." She was silent for a long time, watching the coals. When she spoke again, something in her voice had changed — not lighter, but settled, the way a bone sets. "There's a postern door below the kitchens. North side. I know it because I harvested the woman who kept it. Old Maret. She kept the keys thirty years, and one day the Harvest came for her memories because she'd seen something she shouldn't in the cellars." Sefa's mouth twisted. "I was on that crew too. I stood in her kitchen and lied to her the same way I lied to Tam."

"What did she see?"

"Doesn't matter now. What matters is the door. It opens to a key kept in a lockbox in the scullery, and the lockbox opens to a word, and the word is *Maret*, because the palace staff are sentimental and stupid and nobody's changed it in thirty years." Sefa looked up. "The feast of Hollow Lights. The whole court will be upstairs drowning in candle-smoke and flattery. The kitchens will be short-handed because half the staff get pulled to serve. If a woman came to the scullery door looking for work — big feast, they hire extra hands off the street, they always do —"

"—she'd be inside," Rosalind finished.

"You'd be inside," Sefa agreed. "And I'd open the postern from the other side tonight, before the shift changes, so you have a way out again. Because hear me, carver, and hear me well: if they catch me doing it, they won't kill me. Killing's quick. They'll put the needle in my arm and take everything down to my name, and I'll spend the rest of my life as a smiling stranger who holds doors for the people who unmade me." She swallowed. "I know what that is. I did it to my brother. I'm not afraid of it happening to me. What I'm afraid of is dying with it still unpaid."

Rosalind reached across the coals and closed her hand over Sefa's wrist — the grip of it warm and hard, a carver's grip, a hand that shaped bone for a living.

"Then we pay it," she said.

* * *

They traveled the last miles before dawn, when the kingdom's dead were thinnest — Rosalind had learned their habits the way a fisherwoman learns tides. Even so, she walked bent under the weight of them, whispering voices braiding through her skull from every grave they passed: soldiers and scullery maids and nameless villagers, all of them tangled in the parasite's net, their voices borrowed, their rest deferred. Sefa watched her suffer it and offered her arm without being asked, and Rosalind took it, and neither of them commented on the fact that a heretic bone-carver and a deserter harvester made a strange pair of sisters-in-arms.

They crouched in the gorse above the palace's northern wall as the sky went the color of dirty pewter. Below them, Ash and Salt seethed with preparation — carts of candles, wagons of wine, banners hung from every tower showing the crowned spiral sigil that Sefa spat at on principle. And beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the palace hummed. Rosalind could feel it through the ground, through the bones of her own feet. Something vast in there was hungry, and getting hungrier.

"There." Sefa pointed to a squat archway half-hidden behind a woodpile at the wall's base, its door iron-banded and low. "Postern. Maret's door." She checked the sky, then the wall-walk, counting guards with a soldier's eye. "Shift changes at dawn bell. I go in through the servants' gate with the morning deliveries — I'm still on the rolls as a laborer, they never cleaned the ledger, the Crown's too busy erasing names to check them. I reach the scullery, I take Maret's key, I come down and open this door. Then I walk out again and vanish, and you never saw me."

"And if you're caught?"

Sefa was already moving down the slope, low and quick. She paused once, looking back, and in the pre-dawn grey her face was younger than Rosalind had ever seen it — stripped of the soldier, stripped of the deserter, down to something almost like peace.

"Then tell the dead my name's Sefa, and my brother was Tam," she said. "Tell them I paid."

Then she was gone, down through the gorse toward the walls of the palace, toward the door she might open and the death she might find on the other side of it.

Rosalind waited in the gorse as the sky brightened, her carving tools against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and counted the bells.

One. The kitchens woke. Smoke rose from the chimneys.

Two. Carts rolled through the main gate. Somewhere above, the parasite-king slept in a body that was not his own, and a cartographer with shredded memories was keeping it talking, keeping it looking anywhere but north.

Three—

The postern door swung inward, slow and silent, and a thin slice of lamplight fell across the frost.

Open.

Rosalind rose from the gorse, whispered a thanks to the dead for their company, and walked down to the door — through it, into the dark of the palace of Ash and Salt, where somewhere below the cellars a hollowed king waited to be remembered, and somewhere above a feast was being laid for a monster.

Behind her, the door clicked shut.

In front of her, the dark began to whisper.
