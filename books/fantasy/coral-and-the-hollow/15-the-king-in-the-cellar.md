## 15. The King in the Cellar

The servant's door was exactly where Sefa said it would be: behind the woodpile in the east court, a low arch of grey stone with a latch worn smooth by generations of frightened hands. Rosalind pressed her palm against it and felt the palace breathing — a slow, wet rhythm, like something enormous asleep beneath the flagstones.

*It knows you're here,* whispered the dead who clung to her like moths. *It has always known.*

"Then let it watch," she murmured, and went through.

Inside, the air changed. The kingdom beyond the boundary stones had hit her like a flood when she first crossed — every ghost within a hundred miles screaming their unfinished business into her skull at once — but here the noise was different. Organized. The dead of the palace did not wail. They stood at attention, ranks and ranks of them lining the corridors like lanterns that had forgotten how to shine, and all of them faced the same direction: down.

Everything in this place pointed down.

Rosalind moved through the servants' passages with Sefa's stolen livery hanging loose on her frame — a man's coat, a man's boots stuffed with rags, her hair bound up and ash-grey under a cap. She had crossed a country to get here. She had slept in ditches while the dead kept watch and walked through a plague village where the parasite's harvesters had left nothing but people with smooth, empty eyes who smiled at her too long. She had learned, from letters Theo smuggled out in the margins of maps, that the Feast of Hollow Lights would fill the palace above with music and applause and the smell of roasting meat, and that on such a night even a god might be too busy performing to count its prisoners.

She had not expected the cellars to be warm.

That was the first wrongness. Palaces were cold at their roots; everyone knew that. But these stairs descended into a heat like a sickroom, a heat with a smell underneath it — tallow, salt, and something sweetly rotten, like fruit left too long in a sealed room. Her candle guttered and righted itself. The dead who followed her stopped at the top of the stairs, every one of them, as if the threshold marked the edge of a leash.

"You cannot come," she told them softly.

*We never could,* they answered, in a chorus like wind through wheat. *No one comes back from where the king keeps himself.*

She went down alone.

* * *

The cellar was not a cellar. That became clear within twenty paces. It had been a cistern once — she could see the old waterline scored into the walls, the iron rings where buckets had hung — but someone had spent decades making it into something else. Shelves lined the curving stone, and the shelves held jars, hundreds of them, glass gone amber with age. She lifted her candle to the nearest and immediately wished she hadn't.

Memories. She knew them on sight now; she'd learned their look from the harvest-towns. A jar of golden light, coiled like a sleeping eel. Another, nearly drained, its glow the colour of weak tea. Each jar bore a label in a fine, patient hand: *name, date, quality.* One label read simply *mother, 40 years, excellent,* and Rosalind set it down with hands that would not quite obey her.

This was a pantry. This was where the thing wearing the king kept its food.

And past the shelves, at the centre of the round chamber, there was a bed.

Not a bed, really. A frame of black iron over a shallow basin, and straps of soft leather — soft, deliberately soft, the cruelty of a careful keeper — and on the frame lay a man.

He was naked except for a linen shift gone yellow with years. He was thin in the way that suggested feeding rather than fasting, kept alive on broth and purpose. His hair had been white once and was now the colour of old bone. And across his chest, his arms, his throat — carved.

Rosalind stopped breathing.

She knew carvings. She had made ten thousand of them, small and large, in the valley where no magic could follow and her gift was only a gift, only words given weight. She knew the difference between marks made in love and marks made in use. These were use. They covered him like a harness, lines and lines of script in no alphabet she recognized, cut deep and re-cut deeper, scar over scar over scar, the way a path is worn into a mountainside by the same feet travelling it for years.

A road. Someone had carved a road into this man's skin, and things travelled it.

"No," said the man on the frame.

His voice was a ruin, barely a breath, but she heard it clearly because in this room there was nothing else alive to hear. His eyes opened — pale, watery, focused on her with an effort that seemed to cost him everything he had.

"Not another one. Not — " he swallowed, throat working. "You've come for the king. Everyone comes for the king."

"I've come for Aldric," Rosalind said quietly.

Something moved behind his eyes. Not hope. Hope was too large a word for what remained in that face. Recognition, maybe — the flicker of a man hearing his own name spoken aloud for the first time in years.

"That name is dangerous," he whispered. "Say it again."

"Aldric."

He laughed. It came out as a cough, wet and thin. "They erased it. Every book, every bell, every gravestone. I watched them do it. I was still — I was still in the front row, so to speak." His gaze drifted to the ceiling, to some middle distance thirty years away. "There was a feast. There is always a feast. I remember the taste of the wine and then I remember waking here, and the thing wearing my face upstairs, learning my smile in the mirror."

Rosalind approached slowly, candle high. Up close she could see the freshest carving, raw pink along his collarbone, still weeping slightly. Maintenance work. A gardener pruning a trellis.

"I'm going to unbind you," she said.

And the man who had been a king looked at her with absolute, exhausted clarity and said: "No."

* * *

She should have expected it. In her line of work she met the dying every day, and the dying said *no* as often as they said *please*. But those were ordinary deaths, clean ones, the kind her knife could honor. This was different. This was a man asking her to leave him inside his own tomb.

"Why?" she asked, kneeling beside the frame. The leather straps creaked as he turned toward her. "Tell me why, and if your reason is better than mine, I'll listen."

"My reason is thirty years old," he said. "My reason is that I have tried this before." He lifted one hand — it trembled like a leaf, but he lifted it, and touched the collar of carved script at his own throat. "Do you know what happens when a carver cuts a binding out of living flesh? The flesh remembers. The road stays. You can dig the words out of my chest and the parasite will still have the shape of me, worn smooth and ready, like a glove. I am not a prisoner here, bone-carver. I am the spare."

"The spare," she repeated.

"It wears me when the current king fails. When the body upstairs grows damaged, or defiant, or merely boring." He smiled without any humour in it at all. "It wore me for eleven years before it found a younger vessel. Do you understand? It grew tired of me the way one grows tired of a coat. And it kept me anyway. Because a wise creature never throws away a coat that already fits."

Outside, faintly, muffled through many floors of stone, the Feast of Hollow Lights had begun. Music. A thousand voices rising together in what the programme called a hymn of gratitude. Rosalind thought of Theo somewhere in that glittering noise, half-hollowed, holding his mind together with cartography and stubbornness, waiting for her.

"I don't have time to argue," she said. "By morning, either you're free or we're both—"

"Then don't waste it arguing with me. Waste it running." He gripped her wrist. His grip was nothing, a bird's bones wrapped in skin, but he gripped with the whole desperate strength of conviction. "Listen to me. Thirty years ago a woman came down those stairs. A carver, like you. She was kind and she was brave and she loved a story about rescue. She cut into me for six hours. Do you know what I felt? Nothing. Do you know what the parasite felt, upstairs, mid-sentence at dinner?" His voice cracked. "*Everything I felt. It wore my pain like a crown. And then it came down here, and it thanked her, and it showed her the shelf with her husband's memory in it, and it asked her very politely whether she would like to hear his voice one more time.*"

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere above, the hymn swelled.

"She stayed three days," Aldric whispered. "Then she climbed those stairs and knelt, and it hollowed her gently, gently, the way you'd stroke a cat. Her husband's jar is still on that shelf. Third rank, fourth row. It likes souvenirs."

Rosalind looked at the shelves. Hundreds of jars. She found herself scanning them despite herself — looking for nothing, finding everything, understanding at last the true architecture of this room. Not a prison. A larder and a trophy case and a warning, all at once, and the warning was written in the patient handwriting of a thing that had never once needed to hurry.

"I'm not her," Rosalind said.

"No," Aldric agreed. "You're kinder. That's worse."

* * *

She sat down on the cold floor beside his bed, which surprised them both. Above them the feast thundered on — a toast now, glasses ringing like tiny bells, and beneath the bells, if you knew how to listen, the thin screaming of whatever poor soul had been chosen for tonight's demonstration.

"I carve messages from the dead," she told him. "That's my gift. Only that. Words, last words, the things people couldn't say while they breathed. In the valley where I was born, magic can't cross the stones, so my whole life my gift has been — quiet. A whisper through wool." She turned her hands over, palms up, the knife calluses plain. "I came here and it's like being deaf my entire life and walking into a cathedral organ mid-note. I hear everything. Every ghost in this kingdom is screaming, and do you know what most of them scream about?"

He watched her, wary.

"Being forgotten," she said. "Being unwritten. There are ghosts down here who can't remember their children's faces because the parasite ate the memory out of the living who loved them, and grief needs two ends of a rope to hold. So no." She shook her head. "I won't leave you on this frame. Not because I'm kind. Because leaving you is exactly what it expects kindness to do."

For a long moment the only sound was the feast, and the drip of water somewhere in the dark, and the man's ragged breathing.

"You speak like someone who has lost something recently," Aldric said at last.

"I have a friend in this building. He's up there in the light, keeping a monster talking. He used to be able to draw a map of anywhere he'd never been." Her voice caught on *used to*, and she hated it, and pushed through. "The thing took pieces of him. If I fail, it takes the rest. So you see, Your Majesty, mercy isn't on the table tonight. Neither of us gets the version where I walk away."

"Then you'll kill me," he said flatly. "If you can't free me, kill me. My heart is under the oldest carving, here—" he tapped his sternum, where the script ran thickest, dense as chainmail, "—and I promise you the thing will feel every second of it. Ruin its feast. At least let my death be the loudest thing in the palace tonight."

"That's not—"

"That is the only gift anyone has offered me in thirty years," he said, and his voice finally broke open, and Rosalind saw the boy he must have been, crowned young, carved young, a coat put on by something ancient and patient and never once taken off. "Please. I have begged before. I begged it, in the early years, and it enjoyed that. I am begging you, and I don't even know if you're real, or if this is another dream it feeds me to keep the meat fresh. But if you are real — bone-carver, whoever you are — do not save me. Save yourself. Save whoever's upstairs keeping it busy. Let me be the distraction. I have practice."

Rosalind reached out and took his hand.

It was ice and paper. He flinched — actual human touch was clearly a currency he hadn't handled in decades — and then, slowly, his fingers curled around hers with a grip like a drowning man.

"I'm going to tell you a secret," she said, "and then I'm going to start cutting, and you can hate me the whole time."

"What secret?"

"The woman who came before me. She failed because she tried to *remove* the carving. Cut it out, dig it up, tear the road out of the mountain." She drew her knife — bone-handled, valley-steel, the blade she had carried across a country — and laid it against the lamplight. "But I've spent the last month reading the parasite's own handwriting. It's arrogant. It signs its work. And anything that has a signature has a name, and anything that has a name—"

"—can be carved," Aldric finished. His breath had gone very shallow. "Carved *out*."

"Carved *under*," she corrected. "Every road has bedrock. I'm not going to remove what it wrote on you, Your Majesty. I'm going to write something older, and deeper, and truer, directly beneath it — and when the two words meet, one of them is going to lose."

Above them, the hymn ended. In the sudden silence, both of them heard it — footsteps on the cellar stairs. Unhurried. Deliberate. Descending one step at a time with the patience of a creature that had never needed to hurry in three hundred years.

Aldric's eyes went wide. "It's coming to feed."

Rosalind blew out the candle.

In the dark, she found his sternum by touch alone, and set her blade against the oldest scar, and began.
