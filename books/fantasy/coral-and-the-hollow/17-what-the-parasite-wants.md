## 17. What the Parasite Wants

The dead of the kingdom did not whisper. That was the first cruelty Rosalind had to unlearn.

In Hollowmere, the dead murmured the way rain murmurs — a background hush she had lived beside so long she sometimes forgot it was there. Here, the dead were *organized*. They stood in rows along the roads at night, invisible to the living, their mouths moving in unison like a choir rehearsing in an empty church. Reporting. The parasite heard through them the way a spider hears through its web, every tremor delivered to the center.

Sefa could not see them. Few living people could. But Rosalind walked out of the deserter camp on the third night with her carving tools against her ribs and her breath fogging in the salt-cold air, and the dead turned their heads to watch her pass — hundreds of them, farmers and soldiers and children, their faces gray as paper — and she understood that she was walking into the parasite's own nervous system wearing the one gift it had reason to fear.

"Be careful," Sefa had said at the camp's edge, pressing a soldier's coat into her hands. It smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's life. "If they take you—"

"They won't."

"That's what my brother said." Sefa's jaw worked. "Come back anyway."

Rosalind had come back every night since. Not to fight — she was one woman with a knife meant for dead bone, not living flesh — but to *listen*. Because the enslaved dead could not help talking to her. She was a bone-carver. To the dead, a carver's hands were a bell tower, and even bound souls leaned toward the sound.

* * *

She found her informant by accident, or perhaps the accident found her, because nothing in this kingdom happened without intention for long.

The eastern field beyond the village of Marrow's Rest had been a battlefield once — she could tell from the way the earth was stitched with old bone, shallow graves never properly closed. The harvest soldiers came here on certain nights, gathering. And here the dead were thickest, standing ankle-deep in their own forgotten resting places, mouths moving, eyes empty.

Except one.

He was young — younger than Theo, older than Rosalind — and he stood apart from the rows, and when Rosalind crossed the fence line his mouth stopped moving. His eyes, which had been fixed on some middle distance where the parasite's will presumably lived, dragged slowly around and settled on her hands.

"You're one of hers," he said. His voice was the dry rustle of pages. "One of the carver's."

"I'm not hers." Rosalind kept her voice low, though she'd learned the dead heard her regardless, the way the deaf feel thunder. "I'm the opposite of hers, if I've understood anything at all."

"There is no opposite." He looked down at his own translucent hands. "There is only the carver, and what the carver holds. I'm Tam. I was a conscript. I died at the ford at Sel two winters ago, and I have been standing in this field ever since, saying the words she makes us say." His gray face did something that was almost a smile. "But you rang, carver. When you came over the hill, every soul in this field felt it. Like sunlight through a crack. She hasn't noticed yet because she's busy — she's *hungry* lately, do you understand? Feeding takes all her attention. But she'll notice. She always notices light."

Rosalind crouched in the frost-killed grass so their eyes were level, which was absurd, since he was dead and she was not, but courtesy mattered; her mother had taught her that courtesy mattered even to things past needing it.

"I need to know her true name," Rosalind said.

Tam went very still, in the way only the dead can be still, absence of even a heartbeat's tremor.

"Do you know what happens," he said quietly, "to a soul that speaks her name aloud?"

"No."

"Neither does anyone else. That's rather the point." He drifted a half-step closer, and up close Rosalind could see the seams on him — faint lines, like scar tissue, running across his ghost-flesh where the parasite's binding gripped. "But I'll tell you what I know, carver, because you rang like sunlight and I have been standing in this field for two years wanting very badly to be disobedient."

* * *

Tam did not know the name. What he knew was older, and stranger, and it came to him not from his own memory but from the chorus itself — because the enslaved dead shared the parasite's hearing, and some of them, the oldest ones, remembered fragments she would prefer forgotten.

"She was born before the kingdom," Tam said. They walked now, or he drifted and Rosalind walked, along the field's edge where the boundary oaks grew crooked from whatever had soaked into the soil. "Before Aldric's line, before the salt throne, before any crown you'd recognize. There are souls in the chorus who remember the first hollowing — not Aldric. A king so old his name isn't written anymore anywhere except inside us, and we're not allowed to say it." He glanced sideways at her. "You want the shape of the story first. I can hear it in you. You want to know how a thing becomes what she is."

"Yes," Rosalind said. Her breath hung white in the air. "I want the shape."

"She was a bone-carver."

The words landed in Rosalind's chest like a dropped stone.

"Not like you," Tam added, watching her face. "Not exactly. In those days the carving was different — cruder, or purer, depending who tells it. Carvers didn't send messages. They made records. They carved a life into bone so the dead couldn't be lost. Whole lives. Names, deeds, the faces of children, the taste of a favorite meal. A good carving could hold a soul steady after death, keep it from fraying. The carvers were shepherds." His mouth twisted. "And she was the best of them. That's what the old ones say. She wasn't a failed student or a bitter mediocrity. She was *brilliant*. She could carve a man's whole self into a knucklebone, everything he was, and hold it."

"What happened to her?"

"Dying happened to her." Tam said it flatly, the way one says *weather happened*. "She fell ill — or was wounded; the versions disagree — but she knew she was dying, and she was the finest carver who ever lived, and she could not bear it. So she did the thing every carver knows is the one forbidden thing. She carved her own soul. Out of her own dying body. Into living bone." He paused at the foot of a crooked oak. "A king's bone."

Rosalind stopped walking.

"The king was dying too," Tam went on, softer now, almost gentle. "Old. Childless. Afraid. She was his carver, his confidante — some say more, but the dead gossip and I don't trust it. She offered him immortality and took it for herself in the same stroke: her soul carved into his living marrow, riding him past death. Only it didn't work the way she planned. Souls aren't ink, carver. You can't carve yourself into something without being changed by the carving. The king's body rejected her and absorbed her by turns. She wore him, and he wore her, and somewhere in the wearing, both of them frayed. The king dissolved first. She didn't. She just... needed a new vessel. And then another. And another. Every generation, a new king. Every hollowing cleaner than the last, because she learned. She's been *practicing*, carver, for longer than this kingdom has existed. Aldric is simply her current coat."

The field tilted, very slightly, though Rosalind knew it was the world tilting and not the ground.

Because she knew this. Not the story — but the *grammar* of it. Her gift had rules, taught to her by her grandmother in a valley where magic pooled thin as winter milk: *a carving binds what it names. Bone remembers. The deeper the cut, the truer the binding.* And the deepest cut of all, the one her grandmother had refused even to describe, saying only, *never carve the living, child, never carve a living soul into bone, because what goes in cannot come out whole* —

The parasite had done exactly that. To herself. To escape death.

And Rosalind, in nine days, intended to do the same thing in reverse — to carve a true name into living bone and unbind a soul — and had known, in some cold cellar of herself, that she was reaching for the same forbidden instrument, turned the other way.

"She's me," Rosalind said. Her voice sounded far away. "That's what you're telling me. She's what I would be if—"

"No." Tam's answer came fast and hard, and for a moment his gray face was fierce. "Don't do that. Don't do what the living always do with monsters — make them a mirror and flatter yourselves with the reflection. She had your gift. She did not have your valley. She did not have grandmothers who forbade. She did not choose, every day, in small ways, not to reach for the forbidden thing." He drifted closer, and his dead eyes held something that in a living face she would have called kindness. "You are not her because you could be her. Everyone could be everyone. You are not her because you haven't been."

Rosalind laughed — a short, wet sound, unexpected even to herself. "You argue like the dead in Hollowmere. My grandmother's sister. She'd have liked you."

"Take me with you someday," Tam said, and then his face shuttered, because somewhere in the middle distance the chorus had shifted — the rows of standing dead in the field behind them had all turned their heads at once, westward, mouths working faster.

"She's feeding," Tam whispered. All his fierceness drained into urgency. "Tonight. A village. That means she'll be deep in it for hours, drinking memory — that's your window, carver, that's when she's blindest—" He grabbed at her sleeve, his fingers passing through the cloth with a cold like deep well water. "But listen. Listen, because this is why I brought you out here instead of just shouting the story at your campfire. The old ones remember a fragment. About her name."

Rosalind went still.

"It isn't spoken anywhere," Tam said. "Not in records — she burned those centuries ago. Not in mouths — she kills the speakers. But the carvers of the old days had a practice, do you understand? Before a master died, her fellows would carve her true name into her own sternum, so that death could be *witnessed* — so that somewhere in the world, the fact of her was recorded. She was a carver. Her fellows carved her name into her bone before she fled into the king." His grip tightened, or tried to. "And carvings survive, carver. Bone survives. Somewhere under this kingdom, in the oldest tomb of the oldest line of kings — the first king, the one she wore first — there is a sternum with her name cut into it. The chorus doesn't know where. But the archive might. Your mapmaker has been in the archive, hasn't he? We see him. The dead see everything in that palace. We watch him move through it at night, careful as water, and we cannot call out to warn him because our mouths belong to her."

The cold that went through Rosalind then had nothing to do with the season.

"Theo," she said. "Is he safe? Is he—"

"He's clever. Cleverer than she credits — she thinks cartographers are servants, instruments, men who draw the world as they're told." Something flickered across Tam's face — the ghost of grim humor. "But she has begun to wonder about him. The courtier she hollowed at the feast — she pulled his last hours out of him, and he had seen your mapmaker's face too often in wrong corridors. She is patient, but she is not *inattentive*. If she decides to open him up and drink him, carver, she will find everything. Your letters. Your name. The plan. And then she will simply stop wearing Aldric and go put on someone no one has thought to rescue."

* * *

Rosalind walked back through the sleeping village with Tam's words arranged and rearranged in her mind like bones on a carving table.

The name was in a tomb. The name was *carved already* — she did not have to find it through the enslaved dead's broken chorus, did not have to bargain with half-remembered ghosts. It existed, cut into old bone by hands that had loved the monster before she was a monster. All she needed was the location of the oldest royal tomb.

And Theo was in the archive. Theo, who copied things. Who had already found Aldric's half-erased name by doing exactly the kind of patient, meticulous, invisible work that cartographers did and kings' parasites overlooked.

At the camp, Sefa was awake, sitting up by the banked fire with her sword across her knees, and she read Rosalind's face before Rosalind got a word out.

"It went badly."

"It went *well*," Rosalind said, and sat down heavily across from her, and told her all of it — the field, Tam, the origin, the tomb. Sefa listened the way soldiers listened, without blinking, and when Rosalind finished she was quiet a long moment.

"A bone-carver," Sefa said finally. "She started as a bone-carver."

"Yes."

"And you're going to carve her name into Aldric's living bone and rip her out."

"That's the plan."

Sefa poked the fire until it spat. "Then you're going to do the same act she did. Carve into living bone. Bind a soul with a cutting." Her eyes came up, dark and direct. "I'm not saying don't. I'm asking if you've thought about what it costs, since apparently everything in this country costs."

Rosalind thought about Tam's fierce dead eyes. *You are not her because you haven't been.*

"I've thought about it," she said. "Every hour since I crossed the stones. The difference isn't the knife, Sefa. The difference is whose name is on it. Hers cuts to keep. Mine cuts to let go." She looked into the fire. "At least — I believe it does. I'll know better when I'm holding it."

Sefa snorted — the closest thing to a laugh Rosalind had gotten from her yet. Then her expression changed. She cocked her head, listening to something beyond the firelight, and her hand found her sword hilt.

"Horses," she said. "Two. Coming fast. That's either a patrol—"

It was not a patrol. It was the dead courier Rosalind had sent toward the palace three nights ago, riding a borrowed horse with the unconvincing vigor of the recently dead, and slung across his saddlebow, wrapped in oilcloth, was a familiar leather satchel — and beside it, a rib bone with fresh carving, the letters cut in a hand Rosalind had seen only once, in a tavern, drawing maps on napkins:

*Found Aldric's name. Found the tomb's location in the archive — she keeps a record of every vessel, going back to the first. The tomb is beneath the palace; the Feast of Hollow Lights opens the undercroft for the Rite of Witness. She suspects me. I don't know how many days I have. Come at the feast. Bring the name. Whatever happens to me — carve her out of him. — T.V.*

Rosalind read it twice. Three times. Then she folded the bone carefully into the coat Sefa had given her and looked up at the dead courier, who sat his horse watching her with the patient blankness of the recently arrived.

"Tell him—" she began, and stopped, because there was no time to carve a reply and the feast was close and the parasite's net was tightening around a man who was spending his memories to buy her a door.

Instead she took out her tools right there by the fire, took a rib from her satchel, and began to carve with quick sure strokes while Sefa saddled the horses: *Coming. Keep him talking. Keep yourself alive. Both orders are equal.*

When she finished, she pressed the bone into the courier's hands, and he turned his horse, and Rosalind watched him ride back toward the palace — toward the web, toward the thing that had once been a woman who couldn't bear to die.

*We could be sisters,* Rosalind thought, and made herself look at the thought squarely, the way you look at a wound to judge how deep it runs. *She was brilliant and afraid and alone, and she reached for the forbidden thing, and eight hundred years later she drinks villages.*

*I am brilliant and afraid and alone, and I am going to walk into her house and use her own first sin against her.*

The horses stamped. Sefa swung up into the saddle. Above the eastern hills, the sky was beginning to bruise toward dawn, and somewhere in that dawn-light distance a palace waited with a cellar full of a king who wanted to die, a mapmaker spending himself piece by piece, and a monster who had once been exactly what Rosalind was.

"Which way?" Sefa asked.

"Toward the tomb," Rosalind said, and swung up behind her. "We're going to need a older bone than I brought."
