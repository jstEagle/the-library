## 18. The Rescue That Costs a Name

### Sefa

The door was nothing. That was the joke of it, Sefa thought as she stood in the servants' corridor with a lamp guttering in her hand. All that planning, all those nights lying awake rehearsing her own execution — and it came down to a door no wider than a coffin, tucked behind the wine racks where no one had looked in forty years. Iron-banded oak, swollen with cellar damp. A lock she could open with a hairpin and a prayer.

Her brother's face kept arriving uninvited. Not his living face — his face afterward, slack and pleasant, the harvest-scars at his temples pink as new coral, calling her *sister* in a voice with nothing behind it but obedience.

*This is for you, Darien,* she thought, and turned the pin.

The lock surrendered with a click so small it embarrassed her. Behind it: stairs going down into the dark under the palace, breathing cold air that smelled of salt and old copper. The cellars. Where the king kept himself.

She heard footsteps above — the feast still roaring two floors up, three hundred courtiers applauding a man who had just been emptied in front of them like a jug. She heard, beneath that, the other sound she had learned to dread: the whisper of the dead who served the parasite, drifting through the stone like water through a cracked wall.

Sefa lifted the lamp, set her jaw the way she had before every inspection of her life, and went down to meet the heretic.

* * *

### Rosalind

The stairs ended in a room that had been a chapel once. She could tell by the alcove shapes in the walls, the empty niches where saints or ancestors had stood; now there was only a table, an iron ring bolted to the floor, and on the ring, a man.

He was chained by wrist and ankle and throat, laid out like something being stored rather than punished. He was thin in the way of furniture, of things used until their shape was only function. His chest rose and fell. That was the worst of it. She had expected a corpse, a relic, a horror. She had not expected him to be simply *alive*, breathing in the dark for years, and no one to know.

The dead crowded the room thick as moths. Outside Hollowmere they were never quiet, and here, in the parasite's seat, they were worse than loud — they were *watching*. Their voices braided through her skull: *the carver, the carver has come, tell it, tell it—*

"No you won't," Rosalind said aloud, and drew her tools from her satchel. "I've salted this room's bones. You'll have to carry your gossip up the stairs like everyone else."

It was half a bluff. It would buy minutes, not hours. But the whispers thinned, sulking, and she knelt by the man on the floor.

"King Aldric," she said.

His eyes opened. They were brown, ordinary, human — nothing like the vacant ballrooms she'd seen upstairs in the thing wearing his face. They took a long time to find her, traveling past her twice before landing.

"You're real," he said. His voice was rust. "She sends real ones sometimes. To punish me. You'll go. They always go."

"I'm not one of hers." Rosalind set her chisel against his wrist-iron, then stopped. Bone-carving worked on bone. But everything she'd read in the archive letters said the binding lived in him too — the parasite anchored itself in its vessel's skeleton, and the anchor could be scored. Weakened. Not yet broken — that was the altar, the true name, the price — but loosened enough that the thing upstairs might feel its grip slip.

"I'm going to carve you," she said. "Just a line. Two lines. It will hurt."

Aldric laughed. There was almost nothing left of it, but what remained was terrible and clean. "Everything hurts," he said. "At least this will be mine."

She carved. One stroke along his radius, shallow, precise, a counter-line to the parasite's anchoring script she could feel humming under his skin like a second pulse. He made no sound. When she finished, his eyes were wet and clearer than they had been.

"There's a version of me upstairs," he whispered. "Wearing my face. Wearing it badly." His gaze sharpened, sudden and awful. "There's a boy here too. Cartographer. It's been drinking him. Nights when it drags me toward him I can feel him holding on — holding onto—" Aldric frowned, groping. "A name. Her name. He keeps saying it like a rope."

Rosalind's hands went still over her tools.

*Rosalind.*

"Yes," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "That's mine."

* * *

### Theo

He was losing rooms.

Not the palace rooms — those he knew now with a terrible intimacy, having spent weeks a prisoner-guest among them. He was losing rooms from his own life. He'd reached for his mother's kitchen last night and found a hole shaped like it, edges smooth, as if the memory had been surgically excised. This morning he'd tried to remember the sea and gotten only the word *sea*, like a label on an empty drawer.

But some things stayed. A smell of beeswax and bone dust. A woman's laugh, low, startled out of her as if she didn't authorize it. Hands — scarred, sure, small — moving over pale material. And a name, which he now guarded the way a drowning man guards air, saying it silently on a schedule, rationing himself, because the parasite had noticed the rationing and asked him about it twice.

*You keep a word behind your teeth,* it had said, wearing Aldric's smile across the breakfast table. *I could take it gently. Or I could wait and see if it keeps you.*

Now there were boots in the corridor outside his cell, and they weren't the usual pattern. Too few. Too fast.

Theo pressed against the wall. The lock didn't break so much as *give* — someone on the other side knew its temper — and the door opened on a woman in a deserters' grey coat, knife out, lamp held high. Behind her, another woman, dark-skinned, travel-worn, carrying a satchel that clinked faintly, and looking around the cell with the expression of someone reading a page.

Reading. Assessing. Like a carver assessing material.

Theo's heart did something complicated. He knew her. He did not know her.

"Rosalind," he said — and heard how it came out, not a recognition but a guess, the name offered up like a coin to a fountain.

She crossed the cell in three strides and took his face in her scarred hands, and the smell of her — beeswax, woodsmoke, something like rain on chalk — hit him so hard his knees nearly folded. Not memory. Deeper than memory. His body remembered what his mind had lost.

"It ate it," she said quietly. It wasn't a question. Her thumbs traced his temples, finding the hollows where the feeding showed, and her jaw tightened. "It ate it out of you."

"Some of it," Theo managed. "I kept the name. You kept—"

"Nothing." She said it flatly, refusing pity, refusing to let him spend time on it. "I carved it away myself. Deliberately. So don't apologize, and don't grieve, and don't look at me like that, Theo Vance, because we have approximately four minutes before this corridor fills with soldiers and you are going to get us out of this palace using maps that lie."

Behind her, the deserter shifted. "Three minutes," Sefa said. "I tripped a ward on the way down."

* * *

### Rosalind

Getting Aldric up the stairs was like moving a shipwreck. He weighed nothing and everything; his body had forgotten how to be carried and how to carry itself. At the top of the cellar stairs Sefa paused at a grate, listening, then led them left through a passage that smelled of tallow and old grease — the servants' spine of the palace, running parallel to the grand halls like a river's dull twin.

They reached the map room. Sefa barred the door behind them while Rosalind lowered Aldric into a chair and Theo went straight to the great table in the center of the room, where the kingdom lay under glass.

She watched him lean over it, and she saw the change come over him. Since the boundary stones, since the letters, she had built a picture of Theo Vance from his words alone — the careful jokes, the cartographer's habit of turning everything into terrain. Now she watched the real thing work. His fingers spread flat on the glass. His eyes moved in long slow sweeps, horizon to horizon, and his frown wasn't confusion. It was offense.

"It's wrong," he said.

"Everyone says the palace maps are beautiful," Sefa said.

"They are beautiful. They're also wrong." Theo tapped the glass. "This corridor bends east here. On paper it runs straight. This stair — it doesn't connect to the north wing anymore, it was sealed, but the map still shows it because the map isn't meant to *navigate*, it's meant to say *everything is known, everything is fixed, nothing escapes us.*" He looked up at Rosalind, and for a moment the fever-brightness in him was exactly the man from the tavern, mid-sentence, sketching a palace on a napkin. "A falsified map tells you the truth constantly, by accident. Every lie has a shape, and the shape points at what they're hiding. Give me ten minutes."

"We may not have ten minutes," Sefa said from the door.

"Then give me six and I'll love you forever."

Rosalind pulled the glass cover off the table herself. "Six," she said. "Carve."

While he worked — pulling parchment scraps from his coat, redrawing the palace in fast confident strokes, correcting, annotating, muttering *there, and there* — Rosalind knelt again beside Aldric's chair. The king's eyes tracked Theo with an expression she couldn't parse until he spoke.

"He redraws it wrong first," Aldric rasped. "On purpose. Then fixes it. Our mapmakers did that when I was young. Show the master you can fail safely before you show him you can succeed." A pause. "I remember being young. I'm not sure whose remembering it is."

"It's yours," Rosalind said firmly. "It's yours, and tonight we're taking it back."

From the door, Sefa said quietly, "Torches in the east courtyard. They've found the cellar door."

* * *

### Theo

He led them through the palace's lies like a man walking home in the dark.

Left at the sealed stair the map insisted existed — it didn't, so instead they took the coal chute passage its absence implied. Down the servants' spine. Through a laundry where Sefa silenced a startled washerwoman with a look and a finger to her lips. Twice they froze in alcoves while patrols passed, close enough that Theo could hear the parasite's dead whispering overhead in the rafters, agitated, seeking — and twice Rosalind put her hand flat against the wood and murmured something in a language that made the whispers flinch and slide away.

"You can do that?" he breathed.

"Grandmother's trick. The dead here are slaves. Slaves can be reminded they were people." Her mouth was grim. "Reminded isn't freed. That's for the altar."

They reached the west gatehouse at moonrise. Beyond it, according to Theo's stolen-and-corrected map, lay the terraced gardens, then the aqueduct path, then the hills — three days of bad country between them and the border forest where Sefa promised sympathies.

The gatehouse was the problem. It was the choke point; the map's lie about it — showing a secondary postern that had been bricked over a decade ago — was precisely the kind of comfortable falsehood the parasite loved. Theo stood in the shadow of the arch, parchment in hand, and did the arithmetic he hated: either they fought through the main gate, or they climbed the aqueduct with a dying king and no rope.

"The aqueduct," he said. "There's a maintenance walk under the channel. Narrow. The dead won't like it — running water confuses their whispers."

"You're certain?" Sefa said.

"No," Theo admitted. "But the map is certain the postern exists, which means whoever drew it wanted people to stop asking questions about the west wall. Questions stop where the truth starts. So the truth starts here."

He was already moving when the pain hit.

It came up through the base of his skull like a hook finding purchase. He staggered; the parchment fell; Rosalind caught him by the elbow and he heard her gasp, because under the skin of his forearm, faint as watermark, lines were surfacing — thin silver script, the parasite's anchoring marks, blooming across his flesh the way they bloomed across Aldric's.

"It knows," Theo gritted. "It's reeling me in. I'm — I'm on its line, Rosalind, and it's pulling."

"How long?"

He tried to calculate it and found, with distant horror, that part of what the hook was taking right now was mathematics. Numbers slid from him like fish. "Hours," he said. "Maybe. Go."

"No," Rosalind said.

"*Yes*," Theo snapped, and gripped her shoulders, and for a heartbeat they stood chest to chest under the gatehouse arch with the whole rigged kingdom around them, and he looked down into the face of the woman whose name he had defended tooth and nail inside his own dissolving mind, and said, softly, "You gave up your memories of me once, to save a stranger-king and break a monster. Let me give up the rest of me to buy you the road. It's not the same sacrifice. It's smaller. But it's mine."

"That's not how this ends," Rosalind said. Her voice shook. Her hands didn't; they were already pulling fresh bone from her satchel, a small curved piece, her chisel flashing. "Because I know something the parasite doesn't. Names carved in bone can be *re-carved*. The dead told me. Whatever it takes from you tonight — hold the shape of the hole, Theo. Hold the outline. I'll fill it. Now move."

And Theo Vance, half-hollowed, hook in his skull, numbers draining out of him like sand — Theo Vance laughed. Startled out of him, low, as if he hadn't authorized it.

They ran for the aqueduct, four shadows under a lying moon, and behind them, from the highest tower of the palace, the hollow king's borrowed voice rose over the walls — not shouting, worse than shouting: *singing*, a sound like a door opening at the bottom of the world, as the thing that wore Aldric came down off its throne to hunt.
