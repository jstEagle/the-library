## 19. Carving the True Name

The bone altar had been a throne once.

Rosalind could see the shape of it beneath the corruption — the high-backed seat where a king of the old blood had sat in judgment, its armrests worn smooth by generations of honest hands. Now it was white as a tooth and twice as long, grown up through the flagstones of the great hall like a tusk from the jaw of the earth, and every inch of it was carved.

Not with names. She had learned the difference in the cellars, in the archive, in Sefa's shaking voice. These were *unmakings* — thousands of them, spiraling up the altar's length, each one a name scraped out of the world. She could feel them the way she felt the dead: a pressure, a chorus, a hunger with no mouth.

"Stop," Theo said behind her, and his voice was wrong — frayed at the edges, like a rope dragged too far over stone. "Rosalind. *Stop.*"

She didn't stop. There was no time to stop.

They had come through the servant's door with Sefa's blood still drying on the latch, through corridors that lied about their own lengths, down the falsified map Theo had redrawn from memory with hands that no longer fully belonged to him. They had the king — Aldric, the true Aldric, half-carried between them, his original body light as kindling and cold as a cellar wall. And they had made it exactly as far as the altar before the doors at both ends of the hall swung open, unhurried, as though the building itself had decided to let them in and then decide not to let them out.

Soldiers lined the walls. Not the memory-harvesters with their iron needles — these carried no tools at all. They didn't need them. The dead came with them, drifting up through the floor in their hundreds, and Rosalind felt every one of them like needles in her own skull. The parasite's voice was in all of them at once, and it did not shout. It never shouted. It simply filled the room the way cold fills a cellar.

*You brought me my king,* it said through a hundred gray mouths. *How thoughtful.*

Aldric stirred in Theo's grip. "Don't listen," the old man rasped. His real voice, thin as paper but *his*. "Carver. Whatever it offers. Whatever it says. Don't listen."

"I'm not listening," Rosalind said. "I'm working."

She knelt at the base of the altar and opened her satchel.

* * *

The tools came out in the order she had laid them that morning, in Sefa's safehouse, by candlelight, when she had finally admitted to herself what the price might be. The chisel her mother had ground from a femur. The mallet of blackthorn. The fine needle-blade she used for names, because names were small and names were everything.

The dead of Hollowmere had taught her the craft in whispers, one letter at a time, when she was a girl who thought the voices in the boundary stones were just wind. They had taught her that every soul has a name older than the one its mother gave it — a true name, the first word the world spoke when it made you — and that bone remembers what flesh forgets. Carve a dying woman's last words into her rib, and the words would outlive her. Carve a soul's true name into living bone, and the soul would be *bound*. Or unbound.

The parasite had been a bone-carver once. That was the horror of it, the thing Rosalind had learned through the enslaved dead in the long terrible night before the feast: a carver, centuries ago, who had grown old and afraid, and who had solved the problem of death the only way a carver could — by carving her own soul into a king's living bone and wearing him from the inside. King after king after king. Each one hollowed, each one replaced, each one's true name erased from every record so that no one could ever carve the parasite *out* again.

But records lie badly, and archives lie worse, and Theo — Theo, who could not stop mapping, who had copied a half-erased name onto the flyleaf of his sketchbook because a scholar cannot bear a gap — had found it.

She had it now, written in his careful cartographer's hand on a torn page she had pressed flat against her knee: *ALDRIC VESS, called the Winter King, born beneath the yew at Hollow Ford.* And beneath it, in the parasite's own ancient hand, scraped nearly to nothing but not quite: the true name. The first word. The one the world had spoken when it made the thing that was now wearing a kingdom like a coat.

*What are you doing?* The voice came from everywhere. The soldiers had not moved. The dead had not moved. But the temperature of the hall dropped, and the torches leaned sideways, and Rosalind understood that the parasite had finally understood.

She set the chisel against Aldric's forearm — the original body, the one in the cellars, the vessel it could re-wear — and found the place where the bone ran closest to the skin.

*That body is nothing,* the parasite said. A soldier's mouth moved; the words came out in a voice like a door in an empty house. *I have worn him for forty years. I have a hundred vessels. I am in the walls of this palace, carver. I am in the stones. Kill this flesh and I will be in the next king before his crown has cooled.*

"Then I'll carve the next one," Rosalind said. "And the next. I've got time. I've got tools." She looked up, and let the parasite see her face, and saw — with a satisfaction that frightened her — the dead flinch. "You were a carver. You know I don't bluff. You know what it costs. And you know I'll pay it."

*You don't know what it will cost.*

"I know exactly what it will cost."

* * *

Because the craft had a law, and the law was this: a carving takes from the carver in proportion to what the carving does. A dying woman's last words cost an hour of sleep. A binding cost a season of color drained from the world. And an unbinding — tearing a soul that had rooted itself in living bone for forty years, tearing it out by its true name — that would take the dearest thing she had.

She had known since the cellar. She had known when she read the name in Theo's handwriting and felt the craft stir in her chest like a living thing, weighing her, pricing her.

It wanted him. Not him — her memories of him. The first date and the awkward silence and the map of a palace he'd never seen, drawn in a tavern while she fell in love with the way he held a pen. His name in a century-old book. The sketchbook's last page with her own name on it. The letters, carved in rib bone, carried back and forth across the dark by the dead — *hold on, I'm coming* — the whole architecture of them, built from nothing, from borrowed voices, from two people who had somehow fallen in love across a boundary and a kingdom and a war.

The craft wanted all of it. Every letter. Every hour. The price of unmaking a name was the carver's own dearest name — and Theo Vance had become, without her permission and entirely with her consent, the truest name she had.

*Rosalind.* The parasite's voice had changed. It was softer now, and that was worse. It came from the dead closest to her, from an old woman in a harvest-smock who had probably been someone's grandmother, and the parasite made the dead woman's face gentle. *I can give them back. The memories. All of them, after. I can wear any face you wish. I can put him beside you, whole, and you would never know the difference. You would have your love and your craft and your long life. Carvers understand each other, you and I. We are the only two of our kind left in the world.*

And that — *we are the only two* — was the cruelest cut of all, because it was true, and because some small starving part of Rosalind had been alone in a valley where magic could not follow for thirty years, and knew exactly what it was like to be the only one.

"I know," she said quietly. "That's how I know how this ends for you."

She raised the mallet.

* * *

The first letter of the true name went into Aldric's forearm like a key into a lock.

The hall screamed. Not the soldiers — the *hall*, the stones, the air, the dead, all of it shrieking in one voice as the parasite felt the blade of its own oldest craft turned against it. Soldiers broke ranks and ran for her and Sefa met them with a deserter's desperate fury and a kitchen knife, screaming words Rosalind didn't hear, because there was no room in her for anything but the work.

The second letter. The third.

Each stroke cost her. She felt it going — not pain, exactly, but *subtraction*. The tavern date went first, and she felt it leave: the nervous way Theo had stirred his tea, the map of the palace taking shape on his napkin, gone, gone, and she carved through the grief of losing it because the grief was the toll and the toll was the proof she was doing it right. Then the sketchbook. Then his voice saying her name. Then — and this was the stroke that made her weep without stopping her hands, because a carver's hands never stop, that was the first law her mother taught her — then his face.

She held onto one thing. She didn't know if she was allowed to, but she did it anyway, greedily, secretly: she held onto the *fact* of him, the bare knowledge, the way you can know a country exists after you've forgotten its maps. *His name is Theo Vance. He is kind. He came back for me with his mind full of holes. I love him.* She said it over and over like a prayer as the craft ate everything around it, the harvest of her own heart, and the parasite screamed and the dead wailed and Aldric's body shuddered under her chisel as something ancient began, letter by letter, to lose its grip on his bones.

*Stop,* the parasite said, and for the first time in four hundred years, it sounded afraid. *Carver. Sister. Stop.*

"No," Rosalind said, and her voice was steady, and she was crying, and her hands did not shake. "You had four hundred years of kings. He had four letters. We're not the same."

The last letter of the true name was a spiral, the oldest sign, the sign that means *a soul, turning, returning to itself.* She carved it into the bone over Aldric's heart, where the parasite had rooted deepest, and she put her whole self into the stroke — every year of loneliness in the valley, every whisper from the dead, every letter carried across the dark — and she struck it home.

The world went white.

* * *

When her sight came back, she was on the floor of the great hall and the bone altar was cracking down its whole length like river ice in spring, and out of the crack — out of the king's own body, out of forty years of worn flesh — something was coming *out*. It had no shape anyone could bear to look at for long. It was a carver's soul gone wrong, all appetite and no hands, and it clawed at the air for purchase and found none, because its name was written now in living bone and living bone does not forget.

Aldric Vess opened his eyes.

They were his own eyes. Hollow, stunned, forty years late, but his. He looked at his hands — the original hands, the cellar hands — and then, impossibly, at the strong hands of the body that had carried him up from the dark, and the two of them, vessel and soul, folded into each other like water finding its level, and the old man on the floor began to laugh and weep in the same broken breath.

The dead were leaving. Rosalind felt them go, hundreds of them, the parasite's grip slipping off their souls like a fist unclenching, and as they went they *thanked her* — not in words, in warmth, in a tide of released voices washing through her like sunrise. The old woman in the harvest-smock paused beside her on her way to whatever comes next, and looked at her with her own eyes at last, and mouthed something that might have been *grandmother's blessing*, and was gone.

The soldiers stood in a stunned ring, weapons hanging, watching their king remember himself.

And Rosalind lay on the cold flagstones and did the arithmetic of what she had lost, and found the wound where it should have been, and could not feel its shape.

She knew the facts. She had kept the facts, hoarded them like coins in a burned house. *His name is Theo Vance. He is kind. I love him.* But when she turned her head to look for him — and she turned it without deciding to, the way a compass turns — she found a tall man on his knees near the shattered altar, holding a torn sketchbook page he seemed to have no reason to be holding, looking at her with an expression she had no memory of ever seeing before and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a house you've never been in from a dream.

"Rosalind?" he said. His voice was frayed rope. "I — you're —" He stopped. She watched him reach for something and find the shelf empty. She watched him do the same terrible arithmetic she had just done.

"It's all right," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle, because she didn't know this man, and she loved him, and both of those were true, and only one of them was *hers*. "Your name is Theo Vance. You're a cartographer. You came back for me."

He stared at her. "Did I?" he whispered. "Did I know you?"

The dead were almost all gone now. But one lingered — the old courier who had carried her letters, the first of them, the rib bone with *hold on, I'm coming* carved in her own hand — and as he faded he looked between the two of them with the soft, knowing sorrow of the dead, and his voice brushed her ear like a closing door:

*Some names, carver, once carved, can be carved again.*

Rosalind Okonkwo knelt on the floor of a falling kingdom, holding her chisel, holding nothing else, and looked at the stranger she had paid everything for.

"Yes," she said. "And I'm going to find out how."
