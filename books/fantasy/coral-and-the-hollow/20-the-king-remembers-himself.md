## 20. The King Remembers Himself

*Theo*

The name went into the bone like a blade into ice.

He heard it before he saw it — not with his ears but with whatever was left of him, the shredded country of his mind feeling a tremor run through its last borders. Rosalind knelt at the palace altar, her carving tools flashing in the light of the burning feast-hall above, and each stroke she cut into Aldric's forearm sang through the stone floor, through the walls, through the thousand dead pressed against the palace like moths against a window.

And the thing inside the king screamed.

It was not a human sound. It was the sound of a house collapsing, of a library burning, of every stolen voice crying out at once. The parasite's true name — *Vessh-Karath-Who-Wore-The-Crown*, or something older than words, something that hurt to perceive — unspooled from Aldric's bone in letters of raised scar, and where it unwound, the hollow places began to fill.

Theo could not stand. He was propped against the altar's base, one hand pressed to his own temple, where the parasite had been feeding. His memories were a map with half the names scraped away. He knew there had been a valley called Hollowmere. He knew he was a cartographer, because his hands kept making small, precise movements, sketching invisible coastlines against his knee. He knew he had refused a bargain, though he could no longer remember what the bargain had offered him.

And he knew — the way you know a room is missing its furniture — that someone was gone from him.

"Rosalind," he said, because the name was carved into him deeper than anything else, a groove worn smooth by repetition even now, even after the eating. "Rosalind."

She didn't look up. Her hands were steady. Her face was wet, and she was smiling, and both of those things were terrible to see.

The king convulsed beneath her tools.

*Aldric*

There was a cellar in him. He had lived there for eleven years.

He had not known it was a cellar until this moment. For eleven years he had believed himself to be a man — walking, speaking, signing decrees, smiling at his court with a mouth that moved when someone else willed it. He had been a passenger in his own skull, pressed flat against the inner walls like a flower in a book, watching through eyes that were not his as the thing wearing him harvested his kingdom one memory at a time.

Now the book was opening.

The name being carved into his arm was a key turning in a lock he had forgotten existed. With every stroke of the carver's blade, Aldric felt rooms open inside himself — dusty, dark, untouched — and memory came pouring back like water into dry channels.

*Maren.* His wife, dead nine years before the parasite came. Her laugh, low and crooked. The garden she'd planted on the east wall, thyme and coral bells.

*The coronation.* Rain on the canopy. His father's hand heavy on his shoulder. The weight of the crown — real weight, honest weight, the kind you carry rather than the kind that carries you off.

*The night it came.* A beggar at the gate during the famine winter. He had brought the man in himself, given him soup with his own hands, because his mother had taught him that a king eats last but never lets another go hungry. And the beggar had looked up at him with eyes like empty rooms, and smiled with a smile that did not belong to any face, and said: *Thank you. You are very comfortable.*

Eleven years of screaming that no one heard, because his mouth belonged to the tenant now.

The carver's blade bit deep, and Aldric screamed — with his own throat, for the first time in over a decade, his voice cracked and rusted and *his*.

The thing inside him fought. Of course it fought. He felt it scrabbling for purchase in the hollows it had made of him, pouring itself into every empty space, whispering in the voices it had collected — his chamberlain, his daughter's old nurse, a soldier whose name he'd never learned — *stop her, stop her, we were comfortable, WE WERE COMFORTABLE—*

But a name is not a whisper. A name is a hook in the world, and once it is carved, it can be pulled.

The carver pulled.

*Theo*

Light poured out of the king.

Not metaphor. Theo watched, shielding his eyes, as Aldric's body blazed from within — a white-gold radiance streaming from his mouth, his eyes, the carved letters themselves, and in that light Theo saw shapes. Thousands of them. Faces, some ancient, some recent, soldiers and courtiers and villagers and children, all the dead the parasite had enslaved, all the voices it had worn as its surveillance web. They poured out of the king like smoke reversing, like a held breath finally released, spiraling upward through the ruined ceiling toward a sky that had been gray for eleven years.

The dead were leaving. The dead were *free*.

One of them paused beside Theo. An old woman, translucent as breath on glass, her face lined and kind. She looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse — recognition, and sorrow, and something almost like gratitude — and reached out a hand that passed through his hair like cold water.

"He loves you still," she said. "Even scraped thin. It's in what's left. Like a word under erased writing."

Then she was gone, rising, and Theo sat with the sentence lodged in his chest like a splinter.

*Loves who?* he wanted to call after her. *Who loves—*

But the woman was sky-bound now, one among thousands, and the light was fading to ordinary torch-glow, and Rosalind was lowering her tools, and the king was breathing.

*Aldric*

Silence.

For the first time in eleven years, silence inside his own head. No tenant. No whispers. No stranger's smile tugging his mouth into shapes. Just the vast, ringing emptiness of a house after the intruder has fled, every door standing open, every room his own again — if a room can be your own after someone else has lived in it so long.

He lay on the altar stones and breathed. Each breath tasted of iron and ash and, unbelievably, of morning. Somewhere above, the Feast of Hollow Lights was still burning, but the fire sounded different now. Freer. As if the whole palace had exhaled.

Hands helped him sit up. The carver — Rosalind, the deserter Sefa had called her, the heretic, the bone-witch — steadied his shoulder with a grip that was gentle and absolutely unyielding, the grip of someone accustomed to holding things together while she worked on them.

"King Aldric," she said. Not a question. She'd read his name in the archive, he supposed, in the records that had failed to erase him completely. "Can you hear me? Are you—" She hesitated, searching for the word. "You?"

Aldric opened his mouth to answer and found he did not know how to say yes. Yes required knowing who had asked. Yes required a self solid enough to affirm itself, and his self was a city after a siege — still standing, but smoking, every street needing to be walked and named again.

"I am," he said at last, and his voice was a ruin, but it was a ruin *he recognized*. "I am. Whoever that is."

"That's more than most of us can say," said the carver, and something in her tone told him she meant it personally, that she too had paid a price tonight that would take years to itemize.

Behind her, slumped against the altar base, was the cartographer. Vance. The mapmaker from the prophecy, the one the boundary stones had stolen across a century and a valley to deliver. He was staring at nothing. His eyes moved constantly — tracking, mapping, cataloguing — but they did not settle on anything the way eyes are supposed to settle on things. On people. On faces.

On her face, specifically. Aldric saw it plainly, the way a man who has spent eleven years watching someone else use his body becomes a connoisseur of the difference between looking and seeing.

The cartographer looked at Rosalind Okonkwo the way you look at a beautiful landscape in a country you've never visited. Interested. Drawn. And utterly without recognition.

*Theo*

She was crying and laughing at the same time, and he wished — with a desperation that alarmed him, that seemed to come from somewhere outside his damaged mind, from some cellar of himself he couldn't find the door to — he wished he knew why.

"It worked," she was saying. She had turned from the king to him, kneeling in the ash, taking his hands in hers. Her palms were slick with blood — the king's, presumably — and her fingers were trembling now that the work was done, now that steadiness was no longer required. "It worked, it actually — you're going to be all right, we're both going to be all right, I got us out, I got—"

She stopped. Because she had seen his face. He felt her see it — felt the exact moment her hope met the truth of him and broke against it, quietly, the way a wave breaks against a seawall, all that force dispersed into spray and silence.

"Theo?" she said.

"Yes," he said carefully. "I'm — that's me. Theo Vance." He tested the name like a bridge that might not hold his weight. It held. Barely. "Cartographer. I think. My hands seem to know things my head doesn't."

Something crossed her face then that he had no shelf in his memory to file it on. Grief, yes, he knew grief the way a burned man knows fire — the parasite had seen to that, had eaten whole years of him in the cellars while he screamed refusals it pretended not to hear. But this was a specific grief, a pointed one, aimed at him from four feet away by a woman with blood on her hands and carving tools at her belt.

"Do you know me?" she asked. Her voice was very controlled. Control like that costs, he thought. He could see what it cost.

"You're Rosalind," he said. "I know that much. It's — it's carved in somewhere deep. Everything else is..." He made a gesture, vague, cartographic. "Blank spaces. Here be dragons. I have a hole in me shaped like the last few months, maybe more, and I think—" His voice caught on something. He didn't know what. That was the worst part, the catching, the grief arriving with no address attached, no return label. "I think you were in it. The hole. I think you were in the part of me that's gone."

Her breath went out of her slowly, like air leaving a lung she'd been holding for hours.

"What do you remember?" she whispered.

He cast about in the wreckage. It was humiliating, honestly inventorying yourself in front of a stranger and finding mostly absence. "A valley," he said. "Stones. Boundary stones, moss-covered, in a ring. There was fog. There was a tavern — I remember a tavern, bad wine, good bread. I remember drawing maps of places I'd never been, and you watching me draw them, and—" He stopped. Something flickered. A laugh, low and startled, in a smoky room. Whose laugh? *Hers.* He knew it was hers the way he knew his own handwriting, and he could not for the life of him summon why he knew that, or what the laugh had been for, or what she'd looked like making it. "I remember you laughing," he finished, helplessly. "That's all. One laugh. It won't come with a picture."

Rosalind Okonkwo put her hand over her mouth.

Above them, through the broken ceiling, the freed dead were still rising — a slow river of pale lights ascending into the dawn-gray sky, and around the palace, faintly, the kingdom was waking. Bells. Real bells, rung by living hands, ragged and joyful and off-tempo. Somewhere a crowd had begun to sing, badly.

Victory, Theo thought. This was what victory looked like. Ash and bells and a woman crying behind her hand, and him sitting in the wreckage of his own mind unable to remember her favorite color, her middle name, whether she took sugar, whether she had ever — whether they had ever—

The blankness ached. That was the thing no one told you about losing memories: the loss itself was painless. It was the *shape* of the loss that hurt, the negative space pressing on everything around it, tender as a bruise. He kept reaching for something and finding the reaching interrupted, a stairway ending in air, and every time, some animal part of him flinched with grief for a person he could not name.

"You should hate me," Rosalind said suddenly, dropping her hand. Her eyes were fierce through the tears. "Or — no. You shouldn't, you couldn't possibly understand enough to, but. The price of the carving. It takes the dearest thing you have. That's how it works, that's the law of it. And mine was—" Her voice fractured and reassembled. "Mine was you. All of you. Every hour. I knew the price when I picked up the knife. I carved anyway. So if you want to be angry—"

"Why would I be angry?" Theo said, genuinely puzzled. "You saved a king. You freed — look up, look what you freed." He gestured at the ascending river of souls. "Whatever it cost you, you paid it for them."

"Not for them." She laughed — and there it was again, that low startled sound, and his chest seized with a homesickness so sharp it made him dizzy, grief for a moment he'd apparently lived and lost, a laugh he'd apparently earned. "Not only for them," she corrected. "For you too. Mostly for you. And now you don't—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Now you don't even know what I saved."

*Aldric*

He watched them, and he understood, because he was an expert now in the economy of hollowed things.

The parasite had taught him more than he'd ever wanted to know about what it means to lose yourself. Eleven years he had been present for his own life without owning it; eleven years his wife's grave had been tended by hands that weren't his, his daughter's letters (before she stopped writing) answered in a stranger's voice. He had come back to himself tonight and found the house intact but the furniture rearranged, the paint faded, the gardens grown wild. Recovery, he understood, was not a door you walked through. It was a corridor you walked down, slowly, room by room, naming things back into yours.

These two would walk their corridors separately, he thought. That was the tragedy of it — she remembered everything, and he remembered nothing, and between them lay a country neither could enter alone.

He got up off the altar. His legs shook. Eleven years of being worn had left his body a borrowed coat, ill-fitting, but it was his, and he stood in it, and he faced his rescuers.

"Bone-carver," he said. His voice was gaining texture by the minute, like a map filling in with detail. "Rosalind Okonkwo. I don't yet remember how to be a king — that will take time, and counsel, and probably several strong drinks. But I remember how to recognize a debt." He inclined his head, deep, formal, the motion rusty but correct. "My kingdom owes you everything. Whatever you ask of me, whatever you need — healers for the cartographer's mind, scholars, archives, time, passage home — it is yours. All of it."

Rosalind rose. She looked at Aldric for a long moment — at the hollow-eyed man standing straighter than his body should have allowed — and then, unexpectedly, she bowed back.

"He'll need you," she said, nodding at Theo without quite looking at him. Theo felt the omission like weather. "His memories. Some of what the parasite ate might still exist — the dead carried everything it stole, and the dead are free now. They might give it back. It's worth trying. I'll stay as long as it takes to try."

"And after?" Aldric asked gently.

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. Beyond the shattered wall, the sun was coming up properly now, the first honest dawn this kingdom had seen in eleven years, gilding the ash, catching the ascending lights of the dead as they thinned into the brightening sky.

"After," she said, "I'm going home to Hollowmere. And I'm going to carve the story of us into bone, every word of it, and I'm going to find out if a thing written on the soul can be written twice."

*Theo*

Later — after the healers came, after Sefa returned from her door with ash in her hair and a survivor's stunned expression, after the king fell asleep sitting upright in a chair like a man setting down a weight he'd carried for a decade — Theo found a piece of charcoal and the back of a torn banner, and did the only thing his hands still trusted themselves to do.

He drew a map.

It was rough, unfinished, the coastlines speculative. But he knew the shape of it without knowing why: a valley ringed in stones, a tavern with bad wine, a workshop smelling of bone dust, a figure standing at a bench with her head bent over her work.

He couldn't draw her face. He tried three times. The lines wouldn't come.

So instead he wrote at the bottom of the map, in careful cartographer's lettering, the only coordinates he had:

*Here lives a woman with carving tools and a laugh I can almost hear. I am going to earn it again.*

He didn't know yet that she was already planning the same journey from the other direction. He didn't know that the dead, free now and grateful, were already carrying whispers between them like thread — that some names, once carved, refuse to stay buried.

He only knew that the blank space in him had a shape, and the shape was her, and that he intended, with the stubbornness of a man who draws coastlines no one has ever seen, to fill it in.
