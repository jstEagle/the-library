## 22. Coral and the Hollow

The boundary stones had not moved. That was the first thing Rosalind noticed, and it undid her more than she expected — that a thing could stay exactly where you left it while everything on either side of it changed shape.

She stood at the edge of Hollowmere in the gold rot of late autumn, her pack heavy on her shoulders, and let the valley look at her. Mist lay along the fields like a sheet drawn up to the chin. The stones rose around the pass, seven of them, gray and patient, older than any name anyone could remember giving them. When she was a child she had touched one and felt nothing at all, which had frightened her less than it should have. Now she knew what lived on the other side of that nothing: everything.

The dead came out to meet her before she took her first step down.

They did that here, always had — drifting up from the churchyard, from the drowned lane beneath the millpond, from the barrow under the orchard where nobody planted anymore. In the kingdom beyond the stones they had been a surveillance network, a scream, a leash. Here they were just neighbors. Muffled neighbors. Even now, even after everything, crossing back into the valley was like going half-deaf; their voices thinned to a murmur against glass.

*You came back,* whispered old Marta, who had been dead sixty years and gossiped like she'd never stopped.

*I came home,* Rosalind answered, though she wasn't sure the words made it through. She patted the strap of her pack instead, where the journal rode against her spine. *And I brought something.*

The dead pressed close, curious, their cold attention like snowfall. They could feel what she carried. Not the journal itself — paper meant little to them — but what was written in it, and what she carried besides, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, wrapped again in wool, wrapped a third time in the last strip of her mother's shawl.

Coral bone. A piece of it, red as a wound, taken from the reef-shrine where the sea gave up its dead things and made them beautiful. She had spent four months finding it. Four months in Aldric's kingdom, in a country learning to say its own name again, teaching Sefa's people what a bone-carver's oath really meant, sitting across from a man whose eyes kept catching on her like a hook in cloth — a man who did not know why.

She started down into the valley.

Hollowmere hadn't changed, because Hollowmere never changed. That was its whole nature, its whole mercy and its whole cruelty: magic could not follow you past the stones, and neither, apparently, could time, or grief, or anything else with teeth. The tavern where Theo Vance had vanished from his chair mid-sentence still stood on the square with its crooked sign. The archive still leaned against the hillside like an old drunk leaning on a wall. Her workshop still smelled of tallow and dust, and when she pushed the door open, her tools lay exactly where she'd left them — laid out neat as surgery, waiting for hands that had been gone almost a year.

She set her pack down and did not cry, which surprised her. She'd assumed she would.

*He isn't far,* the dead murmured from the corners of the room, from the seams of the floorboards. *He took the cottage by the mill. He walks past your door every third day. He pretends he is sketching.*

Rosalind laughed — a rough, cracked sound, the first in this room in nearly a year. *Of course he does.*

*He remembers nothing,* said Marta gently. *But he comes anyway. That is a kind of remembering, girl. Perhaps the truest kind.*

* * *

She found him three days later, or arranged to be found — she was honest enough with herself to know the difference. She was carrying a basket of bread down the mill path when he came around the bend with a leather folio under his arm, pencil smudges on his fingers, and stopped as if he'd walked into glass.

Theo Vance. Thinner than she remembered. A scar over his eyebrow she didn't recognize, from the feast or the flight or the parasite's slow feeding — she tried not to guess. His eyes were the same and not the same: there were rooms in them now that had been emptied and refilled badly, furniture stacked by someone who had only ever seen a drawing of the original.

"Sorry," he said. "I— excuse me." And then, helplessly, because some part of him had never been hollowed and never would be: "Have we met?"

"Yes," Rosalind said.

He waited. She watched him wait, watched him search his shredded memory for her the way a cartographer searches a coastline for a feature he is certain the charts have left off. It hurt. It hurt in the exact place the carving had taken its price from, and she found she could bear it, because bearing it was the point. You paid in the dearest coin you had or the magic didn't take.

"We haven't," he said finally, uncertainly.

"Not yet," she said, and offered him the bread. "I'm Rosalind. I carve messages for the dead. Would you like to see something I've been working on?"

* * *

That evening, in her workshop, with the lamp turned low and the dead crowded thick along the walls — they loved a story, the dead, they had so few new ones — she unwrapped the coral bone.

It was beautiful. She had known it would be, but knowing had not prepared her. Red as embers under water, veined white where the sea had written its own history into it, dense and warm-seeking in her palms. Coral bone was the rarest medium a carver could work: it held a carving longer than stone, longer than memory, longer, the old stories said, than death itself. Her mother had spoken of it the way priests speak of relics. There was enough here for one story. One story, carved forever.

"What is it?" Theo asked. He sat where she'd pointed him, in the client's chair by the bench, and he couldn't stop looking at it. Of course he couldn't. He was a mapmaker; the bone was covered in lines that went somewhere. "It looks like a coastline."

"It's a book," Rosalind said. "Our book. Yours and mine."

"I don't—"

"I know." She picked up her finest graver, the one her mother had ground thin as a reed. "Let me read it to you anyway."

And she began to carve, and as she carved, she spoke.

She carved him a tavern first, small in the curve of the bone, two chairs and a candle between them — and told him about a blind date that had gone awkwardly well, a cartographer who drew maps of places he'd never been, a woman who recognized a palace in his sketchbook because she'd once carved its likeness from a dead woman's eye. She carved the archive next, and a book dated 1897, and his own name written in it a century before he was born. She carved the boundary stones, and a chair standing empty, mid-sentence.

Theo had gone very still. "I dream about that," he whispered. "The tavern. I dream I'm talking and then I'm somewhere else and everyone is wearing armor made of teeth."

"You aren't dreaming it," Rosalind said. "You're remembering it wrong-side-out. Keep listening."

She carved the kingdom of ash and salt. She carved a false map lying like a painted face over a true one, and a man whose instincts could read the truth underneath. She carved letters — dozens of them, tiny as insect wings, traveling both directions through the dark: carved ribs carrying her voice to him, stolen paper carrying his back to her. *Hold on. I'm coming.* She carved a deserter named Sefa, tall and bitter and brave, opening a servant's door with her own death warrant folded in her pocket. She carved a king in a cellar begging to be allowed to die, and a scholar in an archive copying a half-erased name with shaking hands, and a feast where applause was the most terrible sound in the world.

The lamp burned low. The dead had gone silent, which they never did.

And then she carved the altar, and the cornered woman before it, and the graver in her hand, and the name — the parasite's true name, the name of a bone-carver who had carved her own soul into a king rather than die — and here Rosalind paused, because the next part of the story was the part she had crossed the world twice to be able to tell honestly.

"The carving demanded a price," she said. "It always takes the dearest thing. For me, that was—" Her voice caught. She looked at him, this man who had walked past her door every third day pretending to sketch, and made herself say it plainly. "It was you. Every memory of you. The date. The letters. Your laugh, which I had only just learned. The way you hold your pencil too tight. All of it, gone, in the space between one stroke and the next. I carved your name into that bone—" she tapped the coral, where his name now gleamed in the deep red, "—and I could not have told a stranger who you were."

Theo's hand had risen halfway to his chest without his permission. "I dreamed that too," he said, barely audible. "A woman carving. Blood-red light. And then waking up and grieving something, someone, and not knowing what I'd lost. Only that I'd lost it." He looked at her across the lamplight, and his eyes were full, and behind the emptiness the parasite had left she saw the whole stubborn architecture of him, rebuilt from nothing, still facing the right direction. "I've been drawing you," he said. "For months. Every town I map, there's a woman in the margin. Carving tools. I thought I was mad."

"You're not mad," Rosalind said. "You're the other half of this." She lifted the coral bone into the light and turned it slowly, and he saw it entire: the whole story spiraling around the curve of it, every scene, every letter, every name — hers, his, Sefa's, Aldric's, even the monster's, carved and thereby bound — ending where it ended, in a workshop in a valley that forgot magic, with a lamp burning low.

"The dead taught me something, in the years I listened to them," she said softly. "Names are not memories. Memories live in the head, and heads can be emptied. But a name, once carved, can be carved again. That's what they whisper. That's the whole of their gospel, and it's short, and it's true."

*Can be carved again,* echoed the dead along the walls, a hundred voices soft as ash falling. *Carved again. Again.*

"So I can't give you back what was taken," Rosalind finished. "Neither of us gets that. But I can tell you the story. As many times as it takes. And you can choose it — choose me — the way I chose you before I ever met you, when I read your name in a book a century old."

For a long moment Theo said nothing. Then he laughed — wet, broken, real — and wiped his face with the heel of his hand, leaving a fresh pencil smudge on his cheekbone, and she thought: *there it is. There's the laugh I paid everything for. Worth it. Worth all of it.*

"Dinner," he said.

Rosalind blinked. "What?"

"Dinner." He was smiling, that crooked, terrified, hopeful smile she had carved into immortal bone on their very first scene, because she had known even then it was the most important line of the story. "I'm given to understand we did this once already and I ruined it by being prophesied at. So. Would you have dinner with me? Properly. Tomorrow night. I'll bring the sketches — the ones of you — and you can tell me if I got the carving tools right." He hesitated, and his voice dropped, gentled. "As if for the first time. Which I suppose it is."

The dead sighed along the walls like wind through winter reeds. Somewhere beyond the window, the boundary stones stood in the dark, keeping magic out, keeping nothing else from entering — not maps, not journals, not stories, not love chosen deliberately over love remembered.

"Yes," Rosalind said. She set down the graver and folded her hands over the coral bone, over their whole history, complete and permanent and only beginning. "Yes. But I should warn you — I'm told I talk to the dead, and they are absolutely going to chaperone."

Theo laughed again, and outside, soft as snow beginning to fall, the dead began — for the first time in a hundred years, the oldest among them swore it — to sing.
