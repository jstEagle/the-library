## 1. The Valley That Forgets

Hollowmere is the kind of place that apologizes for existing.

It sits in a bowl of green hills like an egg in a spoon, and everything about it — the crooked lanes, the low slate roofs, the way conversation drops to a murmur whenever anyone mentions the ridge — suggests a town holding its breath. We are famous for three things: wool, apples, and forgetting. The wool is coarse but honest. The apples make a cider that strips paint and grief in equal measure. And the forgetting is so thorough that most people here can't tell you what's on the other side of the ridgeline, only that you don't go there.

I've lived my whole life in this apology of a valley, twenty-six years of it, and I have never once seen the stars.

Let me be clear about that, because outsiders never believe it. There *are* stars — everyone knows there are stars; the almanac says so, the priest says so, old Marta says she saw them once as a girl before her family moved inside the wards. But from Hollowmere, looking up at night, you get a sky like a lid. A smooth grey-dark ceiling with no seams. The elders call it the Quiet. They say it fondly, the way you'd praise a good dog. *The Quiet keeps us safe. The Quiet keeps us whole.* Nobody asks what a ceiling is for if not to keep something in.

Or out. Depends who you ask, and nobody asks.

I carve bone for a living, which sounds more sinister than it is. Combs, buttons, knife handles, little birds for children's cribs. My mother taught me, and her mother taught her, all the way back through a line of Almeidas who apparently never did anything interesting enough to be remembered, because when I asked about our history I got shrugs and one story about a great-great-aunt who could cure warts by glaring at them. The valley remembers nothing about us. Sometimes I think the valley would forget my funeral halfway through the eulogy, provided the eulogy was boring enough.

The bone work is good work. Patient work. You hold a piece of sheep femur or whale rib against the wheel and you find out what's hiding inside it, the shape that was waiting all along, and there's a satisfaction in that I've never found anywhere else. People trust a carver. They hand you their dead grandmother's knitting needle and ask for a memorial ring, and they don't blink when you take it into your back room alone.

Which is fortunate. Because of the whispering.

* * *

Here's the thing I've never told a living soul: bone talks.

Not all of it. Most bone is just bone, dense and dumb as a brick. But every so often a piece comes through my hands that hums under the graver — a vibration too fine to hear, felt instead in the wrist, behind the eyes — and if I lean close, if I let myself lean close, it whispers.

The first time was a cow knuckle, when I was nine. It said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole: *cold down here.* I dropped it in the scrap bucket and didn't sleep for a week. The second time was a deer antler, and it sang half a lullaby in a language I didn't know, over and over, patient as water wearing stone. By the fourth or fifth time I'd worked out the pattern: the older the bone, the louder the voice. Fresh bone from Marta's spring slaughter was silent as scripture. But the ancient things — the mammoth tusk fragment some trader once swapped for a hat, the fossil vertebrae the river coughs up after floods — those had *people* in them. Or echoes of people. Or something that remembered being people, which amounts to the same heartbreak at three in the morning.

So I learned two trades. The first, I sell at market. The second, I practice alone, door barred, lamp hooded, listening to the dead mutter their fragments — a name, a date, half a prayer, always fragments, like overhearing strangers through a wall — and carving them small comforts. Little doors. Little beds. Once, a tiny carved chair, because the voice in that particular rib had spent forty years repeating *I stood, I stood, I stood*, and it seemed rude not to offer a seat.

Did it help? I genuinely don't know. The voices never said thank you. They never said anything *to* me, exactly; they said things near me, the way rain falls near a man under an awning. But when I finished a piece and set it aside, the whispering in that bone went quiet, and quiet is its own kind of gratitude, if you're desperate enough to read it that way.

I was desperate enough. For years I read it that way.

You'll notice I haven't mentioned telling anyone. Mother found me once, age eleven, sitting cross-legged on the floor with a circle of whispering fossils around me like candles, and she went white as the whalebone and said, "Kestrel. Those are rocks." And I said, "They're talking," and she said, "No. They aren't," in a voice with a locked door in it, and after that neither of us ever spoke of it again, and then she died when I was nineteen, and now there's no one to speak of it with even if I wanted to, which I don't, mostly, except at night, except constantly.

That's the arithmetic of a secret like mine. One confession costs you everything; silence costs you a little every day, forever. I did the sums young. Silence won. Silence usually wins, in Hollowmere especially, where the whole town has been making the same bargain since before anyone can remember.

* * *

Market day. Autumn coming on, the apple carts out, the smell of cider presses and wet wool. I had a commission to deliver: a crib-bird for the cooper's new grandchild, carved from a length of whalebone the cooper's son had bought off a river barge at scandalous expense. Whalebone doesn't come upriver often. When it does, the carvers fight over it like gulls over a fish head, because it takes detail like nothing else — you can put feathers on a whalebone bird finer than thought.

I'd picked up the piece a week ago, and I'll admit I chose it partly because the moment my thumb touched it in the cooper's shed, my wrist buzzed like a struck bell.

Old bone. *Very* old. Old in a way that made the other pieces in my workshop feel like gossiping children.

All week it had sat wrapped in linen in my back room, humming faintly, and all week I had told myself I'd get to it after the commissions, and all week I had not slept properly, lying awake while it murmured at the edge of hearing like someone reciting a list in the next room. Names. I was almost sure it was names.

So market morning, I delivered the cooper his bird — he wept a little, coopers being sentimental — and went home, and barred the door, and unwrapped the whalebone like a man opening a letter he already knows is bad news.

It was beautiful raw material. Dense, cream-pale, curved like a question mark. Under the lamplight I could see the grain running through it, tight and regular. Whatever whale this had been, it had swum oceans. It had been somewhere. Everything about it was foreign — the size, the grain, the deep-sea smell it gave off when oiled — and I loved it immediately, the way you love anything that proves the world is bigger than your street.

I set it in the cradle clamp. Took up the finest graver. Made the first scoring cut along where the spine of a bird would go.

And the voice said:

*Maren Vosk. Killed at Brackish Ford, the third of Thawmonth, in the four hundredth year.*

Not a fragment. Not a lullaby. A sentence. Whole, deliberate, clear as a bell rung indoors.

My hand slipped and gouged a furrow across the wing. I set down the graver very carefully, the way you do when the alternative is throwing it.

"Hello?" I said. Which is ridiculous, saying hello to a rib, but there you are. Twenty-six years of loneliness will make you polite to furniture.

*Maren Vosk,* the bone said again, patient, precise. *Covenant-maker. Killed at Brackish Ford, the third of Thawmonth, in the four hundredth year. Remember her.*

"I — " My mouth had gone dry enough to sand glass. "Who's remembering? Remembered by whom?"

Silence. Long silence. Then, softer, almost gentle, the way you'd speak to someone slow: *You.*

I looked at the door. Barred. At the window shutter. Latched. At the lamp, guttering slightly in a draft that shouldn't exist, and I noticed — distantly, the way you notice a tooth is loose — that my hands were shaking, and also that beneath the shaking, underneath the fear like cold water poured down the collar, there was something else.

Relief. God help me, relief. Because in twenty-six years the voices had never once said *you*. They had never once known I was there. I had spent my whole life eavesdropping on grief, a ghost to the ghosts, and now one of them had turned around and looked at me, and it was terrible, and it was the single least lonely moment of my life, and both of those things were true at once, which is a thing nobody warns you about how being different works: it doesn't feel one way. It feels like drowning and being seen, together, in the same breath.

"Covenant-maker," I repeated. The word meant nothing. There were no covenant-makers. There was wool and apples and forgetting, and a grey lid of sky, and a family crest — a kestrel rising over water, ha, very funny, the world has a sense of humor — stitched on exactly one surviving christening robe in my cedar chest.

*Killed,* the bone said. *Murdered. All of them. Every maker of the Oath, cut down, and the sky put out, and the valley built over it like a lid on a pot. Remember us. Someone must remember us. Find—*

And then it stopped. Mid-word. Like a door closing. Like a hand over a mouth.

I sat there a long time. Outside, the market noise faded as afternoon tipped toward evening; someone laughed; a dog complained about apples. Hollowmere doing what Hollowmere does, breathing its small warm breath, keeping its ceiling overhead, forgetting beautifully, professionally, generation after generation.

*Find,* the bone had said. Find what? Find whom?

I should burn it, I thought. That was the sensible thought, the Hollowmere thought. Drop it in the stove, sweep out the ash, deliver the crib-bird tomorrow, live quietly, die forgotten, keep the sum balanced.

Instead I wrapped it back in its linen, carried it upstairs, and put it under my bed, where I could hear it — faintly, patiently, like a stranger reciting names of the dead through a wall — while I lay awake staring at the seamless grey dark where stars ought to be.

* * *

Three nights later, the sky over Hollowmere went black.

Not Quiet-black. Not the familiar lid-grey I'd slept under all my life. This was black like ink, black like a hole cut in the world, and where the border of the valley met the ridgeline, the air itself lit up — thin white lines crackling along the crest of the hills like cracks spreading across a windowpane, and then, with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard, the wards shattered.

Every ward at once. Glass breaking across the whole horizon of my life.

And above the ridge — above, beyond, through the broken ceiling — something fell into view that I had no word for, because I had never seen one. Small. Cold. Burning.

A star.

Then another. Then hundreds, thousands, spilling through the wound in the sky like water through a cracked dam, and the whole valley came out of its houses into the lanes, silent, staring upward, a town full of open mouths, and somewhere beneath my sternum, under my skin, in every piece of old bone in my house, the dead began to sing.
