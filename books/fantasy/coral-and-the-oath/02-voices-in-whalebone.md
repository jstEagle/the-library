## 2. Voices in Whalebone

The commission arrived on a Tuesday, which I mention only because Tuesdays in Hollowmere are reliably dull, and this one had no business being otherwise.

Mistress Corrag Hale wanted a mourning piece for her husband. Whalebone, because Bram Hale had been a whaler before the valley closed and the sea became something people only talked about, and she wanted it carved with a lighthouse. *He always said he'd build me one,* she told me, wringing her apron in my doorway like it owed her money. *Never did. So carve it instead.*

I said yes, because I always say yes, and because grief is the one currency I've never learned to refuse.

Here is what you need to know about bone: it remembers. Not in the poetic way the ballads mean—I've heard those ballads, and they are insufferable—but in the literal way that wood remembers rain and iron remembers fire. Bone was alive once. It listened to a heart for years, decades sometimes, and when the heart stops the listening doesn't entirely stop with it. It goes quiet. It goes patient. And if you're built wrong, the way I'm built wrong, then the moment your chisel touches it, all that patience comes looking for somewhere to go.

Most of the time what I get is weather. A calf's rib might hold the smell of a summer field, or the particular boredom of standing in rain. Sheep bone hums with flock-songs, mindless and pleasant, like a tune stuck in someone else's head. It's company, of a sort. The sort where nobody expects anything of you.

I set up at my bench that Tuesday afternoon with Mistress Hale's whalebone across my knees—a slab of it, dense as slate, older than the valley's oldest fence—and I drew the outline of her lighthouse with charcoal first, the way Da taught me before he taught me anything else. *Charcoal forgives,* he'd say. *Bone doesn't.* Then he'd smile his terrible smile and add, *Neither do the Almeidas,* though I never knew what he meant by that, and by the time I was old enough to ask properly he'd stopped smiling altogether.

I made the first cut at the base of the tower, where the rock would be, and the bone sighed.

Not weather. Not flock-song. Not the usual low murmur of something that used to be alive and hadn't quite finished forgetting it.

A voice. Clear as a bell rung underwater.

*Third day of the Dying Moon. They took Edric Voss at the ford. They took his hands first so he could not ward them.*

I lifted my chisel. The voice stopped, the way voices do, and left behind that specific silence that isn't empty but full—the silence of a room where somebody has just finished saying something and is waiting to see whether you were rude enough not to listen.

"Right," I said to the bone, because talking to bone is already mad and there's no sense doing it halfway. "Let's try that again."

Second cut. Same place, a hair deeper.

*Third day of the Dying Moon. Edric Voss, warden of the western stair. His hands, so he could not ward them. His tongue, so he could not speak the terms. His name, last of all, so no one would know who had been unmade.*

Now, I want to be clear about the state of me at this point, because hindsight makes everything sound like destiny and I refuse to flatter myself. What I felt first was not awe or dread or any of the noble emotions. What I felt was professional alarm. This was a commission. Mistress Hale wanted a lighthouse. Lighthouses do not come with murder in them.

But the voice kept going.

*Fourth day. Sera Ilvane, keeper of the salt records. She ran. They let her run for two days, which is its own kind of cruelty, and caught her at the cliffs. She would not say where the anchor slept. They broke her fingers one at a time and she counted them aloud, all ten, because counting kept her from begging, and she was proud of that. Remember that she was proud of that.*

I put down the chisel.

I went and got water. Drank some, poured some on my wrists, which is a thing Ma used to do when I came out of a carving pale and shaking, back before she decided—kindly, sensibly, in the way that hurts most—that whatever I had was best treated by never mentioning it again. *It's just an echo, love,* she'd say. *Old things leave echoes.* And I'd nod, because what else do you do at eleven years old when your mother has decided your strangest part is unspeakable? You learn to be quiet about the loudest thing in you. You get very good at it. You get so good at it that twenty years on you can stand in a workshop full of murmuring bone and call yourself alone, and almost believe it.

This was not an echo.

Echoes don't have dates. Echoes especially don't have *structure*. Listen to how that voice worked: day, name, office, wound. Day, name, office, wound. That wasn't memory leaking out of old material. That was testimony. That was a ledger being read aloud, and ledgers imply bookkeepers, and bookkeepers imply—

*Fifth day,* said the bone, patiently, as if I hadn't gone anywhere. *They took Tam Osgar in his sleep, which even the others called cowardice. He was ninety-one and blind and he greeted them by name, every one of their names, though they wore ash over their faces. He said, "You were children when I knew you. I christened two of you myself." They killed him anyway. Write it down. Someone must write it down.*

"I'm writing nothing down," I told the bone, my voice coming out higher than I'd have liked. "I'm carving a lighthouse."

*Carve it into the light,* the woman said. *No one looks closely at a lighthouse. Carve us into the light and we will keep.*

And here is the thing I have never admitted to anyone, the thing I am admitting now only because a book has no face and can't look at me while I say it: I wanted to.

Understand what my life was, before all this. I was the bone-carver of Hollowmere, competent and quiet, the person you brought your dead dog's skull to when you couldn't bear to bury it whole, the person whose work everyone praised and whose self nobody looked at twice. I heard whispers in everything I touched and I had spent thirty years pretending I didn't, and the pretending had worn grooves in me deeper than any chisel cut. Loneliness does that. It doesn't stab; it erodes.

And here was a voice—not a whisper, a *voice*, organized and urgent and terribly awake—asking me, of all people, to listen. Asking me to *keep* them.

Dread, yes. There was plenty of dread. But underneath the dread, in the place I keep the shameful things, something leaned toward that voice the way a cold hand leans toward a stove.

* * *

I didn't carve the murders into the lighthouse. Let the record show I resisted, at least initially, with the tremendous moral courage of a woman who simply moved her chisel four inches to the left and started on the lamp room.

The bone didn't like that.

Every cut I made in the lamp room came back doubled, trebled, the voice pressing up through the material like water through a floorboard. And it wasn't just recitation anymore. It had noticed me. That sounds mad—bone noticing—but there's a difference between speech addressed to a room and speech addressed to *you*, and I know the difference the way you know the difference between hearing your name called in a market and hearing it called by your mother.

*You hear me,* the woman said. It wasn't a question, and her tone did something complicated—relief and fury braided together, the way you'd feel if you shouted down a well for a hundred years and finally heard boots above. *How long have you heard the others?*

"The others," I repeated, because stalling is free.

*The mutterers. The weathers. The ones with nothing left to say.* Impatience crackled through her like frost forming. *They are debris. I am not debris. My name was Maren Solace and I was a covenant-maker's scribe and I died at the cliffs of Vael with ten broken fingers and my pride intact, and I have been waiting in this bone—it was mine, this was my walking-staff, they never found it—for two hundred and eleven years for ears like yours.*

Two hundred and eleven years. I did the arithmetic without wanting to. Two hundred and eleven years ago would put her death right around the founding of the valley wards, right around the era Da's half of the family Bible went silent about, right around everything Hollowmere agreed, collectively and without ever discussing it, not to remember.

"We don't have covenants," I said. "We don't have—whatever a covenant-maker is. We have turnips."

The silence that followed had texture. If she'd had lungs, she'd have drawn breath to say something devastating. Instead:

*Child,* Maren Solace said, with the gentleness that is somehow worse than scorn, *what do you think the wards are for?*

And I want to tell you I answered her. I want to tell you I demanded explanations like a heroine in one of Odile Vane's scandalous pamphlets, that I stood at my bench with my jaw set and my chisel raised and said *tell me everything, starting now.* What I actually did was drop the chisel, knock over the water jar, swear with a vocabulary Ma would have charged me for, and sit down hard on the floor with my back against the bench legs, in the middle of the spreading puddle, because apparently my body had decided that if the world was going to tilt, it might as well do it from a seated position.

Maren waited me out. Dead women, I was learning, are excellent at waiting.

"The wards," I said finally, to the ceiling, "keep magic out. Everyone knows that. That's the whole point of Hollowmere. Nothing gets in. No wonders, no curses, no—" I gestured vaguely at everything and nothing. "It's quiet here. It's safe. That's what it's *for*."

*Is it?* she said. *Then why does the quiet end exactly at your family's door?*

"My family's door?"

*The Almeida house sits on the wardstone's root. Every child in this valley knows not to build within a mile of the Almeidas, and every child has forgotten why, and you—you of all of them—have never once wondered what your grandfather did with his evenings?*

I opened my mouth to say something cutting and discovered I had nothing. Because she was right, and worse than right: I *had* wondered. My whole life I'd wondered. Why our house stood apart. Why the bone sang louder near our land. Why Da would walk the perimeter of our property at dusk, touching the fence posts like a man taking a pulse, and come inside gray-faced and pour himself a drink with hands that weren't quite steady, and say, when I asked, *"It's nothing, Kess. Old habits."*

Old habits. Of what profession?

*I watched this valley get built,* Maren said, quieter now. *I was dead by then, but the staff was buried in the peat below the eastern ridge and bone hears the way roots hear. I felt them lay the first stones. I felt them seal the last one. And I felt the shape of what they sealed, and it was not a wall against magic, little carver. Walls keep things out. Prisons keep things in.*

The water had soaked through my trousers and gone cold. Somewhere outside, the ordinary sounds of Hollowmere continued—someone's cart, someone's dog, the mill turning its slow mill-turning—and each ordinary sound now sounded staged, like scenery painted on the far side of a door I'd been leaning against my entire life.

"What did they seal?" I asked. My voice came out small. I hated it and kept going anyway. "What's inside?"

*That,* said Maren Solace, *is not my part of the story. I kept the record of deaths, not of doors.* A pause, and when she spoke again there was something new in her tone, something that took me long seconds to identify because I'd heard it so rarely directed at me: urgency, yes, but underneath it, care. *Listen to me now. I have been patient for two centuries and I am nearly out of patience, because I can feel what you cannot—the wards are thinning. Whatever your ancestors locked away is working at the lock, night after night, year after year, and the lock is failing, and when it fails the sky itself will pay for it.*

"The sky," I said. "What does the sky have to do with anything?"

*Everything,* she said. *The stars are witnesses. The oldest kind. And there are powers in this world that cannot finish their work while anything living is watching.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin, the laugh you make when your body needs to discharge terror and comedy is the nearest exit. "The stars. You're telling me the stars are going to—what? Blink?"

*Count them tonight,* she said. *Count them tomorrow night. Count them every night, and mark what changes, and when you have marked enough, ask yourself what kind of heir ignores a dying woman's last request.*

"You're not dying. You're dead. Two hundred years dead."

*Yes,* Maren Solace said, and for the first time her voice frayed, just slightly, at the edges, like thread pulled too taut. *And it is the loneliest thing there is, and I would spare you the finding out. So carve us into the light, Kestrel Almeida. Names and days. Edric Voss, third of the Dying Moon. Sera Ilvane, fifth. Tam Osgar, sixth. All of them, and there were forty-one, and no one has spoken their names since the night they stopped breathing. Do this and I will teach you what your family was. Refuse—*

She stopped. The bone went still under my hands, genuinely still, the way it never was, and the absence of her was so sudden and total that I understood, with a lurch, that she could simply *leave*, that every voice I'd ever half-heard could simply leave, and I would be alone in the truest sense, the sense I'd been pretending to enjoy for thirty years.

"—and I will still be here," I finished for her, to the empty air. "Because I always am. That's the whole tragedy of me."

* * *

I carved until the candle drowned in its own wax.

I carved the forty-one names into the lamp room, tiny as seed grain, stacked in the spiral pattern Maren dictated—she was exact about the order, said order was *the whole point*, said the sequence itself was a key, which I wrote off at the time as the dramatic excess of a woman who'd been talking to herself for two centuries. I carved dates beneath names, and offices beneath dates, and around the base of the tower, where the rock would be, I carved the cliff of Vael and the ford and the sleeping-house where Tam Osgar died, so that anyone who looked closely—and no one ever looks closely—would see that the lighthouse stood on a foundation of graves.

Mistress Hale would hang it above her hearth and see only a lighthouse, and feel only Bram, and that was fine. That was better than fine. Some commissions are cover stories.

Near dawn, with my hands cramping and the light going gray at the windows, Maren's voice thinned to a thread.

*One more thing,* she said, *and then I will rest, and you should too, though you won't.* A pause, the length of someone gathering courage across two centuries. *The fortieth name. I have saved it. When you carve it, understand what you are carving.*

"Give it to me," I said. My voice was wrecked from arguing with bone all night. "Fortieth of forty-one."

*Ninth day of the Dying Moon,* she said. *Taken at the altar itself, at the moment of the binding, by the founder of the Ashen Court with his own two hands. The strongest of us. The one who trusted him enough to turn her back.*

The chisel hovered.

*Her name,* said Maren Solace, and the words came slowly, each one placed like a stone in a wall, *was Ysolde Almeida.*

Outside my window, over the eastern ridge, the sky was doing the thing it always did at dawn, going from black to gray to the colorless non-color of a valley morning. Ordinary. Safe. Warded.

Except that as I sat there with my ancestor's name ringing in my skull and forty murdered strangers nested in the whalebone under my hands, I noticed—really noticed, for the first time in thirty years of dawns—that the brightest star above the ridge was flickering. Not twinkling. Flickering. The way a candle flickers when someone, somewhere, has put their hand around it.

I didn't sleep that day. I got out my grandfather's old star almanac instead, the one nobody had opened since before I was born, and began to count.
