## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with living your whole life inside a lullaby is that you forget it's a song. You think the hush is just how the world is. You think quiet is a place, not a thing done to you.

I was thinking none of that that night. I was thinking about mutton.

Specifically, I was thinking about the leg of mutton Widow Sorrel had promised me for carving her husband's memorial plate—a fat, glistening, frankly indecent leg of mutton that would feed me for a week if I didn't get greedy and eat it in two sittings, which I absolutely would. I'd carried it home wrapped in cloth, set it on my workbench next to the sheep knuckle I was carving for her, and stood there in the doorway of my little shop at the crook of Mill Lane, feeling like the richest person in Hollowmere.

It was early autumn, the kind of evening where the valley pulls its blanket of mist up around its shoulders. Down the lane, lamplight leaked yellow from windows. Someone's supper smoke smelled of onions. Old Tam Harrow was arguing with his mule again, losing, as usual. The whole valley did its small warm business, and overhead—well.

Overhead there were no stars. There never were. Hollowmere sat under a ceiling of dark like a pot lid, and beyond the ridge that ringed us, whatever sky the rest of the world got, we weren't invited to it. Children asked about it once and were told the mountains were tall and the weather was strange, and children in Hollowmere learned early which questions made adults go stiff, so they stopped asking. I had stopped asking before I could write. Mostly.

I remember thinking—and this is the part I keep turning over, because it felt so ordinary—that the night looked exactly like every other night of my life. The lid was on the pot. Everything was fine. I had a leg of mutton.

Then the dead woman started screaming.

* * *

She hadn't stopped since morning. All day she'd been in the whalebone, louder than any voice I'd ever heard there, naming names and dates like a clerk reading out an indictment: *Maren of the Second Seat, murdered at the turning of the year. Ossian Vale, drowned with stones in his coat. Ilse Almeida—*

That one had nearly made me drop my chisel. *Almeida.* My name. Nobody in my family had died anywhere interesting; we were bone-carvers and beekeepers going back as far as the parish book reached. But she kept saying it, patient and terrible, and by suppertime she'd stopped enumerating and simply begun to keen, one long unbroken note that sat behind my teeth like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

So maybe I shouldn't have been surprised when the world ended at half past nine. But I was. You're always surprised. That's the joke of catastrophe—it ambushes you mid-mutton.

The keening cut off. Not faded—*cut*, like a door slammed on an organ pipe. And the silence afterward was so total that I heard my own heart stumble. Even Hollowmere-quiet has texture: wind in the chimney, mice in the wall, Tam Harrow's mule expressing opinions. This was nothing. A held breath the size of a valley.

Then the light changed.

I don't know how else to say it. There was no lamp flicker, no moon doing anything dramatic—the mist outside simply went wrong, the way milk goes wrong, and every shadow in my shop leaned a little too far east, as if something enormous had stepped between the valley and everything else.

I walked to the door. My legs did it without consulting me. Outside, neighbors were emerging into the lane the same way, drawn by the same wrongness, faces tilted up. Tam Harrow had abandoned his mule mid-argument, which is how you know things were truly serious.

"Kest," called Marta Chen from her doorstep, three doors down, her youngest peeking around her skirts. "Is it weather?"

"I'm a bone-carver, Marta."

"You're the one who knows odd things."

Which was true, and flattering, and useless. I knew odd things the way a fish knows water: intimately, secretly, and with a horror of anyone finding out. So instead of saying *the dead have gone silent all at once and silence from the dead is worse than any noise they've ever made,* I said, "Maybe it's weather."

And then the stars went out.

I want to be precise here, because people always assume I mean clouds covered them, or some ash-fall dimmed them. No. We had no stars to lose—not visible ones. But every soul in that lane that night swore afterward that they saw them anyway: a scatter of cold points overhead, faint as breath on glass, shining down through the lid that had hidden them for longer than anyone could remember. For perhaps ten heartbeats, Hollowmere had a sky.

I remember thinking, with a clarity that still shames me: *oh. So that's what we've been missing.*

Then they went out. Not covered—*extinguished*, all together, like candles pinched by one hand. And the sound that followed was the sound the world makes when it breaks.

* * *

Glass, everyone said later. The wards shattered like glass.

They were right, in the way that frightened people reaching for familiar words are usually right. From my doorway I heard it come from all directions at once—from the ridge, from beneath us, from everywhere the valley touched the rest of the world—a crack, then a splintering rush, like the whole horizon was a window and something on the far side had come through it. The lamplights guttered. The mist peeled back from the slopes like a bandage coming off a wound.

And then the magic came in.

You'll have to bear with me, because I don't have words for it either. Nobody in Hollowmere did. Magic wasn't a rumor to us; it wasn't even a story. Stories require someone to have seen. What we had was an absence so old it had become geography, like asking a man born in a cave to describe the sun. The valley was *quiet* the way stone is quiet. It had never occurred to me that quiet was a shape, and that shapes can break.

What flooded through the broken border felt—this is the closest I can get—like color poured into a life lived in charcoal. It came over the ridge on the wind, and where it touched, the world remembered things. The grass silvered and lay flat as if a great hand had stroked it. Marta's goat stood straight up on its hind legs, considered its situation with what I swear was dignity, and spoke one perfect sentence in church Latin before collapsing back to all fours, deeply embarrassed. Down by the mill, the millpond froze solid in August-warm air, then unfroze, then bloomed—water lilies in a single violent heartbeat, opening white and enormous, petals the size of dinner plates.

My own hands were burning.

Not pain, exactly. Heat, and pressure, and underneath both, a rising hum, like standing too close to a hive. I looked down expecting to see the skin charred off my fingers and found instead that every scar I owned—every nick and chisel-bite from fifteen years of carving—was glowing faintly gold, seams of light stitched through me like the valley itself was full of fault lines and I was one of them.

Around me, Hollowmere was coming apart politely. Mrs. Chen's washing line had levitated, sheets billowing in windless air. Tam Harrow's mule had achieved flight, briefly and badly. Somewhere a churchbell rang itself, arrhythmically, frantically, a bell with no ringer trying to shout. And people were screaming—not the sharp screams of danger but the low continuous moan of a village discovering that the floor of the world was not the floor.

"Get inside!" someone shouted—the reeve, I think, Bram Ostler, wading up the lane with a lantern that burned green instead of yellow. "Everyone inside! Bar your doors!"

Good advice, probably. I didn't take it. I couldn't have told you why at the time. Now I know: the dead had gone silent when the stars died, and silence, for me, was unbearable, because their voices had been my secret company since childhood, hateful and precious in equal measure, and their absence left a hole shaped exactly like loneliness. I stood in the middle of a breaking world with my hands full of light, listening for them the way you press an ear to a rail for a train you've been promised.

"Kestrel!" Marta had her boy by the collar and was retreating indoors. She looked at me—at my glowing hands—and her face did something complicated. "Kestrel, come inside, love."

"In a minute," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it, because she pulled her boy in fast and barred the door, and through the window I watched her watch me with an expression I'd spend the next several days learning to recognize: fear, wearing politeness's clothes.

The humming in my hands rose to a pitch. The whalebone on my workbench—I could feel it through the open door, feel the dead woman in it gathering herself like a swimmer before a dive. And all across the valley, I realized, every piece of bone I had ever carved was singing: the memorial plates in the churchyard, the buttons, the combs, the little protective figures mothers hung over cradles because a bone-carver's work was supposed to be lucky. Lucky. Fifteen years of whispering trinkets, and tonight they all woke at once.

The dead woman spoke.

Not in the bone. Everywhere. Her voice came out of the ground, out of the broken air, out of my own glowing scars, vast and close and shaking with something I finally understood was not grief but urgency:

"*Now, child. The door is broken and they will come through it. You must run before they tell you whose door it was.*"

"Whose—" I said aloud, to empty air, in the middle of the street, like a madwoman. "Whose door? What's happening?"

But she was already fading, or being dragged away, her voice thinning out like smoke down a flue, and the last thing I heard her say, very small now, almost tender, was:

"*Ilse Almeida. Ask yourself why you never heard of her.*"

Then the second wave hit.

I've tried to describe the wards' collapse to people who weren't there, and the honest version is this: imagine every sound you've ever kept inside you—every swallowed word, every held breath, every scream you were too proud to loose—all released at once, by everyone, forever. The border wards hadn't just broken. They had *unraveled*, centuries of woven silence paying out all at once, and the sound of it went through the valley like a scythe through wheat. Every window in Hollowmere burst inward. Every bird fell out of every tree, stunned. I was thrown to my knees in the lane, palms flat against cobblestones that hummed like plucked strings, and above me the darkness where the stars had flickered churned—actually churned, visibly, a slow black rotation, as if the lid on the pot had cracked and something behind the sky was looking down through the gap.

I looked up into it. I couldn't not.

And something looked back.

I won't dress it up. I felt attention—cold, deliberate, ancient, *interested*—come down through that wound in the night and settle on our valley, on the lane, on the kneeling bone-carver with light leaking out of her hands like a badly made lantern. It regarded me for the length of three heartbeats. In those three heartbeats I understood, without words, the following: that the quiet of my whole life had been a wall; that walls are built to hold something in or keep something out; and that I had never once asked which side of the wall I was living on.

Then the attention moved on, sweeping north along the broken border, hunting, and I was left on my knees in the ruins of the only world I knew, shaking so hard my teeth chattered.

* * *

I don't know how long I stayed there. Long enough for the green-burning chaos to settle into something worse: order. Long enough for the reeve to organize lantern patrols and for the churchwarden to start a list of who was hurt (the miller's boy, a broken arm; two of the Ostlers, unconscious and wouldn't wake; old Nan Pellow, dead in her bed, though everyone agreed she'd been halfway there for years and would have been furious to miss the excitement). Long enough for me to walk home on legs that belonged to someone else, bar my own door, and sit down at my workbench in front of a leg of mutton that now seemed like an artifact from a lost civilization.

The shop was full of light. Every bone in it glowed—my finished stock, my tools' horn handles, the knuckle I'd been carving for Widow Sorrel, all of it lit from within, soft gold, breathing. Breathing. That's the word, and I know how it sounds. The bones breathed, slow and rhythmic, like sleeping things finally allowed to sleep.

And the whalebone—the big curled slab I'd bought off the peddler two years back, the one the shouting woman lived in—was blazing like a window at sunset.

I crossed the room and put my hands on it.

The moment I touched it, the floodgates opened. Not one voice. Hundreds. Thousands. They came through me in a torrent—names, dates, places, griefs stacked on griefs stacked on griefs, centuries deep, all of them speaking at once except that now, for the first time, they were speaking *together*. Chorus, not cacophony. Organized. Like an army that had spent a very long time waiting for a signal.

And the signal, I understood with sick certainty, had just been given. The wards broke. The stars died. And every dead soul within reach of me turned, as one, toward the last heir of—

Of what? I pressed my palms harder against the burning bone. *Heir of what?*

One voice separated from the rest. Hers. Closer now, exhausted, as if screaming across a broken country to reach me had cost her everything she had.

"Listen," she said. "Listen, bone-child, because they are coming and I have perhaps minutes. The wall around your valley was not weather. It was not geology. It was built, and maintained, and paid for, and the crest of those who built it is carved into the heart-stone of the border, and you have worn that crest your whole life without knowing it." A breath she didn't need. "Look at your hands, Kestrel Almeida. Look at your family's mark. And then run, because the ones who killed us have noticed the wall came down, and they will come to make sure it stays down, and you are the only thing left that could raise it—or anything better—again."

"My family's mark," I repeated stupidly. "The Almeidas are beekeepers."

"Are they," she said, and there was something awful in the gentleness of it. "Go to the western border-stone, child. See what's carved on it. See what's been carved on it since before your parish book began. And then decide whether to run or hide—but decide fast."

Her presence thinned, frayed, and was gone, leaving me alone in a glowing shop with a dead woman's warning and a leg of mutton.

Here is what I did, and I record it honestly because someone should know that courage is mostly a costume panic wears: I sat down. I put my head in my glowing hands. I thought about Marta barring her door against me. I thought about Nan Pellow, mercifully past caring. I thought about how at half past nine I had been the richest woman in Hollowmere, and how it was now barely midnight, and how I wanted, more than anything in the world, for it to be half past nine again, and for the lid to be on the pot, and for the dead to go back to whispering instead of shouting, and for me to go back to not knowing what my own name meant.

Then I got up, because the dead woman had told me to do one small concrete thing, and when the world ends, small concrete things are all that's holding.

I took my heaviest knife, a hooded cloak, and—for reasons I genuinely cannot explain except that my hands insisted—the little carved knuckle I'd made for Widow Sorrel. I let myself out the back and cut up through the orchards, past the sleeping hives (the bees were awake, all of them, humming in the dark in perfect unison, which I chose not to think about), climbing the western slope toward the border-stone I'd passed ten thousand times without once looking at.

It stood where it had always stood, a grey finger against the churning sky, moss-grown, boring, the most forgettable rock in the valley.

I scraped the moss away with my knife. The moon—if there was still such a thing as the moon somewhere—was no help. So I cupped my palm over the stone, and my skin glowed, and by my own light I read what was cut deep into the border-stone, worn by centuries but unmistakable, a device I had carved into a hundred commission pieces without ever asking where the pattern came from because it had simply always been in my hands:

A valley, ringed by mountains. Above it, seven stars.

And beneath the stars, worked into the design so cleverly you'd never see it unless you were looking—two figures, kneeling, their wrists bound to the mountainsides.

Chains. The crest of my family was chains, and I had been decorating gravestones with them my whole life.

Behind me, down in the valley, the churchbell began ringing again—not frantic this time but steady, measured, the alarm-pattern reserved for war. Far to the north, along the broken line of the border, a red glow was rising that was not fire and not dawn.

And on the stone under my hand, faint but unmistakable, the carved chains had begun to warm—as if something inside the mountain had felt me touch them, and woken, and was waiting to see what the last Almeida would do next.
