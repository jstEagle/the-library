## 4. What the Wards Were Hiding

I did not sleep. I don't imagine anyone in Hollowmere did. When the sky goes black over your entire life and stays that way, sleep becomes a luxury for people with shorter memories.

I sat up through the small hours in my workshop with a candle stub burning down to nothing, listening. The valley had always been quiet — that was its whole personality, really. Hollowmere: the place where nothing happened, on purpose. But this was a different quiet. This was the quiet of a room after someone has screamed.

And under the quiet, the dead were louder than they had ever been. Not the murmurs I'd grown up translating into bone — those patient, watery voices that came through whale rib and antler like sound through a locked door. These were urgent. They pressed against the inside of my skull like hands against a window.

*The stone*, the drowned woman kept saying. *Go to the stone. See what your family built.*

Which is a strange thing to say about wards my family had supposedly been protected by, but the dead have never been great at phrasing things tactfully.

So when the first grey suggestion of morning seeped under my door — greyer than it should have been, because there were no stars left to spend themselves against — I pulled on my coat, took the good carving knife because I am not an idiot, and walked out into a world that no longer made sense, heading for the border ridge where the wards had lived for as long as anyone could remember.

* * *

The walk to the ridge took two hours in daylight and closer to three that morning, because everyone in Hollowmere had apparently had the same idea and the road was clogged with neighbours staring upward like a congregation of startled owls.

"Kestrel," called Marta Ostrey from her doorway, holding her youngest by the collar the way you hold a kite string in high wind. "You're the one who knows about old things. What's happened to the sky?"

"I'm a bone-carver, Marta. I know about buttons and brooches and once, memorably, a chess set."

"But your grandmother—"

"My grandmother knew about old things too. She's also dead. If the sky's problem can wait for a séance, I'll get right on it."

That earned me a look, but people let me pass. They always let me pass. That was the arrangement of my whole life: Kestrel Almeida, useful and odd, tolerated the way you tolerate a well that runs cold. I had told myself for thirty years that this was community. Standing in that crowd of frightened faces that parted for me without quite meeting my eyes, I understood it had been something more like quarantine.

I should note here what the valley looked like without stars above it, because I want to remember it honestly even if remembering hurts: it looked exactly the same. That was the worst of it. The hills were still green-brown, the mere still lay flat as a scoured pan, the pines still stood in their patient ranks. The world hadn't ended. It had simply been shown to us naked for the first time, and we discovered we had never actually seen it before. Every view I'd loved had been framed by something I didn't know was there. Take away the frame and the picture doesn't change — but you do.

The dead murmured directions at me the whole way up, which I pretended not to follow and followed perfectly.

* * *

The wardstone sat in its hollow below the ridge crest, and it was ruined.

I want to be precise, because "ruined" doesn't cover it. The great standing stone that generations of Almeidas had tended — polished, oiled, spoken to at solstices with words I'd learned as a child without ever asking what they meant — had shattered from the inside out. Not broken like a dropped plate. Burst, like a frozen pipe, like something that had contained pressure for centuries and finally been allowed to release it. Chunks of it lay flung across the hollow in a rough spiral, some of them embedded in the turf, one lodged six feet up the trunk of a pine as though the mountain had spat it.

And the air. Gods, the air. I had lived my entire life inside the wards, and I had thought — everyone had thought — that the valley's stillness was just how the world was. It wasn't. The wards had been pressing down on everything, all the time, a weight so constant it became silence. Now that weight was gone, and the hollow felt like the moment after you pop your ears at altitude. Sounds arrived too vividly. My own heartbeat was an intrusion. When I exhaled, the trees seemed to lean in to listen.

Magic. That was magic, or the absence of its absence, or whatever you call it when a thing floods into a space that has been vacuum-sealed against it since before your grandparents were born. It tasted like copper and rain and someone else's memories.

I picked my way to the largest fragment and knelt beside it.

The dead had gone very quiet. That should have warned me. Dead things only hush when they're watching.

The fragment was taller than me, sheared clean along one face, and that face — the inner face, the face that had pointed inward at the valley all these years, that had faced us instead of the world — was carved.

Not weathered. Not decorated. Carved, deep and deliberate, with lines and interlocking curves and a central device I would have known anywhere, because I had held it in my hands a thousand times. Because it hung around my neck right then, on a cord my mother had tied there before I could talk. Because it was stamped on the flyleaf of every ledger in our family's keeping and branded into the lintel of our workshop door.

A kestrel in flight, wings spread, enclosed in a ring of eight points.

My family's crest. Cut into the heart of the border ward, facing inward.

"That's not possible," I said, to no one, in the tone of a person negotiating with reality.

Except it was worse than the crest alone. As I circled the fragment, running my fingers over the grooves, the full design emerged. The eight-pointed ring wasn't decoration. It was a diagram. Eight points, eight lines radiating outward and downward into a lattice of smaller figures — names, or what I slowly realized were names, dozens of them, hundreds, cut in a script I had only ever seen in the oldest of Grandmother Sera's books, the ones she kept wrapped in oilcloth and never let me read. And beneath each name, a smaller mark. Some kind of tally. Or a date. Or—

I stopped counting when I found our own name. *Almeida.* Cut into the lattice like all the others. Not honoured above them. Not set apart. Just one line among hundreds, bound into the same net.

"See," said the drowned woman's voice, very close now, very clear, speaking through no bone at all. Speaking through the stone itself, or through the morning air, or through me. "Now you understand."

"Understand what?" My voice came out thin. "Understand *what*?"

"What the valley is."

"It's home."

"Home." She said the word the way you'd test rotten floorboards. "Kestrel Almeida. Look at the shape of the wards. You're a maker; look with your hands, not your hopes."

And because I am, in the end, a craftsman before anything else, I did. I walked the spiral of fragments, and I read the structure the way I'd read any piece — grain, stress, intent. And the intent was unmistakable, once I let myself see it.

Wards keep things out. Everyone knows that. That's the story: the wall, the gate, the watchman. But these weren't built like walls. A wall has one face outward. These had their finest work turned *in*. The deep carving, the binding script, the anchor-points — all of it faced the valley. The outer faces were nearly plain. Whatever the wards spent most of their strength restraining, it had never been outside.

They were built to keep something in.

And the thing they were built to keep in — the name at the centre of the lattice, the kestrel in its ring of eight points, repeated at every junction of the net like a knot in a net is repeated — was mine.

* * *

I sat down in the wet grass beside a fragment of my family's prison and laughed. I'll admit that. It wasn't a good laugh. It was the laugh of a woman discovering that the floor of her life had been painted on canvas the whole time.

Because here is what I had believed, the way fish believe in water: the Almeidas were ward-keepers. Custodians. Trusted with the border, honoured with the burden, a little dull, a little revered, passed over for festivals because we were *needed elsewhere*. My grandmother had tended the stone and taught me the solstice words and never once, not once in all her careful years, said the words *Oathkeeper* or *prison* or *you*. She had given me the crest and the craft and the whispers, and she had let me believe the whispers were a quirk, a burden, a secret to be managed rather than examined.

She had known. Of course she had known. She had died knowing, and she had never told me, and now I would never get to ask her why, and the anger I felt at her had nowhere to go except round and round inside me like water finding a drain.

The dead watched me grieve and rage in about equal measure, and when I had worn myself down to something quieter, the drowned woman spoke again.

"There were seven families," she said. "Seven who swore the covenant at Coral, and seven who were meant to hold it together, each anchoring one of the seven realms. The Almeidas were the seventh. The last. The ones meant to hold the anchor itself."

"The anchor of what?"

"The oath. The living link. The one who feels what all the realms feel, so that no realm can forget the others." A pause, and when she resumed, her voice had the flat care of someone delivering news they've rehearsed. "The first Almeida was meant to be that anchor. Instead he ran. He sealed himself and his bloodline inside the strongest wards his fellow oath-makers could raise — wards paid for with their own lives' work — and he told his children it was protection."

"He told them it was safety," I said. My mouth felt full of ash. "He walled his family in with the bones of the people who trusted him, and he called it love."

"Yes."

"And the other six? The names on the stone?"

"Murdered." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Each in turn, across two hundred years, by the court that rose from the covenant's breaking. The Ashen Court does not leave loose threads, Kestrel. Six anchors killed. One hidden behind wards so thorough that even the Court lost the scent for a long age. Until three nights ago, you were the safest and the least free person in either realm."

I looked at my hands. Carver's hands, scarred and competent, hands that had made beautiful small things out of death for thirty years and called it a living. They were shaking.

"So the whispers," I said. "The voices. It isn't a gift. It isn't a curse. It's—" I groped for it. "It's inheritance. The dead aren't talking to me because I'm special. They're talking to me because I'm *theirs*. I'm the unclaimed property of a broken contract."

"They are talking to you," the drowned woman said gently, "because you are the only one left who can hear."

* * *

Here is the thing about betrayal by inheritance: you cannot return it. If a stranger robs you, you can hate them cleanly. But when the theft turns out to have been committed by your own grandmother, in slow installments, across your entire childhood — every lullaby a brick, every solstice ritual a bar in the window — then the hatred has to live in the same house as the love, and neither one will move out.

I thought about her hands on the wardstone. I thought about her teaching me the tending-oil recipe, four parts linseed, one part salt, saying *this matters, Kestrel, do it properly*, and meaning *keep your cage oiled, child*. I thought about all the times I'd asked what was beyond the ridge and she'd said *nothing you need*, and I had accepted it, because she was the only person in the world who had never looked at me like I was strange, and being believed by her had seemed worth any amount of not-being-told.

I cried for a while. I won't describe it. There are things even a wry narration shouldn't have to carry.

When I finished, the sun — such as it was, a pale smear behind the ash-dark — stood at midmorning, and practical thoughts began their usual rude invasion of my grief. Chief among them: *if the wards are down, and the wards were hiding an Almeida, then everyone who wanted to find an Almeida can now find one.*

As if summoned by the thought, the dead all spoke at once.

*Rider,* they said. *On the north road. Riding ash.*

* * *

I got down off the ridge faster than I'd come up, cutting the shepherd's path through the pines, and I made it almost to the tree line before the smell reached me — woodsmoke and cold iron, wrong in the green morning air — and I heard the hoofbeats.

One horse. Unhurried. Whoever rode it was not chasing me, which was somehow more frightening than pursuit. Pursuit means urgency. This rider moved like a person collecting a debt they were certain of.

I crouched behind a fallen pine, knife in hand, feeling deeply stupid about the knife. Through the branches I watched the rider come down the north road into the valley's edge: tall, wrapped in grey that might have been cloth and might have been the same ash that had eaten the sky, trailing faint smoke from the hem. Where the face should have been there was a smooth porcelain mask, featureless except for a single seam of light.

The horse stopped in the open ground below my hiding place. The masked head turned — precisely, deliberately — until it faced my stand of pines directly.

"Kestrel Almeida," the rider said. The voice was dry and pleasant, like paper turning pages. "Be at ease. The Court sends courtesy before force. Always."

I stayed still. The dead crowded close around me, and for the first time in my life their presence felt like shelter.

"We know the wards fell," the rider continued. "We know what woke. We come with an offer of protection — for your blood is precious, heir of the seventh, and there are those in this darkened world who would waste it. The Ashen Court asks only a small thing. A vial. A few drops, freely given, to seal certain... arrangements."

"And if I refuse?"

The porcelain tilted, a courteous fraction. "Then the Court's next messenger will be less conversational. You have until the new moon."

The horse turned. The smoke-trailing figure rode back up the north road, unhurried as sunrise, and the dead breathed again around me like a crowd releasing a held breath.

I stayed in the bracken a long while, knife useless in my fist, doing arithmetic. New moon: nine days. A vial of my blood in the hands of the people who had murdered six of my seven kin. Protection offered by the very court whose founding act, if the drowned woman was honest, had been a two-century campaign of murder against my family.

There was no decision to make. There was only the direction to run it in.

I went home once — quickly, guiltily — and packed what fits in a carver's satchel: tools, oilcloth, Grandmother Sera's oldest forbidden book, jerky, the crest on its cord. I fed the neighbour's cat because someone had to and I am sentimental about animals and cowardice in equal measure. I left no note, because what would it say? *Gone to fulfil a murderous ancestral contract. Back whenever the dead say so.*

Then I walked out of Hollowmere at the bottom of the valley, following the river downstream, following the drowned woman's voice toward a place she called Coral — and I did not look back at the ridge, at the shattered stone, at the valley that had been my whole world and, it turned out, my whole cage.

Behind me, somewhere on the north road, ash was already falling where no fire burned.

Ahead of me, the river ran dark, and the dead counted my steps like a rosary.
