## 5. The Rider in Ash

I want it on the record that I did not sleep that night. I lay in my bed with the whalebone under my pillow like a talisman, which is exactly the sort of thing a sensible person does when the sky has eaten itself, and I listened to the dead woman count.

She'd been counting all night. Not numbers, exactly—names. *Maren Vos, drowned at the ford. Maren Vos, drowned at the ford. Maren Vos—* the way you'd tap a finger against a locked door, patient, certain that eventually someone would open it.

The wards had come down the night before. The stars had gone with them. And in the morning, with Hollowmere sitting under a ceiling of ash-grey nothing, the whole valley came out of its houses to stand in the lanes and stare upward like a congregation of drowned men.

I did not go out to stare. I went out to check the wardstone.

Here is what I knew by then, or thought I knew: the border wards had shattered like a dropped plate, and the shards of the great stone at the valley's mouth bore my family's crest—a heron holding a key in its beak, which I had always assumed was some dull heraldic joke about watchfulness. My grandmother had the same crest stamped into her thimble. My mother had it inked inside her wedding ring. We were, as far as anyone in Hollowmere knew, bone-carvers and coffin-makers and occasionally, when the river flooded, amateur sandbag engineers.

The dead woman had other ideas. All night she'd been saying a word I hadn't wanted to hear: *Oathkeeper. Oathkeeper. Oathkeeper,* in the tone of someone reminding a debtor at the door.

So I went to the valley's mouth at first light, with my coat collar up and my hands in my pockets like a person with nothing worse than a chill, and I looked at the broken wardstone, and the broken wardstone looked back.

The heron. The key. Carved three feet deep into a stone the size of a chapel, and around it, in a script I'd only ever seen scratched into the underside of my grandmother's workbench, the remnants of the binding-glyphs. I knew them the way you know a lullaby—without ever having been taught, without ever having asked. My hands ached to complete them. That was the worst part. Not the betrayal of it, not yet. The ache. Some part of me had been born knowing what those glyphs were for, and it woke up now and stretched, and I hated it the way you hate a limb that's been asleep.

"You're the carver."

I turned. Old Bram Hesse stood behind me, the miller, with his hat in his hands, which he did when he was frightened and didn't want anyone to know. Everyone in Hollowmere had come to me with their broken things for twenty years. Combs, buttons, keepsakes, the occasional honest-to-god femur for a grave-marker. I was useful. Useful is a fine thing to be, right up until the morning it isn't.

"Bram."

"The sky's wrong," he said, as though I might not have noticed. "And the wards—" He gestured at the stone. "My grandfather said the wards would hold until the end of the world. He said it like it was a comfort."

"Grandfathers lie," I said, "usually kindly." Then, because he was looking at me with the particular hollow expression of a man who has just realized his whole life was built on a foundation he'd never inspected: "Go home, Bram. Bar your door. It's probably nothing."

It was not nothing. It was never going to be nothing again. But I said it because it was what you say, and because I wanted one more hour of being a person to whom things were said, rather than a person to whom things were done.

*Oathkeeper,* said the dead woman, from the bone in my pocket.

"Later," I muttered, and Bram gave me a look, and I pretended I'd been talking to him.

* * *

The rider came at noon.

I was in the workshop when the dogs started—not barking, which dogs do, but that low continuous groaning bark that means the animal has seen something its instincts have filed under *no*. Then the groaning stopped all at once, which was worse. Silence from a dog is a decision.

I went to the window. The road from the valley's mouth ran past the workshop, and on it, riding a horse the colour of wet slate, came a figure in grey. Not riding so much as arriving—the way weather arrives, without any visible effort, at exactly the pace it intends.

The rider wore ash. That's the only way I can put it. The cloak, the hood, the gloves, all of it the grey of a fire three days dead, and it moved when they moved with a soft sifting sound, like something settling. Where the horse's hooves touched the mud of the road, the mud went pale. Not burned. *Paled*, the way a face pales. The road looked like it had been asked a question it couldn't answer.

The rider stopped at my gate. Did not dismount. Raised one grey-gloved hand and knocked on empty air, and the knock sounded inside my workshop, inside my skull, polite as a visiting aunt.

"Kestrel Almeida," said a voice like paper being folded. I could not tell if it was a man or a woman or neither; it had the evenness of something that had decided gender was a tool it had put down a long time ago. "I bring the compliments of the Ashen Court."

I did not open the door. This was, I want to be clear, the single smartest thing I did that week.

"The Court regrets the disruption to your valley," the voice continued, conversational, as though we were discussing a late delivery of flour. "The collapse of the wards was... an inevitability long deferred. The Court also regrets the inconvenience of the darkened sky, though I'm told your people never had much use for stars. Insular folk. Sensible, in their way."

"We liked them fine," I said through the door. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is one of the two or three useful tricks my body knows.

"Did you. Well." A pause with the texture of a smile. "The Court extends its protection. The world beyond your valley has changed, Kestrel Almeida. The old bindings are loose. Things are walking that have not walked in an age, and your neighbours—" the rider's hood turned, very slightly, toward the village, where I could see faces at windows, "—are soft. Sheltered. They will need someone to stand between them and what's coming."

"And the Court offers this out of the goodness of its ash-filled heart."

"The Court offers it in exchange for a very small thing." The rider dismounted now, and the sound of their boots on my path was like sand poured on a drum. "Your blood. A few vials. Taken kindly, with your consent, at your convenience."

I stood in my workshop, among my shavings and my half-carved spoons and the whalebone that had started whispering names, and I felt the dead woman go very still, the way a bird goes still when a cat comes into the garden.

"What," I said carefully, "does the Court want with my blood?"

"Ah." The paper voice folded itself into something almost warm. "You've been to the stone. You've seen the heron. Good. That saves time. The Almeida line are Oathkeepers, Kestrel. The last of them. Your blood sealed the old covenant, and your blood can seal a new one—or unseal what remains of the old. In the wrong hands, you are a key. In the Court's hands, you are a *kept* key. Safe. Honoured. Your valley protected, your neighbours fed through the dark, your name written in the Court's rolls as a friend of the new order."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the Court grieves," said the rider, "and the world remains a dangerous place, and the Court cannot be answerable for where the danger falls."

There it was. I've thought about that moment often since—the plainness of it. No thunder, no threat with teeth in it. Just a merchant's pause, a price adjusted, the smallest shrug of consequence. Evil, when I'd imagined it—and I had, in the safe way you imagine things in a valley with no magic in it—had always seemed to me like it would announce itself. It doesn't. It knocks politely and offers to help with your groceries.

*Don't,* said the dead woman, from my pocket, in a voice like a struck bell. *Don't don't don't.*

"I need to think about it," I said.

"Of course." The rider's tone suggested I had passed some small test, or failed one; I couldn't tell which, and I have thought about that too. "The Court is patient. I will call again tomorrow at noon."

I watched through the shutter as the rider mounted and rode back down the road, and where the horse had stood, the grass stayed pale, and it stayed pale for years, long after I was gone, long after anyone remembered why. Bram Hesse told me that once, in a letter. The grass at Kestrel Almeida's gate, grey as a scar. The valley kept the shape of that morning the way a body keeps the shape of a blow.

* * *

I did not sleep that night either, and this time it wasn't the dead woman's counting. It was arithmetic.

A few vials. Protection. My neighbours fed through the dark. I turned it over and over like a stone in the hand, looking for the flaw, and the horrible thing was that the flaw wasn't hidden—it was the whole shape of the thing. *Kept key.* I had spent my entire life in a valley built, as far as I could now tell, around my own family like a sheath around a blade, and I knew what it was to be kept. The wards hadn't protected the Almeidas. The wards had *contained* us. Every kindness Hollowmere had ever shown me had been, in some way I couldn't yet trace, a wall.

And now the Ashen Court wanted to build a newer, politer wall, and call it protection, and I would live inside it the way I had always lived inside things, and my neighbours would be fed, and I would be safe, and every morning I would wake up and not be able to hear the dead woman say *Maren Vos, drowned at the ford*, because the Court would have what it needed to make sure no covenant was ever sealed again, and the dead would stay dead in the way the Court liked them—quiet, used, ash.

The stars were gone. The wards were down. The world outside was full of walking things. And the only voice telling me what any of it meant was a murdered woman three centuries in her grave, counting names against a locked door.

I want to say I chose bravely. The truth is I sat up until the grey hour with my grandmother's thimble in my hand, turning the heron and the key under my thumb, and what I felt mostly was fury—cold, specific, well-organised fury, the best kind, the kind that gets things done. My family had been locked in this valley for—how long? Centuries. Generations of Almeidas carving bones and burying neighbours and never once being told they were the hinge the whole world swung on. And now the people who'd let the wards rot—the Court, the *inevitability long deferred*—wanted to collect me like a debt.

"Tomorrow at noon," I said to the empty workshop. "We'll see about tomorrow at noon."

The dead woman said, with unmistakable satisfaction: *Run.*

* * *

I left before dawn, and I was not graceful about it.

I took the whalebone—the big piece, the one with the names—and my carving knives, and my grandmother's thimble, and food that would keep, and I stood in my workshop for one long stupid minute looking at everything I wasn't taking, at the spoons and the combs and the half-finished cradle-rattle for the Tanners' baby, and I thought: *this is what it costs, this exact minute*, and then I left.

I left a note. I'm not proud of it. It said: *Gone to see about the sky. Back when I can. Feed the dog.* We didn't have a dog. I have never known why I wrote that. Grief, probably. Grief makes you leave instructions for animals that don't exist.

The dead woman directed me. That's the part I still find strange to say, all these years later—that I walked out of the only home I'd ever known with no map but a murdered woman's voice in a piece of whalebone, and I trusted it, because she was the only creature in creation that had ever told me the truth without wanting anything for it. She said *south* when I would have gone east. She said *the ford, the ford* when the road bent toward the ford, and at the ford, in the grey before grey, I looked back once at Hollowmere under its lid of starless sky, at the smoke of the morning fires rising straight and small, and I felt the grief come up through my boots like cold water.

*They'll be all right,* I told her, and didn't believe it.

*You'll be all right,* she said, and I believed that even less, and we went on together, the liar and the liar, down to the ford.

The sun rose—or the sky lightened to the colour of dirty pewter, which is what passed for sunrise now—and that was when I heard the hooves.

I knew before I turned. Some animal part of me knew, the way dogs know. The hooves made no splash at the ford, and hooves always splash. I turned, and there on the far bank stood the slate-grey horse and the rider in ash, and the rider's hood came up, and the folded-paper voice said, with what I swear was genuine regret:

"The Court hoped you'd choose better."

"You said tomorrow at noon," I said. "I had a whole night of agonising scheduled."

"The Court is patient," the rider said, "but the Court is not *foolish*. You went to the stone. You spoke to the bone. You have the look of a person doing arithmetic, Kestrel Almeida, and the Court cannot wait for you to finish the sum." The rider raised one gloved hand—not a threat, not yet, a *gesture*, the way you'd gesture to a child standing too near a cliff. "Come. A few vials. Your neighbours fed. It is not so much to ask."

And here is the thing I learned about refusal, the first time, in the shallows of a ford with ash riding toward me: it doesn't feel like courage. It feels like the moment a bone snaps—clean, terrible, and then strangely quiet, because everything that was holding the shape together has let go at once.

"No," I said.

The word came out small. It came out mine.

The rider sighed, and the sound sifted, and the ash of the cloak began to *move*—rising off it like smoke off a snuffed candle, but with intention, with fingers—and I did the only thing my body had ever been trained to do, the thing I'd done ten thousand times in a quiet valley for quiet neighbours.

I threw the whalebone at them.

I'd like to tell you it was strategy. It was panic. But a bone that has carried three centuries of murdered names is not an empty thing, and when it left my hand the dead woman *shouted*—not words, a sound like a door breaking—and the rider's horse screamed and reared, and the ash-fingers flinched back from the spinning bone as if it were a brand of iron, and in that half-second of chaos I did the second-smartest thing of my week.

I ran.

Not along the road—*the ford*, the dead woman was screaming, *the water, the water*—and I went into the river up to my thighs with my pack held over my head like an idiot, and the cold closed around me, and behind me I heard the rider's voice say something in a language that made my teeth ache and my scars itch, a language I somehow knew and had never learned.

The water did something. I don't know what. The dead woman knew, and the river knew, and the two of them together put a wall of cold and murk between me and the bank, and when I came out the far side, soaked and sobbing and laughing in the ugly way people laugh, the rider stood on the far bank and did not follow. The horse would not enter the water. I saw that much—the animal planting its feet, the pale marks of hooves on the shingle, the rider's hooded head turning toward me with what I have decided, in my more generous moments, was respect.

"Tomorrow at noon," the paper voice called across the water, "I will be at your workshop. The Court keeps its appointments, Kestrel Almeida. Do not imagine distance will keep it."

Then the ash folded down around them, and the horse turned, and they were gone up the road toward my valley, toward my neighbours, toward my empty workshop with its note about a dog.

* * *

I walked south all that day, wrung out and dripping, and the dead woman counted for me—not names now, but miles, or something like miles, a rhythm to put one foot after the other by. *Maren Vos, drowned at the ford,* she said, and I understood, finally, why she'd been saying it all this time. She wasn't haunting me. She was *warning* me. She'd died at a ford. She was telling me where the water was kind.

Toward evening I crested a low ridge of gorse and broken wall, and looked back the way I'd come, and saw, far off, a thread of grey smoke rising from the direction of Hollowmere that was not a chimney's smoke. It rose straight and slow and wrong, the way ash rises.

I stood there until my legs shook, doing the arithmetic I'd fled to avoid. Every step south was a step my neighbours paid for. Every vial I refused would be taken from someone who couldn't refuse. The rider had said it plainly, and I'd heard it, and I'd run anyway, because the other choice was to be a kept key in a grey court while the dead stayed buried and the sky stayed empty and I stayed—safe. Contained. An Almeida in a valley, forever.

*You can't go back,* said the dead woman, not unkindly.

"I know," I said.

*You can't stay here.*

"I know that too."

*South,* she said. *There's a smith at Brackish Ford who owes the dead a debt. South, and down the water, and don't stop where the ground is pale.*

So I went south, down the water, past the pale ground, with my soaked pack and my whispering bone and the smoke of my old life standing straight behind me in the windless grey. I did not know yet what an Oathkeeper did. I did not know what the drowned altar was, or who was waiting along the road—though the dead woman, in her patient, terrible way, was already counting them too, name by name, like a guest list for a wedding no one had agreed to attend.

I only knew that I had said *no* for the first time in my life, and the world had tried to kill me for it, and I was still here, walking, and that the sky overhead was empty in a way that felt less like night and more like a held breath.

Somewhere behind me, the rider in ash kept its appointment at an empty workshop.

Somewhere ahead, the road bent down toward a river town where a drunk ward-smith was about to have a very bad evening, though neither of us knew it yet.

And somewhere overhead—caged, I would learn; caged and burning—the stars waited for me to come and let them out.
