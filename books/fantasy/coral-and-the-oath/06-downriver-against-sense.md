## 6. Downriver, Against Sense

I left Hollowmere the way you leave any place that has stopped being home: quietly, before it could change its mind about you, or you about it.

The dead woman—Maren, she'd finally told me, Maren of the Salt Roads, murdered in the year the bells cracked—insisted I go downriver. Not upriver, where the hills folded politely over themselves and a person might disappear for years among goat-farmers who asked no questions. Downriver. Toward the sea. Toward Coral.

"Downriver is where rivers go to drown," I told her, carving her name into a sliver of whalebone because my hands needed something to do besides shake. "Why would I walk toward the thing everyone else flees?"

"Because everything everyone else flees," she said, in that voice like tide over gravel, "is fleeing toward you."

I want it noted, by whoever keeps the records of poor decisions—and after last night, someone must—that I argued. I pointed out that I had never left the valley. That my boots were meant for wardstone paths and market mud, not whatever lay beyond the ridge. That the last time magic had touched me directly, it had come out of a shattered stone wearing my family's crest like an accusation. That I was, in summary, a bone-carver, not a hero, and the difference between those two professions is that only one of them gets killed on rivers.

Maren listened to all of it with the patience of someone who had been dead for three hundred years and found the living charmingly slow.

"You are an Almeida," she said. "You were born to be hunted. The only question is whether they hunt you somewhere useful."

That shut me up. It's hard to argue with your own obituary read aloud.

* * *

Packing for exile takes less time than packing for market day. That's the joke nobody tells you. I took my father's good knife—the one with the whalebone grip he'd let me carve when I was nine, badly, so that it still fits my hand like a confession. I took a week of bread that would become a brick by Thursday. I took the roll of bones I'd carved over the years: every voice I'd ever caught in them, filed and labeled in my own cramped script, because if the world was ending I was not leaving my library behind.

I did not take the shattered piece of wardstone. I stood over it a long time in the ruin of our cellar, that crest staring up at me—a kestrel wings-spread over three linked rings—and I hated it with a thoroughness that surprised me. My family. The Oathkeepers. The people who had built a valley-sized cage around their own bloodline and called it peace. Who had taught their children that the whispers in the bone were an illness to be managed, never asking why the dead spoke to us at all.

They'd known. Some great-grandmother of mine had known exactly what I was, and had chosen walls instead of answers.

So no, I didn't take the wardstone. But I did kneel down and press my thumb into the dust of it, once, the way you'd close a stranger's eyes. Somebody in my line had owed somebody an apology for a very long time. I wasn't sure which of us was which anymore.

Then I climbed out of the cellar, out of the house, out of the valley, and the wards—or what was left of them, a shimmer like heat-haze with holes in it, weeping light onto the road—let me pass without a sound. They didn't stop me. Of course they didn't. Whatever they had been built to contain had walked through them every day of its life, buying turnips, complaining about weather.

The stars were still gone. Above the ridge, where the sky opened past the ward-line, there was simply nothing. Not cloud, not dark—a nothing with texture, a black that pressed. I looked at it for as long as I could stand, which was not long.

"Downriver," Maren said, from the bone at my throat.

"Against all sense," I said.

"Yes," she agreed. "That's how the important ones go."

* * *

The river called the Liss runs out of Hollowmere through a gap in the southern hills that I had assumed, my whole life, was just scenery. It turns out it was an exit. Someone had designed my prison with a drain.

I followed it. There's a particular loneliness in walking a road that has no footprints but yours—not fresh snow empty, but *old* empty, empty on purpose, empty the way the valley itself had been emptied of anything true. The first village downstream, Tarnwick, sat shuttered and half-deserted. People peered at me through cracks. A child watched me fill my waterskin and didn't run inside until I waved, and then ran anyway.

Word travels faster than walkers. *The sky went black. The wards fell. Something got out.*

Something got out. I turned the phrase over while I ate my bread-brick by the water. It had a nice ring to it. In Tarnwick's telling I was probably eight feet tall and made of smoke. I found I minded less than I expected. Better a monster than a girl who hears ghosts; monsters get feared, girls who hear ghosts get pitied, and I had been pitied my whole life in the politest possible way—*poor Kestrel, odd Kestrel, such a talent with the carvings though, isn't it strange.* Let them have their smoke-thing. Smoke-things don't owe anyone explanations.

Except, of course, the dead. The dead collect debts like river silt.

By the third night I had learned the shape of Maren's instructions. She didn't speak in whole maps—dead minds apparently hold directions the way sieves hold water—but she gave me landmarks, one at a time, each one surfacing only when the last was behind me. *Follow the Liss until the willows grow white. Then leave the water. Then find the ford where the fish have teeth.* She said it like a woman reciting her own funeral route, which, I suppose, in a sense she was.

"The drowned altar of Coral," I said to her that night, feeding sticks to a fire I was too tired to properly fear. "Tell me what it actually is. You've had three centuries to think about it."

The firelight moved in ways firelight shouldn't, and her voice came closer than voices should. "It is where we made the bargain," Maren said. "Nine of us. Nine realms bleeding, and nine of us stupid enough to think love could be written into law." A pause, long enough that I thought she'd gone. Then: "We wrote it well. We wrote it so well it outlived its makers. That was the mistake. That was always the mistake."

"And the Ashen Court killed you for it."

"They killed us slowly," she corrected, with the mildness of the truly old grief. "First they took our names from the records. Then our names from the songs. Then—" the tide-gravel voice caught on something, "—then they took the rest. One by one. And the ninth, the youngest of us, ran. Ran and hid his heirs behind wards and let the covenant starve in the dark rather than pay what it asked."

My hands went cold around the stick I was holding. "The ninth. The Almeida."

"The Almeida," she said, gentle as a knife going in. "Yes."

I fed the stick to the fire and watched it burn and thought: *every story about my family was a wall, and I lived inside it, and I called it home.* Grief, I was learning, comes in several grades, and the worst of them isn't loss. It's finding out the thing you lost was never real.

"Keep walking, bone-carver," Maren said softly. "Grieve downstream. It carries better."

* * *

On the fifth day the willows went white.

Not pale—white, bleached, bark gone the color of bone, which even I recognized as a message aimed specifically at me. I left the river there, as instructed, striking west across country that grew steadily less polite: gorse, scree, the skeletons of farms whose owners had either fled or been fled from. Twice I saw riders on far ridgelines, and both times I did what my family had evidently done for generations.

I hid.

There is a specific shame in crouching in a gorse bush while men in ash-grey cloaks sweep the valley below you. My heart hammered. My breath came loud as bellows. And in my pocket, unbidden, my thumb found the newest carving—Maren's name in whalebone—and pressed until the whisper steadied me like a hand on the back of the neck.

"They're not hunting you yet," she murmured. "They're mapping. The Court moves slow when it thinks it owns the board."

"And when they stop thinking that?"

"Then you'll wish for these days back. Walk."

* * *

Here is what I knew of the Ashen Court, before I learned better: nothing. Hollowmere had no use for outside politics, and outside politics had no use for a valley that produced bone combs and silence. But the dead know things, the way rain knows low ground, and in the nights that followed, as I moved from landmark to landmark—*the ford where the fish have teeth; the cairn with the liar's face; the tree split by lightning that never struck it*—other voices began to surface in the older bones. Fragments. A peddler dead two hundred years muttering about grey riders collecting "tithes of light." A drowned ferryman describing a court where the throne was made of a single extinguished star, kept dark on purpose, worshipped in the dark.

They had done this, then. Or their founders had. When the stars vanished, it hadn't been catastrophe. It had been policy.

I lay under that starless sky—I was starting to hate it personally now, the way you hate a face—and felt something settle in me that I didn't have a name for yet. It wasn't courage. Courage implies choice made cleanly. This was more like a joint setting after a bad break: painful, necessary, and permanent.

They had put out the lights of the world and called it order. My family had hidden from their duty and called it protection. Between those two lies, a bone-carver with a bag of talking scraps was supposed to make a difference.

Fine. I'd been underestimated my whole life. I was very good at being more than people expected. It was, honestly, my only marketable skill.

* * *

*

Far to the north, in a hall roofed with slate the color of a closed eye, the Ashen Regent received her riders' reports the way she received everything: without hurry, because hurry was a habit of people who feared time, and she had arranged matters so that time feared her instead.

"The ward-line at Hollowmere is broken," said the rider, kneeling on ash that had once, perhaps, been a carpet. "The border stones failed on the night of the Unlighting. All of them. Simultaneously."

"All of them." The Regent turned a small object over in her fingers—a shard of glass-dark starlight, caged in iron filigree, dim as a dying coal. "Wards do not fail simultaneously, Captain. Wards fail locally, tragically, one at a time, so that the responsible party may be identified and billed. Simultaneous failure is a *release*." Her voice did not rise. It never needed to. "Someone unlocked a door that was welded shut four hundred years ago. From the inside."

"The local reports mention a bone-carver. An Almeida."

The Regent went very still, in the way of a spider feeling the far edge of its web tremble. Around her, the hall's other occupants—courtiers grey as their names, hooded acolytes who tended the Court's hoards of stolen light—drew inward without appearing to move.

"An Almeida," she repeated. "How nostalgic. The cowards bred, then. After all that effort to wall themselves off from consequence." She set the caged shard down atop a map of the realms, precisely, deliberately, over the mark where the sea met the coast. "Find them. Do not harm them—the blood decides, and we do not spill what we may need to pour. Offer them courtesy. Offer them shelter. Offer them, if they're foolish enough, a place at my side. The Oathkeepers built cages because they understood exactly what their blood could do." A thin smile, like frost forming. "I intend to be more honest with the child than its ancestors were."

"And if it runs?"

"They always run. Running is hereditary." The Regent looked down at the map, at the small inked sea, at the drowned place beneath it that every chart labeled only *here be the old vow*. "Follow them downriver, Captain. Everything worth having flows downhill eventually. Even oaths. Even children."

She picked the caged star-shard up again and held it to her ear, almost tenderly, as though listening for a heartbeat.

"Even light," she murmured, "if you keep it in the dark long enough."

* * *

*

The ford where the fish have teeth turned out to be a real place, which after a week of Maren's riddles felt like a personal insult. Greyling, the locals called the fish—flat-eyed, wide-mouthed things that snapped at anything that broke the surface, including, I can confirm, a bone-carver's fingers. I washed my face upstream of them and laughed, briefly and slightly hysterically, at the idea that my life had narrowed to following ghost-directions to a river full of biting fish.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said aloud, not even to Maren, not even to the fire: some part of me had wanted the riddles to lead nowhere. Some part of me had hoped the drowned altar of Coral was a story the dead told themselves to pass eternity, and that in a month I'd be settled in some river town carving combs and hearing only the ordinary murmur of the recently dead, and none of it—no oath, no court, no ancestral cowardice, no burden—would ever actually arrive.

But the ford was real. The white willows had been real. The cairn with the liar's face had been real, and had grinned at me with a mouth I swear I did not carve.

Which meant the rest was real too.

I stood at the crossing as the sun—it still rose, thank whatever remained merciful, a thin bruised thing behind ash-cloud, but it rose—came up over the water. Somewhere downstream, past the biting fish, past the next landmark and the next, lay an altar at the bottom of the sea, holding the corpse of a promise my family had made and then fled.

"I'm coming," I told the water, and meant it in the way you mean things when you've finally run out of alternatives and discovered, to your astonishment, that you don't miss them.

Maren stirred against my collarbone, warm as a held hand. "Downstream, bone-carver."

"Against sense."

"Mm," she said, satisfied. "Now you're learning."

The ford was shallow at its western edge, and beyond it the land rose into moorland, and beyond the moorland—according to a tinker I bribed with a carved button—there was a settlement called Brackish Ford, where the river traffic ended and the salt marsh began, and where, if a person needed ward-work done and didn't ask too many questions about by whom, a person might find someone.

I didn't know yet what waited for me there: a woman with scarred hands and a bottle and a grudge four hundred years deep. I only knew that Maren had gone quiet in a new way—expectant, the way the dead go quiet when the living are about to walk into the next part of the story.

I tightened my pack straps, checked my knife, and stepped across the water.

The fish snapped at my heels the whole way over. Even the river, it seemed, wanted a piece of the last Almeida.

It would have to wait in line.
