## 7. The Ward-Smith of Brackish Ford

Brackish Ford smelled like a fish that had died wealthy.

That was my first thought as I came down the last hill and saw the town sprawled along the river's bend: mudflats, drying racks hung with silver-bellied catch, and a haze of brine and woodsmoke that sat on the air like a guest who'd overstayed. After three days alone on the river road with nothing for company but the dead, the smell was almost a comfort. Almost.

The dead had opinions about it too.

*Salt keeps,* said the woman in the whalebone, the one who'd been talking to me since Hollowmere, since the night the sky went black and my whole life turned out to be a lie with a family crest on it. Her name, she'd finally given, was Maren. She'd been dead two hundred and eleven years, and she had the patience of someone who'd had a long time to practice. *Salt keeps, and salt remembers. That's why the ford was built here. That's why you're here, though you're too stubborn to admit you're following my directions.*

"I am not following your directions," I muttered, to the alarm of a fishwife hauling a basket past me. "I'm following the river. Rivers go where rivers go."

*And you just happen to be walking the exact road I told you, at the pace I suggested, toward the town I named.* Maren's voice lived somewhere in my skull, dry as old bone, which was fitting, since she'd been speaking through a piece of one. *You Oathkeepers were always like this. My grandmother said so, and she watched them build the wards.*

"I'm not an Oathkeeper."

*You're the last one, child. That's rather the point.*

I had the whalebone pendant—the commission I'd been carving when the world ended, a simple luck-charm for a neighbor, except the neighbor was dead now, or the valley was, or the distinction had stopped mattering—and Maren lived in it, or spoke through it, or was it. The metaphysics were above my pay grade. My pay grade had been bone-carver, population one, in a valley that kept magic out and kept me in, and it turned out my family had built the walls. Both sets of them.

The other voices were quieter. They always were, out in the open country, like whispers in a large room. It was towns that made them loud. Towns were full of ground where people had died and stayed, and Brackish Ford had been a town for a long time.

I felt them before I crossed the bridge. A low murmur under the world, like a hive behind a wall. Drowned fishers under the ford. Fevered children under the chapel floor. An old smuggler in the river mud who'd been there so long he'd achieved a kind of serenity about it. They didn't speak to me the way Maren did—they were the murmur, the many, the undifferentiated hum of the dead going about whatever the dead go about—but they noticed me. They always noticed me. Walking through a town was like walking through a room where everyone had turned to look.

I kept my head down, bought a heel of bread and a hunk of cheese with the last of the coin I'd found in my mother's strongbox, and asked the baker, casually, where a person might find a ward-smith.

The baker laughed at me.

Then she saw I wasn't joking, and her laugh curdled into something else. "Vane?" she said. "You want *Vane*?"

"Is that a no?"

"That's a *gods help you*." She leaned across her counter, lowering her voice as if the bread might repeat it. "Odile Vane. Down past the salt sheds, the house with the crooked chimney. She used to be somebody, before." A pause, weighted. "Before the exile. She'll take your coin. She'll take anyone's coin, if they let her drink half of it first. But I wouldn't sleep under her roof, and I wouldn't turn my back in her workshop, and I'd count my fingers after shaking her hand."

"Charming," I said.

"She's the only ward-smith within a hundred miles, if that's what you're asking, and the only one within three hundred who isn't dead or sworn to the Court." The baker's eyes went to the sky—ash-grey, starless, the same sky that had swallowed the stars over Hollowmere and apparently everywhere else—and her voice dropped further. "Things have been coming out of the dark since the wards went. Things that shouldn't. If you've got wards needing done, do them quick."

I thanked her and left, and Maren made a sound in my skull that was, I'm fairly sure, laughter.

*The Vane girl,* she said. *I remember a Vane. Ward-smiths all. They were the covenant's hands, back when there was a covenant. Good people. Proud people. The kind of proud that turns to spite when it's kicked.*

"Great," I said. "I'm collecting kicked, proud people. That's the whole plan, apparently."

*It's a better plan than most Oathkeepers had,* Maren said, *and I knew most of them. They're dead, and I'm dead, and you're not, so the bar is on the floor and you're still tripping over it.*

I was starting to understand why the dead had needed someone to advocate for them.

* * *

The house with the crooked chimney sat at the town's ragged edge, where the salt sheds gave up and the marsh took over. It had been a fine house once—stone footings, a real slate roof—but it had the look of something maintained by spite alone, which is to say: standing, ugly, and daring you to comment.

The chimney leaned like a drunk against a wall.

The door was open.

I knocked anyway, on the frame, because my mother had raised me polite, and look how that had turned out—her a liar, me a fugitive, politeness the only inheritance that hadn't been a trap.

"Go away," said a voice from inside. It was a good voice, low and gravelled, like a riverbed. "Unless you're the tax man, in which case go away faster."

"I'm looking for a ward-smith."

"Then you're lost, and I'm a hermit, and we're both happier for it."

"The baker sent me. Odile Vane?"

The sound that came out of the house was not a laugh. It was the noise a laugh makes when it's been left out in the weather for ten years. "That traitorous crone. I fixed her oven's heat-ward for free in the famine year and this is my thanks." A pause. "What do you want warded?"

I hesitated. This was the part I'd been turning over on the road, the part where I said it out loud to a stranger: *I'm the last heir of the Oathkeepers, the wards around my valley just shattered, and the dead are telling me to go to a drowned altar at the bottom of the sea.* Any one of those sentences would get me locked up in a sane town and worshipped in an insane one, and I didn't know which Brackish Ford was.

"I need someone who knows old wards," I said. "Covenant-era."

Silence. Then footsteps, uneven, and the door swung wider, and I got my first look at Odile Vane.

She was perhaps fifty, built like a barrel with opinions, grey-streaked hair hacked short with what looked like a fish knife. Her hands were the thing I noticed first, because I was a carver and hands were my trade's whole theology: broad, scarred, the fingertips burned and cross-hatched with fine white lines, the kind of damage you get from a lifetime of handling live ward-work. Ward-smiths, I'd learn, paid for their craft in skin.

She looked me over with the flat assessment of someone pricing a fish. "Covenant-era," she repeated. "Nobody asks for covenant-era. Nobody's asked for covenant-era in twenty years." Her eyes narrowed. "Who told you I was exiled?"

"The baker. Sort of."

"Sort of." She snorted. "Come in, then, if you're coming. Mind the threshold."

"Why? Is it warded?"

"It's *spiteful*," she said, and shut the door behind me.

* * *

The inside of the house was a workshop that had won a war against the furniture. Workbenches under every window, tools hung in rows with the obsessive precision of someone who needed the world to be orderly somewhere. Ward-frames in various states of completion. And everywhere, on every surface, bottles. Brown bottles, green bottles, a jug with a cork and a face scratched into it.

Odile Vane poured herself a cup from the jug without offering me one, which I respected.

"So," she said. "Covenant-era. Talk."

I'd planned a careful version. A version with omissions. I got as far as "My name is Kestrel, and I'm from Hollowmere," before she went still.

Still, for Odile Vane, turned out to be a very loud thing. The cup stopped halfway to her mouth. The fire popped. Somewhere in the marsh, a bird made an idiot of itself.

"Hollowmere," she said. "The valley. The warded valley." She set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way you do when your hands want to do something else. "Say your name again."

"Kestrel."

"Full name."

I could have lied. I'd had three days to get good at it, and I wasn't bad. But something about her—the burned hands, the leaning chimney, the sheer *spent* quality of her—made the lie feel like kicking a dog that had already been kicked by professionals.

"Kestrel Almeida."

The cup went over. She didn't notice. She was staring at me with an expression I couldn't read, and then I could, and I wished I couldn't, because it was hatred. Pure, distilled, vintage hatred, aged in the cask for twenty years and opened all at once, and it was pointed at me like a drawn blade.

"Almeida," she said. "You walk into my house. You knock *politely*. You say that name to my face."

"I didn't know it was my name until a week ago," I said. "I thought I was a bone-carver. Then the wards broke and I found my family's crest cut into the border-stone, and I've been having a very bad month since."

"Your family's crest." She laughed, the weathered laugh again, worse this time. "Your family *built* the wards, you mean. Your family built every ward that ever mattered, and then they built the one around themselves and left the rest of us to hold the edges of the world together with spit and scar tissue while they sat in their quiet little valley playing at being *safe*." She was on her feet now, and I was backing toward the spiteful threshold. "Do you know what an Oathkeeper was? Do you know what your ancestors *did*?"

"I know they made a covenant. I know they broke it. Or fled it. I don't know which—"

"They *abandoned* it." The words came out of her like shrapnel. "The covenant held the realms together, and the Oathkeepers were its anchors, and one night—generations back—they walked away. Every one of them. Into that valley. And the ward-smiths' order, my order, was left to patch the holes. Do you know what that work does to you?" She held up her hands, the ruined, beautiful, terrible hands. "This. This is what it does. Every ward I ever raised for the realms, I raised in the shape of the hole your family left. And when I asked *why*, when my whole generation asked why, do you know what the masters said? *The Oathkeepers have their reasons.* Twenty years I asked. Twenty years. And then I built a ward the masters didn't like—a ward that would have *shown* people where the covenant's power actually went, who was drinking from it—and they exiled me for it. For asking with my hands instead of my mouth."

The fire crackled. The bird in the marsh had found a friend. Both birds were being idiots together.

"So no," Odile Vane said, quiet now, which was worse, "I don't ward for Almeidas. Get out of my house. Mind the threshold. And take your family's name with you, because it's been rotting longer than I've been alive and it's starting to smell."

I should have gone. Every sensible bone in my body said *go*. But Maren was awake in the whalebone, and I could feel her leaning forward, the way she did when something mattered, and she said, very softly:

*Ask her about the drowned altar.*

And because my whole life had become a series of things I did against my better judgment, I said: "Coral."

Odile Vane stopped moving entirely.

"The drowned altar of Coral," I said. "I have to get there. The dead are sending me. The murdered dead—the covenant-makers, the ones who were killed when the oath broke. They've been whispering to me my whole life and I thought I was mad, and now the sky's gone black and they're giving me names and dates and directions, and every road they've pointed me down has been real so far, and this one ends at Coral." I swallowed. "I need someone who knows covenant-era wards, because I'm apparently the last person alive who can reforge the thing my ancestors ran from, and I have no idea how, and I'm scared, and you're the only ward-smith within a hundred miles, and you hate my family, and I'm sorry, but I don't have anyone else."

The silence went on long enough for me to count the bottles. Eleven within arm's reach. She had a system.

"Coral," she said at last. Her voice had changed. The hatred was still in it, but it was sharing the room with something else now. Something older. "The altar-city under the luminous sea. The place the covenant was first sworn. The place the ward-smiths' order was *founded*, before it was ever an order—before it was twelve people on a rock, swearing with an Almeida, holding hands around a fire that the first star ever lit."

She looked at her hands.

"They erased that from our teaching, near the end. That we began with them. That we were *partners*. They taught us we were servants, and servants don't ask questions, and when I asked questions they took my name out of the rolls and put me here." A short, harsh breath. "I spent twenty years telling myself the Oathkeepers were tyrants. It was easier. Tyrants you can hate. But if the dead are walking, if the sky's gone dark, if the murdered are *organized*—" She sat down heavily. "Then the story I was fed was wrong, and the story I fed myself was wrong, and I am too old and too drunk to enjoy being wrong twice."

"I'm not asking you to forgive my family."

"Good. Don't. They don't deserve it, whatever they did or didn't do. Forgiveness is for people who apologize, and dead cowards can't." She looked up at me, and her eyes were bloodshot and clear in a way that frightened me more than the hatred had. "Here's what I'm asking, Kestrel Almeida. You want my hands, you get my grudge. We go to Coral, and when we find out what your ancestors did—and we *will* find out, because the truth is the only thing at the bottom of the sea besides water—you stand there and you *hear* it. All of it. You don't flinch, you don't excuse, you don't tell me they had their reasons. You hear what your name cost my order, and my hands, and my life. And then, if there's anything left of the covenant worth reforging, I'll ward it with everything I have left. Not for you. Not for them."

"For spite," I said.

The ghost of something that had once been a smile crossed her face. "For spite," she agreed. "The best fuel there is. Burns long, burns dirty, and it doesn't care if you deserve it." She stood, and started pulling tools off the wall with the brisk violence of a woman who had made a decision and was furious about it. "Pack light. We leave with the tide."

"The tide? I thought we were going overland—"

"Overland to the coast, coast to the sea, sea to the altar-city at the bottom of it." She glanced back at me, and now the smile was fully present, and it was not a comforting smile. "You did know Coral was underwater, didn't you?"

"I," I said, "had been trying not to think about it."

*She's lying about something,* Maren murmured in my ear, quiet as a tide coming in. *Not about the spite. The spite is true as bone. But there's a piece she's holding back, Almeida. Ward-smiths always know more than they say. It's in the order's blood.*

I looked at Odile Vane, wrestling a battered travel-case out from under her bed, muttering curses at it in what I suspected was ward-cant, and I thought: *of course she is.* Everyone I'd met since the stars went out was lying about something. The dead lied by omission. The Regent's rider had lied with a smile. My own ancestors had lied so thoroughly that their lie had a valley and a family tree.

Maybe, I thought, the trick was just to pick the liars whose lies were pointed in the same direction as yours.

Outside, the ash-dark was settling over Brackish Ford, and the murmur of the town's dead rose with it, and under the murmur—faint, far off, wrong in a way I couldn't name—I heard something new.

A second voice. Not Maren's. Not the murmur's.

A man's voice, old and formal, speaking a language I didn't know, repeating one word over and over like a bell struck in another room.

And Odile Vane went still over her packing, and turned her head toward the marsh, and her face went the colour of the ash above us.

"That," she said slowly, "is a ward-song. And it hasn't been sung since the covenant broke. And someone—or *something*—is singing it *toward* us."
