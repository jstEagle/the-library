## 8. A Market Without Sky

The dead woman had said *follow the river until the river smells of smoke*, and she had not been lying, though she might have been kinder to mention that the smoke would smell like a house fire that never ended.

Three days downriver from Hollowmere, the valley's green gave way to grey fields, and the grey fields gave way to a road, and the road gave way to carts—carts full of turnips, carts full of people, one memorable cart full of live eels that I did not ask about. And above it all, where sky should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not night. A flat, matte dark, like the inside of a closed lid.

I had grown up under a roof of wards that kept out magic. These people lived under a roof of ash that kept out everything.

"Cheer up," I told myself, in the tone Odile used when she wanted me to stop feeling sorry for myself. "You're alive, ambulatory, and only moderately hunted."

The voice in my ribs—the woman's voice, Vessa Corane, murdered in the year the bells were recast, who had opinions about everything and patience about nothing—murmured something I chose to interpret as agreement.

The city announced itself first by smell: tallow, wet wool, woodsmoke layered on woodsmoke, and underneath it all a thin mineral stink like a struck flint that wouldn't stop striking. Then by sound, which surprised me. I'd expected hush. Instead: bells. Hundreds of them, small ones, tuned to different notes, hung from every eave and doorway, ringing in the wind off the river.

*Why bells?* I asked Vessa, because asking her was getting easier, which worried me more than the answer.

*So you know when someone comes near,* she said. *You can't watch the dark for strangers if there is no light to watch. So you listen instead.*

That was my introduction to Brackish-in-Ash, which everyone just called the Market, because when you take away the sun and the stars, apparently you take away everything else decorative too, including the name.

* * *

The gates were iron and open. Nobody guarded them; nobody needed to. What was I going to steal? The dark?

Inside, the city ran on lamplight the way other cities run on money. Every window glowed. Every doorpost held a lantern, and every lantern was hooded with tin shutters so its light fell in a narrow, hoarded cone. People walked through the streets inside little moving circles of brightness, like snails in shells of glow, and when two light-shells met at a corner, their owners negotiated passage with the wary courtesy of dogs meeting on a leash-line.

"Fresh," said a woman selling what appeared to be candles made of something that was definitely not wax. "First time under the sky?"

"Is that what we're calling it?"

She laughed without warmth. "Everyone calls it something. Under the Sky. The Long Dark. Ashfall." She leaned closer to her own lantern, and I saw her face properly for the first time: windburnt, competent, missing two teeth on the left side. "Newcomers always look up. Give it a week. You'll stop looking up, because looking up tells thieves where your light is."

I looked up anyway, out of spite, and saw nothing, and understood her completely.

*Ask her about lamps,* Vessa prompted. *Ask her who owns the light.*

I bought a candle—I didn't even want it; you buy things in markets, it's a reflex—and asked.

The candle-seller's face did something complicated. "Lamplighters' Guild holds most of it," she said. "Wick-rights, oil-rights. There's the Sunward Chapel too, if you've got coin or faith. They say the Radiance remembers your name if you pay enough."

"And if you don't pay?"

"Then you sit in the grey quarters, where the light is shared." She said *shared* the way you'd say *infected*. "Now move along, fresh. You're letting the cold in."

There is no door in an open street. But I moved along.

* * *

Odile found me by the fountain—or what passed for a fountain here, a stone basin filled with pale luminescent sludge that cast no shadows and comforted no one. She had a bottle in one hand and the expression of a woman conducting a lifelong argument with the entire concept of municipal governance.

"Well," she said, surveying the street, "this is grim."

"You're drunk."

"I'm *acclimatizing*. There's a difference, and the difference is dignity." She took a pull from the bottle and squinted at the hooded lanterns, and I watched something happen in her face—the ward-smith waking up under the ruin of the person. "Those are limiter-hoods. Crude ones. Someone's rationing light the way a lord rations grain." She spat, with feeling. "Magic gets walled up in one valley and light gets walled up in every city. Turns out people don't need the Court to make cages. We'll build them ourselves, out of anything."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm a barrel of laughs. Ask anyone who's stopped talking to me." She gestured at the basin of glowing sludge. "And *that* is grave-glow. Rendered from—no, actually, you don't want to know before you eat. Come on. You need to see the chapel."

"Why?"

"Because you're going to hate it, and hating things together is the foundation of every good partnership."

* * *

The Sunward Chapel occupied the center of the Market the way a wound occupies a body. It was built of pale stone that must have been brought from somewhere else—nothing here was pale—and its doors stood open onto a light so bright and so steady that my eyes watered.

Inside, no flame. No lamp. A sphere of radiance hung above the altar, about the size of a child's head, humming faintly, and beneath it perhaps two hundred people knelt with their faces turned up, drinking the light like water.

A priest in gold-embroidered grey stood at the altar, and his sermon carried to the doors:

"—and the Radiance does not forget, brothers and sisters. When the stars were extinguished by the sin of the ancients, the Radiance alone remained faithful. Hoard your light. Guard your light. For the dead walk now, beyond the walls, in the unlit places, and only the faithful may be sure of seeing them coming."

I felt Odile go still beside me.

"The dead," the priest continued, "are restless since the sky broke. They pass through the grey quarters nightly. They knock at doors. They *whisper*." He let the word hang, and I watched two hundred faces flinch in unison, a field of wheat bending under one wind. "Give generously to the Sunward, and the Radiance will keep your threshold bright against them."

*They mean you,* Vessa said, in my ribs, very quietly. *They mean me.*

I backed out of the chapel. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, because I hadn't decided to shake them.

Outside, in the hooded-lantern gloom, Odile studied me with the clinical interest she probably usually reserved for cracks in wardstone.

"So," she said. "The dead walking. That's new information, isn't it. You've gone the color of old porridge."

"They don't walk," I said. "They whisper. They've always whispered. I'm the only one who—" I stopped. Odile already knew about me; she'd known since the ford, since she'd seen the crest on the broken wardstone and looked at me like I was both artifact and archaeologist. "They're not monsters. Vessa was a woman. Someone killed her. Someone killed all of them, and they want—"

I stopped again, because the next part was the part I'd only said aloud once, to a drunk woman on a river barge, and saying it in a city where priests called the dead a plague felt like painting a target.

"They want what?" Odile asked. Not mocking. Genuinely asking.

"Restoration," I said. "Of something called the covenant. And I think I'm the last person alive with any obligation to give it to them."

Odile drank, thoughtfully. Around us, the market went on: bells ringing, light-shells negotiating, a child crying somewhere in the grey quarters because the dark between lamps was too wide to cross.

"You know what I hate about this city?" she said at last. "It's not the dark. Dark's honest. It's that they've taken one catastrophe and built an economy out of it. Light as rent. Fear as tithe. Somebody broke the sky, and a hundred little lords found ways to charge admission to the surviving." She tossed the empty bottle into the gutter, where it rang against the cobbles like a tiny, bitter bell. "My order used to say wards exist to protect the weak. Look at this place. Wards of tin and hoods and guild-fees. Protection for whoever can pay, darkness as the default state of everyone else."

*This is what the valley kept from you,* Vessa murmured. *Not danger. Scale. The world is large, Kestrel Almeida, and it has learned to eat its own.*

I looked down the long street, at the receding points of hoarded light, at the black gaps between them where people moved carefully and quickly and alone, and I thought of Hollowmere, our quiet green valley where nothing ever happened because nothing was allowed to.

I had spent my whole life thinking the wards kept the world out.

It was occurring to me, standing in that bell-ringing, lamp-hoarding, fear-tithing dark, that maybe they'd kept us from learning how the rest of you live.

* * *

We took a room at an inn called the Second Candle, which advertised itself with exactly one, and ate a stew that tasted mainly of smoke and salt. The common room was full of cart-drivers and guild-men, and their talk ran in a groove worn so deep I could have walked through it blindfolded.

"—third caravan this month gone dark on the south road—"

"—not bandits. Bandits leave the carts. Whatever it is takes the *flames*, douses them clean, like fingers pinching wicks—"

"—my cousin swears he saw one. Tall. Grey. Standing in the middle of the road where the lantern died, just *standing*, and when he blinked it was closer—"

"Drink up, Joss, you saw a shadow."

"I saw a *shadow that walked uphill*."

Odile caught my eye across the table and raised her eyebrows a fraction. I shook my head slightly. I didn't know what was on the south road. The dead I heard were old, settled into their centuries, patient as sediment. Whatever pinched out flames on dark roads was something else, or something else wearing the dead like a coat.

But around me, forty people in a lamplit room all believed, without question, that the dead were coming back. And not one of them asked *why*. Not one of them asked *what do they want*. It was enough that they should be feared, that thresholds should be lit, that tithes should flow upward to the Radiance.

I thought of Vessa, who had been murdered with her own hearth-hook by a man whose name she repeated to me every night like a prayer said backward, and who wanted, more than vengeance, more than rest, simply to be *counted*. To have her name spoken in the record of things.

Nobody in this city would ever count her. They'd only bar their doors against her kind.

I pushed my bowl away.

"Eat," Odile said. "Grief on an empty stomach makes people preachy."

"I'm not grieving."

"You're grieving. You've got the face. I invented that face." She leaned back. "Tomorrow we find your astronomer."

"We don't even know if there *is* an astronomer. 'The man who counts' —that's a rumor from a bargeman."

"Bargemen know everything. Bargemen are the gossip's guild of the water." She counted on scarred fingers. "Sef Ardent. Used to keep the observatory at Lumenhold, before. Now supposedly lives over the map-shop in the Tallow Ward and charts stars that aren't there. Which is either madness or the most useful stubbornness in the world, and I've known both."

"And you believe he can read what the bones are saying?"

"I believe a man who spends ten years mapping an empty sky knows how to read coordinates better than either of us." She stood, joints cracking like the inn's stairs. "Get some sleep, Almeida. Tomorrow we bother a scholar."

* * *

I slept badly, which is to say I listened.

Vessa talked. She always talks, but in the dark of a strange city, under a stranger's roof, with bells ringing their nervous music outside, her voice came clearer than usual, as if the ash-dark itself were a kind of silence that let the dead carry farther.

She named names. She always starts with the names—it's the litany, the thing the dead do, the roll-call that keeps them themselves. *Corane, Vessa. Aldery, Maren. Sixsmith, Tam. The Oathkeepers of the western reach, sworn at Coral, silenced in the year the bells were recast.* Eleven names tonight. Some nights it's eleven hundred.

And then, near midnight, a second voice joined hers. Faint. Wrong. Not the settled weight of the old dead but something thinner, newer, moving—

*—cold on the road, the flames go out, the flames go out, we cannot see, we cannot be seen, put out the lights, put out the lights, the lights are how they find us—*

I sat up in the black room, heart slamming.

"What," I whispered. "What finds you?"

*—put out the lights, put out the lights—*

And then, closer than any dead voice had ever been, right against the shuttered window, a sound like a breath drawn through grit, and outside, below, the little bell on the inn's eave rang once and went silent mid-peal, strangled, and the single candle in the hall beneath my door guttered out, and the dark that came in was not empty.

Vessa's voice cut through the new one like a blade through silk: *Do not light anything. Do not speak your name. They hunt by name and flame.*

I sat in the dark with my hand pressed hard over my mouth, listening to something in the street below move past the inn—slow, patient, pausing at each door as if reading it—and I understood, finally and completely, what the priest had known and charged his flock to fear.

The dead were walking. Just not the ones anyone here expected.

In the morning, when the bells woke the city and the light-shells opened like slow flowers, Odile found me awake, dressed, and packed.

"South road?" she said.

"South road," I said. "But first—the astronomer. Because whatever that was, it's heading the same direction we are, and I want to understand what my bones are saying before it catches up."
