## 9. The Astronomer Who Counted Nothing

The astronomer lived above a chandlery, in a room that smelled of tallow and old grief, and when Odile knocked on his door he answered it with a crossbow.

"Lower it," she said. "We're not Court."

"That's what the Court would say." But he lowered it, because his hands were shaking too badly to aim it at anything smaller than a barn.

He was younger than I expected — nineteen, maybe twenty, with ink stains up both forearms like he'd been caught in a rain of it, and eyes that had the particular redness of someone who had cried recently and intended to deny it forever. Behind him, through the doorway, I could see a room that had once been magnificent. Charts covered every wall, floor to ceiling, thousands of them, each one dense with the fine, patient geometry of a sky that no longer existed. Someone had drawn the heavens with the kind of love most people reserve for lovers or gods.

Someone had also drawn a line of black ash across every single chart. One clean stroke each, right through the middle, like a wound stitched shut.

"Sef Ardent?" Odile said.

"No one by that name lives here." He said it flatly, a sentence worn smooth from repetition. "The man who used to live here counted stars for a living. There are no stars. So there is no man. You've wasted a climb."

"We have whalebone," I said.

His expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a door opening somewhere deep in the house of his face. "Whalebone," he repeated.

"With writing on it. Old writing. Nobody in this city can read it, and the two people who *can* read it disagree about everything else, so we need a third opinion from someone with nothing left to lose."

Odile shot me a look that said *that was supposed to be tactful*. I'd been working on tact. It kept coming out as honesty with better posture.

Sef stepped aside and let us in.

* * *

The room, up close, was worse than magnificent. It was a mausoleum. Every chart was labeled in a small, precise hand — dates, declinations, the rising times of constellations across whole centuries — and every chart had its black stroke. On the desk sat a single candle stub, unlit, beside an astrolabe polished so often it had gone bright as a mirror even without any stars to catch in it. He'd been keeping the instrument clean for a sky that wasn't coming back. I knew that kind of devotion. I'd spent my whole life carving bones no one would ever hear.

"You're the bone-carver," Sef said, not looking at me. Looking, instead, at the wrapped bundle under my arm. Word travels strangely in a city without a sky; it moves mouth to mouth in the dark like warmth. "The one from Hollowmere. They say your valley still has light. Real light. Lamplight you don't have to ration."

"They say a lot of things about my valley."

"They say it's gone now." He finally looked up. "That the wards came down and the dark came in, same night the rest of us lost ours. Is that true?"

It was, and I hated how it sounded when he said it — like I'd brought the plague with me, wrapped in oilcloth. "The wards came down," I said. "What came after isn't the same thing as your dark. Trust me. I've seen both."

"And what are you now? Refugee? Prophet? Criminal?"

"Bone-carver," I said. "Mostly unemployed."

Something almost like a smile touched him and died of exposure. He held out his hand for the bundle.

* * *

I unwrapped the rib on his desk with more care than I'd shown anything in weeks. The dead woman's voice hummed up through the whalebone the moment my fingers touched it — that low, patient murmur, names and numbers braided together — and Sef went absolutely still.

"Do you hear that?"

"I hear it constantly," I said. "It's the reason I'm here. It's been reciting the same thing since Hollowmere: names, then numbers. Over and over. Like a lesson neither of us can quit."

"Say some of it."

I closed my eyes. The words came up through the bone and out of me half-formed, the way they always did, a language I could carry but not speak: *"Vess of the Third Lamp, who died at the turning of the year. Nine-and-ninety steps below the salt line. Seven-and-thirty degrees from the drowned spire. Mereth, who died in the water. Four-and-twenty fathoms. Eleven degrees north of—"*

"Stop." Sef's voice had changed completely. All the grief was still in it, but now it had a spine. "Stop. Say the numbers again. Just the numbers."

I said them. He grabbed a fresh chart off the pile — one of the few unstroked ones, blank vellum waiting for a sky that never arrived — and began to write, fast, muttering.

"Fathoms. Degrees. *Below* the salt line, *north of* the drowned spire." His pen scratched. "These aren't elegies. This woman — whoever she is, whatever she is — these aren't mourning verses. Bone-carver, do you know what you're carrying?"

"A very persistent ghost."

"You're carrying a map." He turned the chart around. On it, in his quick precise hand, were maybe forty points plotted against axes I only half understood — depth against bearing, bearing against distance from a landmark marked *Coral, traditional site, disputed.* "Every number she gives you is a coordinate. Every name is attached to coordinates. If I overlay all of them—"

He worked while we watched, drawing lines between the points, and I felt Odile go tense beside me in a way I'd learned to read over the past few days. She recognized the shape before I did.

"That's the drowned altar," Odile said quietly. "That's Coral. Not the way the ballads draw it — the *actual* site. My order's charts put it three leagues east of there. We've been navigating to a lie for four hundred years."

"Your order's charts were made by people who wanted it hard to find," Sef said. He was pale now, and his hands had stopped shaking. "Whoever's in the bone doesn't want it found by everyone. Only by someone who can hear her." He looked up at me, and for the first time since we'd climbed his stairs, he looked like a man instead of a ruin. "How long has she been talking to you?"

"Since before the wards fell. Weeks. Maybe longer — I thought she was just another voice. I hear a lot of voices." I said it lightly. It didn't land lightly. It never does.

"You thought the most important document in the world was noise," Sef said, "because nobody taught you how to listen."

"There wasn't exactly a curriculum."

"No," he said. "There wouldn't have been. That's rather the point, isn't it? They built a valley to keep you from ever learning what you are." Then he seemed to hear himself, and flushed, and busied himself squaring the chart on the desk. "Sorry. That was — I've had a bad season."

"Join the club," Odile said. "Dues are paid in blood, mostly."

* * *

Here is what I learned over the next six hours, sitting in a dead man's observatory while a live man refused to be dead:

Sef Ardent had spent nine years mapping the sky. Not casually — obsessively. He'd begun at eleven, a chandler's son with bad lungs and good eyes, lying on the roof counting stars because counting was the only prayer anyone had taught him that worked. By fifteen he was corresponding with scholars in three cities. By seventeen he'd published a correction to the accepted precession of the winter constellations that got him laughed at by two academies and secretly thanked by a third. His life's work was a single unified chart of the heavens, accurate to a hair, beautiful beyond argument.

And on the night the stars went out, he'd been on his roof with it finished, ready to send copies abroad in the morning.

"I checked my figures eleven times," he told us, staring at the stroked charts on his walls. "Eleven. I thought I'd made an error so catastrophic the stars themselves had left in protest. I sat up there until dawn — until what used to be dawn — and then I walked down into the city and discovered that everyone's sky had failed at once, and do you know what the criers were saying? Do you know the official explanation?"

"The Court's version," Odile said. "Divine displeasure. The covenant-sins of the old world come due."

"*Stars don't work that way,*" Sef said, and his voice cracked on it, and I understood suddenly that this was the first time he'd said it out loud to anyone. "Stars are not moral actors. Stars do not *punish*. A star going dark is a physical event with a physical cause, and if every star went dark on the same night, then either the universe ended — in which case we'd have noticed — or something is *in the way*."

"In the way," I repeated.

"Between us and them. Or around them. Containing them." He tapped the chart of Coral, with its web of coordinates. "And here's the thing that's kept me from burning this room down: the woman in your bone died *before* the stars went out. Centuries before. She's giving you bearings to a place under the sea, measured against landmarks — including, look, *look* —"

He jabbed his pen at one plotted point, then another, tracing a line upward off the edge of the chart, up toward where the sky should be.

"— including positions in the sky itself. She references stars, Kestrel. By name, by position, by relation to each other. Names I've never seen in any catalogue. And her sky matches mine. Her sky matches the sky I mapped, exactly, to within my margin of error." He looked at me with an expression I recognized from my own mirror — the look of someone whose whole world has just inverted and who is deciding to be thrilled about it instead of terrified. "She's not haunting you. She's *corresponding* with you. Across several hundred years, via whalebone, but the principle is the same."

"She's dead," I said. "The correspondence has certain limitations."

"All correspondence does. Have you ever tried to get a reply out of the Academy of Vell? At least your correspondent answers promptly." He stood, and paced — three steps, turn, three steps, the orbit of a small room. "If her sky is our sky, then the stars didn't die. Stars don't die all at once, in formation, politely. Something happened to *us*, or to them, and her coordinates are the oldest independent observation we have. Which means the answer to the worst thing that ever happened to me is at the bottom of the sea, and the key to it is a bone-carver who hears ghosts."

"Technically the key is the bone," I said.

"The bone is paper," Sef said. "You're the reader. Paper is common. Readers are not." He stopped pacing. When he turned around, he had the look of a man stepping off a ledge and discovering he'd meant to do it all along. "I'm coming with you."

Odile raised an eyebrow. "Nobody asked."

"Nobody asked you either, ward-smith, and yet here you are, guiding them for spite. I'm guiding them for spite as well. My spite is simply better educated." He was already pulling charts down off the walls, rolling them, sleeveing them in oilcloth with the brisk efficiency of a man packing his own heart for travel. "I spent nine years learning the sky and the sky vanished, and I have spent this entire season being politely encouraged to take up candle-making, which is a fine honest trade and which I would rather die than do. You are walking toward the place where my life's work might come back to life. You couldn't keep me here with chains."

"He'll slow us down," Odile said to me, in the tone of someone conducting an objection out loud so it could be properly buried.

"He reads dead languages," I said. "He just read one off a ghost. And he knows the sky the dead remember better than the dead do."

"I heard that," Sef said.

"You were meant to."

* * *

We left before the ash-shift, when the city was at its dimmest and the lamplighters' guild was making its bribed rounds. Sef carried his rolled charts in a waterproof satchel he'd clearly prepared months ago and never had the courage to use; the astrolabe rode inside his coat, against his chest, like a second heartbeat. At the top of the stairs he paused and looked back at his room — at the stroked charts, the unlit candle, the whole small museum of a finished life.

"You could sell them," Odile said. "The charts. Collectors pay stupid money for pre-darkening work."

"No," Sef said. "They're not finished. None of them are finished. That was the mistake — I thought a chart of the sky could ever be *done*." He shut the door. "They'll keep. If we're right, I'll want them to check my work."

We went down through the sleeping streets, three shadows among shadows, and I felt the whalebone humming against my ribs with that patient recitation — names, numbers, names, numbers — and for the first time it didn't sound like a burden.

It sounded like a colleague.

* * *

It was two nights later, camped in the lee of a sea-wall with Sef cross-checking the bone's coordinates against landmarks visible from shore, that the dead woman changed her tune.

Sef had just confirmed our heading — "three days along the coast, then the marshes begin, assuming her fathoms are honest fathoms" — when the whisper beneath my fingers shifted register. Slowed. Deepened, the way a choir deepens when it stops singing and starts testifying.

*Kestrel,* the bone said — my name, clear as a bell in a quiet church, the first time she'd ever used it. *Before you walk the road to Coral, you must know why the road is empty. You must know what waits there, and who killed us, and how. One of the killers bore your name.*

I must have made a sound, because both of them looked up.

"What is it?" Sef asked. "New coordinates?"

"Not exactly." I looked at the fire, at their faces lit orange against the enormous ash-black dark, and felt the weight of the rib in my hands like something alive and heavy-lidded, waking. "She wants to tell me a story. About the last time someone swore an oath at Coral." I swallowed. "And apparently my family was there."

Odile's face went carefully, professionally blank — the face of a woman who already knew part of this story and was deciding how much to confess. Sef just leaned forward, hungry for it, a scholar offered the one manuscript the world had lost.

"Tell us," he said.

The bone grew warm under my hands. Out over the black sea, where stars should have been, nothing shone — but I swear that for one moment, something far below the water did.
