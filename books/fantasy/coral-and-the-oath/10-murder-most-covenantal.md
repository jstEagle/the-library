## 10. Murder Most Covenantal

I have always thought of guilt as a splinter: small, buried, easy to ignore until you press on it. That night, pressed against Odile's fire at Brackish Ford while rain hammered the canvas awning she'd rigged over her ruined workshop, I learned that guilt can also be an ocean. You don't notice you're standing in it until someone shows you the waterline.

The dead woman—the one who had been whispering names into whalebone for weeks before the stars went out—had a name now. Sef had found it, because Sef finds everything that can be counted.

"Vessaline Ond," she said, laying out her charts on Odile's workbench, pinning them with bits of bone I recognized as my own carvings. I tried not to be offended. "Died in the Year of the Long Frost, which your valley's records won't mention because—" she tapped the page, "—your valley's records don't exist. I checked the trade registries instead. Vessaline Ond, signatory of the Coral Covenant, Keeper of the Northern Tide. Recorded dead in a shipwreck."

"Was it a shipwreck?" Odile asked from the corner, where she sat sharpening a graver with more force than the task required. She'd been drinking since noon and was at the precise stage where her hands were steadiest and her temper thinnest.

"No," said the bone in my hand.

I hadn't meant to say it out loud. The voice came up through the carved whalebone like water through chalk, and my mouth simply opened and let it through. All three of us—me, Sef, and the dog Odile kept despite claiming to hate it—went still.

"No," I repeated, because apparently once started, the dead expect a conversation. "It was not a shipwreck. It was a knife, and it was cold, and she saw the face of the man who held it, and she wants—I want—" My throat closed around the pronoun. That was always the worst part, the moment where the dead's grammar became mine. "She wants you to know his name."

Sef had her pencil ready. She was always ready. It was, I would learn, how she survived things.

* * *

Here is what the dead told me over the next three days, in fragments that arrived like slivers worked loose from skin, usually at the worst hours—mid-carving, mid-meal, mid-attempt-to-sleep:

There were thirteen makers of the Coral Covenant. Thirteen men and women who walked to the drowned altar three hundred years ago and bound the realms together with an oath written in living blood, so that magic would flow where it was needed and pool nowhere it could rot. They were farmers and tide-priests and smiths and one astronomer—Sef laughed until she cried when she learned that—and they were, by every account the dead gave me, ordinary people who agreed to carry something enormous because no one else would.

And then, one by one, they were murdered.

Not all at once. Not in a war. Over forty years, quietly, in ways that looked like accidents. A fall on stairs. A fever that burned too fast to be natural. A boat lost in a calm sea. Fire in a granary with the doors barred from outside. Vessaline Ond, Keeper of the Northern Tide, knifed on the deck of her own ship by a man whose name she spoke with her dying breath, and whose name the bone now spoke into my ear every time I closed my eyes:

*Aldric Almeida.*

My family keeps no records. My grandmother kept no stories beyond the warding songs, and even those we sang without knowing what the words meant. For twenty years I believed this was modesty, or poverty, or the plain amnesia of small places.

It was none of those things. Hollowmere wasn't a home that forgot its history. Hollowmere was a history that had been buried alive.

"Aldric Almeida," Sef said, when I finally managed to say it aloud. She wrote it down in her careful surveyor's hand, and then she looked at me the way she looks at instruments that have stopped making sense. "That's your—"

"Cousin. Ancestor. Grandmother's grandmother's people." I set the whalebone down on the workbench as if it might go off. "He was one of them. One of the makers. And he turned."

Odile stopped sharpening. In three days I had learned that Odile Vane did everything loudly except her thinking, which happened in silences long enough to hang laundry in. This silence lasted the length of ten whetstone strokes.

"Or he never turned at all," she said finally. "Maybe he signed the covenant planning to break it. Maybe he was the Court's man from the start, sitting at the altar with murder already folded up in his coat like a letter he meant to post later."

"That's worse."

"Most true things are." She picked up the graver, inspected the edge, put it down again. "You know what a founder is, girl? A founder is someone who wins early enough to write the story. The Ashen Court didn't rise after the covenant fell. Read your dates. The Court *was* the breaking. One man, forty years, thirteen knives, and then a dynasty sitting on the corpse of a promise, calling itself mercy."

"The Regent calls it mercy," Sef said quietly. "We heard her herald say it in the market. Magic hoarded starves the many."

"And your ancestor made sure there was nothing left to hoard or share." Odile looked at me, and her eyes were not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "So. The family trade isn't warding, Kestrel. It's aftermath."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat up with the whalebone in my lap and let Vessaline Ond show me everything, because I had decided—if you can call 3 a.m. arithmetic a decision—that flinching was a luxury I'd forfeited the moment Aldric's blood reached down the generations into my hands.

She showed me the deck of the *Kindly Tide*, the moon gone behind cloud, the smell of pitch and cold fish. She showed me the man's face: young, younger than I expected, with our jaw—there was no escaping it, that heavy Almeida jaw I see in every reflective window—and our habit of looking slightly away from people when speaking to them. He apologized while he did it. That was the detail that broke me, lying in the dark with my teeth clenched: *he apologized.* Said he was sorry, truly sorry, said it had to be done, said the covenant asked too much of thirteen frightened people and nothing at all of everyone else, and that unbalance like that always, always tips.

Then he knifed her anyway.

The dead do not editorialize, mostly. They show you the thing and let you bring your own weather. But when the vision released me, Vessaline's voice lingered, and it sounded less angry than tired, the way a teacher sounds with a student who has failed a test she warned him about three times.

*He was afraid,* she said. *That's all any of them were. Fear with knives. Fear with laws. Your line has been running from that night for three hundred years, carver. Running so fast they built a whole valley to hide inside.*

*I didn't build anything,* I told her.

*No. You just lived in the walls of it. There's a difference, but it's smaller than you'd like.*

Thanks, I did not say. Thank you, Vessaline, for your candor, and also please stop haunting me, I did not say. Instead I asked the question that had been sitting in my chest since the wardstone cracked and showed me my own crest.

*Why me? Why does my family hear the dead? If we were the murderers—*

*Because,* she said, with the terrible patience of the very old, *Aldric's daughter found out what her father did. Found his journal, found the names, found everything. And she swore—not to restore the covenant, she wasn't strong enough for that—but to remember it. To hold every murdered name in her head until the world was ready to hear them again. Her oath was smaller than the original, but it was made of the same material, and oaths like that soak into blood the way dye soaks into cloth. Every child born to your line since has been born holding thirteen names they cannot put down.*

*That's not a gift. That's a punishment.*

*Mm,* said Vessaline Ond. *Funny thing about those two. Give it three hundred years and even the dead stop telling them apart.*

* * *

Morning came gray through the cracks in Odile's shutters, and with it came the reckoning I had been dreading more than any vision: telling the others.

They were at the workbench when I came down, Sef annotating charts, Odile pretending to repair a ward-frame she'd sworn was scrap metal. The dog raised his head, considered me, and put it back down, which I chose to take as moral support.

"Aldric Almeida helped found the Ashen Court," I said, because Odile had taught me in one week that preamble is for cowards. "He was one of the thirteen. He killed at least four of the others himself and arranged the rest. My family aren't the heirs of the Oathkeepers. We're the heirs of the man who broke it—and the penance he ran from. Everything since, the valley, the wards, the quiet—it was all built to bury what he did."

Silence. Rain on the roof. The dog sighed theatrically.

"Well," said Odile, and set down her tools. "Now the crest makes sense."

"Is that all you have to say?"

"What would you like? Tears? I've seen worse sins from better houses, girl." But she crossed the room, took my chin in those scarred fingers, and tilted my face up to hers, and for once there was no drink in her voice at all. "Listen. I designed containment wards for a court I knew was rotten, because the pay fed my sister's kids and the lies were comfortable. When I finally understood what I'd built, I drank for six years instead of fixing it. You found out your inheritance is a massacre and within a week you're walking toward the altar to fix it. Sit down before you compare yourself unfavorably to me ever again."

"It doesn't feel like enough," I said. My voice did something embarrassing on the last word, and I let it, because I was tired of being competent. "Three hundred years, Odile. Thirteen murders. And the fix is me carving some bones and walking downhill? Shame should cost more than that."

"Shame is fuel, not payment." She let go of my chin. "Payment happens at the altar. Fuel burns now, today, in the direction you choose. So choose, and stop standing in the doorway of your own guilt admiring the view."

Sef, who had been silent through all of it, slid a chart across the bench. On it, thirteen marks arranged in a rough ring, each annotated with a date and a place.

"I plotted them," she said. "All thirteen deaths. Do you know what shape they make?"

I looked. The marks traced a slow spiral, tightening inward, each death closer than the last to—

"The drowned altar," I said.

"Each keeper died a little closer to Coral. Like something drawing a line toward it. Or clearing a path." Sef's finger traced the spiral, and her voice went very quiet, the way it did only when she was frightened and refusing to say so. "Kestrel, the Court hasn't been killing randomly for three hundred years. They've been excavating. Every murder removed one person who could stand at that altar and swear the old oath. They weren't destroying the covenant's memory." She looked up. "They were clearing the claim to it."

Odile swore, long and fluently, in two languages.

"So when the Regent's herald offered protection for my blood," I said slowly, "she wasn't threatening me. She was recruiting me. Or trying to own me. Because I'm not just the heir of the man who broke the oath." The whalebone sat on the bench between us, and I swear it was warm. "I'm the last person alive who could renew it. And so is she."

Outside, far off downriver, a sound rolled through the rain that none of us mistook for thunder: horns. Three notes, flat and deliberate, repeated twice.

Odile was moving before the second repetition ended, kicking aside the rug to reach the floor-hatch where she kept the ward-chalk she'd sworn she'd given up. "Ashen signal," she said. "Two miles, maybe three, coming up the ford road. They tracked you here faster than I hoped and slower than I feared, which means they're good." She threw a satchel at Sef, another at me. "Congratulations, carver. Your shame gets its first field test. Everybody pack like the house is on fire—because if we're still here when those horns finish their song, it will be."
