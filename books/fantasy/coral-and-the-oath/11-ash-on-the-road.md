## 11. Ash on the Road

The stranger joined us on the third morning out of Brackish Ford, and I want it on the record that none of us invited him.

He was simply there at dawn, sitting on a milestone with his pack between his knees like a man waiting for a coach that had stopped running years ago, eating an apple with the methodical devotion of the truly hungry. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with a face like a door someone had forgotten to paint — unremarkable, weathered, easy to look past. He wore the faded ochre sash of a light-warden, one of those volunteer orders that used to tend shrine-lamps along the pilgrim roads until the stars went out and gave them nothing left to tend.

"Morning," he said.

We looked at him.

"You're going south," he said. "Toward the coast road."

"We're going nowhere in particular," Odile said, which was a lie delivered with the flat conviction of a woman who had lied to tax collectors, husbands, and ward-inspectors, in roughly that order of frequency.

"Right." He finished his apple and buried the core neatly at the milestone's base, as if planting it. "Name's Tammas Reeve. I'll walk behind you at a respectful distance and mind my own business, if that suits."

"And why would you do that?" Odile asked.

Tammas considered the question honestly, which already put him ahead of most people I'd met. "Because you three are walking like people who know where they're going," he said, "and I haven't known that in eleven years. It seems restful to be near it."

Sef, who trusts everyone because they have never once been trusted themselves and don't know the warning signs, said, "That's sad."

"Sef," Odile said.

"What? It *is*."

And that was how Tammas Reeve came to walk behind us at a respectful distance for four days, minding his own business with such diligence that we forgot, mostly, that he was there. He cooked when we were too tired to. He knew which wells were poisoned and which merely tasted of regret. Once, when Odile's hands shook too badly to strike flint — she hid them in her sleeves whenever she thought we weren't watching, and we were always watching, and she knew that, and hid them anyway — Tammas struck it for her without comment, and she let him, and neither of them ever acknowledged that this had happened.

I mention all this because I want you to understand what we lost, and I can't make you understand it by telling you about his death before I've told you about his apples.

* * *

On the sixth day the road dropped out of the hills into a stretch of country called the Hollows, though every country we'd crossed since leaving my valley seemed to be named after some variety of emptiness, and I was beginning to take it personally. The Hollows were old pasture gone to scrub, stone walls collapsing into green riddles, with a single defile where the road pinched between two ridges. Any child raised on soldier stories could tell you it was a perfect place for an ambush.

"I don't like it," Sef announced, a quarter mile out.

"Neither do I," said Odile.

"Then why are we walking into it?"

"Because the alternative is walking around it, which takes two days, and because nine times in ten the dread is just breakfast disagreeing with you." Odile said this with her chin up, but I had learned to read her by then, and I saw her free hand sketching shapes against her thigh — small, quick gestures, the ghost-grammar of ward-work she could no longer properly perform. Her fingers hadn't stopped trembling since Brackish Ford. She'd told me it was the drink leaving her blood. We had both agreed to believe this.

For my part, the dead had been restless since morning.

They were always restless now — since the wards broke, since the sky emptied, since I'd learned that the whispers in my carved bone were not madness but testimony — but there was a difference between the dead's ordinary murmur and what I heard coming down into the Hollows. The ordinary murmur is like wind through a house: everywhere, sourceless, mostly grief. This was more pointed. This was a woman's voice, the same woman who'd been naming names and dates since that first night in my workshop, saying, over and over, with the patience of someone who has said a thing a very long time and expects to say it longer:

*Ash comes down the hill. Ash comes down the hill. Kestrel, ash comes down the hill.*

"You could mention," I said to her under my breath, "that you might have led with this yesterday."

She didn't answer. The dead are not big on dialogue. They deliver; reception is your problem.

I almost said something. I want you to know that. I opened my mouth in that green defile with the ridges rising on either side, and I thought: *they'll think I'm mad, or worse, they'll think I'm commanding them again,* because I'd spent six days learning exactly how much Odile Vane hated being told things by the last Oathkeeper's heir, and how much of Sef's new-minted purpose depended on our being competent, and how heavy a thing it is to say *the dead warn me of danger* and watch people decide whether to believe you.

So I hesitated.

I have made a great many mistakes since the stars went out, and I expect to make a great many more, but that hesitation is the one I carry in my chest like a swallowed stone, and I will carry it as long as the oath holds, which is to say, forever.

The ash came down the hill.

* * *

There were eight of them, though I only counted five before the fight started and never did get an accurate total, because counting becomes difficult when the world is on fire. They came off both ridges at once, in grey lacquered armor the color of a drowned sky, and where their boots touched the grass the grass went gray and crisp, as though winter were following them downhill on a leash.

"Hunters," Odile snapped, in the tone of a woman identifying a disease. "Court regulars. Down, both of you, *down*—"

Sef went down. I went down. Tammas, who owed us precisely nothing, came *forward*, and I remember thinking with a strange clarity: *that's the wrong direction, friend.*

Odile was already working. I will describe what she did, but I want to be honest that describing it does a disservice to it, the way a map does a disservice to a country. She had no proper materials — no chalk, no silver, no prepared stones, because proper ward-smiths travel with workshops and Odile traveled with a bottle and a grudge. What she had was the road itself: its boundary-stones, its old waymarks, its packed and patient earth. She knelt, drove her bare hand flat against the ground, and *pulled*. I felt the world resist, felt something structural groan, like a ship changing its mind mid-wave. Then light leapt from waymark to waymark, thin and blue and flickering, and a wall of it — less a wall than a suggestion of a wall, the memory of a wall, a wall drawn from instructions read over someone's shoulder — rose across the mouth of the defile.

An ash-hunter hit it and staggered back, armor smoking.

"It'll hold," Odile said, through her teeth. Her hands, pressed to the earth, were bleeding from six fingernails and shaking like leaves. "It'll hold maybe a minute. Maybe less. Don't quote me."

"What do we do?" Sef's voice was very high. I'd have found it funny any other day; we'd all been high-voiced lately.

"The flankers—" Tammas began, and then everything happened at once, the way it does.

Three hunters had come over the ridge instead of around, dropping into the defile behind us. One carried something like a net woven of gray smoke, and threw it, and it settled over Odile's light-wall and the wall guttered like a candle underwater. Another came straight at me — at *me*, specifically, ignoring Sef and Tammas entirely, and I understood with a lurch that this wasn't a skirmish, it was an extraction, and I was the cargo. The Court wanted my blood whole, not spilled. There's a compliment in that somewhere. I've looked for it. I haven't found it.

I did the only thing I knew how to do. I reached for the dead.

Not deliberately — I hadn't yet been taught deliberate; that lesson waited down the road with a dead knight whose name I did not yet know. I reached the way a drowning person reaches, all panic and no technique, and the dead answered the way they always answered: too much, too fast, all of them at once. A hundred voices poured through me, centuries of murdered covenant-makers, and I couldn't shape any of it, couldn't aim it, so what came out of me was a sound — not a word, not a scream, something older — and the air went cold enough to crack, and the lead hunter stopped as if he'd walked into glass.

It bought three seconds. Three seconds is nothing. Three seconds is also everything; ask any dead soldier.

In those three seconds, Tammas Reeve stepped between me and the hunter's spear.

I want to tell you he did it gracefully, or shouting some defiant last word, but the truth is smaller and worse and better. He did it the way he'd done everything: quietly, without ceremony, stepping in front of harm aimed at someone else, with an expression of mild regret, as if apologizing for the inconvenience. The spear took him under the collarbone and came out his back. He sat down. Just sat, the way a man sits down at the end of a long walk.

Odile screamed something and the light-wall detonated — she later told me she'd reversed the flow, dumped everything at once, a trick that should have killed her and only cost her the use of her hands for a week and most of the feeling in them for good. The hunters on the wall went down smoking. The one inside the defile wavered, spear still extended, staring at Tammas, at me, at the cold.

And then Sef Ardent, astronomer, counter-of-nothing, picked up a waymark stone — a boundary marker older than anyone's grandmother — and hit that hunter across the head with it hard enough to ring him like a bell.

I have never loved anyone the way I loved Sef in that moment, and I include in that statement several people I have actually been in love with.

The remaining hunters withdrew. Just — withdrew, backing up the slopes, gray figures into gray scrub, because their orders were for a living Oathkeeper, and a fight to the death against three desperate nobodies in a warded defile was not in the pay grade. That's the part that keeps me up at night, actually. Not their ferocity. Their *bookkeeping*. We were a line item. Tammas was not even that.

The silence afterward was enormous.

* * *

Tammas lived for a quarter of an hour. I know because I counted, which is what you do when there is nothing else you can possibly do — you count, because counting means time is passing toward something, even if what it's passing toward is the end of a man.

We dragged him behind the collapsed wall, out of the wind. Odile did what she could, which was nothing, and said so, which cost her more than the fight had. Sef held his hand and told him facts about stars, because Sef's entire treasury of comfort was astronomy, and the stars were gone, and it was somehow the kindest thing anyone has ever done — reciting the coordinates of vanished lights to a dying man as if promising him they'd come back.

They will come back, incidentally. But I get ahead of myself.

"Didn't even know your name," Tammas said to me. His voice was doing very little work. "Whole name. Kestrel-something."

"Kestrel Almeida."

"Almeida." He tried a smile. Blood came with it. "Knew a family of that name. Kept the shrine-lamp at Millward Cross lit thirty years past reason. Stubborn lot."

"That was us," I said, though I had no way of knowing if it was true. It felt true. Everything feels true at the edge of a death.

"Mm." He looked up at the black sky with its nothing in it, unbothered, unsurprised, a man reading familiar weather. "You're worth following. Thought you should know. Most folk aren't. It's not a compliment." A breath, wet and slow. "Comes due."

"Tammas—" I said, and then discovered I had no sentence to put after his name. I had carved the names of strangers into whalebone for twenty years and suddenly could not produce one syllable of eulogy. "You didn't owe me anything."

"No," he agreed, comfortably. "That's rather the point."

He died between one starless breath and the next, holding Sef's hand, with his face turned toward a sky that had nothing in it, and I sat in the grass of the Hollows beside a stranger who had owed me nothing and paid everything, and I understood — finally, viscerally, in the marrow the dead speak to — what the woman's voice had been trying to tell me for six days, and what it had not been trying to tell me, because no ghost could have saved him, and no ghost could have warned him, because the warning was never about ash on the hill.

The warning was about me. About what follows me. About the price other people pay for walking near the last heir of the Oathkeepers.

Ash comes down the hill. And people step in front of it.

* * *

We buried him at dusk under a cairn of waymark stones, which Odile said was technically illegal and Sef said was technically an honor and I said nothing, because I had finally learned the difference between guilt and grief, and it is this: guilt asks *what did I do*, and grief asks *what do I do now*, and only one of them has an answer.

What I did now was stand over the cairn and speak, badly, to his body and whatever listened beyond it — which, I now knew, was more than I had ever dreamed, and less comforting than I'd hoped.

"You planted an apple core at a milestone," I said. "That's — I don't know why that's the part. It's the part. You planted an apple core like it would grow, in a world where nothing grows right anymore, and then you followed three liars and a coward down a road you had no business on, and you died for it, and I am going to spend whatever I am owed making it mean something. That's not a promise to you. Promises need an oath, and oaths need an anchor, and I'm not — yet. It's a threat. To the universe. Consider yourself avenged in advance."

Sef cried. Odile put her ruined hand on my shoulder, briefly, the most expensive gesture she owned.

We walked on south in the dark, the three of us, and the road bent toward the coast, and somewhere far ahead of us, though we could not have known it, an old knight stood a post he had held for three hundred years, guarding the way to Coral, waiting for travelers worth stopping.

Behind us, faint on the night air, the dead were singing.

For the first time since I'd begun hearing them, they were singing *in tune* — and the song was a marching song, and it was in a tongue I did not yet know, and it was going the same direction we were.
