## 12. The Revenant Knight

The dead had been arguing about the road for two days.

Not the usual murmuring, either—the low tide-sound of whispers I'd learned to live inside like a hermit crab. This was actual argument, sharp and directional, the way a compass needle is directional. *Left at the split. No—left leads to the marsh-road, and the marsh-road is drowned twice over. Left.* Then a different voice, older and flatter, like a blade drawn slowly from a scabbard: *Left.*

"You could just say which left," I muttered to the whalebone shard in my pocket. Sef, walking beside me with his star charts wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle, gave me the look he gave me whenever I talked to things that weren't there—which was often, and which he'd stopped apologizing for around the time he realized his life's work was dead stars and mine was live corpses, and neither of us was in a position to judge.

"The road's arguing with you?" Odile called from ahead. She didn't slow down. Odile never slowed down; slowing down was for people whose livers hadn't declared independence.

"There's a knight," I said. "Or something that talks like one. It keeps saying *left* like it's issuing a legal decree."

"Knights are worse than ghosts," Odile said. "Ghosts only want what they lost. Knights want what they were ordered to want, forever. Worst kind of employee."

Ahead, the road did split—one branch hugging the river down toward a haze of grey salt-flat, the other climbing into hills where the fog sat thick as wet wool. The whisper sharpened to a point I could feel behind my eyes. *Left. The oath holds the high road. The low road belongs to the Court now.*

I looked at the low road. It was beautiful—flat, easy, running silver beside the water. Which is exactly why I turned left, uphill, into fog thick enough to chew, because lately every beautiful easy thing I met had turned out to be bait.

"That way," I said.

Odile squinted at me. "Because?"

"Because a dead man told me to."

"And we trust the dead man now."

"We trust the dead woman who got us out of Brackish Ford ahead of the hunters," Sef pointed out mildly. "Same management."

Odile grumbled something about being surrounded by people who took directions from the deceased, but she turned uphill. That was the thing about Odile Vane: she complained through every decision and then made it without hesitation, like a woman crossing a rope bridge while explaining at length how much she hated rope bridges.

We climbed. The fog closed around us like a held breath. And somewhere above us, on the road we had chosen, something began to walk down.

* * *

You hear a revenant before you see him, if you're me, and hearing him before you see him does not help, because what you hear is silence.

Understand: I have never heard silence. Not once, not ever, not even in Hollowmere, where magic was locked out and the dead were thin on the ground. There was always a hum, a static, the far-off murmur of voices leaking through the walls of the world. It was my whole life. It was also, I understood later, the sound of the wards straining—of the covenant's wound kept bandaged and festering at once.

So when the footsteps came down through the fog and the whispers around me went quiet—not faded, *quiet*, the way a room goes quiet when the head of the household enters—I stopped dead on the road and felt more naked than I have ever felt in my life.

Sef bumped into my back. "What—"

"Don't." I couldn't even tell him what not to do. Everything, maybe. Don't exist. Don't move. Something is coming and it has taken away the only constant of my entire miserable life and I would like a moment to grieve the voices, which is an insane sentence, I know, I've had time to sit with it.

He came out of the fog the way a ship comes out of fog, which is to say all at once, wrong in scale, too much of him. Tall—taller than any man I'd seen, though revenants don't have to obey the same logistics as the living—and armored in grey steel gone green-black at the joints, rusted like old blood. No helm. His face was a face, mostly: jaw, cheekbones, a nose broken and reset badly centuries ago. But his eyes held no light at all, and light is one of those things you don't notice missing until it's gone, like stars, like safety, like the murmur of the dead.

A sword hung at his side. Old pattern, long as I am tall. On the breastplate, worn nearly smooth by weather and grief, was a sigil: seven interlocking circles around a single point.

I knew the sigil. I'd been carving it into bone since I was nine years old, before I knew what it meant, because my hands knew it the way they knew my own name. The covenant-mark. The Oathkeepers' seal.

"Oh," said Odile, in the voice of someone watching their worst suspicion get up and pour itself a drink. "Oh, you have *got* to be joking."

The revenant stopped six paces away. Fog pooled around his boots like spilled milk. When he spoke, his voice was the flat voice from the whispers—the one that had said *left* with such legal certainty—and up close I could hear why it sounded like that. It wasn't a voice that had grown hoarse. It was a voice that had been used so long, so steadily, for so many centuries, that all the variation had been worn off it like tread from a boot.

"Travelers on the road to Coral," he said. "State your purpose or turn back."

"Purpose," Odile repeated. She had her hand on her kit, and I saw her fingers moving through ward-glyphs the way other people count rosary beads. "It speaks. Wonderful. They usually just moan."

"I'm not a *they*," the revenant said. "I am Ser Ilex of the Ninth Vigil, sworn warden of the pilgrim road, bound until the oath is restored or the world ends, whichever comes first." A pause. "I would prefer the former. The latter has been under active consideration for some time."

Sef, bless him, stepped forward. Sef is the sort of person who meets uncanny horror with curiosity and saves his terror for grant applications. "You're bound? Bound by whom? By what mechanism? Is it a geas, a ward, a—"

"A promise," Ser Ilex said, with the faintest edge, as if Sef had asked whether his mother had loved him. "Yes. State your purpose."

And here is where I should have said something clever. I am told, by Odile, frequently, that I am capable of cleverness when I stop letting the dead do my talking for me. But the silence was still pressing on my ears, this man's presence smoothing the world flat the way a heavy book flattens a flower, and in that terrible clean quiet my mouth simply opened and told the truth.

"I'm Kestrel Almeida," I said. "I hear the dead. The murdered covenant-makers send me. We're going to Coral to restore the oath."

The fog moved. Nothing else moved. Somewhere below us, distant, a bird made a small complaint about existing.

Then Ser Ilex of the Ninth Vigil did something I will remember until I die, which given recent events may be a longer interval than most people get: he knelt. The whole creaking ruin of him, armor grinding, sword laid flat across both palms, head bowed, in the middle of a fog-bound road, before a bone-carver with river mud to the knees.

"Heir of the Oathkeepers," he said. "The vigil is answered. I am yours."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about having a legendary undead knight swear fealty to you on a mountainside: it doesn't fix anything. You still have wet boots. You still have no plan worth the name. You now additionally have a companion who does not eat, does not sleep, and watches you while you sleep, which Odile informed him was "deeply unsettling" and which he received with the serene confidence of a man who has heard every possible opinion on the subject and outlived everyone who held them.

We made camp that night in the lee of a standing stone that Odile examined, pronounced "old ward-work, badly maintained, typical," and then quietly reinforced anyway, because Odile's love language is insulting infrastructure and then fixing it. Sef built a fire with the particular joy of a man who had spent three years in cities where open flame attracted attention you didn't want. And I sat across from our new knight and tried not to stare, and failed, and eventually gave up failing.

"You knew them," I said. "The covenant-makers. You remember them."

"I remember everything," Ser Ilex said. He sat with his back to the stone, sword across his knees, and the firelight did strange things in his eyeless face—lit him without illuminating him, like a portrait that refuses to be a portrait. "That is part of the binding. A warden must know what he guards. I walked the road when the covenant was fresh and the makers still breathed. I carried their letters between the realms for eleven years. I attended the last convocation at Coral." His voice never changed register—it was all one flat iron note—but something in its rhythm shifted, the way a long-held organ note changes when another pipe joins it. "I was on the northern road when they died. All of them. In a single night. And I felt the oath tear through me like a hook through the sternum, and I turned around, and by the time I reached Coral the sea had taken the altar-city and the makers were ash and rumor." He tilted his head toward me. Firelight slid off his cheekbone. "So. You are the heir."

"I'm a bone-carver," I said. "From a valley full of sheep."

"Yes," he agreed, with terrible mildness. "That is rather the point, isn't it."

I thought about the shattered wardstone in Hollowmere, my family's crest ground into it like a brand. *The Almeidas were Oathkeepers—the valley was a prison built around them.* I'd been carrying that knowledge for weeks now and it still fit badly, like a coat cut for someone taller. "My ancestors ran," I said. "They hid. They built walls to keep the duty out and raised children—" I gestured at myself, comprehensively, "—who never learned there was a war on."

"They waited," Ser Ilex said.

"Don't romanticize it. The dead don't."

"The dead," said Ser Ilex, "have been dead a very long time and have developed strong opinions about patience." He shifted, armor whispering. "Your ancestor Maren Almeida fled the anchor-burden, yes. I knew her. I held her horse while she wept and swore she was not a coward, and I believed her, because she was not a coward—she was afraid, which is a different animal that wears similar fur. Cowardice runs from duty. Fear runs from pain and can be reasoned with. She built your valley not to escape the oath but to keep the bloodline alive until someone could bear it. That is not the act of a woman who abandoned her post. That is the act of a woman buying time." He paused. "She bought four hundred years. Whether that was thrift or waste is not my judgment to make."

I didn't say anything. There was a knot in my throat the size of the wardstone. Four hundred years of sheep and quiet and my grandmother's recipe for barley soup, all of it a fortification, all of it a delay, and nobody left alive to ask whether Maren had died hoping or died ashamed.

Across the fire, Odile was pretending very hard not to listen, which meant she was listening with her whole body. Sef had actually produced charcoal and a scrap of paper and was sketching the covenant sigil from the knight's breastplate, muttering about geometry.

"Ser Ilex," Sef said, without looking up. "When the oath was whole. What did it feel like? To live under it?"

The revenant was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Revenants, I was learning, experienced time differently—they could stand at a gate for a century without noticing, and then answer a question with the precision of a clerk.

"Whole," he said at last. "You are young. You have never known the sky to be anything but wounded, so let me describe it badly. Under the covenant, magic was not a treasure to be hoarded nor a plague to be walled out. It was a *conversation*. Every realm could hear the others. The living could hear the dead—not as you hear them, carver, suffering their whispers, but properly, the way one hears music in another room. Grief was shared, and so weighed less. Joy was shared, and so weighed more." His empty eyes found the fire. "I died in the year the covenant broke. I did not notice immediately. That is how I knew it was true. The world became quieter and I mistook it for peace, and it took me three days to understand I was standing in a wound."

Nobody said anything after that. The fire cracked and spat. Above the fog, above the hills, above everything, the sky stayed black and starless, and I thought: *a wound.* All my life I'd lived inside the bandage and called it safety.

It was Odile, finally, who broke the quiet, because Odile could break the quiet at a funeral and probably had. "If you've been guarding this road since the covenant broke," she said, "you've watched the Ashen Court grow for four centuries. You know their strength. Their routes. What they'll send after us." Her eyes were narrow over the fire. "You're not just a history lesson, corpse. You're reconnaissance."

"I am whatever the heir requires," Ser Ilex said.

"She requires sleep," Odile said, jerking her chin at me. "And so do I, and the astronomer, and none of us can afford it with the Court two days behind. So: reconnaissance. Now."

And Ser Ilex—who knelt to me, who called me heir with four hundred years of dust in his mouth—turned to Odile Vane, disgraced drunk of Brackish Ford, and inclined his head with what I can only call professional respect. "Yes, warden," he said. "Though you will not like the numbers."

"I never like the numbers," Odile said, reaching for her flask. "Tell me anyway."

* * *

The numbers were bad. I won't repeat all of them—Sef wrote them down and annotated them and drew little diagrams, and looking at those diagrams made me want to lie down in a river—but the shape of it was this: the road ahead narrowed through hill country for three days, and the Ashen Court did not send lone riders after oath-heirs anymore. They sent companies. And Ser Ilex knew, because he had watched them pass for centuries, exactly which company would come, because it always came, because it was led by something that had been hunting this road as long as he had been guarding it.

"It knows me," Ser Ilex said, when Odile pressed him on it. "We have met. Eleven times."

"What happened the other eleven times?" Sef asked.

"I withdrew," said Ser Ilex, with the flat finality of a man stating a law of nature. "A warden conserves himself for the vigil. My orders were to guard the road, not to win battles. I let it pass each time and repaired what it broke."

"And this time?" Odile said.

This time, Ser Ilex looked at me. There is a particular quality to being looked at by someone with no light in their eyes—you are not seen so much as *read*, the way I read bone, layer by layer, history by history.

"This time the heir travels the road," he said. "This time the vigil has a purpose again. If the hunter comes, I will hold it." He said it the way he'd said *left*. A legal decree. An appointment already in the calendar.

"No," I said.

All three of them looked at me. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"No," I said again, and stood, because my legs wanted to. "I've had enough of people deciding for me what I'm worth. The Court thinks my blood's a key. The dead think I'm an heir. My ancestors thought I was a seed to bury in a valley and water for four hundred years. Everyone's got a use for me and nobody's asked whether I mind." I pointed at Ser Ilex, which in retrospect was not a gesture with much authority behind it, mud-spattered and shaking slightly, but he received it like a proclamation. "You don't get to schedule your death for my convenience. Nobody dies for me. Not strangers on the road, not drunk ward-smiths, not knights with four hundred years of seniority. If we fight, we fight *together*, or I turn around and go home and the sky can stay broken."

Silence. Real silence, not revenant silence.

Then Ser Ilex did the second thing I'll remember until I die. He smiled. It was a bad smile, a ruin of a smile, muscles remembering an expression the face hadn't made in centuries—but it was real, and it changed him completely, the way a lamp changes a room.

"Maren Almeida never once," he said, "in all the years I knew her, told anyone what to do with their death or their life. It is why she fled, and why I loved her—" the word landed among us like a dropped coin, "—why I followed her as far as I could." The smile settled into something steadier. "You are more hers than the valley ever admitted. Very well, carver. Together. Though I warn you: I have fought beside the living exactly once, and they complained about my eating habits."

"You don't have any eating habits," Sef said.

"Exactly. They found it unsettling."

Odile snorted, took a long pull from her flask, and capped it without drinking again—a good sign, with her, practically a toast. Sef went back to his diagrams with the small fierce pleasure of a man adding a variable he'd been missing. And I sat back down by the fire between a dead knight and a broken ward-smith and a grieving astronomer, and felt—for the first time since the stars went out—not alone, exactly. Accompanied. Audited, certainly. But accompanied.

I slept better than I had any right to. The whispers came back around midnight, once the vigil's silence eased and the world's murmur leaked in again, and among them I caught the dead woman's voice, the one who'd started all of this, calm and certain:

*He remembers the terms,* she said. *Ask him what the anchor costs. Before you swear anything. Ask him, child, before you reach the water.*

I woke with it lodged in me like a splinter, and over the cold ashes of the fire, while the others slept, I looked at Ser Ilex standing his watch against the black sky, patient as a mountain that had learned to walk, and understood that the next thing he taught me might be the last thing I wanted to learn.

"Ser Ilex," I said quietly. "Tell me about the anchor. Tell me what it cost the ones who wore it."

He turned. And in the dark, for a long moment, the old knight said nothing at all.
