## 13. Lessons in Dead Tongues

Ser Ilex's first lesson was to make me stand in a cold river at dawn and do nothing.

"You are a door," he said, "and doors do not chase their guests into the street."

"I'm fairly sure doors don't stand in rivers either."

"Doors that have been left open for thirty years," he said, with the patience of a man who had been dead long enough to find everything amusing and nothing surprising, "will say anything to be noticed. Stand."

The river ran down from the high fells toward the lowlands we'd crossed a week ago, and it was the kind of cold that made arguments feel like luxuries. I stood. The water found the gaps in my boots with the efficiency of an old creditor. Somewhere behind me, Odile was pretending not to watch while she re-soldered a ward-frame, which meant she was absolutely watching, and Sef had climbed onto a boulder with his charts spread out like a man reading letters from a lover who had stopped writing back.

"Close your eyes," Ilex said. "What do you hear?"

"Water. Odile swearing at a solder joint. Sef counting under his breath—he does that when he's worried, you know, did you know that?"

"Underneath."

I listened underneath. And there it was, as it always was: the murmur. Not words, exactly. More like the sound of a house where many people are talking in distant rooms—voices layered on voices, some sharp, some soft, all of them going on without me, patient as weather. I had heard it since I was six years old. I had learned to treat it the way you treat a bad tooth: never bite down on it directly, live around it, and tell no one.

"They're always talking," I said. "It doesn't stop. I've just gotten good at not listening, which is apparently the opposite of what you want."

"It is precisely what I want," Ilex said. "You have spent your life holding a door shut with your shoulder. Now we are going to open it one inch, on purpose, and learn the names of what stands outside." He drifted closer—I still hadn't gotten used to how he moved, like a reflection deciding to walk—and his ruined armor caught the early light. Under the river-noise, under the murmur, something leaned toward him. Recognition, maybe. The dead knew their own.

"The first thing they will teach you," he said, "is grief. Grief is loud. It is the loudest thing most of them have left. If you listen for grief alone, you will hear only grief, forever, and you will mistake it for the whole of them. So we begin with names."

"Names."

"A dead thing is not its death, Kestrel Almeida. It is a person who died. Learn the person first. Ask a name. Ask what they loved. Ask what they would have wanted for breakfast, if it comes to that." A pause, and then, dry as old parchment: "Ask nothing about how they died until you must. Curiosity about deaths is a hunger, and hungers of that kind grow teeth."

I thought of the woman in the whalebone—the one who'd started all this, naming murders and dates like a clerk reciting inventory—and felt a small, guilty flare of exactly that curiosity. She'd given me her name eventually. Maren Ostrey. She'd liked honey in her tea. She'd hated her sister, then missed her sister, then forgiven her, in that order, over three nights of whispering while I carved. She had not once asked me for anything except to write it down. *Someone should remember the order of things,* she'd said. *Someone should remember I was a person before I was a grievance.*

"All right," I said. "One inch."

"One inch."

I closed my eyes. I stopped holding the door shut with my shoulder—which turned out to be a real thing, a muscular sort of clenching behind my sternum I'd been doing so long I'd forgotten it was effort—and let the murmur come up through the water and the stone and the soles of my feet.

One inch was too much. One inch was a floodgate. A hundred voices arrived at once, all of them mid-sentence, all of them urgent, and I staggered, and the river seemed to tilt sideways, and somewhere very far away Sef was shouting my name—

A hand on my shoulder. Cold as river-stone, steady as bedrock. Ilex.

"Breathe. You opened the whole door."

"I opened one inch!"

"To you. To them, every inch is the whole sky." His grip anchored me. "Again. Smaller. Do not open the door. Put your ear against it."

That, I could almost do. Almost. It took me eleven tries and most of the morning, and by the time the sun cleared the ridge I was soaked to the thigh, shaking, and grinning like an idiot, because pressed against the wood of that door I had heard—not the flood, but one voice. One woman, faintly, humming. Just humming, some old tuneless song, content as bread rising.

"She's not sad," I said stupidly.

"No."

"She's just... singing."

"People do not stop being people," Ilex said, "when they stop being alive. That is the entire lesson. Everything else is technique."

* * *

Technique took two weeks, and it took more out of me than the ambush on the road had.

Odile set up camp in the ruins of a waystation chapel whose roof had fallen in decades ago, leaving walls that held wards better than any tent—"old worship is basically ward-work with better marketing," she said, and dared anyone to argue—and each day Ilex drilled me the way he must once have drilled squires, except his drills were patience itself.

"Say their name before you speak," he'd say. "Names are handles. Without one, you are grabbing at smoke."

"Ask; do not demand. The dead owe you nothing. Less than nothing—you are warm and they are not, and warmth is a cruelty when waved about."

"When the voices rise, count. Counting is an anchor with rope on both ends. Your friend knows this already." A nod toward Sef, who went pink and pretended to study his charts. "Four in, seven hold, eight out. The dead cannot count with you unless you let them. It is yours. Guard it."

And slowly—grudgingly, the way I learned everything—the whispers organized themselves. What had been weather became conversation. I learned to press my ear to the door instead of flinging it wide, and behind the door I found not a mob but a country: villages and roads and market-days of the dead, some clustered around their grievances, some simply going on with whatever they had been going on with when they died, because eternity is long and even grief gets boring.

Maren came easiest. She was always nearest the door, as if she'd appointed herself my correspondent.

*You're getting better,* she said one evening, while I sat sharpening a carving knife by the fire and pretending to myself I wasn't listening. *Your edges are cleaner now. When you reach for us it doesn't feel like being dragged.*

"That was never the intent."

*I know. Intent isn't the same as effect. Ask your knight.* A pause, and her tone shifted into something wryer, sharper—the voice of a woman who had run a household and a smuggling operation and seen no contradiction. *He remembers what it was like on the other side of someone else's gift.*

I looked across the fire at Ilex. He sat apart, as he always did—not from dislike, but because revenants radiate a chill that makes sleep difficult for the living, and he was courteous enough to keep his death at arm's length. His helm rested in his lap. He didn't wear it anymore around us; I'd seen the face beneath it once, by accident, and understood why he kept it covered: it wasn't horrifying. It was young. Death had taken him at perhaps twenty-five and declined to age him further, and there was something unbearably sad about a man frozen at the age when he'd known least.

"Maren says you remember being on the other side of someone's gift," I said.

His hand stilled on the whetstone he didn't need. "She would know. She was there."

"At Coral? Before—"

"Before." The word dropped like a stone in a well, and I listened to it fall. "The Oathkeepers were not cruel, Kestrel. That is the trap of it. They were kind, and tired, and certain, and every one of those virtues curdles if you carry it far enough. There was a woman among them—a bone-carver, as it happens—who could hear the dying from three realms away. She heard everyone. All the time. She called it her gift and her order called it their treasure, and no one, not once, asked whether she wished to put it down." He looked into the fire with eyes that reflected none of it. "They built her a beautiful room with no doors and told her it was an honor. I carried messages to that room. I was proud to. I was twenty-three and I thought duty was a ladder."

"What happened to her?"

"You know what happened to her. You have her hands."

I looked down at my own hands—carver's hands, scarred, competent, mine—and felt the ground shift under the question of whose they'd always been.

* * *

On the ninth day of lessons, the dead taught me the covenant's true terms, and I learned that everything I thought I knew about my inheritance was a polite fiction.

It happened without ceremony. Ilex had me practice "deep listening"—his term for pressing through the first layers of murmur into the older strata beneath, where the voices got quieter and heavier, like descending through water into silt. Most of what lived down there was fragmentary: half-names, half-songs, the sediment of centuries. But the covenant-makers had been bound into the land itself, and land holds a shape the way bone does.

I went down through the murmur, past Maren's layer, past the older dead, past voices in languages Sef would have traded a finger to record, and reached the bottom stratum—and there the fragments weren't fragments at all. They were pillars. Seven of them. And they were waiting for me as though I were expected.

*Terms,* said a voice like frost forming on glass. *You have come for terms.*

"I've come to learn," I said—or shaped saying, because down there my mouth was a formality.

*Then learn. The Oath was not a spell. Spells end. The Oath was an agreement, and agreements have prices, and the price of ours was written in living flesh.* Images came with the words, the way meaning comes with pain: a sky full of stars, each star a knot tied between realms; magic flowing along those knots like sap in a tree, feeding everything evenly, the way rain falls on the just and unjust alike; and at the center of the web, one living heart doing the counting.

*The Anchor,* the voice said. *One mortal, freely sworn, who holds the tally of every realm's joy and every realm's ache. Not ruling. Feeling. All of it. Forever, or until they find a successor willing to take the weight from them.*

"How many have held it?"

*Three. The first, Veyra, held it nine hundred years and went into it gladly and came out of it mad, and was mercy-killed by her own daughter. The second lasted four hundred and walked into the sea at Coral rather than pass the weight to her children. The third—*

The voice paused. Down in the silt, seven pillars of attention all bent toward me at once, and I understood with a lurch that I already knew the answer.

*The third fled,* the voice said. *Fled the altar on the night of the swearing. Her order chased her to a green valley behind the hills and sealed her there—not to punish her, child. To protect the realms from what happens when an anchor breaks mid-oath. And they sealed her descendants after her, generation upon generation, because blood remembers duty, and they feared the duty would remember blood.*

The wards. Hollowmere. My whole life—the quiet valley, the missing stars beyond the ridge, the mother who went pale whenever I mentioned hearing voices, the family trade in bones and silence—all of it, a cage built around a cowardice, maintained by people who loved us enough to lock us in.

*And now the wards are broken,* the voice said, gently, terribly, *and the blood has walked out on its own feet, and come looking for the terms. So hear them plainly, Kestrel Almeida: we do not ask you to save us. We ask you to hold us. Every death in every realm, arriving in you like weather, forever. We ask because someone must, and because you can, and because no one else has ever been able to choose it freely—that is new, that has never happened, that is why the stars went dark rather than go out entirely. They are waiting for a yes that no one forced.*

I came back up through the layers like a diver surfacing too fast, gasping, and found Ilex crouched beside me with both cold hands on my shoulders and Sef hovering with the counted rhythm ready and Odile gripping a warding rod like a club.

"How much did you hear?" I managed.

"Nothing," Sef said. "You didn't speak. You were under maybe ten minutes. You cried the whole time, though. Silently. It was—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It was awful to watch."

I laughed, which surprised all three of us. "Good news. It gets worse."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell them, sitting by the fire that night while Odile cooked something involving eels and optimism:

Down in the deep strata, after the terms, after the pillars withdrew, one other presence had remained. Small. Bright. It hadn't been there before—it had no place in the geology of the dead—and it spoke in a voice like a struck bell wrapped in velvet, and it said only this:

*You could also say no, little carver. And I know another way. A kinder way. No weight. No weather. No voices at all—ever again. Silence, clean and permanent, and your life returned to you like a coat you'd lent out. Wouldn't that be rest?*

I told myself it was residue. Echo. Some trick of the deep listening, the mind making shapes out of pressure the way it makes faces out of shadows.

I told myself that twice.

The third time, I didn't tell myself anything. I just lay awake in the ruined chapel, listening to Odile's snores and Sef's counted breathing and the faint, patient hum of the dead beyond the walls, and I let myself imagine it—true silence, a whole life with the door shut and locked from my side—and the imagining was sweet, and that sweetness scared me more than the Ashen Court ever had.

Because power, I was beginning to understand, doesn't announce itself with thunder. It comes to you quietly, at night, wearing your own longing like a borrowed coat, and it asks for nothing.

Yet.

In the morning, Ilex woke me before dawn for the next lesson, and his face beneath the pushed-back hood was graver than I'd ever seen it.

"Something spoke to you last night," he said. Not a question. "Below the pillars. Something that was not there before."

The river-cold of his presence raised gooseflesh along my arms. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because I am dead," said Ser Ilex, "and last night, for the first time in three hundred years, I heard the stars whisper back."
