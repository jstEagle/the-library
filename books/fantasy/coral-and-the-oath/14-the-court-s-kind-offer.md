## 14. The Court's Kind Offer

I have been called many things in the years since the stars went out. Tyrant, mostly. Occasionally monster, when the speaker was feeling imaginative and far from my soldiers. Once, memorably, "that gray bitch in Lumenhold," by a smuggler who did not know I spoke his dialect, and whose tongue I let him keep because it was such a fine turn of phrase.

But no one has ever called me kind, which is a pity, because kindness is precisely what I have been offering this whole time.

Let me explain myself. I am told that is what villains do—explain themselves at length to people who are about to die or have already stopped listening. I would rather do it here, in the quiet of my study, where no one can interrupt with screaming. Consider this chapter a confession without a confessor.

* * *

My name before the Regency was Vesna Marrow. You will not find it in any record now; I burned the records, along with everything else that remembered me as a person rather than an office. But I was born in the Weeping Marches, third daughter of a barley farmer, and if you want to understand the Ashen Court you must first understand what it is like to be third daughter of a barley farmer in a land where magic exists and is not for you.

In the Marches, the old covenant had teeth. That was its whole design, after all—the Oathkeepers bound the realms together, and binding means someone holds the rope while someone else dangles from it. The covenant-makers sat at their drowned altar in Coral and felt everything: every blight in every field, every fever in every village, every grief from the salt coast to the high passes. They were supposed to feel all of it so the realms could be balanced against each other. Fairness through shared suffering. A beautiful idea. I have wept over its beauty, genuinely, more than once.

And then I grew up and noticed who was doing the balancing.

The Oathkeepers' line thinned over the centuries, as noble lines do—they married inward, hoarded their gift like misers hoard coin, until the great feeling became a family heirloom kept in a locked room. The last of them retreated into a valley behind wards thick enough to smother a candle. Do you see? The people charged with feeling the world's pain built themselves a box where they could feel nothing at all, and called it duty. The rope went slack, and everyone dangling pretended not to notice, because noticing meant admitting there had never been anyone holding the other end.

Meanwhile, the rest of us lived under the arrangement anyway. Magic pooled in certain bloodlines, certain orders, certain towers. The ward-smiths sold protection by the season, priced beyond a farmer's reach. The astronomers charted fates written in stars that had never once consulted a barley harvest. And when the blight came to the Marches—and it came twice in my childhood, once to our fields, once to my sisters—I learned the covenant's true terms: the many starve slowly so the few may hoard the means of mercy.

My eldest sister died of the second blight. Her name was Ilka. She was eleven. She liked to catch frogs and put them in my shoes. I do not tell you this to make you love her; I tell you because she was real, and because the histories the Court burns always forget the real ones. She died in a house full of prayers to powers that existed, that demonstrably existed, that could have saved her—and did not, because her family had nothing the powers wanted.

I was fourteen when I decided that was obscene.

* * *

You will notice that I am not telling you I was wrong.

This is the part where a better villain might falter, might admit some flaw, some doubt, some wound that explains but does not excuse. I have read those stories too. I find them self-indulgent. Doubt is a luxury of people whose sisters lived.

Here is what I know. When the last Oathkeeper fled into Hollowmere, the covenant did not break—it rotted. It went on existing as a shape, a habit, a set of privileges defended by men with swords, long after the heart of it had died. For two hundred years the realms paid tithes to a corpse. The ward-smiths grew rich on fear of a collapse they themselves maintained. The star-priests promised intercession from beings that had stopped listening when their anchor stopped feeling. Everything above the common level of life was held up by scaffolding around an empty throne.

Breaking it was not cruelty. Breaking it was triage.

The night the stars went out, my people and I did not extinguish anything. Understand this clearly, whoever finds these pages: the stars were already caged. They had been caged for generations inside Lumenhold's vaults and a dozen private reliquaries—hoarded light, drawn down through loopholes in the old covenant by families who wanted immortality on tap. What we did was simpler. We broke the locks on the cage doors and left them shut, and we took the keys.

Yes. We took the light for ourselves. I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not apologize in advance for what I am about to justify, because I intend to justify it completely.

* * *

Come walk with me through Lumenhold. I do this sometimes—walk the ash-dark streets incognito, hood up, listening. A ruler who only hears flattery hears nothing.

The city under the darkened sky is not the horror your ballads describe. Come to the Lamplighters' Quarter at dusk, when four thousand souls share the glow of one licensed flame and divide the hours of it by lottery, fair as anything ever devised by desperate people. Come to the sun-cults at dawn, where farmers' widows chant to a memory of warmth and mean by it something kinder than any theology: *we are still here, we still gather.* Come to the markets, where a candle-end trades at a price that would embarrass you and is nonetheless spent freely on the sick and the newborn, because darkness has taught these people a economy of care that starlight never did.

Is it hard? Yes. Of course it is hard. Children born since the vanishing ask questions that crack their parents open. Old women die asking the sky why. I carry every one of those deaths in a ledger I keep myself, in my own hand, because a ruler who outsources guilt has already lost the argument with herself.

But hardness honestly distributed is not cruelty. It is arithmetic. Under the old covenant, the suffering was concentrated exactly where power said it should be—in the Marches, in the mud, in the houses without wards. Now it falls on everyone, and because it falls on everyone, everyone is finally in the same conversation. I have watched nobles trade rings for bread. I have watched ward-smiths' daughters teach farmers' sons the letters. The dark does not care what your grandfather bought. The dark is the first honest magistrate these realms have ever had.

That is the philosophy. Hoarded magic starves the many; broken hoards feed them—or at least make them equals in hunger, which is the beginning of every justice worth the name. The Ashen Court does not want worship. We want the end of exception. No bloodlines above the law of winter, no altars that answer only to the well-born, no valley in the world where one family sleeps soundly behind wards bought with everyone else's silence.

And then, of course, there is Kestrel Almeida.

* * *

I knew of the child years before the wards fell. One does not run a revolution for two decades without learning where the loose threads lie.

An Almeida survived. Living in the prison-valley itself, no less—our own design, though I confess the valley predates me; Hollowmere's walls were raised by the founders, by the very people who murdered the covenant-makers, and I inherited the crime along with the cause. The last heir of the Oathkeepers, hidden among bone-carvers, hearing voices. I have reports going back to their eighth year. Carvings that whispered. Neighbors who found them unsettling. A child alone in a town of the living, keeping company with the dead.

Do you understand what I felt, reading those reports? Not fear. Recognition.

Because I know what it is to hear what others cannot. I heard the Marches starving when the courts heard music. I heard my sister coughing when the priests heard hymns. There is a particular loneliness in being the only one listening to a true thing, and I would not wish it on anyone—which is, perhaps, why I have wished it on exactly one person, and am now writing the sentence that explains why.

Kestrel Almeida is not my enemy. Let me be precise, because precision is the one honesty left to people in my position. Kestrel is my mirror.

They hear the dead. So do I—the dead of the Marches, dead of the blights, dead of every famine the old covenant shrugged at. The difference is that their dead speak in names and dates and demands for restoration, and mine speak in ledger entries, and I have chosen the ledger because ledgers can be acted upon tonight, whereas restoration is a dream you can drown in.

They were born into the family that broke the world and hid from the consequences. I was born into the family the consequences ate. Between us stands everything the old order built: the wards, the tithes, the drowned altar with its terrible price—a living anchor, feeling every realm's pain forever, alone.

Alone. That is the word the whole covenant turns on. The original terms required one soul to hold the weight so the rest could be free of it. One soul, in perpetuity. My sister Ilka died of a blight the anchored Oathkeepers felt and declined to relieve, and you will forgive me if I decline to call that system sacred merely because it is ancient.

So yes. I sent the Rider with an offer, and the offer was refused, and I sent hunters, and hunters are never gentle, and I regret the manner of these things the way one regrets weather. But the offer itself was sincere, and I will make it again, and again, because unlike every lord and priest before me I am willing to say the quiet part aloud:

We do not need an anchor. We never needed an anchor. The idea that one person must suffer for the many is not holiness. It is the oldest scam in history, dressed in vestments, and I have come to burn the vestments off.

If Kestrel joins me, they need feel nothing but their own share. If Kestrel opposes me, they will be asked—no, compelled—to become the new anchor, the sole sufferer, the sacred solitary, and everyone who loves them will call it noble right up until they watch it hollow the child out from the inside. I have seen the hollowing. I grew up downstream of it.

Which fate is kind? You tell me. You are reading the words of a tyrant, and I am asking you sincerely: which of us is offering the child a cage?

* * *

There are days I almost believe myself. I want to be honest about that too, since honesty is apparently my one virtue this evening.

Almost. Because some nights I stand at my window above the vaults, where the caged stars burn in their thousands behind glass and lead and ward-iron—enough light to end the dark tomorrow, if I chose—and I do not choose. I tell myself the realm is not ready. I tell myself distribution requires administration, and administration requires time, and time requires the light stay locked a little longer. I tell myself Ilka would understand.

Ilka was eleven. Ilka caught frogs. Ilka would have taken a hammer to those vault doors before breakfast and laughed doing it.

Some nights I hear her voice the way Kestrel hears their dead—not as a haunting but as an audit. *You have become the thing you burned,* she says, or something with that shape. And I answer, as I answer here: someone must hold the keys until no one needs keys. Someone must be the tyrant until tyranny becomes impossible. It is the anchor's logic, exactly, turned inside out—one sufferer, holding the weight, so the many may be free.

Do you see it yet? Do you see why I need the Almeida?

Not for their blood, though the blood matters. Not for the crest, though crests open doors. I need them because if I am to argue that no single soul should bear the world's pain alone, then I cannot bear mine alone either, and I have been bearing it alone for thirty years, and I am so tired, and tiredness in a ruler is how massacres start.

The Rider reported back yesterday from the borderlands. The child refused the offer—of course they did; refusal is the family disease—but they did not refuse alone. A disgraced ward-smith walks beside them, one of Vane's line, which stings more than I expected; Odile Vane helped build the very cages I inherited. An astronomer too, young Sef Ardent, whose life's work the dark destroyed and who follows the Almeida anyway, counting something in those whisper-bones. Counting. As if the universe were a problem with an answer.

And there is a fourth report, this one stranger. Soldiers on the road to Coral swear the old knight-statues have been leaving their plinths. Something revenant walks the pilgrimage way again, in armor three centuries out of fashion, guarding the drowned road. Ser Ilex, if the heraldry is to be believed. Which means the dead are not whispering anymore.

The dead are organizing.

I set down my pen and looked at that sentence for a long while. The dead are organizing. Two hundred years of scattered grief, and now a direction, a destination, a living heir walking toward Coral with a ward-smith and a counter of stars. If they reach the altar, they will be offered the old terms—the anchor, the solitude, the beautiful obscene bargain—and I know, I know with the certainty of a woman who has audited her own heart for three decades, that the child will be tempted to say yes. Noble children always say yes. That is what makes them useful, and what makes them food.

Unless someone arrives first with different terms.

I rang for my Rider. I gave her new instructions and a letter sealed in gray wax, and I told her: offer the girl everything, and if she refuses, offer the ones she loves instead. Everyone has a door. Find theirs.

Then I went to the window and stood a long time in the dark, listening to my sister count my failures, and outside, far to the south, the horizon did its strange faint shimmering that the astronomers insist is nothing, that Sef Ardent would know in a heartbeat is everything—

The stars are stirring in their cage. Someone is picking the lock.

I had best get to Coral before hope does. Hope is the one enemy I have never learned to fight.
