## 15. Odile's Confession

The trouble with trust, I've decided, is that it doesn't announce itself when it breaks. It doesn't shatter like the wards did over Hollowmere, all noise and falling light. It goes out quietly, the way a candle gutters when someone walks past the doorway too fast, and you're left sitting in a room you thought was lit, wondering when the dark got in.

We were three days out of the ash-dark market city, camped in the lee of a ruined tollhouse that some forgotten king had built to squeeze coin from travelers who no longer existed. Sef had a fire going small and disciplined, the way they did everything. The starlight shard sat wrapped in three layers of cloth at the bottom of my pack, humming to itself like a wasp in a jar. And Odile Vane was re-cutting the ward-lines on my coat hem for the fourth time since morning, muttering measurements under her breath, her scarred fingers moving with the particular violence of a woman who loved her work and hated everything else.

"You're doing it wrong," she said, without looking up.

"I'm sitting still."

"Exactly. You sit like someone being watched. Relax your shoulders or the wards read your tension as a breach and I'll be re-inking this seam every hour until we reach the coast."

I relaxed my shoulders. Mostly. It's hard to relax around a woman who has spent two weeks teaching you to survive while drinking from a flask she pretends is medicinal, and harder still when you've started—without deciding to—to think of her as the closest thing to family you have left.

That, of course, is precisely when the floor gives way.

* * *

It began with the dead, as most of my worst evenings do.

They had been quiet since the market city—a mercy I hadn't trusted, because the dead are never quiet out of kindness. They go silent the way hunters go silent. That night, as the fire burned down to orange teeth, one voice rose through the others like a single thread pulled from a knot: the woman from whalebone, the one who had started all of this, whose names and dates had dragged me out of the valley and into the world's teeth.

She didn't give me a name this time. She gave me a place.

*Brackish Ford,* she said. *Ask her about Brackish Ford.*

"Kestrel." Sef's voice, careful. "You've gone white as your own carvings."

"The dead want me to ask Odile something."

Odile's flask stopped halfway to her mouth. In the firelight I watched her face do something complicated—not guilt exactly, not yet, but the thing that comes before guilt, the way thunder comes before rain. "What thing?"

"They won't say. Just—" I swallowed. "Brackish Ford."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire and the wind worrying the tollhouse roof. Then Odile set the flask down on a stone with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a lock turning.

"Well," she said. "I'd hoped to die before telling you this, but apparently the dead keep worse books than any tax collector." She laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "Right. Brackish Ford. Where I earned my exile."

Sef leaned forward. "Odile—"

"No. Let me say it badly and quickly, because if I take my time I'll dress it up, and Kestrel deserves it plain." She turned to face me fully, and I saw that her hands—the hands I had watched mend wards and break curses and hold my arm steady through the marshes—were shaking. "You asked me once why I left the Ward-Smiths' Guild. I told you it was over a disagreement about methods. That was true the way a knife is true. It cuts, but it isn't the whole shape of the thing."

"Odile," I said again, though I don't know what I meant to follow it with.

"Twenty-two years ago," she said, "the Guild was commissioned to build a ward. A great one. The kind they hadn't raised since the old covenant fell. Sealed valley, triple lattice, anchored to a bloodline crest so it would renew itself generation after generation without a smith ever having to touch it." Her eyes came up to mine, and I felt the world begin, very slowly, to tilt. "A self-sustaining prison, Kestrel. We were told it would contain a weapon. The last weapon of the Oath War. Something that could unmake realms if it ever woke."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, an owl asked its stupid question and got no answer.

"We were told," Odile went on, quieter now, "that the bloodline anchoring it was extinct. That the crest was a dead sigil, kept on the stone out of tradition. That no living person stood inside those wards." She drew a breath that seemed to cost her. "And I believed it. Gods help me, I wanted to believe it, because the commission paid for my mother's care and because the Guild master swore it on his oath-cord, and because it is so much easier to carve a cage when you tell yourself there's nothing inside but danger."

My mouth was very dry. "The Almeida crest," I said. My own voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing far away. "On the wardstone. Under our valley."

"On the wardstone I helped design," Odile said. "Third lattice, eastern anchor. My lines. My hand. I drew the geometry that held your family inside Hollowmere for six generations, and I did it believing I was keeping a sword from falling into the wrong hands, and then I found out what the sword actually was, and by then—" She stopped. Started again. "By then I'd already been cast out. Not for the ward. For asking questions about it. Do you see the joke? They exiled me for the one honest thing I ever did, and let me keep the guilt for free."

* * *

I don't remember standing up. But I was standing, and the fire was between us, and Sef had risen too with their hands half-raised in that way people do when they're trying to be a wall made of nothing.

"You knew," I said. "In Brackish Ford. When you saw my crest on the bone tools. When you recognized the name Almeida. You knew what my family was, and what your wards had done to them, and you—" My voice cracked and I hated it, hated the crack, hated how young it made me sound. "You looked at me and you saw your own crime walking around wearing a coat you'd warded. And you said nothing."

"Yes," Odile said. No flinch, no excuse. Just the word, laid down like a card she couldn't take back.

"For weeks. Through the marshes. Through the ambush where Tam died—for the stranger who owed us nothing, who died because he chose to follow *you*, following *me*—" I was shaking now, and the shard in my pack had begun to hum louder, feeding on the heat in me the way it fed on everything. "Every lesson. Every ward-line you cut into my clothes. Every time you called me 'carver' instead of my name because you couldn't bear to say Almeida out loud. Was that penance? Was I your penance?"

"If I'd told you in Brackish Ford," she said, "you'd have turned around. You'd have gone back into the valley, back behind the walls, because that's what the walls were *for*—and the dead would have gone on screaming, and the sky would have stayed black, and the Court would have won by default. So yes. I used you. I aimed you at Coral like an arrow, because an arrow doesn't need to forgive the bow." Her jaw tightened. "I told myself I'd confess when we arrived. When it wouldn't matter anymore. Which is what cowards call honesty."

"That's not—" Sef began.

"It is," Odile said flatly. "Don't soften it, astronomer. I've spent twenty years softening it and look where it got us."

I should have said something then. Some sentence that would either end it or save it. Instead what came out of me was the smallest, meanest truth I owned:

"My grandmother died behind your lattice."

Odile closed her eyes. "I know."

"She carved bones too. Did you know that? She heard them whisper and everyone in the valley thought she was touched, and she never once—she never got to know *why*. She died thinking she was broken, and she wasn't, she was *imprisoned*, and you drew the lines that—"

"I know," Odile said again, and opened her eyes, and they were wet and furious and utterly undefended. "Every line I ever cut, I can show you where my hand went. Every one. I have redrawn them in my head ten thousand times trying to find the flaw that would have sprung the seal, and there isn't one, because I'm *good*, Kestrel. That's the whole rot of it. I was good at my work, and my work was a cell, and your grandmother lived and died inside my competence."

The shard hummed. The fire sank. And I stood there hating her, and hating that underneath the hate was something worse: understanding. Because I knew what it was to hear voices and wish you could carve them out of your skull. I knew what it was to be handed a truth about yourself you'd have given anything not to carry. If our places had been turned—if I'd drawn the lines and she'd worn the crest—I could not swear, even now, that I'd have done better.

Understanding, I have learned, is not the same as forgiveness. But it lives next door, and it borrows things.

"I need to walk," I said, which was not forgiveness, and was not ending it either. It was just the only sentence I could carry.

Nobody followed me. That, at least, was a mercy somebody in this company still understood.

* * *

I walked the perimeter of the ruin until the moon—starless, moonless, the whole sky a held breath—gave up pretending to be anything but dark. The dead murmured at the edges of me, not pushing. Even they seemed to understand that tonight was not a night for demands.

The woman from whalebone came anyway. Of course she did.

*You are angry,* she observed.

"She caged my family."

*She caged us too, in a manner of speaking. Every year the wards held, the covenant stayed broken, and every year the covenant stayed broken, we stayed murdered.* A pause, and then, with something almost like gentleness: *We do not ask you to forgive her, anchor. We ask you to use her. Forgiveness is yours to give or withhold when the oath is sworn. Her hands, however, are needed tomorrow.*

"Tomorrow?"

*The road bends east through the salt flats. There is a gate there—an old one, Guild-made, sealed with the same lattice-work she designed. It bars the pilgrims' path to Coral. It has barred it for two hundred years.* The dead woman's voice took on the dry edge of someone enjoying a joke at the universe's expense. *No one alive knows how to open it. Except, perhaps, the woman who helped invent the lock.*

I stood in the dark a long time after she faded, turning that over. The dead had a talent, I was learning, for making my grudges inconvenient.

When I came back to the fire, Sef was awake, sitting cross-legged with their star charts spread across their knees despite the fact that there was not a single star left to chart. Comfort reading, they'd once called it. Grief with numbers in it.

"She's packing," Sef said quietly, nodding toward the tollhouse. "Quietly. She thinks if she leaves before dawn it'll spare you the awkwardness."

"Of course she does. She'd turn her own execution into a courtesy."

"Kestrel." Sef folded their charts with exaggerated care, buying time the way they always did. "I'm not going to tell you how to feel. Nobody gets to tell you how to feel about this, least of all me—I lost my life's work to the dark sky, not my family to a ward. But I'll tell you what I know as a person who counts things." They looked up. "She could have run in Brackish Ford. She could have run at the ambush, or in the marshes, or any night since. Instead she's spent three weeks cutting protection into your hem and teaching you everything she knows, including, tonight, the thing that could make you leave her. That's not what innocence looks like. But it might be what repentance looks like. I've studied the stars my whole life. I know the difference between a light that was never extinguished and one that's fighting its way back."

I said nothing. Sef, wisely, didn't require me to.

* * *

I found Odile at the eastern arch of the tollhouse, lashing her bedroll with the quick, angry knots of a woman who had done this leaving many times and expected it to hurt every time. Her pack was already shouldered. She didn't hear me until my boot scraped stone, and then she went very still, the way you do when you've been caught at something and can't decide whether to fight or apologize.

"I'm not doing this to spite you," she said. "I want that on the record. I'm doing it because every mile I walk beside you is a mile you spend remembering what I am, and you need your strength for Coral, not for—"

"There's a gate," I said.

She blinked. "What?"

"East, past the salt flats. On the pilgrims' road. Two hundred years sealed, Guild lattice, triple-anchor design." I stepped closer, into the grey light leaking off the horizon. "The dead say nobody alive can open it."

Understanding moved across her face slowly, like weather. "Kestrel—"

"I'm not forgiving you." I held up a hand before she could speak. "I want to be clear about that, because you value clarity, and because you deserve to hear it plainly, the way you gave it to me. What you did was monstrous, and the fact that you did it kindly, or ignorantly, or with excellent craftsmanship, does not make it less monstrous. My grandmother is dead. Six generations of my family lived and died in a lie you helped draw. That doesn't wash out. It doesn't wash out in a confession by a fire, and it won't wash out at the altar, and maybe it doesn't wash out ever."

"Good," Odile said hoarsely. "Keep it. Anger's the only honest inheritance anyone's ever given me."

"But." I took a breath, and here is the part I will remember for the rest of my long, strange, burdened life—the part where I chose, deliberately, with my eyes open, not to be the valley anymore. "But you're the best ward-smith I've ever seen, and possibly the best one alive, and ahead of us is a drowned city full of dead covenant-makers who need a living anchor, and between us and them stands every lock your Guild ever built. I can hate you and still not be fool enough to waste you." I let that land. "So here are the terms. You walk with us. You teach me everything, including the parts that shame you—*especially* the parts that shame you. You don't get to leave in the night again, and you don't get to decide what protects me. And when we reach Coral, if the dead want a reckoning from you, you stand for it. Whatever form it takes."

Odile stared at me. Then, slowly, she slid the pack off her shoulder and set it down on the stone, and it made a sound like surrender.

"And if they want more than I can give?" she asked.

"Then you give it," I said, "or you tell me honestly that you can't, and I decide whether that's enough. That's the deal. That's the whole deal. No more cages, Odile. Not for me, and not between us."

For a moment she just breathed. Then she laughed—that same dry note as before, except this time there was something underneath it, something that might, in better light, have been relief.

"Gods," she said, wiping her eyes with the heel of a scarred hand. "Six generations of Oathkeepers, and the first one worth a damn turns out to be a bone-carver who negotiates like a fishwife." She stuck out her hand, formal as a guild ceremony. "Terms accepted, Anchor."

I shook it. Her grip was steady now. Whatever else had broken tonight, her hands had held.

Behind us, Sef cleared their throat from the fire. "Not to interrupt the treaty-signing," they called, "but the charts say the salt flats bend east at first light, and if the dead are right about a gate..." They hesitated, and I saw the hope move across their face like a small, cautious sunrise. "...then someone should wake early. Someone who knows the lock."

Odile looked at me, and I looked at her, and neither of us smiled, quite. But something passed between us that was sturdier than smiles—two people agreeing, without saying it, to carry the weight on opposite ends of the same pole.

"Someone should," she said.

And far to the east, past the salt flats, past the gate none of us yet understood, the drowned lights of Coral waited—and so, I would learn soon enough, did the Ashen Court, whose riders had picked up our trail three days prior and were, at that very hour, making camp close enough that we could have smelled their fires, if any of us had known to look.
