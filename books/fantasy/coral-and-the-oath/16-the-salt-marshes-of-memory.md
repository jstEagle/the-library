## 16. The Salt Marshes of Memory

The marshes began where the road gave up pretending.

One moment we were walking a perfectly respectable track, rutted and muddy but at least committed to the idea of being a road, and the next it simply dissolved into brown water and grey reeds that stretched to the horizon like the world had been left out in the rain. Sef consulted their charts, then the sky—which was no help at all, being uniformly ash—then the charts again.

"The maps say there's a causeway," they said. "Somewhere."

"Maps also said the last town would have an inn," Odile muttered from under her hood. "That town had one wall and a goat."

"To be fair, the goat was very hospitable."

I stood at the edge of the water and felt it before I heard anything: a low hum, like a struck tuning fork buried somewhere under the mud. The dead were here. Not whispering, not murmuring the way they did in Hollowmere's quiet—a hum, layered, patient, the sound of a great many voices waiting for something.

"They're here," I said.

Odile's hand went to the ward-chalk at her belt. "How many?"

"All of them, possibly."

* * *

The woman in the whalebone—the one who had started all this, who had named names and dates into my carving knife until my hands shook—had told me about the marshes three nights before, while I carved her likeness into a length of antler by firelight. Her name was Maren, though she hadn't used it in centuries; names, she'd said, are doors, and she'd been keeping hers shut.

*The salt marshes remember,* she'd said. *We walked them when we still walked anywhere. If you cross them, boy-carver, you will not cross them alone. The ground is thick with us.*

*I've never crossed anything alone,* I'd wanted to say. *That's rather the whole problem.* But she was murdered four hundred years ago and I was carrying her voice around in a piece of bone, so I kept my complaints to myself.

Now I understood what she meant. Each step onto the causeway stones brought the hum up through my boots, through my shinbones—which felt suddenly less like mine and more like antennae—and settled behind my teeth.

"You've gone pale," Odile observed. "Paler. You have a whole aesthetic happening."

"The dead here are... close. Not whispering. Waiting." I flexed my fingers. Since Coral had come up in our conversations more and more, since the bone-whispers had sharpened from weather-noise into something like intent, the dead had been treating me differently. Less like a stray dog that might tolerate scraps. More like a key someone had finally picked up off a hook.

Sef, who had spent the first weeks of our acquaintance terrified of the dead and had since graduated to treating them as a fascinating dataset with poor manners, peered at me over their charts. "Do they know you're coming?"

"I think," I said slowly, "they've known longer than I have."

* * *

The causeway was old work—stones laid by hands before the wards, before Hollowmere, before anyone thought to build valleys with the sky locked out. In places it had sunk, and we waded thigh-deep through water that smelled of iron and rot and, strangely, of flowers. Marsh roses grew on the hummocks, white and fat-petaled, blooming in a season that should have killed them.

Odile touched one and snatched her hand back. "Warded. Old warding. Pre-imperial, if my training holds."

"Your training is drunk," Sef said.

"My training is *resting*." She wiped her fingers on her coat. "But it's not wrong. These aren't wards against anything. They're wards *for*. Preservation wards. Someone wanted this place kept exactly as it was."

I put my palm flat on the nearest stone, and the marsh answered.

It wasn't like the whispers. It wasn't even like Maren's voice, which came to me shaped and deliberate, a letter dictated across centuries. This was a door swinging open. This was falling.

* * *

I am on the causeway but it is not a ruin yet, the stones are new-laid and pale, and there are people everywhere—hundreds, thousands, moving in procession, and the sky above them is full of stars, real stars, so many that the dark between them looks bruised and rich, and I am wearing someone else's hands. Brown hands, scarred, holding a lantern. I am tall. I am old. My knees ache with an ache that is not mine and the crowd around me sings in a language I shouldn't understand and do, completely, the way you understand hunger—

They are going to Coral. All of them. And I—I am Brannoc Almeida, and I am forty years old, and I have never in my life been happier, because my sister has just been chosen as covenant-maker for the western realms, and the whole world has turned out to sing her across the marshes—

I came back to myself face-down in the reeds with Odile's hands gripping my shoulders and Sef counting loudly—they counted in sevens, always sevens, it was how they anchored me now, seven breaths, seven names, seven stars they could no longer see but recited anyway like scripture.

"Don't do that," Odile was saying, her face very close, her breath smelling of the flask she swore she'd given up. "Don't you ever do that. Your eyes went white as fish-belly. You were gone two minutes. Two minutes you weren't breathing, Kestrel."

"I was breathing."

"You were *being breathed*. There's a difference and it's a bad one."

Sef crouched beside us, water soaking their coat, and asked the only relevant question: "What did you see?"

I sat in the mud, dripping, my heart slamming, and tried to explain that I had been a man four hundred years dead and that his happiness had fit me like a coat cut exactly to my measure.

"He was going to Coral," I said. "For his sister's swearing-in. The marshes were full of people. The stars—" My voice caught. "You'd have loved it, Sef. There were so many."

Sef didn't say anything. They just looked out over the grey water with an expression I'd learned meant they were filing something away to grieve later, privately, in the dark, where it wouldn't slow anyone down.

"We camp on the causeway," Odile announced, in the tone that ended discussions. "And tomorrow, Kestrel touches nothing without telling me first."

* * *

Of course I touched things. Not out of defiance—or not mostly—but because the memories came whether I touched or not, once the marsh had decided to share. That night, half-asleep against my pack, I fell into Brannoc again.

He was older. The joy was gone out of the stones of him, replaced by something dense and dutiful. He stood in a chamber beneath the drowned altar—Coral, before the drowning, its halls dry and lit with caged starlight—and watched his sister swear the oath, and he was proud, and he was afraid, because he knew the terms. Everyone close to the covenant-makers knew the terms. A living anchor feels everything the realms feel. Every famine, every massacre, every small private grief in every village, forever. His sister had volunteered. His sister had smiled as she swore.

And Brannoc, standing in the dark of that beautiful hall, thought: *I could never.*

I woke with tears freezing on my face and did not know whose they were.

"It's him again," I told the fire, quietly, so as not to wake the others. "My whatever-he-is. Great-something-grandfather."

Maren's voice rose from the antler in my pack, dry as paper. *Brannoc. Yes. He was kind, your Brannoc. Kindness is often cowardice wearing better clothes.*

"That's cruel."

*So was what he did.* A pause, and then, gentler: *But love him anyway. We did. Love is not an endorsement, carver. It is just love.*

* * *

By the second day the memories stopped waiting for sleep.

They came in flashes, triggered by nothing—by the smell of the water, the cry of a bird, the particular slant of ash-light on reeds. I saw the marsh in high summer, silvered with salt-flowers, crowded with pilgrims. I saw it in wartime, trenches cut through the same reeds, wounded men carried on the same causeway stones. I saw a wedding, two women from villages on opposite shores, marrying on the causeway because it belonged to neither and both. I saw a funeral. I saw a hundred funerals. I saw a child slip off the stones and drown in three feet of water while her brother screamed, and I felt the mother's grief arrive in my chest like a thrown stone, and I sat down hard in the mud and sobbed for ten minutes over a girl who had died before the valley of Hollowmere was founded.

Odile held my head between her scarred hands until it passed. She didn't ask if I was all right. She'd stopped asking, because the answer was always some version of *no, and also yes, and also neither*, and she'd decided that holding on was more useful than interrogating.

"Why does it hurt like this?" I managed. "The good ones hurt worse than the bad ones."

"Because the good ones are gone," she said, simply. "Grief isn't picky about direction, Kestrel. Missing the past hurts same as missing a person. Same wound, different century."

She said it like someone who knew. I didn't ask about that either. We were developing a whole vocabulary of things we didn't ask, and it worked better than it sounds.

* * *

On the third day, the marsh showed me the fall.

I was gathering firewood—driftwood, bleached white as bone, and yes, I noticed the irony, thank you—when the memory took me standing up, which had never happened before. No warning, no dimming of the world. Just a lurch, and I was Brannoc again, sixty years old now, running through these same marshes at night, running *from* something, and the sky overhead was wrong, the stars were going out one by one like lamps being shuttered, and behind me Coral was burning beneath the water—

No. Not burning. Being *sealed*. I could feel it through the soles of his feet, through the bones he'd lent me: the great working settling into place, wards upon wards upon wards, magic locked away, the dead silenced, the sky darkened—not as an act of war but as an act of *fear*. Brannoc had helped build it. Brannoc, who could not bear the anchor's weight, had convinced the others that no one should bear it, that the covenant itself was the wound, that the merciful thing was to cut the realms off from each other and from their own dead and call it peace.

*They'll thank us,* someone said beside him—young, breathless, another Almeida, a niece maybe. *When the grief fades. When it stops hurting.*

*Yes,* Brannoc said, and did not believe it, and built the walls anyway.

I came back to myself with driftwood scattered around me and all three of my companions staring. I hadn't just stood still this time. I had spoken. Sef, grimly, handed me a scrap of charcoal and the back of a chart. "You said 'they'll thank us.' Then you said 'the valley will keep them safe.' Then you said a name."

"What name?"

"Yours."

* * *

We didn't walk after that. We sat in a circle on the widest stretch of the causeway, cold wind pulling at our hoods, and I told them all of it—Brannoc, the sister, the oath, the fear, the founding of Hollowmere as a prison built around the last Oathkeepers' descendants, generations of Almeidas walled into a starless valley for their own protection, or for the world's, and no one left alive who could tell the difference.

"So your family didn't just get locked in," Odile said slowly, when I finished. "Your family built the lock. Then lost the key. Then raised children in the lock."

"And I inherited the bolt cutters." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and sharp. "All those years thinking I was strange, thinking I was broken, thinking the whispers made me unfit for company—and it turns out I'm the punchline of a four-hundred-year-old joke. My ancestors couldn't bear to hear the dead, so they sealed the whole sky, and I was born in the quiet they bought, hearing the dead anyway."

Sef spoke with unusual care, choosing words the way they chose numbers. "You aren't the joke. You're the correction."

"The correction?"

"Think about it mathematically. Brannoc removed a variable—the covenant, the connection—and called the resulting equation peace. But the equation was never solved. The dead stayed. The pain stayed. It just went unaccounted for." They tapped my knee with one finger, precise as a compass point. "Four hundred years of rounding error. And then you. Born able to hear exactly what was silenced. That's not a punishment, Kestrel. That's the ledger demanding reconciliation."

I wanted to tell them they were romanticizing arithmetic. I wanted to tell them that Brannoc had been afraid, genuinely afraid, and that fear deserved some pity even when it built prisons. I wanted to say that I understood him—that some nights the whispers got so loud I'd have traded anything, *anything*, for silence, and if someone had offered me a valley with walls against the dead I might have taken it too.

I loved him. That was the horror of it. Across four centuries, through borrowed knees and stolen tears, I loved this frightened, dutiful, cowardly man, because his happiness had fit me and so had his fear, because his homesickness for a world I'd never seen lived in my chest alongside his shame. Empathy, it turns out, is not a warm feeling. It is a haunting you agree to.

"He thought they'd be thanked," I said finally. "That's the part that breaks me. He really thought that someday, generations on, we'd look at those walls and be grateful."

Odile was silent a long moment. Then: "Were you? Before all this?"

"No," I admitted. "I was lonely. I didn't know what I was lonely *for*, but I knew the walls weren't kindness. Children always know."

"Then he was wrong," she said. "And knowing you'd know it—he built them anyway. That's not tragedy, Kestrel. That's just selfishness with a long shadow. Forgive him if you need to. But don't you dare inherit the excuse."

* * *

That night the marsh gave me one last gift, and it was the strangest of all.

I dreamed—I think I dreamed; the border between dreaming and being haunted had worn thin as wet paper—of Maren, alive, young, walking this causeway among the pilgrims, laughing at some joke with a woman whose face I couldn't hold. She turned and looked directly at me across the gap of centuries, the way you sometimes catch a stranger looking at you, and she said:

*You're nearly there, carver.*

"Maren."

*Lumenhold first. Don't flinch at what you find there. The Court hoards light the way your Brannoc hoarded silence—one man's fear scaled up until it looks like policy.* Her image flickered, and for a moment I saw her as she must have died: afraid, defiant, in a room full of people in ash-grey robes. *The stars are not dead, Kestrel. Remember that when it matters. Whatever they tell you in Lumenhold, whatever the cages look like—*

The dream tore like wet paper. I woke gasping, and found Odile already awake, already watching me, a ward-circle chalked around us both in the dark.

"She was here," I said. "In the dream. Or actually here. With her it's honestly hard to say."

"What did she want?"

"To warn me. About Lumenhold." I rubbed my face. My hands smelled of salt and old wood-smoke, and under that, faintly, of something sweeter—starlight, maybe, or the memory of it. "Odile, she said the stars aren't dead. She said they're caged. And she said the Court hoards light the way my family hoarded silence, and—" I paused, because the last part had arrived only as I spoke it, clicking into place with the terrible neatness Sef would appreciate. "And she said I'd understand why, when I saw it. Because I'm the descendant of a man who locked away a whole sky because he couldn't bear what it asked of him."

Odile looked at me for a long time. Wind moved through the reeds, and underneath the wind, faint and patient, the hum of the marsh went on—the sound of everyone who had ever been failed by a wall built out of fear.

"Well," she said at last, reaching for her flask and, for once, not drinking from it. "Then let's go rob them."

Ahead of us, far across the marsh, past the drowned fields and the sleeping birds, a smudge of light sat on the horizon. Not dawn. Dawn had been extinct for years. It was Lumenhold, burning with everyone else's stolen brightness—and now we knew exactly what it cost, because I carried the receipts in my blood.
