## 17. City of the Hoarded Light

Lumenhold announced itself three days before we reached it, by smell.

"Is that—" Sef began.

"Ash," Odile said. "And under the ash, tallow. They burn rendered fat in the lamps when starlight runs short." She said it the way you'd recite a recipe you hated having memorized. "Welcome to civilization."

I had imagined a city of hoarded light would *glow*. I'd built it up in my head during the long gray march downriver: towers like lanterns, streets washed gold, something almost holy. What we actually saw from the ridge road was a sprawl of slate roofs under a sky the color of old dishwater, and rising out of its middle, one tower so tall it seemed embarrassed by everything around it—the Spire, where the Ashen Court kept whatever light it hadn't yet spent. Even at this distance I could see that the light around the Spire was wrong. It didn't fall on things. It pooled around them, like water refusing to soak into ground that had forgotten how to drink.

"It's not shining," Sef said quietly. "It's *leaking*."

"That's the Court for you," Odile said. "They can't even steal properly."

We went down toward the gates with our hoods up and our story rehearsed: pilgrims of the Duskfold, a sun-cult small enough and sad enough that nobody bothered checking its rolls. Odile had forged us papers on the back of a hymnal page, which she claimed was traditional and I suspected was blasphemy she found funny.

The dead were loud here.

I don't mean louder than usual—I mean *denser*, the way a market is denser than a field. Hollowmere's dead had been scattered whispers, one voice at a time, patient as weather. The dead of Lumenhold pressed against the inside of my skull like a crowd at a door. And they weren't whispering about murders or covenants. They were saying ordinary, unbearable things. *My daughter doesn't remember my face. The lamp-oil is dearer again. It has been forty years and I still wait for morning.*

"You've gone white," Sef murmured.

"The city is full," I said. "Of everyone."

Sef looked at me for a moment, then did the thing they always did when they couldn't fix a problem: they started counting. Steps, gateposts, guards. Their lips moved faintly. It steadied me more than any comfort could have, because it meant someone near me believed numbers were still real, and right now I needed one person who believed anything was.

* * *

The gates of Lumenhold were iron, and the queue to pass them moved at the pace of grief. Guards in ash-gray livery checked papers with the bored cruelty of men who had never once been surprised. When our turn came, the guard looked at Odile's forgery, looked at her hands—scarred, ink-stained, unmistakably a smith's hands—and looked back at the paper.

"Duskfold," he said. "You're a long way from your shrine."

"Pilgrimage," Odile said, with the flat confidence of a woman who had lied to better men than this and gotten paid for it. "We walk the dark to honor the light. You'd know if you'd ever read your own catechism."

Something flickered across his face—shame, maybe, or the memory of a grandmother who'd been devout. He waved us through. As we passed, I heard the dead man standing behind him say, *He checks everyone but he never sees anyone,* and I thought: neither do I, most days. Neither does anyone. That was how cities worked. That was how the Court wanted them to work.

Inside the walls, Lumenhold was a lesson in what darkness does to people over generations.

The streets were narrow and the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators. Every window had shutters, and every shutter had a slit, and every slit was angled toward the Spire—not toward the sky, because there was nothing in the sky to angle toward, but toward the single point where light was permitted to exist. People arranged their whole lives around those slits. Shopkeepers hung mirrors to bounce stray lamplight deeper into their stalls. Children played a game where one held up a shard of glass to catch a lamp-beam and the others chased the spot of brightness across the cobbles, shrieking, and my chest hurt watching it because it was joy, real joy, built on a ration.

"They don't remember stars," Sef said. We'd stopped at a corner while a funeral procession crossed—an actual procession, with a bier and singers, and atop the bier a lantern lit, because here you were buried with light or you were buried with nothing. "Not as things. Ask a child here what a star is and they'll tell you it's a story the priests use."

"Do the priests say the stars are dead?"

"The priests say the Court *saved* us from them." Sef's voice had an edge I'd learned to recognize: the sound of an astronomer biting down on grief. "That the stars demanded worship, that their light burned the unworthy, that the Court gathered the last of it before it guttered out and now shares it fairly." They laughed, one dry note. "I spent eleven years charting objects that 'guttered out.' Stars don't die politely all at once. They don't die *synchronously*. Someone turned off the sky, Kestrel. I just never imagined I'd meet the family that knew why."

I let that sit between us, heavy as it deserved to be. An Almeida had helped murder the covenant-makers. Whatever the Court had done since, my blood was in the foundation of it.

"Then let's go find proof," I said, "that the lights are prisoners and not corpses. Because if they're prisoners—" I heard myself say it aloud for the first time, and the street seemed to lean in— "prisoners can be freed."

* * *

Odile found us lodging above a chandler's shop, which she chose, she explained, because chandlers' apprentices see everything and gossip constantly and can be bribed with candle-ends.

The room had one window, shuttered, and a bed wide enough for two people who were pretending not to be careful around each other. Odile took the floor without discussion, claiming her back preferred boards, which was a lie, and I let her have it, which was mine.

That night I sat by the shutter-slit and listened to the city's dead until my ears rang.

It was different from the murdered covenant-makers. Those voices came with names and dates, organized as ledgers, furious as law. These were just... accumulated. Centuries of ordinary dying, packed into stone that had never been warded, never been quieted. Hollowmere's wards hadn't only kept magic out. They'd kept the dead *muffled*. I had grown up thinking my gift was a curse in a quiet place. Here I understood it was a curse anywhere, and that the valley had been a mercy I'd mistaken for a prison—which, to be fair, it was also.

*Kestrel.* The woman's voice. My first ghost, the one who'd started all this, naming names and dates in whalebone. She'd been quieter since the marshes, since I'd walked through my ancestors' memories and come out the other side knowing her name: Vessa Almeida, three hundred years dead, murdered second-to-last of the covenant-makers.

*I'm here,* I thought at her.

*The Spire. You feel it?*

I did feel it, now that she asked. Underneath the crowd-noise of the city's dead, there was a hum, low and rhythmic, like something breathing in a locked room. It came from the direction of the tower. It came in waves, slow, patient, *many*.

"What is it?" I whispered aloud.

*What they took. It isn't light, child. Light is just what it looks like when you cage a living thing long enough.*

I sat very still. Somewhere below, the chandler's apprentice was singing a song about a girl who stole the moon and was forgiven, because even here they made songs about theft, as long as the thief repented.

*Tomorrow,* Vessa said. *Find the counting-room. Your astronomer will understand.*

* * *

Understanding arrived the next morning wearing a chandler's apron and carrying a tray of tallow dips, in the form of a boy of perhaps fourteen named Pip who had accepted three candle-ends and a silver bit to talk, and who talked the way water talks once you break the dam.

"The Spire's got floors nobody goes to," he said, laying candles in the window one by one, arranging them with the seriousness of a general deploying troops. "Wardens go up, wardens come down, but us trade-folk only get the bottom two levels, and that's for buying. You want light, you buy light. You want *bright*—" he lowered his voice with relish— "you pay the tithe and they take you up to the Chapel of the Kept Flame, and you look at it through the grate, and people cry, some of them. Grown men. My da cried."

"What is it, actually?" Sef asked. They had adopted their scholar voice, mild and endlessly curious, the voice that made people tell them things and only afterward wonder why.

"No one says. But my cousin's a drayman, right, and he hauls for the Court, and he says the carts that go up the Spire ramp come back *lighter* than they went, and cold. Cold in summer. And the wardens—" Pip glanced at the door, then at each of us, deciding we were worth the risk of the next sentence— "the wardens who work the high floors retire early. Or they stop talking. There was one, old Master Ellery, used to sing. Worked the high floors two years. Now he sits by the south wall and counts his fingers, over and over, and if you ask him about the Spire he says, very polite, 'It sings at night, but it isn't a song,' and then he counts his fingers again."

The room was quiet. Odile had stopped pretending to examine the candles.

"Sings at night," Sef repeated softly. "But isn't a song."

I felt Vessa stir in the back of my skull like a cat hearing its name. *Ask him about the counting-room.*

"Pip," I said, "is there a place where they keep records? Ledgers, tallies—of what comes in and out of the Spire?"

"Counting-house, you mean. East side, attached to the Spire by a bridge, third level. Why?"

"Because," Sef said, and I watched them decide to trust a fourteen-year-old with the shape of a revolution, "I believe your city's masters are keeping accounts of something alive. And I'd very much like to read the entries."

Pip looked at us for a long moment. Then he picked up his unsold candles, tucked the silver bit away, and said, "Counting-house changes clerks at midday bell. Bridge door sticks; you have to lift, not push. And if anyone asks, you're from the Duskfold, because everyone leaves the Duskfold alone—they think you're mad, and mad folk are bad luck to trouble." He paused at the door. "My ma says the stars'll come back someday. Everyone laughs at her." His chin came up. "I don't."

He left. Odile exhaled slowly.

"I hate cities," she said. "And I love that child. Both things are true and I refuse to reconcile them."

* * *

The counting-house smelled of dust and ink and, underneath, that same breathing hum, stronger here, rhythmic as a sleeper's chest. We got in past the midday clerk change exactly as Pip had promised—lift, not push—and Odile stood watch at the bridge door while Sef and I went up the narrow stair into the archive.

If you have never seen a room built to contain infinity in columns of figures, I envy you. Shelf after shelf of ledgers, bound in gray leather, dated back two hundred years. Sef made a sound I'd only heard from them once before, at the ruins of their observatory—a small, wounded intake of breath, the sound of a person finding the corpse of their life's work and realizing it had been murdered rather than died.

"Kestrel." Their finger trembled against a spine. "These are *observations*. Star charts. Pre-Collapse charts, annotated, corrected—" They pulled a volume down with hands that shook. "This is my notation. Well—not mine, but my tradition. Someone here was *charting* after the sky went dark. Charting what?"

The answer was on the next shelf. Ledgers labeled not with dates but with designations: *Vessel 1 through Vessel 400*. And inside, in clerk's hand, entries that made my skin crawl:

*Vessel 112: dimming. Recommend rotation to inner ring.*
*Vessel 87: agitated. Singing audible through containment. Sedative lattice reinforced.*
*Vessel 3: responsive to hymn-form 9. Note: responds to grief more strongly than to praise.*

"Agitated," Sef read aloud, very quietly. "Singing. Responsive to grief." They closed the ledger with exaggerated care, the way you handle a thing when you're afraid you'll otherwise throw it through a wall. "Kestrel. These aren't lamps. These aren't stores of fuel. These are *individuals*. Each entry is a—"

"A star," I said. "Each vessel is a star."

"And they're catalogued like livestock." Sef sat down abruptly on a stool, ledgers fanned around them, and laughed—one cracked, disbelieving note. "All those years. All those years I mourned them. Held funerals, practically. Wrote elegies in the margins of my charts. And they were *here*, four hundred of them, singing in the dark, and the whole world was told they were dead." They looked up at me, and their eyes were wet and blazing at once. "The Court didn't extinguish the sky. They *herded* it."

*Now you see,* Vessa breathed. *Now you understand what the oath was for. Not to own the light. To keep it free. A covenant is a promise between equals, child—that was the sin of the Court, and the sin of my kin. We forgot the light was a party to the treaty.*

I put my hand flat on the ledger, on the word *agitated*, and through my palm, faint as a pulse through a wrist, I felt it: the hum, the breathing, the many. Four hundred hearts that the world had mourned as dead.

"We can't fight the Court," I said slowly, working it through. "Not with four people. We can't storm the Spire. But if the stars are alive—if they're *inmates*—then the task isn't destruction. It's release. And release needs a key."

"Or a thief," Odile said from the doorway, making us both jump. She'd come up unheard, which for a woman her size and opinions was a professional accomplishment. She was looking at the ledgers with an expression I was learning to dread: the look of a smith seeing a mechanism she could improve. "One star. One small one, dim enough they won't miss it, close enough to the surface to reach. We take a shard of it—a splinter, a *spark*—and a spark of a living star is a compass, a key, a seed. It points home to the rest of the flock." Her grin was crooked and terrible. "Also, it will scream, and we will deserve whatever happens next."

"It will *what*?" I said.

"Did you think caged light doesn't know it's caged?" Odile said. "Everything alive knows. That's the whole horror of it." She picked up the ledger of Vessel 3—the one that responded to grief—and read the entry twice. Then, softer, in a voice I'd rarely heard from her: "Responsive to hymn-form nine. Somebody in this building sang to it. Some clerk, some warden, somebody with a kind voice and a guilty conscience." She set it down. "Good. Then it already knows humans come in kinds. Let's make sure it meets the right kind."

* * *

We left the counting-house the way we'd come, and no alarm rose, and the city went on burning its tallow and chasing its rationed beams across the cobbles. But something had changed in the geometry of our little band, and I felt it walking back through the dusk-slitted streets: we had come to Lumenhold with a quest, vague and holy and far away, an altar at the bottom of the sea. We left with a prize. Concrete. Reachable. *Stealable.*

Hope, I discovered, is heavier than despair. Despair you can carry slumped and silent. Hope makes you walk differently. It makes you plan. It makes you lie awake composing arguments to present to a star.

In the chandler's loft, Sef spread their salvaged charts on the floor and began, with a stolen pencil, to draft the shape of the Spire's upper floors from Pip's gossip and Odile's memories of Court architecture. Odile sharpened tools. I sat by the shutter-slit with my palm open, listening to the hum, and for the first time since the night the sky went black, the dead in my head were not grieving.

They were *counting along* with Sef. All of them. The whole crowded city of the dead, keeping time.

*They're waiting too,* Vessa said. *Everyone is. The living just don't know yet what for.*

Below us, faint through the floorboards, the chandler's apprentice had begun to sing again—the song about the girl who stole the moon. Except tonight, for the first time, I heard the last verse properly, the one everyone mumbles:

*She stole the moon to light her way,*
*but moons are not for keeping—*
*she opened up her two closed hands*
*and oh, the sky came leaping.*

I smiled in the dark, and outside, high on the Spire, something that was not a song sang back.
