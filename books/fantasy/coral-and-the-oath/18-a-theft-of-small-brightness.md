## 18. A Theft of Small Brightness

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a group of people who have just agreed to do something criminal. I had heard it before, in miniature, when Odile talked me into stealing bread from my own pantry because "it's funnier that way." But this was the full orchestral version: four people around a guttering lamp in a rented room above a dye-shop, none of us looking at each other, all of us listening to the sound of our own consciences clearing their throats.

"So," Odile said at last. "We're agreed. We're going to burgle the most heavily warded building on the continent."

"When you put it like that—"

"I'm putting it like that so you can't pretend otherwise later." She was cleaning her tools with a rag gone grey with use, and her hands were steady, which frightened me more than trembling would have. Steady hands meant she'd done this before. Many times. "Lumenhold's vaults sit under three layers of wardwork. The outer ring is Court-standard, ugly but effective. The middle ring is older—pre-Court, actually, which means it's better than anything they've built since and they don't even know it. And the inner ring—" She paused, and for once the theatrical relish drained out of her voice. "The inner ring is mine."

Ilex's helm turned toward her with the slow creak of old leather. "Yours."

"Mine. Or rather, mine and eleven colleagues', back when I still had a guild, a name, and a liver that didn't hate me. We designed the containment lattice for the caged starlight. It was supposed to be temporary storage while the realms argued about what to do with the stuff." She laughed, short and joyless. "That was thirty years ago. The argument never ended. The lattice held. And now I'm going to walk in and unpick my own masterpiece, which is either poetic or pathetic, and I've decided not to find out which."

Sef had been quiet through all of this, which was unlike them. They sat by the window with their star charts spread across their knees—the useless ones, the ones mapping skies that no longer existed—and when they finally spoke, their voice had the flat carefulness of someone handling something breakable.

"The stars aren't dead," they said. "You're all very comfortable saying *caged* now, but I want you to understand what that means. Every light in Lumenhold's vaults is a star. A whole star, folded down small. Somewhere up there—" they pointed at the ash-grey ceiling as if they could see through it to the black sky beyond, "—there are holes where those stars used to be. Empty sockets. I found them years ago and thought I'd made an error. I spent four years trying to find the error." Their hand closed slowly around the edge of a chart. "There was no error. So yes. Let's go steal one back."

* * *

Lumenhold by day was a city pretending very hard to be fine.

I had seen the trade city under its permanent ash-dark, with its sun-cults and hoarded lamps, but Lumenhold was another order of pretense entirely. Here the streets were swept. Here the lamplighters wore uniforms with silver braid, and the lamps themselves burned with a color I couldn't name—not gold, not white, something that made your teeth ache faintly and your heart lift against your will. Stolen starlight, burning in the gutters of a city that had learned to call theft *order*.

The dead, for once, were quiet. That should have warned me. When the dead go quiet in a place, it's usually because there's nothing left for them to say—or because they're watching.

"Keep moving," Ilex murmured beside me. Out of his armor he looked almost like a man, tall and grey-faced, wearing a hooded cloak that did nothing whatsoever to disguise him and everything to make people decide not to look closely. Revenants have a way of sliding out of attention, like a word repeated until it loses meaning. "The Court's eyes are everywhere, but they look for armies and oaths. They don't look for four nobodies buying chestnuts."

"We are not nobodies," Odile said. "I am a disgraced master of my craft, you are an abomination, Kestrel is the last Oathkeeper, and Sef is—" She considered. "Actually, Sef might be a nobody. No offense."

"Some taken," said Sef. "It's the safest thing anyone's offered me all year."

We had spent two days being nobody. Two days of walking the city's rings, learning its rhythms: how the watch changed at the hour when the great hoarded lamps were trimmed, how the pilgrims queued at the Vault of Light to press their palms to the viewing-glass and see, for a coin apiece, a single caged star turning in its prison of glass and wardwork. How the guards outside the vault yawned between the second and third bell, because nothing had ever happened there. Nothing ever happened anywhere, in a city that had outlawed happening.

And underneath it all, threaded through every street like water through limestone, ran the hum of the wards. I could feel them in my teeth. In the bones I carried, too—the little carved pieces in my satchel shifted and murmured whenever we passed a wardstone, the dead pressing close to whatever boundary they could find.

"They hate it here," I told Odile on the second evening, as we stood in the shadow of the vault's outer wall, ostensibly admiring the architecture. "The dead. The wards don't keep them out exactly, but they... flatten them. Like pressing flowers."

"That's what wards do." Odile traced a line in the air, following the lattice's geometry with one finger, seeing a structure I couldn't. "Wards are arguments. *Stay there; I'm here.* Most people build them out of fear. The good ones are built out of love—you know, *stay safe; I'll hold the line.*" Her mouth twisted. "The Court builds them out of greed. *Stay small; stay mine.*"

"And yours?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Mine were built out of spite, mostly. Spite holds surprisingly well. But we'll get to mine." She nodded at the wall. "Tomorrow night. Third bell, when the watch changes. Are you ready?"

"No," I said honestly.

"Good. Ready people do stupid things confidently. Nervous people check their work."

* * *

The thing about breaking into a place designed by Odile Vane is that Odile Vane remembers every flaw she was forced to leave in it.

"There," she whispered, crouched in the maintenance gallery beneath the vault, her fingers hovering over a junction of ward-lines that glowed faintly through the stone like veins under skin. "Eleven masters signed off on this lattice, and ten of them wanted a clean design. I insisted on a service corridor here—for repairs, I said. Really because Master Corvane had called my draftsmanship 'adequate' and I wanted him to have to crawl through a pipe someday. Petty? Yes. Useful tonight? Also yes."

"You built a secret entrance out of pettiness," Sef said.

"I built *most things* out of pettiness. Pettiness is durable. Love gets tired; spite keeps excellent records."

The corridor was exactly wide enough for a person who regretted their life choices, and Ilex, who was broader than any of us and also technically dead, had to turn sideways and shuffle. He bore it with the patience of a man who had been dead for centuries and had stopped expecting comfort. Only once did he speak, low, as we passed beneath the main vault floor and felt the weight of caged stars humming overhead like a struck bell that never finished ringing:

"I attended the lighting of this vault. Before I died the first time. There was a ceremony. They promised the stars would be freed within a generation." A pause, filled only by the shuffling of his boots. "I have since learned what promises are worth. Hold the line anyway. That is the whole of duty: hold the line anyway."

Then we came up through a floor hatch into the dark beneath the display hall, and I saw the stars.

Not the sky-stars. These. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, each one suspended in a globe of glass and woven light, each one turning slowly, slowly, like a sleeper who cannot wake. The room was vast and cold and utterly silent, and it was full of light without warmth, brightness without morning. My eyes filled with tears I hadn't authorized. Beside me, Sef made a sound I will hear until I die—a small, cracked note, like a string parting.

"They're beautiful," Sef whispered. "They're *right there*. All this time they were right here, and everyone walks past and pays a coin to look, and calls it worship—"

"Sef." Odile's voice was gentle, which from Odile was more alarming than shouting. "Focus. We take one. One star, one globe, minimal disturbance. If we free them all tonight, we die tonight, and the Court moves them somewhere worse. One. Say it back."

"One," Sef said, in the tone of someone agreeing to carry a single grain of sand off a beach.

The plan was simple, which is the only kind of plan that survives contact with reality. Odile would unpick the containment lattice around a single globe—I had watched her choose it, a smaller star near the gallery's edge, one whose cage showed signs of age, hairline fractures in the wardwork that she read like a physician reading a pulse. Sef would hold the charts and calculate the moment of extraction, because pulling a star out of its lattice, apparently, was not like picking an apple; the lattice would resist, and the resistance had to be met at the precise interval when it exhaled. Ilex would stand at the hatch and be large and unkillable. And I—

I would listen.

That was my part. Not the bone-carving, not the voices of the murdered covenant-makers, though they were there too, faint, at the edge of my hearing like a congregation settling into pews. My part was simpler and stranger: the caged stars made a sound. Not with ears—with whatever it is in me that hears the dead. Each star sang a single sustained note, and the notes were wrong, bent, flattened by decades in their prisons, and Odile needed to know the exact moment the song changed as she cut the lattice, because that change was the star noticing it was being freed, and reaching for the opening.

"You're telling me," I had said, when she explained this, "that I'm the tuning fork."

"You're the tuning fork," she agreed, "and also the canary, and possibly the choir director. Try to grow into the role."

So I knelt beside her in the cold and the enormous silence, and I listened, and the dead gathered behind me like relatives at a deathbed, and Odile began to work.

Her hands moved without hurry. That was the uncanny thing about watching Odile at her craft—all her bluster and drinking and theatrical despair fell away, and what remained was pure attention, the kind most people only give to a lover or a fire. Her picks slid into the lattice's seams. The ward-lines dimmed where she touched them, then flared, then dimmed again, sulking.

"It knows," she breathed. "Clever old thing. It's remembering what I taught it." A thin thread of light came away in her fingers, coiling like a hair pulled from a knot. "Kestrel. The song."

I listened past the dead, past my own heartbeat, into the star's bent note. "Lower," I said. "It's—it's leaning. Toward you. Like a plant toward a window."

"Good. That's the exhale coming. Sef?"

"Interval holds," Sef whispered, eyes on their charts, lips moving through numbers. "Forty seconds. Thirty-five."

Odile's hands blurred. Another thread came loose. The star's note climbed—faintly, terribly, like a voice remembering hope—and the dead behind me stirred, and one of them, the woman with the names and dates, spoke clearly for the first time since we'd entered the city:

*This one watched us swear the first oath. It remembers.*

I nearly lost the count. "Odile—"

"I heard you flinch. Don't. Twenty seconds, Sef?"

"Fifteen. The lattice's resistance peaks in eight—no, seven—"

"Then we go on seven." Odile set both hands against the globe's seam. "Kestrel, when I say *now*, you tell me the instant the note breaks. Not before, not after. The instant."

The dead pressed closer. The star leaned. Somewhere far above, a guard laughed at something, the sound falling through the floor like a dropped coin.

"Now," Odile said.

I listened with everything I had. The note strained, thinned, stretched like ice on a warming pond—

"Broke," I said. "*Now.*"

Odile pulled.

For one perfect moment, the star came free. It rose out of its shattered globe like a bubble rising through water, and it was small, no bigger than a quail's egg, and it was the brightest thing I had ever seen, and every dead soul in that room cried out at once—not in grief, for once, but in something I had never heard from them before. Recognition. Homesickness. Joy.

And then the alarms woke up.

* * *

Later, Odile would claim the escape went according to plan. This was a lie, and we all knew it was a lie, and we let her tell it because the alternative version involved more screaming.

What actually happened: the lattice, wounded, did what lattices do—it called for help. Light flooded the gallery in bands of white and red. Bells began somewhere overhead, deep and arrhythmic, the sound of a city remembering fear. And the remaining caged stars, hundreds of them, all turned at once in their globes to watch their sibling leave, and the sight of that—of all those prisoners turning together—nearly buckled my knees.

"Hatch!" Ilex roared, in a voice that had commanded charges against things with too many wings, and we ran.

The maintenance corridor was suddenly much narrower, or I was suddenly much less brave; both, probably. Behind us I heard boots, shouts, the crackle of ward-work being spun into barriers. Odile ran backwards half the time, throwing improvised wards over her shoulder like a woman scattering seed—little snarls of geometry that burst into walls of force, bought seconds, each one costing her something I could see in the grimace she tried to hide.

"Left at the junction!" she shouted. "The petty corridor! I told you pettiness pays!"

We went left. The corridor became a crawlspace, the crawlspace became a drainage channel, and the drainage channel became the cold black canal behind the dye-shop, into which we fell, one after another, like a series of increasingly undignified dominoes. Ilex surfaced last, holding the star.

He had wrapped it in his cloak, and through the wool the light came anyway, steady and patient, like a candle in a window. We lay in the shallows of a filthy canal under an ash-black sky, gasping, and in the palm of a revenant knight's dead hand, a stolen star burned on.

"Everyone," Ilex said gravely, "is a disaster."

"Yes," Odile said, spitting out canal water. "A successful disaster. Note the distinction."

* * *

We didn't stop running until the rooftops of the dyers' quarter gave way to the tanner flats, and the tanner flats to the marsh road, and the marsh road to darkness deep enough to trust. Only then did we gather around the star and let ourselves look at what we'd done.

Sef unwrapped it with hands that shook. The star sat in their cupped palms, turning slowly, and up close I could see it wasn't uniform—it had structure, currents, weather, like a tiny furious sea. Its bent note had straightened the moment it left the lattice. Now it sang clear and high and constant, and the dead around me had arranged themselves in a circle to listen, the way people gather around a fire.

"It's pointing," Sef said suddenly.

"What?"

"Look." They turned their hands slightly, and the star—gently, unmistakably—drifted against their palms, always leaning the same direction. South and west. Toward the sea. "It's not random. Caged stars are kept still by the lattice; free, they orient. Toward—" Sef's voice caught. "Toward home. Toward the sky they were taken from. And the nearest point where sky and sea meet, where the old covenant was sworn, where the drowned altar—"

"Coral," I said.

"Coral," Sef agreed. "It's a compass. Our burglary gave us a compass."

We sat with that a while. The marsh breathed around us, frogs and reeds and distant, ordinary night sounds—the first ordinary night sounds I'd heard since the stars vanished. Ilex stood watch, a grey shape against the greyer dark. Odile was binding the burns on her fingers where the lattice had bitten her, hissing through her teeth, refusing help.

And I held the star.

Just for a moment. Sef had passed it to me while they repacked their charts, and its warmth sat in my hands like a sleeping bird, and its song rang through me, and the dead sang with it, and I felt—

I felt *good*. That was the shameful thing. Crouched in the mud with alarm bells still faintly ringing miles behind us, soaked in canal water, guilty of a crime against the most powerful court on the continent—I felt quick and bright and clever, felt the clean animal pleasure of having gotten away with it. Of deception worn like a glove that fit perfectly. I had lied to the gate guards with a smile. I had walked past the pilgrims queuing to worship the very thing I intended to steal, and I had enjoyed it, enjoyed the secrecy, enjoyed being the only one in the crowd who knew.

The dead noticed. The dead notice everything. The woman with the names and dates drifted close and said, not unkindly:

*The first Almeida liked this feeling too. Write it down somewhere you'll find it again.*

I handed the star back to Sef very quickly.

"Kestrel?" Sef glanced up. "You went strange."

"Tired," I said.

It was the first lie I had told this family, and it fit as well as the other glove had, and that frightened me more than the Ashen Court ever had. Because the Court had to hunt me. The lie just had to wait.

Above the marsh, the sky stayed black. But in Sef's cupped hands, the small stolen star leaned south and west, patient as a promise, singing its one clear note toward the drowned altar of Coral—and I could have sworn that as we packed to move, its song changed by a single degree, the way a voice does when it starts paying attention to you.
