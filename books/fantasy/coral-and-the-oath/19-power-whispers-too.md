## 19. Power Whispers Too

I want it on the record that I tried to throw the thing in a river.

We were three days out of Lumenhold, camped in a hollow of grey hills where the ash fell soft as flour and Odile had rigged a ward that smelled faintly of hot iron and burnt sugar. I took the shard out of its wrapping—Sef's spare sock, because we are professionals—and walked to the stream at the bottom of the hollow with every intention of letting the current have it.

The current refused.

Not dramatically. No wall of water rising up to defend its treasure, no voice thundering from the deep. The shard simply sat in my palm while the water went around my hand as though my fist were a stone that had always been there. I stood ankle-deep in cold river for the better part of an hour, and the water parted around me with the polite indifference of a crowd avoiding a beggar, and the shard grew warm against my skin like a cat settling in.

"Fine," I told it. "But you're not sleeping in the sock anymore."

It didn't answer then. That came later, which is the part I should have understood sooner: things that whisper learn patience first.

* * *

Here is what the shard was, for those keeping inventory. When Sef and I lifted it out of the Court's vaults—out of a cage of black iron shaped, if you looked too long at it, like a rib—it had been a splinter of star no bigger than my thumbnail. It hummed. It made the hairs on your arm stand up. Sef held it and cried without seeming to notice, the way you cry at a song from childhood you'd forgotten you knew. For two days afterward she slept with it near her pillow and drew charts by its light, real light, the first true starlight her instruments had tasted since the sky went out, and when she talked about it her voice did something young.

That was before I noticed it was talking to me.

Or—no. Let me be exact, because exactness is the only honesty I have left this week. It wasn't talking *at first*. It was listening. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. A voice that talks at you is a stranger shouting across a market. A voice that listens is someone standing very close behind you, breathing.

* * *

It started small. We were walking—the long road south toward the coast, toward the cliff where Odile swore we'd see Coral if the weather and the gods cooperated—and the dead were doing their usual work on me.

You should understand what "usual" means. Since Hollowmere's wards broke, the whispers haven't stopped. They're quieter on the road than they were in the valley—distance helps, and so does having somewhere to be—but they never leave entirely. It's rain on a roof. You stop hearing it until it stops, and then you can't hear anything else. Names mostly. Dates. Fragments of argument, lullabies, last words said to people who weren't listening. A woman in Brackish Ford who spent forty years apologizing to a sister. A soldier counting his steps because counting was all he could still do. The murdered covenant-makers, loudest and oldest, speaking in that layered way they had, many throats one sentence.

And threaded through it, that night on the road: quiet. Not silence—the dead don't grant silence—but a hush, like snowfall over a battlefield. I stopped mid-stride.

"You all right?" Sef asked. She was walking backward in front of me, which she does when she's excited about terrain, a habit that will eventually kill her.

"Fine," I said. And the hush folded itself around me like a coat, and the shard in my pack against my spine felt warm as a hand.

That should have been enough. Warmth where there shouldn't be warmth, quiet where there shouldn't be quiet—I've carved bones long enough to know that comfort offered by an object is never free. But here's the shameful arithmetic of it: I hadn't had a full night's sleep in weeks. The dead woke me at every watch change. And for one mile, one single mile, my skull was mine.

I kept walking. I didn't tell anyone.

* * *

Odile noticed the sock first, because Odile notices everything that isn't herself and most things that are.

"Why does your pack smell like a forge?" she demanded over breakfast, which was ash-oats again, which taste like eating a chimney's regret.

"Sef's sock."

"My socks don't smell like—" She leaned over, squinting at the bundle. Her hands, scarred white from thirty years of ward-work gone wrong, hovered over it the way they hover over anything magical, reading heat and hum through the air. I watched her face change. "It's brighter."

"It's what?"

"Since Lumenhold." She looked at me, and her eyes had the flat sharpness she gets when she's about to say something technical and mean it. "Shards decay. Caged starlight bleeds off. I told you—we have maybe a season before it's just a pretty rock. It should be dimmer by now. It's not. It's—" she turned it over in her wrapped fingers, careful even now, even hungover, "—it's feeding."

"On what?" Sef asked.

They both looked at me. I shrugged with the whole practiced looseness of a person who has been lying for exactly four days and has gotten good at it. "How would I know? You stole it."

"*We* stole it," Odile said. "You carried it out under your coat while I caused a diversion that cost me my best ward-kit and nearly my eyebrows."

"And it was beautiful," I said. "Truly. Your eyebrows have never been braver."

She let it go, because that's the deal between us—she lets go of things, I let go of things, and whatever accumulates in the middle we pretend is trust. Sef went back to her charts. The shard sat in its wrapping, bright and fed, and said nothing, because it had already learned the first lesson of whispering: never speak where others can hear.

* * *

The first time it used words, I was alone. Of course I was alone. It waited for me to be alone the way a debt collector waits for payday.

We'd made camp in the ruin of a shepherd's cot, walls waist-high, roof open to the ash-grey dark. Odile was drunk—not badly, functionally, the way a smith is functional at a forge—and arguing astronomy with Sef, who was sober and merciless. Their voices made a comfortable noise, the sound of people who belong to each other even when they're wrong. I went out past the wall to take my watch, because taking watch alone is the only privacy I get now, and sat on a fallen lintel with the shard in my palm.

The stars were absent overhead, as they have been since the night everything broke. But the shard threw a little light upward, a needle-thin point of white, and for a moment—if you squinted, if you wanted to—you could almost pretend one star had survived.

*There,* said the shard. Not in my ears. In the place behind my ears where the dead live, which is worse, because it meant it had found the door the dead use and knocked instead of breaking it. Polite. Courteous. *You miss them.*

"The stars," I said aloud, because answering aloud felt safer than answering inside. "Yes. Everyone misses the stars. It's rather the crisis of our age."

*Not them.* The warmth in my palm deepened. *Them.* And beneath the word, a pressure, a shape—a suggestion of the whispers, the rain-on-the-roof chorus I carry everywhere. *They hurt you.*

"They don't hurt me." Which was a lie of the second rank; the first-rank lie had been telling myself the quiet on the road was nothing.

*Every night,* the shard said gently, the way Odile says *you knew the wards were failing* when she wants me to arrive at a conclusion myself. *Every morning. Every watch. You flinch when they say certain names. You sleep sitting up so you can wake faster. You love them and they are killing you, and you cannot put them down, because putting them down means being empty, and empty frightens you more than full.*

I looked at the little point of light. "What are you offering?"

*I'm not offering anything.* A pause, delicate as a knife laid down beside a plate rather than pointed. *I'm a piece of a star, bone-carver. Do you know what stars are?*

"Enlighten me."

*Burning. All of them, always burning. And they do not scream.* The warmth pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat deciding to be calm. *Your dead are noise because they are unfinished. Grief unspent, oaths unsaid, anger with nowhere to go. But finished things are quiet. I am finished. Hold me and listen.*

And I did—I'm confessing it, I held it and listened—and for ten heartbeats there was nothing in my head at all. Not hush. Nothing. The clean white nothing of fresh snow, of unwritten paper, of the moment before a scream when the lungs are still full. My own mind, vacant and mine, lit from within by a splinter of stolen fire.

Then Sef laughed at something inside the ruin, high and startled, and the spell cracked, and the whispers rushed back in like water into a footprint, and I was sitting on a broken lintel in the ash-dark holding a fragment of a caged star and shaking.

*Think about it,* the shard said, already receding, patient as rot. *I'm very good at waiting. It's most of what stars do.*

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the cold fire pit and did the honest work, the work I'd been avoiding for four days: I asked myself what it wanted.

Because it wanted something. Nothing gives without wanting. The Ashen Court gave Hollowmere its quiet valley and wanted Oathkeepers penned like animals. The dead give me their voices and want restoration—they've never pretended otherwise. Even Odile's friendship comes with terms, though hers are written in spite and grudging affection rather than ink. Everything in this world is a covenant. The question is only whether you read the contract before signing or after.

So: what does a shard of starlight want?

It wants to matter, maybe. It was ripped out of the sky, caged in iron shaped like a rib, hoarded in a vault by people who fed on darkness. If it hates anything, it hates the cage and the hands. And here's the thing that kept me awake until the ash began to pale: I could use that. A shard that wants to be free, that hates cages—that might help us free the rest. Sef's charts said the stars aren't dead, only imprisoned, and that they can only be freed during the swearing of a new oath. Two acts, one moment. A splinter of starlight at the altar of Coral might be the key that turns both locks.

Or it wants what it offered. Silence. And the way it offered it—gently, reasonably, with such perfect sympathy for how tired I am—that wasn't the voice of a prisoner. That was the voice of a recruiter.

Both can be true. That's the trap of it. The best lies are load-bearing.

* * *

In the morning, Sef caught me looking at it.

Not doing anything. Just looking. She came to sit beside me at the fire with her charts rolled under her arm and her hair doing whatever it does, which is defy several natural laws, and she followed my eyes to the bundle by my knee.

"It talks to you," she said.

My stomach dropped through the earth and kept going. "What?"

"Not words. I've been watching the readings." She tapped the chart. Lines of her cramped notation marched across it, and among them a series of marks I recognized as her shorthand for resonance. "Since Lumenhold the shard's output fluctuates in patterns. Speech-patterns. Rhythmic, structured, responsive. Instruments pick up structure; they don't pick up meaning. Whatever it's saying, it's saying to you." She looked up, and her eyes were clear and kind and absolutely without suspicion, and that was somehow the worst part. "What does it feel like? Being spoken to by starlight. Nobody's ever been able to ask before."

A door stood open in front of me. Tell her, said the decent part of me, the part that had watched a stranger die on the road for my sake, the part Ser Ilex would have—wherever he walks his endless road—looked at steadily. Tell her it promises silence. Tell her it waits until you're alone. Tell her you liked it.

"It feels like home," I said. "Like the wards in Hollowmere, before they broke. Quiet."

Which was even true, in the way that a noose feels like a collar and a collar feels like belonging, if you've never been allowed to choose.

Sef smiled, wrote something down, and moved on to argue with Odile about declension tables. And I sat by the fire with the shard warm against my boot and understood, with the particular clarity that comes only after you've done the unforgivable small thing, that I had just chosen. Not once, finally, but first—first of however many times it would ask, and it would keep asking, because I had taught it that I keep secrets well.

The dead murmured their names against the inside of my skull. The shard glowed its small patient glow. Somewhere south, under a luminous sea, an altar waited to learn what kind of person was coming to swear upon it.

*Quietly now,* the shard whispered, as Odile called my name and I rose and smiled and said nothing. *We have so much time.*
