## 20. The Turning of Ser Ilex

Ser Ilex stopped sleeping.

I want to be precise about that, because revenants don't sleep the way the living do—they go still, they go quiet, they let the grave take a small interest payment on what it's owed. He'd done it every night since we'd raised him on the road to Coral, or rather since he'd raised himself out of politeness and decided to travel with us because we were going his way. Every night he'd fold down onto whatever ground was flattest, set his sword across his knees, and become a statue with opinions waiting to be resumed in the morning.

Twelve days ago he'd stopped even pretending. Now he stood watch at every hour, and when I came out of my bedroll before dawn to warm my hands over the coals, I'd find him already standing at the edge of camp, facing the dark like a man expecting correspondence.

"You could lie down," I said, one of those mornings. "The ground won't judge you."

"The ground has judged me for two hundred years," he said. "We've reached an understanding."

His voice had changed. That was the thing I kept not saying out loud. When we'd first met him he'd sounded like a dry well—deep, echoing, a little sad. Lately he sounded like the well was filling up with something. There was a rattle under his words, a faint double-exposure, as if someone else were speaking slightly behind him, half a breath out of time.

I knew what that was. I heard voices for a living.

"Your bond is fraying," I said.

He didn't turn around. In the grey light his armor looked less like steel than like ash pressed into the shape of a man—which, I had begun to suspect, was closer to the truth than any of us liked.

"My bond is *holding*," he said. "There's a difference. Holding is what bonds do while they decide."

"Decide what?"

"Whether they were worth making." He finally turned, and his eyes did their unsettling trick of catching light that wasn't there—the last reflected starlight, Sef said, stored in him like coins in a miser's mattress. "You hear the dead, Kestrel Almeida. Tell me what you hear when you listen to me."

I told the truth, which is a habit I recommend only in moderation. "I hear an oath. And under the oath, a man asking whether keeping a promise for two centuries counts for anything if everyone he made it to is gone."

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, not quite grief, some weather that belonged to both. "Then your hearing is as good as your carving," he said. "Go wake the others. We make the salt flats by noon, and I would like to arrive before whatever is following us does."

*Whatever*, he said. Not *whoever*. I filed that away the way you file away a splinter: painfully, and where you can feel it every time you move.

* * *

We made the salt flats by noon, which should have been a triumph and instead felt like walking onto a page that hadn't been written yet. The marsh behind us gave way to a white plain so flat and so bright under the ash-dark sky that it seemed lit from below, as though the world had a candle under its skin. Odile hated it immediately.

"No cover," she said, squinting across the white. "No stone. No wood worth warding. If something comes at us out here, we'll see it coming, which will be a lovely change, and then it will kill us anyway."

"Optimism," said Sef, unpacking her charts. "I've read about it."

"I'll lend you my notes."

They bickered the way old friends bicker who refuse to admit they're friends, which after everything on the road felt like the truest sound in the world. I went with Ilex to walk the perimeter, because he asked, and because lately when he asked for anything my chest did a small frightened thing I didn't have a name for yet.

He walked the flats like a surveyor, pausing here and there, pressing his gauntlet flat against the salt crust. On the fourth pause he stayed crouched a long time.

"Ash," he said.

"It's the Ashen Court's whole aesthetic," I said. "Ash on the roads, ash in the wind, ash in your tea—"

"Not weather-ash. Marked ash." He lifted his hand, and between his fingers the crust crumbled into grey powder that moved against the wind. Toward us. "Someone has seeded our trail. Every league or so, a pinch. It's how the Court tracked the old covenant-bearers, back when I—" His jaw closed. The rattle under his voice rose, then settled. "Back when I guarded them."

I crouched beside him. Up close the marked ash wasn't just moving against the wind; it was *organized*, drifting in slow spirals like iron filings around a hidden magnet. Somewhere, something was pulling.

"How long?"

"If they seeded the marsh crossing? Three days. If they started at Brackish Ford?" He stood, and the motion cost him something—I saw it, a hitch like a door sticking in its frame. "Since Odile."

Odile. Who had designed wards for the Court once upon a time, before her exile. Who knew their methods because she'd built half of them. My stomach performed a maneuver I declined to name.

"She wouldn't," I said.

"I didn't say she would." Ilex looked north, toward the horizon where the ash-dark sat thickest. "But the Court doesn't need traitors, Kestrel. It needs habits. People are legible. They return to their patterns, and patterns can be read like tracks in snow. Your smith walks the way she walked when she served them. Your astronomer checks the sky at the same hour each night. You carve when you're anxious—I have watched you whittle three horses and a duck this week, all of them worried-looking."

"The duck isn't worried. The duck is contemplative."

"The duck is evidence."

I laughed, and it came out wrong, too high, the laugh of someone standing on ice and listening to it think. Because he was right, and worse, he was right in a way that meant none of us had betrayed anyone—we'd simply been *ourselves*, loudly, in a language our enemy could read.

"What do we do about it?" I asked.

"Tonight," Ilex said, "nothing. Tonight we make camp and behave exactly as we always behave. And tomorrow I will tell you why I have not slept in twelve days, and you will wish I had lied."

* * *

He waited until full dark. Odile had thrown up a ring of improvised wards—salt-lines and knotted cord, quick work, ugly work, the ward-smith's equivalent of bolting a chair under a doorknob—and Sef was asleep sitting up against her pack with a chart draped over her face like a shroud she'd chosen herself. I sat across the coals from Ilex, and he laid his sword flat on the salt between us, the way you'd lay down a letter you'd been carrying too long.

"Do you know what binds a revenant?" he said.

"Honestly? I assumed spite."

"Spite holds many of us, yes. Guilt holds more." He turned his gauntleted hand palm-up on his knee. Under the steel, where living flesh would show veins, there was a faint tracery of light, dimmer than it had been in autumn. "I died holding the road to Coral. Two hundred years ago, give or take a decade nobody bothered to count. The last Oathkeeper—the one who fled the altar, whose blood you carry—asked me to hold the gate until they returned. I said yes. They never returned. I held it anyway, because a yes is a load-bearing word, and if you remove it the whole sentence falls."

"And now the sentence is falling," I said quietly.

"Now the sentence is falling." The rattle in his voice surfaced fully, and beneath it I heard—for the first time with my own ears rather than my bone-carving ears—a second voice, thin and patient, speaking in a tongue I almost understood. "My oath was anchored to the old covenant. The covenant broke. What's left of my binding is running on residue. Residue runs out. When it does, I don't get to die, Kestrel. I died already. I get to *unravel*. Slowly, if I'm lucky, and I have never been lucky."

The fire popped. Out past the ward-ring the salt plain glowed faintly, patient as paper.

"Is that why you haven't slept?" I asked. "Because resting spends the residue faster?"

"Resting spends it faster. Fighting spends it faster. Speaking spends it, thinking spends it. Everything I am is a candle burning at both ends and also possibly a third end I haven't found yet." He almost smiled. "So when the offer came, you understand, it was not a difficult temptation to construct. Only a difficult one to refuse."

The cold walked up my spine one vertebra at a time. "*Came.*"

"Three nights ago. While the rest of you slept. She didn't come in flesh—she came the way the Court comes, through ash and through the frayed edges of things. Through me." His eyes found mine across the coals, steady as a fixed star, which was ironic given the general state of stars. "The Regent herself. Or the part of her that does the offering; I suspect the whole of her is too busy being certain to travel."

"And she offered you—"

"Rest. True rest. Not unraveling—*resting*. A soldier's death, honored, complete, the oath formally released by the authority that inherited it. She can do that, you know. The ones who break a covenant inherit the right to unmake what it bound. It's the foulest clause in the foulest contract ever signed, and she has learned to use it like a scalpel." He said it without bitterness, which frightened me more than bitterness would have. "All she required in exchange was a gate opened at the proper hour. Yours, specifically. A door held open for the hunt."

The fire seemed very loud suddenly. I made myself ask the question, because the dead had taught me that the questions you avoid are the ones chewing on you anyway.

"What did you say?"

"I said no."

"That's it? Just—no?"

"She made a speech first. They always do. She said I had kept faith with a coward for two hundred years—that the Almeida who fled the altar left me holding a door for a ghost, and that loyalty to the absent is just grief wearing armor." His gauntlet flexed slowly, open, closed. "She's not wrong about the armor."

"But?"

"But I didn't make the promise to him." Ilex looked at the sword between us, at the old nicks along its edge like a calendar of small disasters. "I made it to the road. To the people who would someday come down that road needing a gate held. I have spent two centuries learning the difference between serving a man and serving what a man ought to have been. The Regent thinks that distinction is sentimental. She's welcome to. Sentiment is the only material I've ever built with that held."

I discovered I was gripping my own hands hard enough to hurt. I made them stop.

"Thank you," I said, and it came out smaller than the thing deserved.

"Don't thank me yet." He picked up the sword and slid it home across his back with a sound like a book closing. "Refusing her has consequences. She offered me rest; I refused; therefore she knows I know we're hunted, and she knows I know the marked ash is ours to find. Which means she expects us to run differently now. Faster, or in a new direction, or split apart." His eyes were very old and very tired and absolutely clear. "So tomorrow we do nothing clever. We keep the pace, we keep the bearing, we keep each other. Cleverness is a language she speaks fluently. Stubbornness she has never once beaten."

"And the residue?" I asked softly. "How much longer do you have, Ser Ilex?"

For the first time since I'd known him, the knight looked away from me.

"Ask me again at Coral," he said.

Which was not an answer, and both of us knew it, and neither of us said so, because some silences are the kindest furniture a friendship owns.

* * *

I couldn't sleep after that, so I did what I always do when I can't sleep: I carved, and I listened.

The whalebone was a scrap from our stores, meant eventually for a hinge-pin, unworthy of art, which made it perfect for confession. I worked the knife along the grain and let the whispers come up through the marrow the way they always did—the murdered covenant-makers, my congregation of the betrayed, murmuring their names and dates like a litany that had never once been answered.

But tonight the litany had shifted key. Tonight, under the old names, I caught something new: a rhythm, faint and far off, like footsteps keeping pace with ours. And threaded through it, thin as wire, a voice I recognized from descriptions—from Ilex's account, from the shape of dread the others carried whenever the Court came up in talk.

The Regent's voice, in the ash, in the marrow, everywhere the world had frayed.

*"The knight refuses,"* it said—to itself, or to the dark, or possibly to me, because nothing that hunts you is ever truly unaware of you. *"Sentiment. Good. Sentiment is predictable. And the anchor-child hears everything... Let the child hear this, then: I do not need his betrayal. I need only his ending. And endings, little heir, I keep in stock."*

The knife slipped. I swore, stuck my thumb in my mouth, and sat very still in the dark with my heart going like a trapped bird.

Three paces away, Ilex stood his watch, facing north, burning at his three ends. Behind him, Odile slept with her scarred hands curled shut. Beside her, Sef's chart had slipped from her face, and in her sleep she was counting something—stars, maybe, or days, or reasons to hope.

Tracked. Read like tracks in snow. And the Regent had just informed me, through the bones of a fish long dead, that she was done being patient.

I woke them both. It took some explaining, and Odile took it badly in the specific way she takes everything—by immediately re-knotting every ward-line within reach—but Sef went pale in a way I recognized, the pallor of a scholar meeting a footnote that changes the whole text.

"If she spoke through the bone," Sef said, turning her astrolabe over and over in her fingers, "then she's not just tracking us. She's *tuned* to us. The marked ash isn't a trail, Kestrel. It's an antenna."

"Then we cut the antenna."

"We can't. But we can make it lie to her." And Sef smiled—thin, fierce, the smile of someone whose life's work had died once already and refused to die twice—and began, right there in the freezing dark, to sketch a false trail in chalk and salt and stubbornness.

Behind her, Ser Ilex watched the northern horizon, where the ash-dark had begun, very faintly, to move.
