## 21. Midpoint: The Drowned Altar Seen

We smelled the sea a full day before we saw it.

I want to be clear about what that meant for me, a person raised in a valley where the most adventurous smell was Odile's cooking after she'd found the wine. Salt. Brine and rot and something underneath both that my bones recognized before my head did—a cold mineral sweetness, like licking an old coin, like standing in a room where someone died singing.

"That's not the sea," Ser Ilex said, nostrils flaring in a face that didn't strictly need to breathe anymore. "That's Coral."

"You can tell from *smell*?" Sef asked.

"I was stationed at its gates for forty years." He said it the way you'd say *I once worked a tedious posting in accounting*. The revenant had a gift for making the miraculous sound like a chore rotation.

The dead had been getting louder for two days. Not the murdered covenant-makers—they were always loud, a permanent congregation in the back of my skull—but all the others, the ones who'd drowned on the pilgrimage roads over centuries, the ones whose bones fed those bone-white coral gardens we'd been skirting since the marshes. They weren't whispering names or dates or grievances anymore. They were humming. All of them, in tune with each other, a sound like a hive made of grief, and every mile east it got warmer and more certain, and I got less able to pretend I couldn't hear it.

"Tell them to stop that," Odile said, third morning, hands jammed in her coat pockets because they shook when she let them out. Ward-scarred fingers. She'd stopped pretending her hands were fine somewhere around chapter fifteen of our acquaintance, so to speak.

"They're not doing it at me. They're just—" I hunted for the word. "Rehearsing."

"Rehearsing what?"

"I don't know. Something they've been waiting a long time to sing."

Odile made a noise that meant *I hate everything about this quest and specifically your skull*, which by then was practically a term of endearment.

* * *

The cliff came at us without warning, the way coasts do—trees thinning, ground rising, and then nothing but sky where the world should continue. We walked out of a stand of wind-bent pines onto a shelf of grey rock, and the four of us stopped at the edge the way one body stops, all at once, like a held breath.

Below us, the sea burned.

Not fire-burned. Luminous. The water went on forever, and every fathom of it glowed a pale blue-green, as though someone had sunk the stars we'd lost into the deep and they'd taken root there and multiplied. Waves rolled in slow and heavy, and each crest shed light like a spilled jar of it, so the whole surface seemed to breathe. Farther out—impossibly far, miles, and yet I could see it plain as the hand in front of my face—the glow concentrated into a shape beneath the water. Rings. Terraces. Spires. A city laid out in concentric circles around a central dome, drowned but not ruined, lit from within like a lantern seen through frosted glass.

Coral. The drowned altar. The thing we'd walked half the world toward on instructions from a murdered woman I'd met inside a piece of whalebone.

"Well," said Sef, in a very small voice. They'd had their journal out before I'd finished blinking, already sketching, and their hand was shaking badly enough that the lines came out like seismograph readings. "Well. It's real. I calculated the light refraction wrong by—I don't even care. It's *real*."

"It was always real," Ilex said. "You keep being surprised by that."

"You keep being surprised that I'm surprised. You've been dead for three hundred years; everything surprises me."

They bickered the way they always did, comfortably, a married couple who'd never married and had in fact only known each other some months but had skipped straight past courtship into mutual exasperation. I barely heard them. I was listening to the sea.

The humming in my skull had resolved. Standing at the cliff's edge, it wasn't a rehearsal anymore. It was a choir, and it was enormous, and it was singing something old and slow and patient, and every note landed in me like a hand pressed flat against my sternum. Not pulling. Not demanding. Just—*noticing*. The way a house notices its door opening.

"Kestrel." Odile's voice, careful. "Your eyes are doing a thing."

"What thing?"

"A glowing thing. A faintly terrifying glowing thing."

I put my hand over my chest, absurdly, as if I could hold the light in. "They're singing," I said. "All of them. Everyone who ever drowned coming here, everyone who was buried in the coral down there, everyone who—" I stopped, because the next part arrived with the weight of certainty rather than inference. "Odile. Those aren't ruins."

She followed my gaze down to the rings and spires beneath the burning water.

"The city isn't a city," I said. "It's a grave. It grew. Every terrace is made of people. The whole altar—" My voice did something humiliating in the middle, cracked sideways, and I pushed through it because they deserved the truth before the truth pushed through me. "The whole altar is built out of the covenant-makers. And everyone who came after. And kept coming. For a thousand years."

* * *

We made camp back among the pines, because none of us could look at the sea and think at the same time. Odile built a ward-ring around the site out of habit and three bent copper pins, muttering the way she did when the wards came easy, which was rare now, which she pretended not to notice when it happened.

Sef sat with their charts spread on a flat rock, weighing little river stones on each corner, cross-referencing sketches against calculations against the bone tokens I'd been carving since Brackish Ford—the dead woman's directions, rendered in whalebone, which Sef had finally cracked outside Lumenhold like a codebreaker finding the alphabet.

"The coordinates converge," they said, not looking up. "There. Where the dome is. That's the altar itself. That's where the oath gets sworn." A pause. Their quill hovered. "Kestrel, the light down there. I've been measuring it. It doesn't behave like bioluminescence. It behaves like—it fluctuates in patterns. Repeating patterns."

"Say what you mean, Ardent."

"I think it's starlight." They looked up then, and their young face had the terrible brightness it got when hope and horror arrived holding hands. "I think the drowned stars fell into the sea, or were put there, or the sea caught them when the sky went dark—and I think the city has been keeping them alive the same way the Ashen Court keeps theirs caged in Lumenhold. Except down there nobody's hoarding them. Down there they're just... shining. For free. The way they're supposed to."

Nobody said anything. The pines talked to each other in the wind. Under everything, faint as a pulse through a wall, the choir sang.

"So it's true," Odile said at last. "It's actually down there. Everything the dead have been dragging you toward." She was turning one of her copper pins over and over, worrying it. "An altar grown from corpses, sitting on a bed of stolen—or rescued—stars. Guarded, if the stories hold, by—"

"Wardens," Ilex said.

Everyone looked at him. He sat apart from us the way he always did, armor dim, sword across his knees, watching the dark where the sea-light stained the clouds above the horizon a faint drowned green. It was the most animated I'd seen him in days.

"When I was bound to the road," he said, "the older revenants told us what waited at the gates of Coral. Things in the shape of people, but wrong. Too tall. Too smooth. Faces like unfinished sculpture. They walk the terraces at the city's rim, they said, and they do not sleep, because they cannot, and they do not attack, because they do not need to. Nothing hostile reaches them. The sea handles most pilgrims. The rest turn back on their own, when they understand what they're looking at." His hollow eyes moved to me. "The first generation of them were worshippers. People who loved the covenant enough to come to the altar and never leave. The coral took them kindly, they used to say. Gave them bodies that wouldn't drown. Made them priests and gatekeepers both."

"And the ones after?" Sef asked quietly.

"The ones after are everyone else. A thousand years of pilgrims who reached the gates and stayed." Ilex turned his sword slowly on his knees, a quarter-turn, the way he did when thinking. "I knew some of their names. Before."

Before meaning: when he was alive, when he was a knight of an order that no longer existed, when he took an oath that killed him and kept him anyway. Ilex's history came out of him in pieces like that, dropped casually between quarter-turns of a blade, each fragment heavier than the last.

"If they don't attack," I said, "then what do they do?"

"They recognize," Ilex said. "That's all. They look at you and they know what you are. And whatever you've spent your whole life pretending to be—" he inclined his head at me, courteous, awful, "—they will see it. There's no arriving at Coral unannounced. The city will know its heir the moment you touch the water."

"Heir," Odile repeated. She said the word like she was testing whether it would bite. It usually did.

* * *

I couldn't sleep, so I went back to the cliff.

This was stupid. I knew it was stupid. Everything in the last months had trained me to know exactly how stupid walking alone at night toward an eldritch luminous sea was, and I did it anyway, because the choir was singing, and because—for the first time since the stars went out and the wards shattered and my whole safe grey life tore open like wet paper—the song didn't hurt.

It fit. That was the horrifying thing. All those years in Hollowmere, the whispers in the bone, the names and dates of murders no living person remembered, the constant low-grade agony of hearing the dead and having nowhere to put what they said—and now, near the altar, the voices arranged themselves. Harmony instead of cacophony. It was like a hand unclenching that I hadn't known was clenched. It was like putting down luggage I'd carried so long I'd mistaken it for a spine.

And that frightened me more than the wardens did. Because Odile had taught me what wards felt like from the inside, and this—this ease, this rightness—felt like a ward too. A lovely one. A welcome one. Come in, it said. Come home. Settle. Stay. And things that wanted you to stay forever, in my experience, were never saying it for your benefit.

I stood at the cliff edge in the dark, and I looked down at the drowned city, and this time I saw them.

They walked the outermost ring. I counted eleven before I stopped counting, pale figures moving along the highest terrace just beneath the surface, unhurried, graceful in the wrong way that tall things are graceful. From this height they were almost beautiful—white shapes against blue fire, leaving faint trails of light, like comets with nowhere to be. Worshippers. Pilgrims. A thousand years of people who loved something enough to be remade by it, still keeping the doors, still walking their rounds.

One of them stopped.

Mid-stride, mid-round, in the middle of that vast slow procession, one white figure went still on the outermost terrace, and tilted its unfinished face upward, directly toward the cliff. Toward me. Across a mile of black air and burning water, across every rational objection I owned, I felt its attention land on me like a coin placed on a sleeping tongue—cold, exact, and unmistakable.

It didn't point. It didn't move. It simply stood there, looking up, and after a moment the singer beside it stopped too, and then the next, and the next, a stillness spreading ring by ring inward through the drowned city like frost crossing a window, until the entire outer procession stood facing the shore. Facing me. The choir in my skull didn't stop singing. It changed key.

It changed to welcome.

Behind me, gravel shifted. I didn't startle; I knew the tread. Ilex came to stand at my shoulder, and I watched him follow my gaze down to the silent, attentive city, and I heard the sound he made, which was not a gasp, because revenants don't gasp—it was lower than that, older, the sound of a man recognizing his post after three hundred years.

"They see you," he said.

"Yes."

"Of course they see you." He drew a slow unnecessary breath. "Kestrel. They're not waiting for the tide, or the season, or the Court. They've been standing those rounds for a thousand years waiting for exactly one thing." His gauntleted hand settled on my shoulder, gentle as snowfall, heavy as a verdict. "They're waiting for you. And now that they've found you—"

Down below, at the center of the sunken rings, the great dome began, very slowly, to brighten.

"—I don't think they intend to let you leave without an answer."
