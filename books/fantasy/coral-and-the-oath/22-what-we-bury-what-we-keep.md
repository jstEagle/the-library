## 22. What We Bury, What We Keep

We made camp that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, because Odile said the stones would hold a ward better than open ground and Sef said the stars—by which he meant the absence of stars, which was all any of us had to work with—were clearer from the ridge, and I said nothing at all, which by then the others had learned to read as agreement.

The tower had come down long enough ago that its stones had opinions about it. I could feel them when I touched the rubble: boredom, mostly, and a faint offended dignity. Stones don't gossip like bones do. They just sit with what happened to them forever, like old men in a tavern corner.

Ser Ilex did not need a fire. He stood his usual post at the perimeter, helm tilted toward the dark road, being patient the way only the dead can be—patient as furniture, patient as geology. But when Odile got the flames up and Sef started rationing out the dried plums like a moneylender counting coin, Ilex turned and came closer, and that alone told me something was happening. Revenants don't drift toward comfort. They drift toward duty. Whatever this was, he'd decided it was one of ours.

"So," Odile said, settling onto her bedroll with her bad knee propped on her pack, a flask balanced on her knee like an offering. "I've been thinking."

"Spare us," Sef said.

"I've been thinking," she continued, with the serene cruelty of a woman who has been interrupted by better people than him for forty years, "that we are four living souls, one dead one, and several hundred dead ones riding in Kestrel's skull, and we have never once sat down and talked about it. About *them*. The dead. What we want from them. What we're carrying."

"That sounds like an evening of cheer," Sef said. He was turning his brass astrolabe over in his hands, not measuring anything with it, just holding it. He did that more lately. It had been his whole life once—the sky, the countable wheel of it—and now it was an ornament to a funeral.

"You have a better idea?" Odile asked. "Because the alternative is me describing my knee in detail, and I have diagrams."

Sef sighed the sigh of a man who has lost arguments to Odile Vane before and knows the pattern by heart. "Fine. What do you propose?"

"A game. A ritual. Call it what you like." She knocked the flask against her knee, twice, like a gavel. "Everyone answers one question, honestly, or drinks. The question is: if you could ask the dead for one thing—one thing, mind, they're not genies—what would it be?"

Silence, except the fire doing its small busy work.

"That's morbid," Sef said.

"We are walking into a drowned city full of corpses to reforge a covenant older than every kingdom on the map," Odile said. "Morbid is our profession now. Drink or answer."

He drank. Then, because Sef Ardent was constitutionally incapable of leaving a question unanswered once he'd admitted it existed, he set the flask down and looked into the fire.

"My mother," he said.

I hadn't known that. I'd known the shape of his loss—the observatory closed, the charts burned as heresy under the ash-dark, a life's arithmetic rendered suddenly worthless—but not the woman inside it. Nobody had. We were all of us experts in other people's silences by then.

"She died two winters before the stars went out. And she—" He stopped. Started again, in the careful voice he used for calculations he wasn't sure of. "She made me promise I'd look up every night. Not study. Just look. She said the sky was the only letter everyone got, free, every night, and it would be ungrateful not to read it." His jaw worked. "I looked the night they went out. I was looking when it happened. And I keep thinking—if I could ask the dead, I wouldn't even ask her anything. I'd just tell her I kept looking. That I never stopped. Even when there was nothing up there but smoke. I want credit for attendance."

The fire cracked. Somewhere off toward the road, Ilex shifted his weight, stone on stone.

"That's not asking for anything," Odile said quietly. "That's worse. That's wanting a witness."

"Yes," Sef said. "Exactly. That's my answer."

Odile nodded like he'd passed some exam she'd invented on the spot, and turned to me, and I felt the question arrive before she spoke it—not through the bone-sense, nothing so dramatic, just the ordinary human dread of being looked at by someone who intends kindness.

"Kestrel."

"I'm thinking," I said.

"Don't think too long," she said. "Thinking is where you keep everything."

* * *

Here is what I might have said, if I had been brave, or drunker, or less practiced at the particular sleight of hand I'd been performing for weeks:

*I carry a piece of starlight in my pack that talks to me.*

That was the truth, sitting three feet away in my satchel between the spare shirt and the salt pork, wrapped in wool like an infant. The shard we'd stolen from Lumenhold. It had started speaking eleven days ago, on the marsh road, low and warm as a hearth-voice, and what it said was simple: *You will stand at the altar and you will be asked to hold every grief in the world until the end of time. Or you could not. You could put the voices down. All of them. Forever. You could finally rest.*

It knew things. That was the worst of it. It knew about the nights I'd lain awake in Hollowmere at nine years old, pressing my palms over my ears because the dead in the whalebone wouldn't hush, because they never hushed, because quiet was the one country I'd been born without papers for. It offered me the one thing no oath, no covenant, no found family could give.

And I hadn't told Odile. I hadn't told Sef. I had let them plan around me, trust me, build their hopes on me like a load-bearing wall, while I carried a secret that could crack the whole house.

So when Odile asked what I'd ask the dead for, the honest answer was tangled up with the lie, and I chose the lie's cousin instead.

"Directions," I said.

Odile raised an eyebrow. Sef frowned. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"When I was growing up," I said, "the voices were weather. Rain, sometimes. Flood, sometimes. I learned to live under them the way you learn to live anywhere—badly, and with a lot of practice. And every voice wanted something. Justice, mostly. Rest. To be *named*, a lot of them—they'd been dead so long nobody remembered they'd ever been alive, and that's its own kind of dying, the second one." I turned my hands over, palm-up, the carver's calluses catching the light. "But there was one voice, years ago, an old man drowned in the mere, who never asked for any of that. Every time he surfaced he'd say the same thing: *ask me the way.* And I was a child and I didn't understand, and eventually he stopped coming, and I've thought about it ever since. So if I could ask the dead for one thing—it would be to find him again. And ask him the way. Ask him what he knew that made him think a direction was worth more than a name."

It was true. That was the trick of it—I'd learned you could hide almost anything behind a smaller truth, the way you hide a blade in bread. Everything I'd said was real. It simply wasn't the whole weight of what I carried, and the gap between those two things was widening every day, and I could feel it widening, the way you feel ice thinning under your boots, and I kept walking anyway.

"The way to where?" Sef asked softly.

"Coral," I said. "I think he knew the way to Coral before any of us did."

Odile studied me for a long moment with those ward-smith's eyes that had taken the measure of a thousand bindings and could probably take mine, if I ever stood still long enough. Then she nodded, slow, and I couldn't tell—and I still can't tell, and some nights that uncertainty is the loudest voice in me—whether she believed me or was letting me keep my secret the way you leave a lamp lit in a window for someone who won't come home.

"All right," she said. "Directions. Fine. But you get a follow-up later. I'm keeping the receipt."

"Noted," I said, and reached for the flask, and she let me have it, which was mercy, or tactics.

* * *

Her own answer came after a fight with herself that lasted most of a plum. I watched her jaw do the arithmetic.

"You all know I helped build the Hollowmere wards," she said at last. "I've confessed as much. I've apologized as much. Kestrel has been disgustingly gracious about it, which is somehow worse than anger." I shrugged; she pointed at me. "Stop that. Anyway. Here's the part I haven't said. When I took that commission—young, stupid, flattered that anyone wanted my work—I didn't just build walls. I built them *specific*. I tuned them. There was a family inside that valley, and I wove the pattern so tight around them they couldn't so much as dream loudly. And I told myself it was containment. A weapon, safely stored. And then I spent thirty years drinking in Brackish Ford telling myself the same story, and the story wore thin, and underneath it was the truth, which is that I never asked what was being contained. I took the gold and I didn't ask." Her hand had gone to her scarred fingers, worrying the old burn ridges the way she did when she thought no one was looking. "So. If I could ask the dead for one thing. I'd ask her."

"Who?" Sef said.

"The last Almeida before Kestrel. Whoever she was. Whoever my wards shut in." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I'd ask her to tell me exactly what it was like. Every year of it. I'd listen to all of it and I wouldn't drink through a single hour. Because I owe her that, and she's dead, and dead women don't collect debts, which is the great convenience of hurting people who can't follow you home."

The silence afterward had texture. Ilex broke it, which surprised me—he spoke rarely, and when he did it was like watching a mountain decide to file paperwork.

"I would ask for my orders," he said.

We all turned. Firelight moved on his helm and found no face to land on, only shadow.

"In life I served the Oath," he said. "In death I serve the road. Both times, someone else held the map. If I could speak to the dead—to *my* dead, the ones who gave me my charge, whoever they were—I would ask them one thing only: *was I right?* Did I understand the orders correctly? Because I have guarded this road for longer than kingdoms have names, and I have killed travelers who strayed from it, and I have buried pilgrims who honored it, and I have never once been certain. Certainty is for the living. But I would like to know, before whatever comes next, whether the thing I gave everything for was the thing I thought it was." A pause. The fire popped. "I suspect the answer is no. I suspect no one understands their orders. I suspect we all guard roads we misread." He tilted the helm toward me, and though there were no eyes behind the visor, I felt seen down to the ledger of my sins. "Ask your drowned man the way, bone-carver. And when he tells you, check it twice."

* * *

We sat with that a while. Odile poured the last of the flask into four unequal shares and one symbolic drop flicked toward the tower stones, which struck me as either superstition or courtesy, and with Odile it was always both.

Then Sef, because he was Sef, ruined the solemnity in the best possible way. "This is statistically absurd," he said. "Do you realize what we are? A disgraced ward-smith, a bankrupt astronomer, a revenant with performance anxiety about his job, and a bone-carver who communes with the murdered founders of civilization. And we're sharing dried plums. In a ruin. Discussing our feelings."

"Feelings," Odile agreed. "At night. Like animals."

"If my colleagues at the observatory could see me—"

"They'd burn you as a heretic, love. They already did."

"—they'd be *astonished*," Sef finished, undeterred, "that the heretic turned out to be right about the sky. Well. Right about the cage, anyway." He went quiet, then, because that was the edge of it: the stars weren't dead, we'd proven that much in Lumenhold, they were *held*—and everything now hung on whether four people and a corpse could spring the lock. He looked across the fire at me. "Kestrel. When we get to Coral. When you swear this thing, if you swear it—we'll be at Lumenhold, Odile and I, doing the other half. We won't see it happen. Whatever you feel, you'll feel it alone."

"I'm used to alone," I said, before I could stop myself, and heard how it sounded, and Odile heard how it sounded, and nobody said anything, which was its own conversation.

The truth was that I was no longer used to alone, and that terrified me more than the Regent did. You can survive anything if solitude is the water you swim in. But I'd spent months learning the temperature of company—Sef counting me calm through the Choir of the Drowned, Odile teaching me ward-craft word by word with ruined hands, Ilex standing his silent vigils as if guarding me were a form of prayer—and I had begun, without noticing, to need it. And the shard knew that too. It whispered differently these days. Not *you could rest.* Now it said: *you could rest and keep them. Silence the dead, swear a shallow oath, go home to your fire. Everyone lives. No one has to lose you.* It had learned to dress its bargain in the language of love, which is how bargains always learn to dress.

And I lay there under the starless sky, wrapped in my blanket beside people I would have died for and probably would die for soon, listening to the shard hum its honeyed arithmetic, and I thought: *tomorrow. Tomorrow I'll tell them. Tonight the fire's good and the plums are gone and Ilex is humming something that might be a lullaby from a country that no longer exists, and tomorrow, definitely tomorrow.*

Every liar's calendar is full of tomorrows. Mine was no exception. I fell asleep mid-resolution, and dreamed of a drowned old man pointing down a long dark road, saying *ask me the way, ask me the way,* while behind him something bright and warm sang my name in a voice I almost trusted.

* * *

I woke to Odile's hand on my shoulder and no fire, only embers, only gray pre-dawn, only her face lit by the last coals looking east along the ridge road where Sef stood with his spyglass braced on the broken wall.

"What," I said.

"Smoke," she said. "Far south. A lot of it. Too much for cook-fires."

I climbed up beside Sef and took the glass and found it: threads of black rising off the horizon in a dozen places, spread wide, methodical, the smoke-signature of lights being put out one by one across the whole southern dark. And beneath the smoke, faint as breath on cold glass, a glow that had been there yesterday and was not there now.

Lumenhold was burning. The hoarded light was going out.

"Regent's moving," Odile said behind us, flat and fast, already kicking dirt over the embers. "She's not chasing us anymore. She doesn't have to. She's closing the whole world's hand." Her eyes met mine over the dead fire, and in them was the question I'd promised her a follow-up on—the question of whether I was telling her everything—and I smiled the smile I'd been practicing since the marshes, and said, "Then we'd better move."

The shard, warm in my pack against my spine, murmured: *See? They'll need you soft. They'll need you rested. Let me carry it.*

I walked east, and said nothing, and the lie walked with me.
