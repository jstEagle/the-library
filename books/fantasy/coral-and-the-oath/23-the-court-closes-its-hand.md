## 23. The Court Closes Its Hand

I want to tell you that we saw it coming. That Sef read the omens in the ash-fall and Odile rigged warning wards along the trade roads and I heard the dead whisper of armies on the move, and that we stood ready, grim and prepared, when the end came for Lumenhold.

We did not see it coming. We were eating stew.

To be fair to ourselves—and I have decided I am always entitled to fairness—it was very good stew. Odile had traded a warding sigil scratched on a copper plate for a haunch of salted mutton, and she'd been defending her claim to the cook pot all evening with the ferocity of a woman who had once fought a duel over a recipe, which, as far as I could determine, she had.

"More thyme," she said, for the fourth time.

"It's perfect," Sef said, for the fourth time, in the tone of a man who has learned which battles are survivable.

"You're both liars and cowards," Odile said, "but you're welcome."

We were camped in the ruins of an old tollhouse two days' ride south of Lumenhold, where the road split toward the coast and the marshes beyond. Ser Ilex stood at the doorway the way he always stood at doorways—like a hinge given opinions. The shard of caged starlight sat wrapped in wool at the bottom of my pack, humming its sweet, poisonous lullaby. *You could rest,* it said, when it thought I wasn't listening. *You could sleep so deeply.*

*I'm eating stew,* I told it.

*Stew ends,* said the shard, which was, annoyingly, true.

The first sign that anything was wrong came not from the dead, or the wards, or any of my carefully cultivated supernatural senses. It came from a goat.

A goat came down the road at a dead gallop, riderless, its load of pots clanging, its eyes white-rimmed with terror. It shot past our camp, took the fork toward the marshes without slowing, and vanished into the dark like a small bleating comet.

"Huh," said Odile.

Then came the smell. Woodsmoke, but wrong—the scorched-sugar stink of hoarded light being burned out of its housings. I had smelled it once before, in the alleys beneath Lumenhold's lantern-quarter, where the light-hoarders kept their wares.

Then came the people.

They came up the road out of the north in a ragged river: families with everything they owned strapped to their backs, carters pushing carts whose wheels had cracked days ago, a woman carrying a birdcage with no bird in it because, she said later, the cage was the last thing her mother ever gave her and she would carry it to the sea if she had to. Their faces were gray with ash-dust. Many of them were crying. Some were past crying and wore the terrible calm of people who have watched their whole world become a story they tell survivors.

"The Court," one man gasped, when I caught his elbow. "The Regent's riders. They went through the Lantern Gate at dusk and they—" He made a gesture, opening his hand, letting something go. "All of it. Every lamp. Every window. The sun-cult's great beacon, the shrine-lights, the street-globes. Everything that burned free."

"They put them out," said a girl beside him, maybe ten, with the flat voice children use when they have decided the adults have failed some final exam. "Not blew them out. *Put* them out. Like pinching."

Behind us, Ser Ilex drew his sword—not fast, not slow, with the weary economy of a man doing arithmetic. "Lumenhold was the last free city," he said. "Every light between here and Coral now answers to the Ashen Throne." He looked at me. "Except one."

I did not look at my pack. I looked very hard at not looking at my pack, which is its own kind of confession, and Ilex saw it, because revenants miss nothing; death has already taken everything else from them, so they keep their attention sharp.

"Kestrel," he said quietly.

"I know."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about becoming someone worth following: it happens by accident, in the space of about twenty minutes, and afterward you are not permitted to resign.

The tollhouse had walls, a roof half intact, and a well. By midnight there were forty-one refugees crammed into it, because word travels faster than fear and slower than goats, and someone—I will never learn who—had told the next family, and the next, that there was shelter at the old tollhouse, that there were people there who knew wards, that one of them glowed faintly if you got close enough.

That last part was the shard, leaking through the wool, and I hated it, and I let it leak anyway, because a faint glow in a black night is worth more than my principles.

Odile worked until her fingers bled. She is the finest ward-smith I have ever met, which is a low bar since she's also the only one, but even accounting for that, what she built that night out of chalk, copper wire, broken cart-axles, and pure spite was a masterpiece: a ring of quiet around the tollhouse that would turn Ashen eyes aside, that would make forty-one exhausted people look like empty ruin to anyone riding past.

"It won't hold against a determined search," she warned me, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime around noon.

"Will it hold against a bored patrol?"

"Bored patrols are my specialty. Bored people don't check corners."

Sef organized the interior, and I learned something about him I should have guessed from the start: an astronomer spends his life cataloguing chaos into order, and forty-one terrified people are just another sky, only louder. He sorted them by need—wounded here, families there, the elderly near the fire, the ones too proud to accept help assigned to guard duty so they could help while pretending not to. Within two hours the tollhouse felt less like a refugee camp than a strange, crowded household.

And me? What did the last heir of the Oathkeepers do?

I listened to the dead.

Because here was the thing: among those forty-one people, seven carried griefs so fresh and heavy that the newly dead had followed them down the road like dogs that wouldn't be left behind. A husband. Two brothers. A daughter, six years old, who had died in the crush at the Lantern Gate when the crowd panicked, and whose ghost stood at her mother's shoulder all night saying *it didn't hurt, Mama, it didn't hurt,* which was a lie, because the dead lie kindly sometimes, and I let her lie, because some mercies are worth the cost of a little dishonesty.

At dawn I found the mother sitting alone by the cold fire, holding the birdcage.

"Your daughter," I said, before I could talk myself out of it. "She wants you to know it was quick. And she thinks the cage is beautiful. She says—" I swallowed. The girl was tugging my sleeve, feeding me the words, and I let them come. "She says you should fill it with candles someday. One candle for every year. She'll count them."

The woman stared at me. I braced for horror—for the valley reflex, the flinch, the *witch, the freak, get away from me.*

Instead she began, very quietly, to cry, and then, even more quietly, to smile, and she said, "She counted everything. Since she was three. Steps and stars and spoons." She pressed the cage to her chest. "Thank you."

I walked away before my face could finish whatever it was doing, and nearly walked into Odile, who was leaning against the doorpost pretending to inspect the hinges.

"Well," she said. "That was almost competent."

"Don't."

"No, really. You've gone and done a kindness. In public. There'll be consequences."

"There already are." I nodded at the road, where the line of arrivals had not stopped. Word was spreading. *There are ward-smiths at the tollhouse. There's a bone-carver who talks to the dead. They're going to Coral.* By midmorning we had sixty-three. By noon, eighty.

"We can't take them with us," Odile said. It wasn't cruel. It was arithmetic. "The marshes will kill half of them. The dive under the sea needs air-wards tuned to four bodies, not ninety. And every extra mouth makes us slower, and every hour slower is an hour closer to the Regent figuring out where we are."

"I know."

"And yet you're standing there making plans to do it anyway, because you have your mother's jaw when you're about to be stubborn."

"You never met my mother."

"I met her crest on a prison wall, which is close enough." She sighed, long and theatrical, and pulled the rag off her shoulder. "Fine. Here's how this works, Oathkeeper. We don't take them to Coral. We build them a place to be. A waystation—a real one, warded, provisioned, defensible. Somewhere the roads can point to. Somewhere that isn't us."

I stared at her. "You want to found a town."

"I want to found a *fortified charitable institution*, which is a town that admits what towns are for." She was already sketching in the dust with a stick: walls, wells, a ward-ring, fields. "There's a spring a mile east. Good stone in the ridge. And these people need somewhere to *be*, Kestrel, and gods know the realms aren't offering. If the covenant gets reforged, someone has to hold the ground between now and then. Might as well be people who owe us their lives. That's how power starts, incidentally. I've seen it done badly several times."

"And if we fail? If Coral goes wrong?"

"Then they'll still have walls and water, and they'll hate the Ashen Court on their own time." She jabbed the stick into the drawing's center. "Hope isn't a plan. But a well is."

* * *

We called it Emberfall, eventually, though at the time it was mostly called "the place with the mad smith" and, after Odile conscripted forty able-bodied refugees into digging a foundation trench, "the place with the tyrant."

It grew the way things grow when necessity does the planting. Walls first, because Odile insisted, laid from ridge stone by hands that shook and hands that didn't. The ward-ring second—she taught as she worked, shouting instructions to anyone who'd listen, and to everyone's surprise a one-eyed carter named Brannic turned out to have a smith's feel for resonance, and by the third day he was closing gaps in the ring she hadn't gotten to yet. Wells third. Gardens fourth, planted in ash-enriched soil that Sef swore would yield double within two seasons, because starless skies make hungry earth.

Sef, meanwhile, had climbed the tollhouse roof and refused to come down except for meals.

He'd built something up there—a frame of cart-poles and salvaged mirror-glass, angled at the sky. Not to see stars. There were no stars. He aimed it at the dark itself, at the places where the sky was darkest, and he measured.

"You can't measure nothing," I told him, on my way past with a bucket.

"You can't measure nothing," he agreed, not looking up, ink to his elbows, "which is why I'm measuring something. The dark isn't uniform, Kestrel. It never was. Even before, there were patches—cold spots in the sky, places where the old charts showed no stars at all. Everyone assumed those were voids." He tapped his charts, curling at the edges. "Voids don't move. These do. Slowly, over centuries, toward Lumenhold. Toward the hoards. The darkness isn't absence. It's consumption. The Court's cages don't just hold starlight—they feed on the sky around them."

"So the Regent didn't just steal the stars."

"She started an avalanche. And every lamp she puts out widens the fall." He finally looked at me, and I saw the thing underneath the scholar-calm: fear, worn smooth by use, like a coin rubbed thin. "Which means the clock on us isn't political anymore. It's physical. Every month we wait, the sky loses a little more of whatever lets it remember how to burn."

I thought of the shard in my pack, humming its lullaby. *You could rest. Stew ends.* I thought about telling him about it—about the whispers, the promises, the way my hand lingered on the wool wrapping a moment too long each night.

I kept walking. The bucket was heavy. That was my excuse, and I used it.

* * *

On the sixth night, the Ashen Court sent someone.

Not riders. Not soldiers. A single figure walking up the road in the full dark, unarmed, unhurried, wearing gray so pale it seemed to drink the firelight rather than reflect it. Our ward-ring sang a low note of warning as she crossed it, and Odile was on her feet with a crowbar before the echo faded, which told you everything about Odile's relationship with proper weaponry.

The emissary stopped ten paces out and raised empty hands. Under the hood was a face like weathered paper, and eyes that had the particular patience of someone being paid by the hour.

"A message from the Throne of Ash," she said. Her voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. "For the Oathkeeper Kestrel Almeida, and companions."

"We don't take messages from murderers," Odile said.

"Everyone takes messages from murderers. Murderers deliver promptly." The emissary produced a scroll sealed with gray wax, and set it on a rock, and stepped back. "The Regent offers terms. She knows where you are—she has known for three days, and chose not to act, as a demonstration of goodwill. She asks only this: that before you reach the drowned altar, you consider what your oath would cost the people behind these new walls. A living anchor feels every realm's pain forever. Do you imagine the realms grow gentler? Do you imagine the pain diminishes? You would spend eternity drowning, Oathkeeper, and your friends would spend eternity watching. Is that the future you're building them?" She inclined her head. "The Regent broke the old covenant because mercy demanded it. She will break the new one for the same reason. Think on it. The road remains open. The Court does not chase what walks willingly into its hand."

She turned and walked back down the road, and the dark folded around her like water.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Brannic, the one-eyed carter, picked up the scroll, looked at it, and threw it in the fire without asking anyone's permission.

"Message delivered," he said.

I loved him immediately and permanently.

But that night I lay awake doing exactly what the emissary wanted, because I am weak in precisely the ways they predicted. *Do you imagine the pain diminishes?* I thought of the choir waiting beneath the sea, hundreds of years deep. I thought of holding all of it, forever, with Odile and Sef growing old beside me, watching me drown slowly in everyone's grief. And underneath that fear, quieter and worse, the shard murmured: *or none of it. Or silence. Or rest.*

I got up. I went to the edge of the ward-ring, where the dark was honest, and I held the wrapped shard in both hands and did not open the wool, and I said, out loud, to it and to myself:

"I know what you're offering. And I know why I haven't thrown you in the marsh yet. Both of those things scare me."

The shard pulsed once, warm as a sleeping bird, and said nothing, and somehow that was more convincing than any argument.

Behind me, gravel shifted. Sef stood at the tollhouse door in his blanket, chart ink still on his chin.

"How much of that did you hear?"

"The part where you argued with your luggage." He came over, arms crossed against the cold. "Kestrel. The shard. It talks to you, doesn't it? It's been talking for a while."

My stomach dropped through the ash-dirt. "Sef—"

"I'm an astronomer. I spent six years learning to detect faint signals in noise. You think I can't hear a second voice coming off my best friend?" He wasn't angry. That was somehow harder. "I'm not telling Odile tonight. Tomorrow, maybe. Tonight I'm telling *you*: whatever it's promising, the price is that you stop trusting us with the truth, and look where that road goes. We've all walked it. Ask Ilex what his oath cost him. Ask Odile about the prisons she helped build because she trusted the wrong certainty." He put a hand on my shoulder, brief and warm. "You don't have to carry it alone. That's the entire point of us."

He went back inside. I stood in the dark a while longer, and for the first time since the shard had begun to whisper, the whisper sounded smaller.

* * *

We left Emberfall on the ninth morning, and leaving was its own education.

Ninety-four people lived there now. Ninety-four people lined the unfinished walls to watch four of us walk out the gate—because Ilex had announced, in his rust-and-thunder voice, that he would march first, and no one had ever successfully disagreed with him about marching. Odile had warded the gate herself, twice, and checked it a third time, and pretended she wasn't crying, and fooled absolutely no one.

Brannic clasped my forearm at the threshold. "Come back through this gate," he said. "However it ends at the sea. Come back and see what we grew."

"If the sky comes back," I said, "you'll see what you planted under real stars."

"Then hurry," he said, and grinned with his one eye, and pushed us gently onto the road.

We walked south, toward the marshes, toward the drowned altar, four small figures on a long gray road. Behind us, smoke rose from Emberfall's chimneys—woodsmoke, honest smoke, fires lit freely by people choosing their own light. Ahead, the land sank toward the salt flats, and the dead grew louder in my bones, and somewhere far off, Ilex said he could feel the old road waking under his feet, the road he'd guarded once, the road home to Coral.

"Kestrel," Ilex said, after a mile. He had stopped, and was looking back the way we'd come—at Emberfall, yes, but past it, north, where the horizon had begun to glow a faint and terrible gray, like dawn that meant harm.

"What is it?"

"That light," he said. "It isn't sunrise. The Regent has emptied Lumenhold, and now she is moving south along the roads behind us, putting out every fire that answers to no one." His sword whispered an inch out of its sheath, testing the air. "She is clearing the board. Which means, Oathkeeper, that everything between here and the sea is about to belong entirely to her—and entirely to us. There is no one left in between."

Odile spat, and settled her pack, and started walking. "Good," she said. "I've always done my best work with an audience."

Sef laughed—one short, startled bark of it—and followed her.

And I stood a moment longer, looking north at the false dawn, feeling the dead stir in my marrow like a tide answering a moon, and then I turned south and ran to catch up, because the sea was waiting, and beneath the sea, the altar, and beneath everything, the choice.

The road to Coral had never been emptier.

Or more ours.
