## 24. Descent Beneath the Sea

The sea at Coral's edge was not blue. I want to be clear about that from the start, because every story I'd ever heard about the ocean—the ones smuggled into Hollowmere in peddlers' songs and my grandmother's grudging bedtime tales—insisted on blue. This water was green-black and luminous at once, like a bottle of wine held up to a lamp, and it glowed faintly from below, as if something down there had been left burning for a very long time and no one had thought to put it out.

"Nobody puts it out," Odile said, when I said as much aloud. "That's the point of a covenant. It burns until someone releases it." She was kneeling on the cliff shelf with her sleeves rolled up, chalk in one hand and a hammer in the other, inscribing wards onto flat stones she'd spent three days preparing. Her hands shook. They had shaken since Coral's ritual had backfired through her ward-work and scorched her palms, and she hid it badly, which for Odile meant hiding it at all. "So don't touch anything glowing unless I tell you to."

"That's going to be difficult," Sef said. He stood at the cliff edge with his charts wrapped around his shoulders like a shawl against the sea wind, staring down at the drowned city. "Everything down there is glowing."

"Then don't touch anything."

"You're describing an entire expedition conducted with our hands in our pockets."

"I'm describing survival. You're welcome."

Ilex stood apart from us, as he always did, at the exact spot where the cliff's shadow met the water. Revenants don't fidget, which makes them excellent listeners and unnerving company. His armor was crusted with salt from the marsh crossing, and his sword—older than any nation currently claiming to exist—rested point-down on the stone like a man leaning on a cane.

"The gates will know you," he said. It was the fourth time he'd said it. "The coral remembers Oathkeepers' blood. It grew from it."

"Yes," I said. "You've mentioned."

"And you are still coming."

"I'm still coming."

He nodded once, satisfied, and went back to being a statue. Ser Ilex's conversational style could best be described as *liturgical*. You got the important parts repeated until they were worn smooth.

* * *

The air-wards were Odile's masterwork, or would have been, if her hands had let her finish them properly. Each of us would wear a ring of script inked onto skin—collarbone for me, forearms for Sef, because he'd insisted on having his free for note-taking, which Odile called "the most astronomer thing you've ever said"—and the wards would hold a bubble of breathable air around our heads for as long as the script held.

"How long does the script hold?" I asked.

"On a good day? An hour."

"And today?"

She looked at her bandaged hands, then at me, and gave me the crooked half-smile that was the closest thing she had to an apology for everything. "Today we find out. That's the spirit of fieldwork, Almeida. Theory is what you believe before the water gets involved."

Sef laughed. Ilex did not laugh, because Ilex had died before laughter was invented, or so it sometimes seemed.

We went over the plan one more time, because plans were how Sef kept fear in its cage: descend along the ridge path where the water shallowed; follow the starlight shard—it hung at my throat now, caged in wire, humming its sweet poisonous hum—down through the coral gardens; reach the outer gates before the hour turned; do not, under any circumstances, answer if the dead called.

"Do not answer," Odile repeated, looking directly at me. "Kestrel. Say it back."

"Don't answer the dead."

"You have the self-control of a cat in a room of unattended fish."

"I'm aware."

"I just want it on record that I said it."

"It's on record," Sef said. "I'll witness it. Everything's witnessed with this group. We should start charging fees."

The shard pulsed against my sternum, warm as a held coin. *They won't call,* it murmured, in a voice that sounded distressingly like my own. *And if they do, wouldn't you rather hear them than fear them? I can give you that. Silence, or understanding. Either way, no more drowning.*

*I said not yet,* I told it, and pressed it flat under my shirt, and hated that my hand lingered.

* * *

The water took us the way night takes a candlelit room—not all at once, but in a slow closing from the edges inward.

Cold first, shocking enough that I gasped, which is exactly what you shouldn't do while sinking, and then the ward flared green-white around my head and the cold pulled back to a dull pressure, like standing in a river in winter. The light above smeared and broke apart. Sound changed entirely: my own heartbeat became the loudest thing in the world, followed by Sef's counting—he counted everything when he was nervous, stairs, stars, seconds—and beneath both, a vast low tone that I realized after a moment was not sound at all but the dead, singing, too deep and too far away to resolve into words.

Not far away. Below. Everything below.

*Don't answer,* I reminded myself, and swam down.

Odile led, kicking with the economy of someone who had grown up around water and resented every minute of it. Sef drifted beside me with his eyes enormous behind the ward-glow, turning slow circles as he fell, cataloguing. Ilex sank like a stone with opinions, armor and all, moving only when he chose to, which somehow made him faster than the rest of us.

And then the gardens began.

I had expected coral the way you expect coral in stories: branches, fans, things like frozen flames. What we descended into instead was a forest of white pillars, each one thick as a tower and smooth as polished ivory, rising out of the dark below and branching overhead into a canopy that filtered the surface light into pale shafts. Bone-white, yes. But not bone-shaped.

Bone-*sourced*.

I knew it the way I knew voices. Every carver knows material by feel, even through water, even through wards—even, apparently, through several hundred years of the sea doing its patient work. The pillars weren't stone. They weren't mineral pretending to be bone. They were the long-dead remains of people, grown together, fused by oath-magic into architecture, and they had been growing here since the covenant broke, feeding on whatever the drowned altar still leaked into the water.

"We're swimming through a graveyard," Sef said. His voice came thin and strange through the ward-bubble. "A graveyard the size of a cathedral."

"A graveyard the size of a *city*," Odile corrected grimly. She touched nothing, but her eyes moved constantly, reading the ward-script that ran along the pillars like veins of silver—old work, pre-Imperial, the kind of craft her order had been founded to preserve and had instead been used to bury. "Look at the joins. Look at the lattice. This isn't accumulation. Someone *planned* this. The dead didn't drift here. They were planted."

"Why?" I asked, though I already felt the answer rising in me like water in a well.

"Because a lock needs teeth," Ilex said.

Everyone stopped swimming. Even the sea seemed to pause, the way it does when something large moves far beneath you.

Ilex hung in the pale light with his hands resting on his sword hilt, and for once he looked less like a statue and more like a man remembering. "When the covenant was whole, the dead passed freely to their rest. The oath carried them, the way a river carries leaves. When your ancestor broke it—" he did not say *broke*, exactly; he said it the careful way, *when it was broken*— "the current stopped. And the dead began to gather where the oath had been strongest. Here. At the altar. They came looking for the door home, and there was no door, and so they stayed. And stayed. And grew."

"Grew into *this*?"

"The dead are patient," Ilex said. "It is nearly the only virtue left to them."

The shard pulsed hot against my chest. *Ask him,* it whispered. *Ask him what happens to a revenant who stands too long in one place. Ask him what he'll grow into.*

I shut my jaw and kept swimming.

* * *

Deeper, the garden changed character, the way a forest changes as you walk from the sunny edge into old growth. The pillars thickened. The silver ward-veins brightened. And faces began to appear in the bone.

Not carved. Grown. Emerged. As if the coral, in its slow centuries of accretion, had pushed its dead gently toward the surface—here a brow, there the curve of a cheek, a closed eye, lips slightly parted as if mid-word. None of them looked afraid. That was the part that undid me. I have heard ten thousand dead voices in my life and most of them were afraid, or angry, or lost, and I had braced myself for a forest of terror. Instead the faces in the coral looked *expectant*. Like a congregation waiting for the service to begin. Like people who had been promised someone was coming.

"They're facing inward," Sef whispered. He'd stopped again, turning slowly, charting without writing. "Every face. All of them. Toward the center. Toward the altar."

"They've been waiting for an Oathkeeper," Odile said quietly. She wasn't looking at the faces. She was looking at me, and her expression had gone soft in a way I'd only seen twice before, both times when she thought I wasn't watching. "For four hundred years, they've been growing toward the door, and now the door is walking down the hill."

"Don't," I said. "Don't make it holy. I'm the heir of a coward who ran from exactly this."

"I'm not making it holy," Odile said. "I'm making it *true*. Those aren't the same thing, and confusing them is how your family ended up walled in a valley for six generations."

It was the closest she'd come to mentioning the confession since the night she made it—that she had helped design Hollowmere's wards, believing she was containing a weapon and not a child—and it landed in the water between us and sank, and neither of us reached for it. Some grievances you carry like ballast. They keep you upright.

The dead began to sing louder.

* * *

I need to describe the singing, and I need you to understand that I am a professional. I have spent my entire life translating grief into language. Words are my trade the way wards are Odile's and numbers are Sef's. And I am telling you that the choir beneath Coral's gardens did not use words, and my profession failed me completely.

It was layered. That's the nearest I can get. Voices upon voices upon voices, centuries deep, and none of them individual anymore—they had melted together the way snow melts into a river, and the river had a direction. It flowed downward. Every voice in that impossible chorus was flowing toward the altar, and the altar was drinking, and the singing never stopped, had never stopped, would not stop until—

My ward flickered.

Just once. A stutter in the green glow, like a lamp in wind. Odile saw it instantly—of course she did, she'd built it—and her hand shot out and gripped my wrist, and her mouth formed words I read more than heard through the water: *Breathe slower. You're burning the script.*

Because that was the secret of her air-wards, the thing she hadn't told us on the cliff: the wards fed on the wearer. Breath, heartbeat, attention. Panic burned them fast. Calm stretched them thin as taffy. Which meant that descending through a singing graveyard of my murdered ancestors required me to be calm, and the universe, as usual, found this funny.

*I can help,* the shard offered, gentle as a lullaby. *One word from you and the voices go quiet. You could breathe easy. You could think. Isn't that what you need right now? Just quiet, just for the descent—*

"No," I said, out loud, into my own bubble of air, and Sef glanced over, and I shook my head: *not you.* The shard subsided, sulking, warm as a fever.

But saying *no* out loud did something. The dead heard it.

The singing changed.

It didn't stop—I don't think it *can* stop—but it shifted, the way a crowd shifts when the person they've waited for finally comes through the doors. A new thread entered the weave, close, very close, right at the edge of my hearing, and it was not the melted centuries-choir. It was one voice. One woman. Clear as a bell rung in an empty room.

*You came,* she said. Not accusing. Not demanding. Wondering, almost. The way you'd speak to a letter that arrived forty years late. *You actually came.*

I knew her voice. Of course I knew it. She had been the first dead voice I ever heard with intention, back in Hollowmere, whispering names and dates through a piece of whalebone while I pretended to carve. The woman who had started all of this, who had organized the murdered covenant-makers into something like a chorus, who had given me directions downriver when every living instinct said stay behind the walls.

*Don't answer,* Odile had said. On record. Witnessed.

But Odile had also said *don't touch anything glowing*, and here we were, swimming through the largest glowing thing in two realms, and rules made on dry land have a way of dissolving.

I didn't answer with words. I couldn't spare the breath. Instead I did the only thing a bone-carver could do in a garden made of bones: I reached out, slow, careful, and laid my palm flat against the nearest pillar—against the brow of some dead maker whose name I would learn soon enough, whether I wanted to or not.

The pillar was warm.

Under my hand, the ward-vein lit silver-bright, and the singing gathered itself around that single note like a tide gathering around a rock, and for one suspended moment the entire garden—every pillar, every face, every drowned century—was silent in the way a held breath is silent.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the whole forest of the dead at once, the choir spoke my name.

Not Kestrel. My other name. The old one. The one on the shattered wardstone, the one on the crest, the one I had been running from since the night the stars went out:

***Oathkeeper.***

The gates appeared below us out of the dark—vast, twin-leaved, wrought from a single span of coral whiter than all the rest, sealed with a knot of silver script older than any alphabet Sef had ever charted. And the knot was moving. Unpicking itself. Thread by silver thread, the lock was opening, and we had not touched it.

Behind me, Ilex drew his sword. The sound rang through the water like a bell through a church.

"Something else heard the name too," he said. "Down in the dark. Many somethings. They are not the dead."

The last silver thread slipped loose.

The gates of Coral began, slowly, majestically, to open—and from the black water beyond them, something answered the choir with a song of its own.
