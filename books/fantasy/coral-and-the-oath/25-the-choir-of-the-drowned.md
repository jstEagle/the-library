## 25. The Choir of the Drowned

I want it on the record that I did not want to go inside the drowned city. Nobody wants to go inside a drowned city. It's right there in the name, between *drowned* and *city*, two words that should never be allowed to share a doorway.

But the altar was inside, and the oath was at the altar, and the dead had been singing at me since I was six years old, so in we went.

Odile's air-wards were a marvel and she knew it, which meant she'd been insufferable about them for two full days. They sat over our heads like invisible bells, holding out pockets of breath in the crushing dark, and she'd woven each one by hand with fingers that shook when she thought we weren't watching. Her hands had been failing since Coral's outer gate collapsed on us. She didn't talk about it. She talked about everything else, loudly, constantly, as though she could drown out her own body by out-narrating it.

"Left at the archway," she said now, tapping my shoulder twice. Underwater, sound traveled strangely; her voice came through the ward-link thin and tinny, like a ghost with a head cold. "Mind the coral. The white kind. It grows toward warmth and you're the warmest thing down here, so it *likes* you, which is worse."

The white coral did like us. It reached. We swam down a boulevard that had once been a street, past doorways furred with bone-white branches, past statues that I refused to look at directly because every one of them had a face and every face was mid-scream or mid-song, and down here I couldn't tell the difference anymore.

Sef swam at my left, counting. They'd been counting since the gates—strokes, breaths, turns, anything with a number in it. I'd thought at first it was a nervous habit. Then I'd realized it was a kindness. Sef Ardent had spent their whole life believing the universe was legible, that if you counted carefully enough it would tell you the truth, and when the stars went out they'd lost the thing they counted. So now they counted us. Four heartbeats in the dark. It was the most romantic thing anyone had ever done and it was completely unintentional, which is somehow even more romantic.

Ilex brought up the rear. A revenant doesn't need air, which meant he swam without wards, which meant he swam *through* the water rather than in it, and watching him move was like watching a memory of swimming. He'd been quiet since the gate. Quieter than usual, and Ilex's usual was a silence you could store grain in.

*We're close,* said the dead, in the way they said everything, all at once, from every direction. *We're close, we're close, we're close.*

"I know," I said, and then remembered the wards didn't carry my voice out, and felt briefly like a madwoman talking to fish.

The street ended at a plaza, and the plaza ended at the thing I'd been feeling in my chest for a mile: the temple. Coral's heart. It rose out of the seafloor like a fist made of lace, a cathedral the size of a mountain, its spires grown over and through with the white coral until it was hard to say where the building stopped and the grave began. Light came from inside it—not torchlight, not starlight, but a pale glow that pulsed, slow, patient, like the city was breathing around us.

And the singing.

I'd heard the dead my whole life. Whispering, mostly. Muttering. The occasional shriek on a bad night. I had a whole taxonomy of it, the way a birdwatcher has birdsong. But I had never heard them *together*. In Hollowmere the wards had kept them out and apart, each voice a single instrument in its own sealed room.

Inside Coral, there were no rooms.

The choir began before we crossed the threshold. It didn't build. It didn't swell. It simply *was*, the way gravity is, the way drowning is—one instant I was Kestrel Almeida, bone-carver, reluctant heir, person with a name and a body and a plan, and the next instant I was four hundred years of murdered covenant-makers, all of them at once, all of them singing.

*

I have tried, since, to describe what it was like, and the closest I can get is this: you know how grief feels when it's yours? That weight in the chest, that heat behind the eyes, that sense of being too large for your own skin because the loss doesn't fit inside you?

Now imagine it isn't yours.

Now imagine it belongs to every dead person who ever swore an oath to hold the realms together, and every one of them was murdered for it, and every one of them has been waiting in the dark under the sea for the length of an empire to be heard, and you are the one person alive who can hear them, and you have just walked into the middle of all of them at once.

It wasn't sound. That's what I couldn't explain to the others after. It was *weight*. It came in through my bones, through the scars on my hands, through the place behind my sternum where I'd learned to keep the whispers folded up small. And it wasn't anger. That was the worst of it. I'd braced for anger. Anger I knew how to carry; I'd been carrying my own for years. This was grief—grief so old it had polished itself smooth, grief that had given up on being heard and simply continued, the way a river continues, the way rot continues.

*We sang the covenant whole,* they sang. *We sang it in four voices and it held the sky up like a roof beam. We sang and the stars answered. We sang and we were answered. We sang—*

*—and then the knives, and then the dark, and then the long quiet, and then the singing again, because what else is there, what else is there, what else—*

My wards held. My body held. I did not.

I was aware, distantly, of Odile shouting my name through the ward-link. I was aware of the white coral reaching for me with what I now understood was not hunger but *recognition*—it had grown from the covenant-makers' remains, and I was the last thing in the world that smelled like home to it. I was aware of my mouth opening, of salt flooding in past the air-ward's edge because I'd stopped maintaining it, because maintaining it required being a person, and I was currently a bell that someone had struck and couldn't stop ringing.

*Hear us,* sang the choir. *Hear us, hear us, heir-of-the-liar, daughter-of-the-runners, last-of-the-oath—*

*Hear us, hear us, HEAR US—*

I heard them. Gods help me, I heard every word of every voice of every century, and there was no floor to it, no bottom, and I went down through my own skull like a stone through lake-ice, and somewhere above me my body hung in the black water of a drowned cathedral with its eyes open, and somewhere above me someone was shouting numbers.

*

"—seven, eight, nine—breathe, Kestrel, *breathe*—four, five, six—"

A hand on my face. Small, cold, precise. Sef's hand, pressed flat against my cheekbone the way you'd press a hand to a ringing bell to still it.

"Seven, eight, nine. Again. One, two, three—"

Numbers. Sef was counting, and not strokes or breaths or turns this time. They were counting *me*. Four heartbeats in the dark, and mine had gone ragged and arrhythmic, and Sef had put their hand on my face and started counting it back into time.

"Four, five, six. You're in Coral. You're in the temple. Your name is Kestrel. One, two, three—"

The choir was still there. That's the thing people don't understand about grief—it doesn't stop because someone loves you. The dead went on singing, all their centuries of it, the full weight of it pressing down on me like the sea itself. But now there was a rhythm under it. A spine. Sef's voice, thin and stubborn through the ward-link, laying numbers across the flood like planks across a river, and I grabbed the first one—*one*—and then the next—*two*—and hauled myself up along them hand over hand.

"Three, four, five. That's it. Stay with me. Six, seven—"

My heart found the count. My breath found the ward's edge again. The choir was still enormous, still everywhere, but it had edges now, and I was on the inside of my own skin looking out through my own eyes, and the first thing I saw was Sef's face an inch from mine, tear-tracked, salt-burned, absolutely furious with fear.

"Don't you dare," they said. "Don't you *dare* go somewhere I can't count to."

"I'm here," I said. It came out as a croak. "I'm—Sef, I heard all of them. All of them at once. There's no—there's no *separating* them, they're—"

"I know." Sef's thumb moved once across my cheekbone, wiping away water that might have been sea or might not. "I watched your face. I know."

Odile was suddenly there too, gripping my shoulder hard enough to bruise, her ruined hands shaking. "Never do that again," she said, in the voice of a woman who had already composed the lecture and was saving it for dry land. "You dropped your own air-ward. You *dropped* it. Do you know what your lungs look like when—no. No, you don't, because you were busy *communing*. Kestrel. Look at me. The next time the dead start singing, you tell me *first*, and I decide whether we stay."

"They weren't singing *at* me, Odile. They were just—" I gestured, helplessly, at everything. The cathedral. The dark. The whole drowned weight of the past. "They're *in* the walls. They're the walls. This whole city is a choir and we swam into the middle of it."

"Then we swim out of the middle of it."

"No." Ilex's voice. He had drifted to the temple's far wall, where the glow was brightest, and the light did strange things to his revenant face—made the old wounds stand out silver, made him look the way he must have looked alive, a knight in his prime standing in a burning doorway. "No, Odile. This is why we came. This is the door." He turned. "Kestrel. What did they say? Before Sef called you back. What did they *say*?"

I closed my eyes. The choir was still there, but quieter now, or I was stronger now, or both—the way a river is quieter once you've learned to swim in it. And under Sef's counting, under the rhythm they were still keeping without being asked, one-two-three-four like a heartbeat with a spine, I could listen the way Ilex had been teaching me. Deliberately. With edges. With a *self* to do the listening from.

*Hear us,* the dead sang. Softer now. Almost gentle. *Heir-of-the-oath. We have waited so long to be heard that we forgot we could be. Come to the altar. Come and hear the terms. We will not ask what you cannot give.*

A pause, and then, in a voice that was older than the others and sadder, a voice I somehow knew was the first of them, the first Almeida's own covenant-sister:

*We will only tell you the truth of it. What it costs. What it cost us. And then, little carver, you may choose—and whatever you choose, we will sing you kindly either way.*

I opened my eyes. Three faces looked back at me through the pale glow—Odile's fierce and frightened, Sef's steady and wet-eyed, Ilex's ancient and unreadable and, I thought, just possibly hopeful, which from a revenant who remembered the original oath firsthand was either a blessing or a warning.

"They want me at the altar," I said. "They want to tell me the terms."

"Terms," Odile repeated flatly. "Oaths have terms. Covenants have terms. Kestrel, in my professional experience, whenever something ancient and powerful uses the word *terms*, the terms are never *good*."

"No," I agreed. "I don't imagine they are."

Sef's hand was still on my face. I realized neither of us had moved to change that, and that I didn't want to, and that the counting had softened from a rescue into something else—a rhythm we were keeping together now, their numbers and my heartbeat, one-two-three-four, a small private meter in the middle of a drowned cathedral full of the singing dead.

"Whatever they ask," Sef said quietly, "you don't decide it alone. That's the whole point of us. That's why there are four of us."

"Three and a half," Odile muttered, flexing her scarred fingers. "I'm working at half."

"Four," Sef said, with the mild immovability of someone stating an astronomical constant.

Ilex pushed off from the wall and drifted toward us, and the glow moved with him, and beyond him, down at the heart of the temple, I could see it now: the altar. A ring of white stone rising from the seafloor, carved all over with the same crest that had been on my family's wardstone—the running stag, the broken crown—and around it, grown thick as a forest, the white coral, and in the coral, faces. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not screaming. Singing.

They were all looking at me.

"Little carver," said the first voice, gentle as deep water. "Come and hear what your family ran from."

I took Sef's hand off my face, held it a moment, and let go. Then I swam toward the altar, and the choir parted around me like a sea around a stone, and every dead voice in the drowned city sang my name.
