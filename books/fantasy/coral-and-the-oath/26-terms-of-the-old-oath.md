## 26. Terms of the Old Oath

The dead did not knock. They never had. But in Coral, beneath a sea that glowed like spilled lantern oil, they at least had the decency to arrange themselves into something like a receiving line.

I stood on the altar floor where my boots had no business being dry—Odile's air-wards held a bubble around our little party, and beyond it the water pressed close as a curious crowd—and I watched the drowned covenant-makers assemble themselves out of coral and memory and grief. There were nine of them. I knew there would be nine before they finished forming, because the whalebone at my throat had been counting down to this moment for weeks, whispering *nine* like a debt collector.

"Charming," Odile muttered beside me. Her hands were wrapped to the wrists in linen bandages, and she gestured with her chin because her fingers were past gesturing with. "Nine ghosts. No chairs. No tea. Very hospitable, your ancestors."

"They're not all my ancestors," I said. "Only one of them is."

"Only one," said Sef softly, "is the problem."

Sef was right, which was becoming a habit of Sef's that I both relied on and resented. The eight others stood in a loose crescent, faces worn smooth by centuries of water, wearing robes and armor and one truly magnificent hat that had somehow survived its own funeral. The ninth stood apart. Younger than the rest by decades, it seemed—or perhaps just younger-looking, the way cowards are preserved in amber by their own refusal to age into consequence.

He wore my face. Not exactly—the jaw was heavier, the hair braided in a style four hundred years out of fashion—but the resemblance sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Corvane Almeida. First of his name, last of his courage. The man who built Hollowmere.

"Well," I said, because someone had to break the silence and apparently cowardice runs in families but so does talking too much, "you have some explaining to do."

* * *

Here is what the dead told me, in voices that came not through ears but through bone, through marrow, through the place where I keep my name:

Once, there had been no border between the realms of quick and dead. That sounds poetic. It was not. It was a wound. Magic bled between worlds without shape or law; the dead walked among the living uninvited, sometimes tenderly, often not. A farmer's grandmother might return to bless the harvest, or a stranger's murderer might return to finish the argument. There was no difference in the rules, because there were no rules. Just weather. Grief-weather. Hatred-weather. It rained the dead on everyone equally, and everyone drowned eventually.

So nine people—eight who believed and one who was talked into it—made an oath. A covenant with structure: the dead would pass through gates instead of walls, guided and witnessed; magic would flow along channels instead of flooding; and holding every gate, every channel, every seam of the world together, there would be an anchor. One living person. Bound to the covenant with their whole self, feeling everything that crossed the borders. Every death in every realm. Every grief. Forever.

"One person," Odile repeated flatly, when the chorus of dead voices finished laying it out. "Feeling *every death*. All of them. At once." She turned to me. "Kestrel. Tell me you're hearing what I'm hearing."

"I'm hearing what you're hearing."

"And you're hearing what *they're* hearing," Sef added quietly, nodding at the crescent of dead. "That's rather the point, isn't it?"

Thank you, Sef. Truly. Your precision is a gift that keeps giving.

* * *

They showed me what anchoring felt like. Not all at once—they were kind enough, or cruel enough, to give me only a taste—but a taste. One breath's worth.

It was every death at once: the soldier in the ash-fields calling for a mother three kingdoms away, the fisherwoman going under in dark water, the old man dying warm and unafraid in a bed that smelled of lavender, the child, the child, the child. It was also every birth, every homecoming, every hand held in the dark—but I want to be honest here, since honesty is supposed to be the theme of this chapter, and say that in that single borrowed breath, the pain did not share the stage. It owned the theater. The joy was there somewhere, the way stars are there during daytime. Technically present. Practically invisible.

When it released me, I was on my knees. Sef had a hand on my shoulder, counting under their breath—one-two-three-four, the rhythm they'd used to anchor me in the choir of the drowned—and Odile was saying something sharp and practical about breathing that I understood only later.

"You fled from one breath," said the woman in the magnificent hat. Her name, the bones told me, had been Maren Ossa, and she had been the covenant's first speaker. "He fled from an eternity of them."

All eight of the true covenant-makers turned—not dramatically, not with pointing fingers, just with the terrible quiet weight of people stating facts—to look at Corvane Almeida.

He didn't look back at them. He looked at me. And his voice, when he finally spoke directly, was the worst thing I'd heard in Coral, worse than the choir, worse than the drowning-grief: it was *reasonable*.

"You don't understand yet," he said. "You think you do, because you've carried whispers your whole life. But a whisper is a candle. The anchor is the sun. I lasted eleven days." He held up his hands, and I saw they shook—not with age, but with memory, which does not fade the way flesh does. "Eleven days of feeling every death in two realms, and I was young, and I was strong, and I went into the mountains and I dug until my nails split and I raised wards over a valley and I told myself I was protecting the world from *them*, the dead, the magic—but Kestrel, look at me. Look at me and tell me what you see."

I looked. I saw my own face wearing guilt like an heirloom.

"A coward," I said.

"Yes," he said, simply, without flinching, and I hated him a little less for not arguing, and hated myself more for the softening. "The valley was never a sanctuary. It was a hiding place. The Almeida wards weren't built to protect anyone from the dead. They were built to protect *one man* from the oath. Everything else—the generations born inside, the quiet, the forgetting—all of it was fallout. I made a valley-sized blindfold and called it peace."

"The wards kept magic out," I said. My voice sounded far away. "My whole life, I thought we were safe. I thought safety was the point."

"It was the point. *My* point." He stepped closer, and the water beyond Odile's ward-bubble shivered, as if the sea itself leaned in to listen. "And now the Court has broken what I built, and the gates stand open, and the choice comes back around like a debt with interest. Feel it all forever—or find another blindfold. And I am telling you, descendant, from four hundred years of experience inside mine: *the blindfold is worse.* You will spend eternity hearing yourself flinch."

"That's a hell of an endorsement," Odile said. "For either option."

* * *

We argued. Of course we argued. I want it recorded that I did not simply fall to my knees and accept my doom like a heroine in a ballad; I bargained, I demanded alternatives, I got genuinely unpleasant about it.

Could the burden be shared? Rotated? The dead considered this with visible sorrow—Maren Ossa's hat brim dipping like a slow bow. It could not. The covenant required a single point of tension, a keystone. Split the anchor among many and you didn't lighten the load; you created seams, and seams were how the Ashen Court had been able to pry things open in the first place. Nine makers, and only one anchor, because the original eight had each offered themselves and been refused by the terms—the oath wanted *one*, whole, willing.

Willing. There was the knife hidden in the bread. The anchor could not be forced, could not be tricked, could not be volunteered by proxy. It had to be chosen with full knowledge. Which meant the dead had brought me here to know everything, and then let me choose anyway. Which meant the cruelty and the respect were the same gesture.

"Is there a time limit?" I asked. "If I refuse. If I walk away. What happens?"

"The gates erode," said Maren. "Slowly, then all at once. The Court consolidates. In a century, perhaps two, the realms fuse back into the old wound, and everything that died in between—" She stopped herself. Dead people should be better at euphemism. They have the practice.

"And if I take it?" I asked. "How long does an anchor last? Do I die at the end of it?"

Corvane answered, and he answered gently, which was somehow the cruelest available option. "You don't die of it. You live a long time. Longer than you want to. The covenant holds you the way a nail holds a beam—by being driven through."

* * *

Odile pulled me aside afterward, into the lee of a coral pillar shaped like a folded pair of hands. She checked the ward-bubble first, always the smith, always the craft before the conversation.

"Say what you're going to say," I told her.

"I don't know yet. That's the problem." She leaned her bandaged forearms against the coral. "Four days ago I couldn't hold a stylus. Now I'm standing in a cathedral at the bottom of the sea listening to ghosts describe a job opening, and my contribution to the discussion is going to be feelings. I resent it."

"Take your time."

"Here's the thing." She fixed me with her gray eyes, and I braced. "Everyone in this room is going to tell you it's your choice. Your ancestors, the dead, even Sef, sweet as they are. And it *is* your choice—that's the whole cursed architecture of the thing, it has to be freely chosen. But I want you to hear from someone who's made a coward's choice and lived inside it." Her voice went low. "I designed those valley wards, Kestrel. The outer ring. I told myself it was containment of a threat. I drew beautiful, elegant lines around your family's prison and slept fine at night for twenty years. And when I found out what I'd actually walled in—" She stopped. Started again. "Cowardice doesn't feel like running. It feels like reasonable. It feels like *someone else should carry this, surely, surely there's another way, surely it isn't mine.* I know the sound of it from the inside. So when you weigh it tonight, listen hard for that voice. The one making it sound sensible. That one's lying."

"And if the voice telling me to accept is also lying?" I asked. "What if wanting to matter is its own kind of vanity?"

"Then you'll be vain and useful instead of humble and hollow." She shrugged. "I know which one I'd rather stand next to."

* * *

Sef found me later—I say found, but Sef had been orbiting me at a careful distance all evening, the way they orbited everything, collecting observations before committing to conclusions. We sat at the edge of the ward-bubble, watching the luminous sea press against it. Small pale shapes moved out there in the deep. Worshippers once, Ilex had said. Things that stayed too long near holiness and became part of its architecture.

"If you take it," Sef said, "I want you to know what I'll do."

"Sef—"

"No. Hear it. Because everyone else is giving you gravity, and I've decided to give you logistics." They unfolded a scrap of chart paper against their knee, though there was nothing to write on it down here and we both knew it; the gesture was habit, comfort, a starless astronomer's rosary. "If you take the oath, I stay. Someone has to map the new channels, calibrate the flows, figure out how light moves through a re-woven covenant. It's the work I was born for, except the sky I studied turned out to be a cage, and now I get to study the actual mechanism of heaven. Do you understand? This is not sacrifice. This is *the best assignment anyone has ever given me.*"

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I stay too. Someone has to help build whatever blindfold comes next, and I'd rather it be honest ones doing the building. Either way, Kestrel, you are not choosing between us and the void. You are choosing which version of us gets to exist." They paused. "Also Odile will drink herself half-blind either way, so that variable's stable."

A laugh came out of me, cracked and surprised, echoing strangely inside the bubble. I hadn't laughed in Coral before. It felt almost indecent, like whistling in a sickroom.

But after Sef drifted off to their charts, the laugh curdled, because underneath it I could feel the other thing. The thing I hadn't said aloud in the firelight chapter of our travels and still wasn't saying: the shard was gone—I had broken it—but its promise had left a groove in me, the way any long-carried weight leaves a shape. *Silence,* it had whispered. *Peace. No more voices, ever.* And now here were the dead offering me the opposite: every voice, forever. And some small shameful part of me noticed that both offers shared a flaw. Both of them treated the dead as noise. Weather to be silenced or weather to be endured. Neither offer asked whether the dead were, you know, *people.*

Maybe that was the question the oath actually hinged on. Not *can you bear it* but *what do you believe they are.*

* * *

I went back to Corvane alone. The other eight had withdrawn into the coral like candles guttering down to wicks; only he remained, standing where he'd stood for four hundred years of not leaving.

"Why are you here?" I asked him. "Not why did you flee. Why do you haunt *this*? If the valley was your escape, why does your ghost stand at the altar you abandoned?"

"Because fleeing isn't a place," he said. "It's a direction. I've been walking it ever since, and it turns out it's a circle." He studied me with my own eyes. "You have questions I can answer better than the others. Practical ones. About what the first days feel like. About the tricks—the way you learn to sort the deaths, to breathe between them, to find the joy-stars behind the pain-clouds. Eleven days taught me things the other eight never learned, because they never broke. Ask me. Whatever you decide, ask me."

"And if I decide to run? Build a new valley? Find a bigger blindfold?"

"Then I'll teach you the ward-work," he said, "and I'll hate myself in your company for eternity, which is, I'm told, approximately what family is."

Despite everything—despite the drowned altar and the waiting terms and the sea pressing its patient weight against the thin bright skin of Odile's wards—I laughed again. Worse than the first time. Practically blasphemous.

But then, through the laughter, through the bubble, through the bone-deep channel that had been my curse and was now apparently my curriculum, something new arrived. Not one of the nine. Something farther off, thinner, moving fast across the water above us like a shadow crossing a lamp.

Ash. I knew the taste of it now. The whole party did. We'd been eating it in nightmares for months.

And threaded through the ash-taste, faint but unmistakable, a voice I recognized—the Regent's voice, silvered and reasonable, saying words that were not meant for me but reached me anyway, the way all words reach anchors-to-be:

*"Open the gates, little heir. Or I'll open them for you—and we'll see whose oath the door remembers."*

Above the drowned altar, far overhead where the surface should have shimmered, the light of the luminous sea began to dim.
