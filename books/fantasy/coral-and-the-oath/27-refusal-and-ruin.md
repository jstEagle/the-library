## 27. Refusal and Ruin

The dead had a way of presenting the worst news as if it were a formality.

*A living anchor must hold the oath,* they sang in the drowned choir-loft of Coral, their voices moving through the water like cold currents through cloth. *One who feels every realm's ache, every death unnumbered, every grief that has no name yet and will not have one for centuries to come. One who does not set the burden down. Ever.*

*Forever,* said the woman from the whalebone—the first voice I'd ever heard, the one who'd started all this—gently, as if she were telling me the weather would be damp tomorrow. *Until the sea gives up its salt. Until the stars forget their names.*

I stood on the altar-steps with water to my knees and my heart doing something I can only describe as filing a formal complaint.

"Forever," I repeated.

*Forever,* agreed four hundred dead covenant-makers, martyrs, murderers, and miscellaneous ghosts, in the tone of people who had already done their forever and were now cheerfully volunteering me for mine.

Here is what nobody tells you about being offered the most important job in two realms: it feels exactly like being handed a shovel at your own funeral and asked to dig.

I looked at Sef, who was watching me with those starless astronomer's eyes—eyes that had spent years learning to find meaning in an empty sky and had gotten frighteningly good at it. I looked at Ser Ilex, whose revenant bones held the memory of the last person asked to swear something enormous, and who had not flinched then either, which should perhaps have been a warning about what he'd expect of me. And I looked at Odile Vane, ward-smith, drunk-in-recovery, keeper of more guilt than any one pair of scarred hands should carry, who was looking at me with an expression I recognized because I'd been wearing a version of it my whole life:

*Please don't do anything stupid to protect us. Please do the brave thing instead. We both know you won't.*

"I need," I said, "a moment."

*Take all the moments you require,* said the dead, who had nothing but time, which is easy to say when you're dead.

* * *

The moment I took lasted most of the night, and I spent it alone in a side chapel of Coral where the drowned light came through the coral lattice in long green-gold bars, like sunlight filtered through a cathedral's worth of kelp. The city breathed around me. That's the only word for it—Coral *breathed*, slow tidal contractions of stone and bone-white growth, and every breath carried voices. Not whispers anymore. Since we'd come beneath the sea, the dead were no longer content to murmur at the edges of my hearing. They spoke. They argued. They sang rounds that made my teeth ache.

And through it all, under everything, warm as a coal in my pocket, the shard spoke too.

The shard of caged starlight we'd stolen from Lumenhold had been quiet for weeks. Quiet the way a knife is quiet in a drawer. But since the choir had laid out the terms of the old oath—since the words *forever* and *every realm's ache* had settled over me like wet sand—it had begun to hum against my hip through the leather wrapping, and when I finally took it out, it was bright enough to read by, and it was speaking in my own voice.

*You don't have to,* it said, in the voice I used inside my own head, which felt like cheating, frankly. *You know that, don't you? You've known since Lumenhold.*

"Go away," I told it. Talking to starlight in an empty chapel. My mother would have been so proud, if my mother hadn't been part of a generations-long conspiracy to wall our family up in a valley and pretend magic didn't exist.

*I'm not the enemy here,* said the shard, reasonably. *They are. The dead. Four hundred of them, and more coming, and they want to pour themselves into you like wine into a cup until there's no Kestrel left, just an anchor-shaped hole that hurts on schedule. Forever.* It pulsed, gently, sympathetically. *But I could make it stop. All of it. Every voice. Every whisper. Every ghost in every realm, gathered up and folded away somewhere they can't reach anyone ever again. Silence, Kestrel. Real silence. The kind you've been dreaming about since you were six years old and first heard a murdered man counting his own wounds.*

I want to tell you I threw it across the chapel right then. I want to tell you the temptation lasted a heartbeat and my better nature won.

What actually happened is that I sat down on a bench worn smooth by centuries of kneeling worshippers, and I held the shard in both hands like a bird with a broken wing, and I listened for a very long time.

Because here is the thing about the valley of Hollowmere, the thing I understood only now, standing in the drowned city my ancestors had fled: the wards hadn't kept magic out. They'd kept the dead out. My family had built a box of silence around themselves and called it safety, and I had grown up inside that box thinking the silence was the world, and the whispers in my carvings were the sickness, and if I just carved well enough, worked hard enough, stayed small enough, the voices would leave me alone.

They never left me alone. And I had spent thirty-odd years wanting exactly one thing: for them to stop.

The shard knew that. The shard had been listening to me the whole way down the river, through the marshes, across the ash-dark markets. It had learned me the way I'd learned whalebone—by pressure, by grain, by finding the places where a thing wants to break.

*You could still swear the oath afterward,* it added, slyly gentle. *After the silence. Swear it as a formality. Anchor of a covenant with no dead in it to feel. A burden with the weight scooped out. Everyone gets what they want. The realms get their stars. Your friends get their hero. And you get to be—*

"Don't say it."

*—empty,* it said, kindly. *Empty and finally, finally quiet.*

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went back to the others at what might have been dawn, though dawn means little three fathoms under a luminous sea, and found them in the antechamber above the altar, arguing in the low urgent register of people who have been arguing for hours and have run out of new things to say.

"—the terms are the terms," Sef was saying. "You can't renegotiate with the dead, Odile, they invented stubbornness."

"They also invented murder, plague, and the ballad 'The Miller's Wife,' which has forty-one verses, so forgive me if I don't defer to their judgment on what constitutes reasonable working conditions."

"The Regent's fleet will be at the gates within days," Ilex said. His voice did the thing it always did, the revenant's trick of sounding calm and final at once, like a door closing. "Perhaps sooner. Whatever Kestrel decides, it must be decided before the Court decides it for them."

They all turned when I came in. Four faces—Ilex's weathered bone-gray calm, Sef's anxious brightness, Odile's guarded hope—and behind them, faint as frost on glass, the shimmer of the choir, waiting.

"Well?" Odile said.

I had a speech prepared. I'd composed it in the chapel, walking the green-gold bars of light, and it was a good speech, honest-sounding, full of reasonable concerns. Something about how the terms were unjust, how the original Almeida's cowardice didn't obligate me to outdo it, how surely two realms' worth of cleverness could devise a covenant that didn't require one person to suffer eternally, how love shouldn't be a life sentence—

What came out was: "No."

Sef blinked. "No?"

"No. I'm not doing it." My voice came out flat and strange, and I realized distantly that the shard was still humming against my hip, and that some treacherous part of me had already decided, sometime in the night, and everything since had been theater. "I'm not becoming your anchor. I'm not spending eternity as a lightning rod for every grief in creation because four hundred dead people and my own rotten bloodline think suffering is a virtue."

"Kestrel—" Sef started.

"No." Louder. The choir stirred; the water in the antechamber's channels trembled. "There's another way. There's been another way the whole time, and I should have seen it, and I'm going to do it now, and none of you have to carry anything."

Odile's eyes narrowed. She'd spent her life designing wards, which is to say she'd spent her life studying how power lies to people. "Kestrel. What's another way."

I took out the shard.

The effect was immediate and awful. The dead went silent—not respectfully silent, but the silence of animals before a rockslide. Ilex's hand went to a sword he hadn't carried since the marshes. Sef stepped backward into a pillar.

"That's caged starlight," Sef whispered. "Kestrel, why do you have that out? Why does it look like *that?*" Because the shard wasn't humming anymore. It was blazing, drinking the drowned light of the chapel, growing brighter as the dead grew quieter, and I understood with the clarity of someone watching their own cart go over a cliff that it had been feeding on them. On their nearness. All this time.

"It offered me a bargain," I said. My voice sounded far away. "Silence. All the dead, everywhere, folded away forever. And then the oath becomes a formality. An empty anchor. Nobody suffers. Everybody wins."

"That's not winning," Ilex said quietly. "That's amputation. The dead are the covenant's memory. Cut them out and you haven't healed the oath—you've lobotomized it."

"You don't get to talk," I snapped, and immediately hated myself, because Ilex had died once already for this road and here I was ranking suffering like a merchant ranking goods. "You don't—all of you keep talking about burdens like they're noble. Easy for you. You're dead, or you're exiled, or—" I gestured at Sef, whose life's work had died with the stars, "—or you've already lost everything, so what's one more thing? I *had* a life. A small one, a quiet one, and I liked it, and every single one of you dragged me out of it, and now you stand there and tell me the price of the exit is *forever*, and you expect me to smile and pay it because it's *meaningful*."

The words hung there, ugly and true, and nobody answered, because there was nothing to answer with. Odile's face had gone carefully blank. Sef was looking at me the way you look at a chart when the numbers stop adding up.

And the shard sang.

It didn't use words anymore. It poured itself up my arms like warmth after cold, like the first sip of wine after a long fast, and it showed me the ritual—not asked for, simply given, the way you give a child a match because it's easier than explaining fire. A circle of the altar-steps. The shard at the center. My blood, which seals or breaks covenants, drawn along the old lines. And then the gathering: every dead voice in Coral, in the realms beyond, pulled inward, folded, pressed down into the shard's small fierce heart, sealed, silenced, done.

It would take minutes. It would be painless—for everyone except the dead, and they were already dead, so really, what was the harm?

God help me, I believed it. For about nine seconds, standing in front of the people who had crossed a continent beside me, I believed that silencing the murdered was a kindness.

"Kestrel," Odile said slowly, "your eyes are doing something I don't like."

"Stay back," I said, and walked down to the altar.

* * *

Here is what the shard did not mention, in all its warm generous whispering: the dead of Coral were not loose coins to be swept into a purse. They were load-bearing.

I learned this the way I learn most things, which is catastrophically and slightly too late.

I knelt at the altar. I opened my palm with the bone-knife I'd carried since Hollowmere, since before, since the beginning of everything, and let my blood run into the channels the old covenant-makers had cut, and spoke the gathering-words the shard fed me syllable by syllable, sweet as honeyed milk.

For a moment—oh, for one shining moment—it *worked*.

The choir rose out of the water like smoke rising off a struck bell. Hundreds of voices, thousands, the murdered and the mourners and the makers, all of them streaming toward the shard's bright heart, and the silence spreading outward from me in rings was the most beautiful thing I had ever felt. Like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was carrying. Like surfacing after a lifetime underwater. Like being, at long last, gloriously, *nobody*.

Then the first pillar cracked.

I heard it even through the bliss—a sound like a giant's knuckle popping, deep in the city's bones. Then another. The coral lattice overhead shivered, shedding pale dust into the water, and the drowned light flickered, and somewhere far below us something vast shifted its weight.

"What did you *do*?" Sef screamed from the antechamber.

The dead understood before I did. Of course they did; they'd built this place. Their song changed key—from grief to warning, warning to fury—and the nearest of them, the whalebone woman, the first voice, the one who had named names and dates while I carved commissions in a quiet valley, tore herself free of the shard's pull and came at me like a wave coming at a sandcastle.

*Fool,* she said, and her voice was no longer gentle. *Child. The dead ARE the walls. The dead ARE the light. We are the mortar of this city and the memory of the oath and you are pulling the threads out of the weave—STOP—*

I didn't stop. That's the shame of it. The silence felt too good, and the shard poured reassurance into me like a drug—*almost done, almost done, nearly finished, hold on*—and I knelt there bleeding into ancient channels while Coral came apart around my ears.

The ceiling of the choir-loft went first. A section of coral-bone arch the size of a ship's hull groaned, tilted, and fell, and the water it displaced hit the antechamber like a fist. I heard Odile shout—I heard her wards ignite, the crackle-hiss of improvised protection she'd taught me to recognize on the road—and then I heard the sound no ward-smith ever wants to hear, the sound of a ward structure failing not all at once but in stages, each stage worse than the last, like stairs collapsing under someone climbing.

"ODILE!" Sef's voice, cracking.

I broke the circle. I don't remember deciding to; I remember the shard screaming in my grip as the gathering unraveled, remember the dead pouring back outward like a tide reversing, remember the silence tearing off me like a bandage off burned skin, and every voice in the realm crashing back in at once, and every one of them screaming.

The last thing the shard managed, as I smashed it against the altar-stone, was not a scream but a whisper, in my own voice: *you'll want it back someday.*

Then it burst into a spray of dying sparks, and the great arch above the altar, relieved of whatever the dead had been holding up for a thousand years, came down.

* * *

I woke to dust in the water and Sef's hands on my face, checking my skull, my jaw, counting my breaths aloud in the steady rhythm he used to anchor me in the choir—four counts in, seven counts hold, because seven was the number of visible stars in his childhood sky and he trusted it more than any prayer.

"Sef—"

"Don't talk. Breathe. You got clipped by falling debris, you've been out maybe a minute, you're fine, you're fine." He wasn't fine. His voice was shredded. Behind him, half the antechamber was gone, replaced by a tumbled reef of fallen coral-bone, and the drowned light was dimming in long slow pulses, like a heartbeat losing its rhythm.

"Odile," I said.

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

I found her under the shelf of hardened ward-light she'd thrown up in the last instant—found her because Ilex had already dug halfway to her with his bare revenant hands, stone scoring his old bones without complaint, murmuring to her the whole time in a tongue dead for a thousand years, which I understood now, and which was a soldier's prayer for the wounded, the one that says *hold, hold, you are not done yet.*

She was conscious. That was the first mercy. Her left side was a ruin of bruise and blood where the ward-shelf had collapsed inward instead of outward, and her hands—

Her hands.

A ward-smith's hands are their whole self. Odile's were mapped with old scars from twenty years of channeling power through mortal flesh, and she'd told me once, drunk and honest at a river fire, that she could read a ward's health through her fingertips the way a physician reads a pulse. Now both hands lay in her lap, and the scars across them had split and blackened, and when she tried to flex her fingers, her right hand obeyed, barely, trembling like a snared bird.

Her left did not move at all.

"Hey, bone-carver," she said. Her voice was steady. That was somehow the worst part. "You want to tell me what in the nine hells that was?"

"Odile, I—"

"Because from where I was lying," she continued, in the pleasant, terrible tone of a woman cataloguing injuries to keep from screaming, "it looked like you knelt down at that altar, ignored the terms the dead bled to teach you, tried to pull a shortcut out of a piece of stolen starlight that has been *obviously* whispering to you for weeks—don't look surprised, I ward things for a living, I could hear the damn thing humming against your hip from across a campfire—and brought half a holy city down on top of us. Did I miss anything?"

"The Regent," Ilex said quietly. He was standing at the breach where the wall had been, looking out through the settling dust toward the distant gates of Coral, and his voice had the door-closing calm again, but underneath it, for the first time since I'd known him, something that might have been fear. "The collapse will have been felt. Seen. The Court will not wait for their fleet to assemble now. They'll come at once, while the city is wounded."

"And our ward-smith," Sef said, very softly, looking at Odile's hands, "is the only person alive who knows how to rebuild the gate wards."

The silence that followed had no dead in it at all. It was entirely ours.

Odile looked down at her ruined hands for a long moment. Then she looked up at me, and whatever was in her face, I deserved all of it and she gave me less than half, and that was worse.

"Well," she said. "Somebody fetch me my chalk. I suppose we're doing this the hard way."

Beyond the breach, far off through the luminous dark, the first red lights of the Ashen Court's vanguard were already kindling against the gates of Coral.
