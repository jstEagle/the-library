## 28. Odile's Hands

I had imagined Odile's anger a hundred times in the hours after Coral's ceiling came down on her. I rehearsed for it the way you rehearse for a storm—bracing, flinching at every rumble of distant stone, waiting for the front to hit.

What I got instead was soup.

She was propped against a fallen pillar in one of Coral's side galleries—a chamber the collapse hadn't reached, all ribbed bone-white arches and that faint underwater glow the drowned city gave off even through its wards, like moonlight filtered through milk. Sef had made a nest of salvaged rope and sailcloth around her. Ser Ilex stood watch at the gallery's mouth with his sword drawn and his face doing whatever revenant faces do instead of worry.

And Odile was eating soup. Badly.

That was the thing. That was the whole terrible thing, laid out in front of me in a borrowed bowl: she held the spoon between two palms, the way a child does, and brought it up in a cupped scoop, and half of it spilled back before it reached her mouth, and she did it again, patiently, without a word, while Sef pretended very hard to study a star chart that was upside down.

"Kestrel," Odile said, not looking up. "You're looming. You loom like your grandmother, did you know that? She loomed at me across a ward-table for six hours once. Worst negotiation of my life."

"I didn't—" My voice came out thin. I started over. "How are your hands."

"Wet," she said, "and full of soup." Then she set the bowl down on her knee—her knee, because setting it down anywhere else would have required hands—and looked at them.

I looked too. It felt obscene, but I looked anyway, because she wanted me to, or maybe because some cowardly part of me needed the proof. The ward-scars had always been there: the pale lattice across her fingers from thirty years of drawing lines of power into her own skin, the burn-freckles up both forearms where a ward had bitten her in Brackish Ford before I ever met her. But now the lattice was *lit*. Faint threads of gold under the skin, like cracks in porcelain filled with light, running from fingertip to wrist and then simply stopping, as if something had drawn a border and told the damage it could go no further.

They were beautiful. That was the cruelest part. They looked like something a jeweler had made on purpose.

"They don't hurt," Odile said. "That's the joke. They ache when it rains and they're stiff in the morning, same as always. What they don't do—" She tried, deliberately, to close her right hand into a fist. The fingers curled halfway and stopped, trembling, like a door caught on a warped frame. "—is work."

The gallery hummed with the sound of the sea pressing down on miles of ward-glass above us. Somewhere deeper in Coral, water dripped in slow time.

"You can still speak the forms," Sef said quietly, from behind their chart.

"I can still *recite*," Odile corrected. "There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, and I'm choosing not to be insulted by the distinction today." She flexed the ruined hand again—halfway, trembling—and let it fall. "Ward-work is hands, boy. Always has been. You draw the geometry in the air and you hold it there while the words seat themselves, and if your hands shake, the geometry shakes, and if the geometry shakes—" she nodded upward, toward the collapsed corridor, toward the place where her last great working had failed, "—the ceiling comes down."

"It didn't fail," Sef said. "It held long enough. We'd all be dead otherwise."

"Yes, thank you, I was there, I'm aware of the arithmetic." Odile closed her eyes. "Held long enough. What an epitaph. *She held long enough.* Put it on my stone. Leave room underneath for *and the soup situation was humiliating.*"

It should have been funny. It was almost funny. But her voice cracked on *humiliating*, just barely, just enough, and Sef went white, and I understood suddenly that this—the soup, the jokes, the epitaph—was Odile Vane's version of screaming.

* * *

Here is what happened, in case you weren't crushed under a mile of ocean when it happened, which most of you weren't:

In chapter twenty-seven—I mean, three days ago; forgive me, the dead have gotten me into the habit of thinking in chapters, everything has a shape now, everything has a *place in the sequence*—three days ago I refused the anchor's burden, and I tried to take the shortcut the shard offered instead. Silence every dead voice forever. A painless eternity. I spoke a rite I barely understood, using power I had no right to hold, and Coral answered by shrugging a quarter-mile of itself into rubble, and Odile threw up a holding-ward with her bare hands to keep the rest of us alive, and her hands paid the toll.

The shard is broken. I broke it later, alone, in the dark, and I will tell you about that when I'm ready, which may be never. What matters tonight is the rubble, and the hands, and the fact that Odile had not said one single angry word to me since.

That was worse. Anger has edges. You can grip anger. This patience of hers had no edges at all. You could drown in it.

So I sat down across from her, on the wet stone, in the milk-light, and I said the thing I'd been circling for three days.

"Teach me."

Her eyes opened. "Teach you what?"

"The final ward-work. Whatever it is you were going to do at the altar. The anchor's warding—the thing that holds a person together while the oath runs through them. You designed it. You've known how since Lumenhold." I swallowed. "You'll need someone to carry it now. So teach me."

For a long moment she just looked at me, and I braced for the storm again, certain that *this*, finally, would crack the patience open—being asked to hand over her life's last work to the person who'd broken her hands.

Instead she sighed the sigh of a woman who has been waiting for someone to ask the obvious question.

"Took you long enough. I was starting to think I'd have to teach the astronomer." She jerked her chin at Sef. "No offense."

"Some taken."

"Good. Keeps you sharp." She shifted against the pillar, wincing, and settled. "Right. Lesson one. Come here where I can see your hands."

* * *

I want to tell you that learning ward-craft from Odile Vane was a solemn and sacred experience, full of meaning, etcetera. It was not. It was being yelled at.

"No. *No.* Stop. Kestrel. Sweet summer child. What are you doing with your thumb?"

"It's—it's part of the form—"

"Your thumb is part of nothing. Your thumb is a stowaway. Tuck it. If the geometry sees a stray line, the geometry assumes you meant it, and then your thumb is part of the ward, and then the ward is trying to hold *your thumb* in place along with everything else, and believe me—" she wiggled her lit-cracked fingers at me, "—I have personal experience with parts of myself ending up inside workings that did not invite them."

We were four hours into lesson one. My shoulders ached. My hands shook. I had traced the same nine-line lattice in the air perhaps two hundred times, speaking the seating-words under my breath, and perhaps six of those two hundred attempts had been clean, and Odile had praised exactly none of them, because praise, she informed me, "is for people who've earned it, and also for dogs, and you're neither yet."

But here is the strange thing I noticed somewhere around attempt one hundred and forty: the yelling was kind. Not gentle—Odile Vane has never been gentle in her life—but kind, the way a whetstone is kind to a blade. Every correction landed exactly where the error lived. Nothing wasted. Nothing withheld. She was giving me her entire attention, hour after hour, the way she had probably once given it to apprentices at the Ward-Smiths' Hall in Brackish Ford, before her order exiled her, before any of it.

"You're drifting," she said, on attempt one hundred and sixty-something. "Where'd you go?"

"I was thinking about the ceiling."

"Ah." She studied me. "Guilt?"

"Guilt."

"Mm. Put it down."

"What?"

"The guilt. Put it down. It's a tool like anything else—you can pick it up when it's useful and set it down when it isn't, and right now it isn't. Guilt makes your hands tight. Tight hands make crooked lines. Crooked lines make ceilings come down." She said it flatly, no cruelty in it, and somehow that was the worst cut of all: she wasn't even punishing me with it. She was diagnosing me. "You'll feel it again tomorrow, I promise, guilt keeps. Now again, from the first line, and *breathe*, you're carving bone, not fighting the bone."

I started over. First line. Second. My thumb stayed tucked. Somewhere around the seventh line, with the lattice hanging in the air between us, faintly gold, faintly *right*, Odile went quiet, and I realized she had gone quiet because she was watching the ward hold steady without her hands anywhere near it.

"There," she said softly. "There it is."

And then, because she was who she was: "Don't get smug. You dropped the eighth line's tension by a hair. Again."

* * *

Sef fell asleep mid-lesson, chart over face, breathing the slow breath of the truly exhausted, and Ilex drifted off to walk his rounds, and the gallery settled into the hush of a house at night—which is an odd thing to say about a drowned city full of singing dead, but wards are wards, and Odile had thrown a silence-shell around our little camp that kept the choir at a courteous distance, and inside that shell it was almost possible to pretend we were just people in a room.

Almost.

Because Odile couldn't sleep. I saw that plainly as soon as Sef's breathing evened out. Her hands lay in her lap, and she was looking at them the way you look at a map of a country you can never return to.

"They used to be the best hands in Brackish Ford," she said, to them, or to me, or to nobody. "Did I ever tell you that? Not boasting. Well. Boasting slightly. Third-best, officially—the examiners were cowards—but everyone knew whose work actually held. Wards I drew in 'forty-one are still standing around the Ford. Probably standing right now. Little invisible knots in the air, keeping the flood-tide off the lower streets, and the people walking through them every day have no idea anyone ever made them." She turned one palm up. The golden cracks caught the light. "That was the whole point of warding, you understand. Nobody's supposed to know. The best day of my life was watching a woman walk through a doorway I'd warded against a storm-wind, dry as a bone, and she never glanced up. Never knew. Perfect craft is invisible craft."

"And now everybody knows," I said quietly. "Everybody in two realms knows your name."

"Yes, and it turns out fame is a poor substitute for a functioning thumb." But she smiled when she said it—a real one, small and tired—and then the smile faded and we sat in the honest dark together for a while.

"Ask me," she said finally.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on for three days. Out with it. You're a bone-carver; you know better than anyone that a thing swallowed whole sits in the stomach forever, but a thing carved gets to be seen. Ask."

My mouth was dry. "Why aren't you angry at me?"

She didn't answer immediately. In the shell-hush I could hear Sef breathe, and the sea lean on the city, and beneath it all—faint, patient—the choir of the drowned, singing the oath's old terms in a tongue I was only beginning to understand.

"Do you remember," Odile said at last, "what I confessed to you on the marsh road? About the valley wards?"

"You helped design them."

"I helped design the cage around your family. I spent eleven years of my life building a prison, and I told myself it was a kennel for a weapon, and then I found out it was a playpen for a child, and I have had to live with that arithmetic ever since." She turned her head and looked at me straight on, and her eyes in the milk-light were very clear. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, Kestrel? I built walls around a child. You knocked down a ceiling. One of those things deserves forgiveness a great deal less than the other, and I have not forgiven *myself* yet, so don't mistake what I'm about to say for absolution, because I'm not qualified to give it."

"Then what—"

"What I'm about to say," she said, "is arithmetic. You broke my hands. Also true: your shortcut would have ended the quest, and the Court would have won, and every free person in every realm would have spent the rest of a starless eternity hoarding scraps of light and calling it living. Also true: I chose to throw up that holding-ward. Nobody forced my hands up. I saw the rubble coming and I put my hands in its way because that is what ward-smiths *do*, it is the entire job, it is the only job—we stand between people and the falling sky. I have done it for thirty years. I would do it again tonight with these exact ruined hands, and I would do it again the night after, until the job finished me." She took a breath. "So. You broke my hands. I spent them. Both statements are true, and neither cancels the other, and if you spend one more minute of your considerable talent drowning yourself in a debt I have already declared paid, I will—"

She stopped. Looked down at the spoon. At the bowl on her knee.

"I will ask you to feed me my soup," she said, quieter. "Which you'd do. That's the horrible thing. You'd do it, and you'd hate yourself while you did it, and self-hatred makes crooked lines, and I need your lines straight for what's coming. So." She lifted her chin. "Forgiven. Declared, decreed, witnessed by the astronomer and the knight and whatever gods are still taking attendance. Forgiven. Now pick up your hands and show me the ninth line, because we haven't got forever, and I refuse to die knowing my best ward died untaught."

I don't remember deciding to cry. I was just suddenly crying, ugly and silent, the kind that comes out of nowhere like weather, and Odile watched me with an expression that was not pity—she would rather have burned than pitied anyone—but something older and more practical. The expression of a woman confirming that a tool she intends to rely on is, in fact, going to hold.

"Done?" she asked, when the worst had passed.

"Not even slightly."

"Good. Grief that admits it's unfinished is honest grief. Again—from the first line."

* * *

We worked until the shell-light shifted, which is how Coral tells time when it bothers to. By the end I could raise the full nine-line lattice and hold it for a hundred heartbeats without shaking, and Odile talked me through the tenth line—the anchor's line, the one meant to wrap around a living soul and keep it whole while an oath poured through it—without touching it herself, describing it entirely in words, her voice steady, her ruined hands folded like sleeping birds in her lap.

"Tenth line doesn't start at your fingertips," she said. "Starts at your sternum. You draw it *outward*, from the body, which means you have to know precisely what your body is worth, because the line takes its shape from your answer. Do you hear me? The final ward is shaped like your self-regard. Build it small, and it'll strangle you at the altar. Build it grand, and it'll be full of holes. Build it *true*—" she shrugged, "—and it'll hold."

"How do I build it true?"

"That, carver, is not a ward-smith's problem." She smiled, tired and fierce. "That one's yours. Sleep. Tomorrow we do the tenth line, and the day after, the Regent arrives, and I intend for you to be insufferably competent by then."

I lay down on the rope and sailcloth beside Sef, who murmured something in sleep about declinations, and stared up at the ribbed arches glowing overhead like the inside of some enormous patient animal.

*Forgiven,* she'd said. Declared, decreed. And I believed her—that was the shaming thing, I believed her completely, and her forgiveness weighed more than any anger could have, because anger I could have argued with. Grace just sat there, being generous, refusing to argue, leaving me alone with the only judge who still had a case: the one in my sternum, where the tenth line would begin.

Build it true.

I pressed my hand flat against my chest, feeling for the shape of the answer, and far away—at the gates of Coral, deep in the drowned dark—I heard the first horns of the Ashen Court.
