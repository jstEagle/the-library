## 29. The Regent at the Gates

Here is a thing nobody tells you about hiding in a drowned city at the bottom of the sea: the water is not quiet. It presses. It hums against the air-wards like a crowd murmuring behind a closed door, and every hour you spend beneath it, you become more aware that the door is thin.

I was sitting on the steps of Coral's outer nave, trying to memorize the first three lines of the oath, when the humming changed key.

I want to be clear that I did not notice this because I am perceptive. I noticed it because my hands stopped working. One moment I was tracing ward-glyphs into the salt-crusted stone the way Odile had taught me, mouthing the old words, and the next, every bone in my body—the ones I was born with and the ones I'd carved and the ones I carried in my satchel, which had opinions of their own—went rigid, like dogs scenting a storm.

"Odile," I said. My voice came out very small in a very large room. "Odile, the water just flinched."

She was three tiers up, elbow-deep in the guts of a broken gate-ward, her scarred hands doing work they should not have been able to do. She didn't look up. "Water doesn't flinch, love. Water's not a thing that flinches."

"The water just flinched."

That got her attention. She wiped her hands on her apron—a habit, since the apron had been gray since Brackish Ford and no amount of wiping was going to save it—and came down the steps at the particular speed Odile used when she was frightened and furious about being frightened, which was her only speed, really.

"Show me."

I pressed my palm to the nave floor. Through it, muffled by a hundred feet of luminous sea, I felt the outer gates of Coral—the ones we'd come through, the ones Ser Ilex had woken to guard—shudder. Not break. *Test.* Something out in the dark water was knocking, politely, the way a creditor knocks.

"Oh," Odile said, in the voice of a woman reading her own obituary. "Oh, that's a siege-engine cadence. That's *ash-forged* siege-engine cadence. How in the nine hells did they find the gates?"

I had a suspicion. The suspicion had a name, and the name was *we have been tracked since the marshes,* and I had been carrying that suspicion around like a stone in my shoe, telling myself it was nothing, telling myself the Court's hunters had lost us at Lumenhold, telling myself a great many comfortable things because comfort was easier than the alternative.

"They didn't find the gates," I said. "They've known where the gates are the whole time. We led them here. Or they let us lead them. I don't think it matters which."

Sef came running down from the observatory spire—Coral had an observatory spire, because of course it did, because the people who built this place had built it to look up even when the sky was a rumor—and Sef came running the way Sef did everything, at a speed that suggested the universe owed them money. They skidded on the wet stone, caught themselves on a pillar carved with a face of some drowned saint, and said, breathless:

"It's them. It's the whole Court. I counted the— the pressure signatures, the ward-echoes, whatever Odile wants to call them, and it's not a raiding party. It's not even an army. It's *everything.* They emptied Lumenhold. They're here with the entire hoard."

"The entire hoard," Odile repeated flatly.

"All of it. Every caged star. They brought the light *with* them."

I looked at the two of them—my prickly, brilliant, impossible found family, lit from below by the pale coral-glow of a dead city—and I felt the thing I had been feeling since the marshes, which was that I loved them in a way that was going to get someone killed, and the someone was going to be me, and worse, I was beginning to suspect that was the *point.*

"They're not here to break the covenant," I said.

Odile squinted at me. "What makes you say that? Because from where I'm standing, they're here with enough firepower to crack this city like an egg, and the egg is the covenant."

"Because if they wanted it broken, they'd have broken it years ago. They've had centuries. They killed every maker of the old oath and let the sky go dark and they *still* didn't break it—because you can't break what's already broken. The covenant's been dead since my ancestor ran." I stood up. My legs were shaking. I stood up anyway. "They're not here to destroy the door. They're here to walk through it."

* * *

The dead, for once, agreed with me, which was its own kind of terrifying.

Since the Choir, since Sef had anchored me with counted breaths while every murdered covenant-maker sang their grief at once, I had learned to open the door in my head a crack instead of having it kicked off its hinges. I sat cross-legged in the nave while the pressure outside the gates grew heavier, and I let the dead speak.

They came in pieces, as they always did. A woman with a voice like river-stones: *They bring the hoard to power the anchor-throne.* A man who had been dead so long his words had worn smooth: *The Regent means to swear it herself. She has prepared. She has practiced. She has made herself into a vessel that can hold the weight.* And then, quieter, from the direction of the deep water, from the direction of the gates, a voice I had not heard before—old, and sad, and terribly patient:

*She does not understand what she asks. None of them ever do. But she will kneel at the altar, and the altar does not care who kneels.*

I came back to my body with Sef's hand on my wrist, tapping out their counting-rhythm against my pulse. One-two-three-four. It was how they'd saved me in the Choir, and it had become a habit between us, a small private metronome that said *you are here, you are Kestrel, you are in your own skull.*

"Well?" Odile demanded.

"She's going to swear the oath herself. Take the anchor role. Become the living covenant." I rubbed my face. "And the dead think the altar will let her."

"Let her?" Sef's voice cracked upward. "I thought the whole— I thought the anchor had to be an Oathkeeper. I thought it had to be *you.* That was the entire premise. That was the entire *book we are apparently living inside.*"

"Blood opens the door," I said. "It doesn't decide who walks through it. That's what the dead keep saying. My family's blood broke the seal on Coral when I first touched the altar-stone, and now the door is open, and an open door doesn't check names."

Odile had gone very still, in the way she went still when she was doing arithmetic she hated. "Then everything we've done," she said slowly, "every mile, every ward, every ambush, the marshes, the theft, all of it—we weren't racing to save the world. We were racing to deliver it."

"Cheerful," I said. "Thank you, Odile. Truly, your gift for morale is the reason I get up in the morning."

"You get up in the morning because I drag you up by the collar, and don't you forget it." But there was no bite in it. She was looking at her hands—the ward-scarred, half-ruined hands that had held the gates at the ambush, that had woven air-shells for our descent, that ached now, always, in the deep cold. "Kestrel. If she takes the anchor—"

"I know."

"—she doesn't just rule. She *feels.* Every realm's pain, forever. The dead said that's the price. That's the whole price, that's the entire terrible bargain, and she'd be—"

"She'd be feeling it and *enjoying* it," I said. "Or worse. She'd be feeling it and deciding it was fair." I thought of the Regent's voice as I'd imagined it through all these miles—reasonable, tired, certain. *Magic hoarded by the few starves the many. Breaking the covenant was mercy.* A woman who had looked at a dark sky and called it justice. Give that woman the pain of every living thing, and she would not be crushed by it. She would *administer* it.

"That," I said, "is the thing that keeps me up at night. Not that she'd fail to bear it. That she'd bear it *beautifully.*"

* * *

The gates held for two days.

I use the word *held* generously. What they actually did was die slowly and loudly, and Odile spent both days inside them like a surgeon inside a patient, her hands moving through the old ward-work, propping up structures that had been standing since before her order existed, buying hours with craft and spite and, I suspect, a certain amount of profanity that the drowned saints of Coral had not heard in a thousand years and were frankly scandalized by.

I helped where I could, which was less than I wanted and more than I expected. The dead taught me. That was the strange mercy of the last weeks—Ilex's lessons had not ended when his bond began to fray; they had *concentrated.* Every dead maker of the old covenant had been, in their living days, a ward-worker, a law-speaker, a builder of doors, and they remembered how doors were made. I would put my hand on the gate-stone and a woman dead four centuries would put her memory over mine like a glove, and together we would knit a seam that should have taken a guild a season.

"You're getting good at this," Odile said on the second night, not looking up from her work. It was the closest thing to a compliment she'd produced since the marshes, and I treasured it the way misers treasure coins.

"Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation as a fraud to maintain."

"Your reputation is safe with me." A pause. Her hands stilled on the stone. "Kestrel. When the gates go—and they're going to go, I can hold them for two days or three, but the Court brought the *hoard,* and caged starlight burns through wards like—"

"Like starlight through fog. I know."

"—when they go, I need you to promise me something." She still wasn't looking at me. In the coral-light her face looked older than it was, and it had always looked older than it was. "Whatever happens at that altar. Whatever she offers you, whatever the dead offer you, whatever the *shard*—" and here my stomach dropped, because I had told no one about the shard, about the whispers, about the painless eternity it kept promising me in a voice like my own— "*whatever happens,* you do not trade places with her out of spite, and you do not refuse the anchor out of fear. You *listen.* That's all. You're the best listener I've ever met, and I've met professional listeners, they're very boring at parties. Just listen, and then decide. Can you do that?"

I could not tell her that the shard had been offering me the opposite of the anchor for weeks—that where the covenant demanded I feel everything, the shard whispered I could feel *nothing,* and that some traitorous chamber of my heart had been listening. I could not tell her, and the not-telling sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"I can listen," I said, which was true, and not the whole truth, and I hated the difference.

"Good." She went back to her work. Then, gruffly, to the gate-stone, as if the stone had asked: "And if she does take it—if the altar lets her—we run. You hear me? We run, and we live, and we come back with something better. There's always something better. That's the whole of ward-craft, really. There's always a second door."

* * *

The gates fell on the third morning, and they fell *inward,* and the sea came in behind the Court like a tide of witnesses.

I had expected ash. I had expected the gray hunters, the pressure-engines, the slow grinding machinery of the Court's power. What I had not expected—what none of us had expected—was *light.*

They came through the breach in a procession. The ash-knights first, rank on rank, their gray armor drinking the water-glow. Then the engines, crawling on crab-legs, each one cradling a cage, and inside each cage a star. Not the cold distant stars of the vanished sky—*caged* stars, the ones the Court had spent centuries harvesting, and they burned white and furious and *miserable,* and I could hear them. Gods help me, I could hear them. They screamed in a register below sound, below thought, and every dead voice in my head went silent out of respect, the way a crowd goes quiet at a funeral.

"They've been screaming like this the whole time," Sef whispered beside me. We stood on the high nave steps, behind the last of Odile's improvised barriers, watching the procession fill the flooded avenue below. "The whole time we thought the stars were dead. They've been *screaming.*" Sef's hands were shaking. Sef, who counted things to stay calm, had run out of things to count. "Kestrel, they brought them *here.* Why would they—"

And then I understood, all at once, the way you understand a trap when the jaws are already around your ribs.

"To power the swearing," I said. "The old covenant was sworn under a full sky. The altar needs the stars to witness. She didn't bring an army." My mouth had gone dry. "She brought the *congregation.*"

The procession parted at the base of the nave steps, and she came through.

I had imagined the Ashen Regent a hundred times—in the marshes, in the firelight, in the shard's whispers—and every version had been wrong. I had imagined a monster. What climbed the steps toward us, unhurried, her gray cloak trailing in the luminous water, was a woman of perhaps sixty with a tired, kind face, the face of someone who had once been a schoolteacher or a midwife or a healer of animals, someone whose hands had spent a lifetime doing small necessary kindnesses. That was the horror of her. That was the whole horror. She looked like someone who would sit with you while you wept, and she had darkened the sky.

She stopped five steps below us. Close enough to talk. Far enough, I noticed, that Odile's last barrier still stood between us, and I had the distinct impression she had measured that distance on purpose.

"Kestrel Almeida," she said. Her voice was exactly as I'd imagined: reasonable, warm at the edges, worn smooth like a stair. "Last of the Oathkeepers. I've been looking forward to this conversation for a very long time, and I confess I've rehearsed it, and I confess further that all my rehearsals have fallen apart, because you keep doing things no one expects." She glanced past me at Odile, at Sef, and something flickered across her face that was almost—almost—envy. "You found people. That was never in any of my plans. People who run from the Court are supposed to run *alone.*"

"We're bad at instructions," I said.

"So I've gathered." She folded her hands, and I saw that they trembled—not with fear. With something that looked terribly like hope, and that was worse than any weapon she could have carried. "Then let me not waste the hours your smith has bought us. I did not come to kill you. I want you to understand that above all things. I could have taken this city while you slept. I did not, because what happens in the next day will shape both realms for a thousand years, and I refuse to begin it with a murder." Her eyes were pale, the gray of ash, the gray of a sky with no stars. "I came to ask you to step aside."

"Step aside," Odile repeated, from my left, in a voice like a drawn blade.

"Step aside," the Regent said gently, "and let me swear the oath."

The dead in my head made a sound I had never heard them make. Not grief. Not rage. *Pity.* Pity, vast and old and bottomless, the pity of people who had watched this exact scene play out before, four hundred years ago, with a different frightened face at the bottom of different steps.

"You'd feel it," I said. "All of it. Every realm's pain, every death, forever. The anchor doesn't rule, it *holds.* It holds everything and it can never put anything down. You'd know every starving village and every drowned sailor and every—"

"Yes." She said it simply. Without flinching. And the terrible thing, the thing I would carry out of this chapter of my life into all the others, was that I believed her. "I have felt it already, in small. I have walked the ash-roads. I have counted the hungry. I have sat with the dying in the dark they made, because I made it, and I would not look away. Your ancestors felt the world's pain and they *ran*—they built your valley and they hid in it and they called their cowardice an oath. I am offering the opposite of running." She spread her hands, and behind her the caged stars burned, and their screaming rose a half-tone, and I realized with a lurch that she could not hear it. She had never been able to hear it. "The realms need an anchor. You are a child who hears ghosts and wants, more than anything, I think, to be left in peace. I am a woman who has spent thirty years carrying weight and knows how to carry it. Give the burden to the one built for it. That is not ambition, Kestrel. That is *triage.*"

And there it was. The trap with no jaws. She was not wrong. That was the unbearable architecture of her—she was not wrong, not about the hoarding, not about my ancestors, not about the peace I wanted. She was wrong the way a perfectly straight road is wrong when it leads off a cliff: every step of it honest, and the destination ruin.

Because I had heard the stars screaming, and she hadn't. Because the altar did not care who knelt, and it would take her, and it would give her everything she felt, and she would organize it, and administer it, and within a century the pain of all living things would have a *budget,* and within two it would have a *policy,* and the covenant would be a crown after all—the very crown it had been built to prevent.

"The dead say no," I said.

"The dead," she said, with the first edge I'd heard in her, "are the people your family's cowardice got killed."

"The dead say," I answered, and I opened every door in my head at once, all of them, the whole choir of the murdered and the mourned and the four-hundred-years patient, and I let her hear it—not with her ears, she couldn't, but I let it stand in my spine and my voice and my face, the way Ilex had taught me, the way the dead had taught Ilex—"*the anchor must be chosen by the weight, not the wearer. And the weight has chosen. It has been choosing for four hundred years, and it chose a bone-carver who talks to ghosts, and I am so sorry, because I would give anything for it to have chosen you instead.*"

The Regent looked at me for a long moment. The water-glow moved on her kind, tired face.

"Then we are at the altar together," she said at last, quietly, "and we will see what it decides. I have caged stars enough to light the rite, and you have—" her gaze flicked, just once, to my satchel, to the shape inside it that I had told no one about, and my blood went to ice, because she *knew,* she had always known— "you have your inheritance. May the better vessel win."

She turned and descended the steps, and the procession turned with her, and the caged stars went with her into the heart of Coral, toward the drowned altar, their screaming fading down the flooded avenue like a hymn sung by the damned.

Odile let her barrier drop. Her hands were trembling. "Kestrel," she said. "What's in the satchel?"

And Sef—Sef, who counted things, who had been counting this whole time, whose eyes had gone wide and fixed on some middle distance where the numbers lived—Sef said, in a small strange voice:

"The rite. The swearing. It has to happen under a full sky. That's why she brought the stars. But Kestrel—the stars are *caged,* and a caged star can't witness, it can only *burn.* For the oath to take—for any oath to take—the stars have to be *free* when it's sworn." They looked up at me, and their face was the face of someone watching a door open where no door had been. "The swearing and the freeing. They have to happen at the same time. In the same moment. And she's about to walk them both into the same room."
