## 30. Sef's Star Charts

Sef hadn't slept in three days. I knew this because I could hear it—not in the dead-voices way, but in the way you hear a clock that's wound too tight, ticking a little too fast, a little too proud of itself. She'd spread her charts across every flat surface in the chamber we'd claimed beneath Coral's outer cloister: on the table, on the floor, on Odile's lap once, which had nearly ended the friendship then and there.

The chamber was one of Coral's drier rooms, if you could call anything down here dry. The drowned altar-city breathed around us like something sleeping—water moving in slow veins through the walls, coral glowing faintly bone-white where it grew through the ceilings. Everything smelled of salt and old prayers.

"Sef," I said, for what I judged to be the ninth or tenth time, "you should sleep."

"Sleep." She said it the way you'd say *plague* or *taxes*. "Kestrel, do you know what I've spent my whole life doing?"

"Counting stars."

"Counting stars," she agreed, jabbing a finger at me, or possibly at a chart; with Sef it was often hard to tell who the finger was for. "Nine years. Nine years of cataloguing positions, magnitudes, drift rates. My master said I was obsessive. My fellowship said I was wasting my prime on 'an inventory nobody will ever read.' And then the stars went out, and you know what happened to my life's work?"

"It became very important," Odile said from her corner, where she was relearning how to hold a stylus with fingers that didn't want to be fingers anymore. Her voice was flat, but I'd learned by then that flat was how Odile sounded when she was paying close attention.

"It became *wrong*," Sef said. "Every chart. Every coordinate. Because here's the thing nobody understood—" she dropped onto the floor amid her papers, cross-legged, surrounded by ink and salt-stain and fury, "—the stars weren't destroyed. We established that at Lumenhold. They're caged. But everyone assumed caging them meant *moving* them, yes? Taking them somewhere else. So all my coordinates would be useless anyway."

"You're saying they weren't moved," I said slowly.

"I'm saying," Sef said, with the particular relish of someone arriving at the sentence she'd walked across a drowned city to say, "that they were never moved anywhere at all."

* * *

I want to explain why this mattered, because at the time I confess it took me a moment.

When we'd stolen the shard of caged starlight from Lumenhold—the theft still sat in my memory strangely, half triumph, half shame at how much I'd enjoyed being someone who lies well—I'd expected it to behave like a piece of the sky. A fragment of elsewhere. Something foreign.

It didn't. It behaved like a piece of home. When Kestrel—that is, when *I*—held it, it pulled, gently, constantly, the way a tooth pulls at a tongue. Not toward Lumenhold. Toward *down*. Toward Coral.

"The stars are still where they always were," Sef said now, spreading a chart so old its vellum had gone translucent. It wasn't hers. It was one of Ilex's—drawn in a hand that hadn't held a pen in four hundred years, back when the sky was whole. "They never left their posts. The Ashen Court didn't steal them away. They built cages *around* them, right where they hung, and darkened the space between. Which means—"

"Which means freeing them isn't a journey," Odile said. Her stylus had stopped moving. "It's an unlocking."

"An unlocking that has to happen everywhere at once," Sef said. "Thousands of locks. Tens of thousands. You can't walk from cage to cage. The wards were woven as one fabric—if you break one thread, the rest pull tight. That's standard sympathetic warding. Even I know that, and I'm the one who failed ward-smithing."

"You didn't fail ward-smithing," Odile said. "You failed ward-smith's *etiquette*, which is different, and frankly a virtue."

"The point," Sef said, with the strained patience of someone refusing a compliment because the argument was more delicious, "is that the locks can't be broken one at a time. But they can be broken *together*. All of them. If—and only if—the binding force holding the cages shut is withdrawn. And the binding force holding the cages shut—"

She looked at me.

Don't look at me, I wanted to say. I've been looked at enough lately. The dead look at me constantly and they don't even have the decency to blink.

"—is the same binding force the old covenant used," Sef finished. "The oath-weave. The Court didn't build new power for their cages. They cannibalized the covenant. The star-cages are made out of the broken oath."

The room went quiet except for Coral breathing in the walls.

"That's why the shard pulls here," I said. My voice came out strange. "It's not pointing at home. It's pointing at the wound. At the place the oath was cut."

"And here's the part that kept me up three nights," Sef said, "which I regret nothing about, because listen: the oath can't be reforged while the stolen power still sits in the cages. And the cages can't be opened while the oath is still broken. It's a knot. Each problem holds the other closed."

"So it's impossible," Odile said. Not bitterly. Just testing.

"It's *simultaneous*," Sef corrected, and her whole face lit like—well, like something we hadn't seen in years. "That's different. Impossible means no order of operations works. Simultaneous just means the order of operations is *now and also now*. You swear the covenant, and in the moment of swearing—when the oath-weave comes back into existence, for one heartbeat, whole—you use it. One heartbeat of living weave, spread across the entire sky, unmaking every cage as it passes through them. The stars come free *as* the oath binds. The binding *is* the freeing. They're the same act seen from two ends."

I stared at her. Odile stared at her. Somewhere in the walls, the dead murmured, and for once they sounded less like grief than like a crowd leaning forward.

"How," I said finally, "does one perform two world-altering rituals in different places at the same moment?"

"That," Sef said, "is the problem I've been solving for three days. And I have solved it. Mostly. In principle. With diagrams."

"She has diagrams," Odile confirmed grimly. "There are forty-one of them."

* * *

Here is what the diagrams said, once Sef talked us through them twice, and then a third time when Odile demanded it, and a fourth when *I* demanded it, because I am a fast learner but this was a woman explaining how to do two impossible things at once and I wanted to be slow about it.

The swearing had to happen at the altar. That was fixed, non-negotiable, written into the terms the dead had given me in the choir-chambers: a living anchor, at the drowned altar of Coral, speaking the covenant whole. There was no alternative venue. Oaths have addresses the way people do.

The unbinding had to happen at the highest point of the sky's reflection—in practical terms, the tower of Lumenhold, where the Court stored the hoarded light and where the master-cage sat over the master-star, the pole star, the nail that held the rest of the fabric up. Break the nail-cage while the weave lived, and the unmaking would ripple outward along the old covenant lines, cage after cage after cage, until the whole sky came loose like a knot finally understood.

Two places. One heartbeat of margin. Half a world apart—or at least a hundred miles and an ocean's worth of drowning apart, which amounted to the same thing.

"You can't be in both places," Odile said. It wasn't a question. It was the wall she was building around the obvious, brick by brick, hoping somebody else would knock it down first.

"No," Sef said quietly. All the mania had gone out of her voice. This was the part she'd been afraid to say. "We can't."

Nobody looked at anybody. The lamp hissed. Coral glowed its patient grave-color.

"We split," I said.

"Kestrel—" Odile started.

"You swear the oath here," I said, "because the anchor has to be me, and the altar has to be here, and those are the only two things in this entire mess that cannot move. Sef goes to Lumenhold to open the cages, because the timing has to be counted to the heartbeat and Sef is the only person alive who can count the sky."

"And you?" Odile said. "Who keeps you alive at the altar while the Regent's whole army is trying to stop you?"

That was the question, wasn't it. I thought of Ilex in the doorway of the map-room earlier that day, standing the way he always stood, like a door that had decided to be a person instead. He'd heard everything, of course. Revenants hear everything; it's most of what they are.

"Ilex holds the gate," I said.

"He was already going to hold the gate," Odile said. "Now he holds it knowing that if he fails even one heartbeat early, your oath dies in your mouth and Sef opens empty cages into a dead sky. You've just put the entire weight of the plan on the shoulders of a dead man, and you did it in one sentence, without asking him."

"He'll say yes," I said.

"That's not the point!"

"It's entirely the point," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, which ruined whatever authority I'd been building. "Odile, he's going to say yes whether I ask beautifully or badly. He's been saying yes since before I was born. The question isn't whether he agrees. The question is whether we spend our last quiet hour pretending he might not."

Odile looked at me for a long moment. Her scarred hands lay open in her lap, the stylus abandoned between them. Then she looked at Sef, and Sef looked at her charts, and something passed between the two of them that I wasn't part of—an old alliance of people who had both lost things and refused to lose the same thing twice.

"Fine," Odile said. "Then we do it properly. If we split, we split like ward-smiths, not like desperate people. Redundancies. Fail-safes. Signals. I am not building a plan where the only thing connecting two halves of the world is *trust*."

"Trust is the strongest—" Sef began.

"Trust is a *feeling*," Odile snapped. "Feelings fray. Copper doesn't. I'll run a ward-line between here and Lumenhold along the old covenant roads—it'll be thin, thinner than anything I've ever worked, and my hands can barely hold a stylus, so I'll need three days and someone to thread for me where my fingers won't go. But it means that when Kestrel swears, the moment of swearing travels that line faster than any rider, any bird, any shout. Sef counts the pulse down the wire and opens the cages on the beat. Not before. Not after. On the *beat*."

Sef was already pulling fresh vellum toward her, eyes bright, pen moving before the sentence ended. Watching her, I understood something about friendship that I hadn't had occasion to learn in Hollowmere, where friendship had mostly been theoretical: that love, among certain people, sounds exactly like logistics. That "I will not let you fail" can be spoken entirely in measurements.

"What's the margin?" I asked. "How exact does the timing have to be?"

"One breath," Sef said. "Maybe two."

"Maybe two," Odile repeated, in the tone of a woman filing the phrase away for later nightmares.

"It'll be closer to one," Sef admitted. "The oath-weave doesn't surge, it *blooms*. There's a peak. The cages have to come down inside the bloom. Too early and the weave isn't strong enough to carry the unmaking. Too late and—" she shrugged, apologetic, terrible— "and the bloom closes, and we've sworn an oath into a dark sky forever, and the Court wins by arithmetic."

"Wonderful," Odile said. "One breath. Splitting the party, splitting the ritual, splitting the fate of two realms, and the whole architecture hangs on one breath."

"It hung on less," said a voice from the doorway.

Ilex filled it the way he filled every doorway—like a memory the room had agreed to make space for. His armor was dim tonight; revenants glow according to something none of us had ever fully mapped, and lately his light had been failing in ways he pretended not to notice and we pretended not to see.

"How long were you standing there?" Odile asked.

"Long enough," he said, which was how he answered most questions about himself. He came into the room and stood over Sef's charts, looking down at them with four centuries in his face. "This is how it was done the first time," he said quietly. "You've rediscovered it, astronomer. The original swearing—there were twelve of them, twelve makers, and the oath needed all twelve voices at once, but they couldn't all stand at the altar. So eleven spoke from the eleven high places of the eleven realms, and one anchored below. Eleven candles and one flame. It worked." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "Until one of the twelve decided one flame was a burden worth fleeing."

My family sin, served up again, whenever anyone mentioned logistics. Convenient of it.

"Then we do it as it was done," I said.

"You'll do it better," Ilex said. "The old twelve didn't trust each other. They coordinated by omen and sacrifice and prayer, and half of them hated the other half by the end. You have ward-line and a counting-woman and—" he looked at me, and his ancient eyes were unbearably kind— "and something the old ones never had. A reason that isn't duty."

"Duty got us this far," I said.

"Duty got the old twelve a valley with walls around it," he said. "Love will get the sky back. I've watched both operate. I have opinions."

* * *

We worked through what remained of the night. Sef drew the count—a sequence of numbers, actually, a rhythm she made me memorize alongside her, because if the ward-line failed she'd need the timing anchored in more than one skull. Odile drafted the ward-line route in exhausted, precise strokes, muttering about copper and grief. Ilex stood watch at the door, which was where he always stood, guarding the future against interruption.

And somewhere past the deepest hour, I caught them all in a single glance—Sef bent over her numbers, Odile's ruined hands steadying a ruler, Ilex burning low and faithful in the doorway—and I felt the plan become real, not because the diagrams were sound but because of who was holding them.

Sef's charts would work because Sef had wasted nine years on an inventory nobody read. Odile's line would hold because Odile had helped build the cages and would be damned before her work failed twice. Ilex would hold the gate because holding things was what remained of him, and he'd chosen, freely, to let it be enough.

Every piece of the plan was made out of what we'd lost. I don't think Sef saw it that way. I think she saw it as astronomy. But I heard the dead in the walls, and they heard it too, and for the first time since the choir-chambers they weren't singing grief.

They were singing time. Counting down.

Three days, the murmur said, under everything. Three days, and then the gate, and then the oath, and then the sky.

And underneath the dead's counting, fainter, from the direction of the sea-road far above: hooves. Many hooves. Coming down toward Coral the way ash comes down toward a candle.

The Court had found us. Of course the Court had found us.

Three days had just become fewer.
