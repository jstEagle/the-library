## 31. Ilex's Last March

Ser Ilex volunteered the way he did everything: by standing up before anyone asked and behaving as though the decision had been made centuries ago, which, knowing him, it probably had.

We were still in the antechamber beneath Coral's outer gates—the drowned ones, the beautiful ones, the ones currently being tested by Ashen siege-engines somewhere above our heads like a very persistent woodpecker with a grudge. Odile was re-taping her bandages for the fourth time that morning. Sef had charts spread across a slab of coral worn smooth by ten generations of kneeling pilgrims, and was muttering arithmetic at them in the tone of a man arguing with a stubborn god.

And Ilex was standing at the archway that led down to the gate-terrace, looking out at the green-lit water, wearing the expression of someone reading a letter he'd been expecting for three hundred years.

"I'll hold the gate," he said.

Nobody looked up. That was the trouble with Ilex—you could say enormous things around him and they'd land like weather reports. It took Sef a moment, and then his quill stopped moving.

"Hold it," Sef repeated. "The gate."

"The gate," Ilex agreed.

"Against the Ashen Court's entire vanguard. Which is, by Sef's count, four hundred soldiers and two of those things they've got that breathe ash instead of air."

"Five hundred now," Sef corrected faintly. "They were reinforced at Lumenhold. And the things are called hollowing-wights, technically, although—"

"Thank you, Sef," I said.

"—I only mention it because precision matters when you're planning to die," Sef finished, and then heard himself, and went pale under his freckles.

That was how the conversation actually began. Not with a speech. With an arithmetic error and a dead man correcting it.

* * *

Here is what you should understand about Ser Ilex, if you never have the misfortune of meeting him, which you won't, because of what happened next.

He is—or was—a revenant knight, raised three centuries ago and bound to guard the road to Coral. He does not eat. He does not sleep, exactly; he stands in the dark with his eyes open and calls it rest, which I find horrifying. His armor is rusted in patterns that look almost deliberate, like lace. When he takes off his gauntlets—and he almost never takes off his gauntlets—his hands are the color of old candle wax, and there are oath-marks burned into the backs of them, covenant-script that glows faintly when he speaks a truth.

He glowed a great deal that morning.

That's how I knew, before anyone else worked it out, that he wasn't proposing a strategy. He was reporting one. The bond that held him together—the revenant's tether, the thing that kept a dead soldier walking—had been fraying since the marshes. We'd all seen it. The way his hand passed through a door latch twice before it caught. The way his voice went distant mid-sentence, like a man listening to another room. Odile had tried to re-weave his bindings in Brackish Ford, before her hands, and she'd told us afterward, quietly, that it was like patching a net that wanted to dissolve.

A revenant's bond can be spent, Ilex told us once. Poured out all at once instead of leaking away slowly. And when it is spent on something the original oath would recognize as worthy—

He'd stopped there. He always stopped there. Dead men make terrible conversationalists about their own endings.

* * *

"You cannot hold that gate alone," Odile said. She'd set down her bandages. Her scarred hands were flat on the table—not gripping anything, because gripping hurt, but flat, deliberate, the way she held herself steady these days. "Ilex. Look at me. You cannot."

"I held this road alone for two hundred years," he said. "Before any of you were born, I stood at the crossroads above the salt marsh and turned back every traveler who meant Coral harm. I did it badly, some seasons. I did it alone, all of them." He smiled—Ilex's smile, which was mostly an arrangement of shadows. "Alone is the one thing I am qualified for."

"That was different. That was turning travelers back. This is holding five hundred armed fanatics and two wights while we—" Sef's voice caught on the arithmetic again. "While we do the other thing."

"Yes," Ilex said. "It is different." He came away from the archway and stood over Sef's charts, and I watched him read them upside down, which he shouldn't have been able to do, because revenants don't read minds or maps or much of anything—but he did it anyway, because Ilex contained multitudes of small impossible competences, like a knife drawer. "You need until the tide turns. Sef says the stars can be freed only while the oath is being sworn, and the oath must be sworn at the altar, and the altar lies past the inner gates, and the inner gates will not open for us while the outer ones stand. So: the outer gates must stand. For as long as it takes Kestrel to walk from here to the water-door. Two hours, perhaps three, if Kestrel dawdles, which Kestrel will, because Kestrel has never once entered a room without first apologizing to the doorway."

"That's not true," I said, and then thought about it. "Okay. It's a little true."

"Three hours," Ilex said, "against five hundred. Alone, my bond might buy forty minutes of honest fighting. Perhaps fifty. But spent—" and here he laid one waxy hand flat against the gate-arch, against the old ward-stone that Odile's predecessors had built into Coral's bones, "—spent all at once, through a bound threshold, anchored by a smith—" he nodded to Odile, "—and timed by a counter of nothing—" he nodded to Sef, "—it buys your three hours entire. It is not even a hard calculation. Sef could do it."

"Don't," Sef said, very quietly. "Don't do the thing where you make dying sound like bookkeeping."

"It is not bookkeeping," Ilex said. "Forgive me. It is gardening. You plant yourself where the wall is thinnest and you grow as long as you can, and then you compost, and things grow out of you. I have had three hundred years to think about where the wall is thin. Let me be useful at it."

Nobody said anything. Above us, muffled by fathoms of luminous sea, something boomed. Dust sifted down through the green water-light, slow as snow.

* * *

Here is the part I have not told anyone until now, writing this, and I suppose I'm telling it now because the dead keep better records than the living and they already know.

I almost let him.

Not easily. Not the way the Regent would have, sliding the arithmetic across the table and calling it mercy. But there was a moment—maybe ten heartbeats long, maybe twenty, I've had three years to count them and I still don't know—when I sat there with my mouth shut and did the same cold sum he'd done. One dead man, already dying, already fraying, already three centuries past his own funeral. Against everyone else. Against the whole litany of names the murdered covenant-makers had taught me, every soul in every realm whose pain I was going to carry for the rest of an anchor's life. One versus all. It was not even a hard calculation.

Sef could have done it too. That was the shame of it—that the math was *easy*, that grief was the only thing complicated about it, and grief doesn't get a vote, does it, not really, not if you're going to be the living anchor of two realms. Duty is the art of choosing which unbearable thing you can live beside.

But here is what tipped me, and I want it written down because it matters: it wasn't the math. It was that Ilex didn't talk about duty. Not once, the whole morning. Everyone assumed he would—he was built out of duty, forged in it, bound by it, the most dutiful object in creation. And instead he talked about gifts.

* * *

"Sit down," I said finally, to Ilex. "All of you sit down. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly, and properly means nobody gets to volunteer for death while standing, it's undignified."

Ilex sat. He sat the way statues sit, which is to say completely and slightly smugly.

"Now," I said, and my voice did something strange, went somewhere between bone-carver and daughter-of-a-liar, "tell me why. Not the tactics. Sef can check the tactics. Tell me why you want this, because I have watched you for months, Ser Ilex, and I know the difference between you wanting something and you obeying something, and I will not spend your life on an order. Even your own."

For a long moment the only sound was the sea, pressing its green shoulder against the world.

Then Ilex took off his gauntlets.

I had seen his bare hands exactly once before, in the marshes, when he'd pulled me out of a memory-flood and the water had stripped the leather. Candle-wax hands. Oath-script seared into every knuckle, dense as a lawyer's margins. But this time he turned them over, palms up, and I saw what I hadn't seen before: the script ended at his wrists. Below it, at the heel of each palm, there was smooth unmarked skin. A small space, both hands, no bigger than a thumbprint each.

"Do you know what this is?" he asked.

"A gap in the fine print," I said.

"The place where the oath did not reach." His voice was very level. "Every revenant has one. The binding covers the sword arm, the spine, the tongue—all the parts that serve. But there is always a little piece the oath could not claim. Mine is here." He touched the smooth skin. "For two hundred years I stood at the crossroads and I told myself that this small unbound part of me was nothing. A remainder. Rounding error. And then four exiles came walking down my road arguing about astronomy, and this small unbound part of me chose to follow them." He looked up. "Do you understand? Nobody ordered me to follow you. The oath was silent. I *wanted* to. It was the first thing I had wanted in three hundred years, and I did not know what to do with wanting, so I followed you badly and argued with Odile about ward theory and learned to lose at Sef's counting games on purpose, and it has been—" 

His voice did not break. Revenants' voices don't break. But it went thin as wire.

"—it has been the best year of my death," he finished. "So do not call this duty. Duty was the chain. This—" he spread his bare hands, the small unclaimed spaces facing us, "—this is the gift I choose to give, freely, from the only part of me that was ever free. You do not owe a gift. That is what makes it a gift."

Silence. Somewhere above, the boom-boom-boom of the Court's patience running out.

Odile was crying without any fuss, the way she cried, tears just falling off her chin onto her bandages like rain off a roof. Sef had covered his face with both hands and was breathing in counted fours, his anchor-rhythm, the thing he'd taught me in the choir of the drowned. And me—I found that my eyes were dry and my throat was full, which is apparently what happens when a bone-carver meets a grief too large to carve.

"Three hours," I said at last. "You give us three hours, and I swear to you—I swear on the oath I haven't sworn yet—we make them count. Every minute. And afterward—"

"Afterward," Ilex said gently, "you will bury me at Coral's edge, where the light comes up through the water, and the dead will sing me home, and it will be a good funeral, and you will cry, and then you will go live the enormous life you're about to have." He picked his gauntlets back up. "I have thought about it. There is very little else to think about, standing at crossroads for two centuries."

"One condition," Odile said. Her voice was rough. "The anchoring weave through the threshold—it needs a smith's hand at the near end. My hands can't hold a full binding anymore. But they can hold *you* for three hours, if you promise—" and here her voice finally cracked, all the way down, "—if you promise you won't send me away from the near end when it starts to hurt you. I've watched enough people carry things alone. I'm done with it. You carry your part and I feel it, Ilex. That's the whole point of everything we've been doing. That's the *oath*."

Ilex looked at her for a long moment. Then he bowed—not a courtier's bow, a soldier's, short and deep.

"Together, then," he said. "To the very end of it. Smith's honor?"

"Knight's honor," Odile answered, and the old words locked between them like a ward clicking shut.

* * *

We spent the last hour before the tide-turn in the small ways people do when speeches are spent.

Sef showed Ilex the star charts—the real ones, the ones with the caged stars circled in red ink—and walked him through which lights he'd be fighting under. "That one," Sef said, tapping a point, "the blue-white one over the northern water—that's the first star that comes back, when you win. It comes back in about four hours. I did the calculations twice."

"When *we* win," Ilex said.

"When we win," Sef amended, and did not look at him, and pressed his finger down harder on the chart, and left a small ink mark there that I suspect he never washed off that copy.

Odile wrapped Ilex's forearms in woven bands—her own work, the last thing her hands ever made cleanly, she told me later—with anchors stitched into the weave. They didn't speak much. Ward-smiths and knights, I've learned, conduct their deepest conversations entirely in equipment.

And I did what I do. I carved.

I had a piece of whalebone in my pack, saved from Hollowmere, from the old commission I never finished. I carved Ilex into it in miniature: the rusted lace of the armor, the level shoulders, the small unmarked spaces of the palms held open. It took forty minutes. He watched the whole time and pretended not to, and when I blew the dust off it and put it in his hands, the whalebone whispered—not the dead, this time, just bone remembering shape—and Ilex listened to whatever it said and then closed his fingers around it.

"For the record," he said, "that a bone-carver chose to carve me, when she could have carved anyone."

"You were the easiest sitter I ever had. You don't fidget. It's unsettling."

"Three hundred years of standing still," he said. "Finally a use for it."

And then the tide began to turn, and Sef called the hour in a voice that shook only a little, and there was nothing left to do but do it.

* * *

At the inner arch, where our roads split—ours down toward the water-door, Ilex and Odile back up toward the outer gates—he stopped me with one gauntleted hand, light as a moth landing.

"Kestrel Almeida," he said. "Last heir of the Oathkeepers. Hear a dead man's counsel, since you'll be colleagues soon enough." The oath-marks on his knuckles glowed. "When you stand at the altar and the weight comes—and it will come, all of it, every grief in every realm at once—there will be a moment when silence seems like kindness. Your own kindness, offered to yourself. Refuse it. Silence is only the absence of us, and we—" the glow brightened, "—we are rather the point."

"I know," I said.

"You say that," he said, "but you have spent your whole life hearing the dead as noise. Hear us as company. That is all the oath ever asked. That is all it was ever for." He straightened. "Now go. Dawdle as little as possible. I have a reputation for punctuality to maintain."

I hugged him. He was surprised—I felt the surprise go through him, armor and all—and then his arms came up around me, careful as a man carrying water, and he smelled of rust and old rain and, faintly, of the salt marshes where he'd stood so long.

"Gift," I said into his pauldron. "Not chain. I heard you."

"Good," said Ser Ilex. "Then go make it worth the giving."

* * *

The water-door took us down. Behind and above us, through fathoms of green sea, we heard the outer gates take their first true blow—and we heard, underneath the boom and grind of ash against stone, a sound none of us expected and none of us will ever forget:

Ser Ilex was singing.

Some old march-song from before the covenant, tuneless and vast, a dead man's voice poured out through a bound threshold like light through cracked glass. Odile's weave held. The song held. Sef counted the intervals and marked them on his chart, and every mark was a minute bought, a minute spent from the only treasure a revenant had, and he spent it gladly, from the small free places in his hands.

Two hours to the altar. Three hours of song overhead, growing thinner.

The stars waited above the sea, caged, and the first of them—the blue-white one, Sef's star—was due home in four hours.

We walked faster.
