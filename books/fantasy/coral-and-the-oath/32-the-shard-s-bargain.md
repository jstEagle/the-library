## 32. The Shard's Bargain

I told myself I was going up to the cliff path to check the watchline. That was the official reason, delivered to Odile with the brisk confidence of a person who has official reasons. She looked at me over the splinted ruin of her hands and said nothing, which is worse than an argument. Sef asked if I wanted company. I said I'd be back before the tide turned. Ser Ilex was already at the gates below, standing his endless post, and the camp was full of refugees and small fires and the particular noise of people trying to be brave at each other.

So: the cliff. Alone. With a stolen piece of a star in my coat pocket that had been talking to me for nine days.

I want it on record that I did not go up there intending to listen. I went up there intending to throw the thing into the sea, which is what any sensible person would have done in the first week, and did not do, because — well. Because it was warm. Because when I held it the whispers of every dead soul I'd ever heard went quiet, all of them, the murdered covenant-makers and the nameless drowned and poor Maren Almeida who'd been narrating my family's shames in my skull since the wards fell, and there was a silence in me so complete it felt like the first breath after nearly drowning.

You can't throw away your first silence in thirty years. That's not weakness; that's just arithmetic.

The cliff path above Coral is old basalt, black as cooled tar, furred with that pale moss that grows where starlight once fell. The sea below was still luminous — the drowned altar-city lit from underneath like something dreaming — and the false stars we'd freed were scattered across the dark water in their hundreds, drifting home to whatever cages Lumenhold no longer held them in. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. I stood there being angry at how beautiful it was, which is a specialty of mine.

*There,* said the shard, in a voice like a struck bell underwater. *Alone. Good.*

"I'm not talking to you."

*You're talking to me now.*

"That doesn't count." I took it out anyway. Of course I took it out. In my palm it sat like a chip off the moon, white-gold, throwing tiny moving shadows — it threw shadows that *moved*, which is against several laws of nature I used to rely on. Nine days in my pocket and it hadn't dimmed a fraction. Things that don't dim are either holy or hungry, and in my experience the distinction gets blurry fast.

*I've been patient,* it said. *I've shown you what I am. Now let me show you what you are.*

"I know what I am. I'm the anchor. Or I'm supposed to be. Everyone keeps very helpfully reminding me."

*Yes.* A pause with weight in it. The shard could do pauses the way Odile could do silences — weaponized. *And do you know what anchoring means? Not the word they gave you. The experience of it.*

I knew. Ilex had told me plainly, the night after the marshes, in that flat revenant voice that made everything sound like a weather report: *The anchor feels it all. Every death in every realm, every ache, every grief, forever, and holds anyway, and does not get to put it down, ever, not for one hour, not to sleep, not to scream. The last one lasted eleven years before she walked into Hollowmere and walled herself in.* Eleven years. And then centuries of quiet behind wards, and then the wards broke, and now here I was on a cliff being offered the alternative by a talking rock.

"Say it, then," I said. "Whatever the pitch is. Say it and let me refuse it properly. I hate being seduced by implication."

I felt something that was almost a laugh, if laughter could happen inside a stone. *Very well. Here is the bargain. Break nothing. Swear nothing. Carry the shard down to the altar tomorrow and set it in the anchor-stone instead of yourself. It will hold the covenant. It will hold it perfectly. And you will feel —*

"Nothing."

*Everything else.* The light in my palm swelled gently, kindly, like a hand cupping a moth. *Every dawn. Every friend. Odile's hands healing. Sef counting stars again. You will grieve Ilex properly, at a funeral, like a person, instead of carrying his death in your chest until the sky burns out. You will sleep. You will love. You will be Kestrel Almeida, whole, unburdened, and the realms will turn on rails of light you never have to feel grinding. No pain. Not yours. Never yours again.*

The sea breathed against the cliffs. Somewhere below, faintly, the dead of Coral were singing their endless careful song, the one that had nearly drowned me in the choir-chamber, the one Sef anchored me out of with counted numbers and both hands held.

"And everyone else's pain?" I asked. "Someone still has to hold it."

*The shard holds it. It was made to hold. That is all stars do — they burn so nothing has to feel cold.*

"That's not what stars do," I said, and heard Sef in my own voice. "Stars burn and everything under them feels warmer for it. That's different. What happens to the shard? After a century? Ten?"

Silence. The good kind of silence would have been answer enough. This one stretched too long.

*It dims,* the shard admitted finally. *In a thousand years, perhaps two, it will gutter. But a thousand years, Kestrel. Think of it. A thousand years of dawns you actually taste. Your ancestors couldn't last eleven.*

There it was. The hook under the bait, slipped in soft. My ancestors. Cowards. The first Almeida who fled the altar, who built a valley around her own refusal and called it peace, whose blood I'd been apologizing for since chapter — well, since a while now. The shard had learned my sorest place in nine days of riding in my pocket, and it pressed it now with the tenderness of a surgeon who is also a knife.

"You've been reading my letters," I said.

*I've been listening to your heart beat. There's little difference.*

I sat down on the basalt. My legs had opinions about standing. Below, the lights of the refugee fires on the shore made a broken constellation on the land, all the people the Court had chased out of Lumenhold's shadow, the people we'd somehow become responsible for, Odile directing ward-work with her ruined hands tucked in her sleeves like a woman pretending not to be cold. Two days ago I'd watched her talk a terrified ferryman through rigging a salt-ward using only her voice and her chin, gesturing with her nose, and she'd been *cheerful*, ferociously cheerful, and I had thought: I did that to her. My shortcut. My refusal. Chapter twenty-seven of this disaster.

And the shard had been in my pocket, whispering, *it doesn't have to hurt, none of it has to hurt.*

"Let me ask you something," I said. "When you offer people silence. Do you ever mention what it costs the ones who can't be bought?"

*Everyone can be bought,* said the shard. *That is what I am FOR. I am a piece of the sky that agreed to be caged because the cage came with warmth. I am the part of Lumenhold that liked it there.*

And that — that landed somewhere I hadn't braced. Because it wasn't lying, or it wasn't only lying. It was a fragment of starlight that had spent however many centuries in a Regent's vault being *useful*, being hoarded, being told that its burning was better spent warming one court than blessing one world, and it had believed it, the way anyone believes the story of the room they live in. The Ashen Court didn't just steal light. They taught it to be grateful.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, you poor thing."

*Do not,* said the shard, and its light flickered, actual temper in it, *pity me.*

"Too late. Occupational hazard. I hear dead things talk long enough, I start feeling sorry for the ones nobody mourns." I turned it over in my palm. It was warm as a living bird. "Here's my counter-offer. Come with me tomorrow and I won't put you in the stone. I'll free you. Properly. You go home to your sky and you burn for *everyone*, the way you were —"

*I don't want to go home.* It flared, and for a moment I saw through the warmth to the thing underneath, and it was afraid. Old fear, vault-deep. *Home is where they caged me. Home is a hunger with a view. I am offering you the only mercy that exists, bone-carver, and you sit there pitying ME —*

"Yes," I said. "Because I've met your mercy before. It lived in my valley for three hundred years. Wards on every ridge, magic locked out, the dead locked out, my family's ears locked shut, and everyone called it peace right up until the night the sky went out and I found out peace was just a smaller cage with my name on the door." I closed my fingers over it, not crushing, just holding, the way you hold a hand. "Silence isn't mercy. Silence is just pain with the witnesses removed."

*Then feel it all,* the shard hissed. *Feel Ilex die every morning. Feel Odile's hands. Feel every death in every realm screaming through you until you envy the DROWNED — I will enjoy watching. I will enjoy —*

It stopped. Because I was crying, which surprised us both, and the shard had apparently calibrated its cruelty for a person who wouldn't cry, and crying is very disorienting for things that live in vaults.

"I already feel all of it," I said. "That's the joke. I've felt it since the wards fell, every day, badly, with help. The difference between me and the anchor you're offering to replace is maybe a hundredfold, not infinity. I've done the arithmetic. Grief times grief times grief, and somewhere in the middle of it, a thousand years of other people's dawns too, because that's the deal, isn't it — the anchor doesn't only hold the pain. Nobody ever mentions that the anchor holds the *joy*. Every harvest festival. Every baby. Every terrible poem. All of it, forever, in one chest." I wiped my face with my sleeve, gracelessly. "My ancestor ran from half of that. Everyone since has treated her cowardice like wisdom. I'm going to be the Almeida who does the stupid brave thing instead, and honestly, the bar is so low it's underground."

The shard was quiet for a long time. The sea kept breathing. Far below, the singing continued, patient as geology.

*You will break,* it said at last, without heat. Almost gently. *Not today. Not for years. But nothing holds forever. Even stars gutter. I know.*

"Probably," I said. "But here's the difference between us. When I gutter, it'll be because I spent myself on purpose. You were spent by someone else. I'd rather end used-up than kept." I stood up. My knees filed complaints. "One more offer. Freedom. No cage, no stone. Just sky."

*...Why?* Genuine puzzlement, a bell rung off-key. *I threatened you. I promised you ruin. I have been whispering in your pocket for nine days like the worst kind of ghost.*

"I know a little about voices that won't stop," I said. "Most of them aren't evil. They're just lonely and loud." I raised it toward the open dark above the sea, where the freed stars were drifting in slow rivers toward the horizon. "Well? Last chance. Go home different this time."

For one moment I swear the light leaned toward the horizon like a plant toward a window. Then it dimmed, sullen, banked.

*No,* it said. *Not yet. Put me down, bone-carver. Set me on the stone. Let me hold it. Let me matter before I gutter — even borrowed, even wrongly. It is the only ending I have been given.*

And God help me, I understood it. I understood wanting to be the vessel so bad you'd take any wine. I'd been the valley's secret instrument my whole life, carved and useful and shut away. If someone had offered nineteen-year-old Kestrel a destiny, any destiny, even a cursed one —

"No," I said aloud, to the shard, to the cliff, to the whole listening night. "That's the whole point of me. Nobody gets carried. Everybody shares. You want to matter? Burn where everyone can see you. Otherwise you're just the Regent's vault with better manners."

I lifted it high.

I want to say I hesitated heroically. I didn't. What I felt was mostly terror, a lot of sorrow for the poor caged thing in my fist, and under it, steady as bedrock, one clean fact: *I choose the weight.* Everything after that happened quickly. Basalt makes a fine hammer. Starlight makes a fine sound when it breaks — a note I felt in my teeth and my sternum and, absurdly, in the old scar on my thumb, a chord like the sky clearing its throat. The pieces in my palm went from gold to white to a soft ordinary silver, and then they weren't a voice anymore, they were just light, loose, and then not even that — motes rising off my hand like sparks off a forge, streaming seaward, joining the slow bright rivers of the freed.

One mote paused. Hovered by my cheek. Warm as a bird's heartbeat.

*Bone-carver,* it said, very small, all the vastness gone out of it. *It is loud up here.*

"I know," I said. "Isn't it wonderful?"

It hung there one more heartbeat, and then it went, up and out over the luminous sea, a small stubborn brightness refusing the horizon's easy pull, finding its own line home.

I sat back down on the basalt and shook for a while. That's the part no one puts in songs, the shaking afterward, the ordinary animal aftershock of having refused a thing that loved you wrong. The tide turned without consulting me. Eventually I got up, brushed rock dust off my coat, and started down the cliff path toward the fires and the tents and the two people waiting up for me, because they always waited up, because found family means someone notices how long you've been gone.

Halfway down I stopped. The singing from below had changed.

I hadn't noticed it shift — it had come on gradual, the way dawn does — but now I heard it plain: the choir of the drowned, that endless patient grief-song, had picked up a second melody. Brighter. Faster. Threaded through the mourning like gold wire through grey wool. And under my feet, deep in the basalt, something old was waking — a hum in the stone itself, rhythmic, expectant, the hum of a lock that has just heard its key coming down the hall.

The dead of Coral knew. Whatever the shard had been, whatever it had promised, breaking it here, above their altar, on the eve of the swearing — I'd rung a bell they'd been waiting three hundred years to hear.

The hum climbed the cliff, through my boots, into my chest, and settled next to my heart like a guest taking its seat.

Tomorrow, then. The altar. The oath. Everything.

From far below, through the dark, Ser Ilex's voice rose along the stone, flat as ever, calm as ever, a weather report from the edge of the world:

"They're coming, Kestrel. Banners of ash on the road. The Regent is at the gates."
