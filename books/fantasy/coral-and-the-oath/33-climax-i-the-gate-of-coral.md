## 33. Climax I: The Gate of Coral

The dead had told me, in the end, that the gates of Coral were beautiful.

They had neglected to mention that the Ashen Court would arrive first, or that beauty, when you are drowning toward it through a war, looks mostly like a place you will never reach.

We came up out of the deep road with our lungs full of Odile's air-wards—thin bubbles of held breath stitched around each of us like second skins—and the sea opened before us like a cathedral someone had flooded on purpose. Which, I suppose, is exactly what it was. Coral rose from the drowned plain in spires of bone-white and blood-red, grown over centuries from the bodies of the covenant-makers themselves, and at its heart stood the altar, luminous even here, even now, a pale star sunk into the dark and refusing to go out.

Between us and it: the gates. And between us and the gates: the Court.

They had come down through the water like ash falling upward. Hundreds of them, gray-cloaked, their faces the color of old paper, moving with that terrible unhurried patience of people who believe time belongs to them. At their center walked the Regent's honor guard, and above them all her banner hung in the current—the closed hand of the Ashen Court, open now, fingers spread, reaching for everything.

"They're already at the gate," Sef said. His voice came thin through the ward-bubble, stripped down to arithmetic. "I count three hundred and forty. Maybe more behind the ridge of coral."

"Then we count faster," said Ser Ilex.

He stood at the front of our little line of four, armor green with centuries of silt, sword drawn, and he was smiling. Revenants shouldn't smile before battles. It's one of the many things about revenants I could have gone my whole life without learning.

"Ilex," I said.

"Kestrel." He turned that smile on me, patient as tide. "You're going to say something noble and I'm going to pretend not to know what it is. Let's save the time."

"The gate opens for me." I kept my voice level, which cost more than any ward I'd ever carved. "The dead will let *me* through. They don't have to let the rest of you. So the plan is—"

"The plan," he said, "is that a gate held by one knight holds longer than a gate held by none. You taught me that. Well. Someone did, four hundred years ago, and I've been repeating it ever since like an echo that learned manners."

"That was a different war."

"Every war is the same war wearing different clothes." He rolled his shoulders, and something in his spectral frame crackled like ice on a pond in spring—that fraying sound I'd been hearing for days now, the bond that held him together coming apart thread by thread. He heard it too. He'd been hearing it since the marshes. That was why he'd volunteered for this without hesitation, why he'd looked almost relieved when Sef laid out the plan and someone had to stay behind.

He'd been waiting for permission to spend himself. And I had handed it to him with both hands because it was tactically sound, and I would hate myself for that later, but later was a country I couldn't afford to visit yet.

* * *

We made our stand in the colonnade before the gates—a corridor of coral pillars wide as old trees, the sea floor shelving up toward the great doors, which were not doors so much as a wall of woven light, the last working piece of the old covenant, humming a note I felt in my teeth.

Odile worked fast. Her hands shook—not from fear; from what the shard-backlash had done to them in the collapse beneath Coral's outer ways—but they still knew their craft the way a heart knows beating. She pulled wards out of the water itself: lines of pressure, curtains of cold, little traps of displaced current that would fold a charging soldier in half. Her lips moved constantly, naming each ward under her breath like a mother counting children she expected to lose.

"Don't look at my hands," she told me, when she caught me looking.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Everyone does now. Look at the wards instead. They're prettier." A ghost of her old grin. "And they'll hold longer than I will, probably. There's a joke in there. I'm too tired to find it."

Sef climbed onto a fallen pillar with his charts strapped to his forearm and his counting-beads in his fist, and became what he always became under pressure: calm as a ledger. "The gate responds to you," he said to me. "Not your blood, not your name. To you. The dead choose who walks through. So walk like someone worth choosing."

"That's the worst advice I've ever received."

"It's also the only advice. Go be worth it."

Ilex set himself in the narrowest point of the colonnade, where two broken pillars made a throat barely three soldiers wide, and planted his feet in the silt like a fence post that had decided, after long consideration, to become a mountain.

"How long can you hold?" I asked him.

His smile went soft. "Long enough," he said, "has always been the whole of my oath."

* * *

The Court came like weather.

There's no other way to describe it. One moment the water beyond the colonnade was gray and empty; the next it was full of them, cloaks streaming, ash bleeding off them in slow ribbons, and the pressure of their approach bent the light. They didn't shout. Ashen soldiers never do. Their silence is the worst part—it makes the whole thing feel less like a battle and more like a funeral procession that happens to include you.

Ilex met the first rank alone.

I have seen a lot of terrible beautiful things since the night the stars went out. I watched a valley's wards shatter like sugar glass. I watched a city hoard light in jars while children coughed in the dark. But nothing has stayed with me the way those first thirty seconds did—Ser Ilex, four centuries dead and fraying at every seam, holding a throat of coral against an army, and *laughing*. Not mad laughter. The other kind. The kind you hear from people who have finally been given work worthy of their hands.

His sword moved like a sentence you can't argue with. First soldier: unmade. Second: folded around a ward Odile snapped into his path. Third and fourth came together and Ilex took them both, turning inside their reach with impossible grace for a man who weighed nothing and belonged nowhere.

"Hold the line!" Odile roared, though the line was one man. "Kestrel—go!"

"Not yet—"

"*Now.* Every second you stand there admiring him is a second he bleeds for nothing."

She was right, which I resented deeply and obeyed immediately. I ran. Down the colonnade's spine, through the gauntlet of fighting, low where the wards thinned, Sef's counted rhythm drumming in my ears—he'd given it to me that morning, a beat to anchor me against the choir of the dead that was already rising as I neared the gate. *One-two-three-four. Breathe. One-two-three-four.*

The dead of Coral sang everywhere now, and they were not gentle. They knew what was coming for their altar. They pressed against the inside of my skull like a crowd at a door.

*She comes,* they sang. *The last one comes. Hold the doors. Hold, hold—*

Behind me, the battle's sound changed. I didn't look back. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone kinder than memory: I did not look back, because looking back was a luxury purchased with seconds, and seconds were being paid for in Ilex.

But I heard it. I heard the moment the first wave broke against him and did not pass, and the moment the second wave came, and Odile snarl a word of warding that turned the water white, and Sef shouting numbers—numbers, always numbers, his grief converted to arithmetic because arithmetic doesn't drown you—and I ran harder.

* * *

The gates of Coral opened the way a flower opens if flowers had opinions about worthiness.

Light poured through the weave of them and read me as I approached—not my face, not my blood, something deeper, the shape of what I intended—and the singing changed key. The great doors unwove themselves thread by thread, and beyond them lay the altar-road: a causeway of pearl and bone running out across the luminous seafloor to the altar itself, where the new oath waited to be spoken.

Twenty paces of open ground between the gates and the causeway's start.

The Regent's honor guard filled all twenty of them.

They'd been holding back—the smart ones always do—waiting for the gate to open rather than dying against Ilex to force it. Now they turned as one, and their captain stepped forward, and I recognized the uniform: the same rider who had come to Hollowmere with kind offers and patient eyes. The Court's courtesy, at the end of everything, still extending its hand.

"Almeida," the captain said, and the water carried her voice perfectly, the way it carries everything. "The Regent offers terms. Walk away, live, forget. Or—"

I did the thing Sef taught me. Not the counting. The other thing—Ilex's lesson, from the nights of dead tongues: *speak with the dead deliberately, and they will lend you what they have.*

I reached into the choir and pulled.

Every murdered covenant-maker who had died within a hundred leagues of this gate came to the surface of the world at once. The water lit up with them—shapes like heat-shimmer, like breath in winter, hundreds of years of grievance given sudden permission—and the honor guard, who had spent their whole careers feeding on a darkened sky and a silenced past, found themselves standing in the full noon of the dead.

They broke. Of course they broke. Courage is easy against the living.

I walked through their scattering and did not stop, and the gates sealed behind me like a wound closing, and then there was only the causeway, the altar, the oath—and the sound, muffled and distant through the coral, of the line behind me beginning to fail.

* * *

Here is what I know about the fight at the gate, reconstructed afterward from Odile's telling and Sef's counting and the testimony of the dead, who see everything and editorialize relentlessly:

The second wave numbered sixty. Ilex killed eleven before his sword-arm began to come apart at the shoulder, glowing along the fractures like a lamp about to gutter. Odile spent herself building walls—nine wards in ninety heartbeats, each one costing her hands something she would not get back—and when the walls failed she fought with a broken warding-rod and her elbows and, at one point, according to Sef, her teeth.

Sef counted. That was his post, his sacrifice, his gift: he stood on his pillar in the open and called the rhythm aloud, louder and louder as the battle swallowed everything else, because he knew I needed it at the altar, and because a man who has lost his stars will find something else to keep by.

And Ilex—

Ilex held.

Not because he was strong enough. He hadn't been strong enough since the marshes, maybe since the day he died. He held because holding was the whole of his oath, and an oath kept past the point of possibility stops being a duty and becomes a kind of song. The dead told me later—they tell me everything now, whether I ask or not—that when the final rank came for him, he was still standing, still smiling, held upright mostly by the corpses of everyone who had tried to move him.

The captain of the honor guard struck the blow herself. Ilex saw it coming. The dead swear he smiled wider.

His last words, carried on the water to Odile's ears, were not for battle. They were: *"Tell the carver: long enough."*

* * *

I felt him go.

That's the thing no one warns you about, being the one who hears the dead. You don't get the mercy of distance. When a soul you love slips its moorings, you feel the rope pay out through your own chest. I was ten steps from the altar, close enough to see the oath waiting in it like fire under ice, when Ser Ilex's presence—which had walked beside me for half a book of miles, grumbling and teaching and quietly deciding to die—went out.

Not faded. *Went out*, like a star.

The grief hit me mid-stride and my knees nearly went, and the choir surged in to fill the space he left, and for one black moment I understood exactly what the original Almeida had fled. This. This weight. This is what an anchor feels, every hour, forever: every death arriving at once and none of them leaving.

I could kneel. Right there on the pearl road, ten steps short, and grieve properly, and let the Court take the altar, and let the sky stay dark, and at least be honest about how small I was.

The shard's old whisper stirred in the ruins of my resolve—*I could make it quiet*—and I thought, with a clarity that frightened me: *not today. Today I want to feel all of it.*

Grief and fury are the same material. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never carried either. They fused in me on that causeway the way coral fuses with bone, and what came out the far side of the fusion was not a feeling.

It was motion.

I ran the last ten steps and put my hands on the altar, and the dead of Coral rose around me in their thousands, singing, and behind me, through the gates, through the failing wards, through Odile's ragged voice screaming my name, the sound of the Ashen Court breaking through.

Let them come. Ilex had bought me the door. The least I could do was be worth the price.

I opened my mouth to speak the first word of the oath—

—and the water at the causeway's far end turned gray as ash, and the Regent walked out of it, crownless, unhurried, smiling the way a knife smiles, and she said:

"No. I believe *I'll* go first."
