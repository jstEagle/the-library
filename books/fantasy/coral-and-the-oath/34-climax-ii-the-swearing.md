## 34. Climax II: The Swearing

Here is what nobody tells you about swearing an ancient covenant at the bottom of the sea: there is a great deal of waiting.

The altar of Coral rose out of the drowned plaza like a rib bone the size of a cathedral, white and grooved and humming. The dead had cleared a path for me—not gently; I did not look too closely at what lay in the margins of that path—and now they arranged themselves around the altar in rings, hundreds deep, their light like moonlight that had forgotten the moon. Ser Ilex was at the gates, holding the line. Odile was somewhere behind me with a broken hand and a voice like gravel, talking me through the first warding. Sef was at the entrance to the choir gallery with the star charts, counting.

Counting. That was my anchor too, in the end. Sef had taught me the rhythm: four beats in, seven held, eight out. "Numbers don't lie," Sef had said, "which is more than I can say for anyone in this party."

So I counted. And I laid my palm on the altar, which was warm, which was the first warm thing I had touched since we came under the water, and I began.

The old tongue doesn't have a word for *hello*. It goes straight to binding. The first phrase of the oath is a greeting, an introduction, a door held open: *I am the one who remains.* My voice came out strange in the water, doubled—the sound of my throat and the sound of every Almeida who had ever stood here, layered like pages of a book pressed together so long they'd grown into one block.

*I am the one who remains,* I said. *Kestrel, of the valley called Hollowmere, child of Liora and Ash, heir of the turning away.*

That last part stung. Even the oath kept receipts.

The dead sang the response, and it went through me like cold water through cloth: *We hear the one who remains.* The altar brightened. Somewhere above us, far above, through fathoms of luminous sea, I felt—this is the only word for it—I felt the sky take a breath.

Three phrases spoken. Eleven remained. The dead had told me it would feel like drowning in reverse, like being pulled up from a depth so long I'd forgotten there was a surface. They had not told me it would feel like being *read*. Every word I spoke opened me further, laid another layer back, until the covenant could see all the way down to whatever small stubborn thing at the center of me was still, technically, Kestrel Almeida, bone-carver, poor businesswoman, person who disliked crowds and had never wanted to be important.

Four phrases. Five.

And then the singing stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, all at once, the way a heart stops—all the more terrible for the silence where the sound should be. The rings of dead around the altar turned their faces toward the western gate, and though they had no breath, I swear the whole of Coral drew in.

She had come herself.

I had met the Regent once before, across a burning field in the marshes, at a distance of two hundred paces and eleven dead hunters. She had not seemed like much, then. A grey woman on a grey horse, neat as a ledger. I had thought, *that's it? That's the woman who killed the sky?* I had been wrong the way you are only wrong about things that matter.

Now she walked through her own breached gate the way a landowner walks through a hedge—annoyed at the state of it, but certain it was hers. Ashen knights preceded her, and behind them came something worse: the caged light. A dozen prisoners of Lumenhold's hoards, star-shards in iron lanterns, and the light inside them strained toward me like dogs toward a whistled name.

*Kestrel,* said the dead, all of them at once, and their voices were very quiet. *She carries your ancestors' work.*

"I know," I said, because I did. Those lanterns held fragments of the binding-craft itself. Light that remembered being law.

Odile's voice came thin through the speaking-ward she'd pressed into my ear before the battle: *"Whatever she offers, the answer is no. Whatever she says, keep swearing. Words are the weapon now. Use yours."*

The Regent crossed the plaza. The dead made way for her, which frightened me more than anything else she could have done—they would not bar her path, because the covenant forbids the dead to strike the living, and she knew it, and she walked in the exact center of that protection like a woman walking a fence-line she had memorized.

She stopped at the edge of the altar's light. Up close, without the horse and the distance, I saw what two hundred paces had hidden: she was tired. Deeply, structurally tired, the way a building is tired when its foundations have shifted. Grey didn't suit her. Grey was simply what was left.

"Almeida," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like an item on an inventory. "You're doing it wrong."

* * *

I want to report, for the record, that my first response to the most dangerous woman alive was pure valley-bred politeness.

"Am I."

"You are." She nodded at the altar, at the grooves where the old words ran like channels for rain. "You're swearing the old covenant. The one that broke. The one that *had* to break." She began to walk the altar's circumference, slowly, reading the grooves, and I realized with a lurch that she could read them—that this woman knew the dead tongue better than I did. Of course she did. You cannot murder a covenant without learning its grammar.

"Four hundred years ago," she said, "an anchor named Ysolde sat where you're sitting and felt a village drown in the east. All of it. Every child. Because the anchor feels everything, Almeida, that is the entire function. And the covenant's answer was: good. Feel it. Hold it. Forever." She stopped walking. "Ysolde's hands shook for the rest of her life. Did they tell you that? Did your precious dead mention what they asked of her?"

"They did," I said. "In the marshes. I carried her memory of the drowned village for three days." I kept my hand flat on the altar. Seven beats in, hold, eight out. "It was the worst thing that has ever happened to me. And I noticed, Regent, that your solution was to darken every sky in the world rather than ease one anchor's burden."

Her expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a flicker, quick as a fish under ice. "The skies went dark because the covenant died," she said. "Not because of anything I—"

"Don't." I heard my own voice come out with harmonics in it, the dead speaking through me whether I invited them or not. "I've heard the confession of Maerwen Almeida, your founder's blade-hand. I know whose hand took the stars. Don't waste both our time pretending the ash fell from nowhere."

For a moment I thought she would kill me right there. The lanterns flared; her knights' hands moved to their hilts; the dead around us pressed inward like a tide meeting a wall. Then she laughed—one short, genuine, exhausted laugh, and somehow that was worse than the steel.

"You really are one of them," she said. "Righteous down to the marrow. Fine. Then let me say what your dead won't." She stepped closer to the altar, close enough that I could see the fine scarring at her temples where, years ago, someone had burned anchors into her skin. I hadn't known that. Nobody had mentioned that.

"The covenant was a machine for making one person suffer so everyone else could look away," she said. "One anchor, holding every grief in the world, alone, forever, while the realms got to call it peace. I was offered the anchor's chair when I was nineteen years old. Nineteen. Do you know why I refused?" Her voice dropped. "Because I did the arithmetic. All that pain, in one vessel. It doesn't stay contained, Almeida. It *leaks*. It leaks out as judgment. As certainty. As a person who has felt everything deciding they therefore have the right to rule everything. Your ancestor fled the chair. I burned it. Between cowardice and arson, I chose honestly."

"And what have you built instead?" I asked. "A court that feeds on the dark. Hoarded light for the few. Refugees on every road. I've *walked* your mercy, Regent. It looks like a market with no sky."

"It looks like a world where no single soul is nailed to a cross of other people's grief," she snapped, and for one bare moment she was not a regent at all, just a scarred, tired woman who had done monstrous arithmetic and lived inside the answer for forty years. "You want to know my philosophy? Here it is, free of charge: *shared* suffering is survivable. *Concentrated* suffering makes monsters. Break the vessel, spread the weight, and let the realms grieve their own dead."

And the horrible thing—the thing I have not told Odile and Sef to this day—is that standing there, palm on the altar, eleven phrases from becoming exactly the vessel she described, I understood her. Completely. The shard had whispered the same promise in a kinder voice: *you never have to feel this.*

"That's the offer, then," I said. "Break it forever. No anchor. No covenant. Just everyone, grieving alone in the dark."

"No anchor," she agreed. "No throne of pain. Yes."

"And who holds the realms together? Who answers when the borders fray and the dead go unheard and some child in some valley starts hearing voices nobody else can?"

She smiled, thin and grey. "Whoever's strong enough to take the job. Which brings us to the actual reason I'm here." She set one hand on the altar beside mine. The dead cried out—not in pain, in *warning*—and the lanterns of caged starlight blazed. "I'm not going to break the covenant, Almeida. I'm going to finish swearing it. Into me. I'll take the anchor's chair, hold the weight, and use it. Properly. With purpose. No more leaking. No more trembling hands. An anchor who *acts*."

* * *

She meant it as horror. I recognized it instantly as ambition, dressed in compassion's clothes, and I think she expected me to argue—to deliver some speech about the corruption of power.

Instead I did what bone-carvers do. I looked at the material in front of me and found the flaw.

"You can't," I said.

Her eyes narrowed. "Excuse me?"

"You can't finish the oath. It's already half-sworn." I lifted my chin at the glowing grooves, at the five phrases hanging in the water like struck bells. "The covenant doesn't accept a second speaker mid-oath. Ask your dead. Oh—" I let the wry note into my voice, because spite is also a resource, and I had learned it from Odile, "—you can't. They don't talk to you anymore, do they? That's rather the point of you."

Something moved behind her face—hurt, quickly murdered. "Then we do this the old way," she said, and drew her sword, and it was not grey. It was made of compressed ash and stolen dusk, and it drank the light around it, and the dead nearest the altar flinched back from it like hands from a stove.

"Regent," I said, "I'm a bone-carver."

"I know."

"I've held a sword exactly twice, and one of those times was to move it off a shelf."

"Almeida," she said, advancing, "I have killed forty people, unmade a sky, and founded an empire of ash. Do you genuinely believe this is a fair fight?"

"No," I admitted. And then I closed my hand around the hilt of Ser Ilex's sword—which he had pressed on me at the gates with the words *you'll need it less than I'll need you to have it*, revenant humor, God help me—and I stood up from the altar for the first time since the oath began, and I let the dead see my face. "But you've made one mistake, and it's the same mistake your whole court keeps making."

"Enlighten me."

"You think I'm alone in here." I smiled at her, and it was not entirely my smile. Three hundred covenant-makers shifted their weight behind my shoulders like a wind changing direction. "You keep fighting *a person*. I'm not a person anymore. I'm a *choir*."

* * *

She attacked first. Of course she did; patience was never the Court's virtue.

What followed was not, in any honest telling, a duel. She was faster, trained, lethal; within six passes she had opened my forearm and driven me back against the altar, and if it had been only steel between us I would have died in the first minute and this book would be considerably shorter.

But it was not only steel.

She fought with her body. I fought with everything I was. When her blade came for me, I spoke the sixth phrase of the oath into her strike, and the words had *weight*—the dead poured their memory into them, and her sword-arm slowed as the plaza around us filled with what she had spent forty years refusing to feel. Ysolde at the drowned village. Maerwen Almeida, weeping as she raised the blade against her own covenant-sisters. The first starving winter after the stars went out, seen through the eyes of a farmer who died blaming the sky.

"You're doing it wrong," the Regent had said. Now I said it back to her, gasping, bleeding, holding. "You want to feel everything so you can *rule* it. That's the leak. That's the monster-maker. The oath doesn't ask you to use the grief. It asks you to *carry* it. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and your court has never once understood it."

Seventh phrase. Her blade rang off mine and I felt the impact all the way up into my teeth. Eighth phrase. She cut me again, hip this time, and the water bloomed red and the dead sang louder to cover my cry, and I understood suddenly, completely, what Ilex had meant at the gates—duty as gift, not chain—because the pain was not separate from the oath. It was the *doorway* to it. Every wound taught me the shape of what I was volunteering for.

"You could stop," the Regent said. We were circling now, both of us bleeding, both of us breathing hard, and there was real entreaty in her voice, which frightened me more than the sword. "Walk away. Let me carry it. I'm stronger than you, Almeida. I've been carrying things since I was nineteen. You're a carver from a valley that hides. Go home. Carve little birds. Live."

And for one heartbeat—seven in, hold, eight out—I wanted it. The way you want sleep, the way you want your mother. A small life, warm and unwatched.

Then I heard them. Not the great dead, not the covenant-makers. The small ones. The ones I'd heard my whole life through whalebone and river-stone and the walls of Hollowmere: a grandmother asking after a garden; a boy asking if anyone had seen his dog; the woman from chapter two of my ruin, naming names and dates, patient, organized, *waiting to be believed*. None of them wanted ruling. None of them wanted thrones or courts or purpose. They wanted, with the whole simple force of death behind the wanting, to be *heard*.

An anchor who acts would use them. An anchor who abdicates abandons them. Only one thing left.

"Ninth phrase," I said, and my voice cracked down into harmonics, and the altar blazed so bright the ash-steel of her sword dimmed in shame. "*I am the one who remains, and I remain for every voice.*"

The Regent struck then—the true stroke, the killing one, all her strength and all her despair behind it—and it never landed, because three hundred dead put their hands over mine on the hilt, and Ilex's borrowed blade came up in a guard no living person knows, taught by no living teacher, and turned her blow into the altar itself.

The covenant drank the stroke. All of it. Four hundred years of her fury, gone into the stone in a single flash, and the Regent stared at her empty hands as though they had betrayed her.

"Tenth phrase," I whispered.

She looked at me across the blazing altar—scarred, exhausted, unmade—and said, quietly, almost kindly: "You don't know what you're taking on. You *can't* know."

"You're right," I said. "Eleventh."

Above us, through the drowned roof of Coral, through fathoms of luminous sea, the last caged lights in her knights' lanterns went dark—not extinguished but *answering*, streaming upward through the water toward a sky getting ready to remember itself.

"One phrase left," the Regent said. She raised her sword again. Behind her, her knights surged forward through the breaking ranks of the dead, and from the western gates came a sound I knew and dreaded—the sound of Ser Ilex's stand beginning to fail.

"One phrase left," I agreed. "Come and try to stop it."

I put both hands on the altar, and opened myself all the way down, and spoke the twelfth and final phrase of the covenant of Coral:

"*I am the one who remains—and I remain willingly.*"

The world went white. And then it went *heavy*, heavier than sea, heavier than sky, every grief in every realm pouring into the vessel I had just volunteered to be—

—and the Regent, unchosen, screamed, and came at me with everything she had left.
