## 35. Climax III: The Anchor

The altar knew my name before I spoke it.

That should have frightened me more than it did, but by then I had been named by so many things—dead women in whalebone, a revenant knight, a shard of starlight with the voice of a flattering merchant—that one more voice knowing *Kestrel Almeida* felt less like doom and more like being expected at dinner.

Coral's heart was a drowned cathedral without walls. The sea pressed in overhead like a ceiling of green glass, held off by Odile's wards, which hummed around us in a lattice of light that was fraying at every edge because Odile had woven it with broken hands and sheer bloody stubbornness. Below me, the altar rose in terraces of white coral, each ring carved with names in the old tongue—the covenant-makers, all nine of them, their names worn smooth by centuries of patient water.

Eight of them were dead in the way dead things are usually dead: finished, quiet, at rest in whatever comes after rest.

The ninth had fled to Hollowmere and built a valley with no sky and called it safety, and her blood sat in my veins right now, hammering.

*You can still stop,* said the shard's ghost in my memory. I had shattered it on the stones outside the choir, but things made of promises don't really die when you break them; they just stop being able to make new ones. Its last offer still hung in me like smoke: silence. A painless eternity of nothing. Every dead voice gone. My own peace, sealed and signed.

*I know,* I told the smoke. *That's why I'm not doing it.*

Sef stood at the first terrace with the star charts spread on wet coral, weighting the corners with bits of shell because the sea had opinions about paper. They were counting. Of course they were counting. Sef Ardent counted the way other people pray—when the world got too large, they reduced it to numbers until it fit inside something bearable. Right now they were counting down to the moment the caged stars above Lumenhold would be loosed, because Ilex was holding the gate below us so that this moment could arrive at all.

"Two minutes," Sef said. Their voice cracked on the second syllable and they pretended it hadn't. "Give or take. The synchronization isn't—" They stopped, swallowed, started over. "It'll be close. It has to be close."

"Close is fine," I said. "I've been close to death all week. We're practically engaged."

Odile laughed—a short, wrecked sound—and put her ruined hand on my shoulder. Her fingers wouldn't close anymore. She rested them there like a bird that couldn't grip the branch. "Kestrel. Before you do this. Look at me."

I looked at her. Her face was ash-smeared and salt-stiff and she had the eyes of a woman who had spent thirty years drinking to forget what she'd helped build and three months sobering up to help unbuild it.

"The old terms," she said. "You heard them. A living anchor holds the covenant. Feels everything. Every realm. Every ache. *Forever.* There's no shame in refusing. Eight people refused before your line took it up, and history has been very polite about calling them prudent."

"That's the thing," I said. "I keep hearing the word *forever* like it's a punishment. But I've been listening to the dead my whole life, Odile. Do you know what forever actually sounds like? It sounds like a woman in whalebone naming the dates her children died so someone—*anyone*—would write them down. It sounds like Ser Ilex remembering the oath not because he was bound to but because he loved the people who made it." My throat did something inconvenient. I talked over it. "Forever isn't the price. Forever is the point. Everything else is just logistics."

"That's either very brave or very stupid," Odile said.

"I've been told it's usually both, in my family."

Sef cleared their throat. "Thirty seconds." A pause. "Also, for the record, it's brave. I did the math."

*The math,* said the dead, all of them, everywhere, a tide of voices rising through the coral beneath my feet like warmth through floorboards. *He did the math.* And then they began to sing—not the drowning grief-choir that had nearly taken me at the gates, but something older and steadier, the song they must have sung the first time, when nine people stood somewhere like this and agreed to carry each other's pain because no one should carry it alone.

I knelt at the center of the altar. The coral was warm. That surprised me every time. Everything here was grown from the dead and everything here was *warm*, and I had spent twenty years thinking of death as cold because Hollowmere taught its children that the dark beyond the ridge was empty. It was never empty. It was full. It was so terribly, magnificently full.

"Terms," I said aloud, because the oath required consent, term by term, spoken whole. That was the flaw in the original binding—or rather, the cowardice. My ancestor had accepted the terms and then fled them, and the wards of Hollowmere were her flinch made permanent, a valley-sized denial. You cannot half-hold an oath. You cannot hold it while also holding yourself apart. I had learned that much from watching Odile try to hold her guilt and her bottle in separate hands.

The sea leaned closer. The dead went quiet, listening.

"First term," I said. "I will feel what the realms feel. Every grief. Every death. Not filtered. Not muffled. Whole."

Something in the water turned over, vast and slow, and I understood dimly that the things that had been worshippers were gathering to witness, and that they considered this a wedding.

"Second term. I will be witness, not ruler. What the dead know flows through me to whoever asks honestly, and to no one else, and I don't get to edit it for the comfort of courts or regents or anyone who'd rather the record said something kinder."

"Third term—" and here my voice failed, because the third term was the one my ancestor had actually run from, and now that I stood where she had stood I finally saw it clearly. The first two terms were burdens. Terrible ones, but burdens can be borne; I'd been bearing whispers since childhood. The third term was different.

"Third term," I said, and made myself say it. "I stay human. No ascension. No becoming something that watches from outside the pain. I hurt with the realms *as one of them*, and I die as one of them, and the oath passes on when I do—to whoever chooses it next, freely, the way I'm choosing it now. The covenant is only as strong as the willingness underneath it. Compulsion rots it. That's what happened before. That's what the Ashen Court grew in the rot."

*And if no one chooses it after you?* asked a voice—not the dead, not the shard's ghost. Something older, the altar itself perhaps, or the accumulated weight of every name carved in the terraces. *Do you understand the risk?*

"Yes," I said. "You're betting on people. So am I. I've met some. They're infuriating and they keep showing up anyway. Sef crossed an ash-dark continent because the stars mattered more than their safety. Odile is standing here on hands that don't work because she owed a debt to a child she'd never met. A dead knight held a gate against an army tonight because duty was his way of saying love." I looked up, into the green glass ceiling of the sea, toward the place far above where any moment now the sky might remember itself. "Bet on people. It's the only wager that's ever paid out."

"One minute," Sef whispered. "Oh. Oh, it's happening—I can see it through the water, Kestrel, the caging wards over Lumenhold just—"

The sea lit.

Not the sea itself—something above it, something so far away it should have meant nothing and instead meant everything: light blooming through fathoms of green water like dawn arriving from the wrong direction. The freed stars, scattering home across the heavens, and their light falling on the drowned altar for the first time in living memory.

Sef made a sound I will carry to my own grave, whenever that comes. It was the sound of a person getting back the only thing they'd ever truly wanted, and it was worth everything it cost to buy it.

"Now," Odile said. "Swear it, girl. Swear it whole."

And the Regent arrived.

Of course she did. I want it recorded that I was not surprised. The Ashen Court had spent three hundred years feeding on a darkened sky, and a woman who builds her power on darkness does not sit in Lumenhold while the lights come back on. She came down through Coral's breached gates the way ash falls—everywhere, inevitable, unhurried—and she walked across the terraces without disturbing a single name, because her whole art was moving through the world without touching it, without being touched by it.

She was not what I expected. She was small. Grey-haired, plain-robed, with the tired face of someone who had once believed something kind and had it break in her hands. Behind her, faintly, I heard fighting—steel and shouting, Ilex's voice roaring a war-cry that had last been heard before Hollowmere had walls—and I made myself not listen, because if I listened I would stop, and I could not stop.

"You're doing it wrong," the Regent said.

Her voice reached me clearly under the water-held dome, which told me she had brought her own magic down here, which told me how strong she was, which I already knew.

"The covenant," she said, pacing the outer terrace like a lecturer, like a grandmother correcting a child's letters. "You're accepting it as a burden. That's the old error. That's why it failed. A burden gets resented, and resentment curdles into your ancestor's valley and my ash-dark sky. Take it differently, child. Take it as ownership. Why should the realms' pain be *distributed*? Pain scattered is pain multiplied—a thousand little wars, a thousand little starvings. Pain gathered is pain governed. Hold it all, and no one else ever has to feel helpless again. Isn't that what you want? You've heard them screaming since you were a child. End the screaming. Be the one who decides."

And the awful thing—the thing I will not pretend away—is that for one full breath, it sang to me. Because I *had* heard them screaming since I was a child. Because I knew exactly what it was to lie awake wishing the pain belonged to someone with the power to organize it. She wasn't offering me a throne. She was offering me relief dressed as responsibility, which is the most seductive shape power ever wears.

"No," I said.

She sighed, genuinely sad. "Your ancestor said it faster."

"My ancestor ran. I'm just declining politely." I put both palms flat on the warm coral. "Here's the flaw in your arithmetic, Regent. Pain gathered isn't governed. It's hoarded. And hoarded pain goes septic—it becomes you. Ash skies and extinguished stars and hunters on the road, all of it grown from one woman deciding everyone else's suffering was hers to manage." I shook my head. "The oath doesn't need a ruler. It needs an ear. I'm not taking their pain *from* them. I'm taking it *with* them. That's the whole difference. That's everything."

"You'll drown," she said simply. "Alone, feeling all of it."

"I'm not alone," I said. "That's the entire point of an oath. It's a rope between people. Look around you. You came down here and you touched nothing and no one touched you, and you think that's strength." I closed my eyes. "Watch what touching does."

And I swore.

I won't tell you the words. Partly because they were in the dead tongue and translation flattens them, and partly because some things belong to the moment and not the report. But I will tell you what it *felt* like, since feeling was rather the job description.

It felt like opening a door I had braced against my whole life and finding not a flood but a congregation.

Every death in every realm came through me—not as attack, though there was pain enough to unmake mountains, and there were moments in those first seconds where I understood why my ancestor had run, moments where the weight bent me double over the altar and Odile's voice somewhere saying *breathe, breathe, count with Sef, count*—but as *company*. Every grief carried its own name. Every ache remembered being whole. The dead did not pour through me like water through a channel. They stood with me, shoulder to shoulder in the dark behind my ribs, an army of everyone who had ever lost anything, and they were not asking me to suffer for them.

They were thanking me for not looking away.

The stars came home above the sea. I felt that too—felt it as a rising note through the whole structure of the oath, felt the realms exhale one vast held breath, felt Lumenhold's cages open and the stolen brightness scatter like seeds on wind. Somewhere above the green glass ceiling, the night sky caught fire with every star there had ever been, and their light fell through fathoms of ocean and landed on the white coral terraces and turned the names luminous, every single one, all nine covenant-makers shining like they had only been waiting in the dark for someone to switch the lamps back on.

The Regent screamed.

I opened my eyes. She stood on the terrace and the light was touching her, actually touching her, and where it touched, she was coming apart—not violently, not dramatically. She was simply *unmaking*, the way ash unmakes when the wind takes an interest. Because she had anchored herself to the darkened sky, and the sky was no longer dark, and there was nothing left under her feet.

"You don't understand," she said, and her voice was going thin and grey at the edges. "If the pain isn't governed—if everyone just *feels* it—then what I did—my whole life—all of it—"

"Mattered?" I said gently. "Yes. It mattered. All of it. That's what I'm for now, Regent. Someone has to witness even this."

She stared at me. And I did the hardest thing I did that night, harder than the oath, harder than breaking the shard: I opened the covenant to her. Let her feel witnessed. Let the last of her be *known*—the kind believer whose kindness broke and curdled, the woman beneath the ash.

For one instant her face was young and horrified and grieving, and she wept two tears that fell upward, or maybe the light fell downward past them, and then she was gone. Wind took her. The sea received what remained, which was almost nothing, which was somehow exactly enough.

Silence, after. The good kind—the kind Hollowmere had counterfeited for three hundred years and never once delivered.

"It's done," Sef said, in a small voice, from the terrace. They were staring up through the water at the blazing sky, chart-forgotten, numbers forgotten, everything forgotten except the light. "Kestrel. It's done. The stars are—they're *home*—"

Odile sank down beside me on the altar. Her broken hands found my shoulders, and she held on as best she could, which was not much, which was everything.

"How do you feel?" she asked. "Honestly. Don't be wry."

I took inventory. Every realm's ache, humming in me like a struck bell that would never fully stop ringing. Every death, named and known. Grief beyond architecture. And threaded through all of it, unbearable and inseparable: the exact weight of Odile's hands, and Sef's crying, and Ilex—

Ilex.

The fighting beyond the gates had stopped.

"Kestrel," Odile said, reading my face the way she read failing wards. "Kestrel, wait—"

But I was already standing, already feeling through the new-made covenant for one particular thread among millions, following it down through the drowned halls toward the gate, toward the old war-cry that had gone silent, toward the friend who had taught me that duty was love wearing armor.

The stars blazed overhead. The sea glowed like a held-up lantern. And somewhere beneath me, in the dark of Coral's outer halls, Ser Ilex was waiting to show me what the covenant's first act of witnessing would be.

* * *

He was sitting up against the gatepost with his sword across his knees like a man resting after a long day's honest work, and he was smiling, and he was almost entirely gone—his edges dissolving into motes of pale light that drifted upward through the water toward a sky that could finally see him.

"There she is," he said. "The Anchor of the Realms. You look terrible."

"You look translucent," I said, and knelt beside him, and the whole covenant rushed in around him—every life he'd lived, every century on that road, every traveler he'd guarded who never knew his name. "Ilex. The oath. I could—maybe there's something in the terms, some way to—"

"Kestrel." He said it the way he'd said it in training, when I was about to do something clever and fatal. "I was bound to that road for four hundred years. Tonight I chose it. Do you understand the difference? Four hundred years of obligation ended the moment you gave me something worth volunteering for." His smile widened. "Duty is a gift, girl, when you get to pick the recipient. You let me pick. Best gift anyone ever gave me."

His hand found mine—solid for now, growing lighter.

"The dead are singing," he said, tilting his head. "Can you hear it? Properly singing. Not mourning. *Homecoming.*" He looked up at the light rising off his own shoulders with frank curiosity, the scholar in him surviving to the last. "Well. About time. I've been curious about the far side of that door for four centuries."

"Don't," I said. It came out very small. "Ilex. Please."

"Anchor," he said gently. "You're going to hear every death now. Every single one. So hear mine *right*: I held the line. They came through the breach and they did not pass. The oath was sworn. The stars are out." His grip tightened, one final instant of solid, warm, impossible weight. "That is not a tragedy, Kestrel Almeida. That is a *good death*, and I have waited four hundred years for one, and you will witness it properly, and you will sing me home."

So I did what I was for.

I opened myself to all of it—every realm's ache, and this one most of all—and I sang with the dead of Coral as the last of Ser Ilex came apart into light, and the light rose clean through the green water toward ten thousand stars, and every star, I am quite certain, leaned down a little to meet him.

The gate stood open behind him. The sea was calm. Above, impossibly, wonderfully, the sky was full.

Odile's hand settled on my shoulder, and Sef's on the other side, and together the three of us watched the light go home.
