## 36. Afterlight

The first thing I felt was the sea.

Not the water — the sea itself, the whole vast animal of it, every current and cold vein of it running through me like I had become its ledger. That was the oath settling in. That was what the dead had tried to warn me about in their patient, terrible way: an anchor does not hold the covenant. An anchor *is* the covenant. Every realm's ache, filed under my name, cross-referenced forever.

I want to tell you it felt noble. It felt like drowning in a library where every book was on fire.

The second thing I felt was Odile's hand on the back of my neck, grip like a dockworker's, and Sef's shoulder under my arm, and I understood — belatedly, the way I understand most things — that this was also part of the design. Not the oath's design. The older one. The one about not doing unbearable things alone.

"Up," Odile said. "You can have your cosmic revelation after you've breathed actual air. Priorities."

"The stars," I managed. My voice came out sanded down to the grain. "Did they—"

"Look up, you absolute legend."

* * *

We surfaced into a dawn that did not know its own rules yet.

The sky over the drowned altar-city was doing something no sky had done in living memory, which is to say: everything at once. The stars we had freed from Lumenhold's cages were scattering overhead in long bright rivers, unspooling toward their old stations like birds released mid-migration who remember, all at once, exactly where home is. Some of them went fast, streaks of white fire. Some drifted, hesitant, the way you or I might pause in a doorway of a house we'd been locked out of for three hundred years.

And above it all, the sun coming up. A real sun, on a horizon with an edge to it, throwing gold across a sea that glowed anyway — Coral's light beneath, the stars' light above, and us bobbing in the seam between them like a stitch holding two fabrics together.

Sef made a sound I had never heard from them before. Later I would learn it was laughing and crying happening simultaneously, which they assured me was "astronomically common among people whose life's work comes true," and that I shouldn't read anything into it.

"Every one," Sef whispered. They had their chart-book out even now, treading water, ink bleeding everywhere, utterly indifferent. "Kestrel. Every star on my grandmother's charts. The ones everyone said were invented. The Shepherds. The Nine Sisters. The Wandering Ox." Their voice cracked clean through. "It was all real. We weren't wrong. We were just *early*."

"You were three hundred years early," Odile said. "Give yourself partial credit."

I laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt, actually, and I want to be precise about that, because it matters: I could feel a fisherman's grief in a harbor two realms east, and a child's fever in a mountain village, and the slow tectonic loneliness of the marshes, and Coral's dead singing — softer now, no longer demanding, just singing, the way you sing over a cradle. All of it. All at once. Forever.

And threaded through it, thin as wire: Odile's hand. Sef's shoulder. Two small mercies the ocean could not out-shout.

"Can you swim?" Odile asked.

"I can float," I said. "Floating is basically swimming with humility."

"That's the exhaustion talking."

"It's the honesty talking. The exhaustion talks less. Mostly it just screams."

Odile hauled me toward the shore-terrace where our boat waited — one of the small boats, ours, the one we'd come down in, miraculously not crushed during the collapse of the Regent's ambitions. Sef swam backward the whole way so as not to miss the sky, which meant they swallowed half the sea and did not care.

* * *

Getting out of the water took a geological age. I had spent the last of whatever kept me upright on speaking the covenant whole, and my body had submitted its resignation. Odile and Sef dragged me up the white coral steps like a sack of very important turnips, and then I was on my back on a terrace that had been underwater since before my family built their cowardly little valley, looking straight up as the last of the stars found their places.

They didn't go out when the sun rose. That surprised me. I'd expected them to fade politely, the way they do in stories. Instead they stayed — pale, ghost-thin, but *there*, silver stitching across the blue, because the covenant was sworn now and the sky belonged to everyone again, day and night alike. You could see them if you knew to look.

I would spend the rest of my life knowing to look.

"Thirsty?" Sef pressed a waterskin to my mouth.

"Starving," I corrected.

"Thirsty first, starving second, philosophical crisis third. Doctor's orders."

"You're an astronomer."

"I'm a person who has read a book. It counts in wartime."

I drank. Somewhere below us, the sea heaved once — a long rolling swell that was not a wave, that was the drowned city exhaling — and the glow of Coral dimmed by degrees, like a hearth banking itself for morning. The worshippers-that-had-been-worshippers, the pale shapes that had guarded the gates, were gone. I reached for them through the oath and found only peace down there, wide and finished, a congregation finally dismissed after the longest service ever held.

"They're home too," I said.

Sef looked up from their ruined charts. "How do you know?"

"Because I'm the anchor, Sef. I get the whole correspondence now." I flexed my fingers against the coral. "Someone just thanked us. In Old Covenant Tongue. It rhymed. Dead things rhyme constantly; nobody warns you about that."

"What did they thank us for?"

"For ending the vigil." I paused, listening to something only I could hear, which was going to take some getting used to in conversation. "And someone else — different voice, much grumpier — wants to know if we're going to do anything about the state of the salt trade, because apparently the covenant covers maritime commerce grievances too. The realms are going to have opinions, Kestrel Almeida. You've signed up to be a complaint box for eternity."

"Oh good," Odile said flatly, wringing out her coat. "A god with an inbox."

* * *

We should have slept. Any sensible account would have us sleeping. Instead we sat on the terrace wrapped in every blanket the boat held, watching dawn finish its work, and none of us suggested sleep, because sleeping would mean the night was over, and none of us wanted the night to be over yet. It had cost too much to end so quickly.

That was when Sef said it. Quietly, the way you set down something breakable.

"Ilex should be here."

The name landed on the terrace between us and stayed there, glowing faintly, like everything else did now.

"He is here," I said.

Sef looked at me — really looked, the way they looked at things they intended to measure. "The oath?"

"The oath." I closed my eyes. Down at the gates, in the churned dark water where he'd made his stand, Ser Ilex of the Road lay under a drift of bone-white coral, and the dead of Coral had gone to him the way water goes downhill, naturally, immediately. He was being sung home. Properly. With rhymes. "He's not alone, and he's not hurting, and he knows — he knew, at the end, exactly what his march bought. I can... I can hear him being pleased about it. He's insufferable. He's telling some other dead knight about the time we met and he's improved the story enormously. Apparently in his version I was gracious."

"Were you not?"

"I threatened him with a bone stylus."

"That tracks."

"And he's—" My throat closed. I let it. I had chosen, twelve hours ago on that altar, to stop closing things. "He's glad, Sef. He waited three hundred years for a post worth dying on, and he got one, and he says—" I had to translate, because revenant gratitude doesn't have a direct equivalent in any living language. "He says duty was never the chain. Duty was the hand that offered to hold the chain with you. And we held it. So."

Nobody spoke for a while. The sea made its old sounds, and underneath them the new sound, the covenant sound, the low hum of eight realms' worth of pain being carried by one exhausted bone-carver on a coral terrace, which is to say: me.

Then Odile, who had been silent so long I'd assumed she'd fallen asleep sitting up, said: "Show me your hands."

"My hands?"

"Your hands, carver. Come on."

I held them out. They looked like my hands. They felt like everyone's hands, which is a distinction I'll explain someday when I've worked it out myself. Odile took them in hers — those ruined, scarred, magnificent hands that might never carve a ward-line again, that had taught me the final working by *voice*, word by word, because her fingers couldn't hold a stylus to save my life, and they saved my life anyway — and she turned them palm-up in the dawn light.

"There," she said. "Still yours?"

I considered. Through the oath I could feel a loom-worker's blistered palms in the west, a miner's cracked knuckles in the north, ten thousand pairs of working hands and their small daily hurts. But these — these calluses from my own tools, this scar where whalebone slipped when I was fourteen — these stayed mine. The oath ran through me. It did not consume me. There was, it turned out, a difference, and the difference was everything.

"Still mine," I said, and my voice betrayed me completely.

"Good." Odile pressed my fingers shut. Then she did something she had never once done in all our prickly miles together: she leaned over, put her arms around me, and held on. She smelled of brine and ward-smoke and three countries' worth of bad taverns. Her grip was still the grip of a dockworker. I am not ashamed to say I made a noise like a kettle giving up.

"Don't make it weird," she muttered into my hair.

"You're making it weird."

"I'm permitted. I taught you the anchor-ward verbally with a collapsed lung. One hug is contractually owed."

"One," Sef agreed solemnly. "Standard rate."

And then Sef piled in too, charts and elbows and all, and the three of us sat there in a heap on the terrace of a drowned holy city while the freed stars thinned overhead into morning, and I felt — everything. I felt everything, and it was unbearable, and I was held, and it turns out that second part makes the first part survivable. Nobody tells you that either. The dead never mention it. Maybe they forget. Maybe, where they went before us, there was no one left to hold on.

There will be, now. That was the whole point. That was what I'd sworn.

* * *

The sun cleared the horizon properly around what Sef estimated was mid-dawn, though they admitted their instruments were full of seawater and their estimates were now "devotional rather than scientific."

We ate the boat's emergency rations, which were terrible, which made them perfect. Hard biscuit, smoked fish gone soft, a jar of honey Odile had stolen from somewhere in Lumenhold and defended through a heist, a battle, and the end of the world. We ate like people who had earned the right to taste nothing.

"So," Sef said eventually, licking honey off a thumb. "What happens now? Practically. You're the anchor of all creation. Does that come with a chair? A hat?"

"A complaint inbox," I said.

"Does it come with a *plan*? The realms are going to notice the stars came back. Some of them will celebrate. Some of them built entire religions around the dark. The Ashen Court is headless but not friendless. Somebody, somewhere, is waking up this morning and deciding that whoever relit the sky owes them an explanation."

"Somebody is going to come looking for us," Odile agreed. She said it without alarm. We had passed through alarm somewhere around the gate battle and come out the other side into a strange calm practicality. "And they'll find three exhausted exiles on a reef, one of whom can hear every secret grievance in eight realms, which is arguably worse than finding an army."

I looked at my hands again. Still mine. And through them, faintly, the pulse of everything else: realms stirring awake under a sky they recognized, wards failing everywhere the old darkness had propped them up, borders suddenly negotiable, the whole fragile architecture of the world rearranging itself around the fact that the covenant lived again — anchored, this time, not in fear but in one extremely tired volunteer from Hollowmere.

"They won't have to look far," I said. "I can feel where every road goes now. And every road—" I almost smiled. "Every road leads somewhere that needs telling."

"Meaning what?" Sef asked.

Meaning, I wanted to say, that somewhere east of us a woman named Brenna had already lit a lamp at dawn out of habit and seen, faint and impossible, a star shining next to the sun, and dropped the lamp. Meaning that in Lumenhold the cage-doors hung open and people were walking into the vaults to see the place where the sky used to be kept, and weeping, and taking pieces home. Meaning that in Hollowmere — my valley, my beautiful coward valley — the wardless border stood open for the first time in three centuries, and my neighbors were standing at the ridge in their nightclothes, staring up, and one of them was saying my name like a question.

But what I said was: "Meaning we should probably fix the boat."

Which got a laugh out of both of them, tired and cracked and real, and for a moment — one whole, unrepeatable moment — the ocean of everything I carried went quiet, not because the pain stopped but because the tenderness balanced it, scale for scale, breath for breath. The stars faded to silver threads in the blue. The sea glowed and settled. Odile fell asleep against my shoulder, mid-sentence, trusting me with her weight the way she'd trusted me with her hands. Sef started sketching the new sky on the back of a soaked chart, tongue between their teeth.

And far off across the water, so faint I might have invented it, I heard sails. More than one. Coming from the direction of the mainland, moving with purpose.

I decided not to mention it until after breakfast.

Some news keeps. Even for an anchor.
