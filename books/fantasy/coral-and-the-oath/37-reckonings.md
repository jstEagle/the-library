## 37. Reckonings

The first thing you learn about peace is that it has paperwork.

Nobody warns you. The songs certainly don't. The songs end with the stars relit and everyone weeping with joy on a beach while gulls wheel overhead like punctuation. The songs do not include the four months of committee meetings that follow, during which you discover that the people who nearly let the world go dark are also the people who own all the chairs.

I should know. I've been sitting in them.

"Anchor Almeida," said the herald at the gates of Lumenhold, on the first morning of the Reckoning, and I had to bite the inside of my cheek not to laugh. Anchor Almeida. As if I were a title instead of a person who still, some mornings, woke up reaching for the quiet of Hollowmere like a missing tooth.

"Just Kestrel," I said.

"Anchor Kestrel, then."

The dead, who never leave me alone and have recently become positively chatty about protocol, found this hilarious. *Insist on the full form,* murmured a covenant-maker named Vess, who had died arguing about quorum rules in 612 and never really stopped. *Precedent matters.* Four hundred years dead and still a stickler. I have come to love her in the way you love a splinter you've decided to keep.

* * *

The Reckoning convened in the Hall of Hoarded Light, because there was a certain justice in it that even the Ashen loyalists couldn't argue with. The vaults where the Court had caged the stars stood empty now, their iron lattice-work pried open, their shelves of stolen brightness shipped home to the sky. But the hall itself remained—a vast circular chamber with tiered seats, built so that whoever stood at the center could be seen by everyone and threatened by no one.

That was where they put me. Center of the room. Anchor of the covenant. Witness to everything.

It is a strange thing, being a witness. I had expected, in the fevered weeks after the swearing, to be made a queen. There had been moments—genuinely, shamefully—when I'd let myself imagine it. The crown. The throne. The satisfaction of telling every person who'd ever called me *that bone-carver's odd child* exactly what I'd become.

Instead, the dead and the living had hammered out something better and far more annoying: I was to hold the covenant, feel its weight, and *report on it*. That was all. No armies. No edicts. When the realms quarreled—and oh, they quarreled—I was to stand in the middle and say what the oath felt like from the inside. Whether it strained. Whether it grieved. Whether someone's clever new arrangement was quietly starving a province of magic the way the old covenant had once starved Hollowmere of stars.

I was, in essence, a very well-dressed weathervane.

"The anchor does not rule," Vess had said, in the last hour of the swearing, when my hands were still smoking with starlight and I was too exhausted to argue. "The last time your family was given power over the terms, they ran to a valley and built walls around their own cowardice. We will not repeat the experiment. You will *feel* the covenant, child. You will not *wield* it."

I had agreed. I had been, at that moment, holding the entire ache of two realms in my chest and would have agreed to anything short of standing on my head.

Four months later, I still thought it was right. Which didn't stop it from being exhausting.

* * *

The first session nearly collapsed before lunch.

The problem was the Ashen Court—or what remained of it. The Regent was ash on the sea floor, but the Court was an institution, and institutions are harder to kill than regents. They arrived in gray robes with gray faces and gray arguments, and their delegate, a dry, precise woman named Magistrate Corvane, stood up on the first morning and said, "We acknowledge the restoration of the covenant. We request, merely, a role in its administration."

"A role," Odile repeated, from my left. She had not forgiven the Court. She had, in fact, made a small ward-sigil that she wore on her wrist which, when she touched it, let her speak with the exact cadence of someone who had not forgiven the Court. "You caged the stars for three hundred years."

"We *preserved* them," Corvane said, "during a period when no one else had the will to act. The old covenant failed. The realms went dark. Someone had to keep the light safe until—" she inclined her head a precise half-inch toward me, "—an heir emerged."

"Someone had to keep the light safe," Odile said. "In a vault. In your capital. Where you charged admission."

"Access was never denied to those with legitimate need."

"Access was *priced*, you ghoul."

I let them go for a while. That was another thing I'd learned: my job was less to speak than to *listen*, and not just with my ears. When Corvane talked about preservation, I felt the covenant hum a low, sour note—the sound of a truth with a lie folded inside it. When Odile talked about the vaults, the hum rang clean and angry and true.

"The oath grieves when you say *preserved*," I said at last, and the hall went quiet, because that was the one thing I could say that no one else could. "It doesn't grieve when the magistrate says *caged*. Interesting. The oath thinks you're lying to yourself, Magistrate, not to us."

Corvane's gray face did something complicated. "And what does the oath say of your companion's language?"

"That she's rude," I said, "but accurate. The oath has a high tolerance for rude."

Someone in the upper tiers laughed—Sef, who was supposed to be taking minutes and was instead, I knew, charting the hall's acoustics for the fourth time, because grief and curiosity in Sef lived in the same room and shared a bed.

* * *

The old orders were the harder problem.

The Ward-Smiths' Guild came first, Odile's former brethren, led by a master smith named Halvern who had once been Odile's teacher and had, by his own account, wept when she was exiled. Odile's account was shorter: *He signed the order and then he wept. Weeping is free.*

Halvern proposed that the Guild be granted sole authority over all new wards in the covenant's name. It was, he said, only sensible. Expertise should govern expertise.

I felt the covenant's hum and it said: *this is how it starts.* Not with crowns. With credentials.

"The last covenant died of exactly this," I told the hall. "A few people who knew things deciding that knowing things entitled them to own things. The Ward-Smiths designed the walls around my valley. My family's prison had excellent craftsmanship. I've seen the joinery."

Halvern went pale. Odile went very still beside me, her scarred hands flat on the table, and I knew what it cost her to hear her life's work named as prison-masonry and to say nothing in her own defense, because she'd confessed it all months ago by a fire in the marshes, and I had forgiven her, and forgiveness apparently required periodic renewal in public.

"The Guild will share its knowledge freely," Halvern said, after a silence, "or dissolve."

"Or teach," Odile said, not looking at him. "Open the archives. Publish the patterns. Let every village have a ward-smith and every ward-smith have rivals. Hoarding is the disease, Master. You taught me the craft. Teach everyone, and the craft survives you. Keep it, and you're just the Regent with better tools."

It was, I think, the cruelest and kindest thing anyone said in that hall for a week. Halvern looked at her for a long moment—looked at her hands, I noticed, at the silver scarring the wards had left—and said, "The archives will be opened."

The sun-cults wanted a seat, which surprised everyone until Sef pointed out they'd been the only people keeping time and calendar alive under the ash-dark. The starless astronomers—Sef's scattered, mostly-dead profession—wanted observatories rebuilt on every coast, and got them, mostly because Sef stood up with a voice that shook and described, in devastating detail, what it was like to devote your whole life to a sky and have the sky go out, and the hall could not look away, and I sat at the center feeling the covenant *mourn* with him, and when he finished, the vote wasn't even close.

The revenants, it turned out, could not attend. There was only one, and he was dead again.

I did not say that in the hall. I am learning which griefs are for committees and which are for later, alone, with a whetstone and a memory.

* * *

The sessions ran long. Evenings, the four of us—or the three of us, I corrected myself, and would keep correcting myself for the rest of my life—gathered in a borrowed house on Lumenhold's terraces, where the freed stars came down close over the harbor like they'd missed the place and couldn't quite believe they were allowed back.

Odile carved. Not wards—her hands couldn't hold the fine lines anymore—but little boats, little birds, things with broad forgiving curves. She'd taken up whittling with the grim determination of a woman renegotiating terms with her own body, and she was winning, mostly.

Sef charted. He had three new star catalogs going at once and talked in his sleep in coordinates.

And I sat with the dead, who had opinions about everything.

*The salt tax clause is a trap,* Vess warned me on the third week. *Read the annexes. Nobody reads the annexes. That's how the first covenant got its teeth.* She was right. The annexes, drafted by the Lumenhold merchant houses, quietly exempted "traditional light-holdings" from the sharing requirements—which is to say, the rich got to keep hoarding if they'd started hoarding early enough. I brought it to the floor. The merchant delegate called it a technicality. I said, "The oath itches when I read it," and there was a new sound in the hall, delegates leaning forward, because everyone was learning what my voice meant: *someone is trying something.*

They redrafted the annexes. The merchant houses sulked. The covenant's hum settled, smooth as a planed board.

"You realize," Odile said that night, whittling a bird with more feeling than the bird probably deserved, "you've invented a new kind of government. It's you, lying awake, feeling itchy."

"It's the dead, lying awake, feeling itchy. I just repeat what they say."

"You repeat what they say with *witness authority*, which is the whole trick. Half of ruling is having the only honest mouth in the room." She blew a curl of wood off the bird's wing. "Your family could never manage it. Too busy being frightened."

"Frightened of the weight."

"Frightened of the weight," she agreed, "and too proud to share it. You share it. That's the whole covenant, Kest. That's the entire trick. It was never meant to be carried alone. One person tried to carry it alone and built your valley."

I thought about Hollowmere then—about the wards coming down, the sky pouring in, my neighbors' faces the first morning we all stood in the square and looked *up* together. I had not been back. There had been no time, and then no courage. The Reckoning was a fine excuse. The Reckoning was always a fine excuse.

"You'll go," Odile said, reading me the way she read ward-lines, which is to say completely and without permission. "When the terms are sealed. You'll go home and show them the sky's still there."

"I know."

"You don't. But you will."

* * *

The terms were sealed on a gray morning in the sixth month, in the Hall of Hoarded Light, with every delegate standing and the freed stars visible even by day, pale as scars on the blue. The covenant hummed through me, through the floor, through the roots of the mountains—steady, watchful, *satisfied* in the way of a thing that has been fed properly for the first time in centuries.

I spoke the witness formula. My voice did the thing it does now, the thing where the dead speak through it and it sounds like many people agreeing, which is either holy or horrifying depending on your constitution. The delegates signed. The annexes were clean. Vess, in my ear, pronounced the quorum language *adequate*, which from Vess was a parade.

Corvane approached me afterward. The Ashen Court had voted—narrowly, bitterly—to dissolve itself and reconstitute as something called the Office of Light Stewardship, which was either reform or rebranding, and I had felt the covenant's hum on the question and reported, honestly, that it *waited and watched*, which was the most ominous thing the oath had ever said and I chose to interpret it as encouragement.

"Anchor Almeida," Corvane said. "A question, off the record."

"Anchors don't have off the record."

"Then on the record." Her gray eyes were dry and steady. "The Regent believed the covenant was cruelty. That no one should hold what you hold. I have watched you for six months. You are thinner. You sleep badly. You flinch at bells." She paused. "Was she wrong?"

I considered lying, out of principle. But the oath itches when I lie, and it itches *loudly*.

"She was wrong that no one should hold it," I said. "She was right that it shouldn't be held alone. The difference is everything, Magistrate, and it's the difference your Court never once tried."

Corvane bowed—deeper than protocol required—and left, and I stood in the emptying hall feeling the covenant turn that exchange over like a stone, looking for what lived under it.

* * *

That evening, on the terrace, a letter came.

It was carried by a rider from the north, road-dusted and apologetic, and it bore the seal of the Hollowmere town council—which was, itself, remarkable, because Hollowmere had never needed a council when the wards made all decisions unnecessary.

Odile read it first, because her eyes were fastest. Then she went very still, in the way she went still before saying something true and expensive.

"Well?" Sef said.

"They've found something." She held the letter out to me. "Under the old wardstone. When they were clearing the foundations. There's a chamber, Kest. There's *writing*. And they say—" she glanced at the letter again, and her voice gentled, which was how I knew it was bad, "—they say the writing is in your family's hand. All of it. Generations of it. And the last entry is dated the night the stars went out."

I took the letter. The dead, all around me, went quiet—the particular quiet they keep when they know something I don't, which is their favorite kind.

*Come home,* the letter said, in the careful round hand of someone writing to power for the first time and hating it. *Come home and tell us what your family buried.*

I looked up at the stars, pale over the harbor, and felt the covenant hum one low, curious note.

"Well," I said. "I suppose I'm going home after all."
