## 38. Burials and Beginnings

The tide at Coral's edge goes out twice a day now, since the stars came back. Nobody can explain it, least of all me, and I have more claim to explanation than most. The sea simply breathes differently when the sky above it is honest. It pulls back in the mornings to show what it kept, and pushes in at dusk to take its share again.

We buried Ser Ilex on the third morning tide, on the shelf of pale stone where the drowned city's gardens break the surface—bone-white coral grown up through centuries of salt into something like a hillside, something like a churchyard, if your churchyard had been grown rather than built and prayed over by the sea instead of any god worth naming.

Odile dug the grave herself. She couldn't hold a shovel properly—her hands were still wrapped in Sef's careful linen bandages, ten fingers that might never again do the fine work they were made for—but she stood over the digging and directed it like a foreman, which was its own kind of grief. Odile Vane does not delegate. Odile Vane has never once in her life let another person carry something she could carry worse herself.

"Deeper," she said, hoarse. "He was a tall man. He'll not thank us for curling him up like a shrimp."

"He won't thank us for anything," Sef said gently. "He's dead."

"He'll find a way," Odile said. "Men like him always do."

She was right, as it turned out. But I'm getting ahead of myself, and Ilex would rap my knuckles with a gauntlet for sloppy storytelling. He rapped them for everything else.

* * *

We had washed him the night before, the three of us, in a pool fed by a spring that ran down from the cliffs—fresh water, sweet water, the first he'd touched in four hundred years that wasn't seawater or blood. His armor we cleaned piece by piece. It was strange work, tender work, unbuckling the straps of a man who could not grumble about how you did it. Under the plate he was only a body, lean and scarred, with an old wound below his ribs that had never healed right even before death got ambitious with him.

There was a mark on his shoulder blade. A small brand, worn nearly smooth by time: a circle crossed by three lines.

"The covenant sigil," I said. My voice came out strange. Everything about my voice had been coming out strange since the altar, since I'd swallowed the whole ache of two realms and found it fit down my throat after all. "They marked their own."

"Of course they did." Odile didn't look up from the greave she was polishing with her forearm because her hands wouldn't close around a cloth. "You don't send a man to guard a door for eternity without stamping him property first. That's what orders do. Even good ones." She paused. "Even ours."

I thought about arguing. I thought about telling her that the Oathkeepers hadn't been hers, that she'd only built the walls, not lived behind them. But I had learned something in the deep places under the sea, holding every death in every realm inside my chest like a coal I'd chosen to cup in bare hands: you don't get to decide which grief belongs to whom. Grief is not a border country. It walks where it likes.

So I said, "Help me with the surcoat," and we dressed him in his colors—the faded blue of a knightly order that no longer existed anywhere except in one dead man's stubbornness—and we carried him out at dawn.

* * *

The whole of Coral came to the burial. I don't mean the party; I mean the dead.

They rose out of the luminous water in their hundreds, then their thousands, the drowned worshippers who had guarded the gates and the covenant-makers themselves, whose bones had become the coral gardens, whose voices I had once nearly drowned beneath. They stood in the shallows and on the shelves of stone, translucent as sea-glass, patient as tide. When Ilex's bier came down to the waterline they began to sing.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was Ilex's religion and I owe him that much. It was not a dirge. Dirges drag. This was a working song, the kind sailors sing hauling rope, the kind masons sing setting stone—a song with a shape to it, verse answering verse, the way the old oaths had been structured, call and response, burden shared. The dead were singing him *home*, and home, for a revenant, is not a place. It's a finishing.

"I never heard them like this," I whispered to Sef.

"You've never heard them *happy*," Sef said. Their eyes were red-rimmed and dry; astronomers cry on a delay, like letters posted from far away. "Listen to the intervals. That's a rising fifth. That's—" their breath caught. "That's the harmonic series. That's the sound stars make. Kestrel, they're singing him the way they used to sing the dawn."

And there, in the middle of the choir, a new voice joined. A low voice, a soldier's voice, roughened by four centuries of standing post, and it knew every word.

Ilex sang his own burial. Of course he did. He'd never trusted anyone else to do a job right.

I laughed, and then I cried, and then I laughed again while crying, which I am told is unbecoming in an anchor of the realms but which nobody present seemed inclined to report.

* * *

We laid him down with his sword—his real sword, the one from before, which we had recovered from the gate where he fell, notched and ash-stained and heavier than sin. Odile insisted it go in the ground with him. "A knight without his sword is just a man in a very expensive coat," she said, and dared anyone to argue. Nobody did. Her hands were bandaged to the wrist. She'd earned her dares.

Sef placed a star chart beside him, folded small—one of the old ones, drawn back when the sky still had lights in it, annotated in Sef's cramped hand with corrections made during our journey. "For the road," they said. "In case the dead keep maps too."

I gave him my words. That's the Oathkeeper's coin; it's the only thing I've ever had enough of. I stood at the head of the grave and I spoke his name into the space the dead had opened, and I felt the name travel—not heard so much as *received*, the way a harbor receives a ship.

*Ser Ilex of the Last Watch. Keeper of the Road to Coral. Sworn before the drowning, faithful after it. He held the door.*

The choir took it up and carried it outward, voice by voice, until the whole sea was saying it, wave after wave after wave, and I understood that this was how the dead remembered: not in monuments but in repetition. As long as someone was singing, he was not finished. And the sea, being the sea, would go on singing long after we'd walked away.

"Good," Odile said quietly, watching the water take the grave-mound's edge. "Let it be loud. Let it be *loud*, Kestrel. I've been to too many quiet funerals. Quiet is what the Ashen Court wanted for everyone." She looked at me, and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "This is what winning sounds like."

* * *

We stayed three days after the burial, because nobody wanted to be the first to say the thing we all knew was coming, which is that found families are not exempt from geography.

It happened on the third evening, on the cliff above the drowned altar, with a fire lit from driftwood and the new stars wheeling overhead—real ones, uncaged ones, doing exactly what stars are supposed to do, which is burn without asking anything of anybody. We'd eaten fish caught by actual fishing, which felt decadent after months of ward-biscuits and mistrust, and Sef had gotten slightly drunk on plum wine traded off a refugee caravan, and was lying on their back cataloguing constellations aloud with the fervor of someone reading love letters.

"—and *that* one's wrong, by the way, the charts all have it wrong, the Weaver's left hand is two degrees east of where everyone insists it is, and I am going to spend the rest of my life correcting every chart in existence—"

"That sounds like a life," Odile said.

"It *is* a life." Sef sat up, suddenly serious, wine and purpose colliding. "That's the thing. I spent six years counting nothing. Now there's a sky full of everything and nobody's mapped it yet. Somebody has to. Somebody has to stand on rooftops in Lumenhold and Brackish Ford and every ash-choked town between and teach people the stars came back, they're *ours* again, and here's how to find north by them, and here's why that matters—"

They stopped. Looked at us. "I'm leaving tomorrow," they said, apologetic and immovable at once. "Aren't I."

"Astronomers belong under open sky," I said. "Not under our feet."

"I'll write," Sef said fiercely. "Charts, tables, everything. You'll get so many letters you'll beg me to stop."

"No one has ever begged an astronomer to stop writing," Odile said. "But write anyway."

Then Odile was quiet for a long time, turning her wine cup in her bandaged hands, and I knew what was coming for her too, because I'd been listening—not to the dead, for once, but to the living, which turns out to be a skill you can practice.

"Brackish Ford," she said finally. "My guildhall's still standing. Half-burned, but standing. There are ward-smiths younger than me who learned their trade building cages because that's all anyone taught anymore. Cages and containment and *keep it out, keep it down, keep it quiet*." Her jaw worked. "Somebody has to teach them the other kind. Wards that hold doors *open*. Wards that protect without imprisoning." She glanced at me sideways, wry and ashamed and brave all at once. "I owe the profession a few apologies. Best delivered in person. With demonstrations."

"You could stay," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded.

"I could," she agreed. "And in twenty years I'd be a comfortable old woman who helped once. Or I can go be useful, and be a tired old woman who helped often." She reached over—awkwardly, with both bandaged hands, the way you lift something precious and broken—and pressed her forehead to mine. "You'll see me again, Kestrel Almeida. I intend to be insufferable for decades yet. These hands will heal or they won't, but either way I've still got a mouth, and mouths are underrated tools for ward-work. Ask anyone I've ever shouted at."

* * *

Which left me.

They both turned to me, and I looked out at the sea, where the last light of the choir still moved under the water like weather, and I told them the truth, which was that I didn't know.

Everyone assumes the anchor must stay at the altar. Stand in one place forever, feel everything, be the living hinge of the covenant until some future generation finds a better arrangement. That was the old terms. But the old terms were written by frightened people building a prison, and I had rewritten them at the bottom of the sea with the whole choir as witness, and my version says something different. My version says the oath holds because it is *carried*, and things that are carried are allowed to move.

"I think," I said slowly, feeling my way along the thought like a carver finding the grain in bone, "the anchor doesn't have to be a stone. I think it can be a pilgrim. I think I need to go back to Hollowmere."

"Hollowmere," Odile repeated. "The valley that forgot."

"It's not forgetting anymore. The wards are down. The sky's open. But the people there—" I thought of my neighbors, my whole life, kind and careful and walled-in without knowing they were walled-in, the way fish don't know about water. "They've never seen a star. They've never heard a dead voice that wasn't a horror story. And I spent thirty years among them being invisible, hiding the one true thing about myself, and they let me, because hiding was the local custom." I laughed a little. "I want to go back and be *seen*. Properly. Show them the records, open the archives, tell them what their valley was built on and why. If the covenant means anything, it has to mean that no one gets walled in again—not for safety, not for peace, not even kindly."

"And the dead?" Sef asked softly. "All of them, everywhere? Carrying that—walking around with the whole weight—"

"They'll come with me," I said. "That's rather the point. I'm never alone again. It's the best and strangest gift anyone's ever given me." I looked at the fire, at their faces, at the sea beyond. "Besides. Someone taught me that duty isn't a chain. It's a gift you keep choosing. I intend to keep choosing it somewhere with bad weather and good neighbors."

* * *

We parted at dawn, because partings at dusk are for people who expect to see each other soon, and we were none of us sure, though all of us determined.

Sef went north along the coast road with a satchel of charts and a bottle of plum wine for the journey, walking backward for the first hundred paces, waving, shouting corrections at the sky. Odile went west toward Brackish Ford with her tools wrapped against her chest like an infant, and at the last moment she pressed something into my palm: a small ward-sigil, carved from whalebone—my whalebone, she'd stolen it from my kit weeks ago, the thief—in the shape of an open door.

"So your wards remember what they're for," she said. "Go home, Kestrel. Go home loudly."

I watched them until the road bent, and then I stood alone on the cliff at Coral's edge with the wind coming off a luminous sea and the dead murmuring around me like a congregation settling into pews, and I turned my face toward the interior, toward the mountains, toward the valley that had raised me and hidden me and, without meaning to, made me exactly strange enough to save the world.

One road. One walker. A chorus for company.

"Well," I said to all of them, and to no one, and to myself. "Let's go remind a valley it has a sky."

And far off, past the ridgelines, past the borders of the map I'd grown up inside, I felt something answer—not a voice, not yet, but a hush, a held breath, a valley remembering. Somewhere in Hollowmere, I would learn later, the old wardstone's shattered crest had begun, impossibly, to glow.

Home, it seemed, had heard I was coming.

And home, it seemed, had questions.
