## 39. The Valley Remembers

The road into Hollowmere no longer ends where it used to.

That's the first thing I notice, and I want you to understand how strange that is. For twenty-some years the road ended at the ridge—ended *hard*, the way a sentence ends when someone slams a door mid-word. You walked up to the border wards and your feet simply forgot what they were doing. Not fear. Forgetting. The wards didn't keep people out so much as they kept the idea of leaving out, and apparently the idea of arriving too. I had lived my whole life inside a parenthesis and never once wondered what the sentence was about.

Now the road goes over the ridge. It goes over the ridge because Odile Vane stood on that ridge with her ruined hands folded in Sef's coat pocket—she refuses pockets of her own, calls them "a lie you wear"—and told the valley's foundations, in a voice like gravel learning manners, that they were released from their duties.

I watched her do it. She didn't use a single tool. She talked to the stones the way you'd talk to an old dog that had been guarding a door so long it had forgotten there was ever anything behind it. And the wards came down not with a crash but with a sound like a long-held breath finally let go, and every bird within three valleys lost its mind at once.

So: the road goes over the ridge now, and I am walking up it, and my neighbors are going to see me for the first time.

Not see me see me—I've been seen all my life. I've been *looked through*, which is different, and I've been *looked at sideways*, which is worse, but I have never been looked at as myself, on purpose, by anyone from Hollowmere who wasn't already dead. The dead looked at me plenty. That was the whole problem, if you remember.

*They're waiting,* says a voice at my shoulder, and I don't jump anymore, which is either growth or exhaustion, and honestly at this point in my life I've stopped keeping records.

"I know," I say.

*I can feel them. Little warm knots of them, all along the lane. Like bread rising.*

"You're being poetic again."

*I'm dead, dear. Poetry is the only thing I've got left that isn't opinions.*

This is Mereth, by the way. Mereth Haldoon, drowned in the year the bridge failed, four hundred years before my grandmother was born, and the reason she's riding my shoulder instead of resting wherever drowned covenant-makers rest is that she has appointed herself my herald, my conscience, and occasionally my heckler. The dead of the new covenant are allowed to linger near me—they say it feels like standing near a lit window in cold weather—and most of them have the decency to drift off after a while. Mereth has decided that lingering is her calling.

"You could wait in the village," I say. "See your old house."

*I burned down in 1211. I'd rather watch you embarrass yourself.*

"Thank you. Truly. Your faith sustains me."

Behind me—far enough back to be polite, close enough to catch me if the road decides I haven't suffered enough—come Odile and Sef. We agreed they'd hang back at the crest until I waved. This was my request, and I'll own it: I wanted to come down into Hollowmere alone first. If my neighbors were going to throw things, I wanted them throwing at one person, not at a disgraced ward-smith and a boy astronomer who never did anything to anybody except count stars that weren't there.

Though knowing this valley, they'd probably blame Sef for the stars coming *back*. People here distrust weather. They'd absolutely distrust astronomy.

* * *

Let me tell you what I did with the records, because I promised myself I'd tell someone and the dead don't count, since they already know everything and are insufferable about it.

The Almeida house—the Oathkeeper house, though we called it something smaller when I was growing up, called it just *home*, which is the biggest lie a building can tell—had a cellar. Under the floorboards under the coal bin under the root shelf, there was a strongbox, and in the strongbox were the valley's ledgers. Every birth. Every death. Every ward-renewal signed in blood-ink by an Almeida hand, generation after generation, and beside each signature a list of names: the people whose memories were to be smoothed over. The woman who almost remembered her son hearing voices. The man who asked too many questions about the ridge and woke up certain he'd never asked anything in his life. Whole pages of edits. My family didn't just live behind the wards. We wrote the valley's memory ourselves, year by year, like scribes correcting scripture.

When I found the box—at fifteen, looking for coin, finding instead the architecture of my entire imprisonment—I put it back. I put it *back*. I want that on the record. I was fifteen, and I was afraid, and I chose the lie because the lie had a roof attached to it.

Last month I took the box out, carried it to the meetinghouse green, and opened it in front of everyone.

I'd like to tell you I gave a speech. I did not give a speech. What I actually did was stand there while forty-odd people read their own names off lists of things that had been taken from them, and the silence got so heavy that Mereth, watching from wherever dead people watch, later described it as "the loudest nothing I have heard in nine centuries." Old Tam Wicker found his daughter's name—the daughter who died at seven, whom he'd been led to believe had never been born, because grief was deemed *disruptive*. Tam sat down on the green right where he was standing, an old man folding up like a chair, and held that ledger page against his chest and made a sound I will hear until I die, which given my line of work may be quite a while.

And then—and this is the part I still can't make sense of, and I've stopped trying—he looked up at me and said, "You brought her back to me. Paper's not much. But it's hers."

I hadn't brought his daughter back. My ancestors had stolen her twice: once from living, once from memory. All I did was stop holding the lid shut.

But that's how it works, isn't it. The ones who benefit from a locked room are never the ones who get credit for opening it. They just get to stand in the doorway and be resented by the light.

* * *

The lane into the village is lined with people.

I knew it would be. Mereth told me. But knowing a thing and walking into it are separated by roughly the distance between a map and the territory, and the territory has teeth.

There's Tam Wicker at his gate, hat in hand, which is somehow more unnerving than a pitchfork would be. There's the Alderys sisters, who used to sell me mutton and never once met my eyes, now standing shoulder to shoulder with their eyes extremely met, thank you. There's children—young ones, born after the wards fell or just before, who have grown up in two years' worth of sky and think stars are normal, little barbarians—and one of them is pointing at me and saying, loudly, in the way of children, "That's the bone-carver. She talks to ghosts," and her mother hushes her, and then, visibly deciding that hushing is exactly the habit the valley is trying to break, un-hushes her and says, "Yes. She does. Ask her politely."

The child asks me politely whether ghosts are cold.

"They're not anything," I say, crouching. "But they remember warmth. I think that's why they like us."

She considers this with the gravity of a magistrate, nods, and informs me her name is Pip, and that she has a loose tooth, and would I carve it into something when it comes out, because her mother says teeth from the bone-carver bring good dreams, and then she runs off before I can answer, which is the correct way to negotiate with me and frankly more people should try it.

*Good dreams,* murmurs Mereth, delighted. *You've become folklore.*

"I've become a tooth vendor."

*Folklore starts somewhere. Usually with teeth.*

Odile and Sef come down the hill when I wave, and I watch my neighbors watch them, and here's the thing I need to write down so I don't lose it: nobody looks away. Two years ago every eye in this valley had been trained, carefully, lovingly, by my ancestors, to slide off anything uncomfortable. Now they look. They look at Odile's scarred hands and they look at Sef's star charts poking out of his bag, and Tam Wicker shakes Odile's hand with the reverence of a man greeting a general, because everyone in Hollowmere knows by now whose voice talked the ridge open, and it wasn't mine.

"Your lot built the cage," Odile said to them once, flatly, when some well-meaning soul thanked her for freeing the valley. "Mine welded half the bars. Don't thank me. Just don't rebuild it."

Tam had nodded slowly and said, "Fair," and I have thought about that exchange roughly nine hundred times since, because that is what accountability sounds like when it's working: short, uncomfortable, and accepted.

* * *

The meetinghouse that evening smells of woodsmoke and lamp oil and, faintly, of the sea, which is impossible, because Hollowmere is four hundred miles from any sea, except that I am the anchor of a covenant that holds every realm including the drowned one, and sometimes the drowned one sends its regards. The salt smell means the coral is dreaming. Sef has started keeping a log of it. He says the pattern correlates with tides in realms that don't officially have tides. He said this with the expression of a man who has decided to stop expecting the universe to behave and start taking notes instead, which I maintain is the healthiest spiritual development available to a scholar.

Inside, the whole valley has turned out. Farmers, millers, the ferryman's widow, Pip's mother holding Pip, old Tam front and center with his ledger page framed in cheap pine and hung around his neck like a medal, which it is, though I'd never say so out loud because he'd hit me with it.

They've cleared the tables away and set up chairs in a circle, which is new. Hollowmere meetings used to happen in rows facing whoever was in charge. Circles mean nobody's in charge. Circles are Odile's doing; she drew the arrangement in chalk on the floorboards six months ago and dared anyone to move the furniture back, and nobody has.

"We voted," says Marta Alderys, oldest of the sisters, in the tone of a woman announcing a siege. "On what to do with the rest of the records. The ledgers you didn't find. The ones the strongbox didn't hold."

My stomach does something complicated. There were other boxes. I knew there were other boxes—Mereth had told me, in fact, months ago, names of archives, the chapel loft, the mill office—but I'd been putting it off, because every box is a fresh batch of stolen memory and I have learned that returning stolen things is a service you perform at your own cost, forever, without applause.

"The chapel loft," I say. "And the mill. And I think there's a cache under the old shrine, if the shrine's foundation survived the—" I gesture vaguely upward, at the concept of the night the sky went out.

Marta shakes her head. "We're not asking where they are. We're asking what to *do*. Because some folk—" she pauses, chooses honesty over diplomacy, which the circle has made contagious—"some folk don't want theirs read out. Tam wanted his. Others want theirs burned unread. And some—" her voice catches, just slightly, and her sister puts a hand on her arm—"some want to know why. Why us. Why this valley. Why did the great Oathkeepers pick a farming hollow to lobotomize for six hundred years."

The question lands in the middle of the circle like a dropped anvil.

Because I'm the anchor, I could answer it without thinking. The wards needed a place where magic pooled thin and quiet, a still pond to hide a fugitive bloodline in. Hollowmere wasn't chosen despite being small. It was chosen *because* it was small, because nothing here could fight back, because my ancestors needed a valley the way a thief needs a dark room. We weren't protected. We were packaging.

But that's the covenant's answer, the cosmic answer, the answer written in dead languages on drowned altars. And these people deserve the plain one.

"My family picked you because you were easy," I say. "That's the whole truth. Small, scattered, trusting. The wards needed somewhere the edits wouldn't be noticed, and a valley where everybody knows everybody is perfect for it, because when someone starts remembering wrong, their neighbors correct them. You policed each other for us. Kindly. Lovingly. That's the worst part. You did it with casseroles."

Silence. The bad kind, then, slowly, the kind that turns over and becomes something else. Because I watch them do the arithmetic—watch Tam think of every neighborly kindness that had also been a muzzle—and I watch them arrive, together, at the same terrible gentle conclusion:

It wasn't their fault either.

"It wasn't our fault either," Marta says out loud, testing it.

"No."

"And it wasn't yours."

I open my mouth to deliver the standard response, the one where I enumerate my family's crimes and my personal cowardice at fifteen, and Tam Wicker stands up.

"I've been thinking," he says, "about the difference between a debt and a weight." He taps the framed page at his chest. "What your people took, girl, I can't get back except this paper. What your people *did*—" he looks around the circle, at the faces of everyone edited and everyone editing—"they did it so it couldn't be traced to them. So somebody else would always be holding the bag. Well. I'm done letting dead cowards decide who carries what." He points at me with a finger that has buried two wives and one resurrected memory of a daughter, and it is not an unkind finger. "You carry the covenant. That's yours, and you chose it, and I'll not take it from you. But you don't carry *us*. We carry ourselves now. That's what the vote's about. We read what we want, burn what we want, and grieve how we want—and you're invited to sit in the circle while we do it. As a neighbor. Not as a keeper."

*A neighbor,* repeats Mereth softly, in my ear, in the voice she uses for things that matter. *Say yes, you idiot. Say yes before you ruin it with a speech.*

"Yes," I say. No speech. Growth.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when the meetinghouse has emptied and the lamps are down to coals—I walk the lane alone, because the anchor of a realm-spanning covenant still needs to walk off dinner like anyone else, and besides, the dead of Hollowmere come out at this hour, shy as cats, and I've appointments.

They gather at the well. That's their custom now, or maybe it was always their custom and the wards just muted it. A dozen of them tonight, translucent in the way that's less like mist and more like remembering a face. The miller who fell asleep drunk in winter. A girl who died of fever in the first ward-year, before the edits were even perfected, one of the first. And Mereth, of course, always Mereth, presiding.

"Well?" she says. "Satisfied?"

"With what?"

"With coming home. With all of it. The ledger on the green, the circle, the child with the tooth. You spent two years saving the sky, and I don't think you once asked yourself whether the ground would take you back."

I lean on the well. Below, the water holds a scatter of reflected stars, and it occurs to me—not for the first time, but with new weight—that I grew up in a valley that taught me reflection was all the sky we deserved, and I have spent two years learning otherwise, and the lesson isn't finished.

"I didn't ask," I admit. "I was afraid of the answer. Which is stupid, because I can face down the Ashen Court, but a farmer's opinion makes me sweat."

"That's not stupidity, dear. That's the whole point of belonging. Courts can't hurt you where it counts. Neighbors can." She drifts closer, and for a moment her outline sharpens the way it does when she's about to say something she's been carrying. "Do you know why I stayed? After the oath. I could have gone on. To the coral, to the choir, to whatever's next. I stayed because I drowned in this valley and no one ever said my name right again. The wards ate my funeral. Four hundred years, and I have been waiting for one person from this valley to look at me and say *there she is.*"

The well water shivers. So, honestly, do I.

"Tonight," she says, "Pip's mother told the circle about me. Read my name off the ledger. Said I drowned saving the ferryman's boy, and that the valley owes my family a stone in the churchyard, and they voted it unanimous." Her voice does something I've only heard the dead do when a wound closes: it goes very quiet and very bright at once. "*There she is,* Kestrel. Somebody finally said it. So I'm telling you, as a dead woman who has waited longer than your entire civilization has existed in this valley: it takes. The ground takes you back. It just needs the truth first. Everything does."

She reaches out—she can't touch, none of them can, but the gesture is the thing—and straightens my collar, the way you'd tidy someone before they walk into a room full of people who love them.

"Now go home," she says. "Sleep in your own bed. And in the morning, there's a boat at the river landing with Lumenhold markings, and a courier who's been asking for you by name since dusk, and she won't say what for, and she's wearing Court grey under a merchant's cloak, which I could see from the ridge, dear, because I may be dead but I'm not *blind*."

I look toward the river. Far off, at the edge of lantern-light, a shape waits by the water, patient as bad news.

"Some days," I tell Mereth, "the peace lasts almost a whole night."

*Cherish those,* she says. *They're how you tell the good stories from the easy ones.*

I go home. I sleep in my own bed, in my own valley, under a sky with all its stars accounted for.

And in the morning, I go down to the boat.
