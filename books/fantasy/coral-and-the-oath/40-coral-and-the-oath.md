## 40. Coral and the Oath

The children arrive at dusk, as they always do, because I tell them the dead are easier to hear when the light is low and the world is tired of pretending to be solid. That's not strictly true. The dead are equally audible at noon. But children who arrive at dusk walk slower, and slow walkers listen better, and I have learned—over years, over mistakes, over one particularly catastrophic afternoon involving a goat, a grave, and Odile's left boot—that the listening matters more than the hearing.

There are nine of them tonight. They come up the hill path to the schoolhouse Odile built without asking anyone's permission, because Odile has never once in her life asked permission for anything, and because the old schoolhouse in Hollowmere still smelled, in her words, "like a place where someone decided things for you." So she built a new one on the ridge, facing the sea-side of the sky, with wide windows and a door that has no lock. The door with no lock was her idea too. "If you build a room for secrets," she said, "you'll only ever teach secrets. Build a room for truths and maybe you'll manage one or two."

She manages more than one or two. She manages them with a hammer.

The children file in—Maren and her twin brothers, the ferryman's daughter Tove who never stops asking questions, the two Lumenhold orphans the roads brought us three winters ago, and little Corvin, who is seven and has already decided he is afraid of everything and has therefore, by Odile's logic, the best head start of anyone. They sit on the benches Sef built to his own exacting specifications, which means they wobble slightly, which means the children shift and fidget all lesson, which Sef insists is a feature. "Restless bodies think," he says. "Still bodies only sit."

I stand at the front, where the light from the lamps Odile wards—small, steady, star-bright, and legal now, all of it legal, that word still fits strangely in my mouth like a borrowed coat—and I hold up the evening's bone.

It's a whalebone rib, curved like a question mark, and it has been in my family for longer than my family has been anything worth being. It used to whisper. Now it sings, if you know how to hold it, and the whole point of tonight, and every night, is that they will know how to hold it.

"Who can tell me the first rule?" I ask.

Nine hands go up, including Corvin's, who does not know the answer and never lets that stop him.

"The dead are not echoes," Tove recites, because Tove always recites; she has a memory like a well—everything dropped in stays down there, whole and cold and retrievable. "They are people who have run out of body, not out of self."

"And the second?"

"The dead do not owe us their stories," says one of the Lumenhold twins, whose name is Pell and who lost more than anyone in this room and speaks about it with the flat practicality of a child counting coins. "We ask. We do not take."

"And the third?" I look at Corvin, because it's kind to let him win one.

Corvin stands up so fast he nearly tips his bench. "If it hurts too much, you put the bone down and you say so, and nobody thinks less of you, and Odile makes tea."

"Odile always makes tea," Maren mutters.

"Odile's tea is a ward in its own right," I say solemnly, and Corvin sits down glowing, and somewhere behind me I hear Odile snort from the doorway where she leans with her arms crossed and her ruined hands tucked in her apron, listening the way she always listens—like a woman auditing the world for errors and finding, tonight, mercifully few.

* * *

I was not always going to teach. I want that on the record, somewhere, in case the record survives me, because the record has a way of growing teeth. For the first year after Coral, after the swearing, after the stars came home like birds to a roost they'd almost forgotten, I wanted nothing in the world but quiet. I had spent my whole life hearing the dead against my will, and then I had spent one terrible night agreeing to hear them forever, on purpose, with my whole self, and I woke the next morning on the shingle above the drowned altar feeling like a house with every window open at once.

Every death. Every realm's ache. That was the term. That was always the term, and the first Almeida ran from it, and built a valley to hide in, and walled it with wards and called the walls protection, and the bill for that cowardice came due on the night the stars went out, in a workshop, in a boy's hands, in me.

I feel it all still. I will feel it all always. That is the oath, and I keep it, and some mornings keeping it looks like lying face-down on the floor of my own house until the tide of other people's grief recedes enough for me to stand. Odile knows. She leaves water by my head and doesn't comment, which is its own kind of warding. Sef knows too, and handles it differently—he sits beside me and counts. Stars, breaths, waves, the names of constellations we freed together. Counting was how he anchored me in the choir of the drowned, when every dead voice in creation sang at once and I nearly went under; and counting is how he anchors me now, on the bad mornings, his voice dry and precise as a chart. "Four hundred and eleven stars in the restored sky. Four hundred and twelve if you count the new one. Which we do. Because we earned it."

We did earn it. Ilex earned it most of all, and he isn't here to be smug about it, which is the great unfinished grief of my life, the ache that doesn't recede with the tide but has simply moved in and pays no rent and never will. We buried him at Coral's edge, in the luminous water, and the dead sang him home properly—every dead, all realms, the whole vast congregation I now carry in my chest—and I felt his going the way you feel a hand let go of yours in the dark. Not gone. Released. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and teaching children that difference is most of why I teach.

Because here is what I learned, down at the altar, with the Regent's ash still settling and the stars igniting overhead like a held breath finally let out: the oath holds because it is shared.

Not because I am strong. I am not strong; I am stubborn, which is a different animal and a worse one. Not because the Almeida blood is special—our blood is special the way a scar is special, it records what happened to us, nothing more. The oath holds because Odile taught half the ward-smiths of two realms to build open wards, wards that hold without imprisoning, and because Sef's star charts hang in every observatory and every schoolhouse and every ferry captain's cabin, so that no one ever again looks up at a caged sky and thinks that's simply how the world is. The oath holds because Tove's mother the ferrywoman knows the old songs and sings them to passengers. Because Pell and his sister plant light-flowers on the Lumenhold ruins where the Court hoarded its stolen brightness, and the flowers grow, and people come from miles to see them, and nobody guards them, because the whole point of light is that you cannot hoard it without killing it.

Power distributed is power uncorrupted. I didn't say that first. Sef did, at the reckoning at Coral, when the realms sat down to argue the new covenant's terms and every delegation tried to hand the whole weight of it to me. He said it flatly, the way he says everything, and then he said the second part, which I like better: "Besides, the anchor would just lose it. Have you seen where Kestrel keeps their keys?"

* * *

"Right," I say, and the whalebone hums under my palms, ready. "Tonight we listen to someone gentle. Corvin, you can hold the other end. If it's too much, you say so."

Corvin nods with the gravity of a general accepting a battle plan.

I lay the bone on the cloth in the center of the room, and the children lean in, and I do what Ilex taught me on the road to Coral all those years ago, in the marshes, when my head was a riot of whispers and he stood there in his rusted revenant patience and said: *You suffer the dead because you treat them like weather. They are guests. Greet them.*

"Good evening," I say to the bone. "We're students. We've come to listen, if you're willing, and we'll be kind, and we'll put you down whenever you like."

The bone warms. It always warms. And into the hush of the schoolhouse, soft as tide over shingle, comes a voice—not a whisper anymore, never again a whisper, because I learned to open the door wide enough that the dead don't have to shout—a woman's voice, old and amused, the voice of a fisherwoman who drowned four hundred years ago and has been, in her own words, "rested and ready" ever since.

She tells them about the herring runs of her century. About her sister who could whistle up fog. About the small bright fish that came to the lantern light, and how you had to be still, so still, and how being still wasn't waiting, it was listening. The children are rapt. Corvin's eyes are enormous. Tove asks three questions and the dead woman answers two and declines the third—"that one's between me and the sea, little one"—and Tove, to her eternal credit, writes it down as a question rather than an answer, which is the whole discipline in miniature, and I could weep with pride, and probably will, later, into Odile's terrible tea.

When the voice fades, the bone cools, and I wrap it in the cloth, and the children sit back with that particular fullness they get, the look of people who have been somewhere and returned.

"Why her?" Pell asks. "Why the gentle ones? Don't we need to practice the hard ones?"

It's a sharp question. I let it sit a moment, the way you let bread rise.

"Because," I say, "for a long time, the hard ones were all I had. The murdered, the betrayed, the angry dead of a broken covenant, all of them shouting into one boy's skull because I was the only door left unlocked in the world. And it nearly broke me, and it nearly broke everything, because when you only hear the dead in pain, you start to think that's all death is. It isn't. Death is mostly quiet. Death is mostly people resting, and watching, and being fond of the living in the way of someone reading a book they already know the end of." I look around at their faces, nine small lantern-lit faces. "We practice the gentle ones so that when the hard ones come—and they will come, because the world is still healing and some wounds sing—we'll know that the hard ones aren't the whole truth. Just a loud part of it."

"Like the Court," says Maren.

"Like the Court," I agree. "Loud isn't the same as true. Ash isn't the same as everything."

Odile pushes off the doorframe and comes to the front, and the children straighten, because everyone straightens for Odile, it's a reflex, possibly a survival trait. She holds up her hands—scarred, stiff, the hands that built the wards that caged my family and then built the wards that freed them, the hands that may never carve fine work again and teach with them anyway, verbally, patiently, cursing with tremendous creativity when the children's knots go wrong.

"Show them the third rule again," she says. "The tea one."

"IF IT HURTS TOO MUCH—" the room bellows, and Corvin loudest of all.

"That's right." She looks at me over their heads, and something passes between us that has passed between us a thousand times and will pass a thousand more: the confession, the forgiveness, the grace that shamed me more than anger ever could, all of it folded into a single glance like a letter read so many times it's gone soft at the folds. "Now go home. And if you hear anything on the walk back—and some of you will, you know who you are—wish it good evening and keep walking. It's mostly just the dead being nosy. They get it from watching us."

They go, tumbling down the hill path in the blue dark, loud and alive, and Odile and I stand in the doorway and watch them until the last small light of Tove's lantern disappears among the valley's scattered lamps—Hollowmere, open to the sky now, walled no more, its records freed and its people free with them, my neighbors who greet me by name, who bring me bread, who once, memorably, elected me to the valley council, an honor I accepted on the condition that I never had to win anything again.

* * *

Later, when the schoolhouse is dark and Odile has gone down to her forge-house and Sef is up on the observatory roof where he always is on clear nights, I climb the ridge alone, because there is one lesson I keep for myself.

The stars are all out. All four hundred and twelve. They wheel overhead in their ancient slow order, and I lie back on the grass and open every window in the house of myself, and I feel them: the whole realm's ache, the whole realm's joy, the dead at rest, the living at work, the tide of it all flowing through me and out again, not a weight I carry but a current I stand in.

The first Almeida couldn't bear it. I understand that now, better than I did at the altar, better than I did in the years after. It is a lot. It is so much, some days, that the only honest prayer is *not tonight, not all of it, please.* But I bear it, because I am not the first Almeida, and because I am not only an Almeida. I am also the person Odile makes tea for. I am the person Sef counts stars to. I am the teacher of nine children who can now recite three rules and mean them, and one of those children, I'd wager the forge on it, will hear the dead more clearly than I ever did, and will not be walled away for it, and will not have to carry it alone, because we built it shared this time. We built the whole thing shared.

That was the flaw in the old oath, in the end. Not the terms. The terms were always bearable—bearable the way birth is bearable, the way grief is bearable, the way anything is bearable with hands holding yours. The flaw was that one person held it alone, and a person alone will always, eventually, choose the wall. The valley. The silence. The locked door.

There is no locked door here. There never will be again.

I lie on the ridge a long while, feeling everything, holding all of it, held by all of it, and the stars turn, and the sea breathes, and somewhere below a lamp is lit and tea is put on for me without my asking, and the oath holds—tonight, tomorrow, all the turning years—

because it is ours.
