## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The salt line ran for eleven miles around the valley, and every inch of it had to be tended by hand.

Yara Ravensworth had known this since she was six years old, when Master Aldous first put a rake in her grip and told her that magic was not a gift but a chore. She had believed him then, in the way children believe anything said with sufficient gravity. Now, at nineteen, kneeling in the grey morning light with salt dust on her knees and her back aching in a way that felt suspiciously permanent, she believed it less.

"You're raking crooked," Aldous said from his stool three paces away. He did not rise to inspect her work. He hadn't risen without help since midwinter, and it was nearly harvest now.

"The line's crooked," Yara said. "It dips here. It always dips here. I've raked it straight four times this season and four times it's dipped again."

"Then the ground wants it crooked, and you'll leave it be." He turned the page of his ledger with a papery click. "The covenant doesn't care for tidiness. It cares for tending."

"The covenant," Yara said, "cares for nothing. It's a rule. Rules don't care. People care, and there are four of us left."

"Three," said Aldous, without looking up. "Brother Fenwick died in spring, and I'm not counting myself for much longer, and you know it, so don't make that face."

Yara made the face anyway. He deserved it. Then she went back to raking, because the salt line did not care for faces either.

The Ravensworth Valley sat in a cup of green hills, and around its rim, like the rim of a bowl, ran the salt. Not a wall. Not a fence. A line of white crystals, raked and renewed, blessed and re-blessed, laid down by the Saltbound Guild two hundred years ago and maintained ever since. Inside the line: the valley. Farms, orchards, the millpond, the village of Hollowmere with its crooked bell tower, and the guildhall, which had once housed forty salt-sworn mages and now housed an old man, a girl, and a cook named Marta who claimed she'd never sworn anything in her life and didn't intend to start.

Outside the line: the world. Which was to say, magic.

That was the whole of the covenant, as Aldous taught it. Magic lived everywhere in the world except here. The salt kept it out. No spell could cross the line, no enchantment, no curse, no blessing, no whisper of the wild old powers that had once — according to the guild's own histories, which Yara had read so many times the pages went soft in her hands — torn the realm apart in the Age of Sundering. The valley was a clean room. The guild were its keepers.

And the keepers' first and greatest law, the one Aldous recited every morning with his porridge like a grace before meals, was this: *What is kept out must not be let in. The guild works no magic within the line.*

Which meant, in practice, that Yara Ravensworth, last apprentice of the Saltbound Guild, had spent her entire life within arm's reach of wonder and was forbidden to touch it.

She raked. The salt hissed under the tines. Somewhere up the slope a thrush was singing the same four notes over and over, either practicing or mocking her.

"You're sulking," Aldous observed.

"I'm working."

"You're working *and* sulking. You've always been able to do both at once. It's your one true talent." He closed the ledger and looked out at the valley, and for a moment his face went somewhere she couldn't follow — down, she thought. Down and back. "When I was your age, I sulked too. Thought the covenant was a cage. Thought the guild was a museum pretending to be a fortress."

"And now?"

"Now I know it's a museum pretending to be a fortress," he said, "and the museum is closing." He said it lightly, the way he said all the worst things, and Yara had learned not to flinch at it, mostly. "Four hundred years ago there were guildhalls in every province. Salt lines around every city. We were the reason the kingdoms slept soundly. Now there's one valley, one line, one hall, and one apprentice who rakes crooked on purpose."

"I don't do it on *purpose*—"

"You do everything on purpose, Yara. That's the trouble with you." He pushed himself up from the stool, one hand on his bad knee, one on his worse knee, and stood swaying at the line's edge like a fence post that had reconsidered its profession. "Come on. The eastern reach wants checking before the weather turns. I can smell it."

"You can't smell weather. Your nose gave up years ago."

"My nose is the last honest part of me. It smells weather. Come."

* * *

They walked the eastern reach together, slowly, because Aldous walked the way ice melted. The valley spread below them in its summer greens — barley going gold at the edges, the millpond flat and bright, Hollowmere's chimneys putting up their little grey flags of breakfast smoke. It was, Yara thought for perhaps the ten-thousandth time, the most beautiful place in the world, and the only place in the world she had ever been.

That was the thing she couldn't say out loud. That she loved it and it was not enough. That she had memorized every page of the guild's histories and every one of them described a world beyond the salt, and she had read those descriptions the way other people read love letters.

"The weather's coming from the north," Aldous said, squinting at a horizon that was still, to Yara's eye, perfectly innocent blue. "A bad one. We'll want the storehouse sealed."

"You always say a bad one's coming."

"And I'm always right eventually. That's the advantage of patience." He stopped at the boundary stone — the eastern marker, a squat pillar carved with the guild's sigil, a circle within a circle, worn nearly smooth by two centuries of thumbs. He rested his hand on it. "Do you know what this stone is for?"

"It marks the line."

"It marks the line. And what's the line for?"

"Keeping magic out."

"Wrong." He said it mildly, but Yara felt it land like a dropped stone in a well. "The line keeps magic *in its place*, which is a different thing entirely. Magic isn't a rat at the door, girl. It's a sea against a seawall. The line doesn't hate the sea. It just remembers what the sea did." He took his hand off the stone and looked at it, at the salt dust on his palm. "Remembering is the whole job. Everything else is raking."

"You've never told me what the sea did. Not properly. The histories go vague right at the Sundering. *There was a great wrong, and the guild made it right* — and then two hundred pages about rake maintenance."

"Rake maintenance is important."

"*Aldous.*"

He looked at her for a long moment. The wind had come up from the north, just a little, just enough to move his white hair around, and something in his face did the thing it did sometimes — the thing where he seemed to be deciding, with great care, what she was and wasn't ready to carry.

"When you're ready," he said finally, "you'll already know. That's how the important things work. They don't arrive. They're uncovered." He turned back down the slope. "Come along. Seal the storehouse. And Yara — if the storm's as bad as my nose says, keep the hall doors barred tonight and don't open them for anyone."

"Anyone?"

"Anyone at all."

* * *

The storm came at dusk, and it was as bad as the nose said.

Yara had lived through a hundred valley storms and knew their manners. This one had none. It came down the northern hills like something chased, slamming rain sideways, and the lightning didn't flash so much as *lean*, long and low and wrong-colored, a greenish white she'd never seen in her life. The old oak by the well groaned. Marta lit every candle in the guildhall and muttered that she'd sworn off swearing and this weather was testing the vow.

Aldous sat by the great hearth with his ledger on his knees and did not read it. He watched the windows. He had watched them since sundown, and when Yara asked what he was watching for, he said, "Nothing," in a voice that meant everything.

The knock came at the ninth bell.

Three small knocks. Barely audible under the wind. Aldous's head came up like a hound's.

"Don't," he said.

"It's a child," Yara said, because she could hear it — could hear, under the knocks, a thin sound that might have been crying or might have been the wind finding a crack. "Master, it's a *child* out in that."

"The covenant says—"

"The covenant doesn't say let children drown in a storm. You told me yourself, the line keeps magic out. It doesn't keep mercy in." She was already moving, already unbarbing the door, and some distant, sensible part of her noted that Aldous did not actually rise to stop her, and filed that away the way she filed away everything he didn't say.

She hauled the door open against the wind and the storm fell into the hall — rain and leaves and one small, soaked, shaking shape that tumbled across the threshold and lay still on the flagstones.

It was a boy, seven years old at most, dark-haired, brown-skinned, dressed in clothes that had been fine once and were ruined now, salt-stained and torn as if he'd come through a briar or a shipwreck. He was shivering so hard his teeth clicked. And when Yara knelt and gathered him up — he was light, terribly light, light as a bundle of dried reeds — he looked up at her with eyes so dark they were nearly black, and his mouth moved.

No sound came out.

"He's mute," she called back over the storm. "Master, he can't speak—"

"Bar the door," Aldous said. His voice had gone very strange. "Bar the door and bring him to the fire, and Yara — do not let him touch the salt stores."

* * *

Marta worked on the boy with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had raised nine children and buried two, wrapping him in blankets, pressing a hot brick against his feet, spooning barley broth into him when he was too tired to lift the bowl himself. He ate like something starved and half-wild, guarding the bowl with one hand even as he let her feed him. He never made a sound. Not a whimper, not a cough, though his chest rattled when he breathed.

Yara sat across from him and watched, and the watching made her chest hurt. There was something in the way he looked at the fire — not the way a child looks at a fire, with hunger for its warmth, but the way a traveler looks at a shore. Like something remembered. Like something *missed*.

"Where did he come from?" she asked quietly. "There's no one on the north road. There's no one *anywhere* on the roads past the line. The valley's the only settlement for thirty miles."

"I know," Aldous said.

"So he came from outside. From beyond the salt." She heard her own voice go careful. "Master, nothing crosses the line. Nothing *can*. That's the whole — that's the first law of everything."

"I know what the first law is. I've been reciting it since before your mother was born." Aldous leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied the boy with an expression Yara had never seen on him. It took her a moment to name it, because she'd never had cause. It was fear.

The boy finished the broth. He looked up at Yara again, and then — slowly, deliberately, the way a much older person might — he held out one small hand, palm up, toward her.

"What does he want?"

"I don't know," Aldous said. "Don't—"

But Yara had already taken the boy's hand. It was cold, even now, even by the fire. Cold as the eastern boundary stone at dawn.

And the boy's mouth moved again, and this time — this time there was sound. Not a voice, not exactly. Something older than a voice, something that came up out of him like water coming up out of a deep well, and the sound was words in a language Yara had never heard and yet understood the way you understand a lullaby, without translation, straight into the blood.

The fire guttered. The storm outside went suddenly, impossibly quiet, as if the whole sky were leaning in to listen.

And the salt.

The salt in the open stores along the back wall of the hall — a hundredweight of it, coarse and grey and dead as any salt — began to sing.

It was not a loud sound. It was barely a sound at all. It was the sound a wet finger makes on a glass rim, multiplied by a thousand, rising out of every crystal in every barrel, a high clear shimmering note that went through Yara's teeth and behind her eyes and settled somewhere under her ribs and *stayed there*, warm, like a coal.

The boy's lips moved again. The note climbed.

"Stop him," Aldous whispered. He was on his feet — Yara would swear later he had not stood unaided in eight months — with one hand braced on the mantel and the other pressed flat over his heart, and his face was grey as the salt. "*Yara, stop him, he's calling it through the line—*"

"Calling *what*? Master, what's he saying? What is that language?"

Aldous looked at the boy. He looked at the singing salt. And Yara watched two hundred years of guild certainty break across her master's face like the storm breaking over the hills.

"It's the Old Tongue," he said. "The covenant-tongue. And it hasn't been spoken inside the line since the line was drawn." He swallowed. "The salt is answering him, Yara. Two hundred years, and the salt has never once made a sound. And it's *answering him*."

The note held. The fire burned low and green. Outside, the storm had gone utterly still, the whole valley holding its breath, and Yara sat with a mute child's cold hand in hers and a coal of impossible warmth under her ribs and understood, with the certainty of the raked line and the recited law and every page of every history she had ever read, that nothing — nothing at all — was ever going to be the same.

The boy looked at her. And though no voice came, though his mouth made no more words at all, she heard him anyway, clear as the singing salt, clear as a bell rung once in an empty hall:

*You're the one I was looking for.*
