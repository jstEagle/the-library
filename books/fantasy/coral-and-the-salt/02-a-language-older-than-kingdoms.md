## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The morning after the storm, my master tried to burn the guild's records.

I want to be clear about how alarming this was, because at the time I did not fully appreciate it. Aldous Ravensworth had spent sixty years treating those records with a reverence most men reserve for scripture and saints. He dusted them on the first of every month. He turned their pages with cotton gloves so his oils wouldn't stain them. Once, when I was nine, I sneezed near the shelf of *Ledgers of the Line* and he looked at me as though I had committed a small blasphemy, which — in his private cosmology, where salt was holy and tidiness next to godliness — perhaps I had.

And now he stood in the guildhall's back room at dawn, feeding forty years of covenant records into the hearth like a man stoking a fire against a cold that lived inside him.

"Master," I said from the doorway, "the bread's rising."

"Let it."

"You never let bread do anything without supervision. You once told me yeast is a responsibility."

He didn't turn around. In the firelight his face was the color of old tallow, and his hands — those steady, patient hands that could mend a fraying wardline in the dark during a gale — were shaking.

"Aldous." I used his name deliberately. He hated it when I did that; said it made him feel like a specimen. It got his attention every time. "What are you burning?"

"The past," he said. "Ideally all of it."

* * *

Let me back up to the night before, because the night before matters.

The child had come to our door soaked to the bone and silent as the space between bells. We'd dried her — no, him, we learned later; the storm-light had fooled us both — fed him barley soup, and put him in my bed while I took the cot by the hearth. He hadn't spoken a word. Aldous had examined him for injuries, found none, and pronounced him "struck dumb by shock, poor mite, happens after floods," with the confidence of a man who had pronounced many things wrongly over the years and would pronounce several more before the week was out.

The child slept like something drowned. That was the phrase that kept coming back to me, though I didn't know why yet. Not *slept deeply*, not *slept soundly*. Slept like something drowned.

In the morning, I woke to singing.

Not the child. The salt.

Every apprentice of the Saltbound Guild learns early that salt sings — that's not poetry, it's practical instruction. A healthy wardline hums when you walk it, low and even, like a hive at rest. Ours had been humming for years in a way that said *tired* rather than *strong*, and Master and I had spent whole seasons arguing about whether that was age, neglect, or the valley itself getting old along with everything in it.

This was not that hum.

The crystals in the dish by the window — the little working-dish I kept for testing grain purity — had begun to ring. High, bright, chiming notes, one after another, like someone running a finger around the rims of glasses. And the child was sitting up in my bed, looking straight at them, moving his lips.

No sound came out of him. But the salt answered anyway.

I have tried, in the years since, to describe what that felt like to witness, and I have mostly failed. The nearest I can manage is this: imagine you've kept a dog your whole life, a good old dog, half-deaf and gray-muzzled, who has never once barked at anything more exciting than the neighbor's cart. And then one morning a stranger walks into your kitchen, and the dog stands up, ears sharp, eyes bright, ten years younger — and *bows*.

That was my salt line. Bowing.

I must have made some noise, because the child turned and looked at me, and there was nothing vacant in that look at all. There was recognition, which frightened me considerably more.

Then Aldous came through the door carrying the porridge pot, saw the singing dish, saw the child, and dropped four feet of good cast iron on the flagstones with a clang that woke every chicken within a mile of the house.

He recovered quickly. Masters are good at recovering quickly; it's most of what mastery is. He set the pot down, smiled at the child the way you smile at a horse you don't trust, and said, "Yara, dear heart, would you fetch more water?"

We had water. There was a well twelve steps from the door. But I fetched more water, because I knew an order to leave the room when I heard one, and because I wanted to know what he'd say when I was gone.

Through the window, while the bucket rope creaked, I watched him kneel by the bed and speak to the child — too low for me to catch the words. And I watched the child answer. Mouth moving. No sound. And then I watched my master go gray, gray, grayer, until he looked like something left out in frost, and I understood that whatever the child had said, it had reached him perfectly.

A mute child. Speaking clearly enough to terrify a man who had taught me everything I knew.

I carried the water in slowly and pretended I hadn't seen anything, which is its own kind of apprenticeship, and possibly the most useful skill the Saltbound Guild ever taught me.

* * *

By noon he had the records out.

I found him in the back room, sleeves rolled, hauling ledgers off the shelves with none of his usual cotton-gloved ceremony. He'd already started the fire too hot for cooking. The room smelled of resin and old vellum.

"All right," I said, leaning in the doorway with my arms crossed, because if you can't lean in your own guildhall's doorway, where can you lean. "Either we're having the world's earliest bonfire festival, or you're going to tell me what that child said to you."

"Bread," he said. "Did you ever take the bread out?"

"The bread can wait. The bread has waited all morning. The bread is patient. I am not."

He held a ledger — the oldest one, the one with the brass corners worn round, the one I'd been told since girlhood never to touch — and for a long moment he just stood there holding it, like a man deciding whether to throw a drowning stranger a rope or a stone.

"Sit down, Yara."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down anyway. Old knees like company at eye level." He lowered himself onto the bench by the hearth with a grunt, ledger still in his lap, and looked into the fire rather than at me. "What do you know about the covenant?"

"What every guild member knows. The valley sits behind a salt line. The salt line keeps the wild magic out. The covenant maintains the line. The Guild keeps the covenant." I recited it flatly, the First Lesson, the one drilled into novices before they're trusted with a broom. "Magic stays outside. We stay inside. Everyone's safe and nobody's sorry."

"Mm." He turned the ledger over in his hands. "And who wrote those terms, do you think? Who decided magic stays out?"

"The founders. The first bound ones." I frowned. "It's in the—"

"In the record you're currently watching me decide whether to burn, yes." His mouth twisted. "You've never actually read it, have you. Novices aren't permitted. Journeymen aren't permitted. Only the master reads the founding texts, and only the master reads them *alone*."

"That's always seemed a bit odd to me," I admitted. "For an organization supposedly built on stewardship."

"It has seemed odd to six generations of apprentices, and each of them became a master who decided it was fine." He laughed, but it wasn't a laugh, it was a sound wearing a laugh's coat. "That's how it works, girl. That's exactly how it works."

Outside, the child was in the yard, crouched by the rain barrel, watching the water with an attention that made me uneasy in some place below thought. I noted it and set it aside. There was a fire in my guildhall eating my inheritance, and I wanted to know why.

"Master. What did the child say to you?"

He was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"He asked," Aldous said finally, "*why the anchor sleeps.*"

The words meant nothing. I want to be honest about that. They went into me like a coin down a well, and I heard no bottom strike. "Anchor?"

"The covenant needs maintaining. You know that much. What the lessons don't say — what the lessons have never said — is that the line isn't merely a wall of salt and repetition and careful bookkeeping." His thumb moved along the ledger's spine, back and forth, back and forth. "A covenant of that size wants a keel. A weight at the center of it. Something living, tied to the terms, holding the whole arrangement steady from the inside. The old texts call it the anchor."

"All right," I said slowly. "So there's an anchor somewhere. A person. Bound to the line."

"Yes."

"Bound how?"

Another silence. This one longer. Somewhere in it I began, very quietly, in the part of myself that notices things before I do, to feel cold.

"The child spoke in the Old Tongue," Aldous said, instead of answering. "Not valley dialect. Not high speech. The Tongue. The language the covenant was sworn in, before there were kingdoms with names we'd recognize. I haven't heard it spoken aloud since I was younger than you. And a child of eight winters carries it in his mouth like milk."

"And that's why you're burning forty years of records."

"That's why I'm burning forty years of records," he agreed. "Because if he speaks the Tongue, then he came from *outside the line*, Yara. From beyond it. Nothing crosses the salt. That is the entire architecture of our lives. And yet here he is, in my kitchen, eating my porridge, asking questions in the language of the founding — and the salt *answered* him. Did you see it? Of course you saw it. The line knows its own tongue when it hears it." He looked up at last, and his eyes were terrible and tired and old. "Which means either the covenant is failing—"

"Or it was never what you told me it was," I finished.

He flinched. Actually flinched, my unshakeable master, the man who once talked a flash flood into changing its mind through sheer stubbornness and correct drainage. He flinched like I'd struck him, and I knew, in the way you know things when the ground starts shifting under a building you love, that I had said something true.

"Aldous," I said, gentler now, hating the gentleness because gentleness meant pity and pity meant loss, "what's in the book?"

Instead of answering, he opened it. And then, before I could lean close enough to read a single word, he stood, walked two steps, and fed it to the fire.

I shouted. I'm not proud of it; I lunged, and he caught my wrist with a grip that sixty-year-old hands had no business possessing, and we stood there grappling over a hearth like children, while page after page curled and blackened and lifted in sparks toward the chimney.

"There were names," he said, over the crackle, over my struggling. "There were terms. There was the original oath, in the founder's own hand, and the amendments, and the account of the Taking—"

"*The taking of what?*"

He let go of my wrist. The ledger was half gone now, the brass corners glowing dull orange among the coals. He watched it burn the way a man watches a funeral he'd postponed too long.

"I don't know," he whispered. "That's the truth, girl, the whole shabby truth. No master has known for two hundred years. Each of us inherited the duty and the shame and a list of things we were forbidden to read, and each of us burned what we couldn't bear to pass on. I burned my own master's additions when I took the office. He burned his. It goes back and back, a chain of fires, each link trying to forget the one before it." His voice cracked. "All I know is that the covenant was not offered. It was *taken*. And something was stolen to hold it. And the anchor—" 

He stopped.

We both stopped. Because from the yard outside came a sound neither of us had ever heard before: the salt line, the great wardline that ringed the valley, the tireless low hum we'd both walked ten thousand times —

It was singing. Loud enough to hear through walls.

* * *

We found the child kneeling in the dooryard mud, and before him, spread out in spilled salt — he must have gotten into the curing shed; I made a mental note to bar the door, and then, immediately, a larger note to stop thinking about curing sheds forever — lay a drawing.

I knelt down beside him. The grains were fine and white against the dark earth, and he had worked fast, the way children work when the picture is burning a hole in them: towers, hundreds of towers, slender and crooked as coral branches, rising out of shapes that could only be waves. Streets between the towers, curved streets, streets that branched like veins. At the center, a shape I couldn't parse at first, until I tilted my head and realized it was a throne — or something throne-like, grown rather than carved, all spire and bloom — and around the throne, tiny figures, dozens of them, arranged in rings.

And beneath the whole city, pressing up from underneath, he had drawn a darkness. A single sweeping stroke of emptiness where the salt simply wasn't, as if the city floated on the back of something vast that had begun, very slowly, to rise.

"That's not anywhere," Aldous said hoarsely behind me. "There's no city like that. There's no city that could be like that. Cities drown, yes, but they don't grow in spirals, they don't—"

"Master."

"—and it couldn't survive underwater, the pressure alone—"

"*Master.*" I touched the drawing's edge, carefully, the way you touch a sleeping animal. The salt crystals there were warm. Not sun-warm. Warm like a hand. "Look at the sky he drew above it."

Above the towers, instead of clouds, the child had pressed salt into a pattern of lines radiating outward — and I knew that pattern. Every guild member alive knew that pattern. It was the signature of a wardline seen from beneath. Seen from *inside*. From underneath the protection, looking up.

The child had drawn the view from below the salt.

He looked up at me then. Mud on his knees, storm-bruise yellowing on his cheekbone, hair full of grit — and absolutely calm. Calm the way deep water is calm. He raised one small hand and pointed at the drowned city. Then at the dark thing beneath it. Then, with terrible precision, at me.

And his lips moved, shaping three slow syllables of the language I didn't know, in the silence I was beginning to understand was not silence at all — because all around us, ringing the whole yard, the salt sang back.

Aldous made a sound I had never heard him make, a small broken note, and sat down hard in the mud like a puppet with its strings cut.

"Anchor," he said. "Oh. Oh, Yara. Oh, child. *The anchor sleeps.*" He was staring at me, at my face, with an expression I will carry to my grave — grief and guilt and something that might, horribly, have been recognition. "It was never a person somewhere else. It was never someone we lost. The Taking — the theft — the thing the guild stole to hold the covenant—"

Behind him, the child folded his small muddy hands in his lap and waited, patient as tide, for my master to finish the sentence.

Aldous couldn't. He looked at the burning guildhall behind us, at the smoke of two hundred years rising straight into the washed blue post-storm sky, and he wept.

I stood in the dooryard with salt warming under my fingertips, listening to a language older than kingdoms sing through the ground beneath my feet, and understood — in the way you understand a floor is giving way, all at once and far too late — that everything I had ever trusted was standing on something buried.

And the something buried had my face.
