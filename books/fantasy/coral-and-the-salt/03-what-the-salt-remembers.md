## 3. What the Salt Remembers

*The boy cannot say it. So he draws it.*

* * *

Tam draws the water first.

He draws it in gray, because that is what the charcoal stick gives him when he presses too hard, and because the water he remembers was gray — not blue like Yara's storybook seas, not green like the pond behind the guildhall where the ducks argue all summer. Gray like a door closed on a room full of light.

The water comes up to the windows. The water comes up to the doors. The water comes up over the towers with their little flags, which he draws as triangles because triangles are easier than flags and the truth of them is the same: they were there, and then they were under.

He sits on the cold hearthstone with the salt spilled out in front of him in a wide white sheet, and he works until his fingers are gray to the wrist.

Yara watches him from the doorway. He can feel her watching, the way you feel a warm stone at your back. She doesn't ask questions anymore. That is one of the things Tam loves about her, though he would not know to call it love. She asks fewer questions than anyone he has met since the water, and she gives bread without being asked twice.

He draws the streets. They curve wrong, because he is drawing them from underneath, from below, the way a fish would remember a city if a fish could be sorry.

* * *

Here is what happened, in the order that Tam keeps it folded inside himself:

First there was a mother with hands like moths, fluttering at his collar, at his hair, saying words that were not valley words. Old words. Words that went down into the ground when you said them, like seeds.

Then there was the sea coming up the road, which everyone said could not happen, because the sea had been pushed back a hundred years ago by people who wore salt-white robes and spoke promises in the Old Tongue, and promises made in that language were supposed to hold like stone holds.

Then there was the water in the house, cold and sudden as an answer.

Then there was going down.

This is the part Tam does not draw well. He has tried eleven times. On the slate, on the salt, once on the wall of the woodshed when he thought nobody was looking (Yara looked; Yara said nothing; she only washed it off before her master came home, and that, too, is a kind of love).

Going down was not like falling. Falling is fast and has an end. Going down was slow and had no end that Tam found. The city sank around him — or he sank around the city, he has never decided which way round it goes — and everything got quieter in layers. First the shouting went quiet. Then the bells. Then his own heart, almost, except hearts are stubborn.

And in the middle of all that quiet, there was light.

Not sunlight. Sunlight does not live that far down. This light came from the coral itself, from the throne at the center of the drowned square, which glowed the way embers glow when you blow on them, softly, reluctantly, like something that wanted very much to sleep but could not, because of what it remembered.

He drew the throne this morning while Yara fed the goats.

It took up nearly the whole sheet of salt. A chair made of branching red-and-pink-and-bone-white, grown not built, its arms curling like ferns unfurling, its back rising into spires. And on it — he hesitated here, his gray stick hovering — a figure. He drew the figure small, hunched, with long hands hanging between its knees, the way old men sit when they are waiting for someone who is late by a hundred years.

Because that is what the figure was doing. Tam knew it the way you know a smell. Waiting. Grieving. Keeping a seat warm for someone who should have come home and did not.

* * *

The strange thing — and Tam is aware, in his wordless seven-year-old way, that it is strange — is that the drowning is not the worst memory.

The worst memory is the hands.

After the throne and the quiet, after however long a child sinks in a sunken city, there were hands under his arms. Big hands, barnacled at the knuckles, gentle the way a current is gentle when it decides to carry you instead of break you. And a voice, not in his ears but in his bones, in the Old Tongue, saying:

*Go up. Go and knock. Tell them we are still waiting.*

So the hands carried him up through the gray layers, past the bells gone quiet, past the shouting, up and up until the water set him down on dry stones far inland, in a river shallows smelling of reeds, and let go of him the way you let go of a letter you have finally managed to post.

He was the letter.

That is the part that sits heaviest. Not the drowning — the drowning was done to everybody, and grief shared is a lighter load, even for one so small. But the sending. He was chosen, or volunteered, or offered — he does not know which word fits, and none of the pictures do either — and he was carried up and set on the shore and pointed at the mountains, at the valley behind the mountains, at the salt line glowing faint and white in the dark like a scar that never healed.

*Knock*, said the voice in his bones.

*Tell them we are still waiting.*

* * *

He knocked.

Nobody answered, because the door was not a door, and the people inside had spent two hundred years pretending the house next door was empty.

Tam walked for days. He ate things a goat would refuse. His voice went away somewhere around the third day — not taken, just left behind, the way you leave wet footprints that dry. Perhaps the voice stayed in the water with the ones who sent him, so they would know their message had been delivered even if the messenger arrived broken.

Or perhaps — and this thought visits him at night, in the loft above the hearth, when the house creaks its old wooden thoughts — perhaps the voice was taken. Perhaps there are people whose whole work is taking voices, and doors that open inward only, and a line of salt that a letter must not cross.

He fell asleep in the bracken. He woke to snow. He walked some more. And then there was a door with real lamplight under it, and a girl opening it, and bread.

The bread matters. He will draw the bread someday, when his hands are steadier. Round, dark-crusted, torn warm, given before any question was asked. In the city under the water they taught him — no, they didn't teach him, he simply knew, the way children know — that the world outside had forgotten how to give things before asking. The world outside asked first. What is your name. Whose child are you. Who sent you. What do you want.

Yara gave first. Asked after.

If kingdoms were founded on that, the sea could go home.

* * *

On the fourth night under Aldous's roof, Tam wakes because the salt is singing.

Not loudly. If he were a grown man he might sleep through it. But children hear the frequencies adults lose, the high thin thread of things, and Tam hears the salt crystals along the window ledge humming to each other like crickets comparing notes.

He climbs down from the loft. The hearth has burned to orange eyes. He pads to the window and puts one finger against the salt crust, and the humming jumps to his finger, up his arm, settles in his chest like a second heartbeat — a slower one, older, patient as tide.

And the salt line — he can see it from here, the pale ribbon running across the valley floor, the boundary Aldous tends every morning like a man mending a fence that hates him — flickers.

Once. Twice.

Like a lamp guttering. Like an eye opening.

Tam snatches his hand back. The humming stops. Outside, the wind moves through the pines, and the salt line lies still and white and ordinary again, and the valley pretends nothing happened, the way valleys do.

But Tam knows what a flicker means. He saw the throne flicker too, down in the quiet, each time the Regent spoke. Flickering is what old lights do when they are being remembered after a long time in the dark.

Something in the valley has been asleep for two hundred years.

His knocking is waking it up.

* * *

In the morning, Aldous burns paper.

Tam watches from the woodpile, where he is supposed to be stacking logs and is instead arranging them into the shape of a city with a drowned quarter. The old man carries an armload of ledgers out to the yard — leather-bound, corner-worn, heavy with years — and feeds them to the burn barrel one by one. The smoke comes up black, then gray, then smells of ink and secrets.

Aldous's face while he does it is the face of the seated figure on the coral throne. Tam recognizes it instantly, the way you recognize a song's melody in another key. Shame keeping a seat warm. Grief waiting for someone who is late.

When the last ledger curls and dies, Aldous stands at the barrel a long moment, an old man watching two hundred years become ash, and then he coughs — the deep wet cough that has been getting worse all winter, the one Yara pretends not to count — and goes inside, and does not meet anyone's eyes for the rest of the day.

Tam adds a small burning barrel to his log-city. Some stories need no charcoal.

* * *

But here is the thing about drawings: they are patient, and secrets are not.

That evening Yara spreads fresh salt on the hearthstone for Tam's drawing, the way she does now, every evening, having decided without discussion that this is simply what evenings contain. And Tam, warmed by stew and by the particular safety of a house where someone expects your picture, draws bigger than he has ever drawn.

He draws the water. He draws the streets curving wrong. He draws the throne, and the small hunched figure upon it with its long patient hands, and — because tonight his chest still hums faintly from the singing salt, and because the picture seems to draw itself, his gray stick moving like something wading through him —

he draws the hands that pulled him up.

And beneath the hands, because a letter should state its message plainly, he writes. Or rather, he sets down the letters the Old Tongue uses, the seed-words that go into the ground when spoken, spelling out the sentence that has lived in his bones since the shallows:

*WE ARE STILL WAITING.*

The salt sings.

All of it — not just the window ledge now, but the hearthstone sheet, the crust along the sill, and beyond the walls, faint but unmistakable, the long white line across the valley, flickering like a lamp remembering fire.

Yara goes very still. Stew drips off her spoon back into the bowl, unremarked.

Aldous rises from his chair so fast it falls behind him, and he looks at the drawing — at the throne, at the hands, at the seed-letters shining faintly where they sit in the salt — and Tam watches the old man's face do something terrible and simple: stop pretending.

"Where," Aldous says, and his voice cracks like ice on a spring pond, "did you learn that word?"

Tam points at his own chest. Then, because it is true and because he is tired of carrying it alone, he points at the floor — at the ground under the house, under the valley, under the salt line, down and down through the gray layers to the quiet place with the glowing throne.

Down where somebody has been waiting two hundred years.

Aldous puts his hand over his mouth. Above his fingers, his eyes go to the window, to the flickering line, and Tam sees him understand — really understand, perhaps for the first time in a life of half-understanding — that the fence he has tended all these mornings was never keeping something out.

It was keeping something quiet.

Outside, the salt line flickers again — longer this time, three heartbeats, four — and far away, at the valley's edge, a sound begins that no living person in that valley has ever heard: a low, rising tone, like a bell underwater, like a door that has waited two centuries beginning, at last, to open.

Aldous whispers something in the Old Tongue. It sounds like an apology.

Tam picks up another piece of charcoal. There is so much left to tell.
