## 4. The Master's Confession

Aldous Ravensworth had spent seventy-one years perfecting the art of looking calm, and he was doing it now from a bed he suspected he would not leave.

The trick, he had learned as a young warden, was in the hands. A man could have fear in his eyes and no one would notice, so long as his hands stayed still. So Aldous kept his hands folded on the blanket like a man napping in a garden chair, and watched Yara move about the sickroom pretending she wasn't watching him back.

She was terrible at pretending. She always had been. She stirred the fever-broth with the particular violence of someone who wanted to stir something else entirely — preferably him, until he confessed whatever it was that made him flinch every time Tam hummed those strange syllables under his breath.

The child sat on the floor by the window, drawing in spilled salt again. He'd been at it for an hour. Aldous could see the shapes from where he lay: towers, curved and branching, like antlers grown by a city instead of a stag. And beneath them, always, the same wide sweep of water.

Every time Tam finished, he swept it away with his palm and started over. As if the picture were a wound he kept reopening to check whether it had healed.

It hadn't. Aldous knew, because he recognized the gesture. He'd been making the same one for sixty years, in ledgers and letters and the careful blank spaces of guild reports.

"Yara," he said, and his voice came out rougher than intended, "put down the broth. It's fighting back."

She turned. Her face did the thing it always did when she thought he was joking — the quick flicker of hope, immediately suspicious. She was her mother's daughter in that, gods rest Mirabel, who had spent thirty years married to a guildsman and never once believed a word he said before breakfast.

"You should eat," Yara said.

"I should confess," Aldous said. "But we can negotiate."

* * *

He had rehearsed this speech for three days, ever since the fever settled into his chest like a tenant who'd signed a lease. In rehearsal it had been eloquent. He had imagined himself rising from the bed, standing at the window, delivering the truth of the Saltbound Guild in the ringing tones of the oath-ceremonies of his youth.

What actually happened was that Yara dragged a stool to the bedside, sat down, crossed her arms, and said, "Start with why you burned the records."

So much for eloquence.

"I burned them because they were true," Aldous said.

"That's usually the reason people *keep* records."

"People keep records of harvests, dear. Of rainfall. Of how many wardens fell off the north path drunk." He coughed, and hated how long the cough took to release him. When he could speak again, the room had gone very quiet. Even Tam had stopped drawing. The boy was watching him with those dark, patient eyes, the way you'd watch a door you expected something to come through.

"The Saltbound Guild was founded to protect a covenant," Aldous said. "That part is real. I want you to hear that first, because everything after it is going to make you want to throw the first part away, and you shouldn't. There *is* a covenant. There is a line between the valley and the world, and it holds back more than weather. Your whole life, you've been taught that magic cannot cross the salt. That's true. What you were never taught is *why*."

Yara didn't move. But he saw her hands tighten on her knees, and he thought: there it is. The same stillness her grandmother used to get, right before she broke a rule.

"Magic doesn't refuse to cross the salt line," Aldous said. "Magic is *kept* from crossing it. By agreement. By terms set down long before any kingdom you've learned the name of. And the covenant has an anchor — a living one. A person, bound into the terms, holding the balance from inside. Without the anchor, the covenant frays. With the anchor, it holds — but only if the terms are honored."

"And are they?" Yara asked quietly.

"No," said Aldous. "They haven't been for two hundred years. That's what I burned."

* * *

He told it badly, and he knew it, and he kept talking anyway because stopping would be worse.

The original covenant, he said, had been a sharing. Magic belonged to the land and to everyone on it, the way rain belongs to everyone — and the salt line wasn't a wall but a *treaty*, drawn so that the valley could live quiet and unclaimed while the wider world lived loud. One person in each generation served as the anchor: not a prisoner, not a sacrifice, but a keeper, chosen by consent, sworn by consent, free to walk away if the weight grew too heavy. That was the old way. The way it was meant to be.

"And then?" Yara asked. Her voice was very controlled. Aldous had heard that control in young wardens right before they did something permanent.

"Then the guild got frightened," he said. "Or greedy — the records disagree, and I suspect the truth was both, because fear and greed are twins and always have been. Two hundred years ago, the anchor of that generation refused to renew her term. She wanted to step down. She had that right. And the guildmasters of the time — my predecessors, my teachers' teachers — could not imagine a world where magic passed out of their keeping. So they took her. Bound her without consent. Rewrote the terms so the anchor's binding passed down through blood instead of choice."

He paused. The words tasted like the medicine Yara kept forcing on him: bitter, necessary, and too late to do any good.

"They stole the covenant," he said. "And then they hid the theft so well that within four generations even the thieves' descendants believed they were guardians. That's the trick of it, Yara. You don't need to fool the world forever. You only need to fool your own children, and let them do the rest."

Tam made a small sound. Not a word — the boy hadn't spoken since he arrived, and Aldous had begun to suspect he couldn't, or wouldn't, or was saving it up the way children save sweets. But he had risen from his salt-drawing and come to stand at the foot of the bed, and he was looking at Aldous with an expression no child should have to wear: recognition. Like Aldous was a page out of one of his drawings.

"What happened to her?" Yara asked. "The anchor they took. The woman who wanted to leave."

"She died bound," Aldous said. "And here is the part I have read forty times and never once understood until this week." He lifted one trembling hand — he let it tremble now; the performance of calm was over — and pointed at Tam. "The binding passes through blood. Through *her* blood. Generation to generation, whether anyone knows it or not. The guild never chose another anchor because they never had to. They already had one. They just didn't tell her."

The silence went on so long that Aldous could hear the salt crystals ticking against each other in the draft from the window, tiny and bright, like distant applause or distant teeth.

"Say it," Yara whispered. "Whatever you're not saying. Say it."

"You know what I'm not saying."

"Say it anyway. If you make me guess, I'll guess wrong on purpose, and then we'll both have to live with that."

Despite everything — the fever, the shame sitting on his chest like a wet cloak — Aldous almost smiled. She'd inherited that from him, actually. The weaponized stubbornness. He'd been proud of it for twenty years and now it was going to be the death of what little peace he had left.

"Your mother's line," he said. "Mirabel's mother's mother. All the way back. You're not just the last apprentice of the Saltbound Guild, Yara Ravensworth. You're the anchor. You've been the anchor since the day you were born, and the covenant has been holding *through you* your entire life, without your knowledge, without your consent — which means the guild committed its crime all over again the moment you drew breath. We didn't stop stealing. We just got quieter about it."

* * *

Yara stood up. He braced for shouting — he'd earned shouting — but she didn't shout. She walked to the window, looked out at the salt line gleaming white across the valley floor, and stood there with her back to him for the better part of a minute.

When she turned around, her eyes were dry and furious, which was far worse than tears.

"How long have you known?"

"Since you were nine years old."

"*Nine?*"

"There was a test. A drop of your blood in the salt at midwinter. It sang. I told myself I'd tell you when you were older. Then I told myself I'd tell you when you were trained enough to understand. Then I told myself the guild would reform itself, or the matter would resolve, or—" He stopped. "There is no good version of this, Yara. Every year I waited, I told myself a story, and the stories got thinner, and I kept telling them anyway. That's the inheritance I gave you. Not the binding. The *lying*. I'm sorry. It's the only apology I have and it isn't worth the salt on your floor."

Tam climbed onto the edge of the bed. Aldous hadn't seen him cross the room. The boy moved like that sometimes — silently, suddenly, as though the space between two places were optional. He sat down beside Aldous's legs and studied him with grave attention, and then he reached out and pressed one small hand flat against the blanket over Aldous's knee.

And the salt on the windowsill began to sing.

It was faint — a high, thin chime, like ice settling on a pond — but Aldous felt it in his teeth, in his chest, in the scar tissue of a life spent keeping magic at arm's length. The sound rose and fell in patterns. Patterns with grammar. The Old Tongue, sung by salt, translated by a mute child's touch.

"He's saying something," Yara breathed. "Can you—"

"Yes." Aldous closed his eyes. "I can. Gods help me, I can."

It was a plea. He'd known it would be, from the moment the storm brought the boy to their door, but knowing and hearing were different animals. The covenant was failing. The stolen terms were collapsing under two centuries of theft, and the realm beyond the salt — the drowned court, the old powers, whatever remained of the world the guild had locked out — was asking for repair. Not demanding. Asking. They had sent a child, unarmed and voiceless, to knock on the door of the people who had robbed them, and ask politely.

The shame of it went through Aldous like cold water through a cracked jug.

"They sent him as a plea," he said aloud. "For repair. And we nearly turned him away at the gate because he didn't have papers."

"Aldous." Yara's voice cracked on his name. "What do I do?"

"Honestly? I don't know. I've spent my whole life maintaining a lie; I'm poorly qualified to advise on undoing one." He fumbled beneath his pillow, and his fingers found what they'd been searching for since the fever came: a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the grey cord of a guildmaster's rank. He'd carried it for sixty years and never once opened it in daylight. "But I know what you'll need. Or rather, I know what I was given and forbidden to use, which is possibly the most guild sentence I've ever spoken."

He unwrapped it on the blanket.

The key was broken — had been broken, he understood now, deliberately, long ago, so that no single hand could wield it whole. Half of it was coral, bleached white as winter bone, grown into a shape too intricate for any human craft. The other half was actual bone, old and yellowed, carved with script that predated every alphabet in the guild archives. Where the two halves met, the break was clean, precise, patient — the work of someone who intended, someday, for the pieces to find each other again.

"It's the key to the drowned court," Aldous said. "To the Coral Throne. My master gave it to me when I took my oaths, along with instructions I was too young and too obedient to question: *keep it, hide it, never seek the lock.* Sixty years I've kept it. Sixty years I told myself that obedience was honor." His laugh came out wet. "Turns out it was just theft with better posture."

Yara stared at the key. "You're giving me this?"

"I'm giving you a choice, which is more than this guild ever gave anyone. Take it, don't take it, throw it in the river — but decide with open eyes, which is the one thing no one in our line has done for two hundred years." He pushed the bundle toward her. "If you go — and I think you will, because you have your grandmother's chin and your mother's refusal to believe me — you'll need to reach the sea. Follow the old sunken roads east. Trust no one wearing grey. And take the boy."

Tam's hand was still resting on his knee. Aldous covered it with his own, carefully, the way you'd hold something that might sing.

"And Aldous," Yara said. "You're coming with us."

He looked at her. Seventy-one years old, lungs full of water that wasn't water, and this girl — this magnificent, impossible girl — was planning logistics.

"Dear heart," he said gently, "I am dying."

"You've been dying for three days and you've complained about the broth the entire time. You can die on the road like a person with somewhere to be, or you can die here while I'm gone wondering if your last act was handing me a broken key and a guilt trip. Which would you prefer?"

Aldous considered. Outside, the light was going gold over the salt line, and somewhere below, Tam's abandoned drawing was dissolving grain by grain in the draft — the drowned city returning to nothing, waiting to be drawn again.

"You know," he said, "your grandmother once threatened me with almost exactly that argument."

"Did it work?"

"It worked *beautifully*. It's how I ended up guildmaster. Everyone in this family gets their way by being unbearable, and I include myself." He closed his hand around the coral half of the key and pressed it into hers. Her fingers closed over it, and the salt on the sill sang one clear, sweet note — like a door remembering it could open.

"Rest tonight," Aldous said. "Bury me in the morning if the fever wins, and if it doesn't, bury me later, but either way—" He stopped, because the next words were the ones he'd been avoiding for three days, the ones underneath all the others.

"Either way," Yara finished softly, "we cross the line."

"We cross the line." He lay back against the pillows, exhausted, lighter than he had been in sixty years, which he supposed was what happened when you finally put down something you'd been carrying. "And Yara — when you meet whatever waits at the end of that road, and it asks you what the guild did — don't soften it. Don't tidy it up for the telling. Tell them the truth, ugly side first."

"Why?"

"Because," said Aldous Ravensworth, last honest liar of the Saltbound Guild, "it's the only inheritance worth passing down."

* * *

He woke once in the deep of night, fever-bright, and for a moment he didn't know where he was — only that the room smelled of salt and tallow, and that someone was humming.

Tam sat in the corner, knees drawn up, singing without a voice — Aldous could feel the shape of the song in the air, in the faint chiming of every crystal in the house. And woven through the Old Tongue, unmistakable now that he knew what to listen for, ran a thread of something else. Not a plea this time.

A welcome.

The realm beyond the salt had felt the key wake in Yara's keeping, and it was — cautiously, hopefully, the way you'd greet a stranger who might be family — opening its arms.

Aldous smiled at the ceiling, at the dark, at the whole ruined and mending world.

"Well," he murmured, to no one, to everyone, to the child in the corner and the girl asleep in the next room with a broken key under her pillow. "I suppose we'd best pack."
