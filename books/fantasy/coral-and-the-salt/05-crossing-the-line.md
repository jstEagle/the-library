## 5. Crossing the Line

I buried my master on a morning so clear it felt like an insult.

Aldous Winterbourne had always said that funerals were for the living, and that the dead — if they had any sense at all — would rather we spent the time eating. So I dug his grave myself at the edge of the salt garden, where the crystals grew palest and the wind came through off the ridge carrying the smell of rosemary he'd planted forty years ago and refused to let anyone else tend. Tam stood beside me the whole while with a small wooden spade that was really a garden trowel, and when I paused to wipe my face, he patted the dirt flat in careful little strokes, as though helping.

"You didn't have to do that," I told him. "You could just stand there looking mournful."

He looked up at me with those grey eyes that seemed older than the rest of him and went back to patting.

That was Tam all over. He hadn't spoken a word since the night the storm brought him to our door — not since those syllables in the Old Tongue that made every crystal along the salt line ring like struck glass. But he communicated plenty. He communicated by drawing drowned cities in spilled salt. He communicated by taking Aldous's hand in his last hours, when even I couldn't bear to hold it anymore because it had gone so light, like holding a bundle of dried reeds. And now he communicated by helping me bury the last master of the Saltbound Guild, which was more than most of the guild's actual members had ever done for the man.

There were no other members left, you understand. That's what nobody outside the valley understood about us. The Saltbound Guild — keepers of the covenant, wardens of the boundary, sworn since before the kingdom of Vell had a name — consisted, at the end, of one dying old man and me. We tended eleven miles of salt line with two buckets, a copper rake, and a great deal of stubbornness.

"Yara." He'd said my name three days before the end, when the sickness had already eaten through him like rust through iron. "Listen. The line is failing. You've seen it."

"I've patched it," I'd said. "The north stretch is thin, but—"

"The line isn't the thing, girl. It never was. The line is only the *seam*." He'd gripped my wrist with what strength remained. "And there's something on the other side of the seam that has been knocking for two hundred years, and we are running out of hands to press against the door."

I'd thought it was the fever talking. I'd made him tea and told him so, gently, the way you tell a dying man anything you don't want to be true.

Then Tam had arrived, speaking words that made the salt sing, and drawn a city under the sea in our kitchen floor, and Aldous had burned forty years of records in the hearth rather than explain them, and then he died anyway, and I was left standing over his grave with a garden-trowel spade, a mute child, and a broken key of coral and bone that he'd pressed into my palm with his final lucid breath.

*Take it across*, he'd said. *Take her across.* And then, quieter, almost smiling: *Forgive me. Forgive us. We stole the door key and called ourselves its guardians.*

I filled in the last of the earth. Tam set a single white stone at the head, then considered, and added a second smaller stone beside it.

"For company?" I asked.

He nodded solemnly.

"Well. He'd have liked that. He complained about being alone for forty years and then chased off every apprentice who stayed longer than a season." I brushed the dirt from my knees. "You and I are going to do something mad now. I want you to know that going in."

Tam tilted his head.

"We're leaving." I said the word and my stomach dropped as if I'd said *we're drowning*. "Out of the valley. Over the line. I've never—" My voice did an undignified wobble, and I steadied it. "Nobody crosses the line, Tam. That's the whole point of the guild. Magic can't cross into the valley, and we can't cross out. Or we're not supposed to. Or—" I laughed, short and bitter. "Or Aldous lied to us about everything, which seems increasingly likely, doesn't it?"

He reached out and took my hand. Small fingers, salt-roughened, very certain.

"All right," I said. "All right. Let me pack first. If I'm going to commit heresy against four centuries of guild law, I'm at least going to bring bread."

* * *

Packing took an hour, mostly because I kept stopping to argue with myself.

The guildhouse held so little. A kettle. Two cups — one chipped, one not, and I took the chipped one because Aldous had always drunk from it and claimed the chip improved the flavor, which was nonsense, but it was *his* nonsense. The copper rake, because some habits don't die just because their keeper does. A coil of good rope. The last wheel of hard cheese, wrapped in cloth. Bread, still warm, because apparently I bake when I grieve; my hands needed something to knead besides the knot in my chest.

The coral key I wore on a cord around my neck. It sat cold against my sternum, colder than bone should be, and twice during packing I caught myself touching it the way you touch a bruise — to check whether it still hurt. It did.

Tam sat by the door the entire hour, perfectly patient, watching me with the gravity of someone who had crossed oceans to get here and could not fathom why I needed to deliberate over cheese.

"It's not the cheese," I told him, cinching the pack. "It's that everything I am is inside this valley. Everything. First steps, first spells, first grave. Out there—" I gestured vaguely at the door, beyond which the world presumably continued, enormous and indifferent. "Out there I'm nobody. In here I'm somebody. Small somebody. Dying-guild somebody. But somebody."

He walked to the door and opened it. Held it open. Looked back at me.

"Yes, thank you, very helpful."

He did not smile — I wasn't sure yet whether Tam smiled — but something around his eyes suggested that if he did smile, this would have been the moment.

We walked out through the salt garden one last time. I touched each bed as I passed, the way Aldous had taught me: *greet the salt, Yara, it's older than your family and it remembers things you don't.* At the gate I turned and looked back at the guildhouse — squat stone walls, sagging roof I'd been meaning to fix for three years, hearth smoke long gone cold. It looked exactly like what it was: the last outpost of something that had ended without anyone noticing.

"I'll come back," I said to it. To him. To both.

Then I took Tam's hand, and we walked north along the line toward the crossing stones.

* * *

The valley in late afternoon is the most beautiful thing I know, or knew, or knew how to know — and I want to be honest, because honesty is all I have left of the guild's teachings, and I mean to keep even that. The terraces go gold and green down the slopes. The river runs shallow and bright over its stones. Smoke rises from the far farms in straight, unhurried columns, and everyone down there believes, as I believed for twenty years, that the salt line protects them from a wildness they're better off without.

They waved at me as we passed the lower farms. Old Maren called out that she'd heard about Master Winterbourne and that she was sorry, truly, and that I should come by for stew whenever the loneliness got loud. I thanked her. I did not tell her I was about to walk out of her life forever, possibly bringing the end of the world with me. Some mercies are just cowardice wearing a kind face.

The crossing stones sit at the valley's narrowest throat, where two granite shoulders pinch the road down to a cart's width. The salt line runs between them — a band of white crystal, knee-high, grown thick and seamless over centuries, glittering like a frozen wave caught mid-break. As children we were told it was a wall. It isn't a wall. Walls have tops. This is more like a scar across the world, or a stitch holding two fabrics together that were never meant to touch.

I had walked up to it ten thousand times and never once stepped over. Guild law. Guild law, and fear, and the fact that Aldous had told me since I was six years old: *the moment magic touches you unfiltered, girl, you'll understand why we built the fence.*

"Here's what I know," I said to Tam, crouching at the line's edge. "Beyond this, magic isn't filtered. Here, the valley itself thins it out — dilutes it, like milk in water — so we can live alongside it without burning. Out there, it's full strength. Aldous said it would feel like—" I searched for the phrase. "*Like drinking lightning.* His exact words. He also once told me eels were born angry, so his credibility is compromised."

Tam put his hand on the nearest crystal. Where his fingers touched, the salt began — faintly, softly — to hum. The same note from the night he arrived. The line lit up in a slow ripple, silver-white light running away from him in both directions like a shiver down a long spine, and somewhere far to the south I heard, or imagined I heard, eleven miles of salt singing at once.

"That's not subtle," I whispered.

He looked up at me. And I swear — I will swear it until I die — he *pointed*. Past the line. *Come on,* said the pointing. *You promised.*

"I didn't promise anything."

The pointing continued.

"Fine." I laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through and came out closer to a sob, and I decided to let it be whatever it wanted to be. "Fine. On three. One—"

He stepped through before I finished counting.

The line parted around him like water around a stone, and he stood on the far side, unhurt, unhorrified, turning back to look at me with an expression I now recognize as the universal expression of children everywhere: *what's taking you so long?*

Which left me. Alone on my side of four hundred years.

My heart was going like a smith's hammer. I remember thinking, absurdly, of the chipped cup packed in my bag. Of Aldous's voice saying *drinking lightning*. Of the portrait I hadn't found yet, the confession I hadn't heard yet, all the stolen truths waiting for me on the other side of a band of white crystal. I remember thinking: if I step over this, I can never be the person who didn't. There is no version of tomorrow in which yesterday's Yara survives this.

Good, said some voice in me that I hadn't met before. Good. She was tired anyway.

I took Tam's hand.

I stepped over.

* * *

People ask — well, nobody asks, because almost nobody knows — but if they asked, I'd tell them: crossing the salt line does not feel like passing through a door. It feels like being born.

The magic hit me before my second foot landed.

It came up through the ground and down through the sky and sideways on the wind, and it was *everywhere*, and it was *loud*, and my senses — my poor valley-dulled senses, tuned for twenty years to watered milk — tore open like paper in rain. Color first. The grass on the far slope wasn't green, it was a thousand greens, each blade arguing its own case, and the light moving through them had texture, weight, intention. Sound next: I could hear roots drinking. I could hear a beetle's heartbeat in the soil beneath my boot. Somewhere east, a fox dreamed, and I *heard the dream*, and the dream was about chickens.

Then the pain. Because wonder, it turns out, is just pain that hasn't finished introducing itself.

I went down to my knees. The world roared. Every story I'd ever been told about the outer lands — and Aldous had told hundreds, mostly as bedtime warnings — was true, and none of them were true, because no story can prepare you for a world where everything is awake. The trees watched. The river remembered. The mountains overhead had opinions, slow and granite-deep, about weather and patience and the small frantic creatures scurrying through the pass below.

I was one of the small frantic creatures. I knelt in the grass shaking, and the grass curled sympathetically around my fingers, and even its sympathy was too much, too bright, too *much*.

Through the roar, small hands found my face.

Tam knelt in front of me, gripping my cheeks, and he made a sound — not a word, not yet, but a sound, low and steady, a humming note, and I realized dimly it was the same note the salt sang. He was giving me something to hold onto. An anchor-note in the flood.

I held onto it. I breathed. In. Out. In. The thousand greens settled into green. The roaring sorted itself into layers — wind, water, birdsong, the deep mountain mutter — and I knelt there in the grass of the outer world and laughed until tears came, because it was terrible, it was unbearable, it was the most alive I had ever been in my life.

"Oh," I managed. "Oh, *that's* what he meant. Drinking lightning. You absolute liar, Aldous, you should have said *drowning in daylight*."

Tam released my face, satisfied, and pointed down the road.

I followed his finger and forgot, briefly, how to breathe for an entirely new reason.

The road ran downhill through country I had no names for. Trees taller than the guildhouse, hung with moss that glowed faintly gold where the light touched it. Wildflowers in colors I'd only ever seen in dyed wool. Far off, a range of hills that moved — actually *moved*, slowly, like animals sleeping standing up. And above it all, spread across the sky like spilled ink, a flock of birds flying in a pattern too deliberate to be chance: a spiral, tightening, pointing south. Toward the sea. Toward, I understood with a chill that had nothing cold in it, the drowned city a child had drawn in my kitchen floor.

The coral key pulsed against my breastbone. Once. Twice. Like a second heart waking up.

"There you are," I murmured to it, and got to my feet on legs that felt borrowed.

Tam slipped his hand back into mine — his idea, this time, not mine — and we started down the road together, two small figures walking out of a valley that no longer had a guild, into a world that had been waiting two hundred years for someone to answer the door.

Behind us, faint and fading, I could have sworn I heard the salt line sing.

Ahead, on the road where it curved down toward the river, someone was watching us. A figure in travel-stained green, half-hidden by the mile-marker post, who had clearly seen everything — the collapse, the humming child, the laughing fool on her knees in the grass — and who did not run, and did not bow, and did not look like any law I had ever been taught to fear.

She stepped out onto the road, and I stopped walking, and that, as it turned out, was where the real journey began.
