## 6. The Road of Small Mercies

The world beyond the salt line smelled like a market that had been left out in the rain.

That was the first thing I learned about the outside, three days after burying my master under a cairn of grey stones: it was enormous, and it had opinions, mostly expressed through smell. The valley had smelled of salt and turned earth and Aldous's pipe smoke — four scents, five on washday. Out here the air was a brawl. Pine resin and river mud and something flowering sweetly enough to make my eyes water, all of it layered over a low animal funk of livestock and distant cookfires and, once, memorably, a cart of fish that had clearly lost its argument with the sun.

Tam didn't seem to mind. He walked beside me with his small hand tucked in mine and his face tipped up like the sky was telling him secrets, which, given everything, it possibly was.

I minded. I minded everything. My skin felt two sizes too small since we'd crossed the line, since magic had come pouring into me like water into a cracked jug, filling every seam I'd never known I had. The guild taught us that the valley's silence was peace. Nobody had told me that the rest of the world wasn't noise so much as *company* — a thousand small voices of growing things and flowing things, none of them using words, all of them talking anyway.

"Almost at the ford," I said, because Tam liked it when I narrated. He'd taken to tapping my wrist twice when he wanted information, three times when he wanted food, and once, solemnly, when he wanted me to stop worrying, which he did approximately forty times a day.

He tapped twice.

"Yes, the ford. And past the ford there should be a village called Marsh Hollow, if Aldous's maps weren't lying, which — " I patted the satchel where the maps lived, curled and brittle and annotated in my master's cramped hand. "Which they probably were. He drew the mountains wrong on purpose, I think, so apprentices couldn't run away."

I'd said it lightly. It landed heavily, the way true things do when you're not ready for them.

Because here was what I hadn't told Tam, walking beside him in the warm green afternoon: Aldous *had* drawn those maps wrong on purpose. I knew now. Chapter and verse, I knew it. The Saltbound Guild hadn't guarded the valley from magic; it had imprisoned it, hoarded it, built its whole quiet righteous purpose on a theft so old the thieves had forgotten they were thieves. Aldous had burned his records rather than let me read them. He'd died pressing a broken key of coral and bone into my palm and whispering *forgive us* like a man paying down a debt he knew would outlive him.

And somewhere behind us, or ahead of us, or both, were guild wardens who would very much like that key back.

Tam tugged my hand. He pointed ahead through the trees, where the land dipped toward a wide brown ribbon of river — the ford, silver-shallow over stones, exactly where the lying map promised.

And on the far bank, hunched under an awning of patched canvas strung between two alder trees, someone was selling something.

* * *

"It's not a stall," said the someone, who was broad as a door and dressed in about nine layers of mismatched clothing, several of which appeared to be curtains. "It's a *practice*. There's a difference. A stall is commerce. A practice is a public service with optional donations."

I stood at the edge of the ford with Tam half-hidden behind my skirt and took inventory. The woman — I decided she was a woman the way you decide a bear is a bear, by overall impression rather than inspection — had grey-streaked black hair tied up with what was unmistakably a wooden spoon. Her hands were stained green to the elbow. Behind her, the canvas shelter sagged over a folding table crowded with jars, bundles, a mortar and pestle worn smooth as a river stone, and one extremely confident chicken.

"What are you practicing?" I asked.

"Medicine." She said it the way other people say *heresy*, with relish. "Proper medicine. Boneset and feverfew and woundwort, and before you ask, yes, I have a license, and no, you may not see it, because it is currently a small pile of ash in a warden's brazier in Tarnwick, along with my good name and roughly eleven years of unblemished professional standing." She beamed at us. "Brannoc Vetch, hedge-witch, disgraced. You look terrible. Both of you. Come across the water and sit down."

"We haven't any money," I said, which was true, and also the least of our problems.

"Donation-optional," Brannoc repeated, waving a green-stained hand at the far bank. "Meaning optional. Meaning sit down before you fall down, girl, because I've been watching you pick your way down that hill for ten minutes and you walk like your bones are full of bees."

She wasn't wrong. That was the humiliating part. Since crossing the line, the magic in me had settled from a flood into a sort of permanent buzzing wrongness — my fingertips numb, my sleep full of colors that didn't exist, my stomach convinced at odd intervals that we were falling. I'd told myself it would pass. Masters were supposed to teach you these things. Mine was under a cairn.

I looked down at Tam. He looked up at me. Then he did something that decided it before I could: he stepped out from behind my skirt, walked straight down to the water's edge, and held out his free hand toward the chicken.

"Oh no," said Brannoc, delighted. "Oh, he likes Hildegarde. Hildegarde, mind your manners." The chicken, evidently minding her manners, permitted herself to be petted. Tam's whole face opened like a window.

We crossed the ford.

* * *

What followed was the strangest hour of my life up to that point, which given recent events was saying something.

Brannoc sat me down on a stump, took one look at my eyes — "*there* it is, that's not sickness, that's *backlog*" — and began pulling jars off her table with the brisk violence of a woman who'd been waiting years for a patient interesting enough to justify her collection. She crushed something that smelled like cold mornings into a cup of river-boiled water, pressed it into my hands, and told me to drink while she interrogated me.

"Where from?"

"A valley," I said. "South."

"Which valley?"

"The one with the salt."

Her eyebrows went up. She kept grinding, but slower. "The closed valley. The guild valley. Nobody comes *out* of there."

"Somebody has to eventually," I said. "Or nobody comes back in."

"Hm." She glanced at Tam, who was by now sitting cross-legged in the dirt having what appeared to be a serious conversation with Hildegarde, conducted entirely in gestures, which the chicken seemed to be winning. "And the little one? Brother? Son?"

"No." I hesitated. Lying suddenly felt exhausting in a way it never had inside the valley, where lies had been theoretical — there'd been nothing worth lying about except whose turn it was to scrub the saltern vats. "He arrived at our door during a storm. He doesn't speak. He speaks — well, he *spoke* a language my master recognized, and then my master panicked and burned thirty years of records, and then he died, and gave me this." I touched the satchel where the coral key rode against my hip. "And now I think people with matching cloaks are looking for us."

Brannoc had gone very still. Stillness, on her, was startling — like watching a landslide pause for thought.

"Show me the cloak," she said.

"There isn't a cloak yet. Grey and white, salt-line knotwork on the shoulder. Wardens."

"Mm. And how many days behind you?"

"I don't know that anyone's behind us at all. I'm being careful, not accurate."

"Careful is free. Accurate costs extra." She took the empty cup from my hands, looked into it as though the dregs might testify, and set it down. "Drink worked, by the way. Color's coming back into your face already. See, this is what I mean about proper medicine. Guild physicians would've charged you eight marks and diagnosed you with an excess of melancholy."

"You keep saying *guild*. Which guild?"

"Any of them, dear. They're all the same animal in different coats." She wiped her hands on a curtain-layer. "Mine was the Physickers' Compact. Eleven years apprenticed, licensed, respectable. And then the year of the marsh fevers, when the Compact's remedy was priced beyond what marsh folk earn in a season, I made the same remedy myself and gave it away." She shrugged, a mountain-range shrug. "Turns out the crime wasn't the curing. The crime was the *without permission*."

Something in my chest went tight and strange.

"That's not a crime," I said. "That's just — helping."

"That is *exactly* what I said." Brannoc pointed at me, vindicated. "At length. To the tribunal. With diagrams. And they struck me off the rolls, and here I am, practicing beside a road, donation-optional, and I'll tell you a secret, girl — I sleep better than I ever did in the Compact's fine white hall." She looked at me sideways, sharp and kind at once. "You've got that look too. The look of somebody who just found out the rules she was keeping were keeping *her*."

I didn't answer. Across the clearing, Tam sneezed, and Hildegarde appeared to take it personally on his behalf.

"About those wardens," Brannoc said, more quietly. "Grey and white, salt-knotwork. When did you leave the valley?"

"Six days ago."

"Then they're not behind you." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Guild law says nothing leaves the closed valley without escort. If a child walked out and an apprentice walked after, the wardens don't chase. They *intercept*. They'll be ahead of you, at every ford and tollhouse between here and wherever you're going, waiting for you to walk to them. Chasing is effort. Waiting is policy."

The buzzing under my skin turned briefly to ice.

"How do you know their procedures?"

"Because disgraced means I still went to the meetings, dear." She was already moving, sweeping jars into a canvas sack with the efficiency of long practice. "It also means I know what they do to unlicensed practitioners found harboring fugitives, which is why I'm coming with you as far as the crossroads, and then we're taking the mill path instead of the high road, and you're going to tell me the whole story from the beginning, starting with why a mute child makes a guild-trained girl flinch when she looks at him."

"He doesn't make me flinch."

"You flinch when you look at him. Noted for accuracy." She whistled; Hildegarde abandoned Tam mid-conversation with zero loyalty and trotted to Brannoc's heel. "Pack up, both of you. Daylight's spending itself and we've a mill to go around."

Tam looked at me. He tapped my wrist twice — *information* — and pointed at Brannoc with an expression of grave inquiry.

"She's asking herself the same thing," I translated, and got up off the stump, and discovered that whatever was in the cold-morning cup, I felt steadier than I had in six days.

* * *

The mill path ran along the river through country so green it looked freshly painted. Brannoc set a pace that suggested she'd done a great deal of walking since her disgrace, and talked while she walked, which I came to understand was simply how she was built — words like weather, constant, mostly harmless, occasionally thunderous.

"So the guild in the salt valley," she said. "Saltbound, you said. What's the trade?"

"There isn't a trade. We keep the line."

"The line being...?"

"A boundary of salt around the whole valley. Magic can't cross it. Can't get in." I paused. "Wasn't supposed to be able to get out either, but apparently it lives in me now, so the paperwork's unclear."

Brannoc snorted. "And what does the line protect the valley *from*?"

"That's what I used to think it did. Protect." I watched the river slide by, brown and patient. "My master told me, at the end, that the guild put the line there. Not to guard the valley. To fence it. The covenant — the old bargain that lets people work magic at all — the guild stole its anchor, generations ago, and hid it inside the salt, and built a religion of gatekeeping on top of the theft. Every rule I ever kept. Every prayer. All of it housekeeping for a crime."

I'd never said it out loud before. Saying it made it real in an unpleasant way, like stepping on a stair that isn't there.

Brannoc was quiet for a long moment. Even her weather paused.

"And the anchor?" she asked carefully. "Stolen anchor. What does that mean, exactly? A stone? A relic?"

"They never wrote it down where I could find it. But Aldous said the realm sent a plea, when the covenant started failing. Sent it to the valley, to the guild, to *us*." I glanced back at Tam, who was walking behind us making friends with a stick. "Aldous thought the plea was him."

Brannoc stopped walking.

She turned and looked at Tam properly — not the quick kindly cataloguing glance of a healer, but slowly, the way you look at a page you realize you've misread. Tam looked back at her, entirely unbothered, and offered her the stick.

"Child," Brannoc said, "do you know what you're carrying around with you?" — and then, catching herself, "No, don't answer, that was rude, I apologize." She rubbed her face with both green-stained hands. "Right. Right. Well. I was only going to walk you to the crossroads."

"You don't have to come," I said. "Really. You've done more than — you gave me medicine, and you didn't even charge — "

"*Donation-optional.*" She resumed walking, faster, jaw set. "And I'm not doing it for you particularly, girl, I'm doing it because in eleven years of licensed respectability I never once got to decide anything for myself, and it turns out I'm *very* good at deciding, I've been saving it up." She jabbed a finger backward at the road behind us. "Also, the tollhouse at Miller's Bend is staffed by wardens, and they take tolls in memory now, actual memories, cut out and jarred, and I am not giving those bastards the recipe for my grandmother's plum wine, which is the best thing about my family."

"They take — memories? As tolls?"

"Road tax for people without papers. Very efficient. Very legal." She smiled, and for the first time the smile had teeth in it. "You wanted to know what the law looks like from underneath, Saltbound girl? Keep walking. It's about half a mile that way, and it looks like a building with teeth."

Behind us, Tam tapped his stick against a stone in a rhythm I almost recognized. Three taps.

Food first, though. There was time for catastrophe after lunch.

* * *

We ate bread and hard cheese on a fallen log above the mill race, and I watched Brannoc watch Tam, and I thought about what she'd said — *the rules she was keeping were keeping her* — and about the strange new fact of kindness with no ledger attached.

In the guild, everything had been permitted. Permission was the entire architecture of my life: permission to study, permission to speak, permission to touch the salt line, granted from above by masters answering to older masters answering, it turned out, to nothing but their own guilty continuity. I had mistaken permission for care my whole life. It had never occurred to me that a stranger might hand a cup of medicine to an unknown girl on a riverbank simply because the girl needed it — no rolls, no rolls to be struck from, no tribunal afterward. Just a woman, a stump, and a choice.

Trust, it seemed, could exist without anyone's signature on it. The discovery sat in my chest, warm and foreign, like a coal carried in cupped hands.

"Brannoc," I said.

"Hm?"

"Why help us? Truly. Not the speech about deciding things. The truth."

She chewed her cheese and considered the river.

"Year of the marsh fevers," she said finally, "there was a boy. Ten years old. His mother carried him eighteen miles to the Compact's dispensary because I'd been struck off by then and she was frightened of me — frightened of the woman who'd *saved her neighbors*, because a piece of paper told her to be. The dispensary turned them away at the door. Fees unpaid." She brushed crumbs from her many layers with great precision. "He died on the road home. And I've thought about it most days since, and here's what I've concluded: the paper didn't kill him. Fear did. Everyone in that chain — the mother, the clerks, me, standing useless in my cottage — everyone was obeying something they were more afraid of than each other." She looked up at me. "So when a guild-marked girl walks out of a forbidden valley holding hands with a miracle child and flinching at her own shadow, I help. Because maybe this time the fear loses."

Tam, finished eating, leaned his head against my arm. Then he raised his hand and tapped my wrist — twice, twice again, and then a third sequence I'd never seen. Five taps. He pointed at Brannoc, then at himself, then at me, and then held up all five fingers again, insistent.

"What's five?" I asked Brannoc, uselessly.

"No idea." But she was watching him with her head tilted. "Could be a number. Could be a word. Could be Old Tongue." Her voice dropped. "Girl — does he tap like that often? Patterns? Repeats?"

"Constantly. It's how he talks."

"Then learn it." She stood, shouldering her sack, and Hildegarde rose with her like a small feathered deputy. "Every pattern, every rhythm, write them down tonight. Because if he's speaking the covenant's own tongue, then those taps aren't habits, they're *sentences*, and I suspect — " she broke off.

Down the mill path, where it bent toward the river bend, a sound drifted through the evening green. Boots. Many boots, unhurried, in step. And beneath them, thin and bureaucratic and horribly familiar from Aldous's descriptions, a bell — the small brass bell that guild wardens rang at tollhouses to announce that the law was open for business.

Brannoc's hand found my shoulder and steered me, gently and absolutely, off the path and down into the shadow of the millrace.

"There it is," she murmured, "half a mile early. Policy, waiting. Come along, both of you, quickly now — and girl, whatever happens at that tollhouse, remember the one thing I know for certain about the law."

Tam's fingers tightened in mine. Above us, the bell rang again, closer.

"What's that?" I whispered.

"It's always hungry," said Brannoc, "and it always eats first."
