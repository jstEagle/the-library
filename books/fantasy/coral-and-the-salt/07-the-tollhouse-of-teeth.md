## 7. The Tollhouse of Teeth

Brannoc had patched enough wounds in her forty-odd years to know that the worst ones never bled where you could see them. The tollhouse ahead was one of those.

It crouched on the causeway where the road dipped toward the marsh, a squat stone thing with a roof like a dropped hat and lanterns burning a green Brannoc did not like. Green fire was guild fire. Guild fire meant wardens. Wardens meant questions with teeth in them, and Brannoc had spent eleven years making sure her name stayed small and her business stayed dull. A hedge-healer who asked nothing of anyone was beneath notice. That was the whole art of survival: be boring.

Except now she was walking up to a tollhouse with a fugitive apprentice and a child who spoke in a language that made salt sing, and boring had sailed off without her.

"Walk slow," she murmured. "Look tired."

"We are tired," Yara said.

"Good. Be it harder." Brannoc glanced at Tam, riding on Yara's back with his chin tucked against her shoulder, watching everything with those grave dark eyes. "Tam. If they ask you anything, you're deaf and simple. Can you look simple?"

Tam looked at her for a long moment, then arranged his face into an expression of such profound vacancy that Brannoc nearly laughed despite everything.

"That'll do," she said. "Gods help me, that'll do."

* * *

The tollkeeper was not what she expected, which was itself suspicious. She'd expected some warden's clerk, ink-stained and petty. Instead a broad woman sat under the green lanterns, mending a net, with two wardens flanking the door behind her — tall figures in grey oilcloth, their faces hidden by hoods that cast shadows a little too deep for honest daylight.

"Passage toll," the woman said, without looking up from her netting. "Two bits a head, or the alternative."

"The alternative being?" Brannoc asked, though she knew. Everyone knew. The guild had introduced memory-tolls twenty years ago, quietly, the way you introduce rot into a beam. Pay in recollection and keep your coin. Most folk paid coin. Some paid memories when they were desperate — a bad winter's grief, a shameful night — and came away lighter and strange-eyed, unable to say why the world felt like a borrowed coat.

The tollkeeper looked up. Her face was weathered and ordinary and her eyes were not. They had the flat shine of someone whose ledger was kept somewhere behind them.

"For travelers without papers," she said, "the toll is one true memory. Given freely. The guild values truth over silver these days." She smiled as if this were a kindness. "Times are uncertain. We collect certainty."

Brannoc laughed, warm and easy, the laugh of a woman who'd shared a drink with every customs officer between here and the coast. "Certainty! That's a fine word for it. Tell me, mistress — do I look like I have any certainty left? I'm a healer. Certainty died in me years ago, along with most of my patients."

The tollkeeper's netting paused. "Papers, then."

"Papers." Brannoc rumpled through her satchel at leisure, muttering, producing first a dried frog (which she put back with dignity), then a twist of willow bark, then half a honey cake wrapped in cloth. "I know I had them. Ah — here." She produced a folded paper that was, in fact, a bill of sale for a goat named Buttercup, aged and smudged. "There. Traveling papers."

The tollkeeper took it. Read it. Looked at Brannoc.

"This says you bought a goat."

"Best goat in three counties. Sad story, actually. Are you certain you wouldn't rather hear it than take my memory?"

For a heartbeat something human flickered across the woman's face — the twitch of someone fighting a smile — and Brannoc thought, *there you are*, because even people inside the machine were still people, and people could be levered.

Then one of the hooded wardens spoke, and the warmth went out of the air like a snuffed candle. "Search them."

"We've done nothing—"

"The girl." The warden's voice was dry as old bone. "Step forward, girl."

Yara stepped forward. Brannoc watched her do it with her heart climbing into her throat, watched the hood tilt as the warden studied her face against some inner page, and understood in one cold drop that this wasn't chance. They weren't collecting tolls out here. They were *fishing*. Casting the road like a net because they'd lost something and wanted it back.

"You resemble," the warden said slowly, "someone of interest to the Guild."

"Do I?" said Yara, and Brannoc could have kissed her, because the girl's voice came out bored, valley-flat, unimpressed. "Everyone resembles someone. My mother said I had a face like trouble."

Behind her, Tam made a small vacant humming sound and picked at his ear, the picture of a boy who lived far inside his own head.

The warden ignored him. Warden training, Brannoc guessed, taught them that children and fools were furniture. It was the single greatest gift Tam had given them all trip.

"Your name," the warden said.

"Marla," Brannoc cut in smoothly. "She's Marla, my sister's girl, simple-minded since the fever, gods rest my sister. I mind her and the boy both. We're going to my cousin's in Marsh Hollow to bury my sister's ashes, which is why we carry no silver, having spent it on the coffin, which I can show you, it's in the cart—"

"There's no cart."

"I sold the cart to buy the coffin and then carried the coffin as far as the river and the river took it, which was very moving actually, the whole family wept—"

"*Enough.*" The warden's hood turned back to Yara. "The resemblance stands. You'll pay the toll in memory so the record can be checked against it. Truth over silver."

And there it was. The trap dressed as procedure. A memory from Yara would tell them exactly who she was, and worse, might strip from her whatever the guild feared she remembered.

Brannoc had bargained with death before — every healer had, in the small hours, trading promises into the dark — and death at least was honest about wanting something. This was worse. This was law wearing hunger's face and calling it duty.

"Memory, is it," she said loudly, blustering forward. "Fine, fine, take mine, I've got dozens, useless ones mostly—" And while she talked, her hands, quick from a lifetime of hiding herbs from tithe-collectors, were doing quieter work: she pressed the little wax-sealed packet of marsh-salt and crushed gullflower into Tam's palm behind his back, and tilted her chin, once, toward the low arch at the side of the tollhouse. The undercroft. Every tollhouse had one; every tollhouse flooded; every tollhouse keeper used it to store the barrels of taken goods nobody wanted to inventory.

Tam's fingers closed around the packet. His eyes didn't move. He went on picking at his ear, humming.

"Now that one," Brannoc announced, jabbing a thumb at herself, "is a *memory*. The night I delivered the marshal's twins in a snowstorm, using nothing but brandy and prayer. Take that. It's worth ten of anybody's."

"The toll is hers," the tollkeeper said.

"The toll is *flexible*, surely, for a woman of your obvious authority—"

"Mother," Yara said suddenly, in exactly the whine of a daughter long embarrassed by her parent, "let them just take it, gods, you'd argue with the tide—"

And while every eye in the tollhouse settled on the two squabbling women — because there is no spectacle so reliably absorbing as family friction — Tam slipped sideways, silent as water finding its level, through the side arch and down into the dark.

* * *

Underneath the tollhouse was cold and knee-deep and smelled of drowned wood and old rope. Tam waded carefully, holding the wax packet above the water like an offering, feeling with his bare feet for the shape of steps. The guild built straight lines even underground. He followed the line.

Above him, muffled through stone, he heard Brannoc's voice rising and falling, magnificent, absurd — the snowstorm, the brandy, the marshal's wife swearing in three languages — a story spun out longer and longer, each detail another handful of seconds bought. He almost smiled. The loud woman lied like other people breathed, generously, and it occurred to him, in his wordless way, that lying so thoroughly for someone else might be its own kind of telling the truth.

He found the drain grate at the causeway's end. It was barred. He considered it, then set the wax packet down on a dry ledge, opened it, and drew a line of the salt-and-gullflower across the bars, the way the loud woman had shown him on the third night, saying *rust-bite, eats iron, don't touch it with skin, use a stick, honestly child, must I think of everything.*

The grate began, very softly, to hiss.

* * *

"—and that's when the midwife said, and I quote, 'that's no baby, that's a weather system'—"

"Enough!" The tollkeeper slammed her palm on the table. Behind her, one warden had drifted toward Yara, close now, too close, and Brannoc saw the hood dip as it drank in her face, and saw the moment recognition arrived — not certainty, but the terrible appetite of almost.

"Hold," the warden said. "Warrant check. The Ravensworth girl is—"

The floor shuddered.

It was not dramatic. A groan of old timber, a belch of green light from the undercroft stairs, and then water — black, sudden, smelling of rust and river — burst up through the floor grates as the drainage seals gave way, as they had clearly been giving way for years, as any honest inspector would have known.

"Undercroft's breached!" the tollkeeper shouted, leaping up, her precious net abandoned. "The ledgers!"

And there it was — Brannoc could have wept with admiration — the tollhouse's true heart laid bare. Not the road. Not the tolls. The *ledgers*, downstairs in the wet dark, all those harvested memories filed like pickled fish, and the keeper's whole soul went rushing after them, wardens striding down into the flood after their precious records, and nobody, nobody at all, was watching two travelers and a simple-minded boy.

"Run," Brannoc breathed. "Now, love, now—"

They ran. Out the far door, along the causeway, splashing through shallows, until the tollhouse was a shrinking green glow behind them and the marsh opened wide and kind around them, all reeds and frogs and forgiving darkness. Tam materialized out of a reed bed as if he'd grown there, soaked to the ribs, grinning — actually grinning, which was new, and Brannoc decided on the spot it was her favorite thing she'd helped make all year.

"Rust-bite," she wheezed, catching her breath against a fence post. "On the drain seals. Child, you are wasted on prophecy. Come be a criminal, we have better snacks."

Yara pulled Tam against her side, laughing shakily, and then stopped laughing. "Brannoc. What did it start to say? Back there. Before the floor broke."

Brannoc hesitated. She was, professionally, a great believer in not telling patients bad news until the bleeding had stopped.

But the girl deserved better than management.

"It said 'warrant,'" Brannoc said gently. "It said the Ravensworth girl. Which means there's paper with your face on it, love, and guild wax on the seal. And warrants come in only two flavors. Capture—" she met Yara's eyes— "or thief."

Yara stood very still in the reeds. Somewhere a marsh bird complained about the interruption to its evening.

"They're calling me a thief," Yara said at last. "Of what? My whole life is in that valley. My master's house. His records. What is there to steal?"

"Maybe it's not about what you took," Brannoc said quietly, thinking of the coral key sewn into the girl's coat, thinking of how much weight a small broken thing could carry. "Maybe it's about what they're afraid everyone will learn was *taken* in the first place."

Tam tugged Yara's sleeve. When she looked down, he pointed east — toward the low hills beyond the marsh — and made the gesture he'd been making for days now, the one Brannoc had finally learned to read: flat hand, rising, like something coming up from underwater.

Then he pointed again, urgently, and this time his hand shaped itself into a claw, and dragged downward through the air.

Coming up from the water — and something else coming down on top of it.

Far off, on the road behind them, the green lantern-light of the tollhouse flared suddenly bright, then began to move. Wardens didn't stay to mop floors. Wardens had horses.

"Well," said Brannoc, shouldering her satchel and taking Tam's free hand. "Marsh Hollow's east, the hills are east, and my knees are forty-three years old, but tonight they're nineteen. Walk fast, love. The law has our scent, and I've a feeling it intends to bite."
