## 8. Ashes of the Guildhall

The guildhall announced itself three miles before we saw it.

Tam smelled it first. He tugged my sleeve and pointed at the sky, where a smear of gray hung over the eastern hills like a bruise that had forgotten how to heal. I had smelled it too — woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, something like burnt sugar — but I had told myself it was a farmstead, a kiln, anything.

"That's not a farmstead," Brannoc said, because she was the sort of person who said aloud the things everyone else was busy not saying.

"No," I agreed.

"Your guildhall?"

"My guildhall."

We walked faster after that, though none of us said so either. Some things you don't say; you just do them, and your legs do the grieving for you.

* * *

I want to tell you what Ravensworth Hall looked like when I was a girl, so that you understand what we found. It sat on its low hill above the millpond like a patient old heron: whitewashed walls gone honey-colored at the corners, a slate roof green with lichen, the salt-glass windows that Master Aldous polished every equinox while grumbling about his knees. Inside there were the archives — two hundred years of ledgers, tide-charts, covenant records in their iron-banded chests — and the long refectory table scarred by every apprentice who'd ever carved their initials when they thought no one was looking. Mine was under the third bench from the window. *Y.R., age nine,* next to a lopsided fish I'd been very proud of at the time.

What we found was a black tooth in a green gum.

The roof had fallen inward. The whitewash was peeled back like skin off an apple, showing charred timber beneath. The millpond reflected it all perfectly, which felt like a cruelty — as if the water wanted to make sure no one missed the details. Ash lay over everything in a fine gray snowfall, and under the ash, the smell. Not just smoke. Paper. Two hundred years of paper, burned.

"Corvane," Brannoc said quietly. "This has Warden's work written all over it. Look." She nodded at the gateposts, where someone had nailed up a sheet of vellum still flapping in the wind. I walked to it and read it twice, because the first time the letters wouldn't sit still.

**BY ORDER OF WARDEN CORVANE, KEEPER-DESIGNATE OF THE COVENANT:** *The Ravensworth cell having proven faithless, its holdings are forfeit to the Guild entire. Let all who aided the thief Yara Ravensworth know themselves outlawed.*

"Thief," I said. "He's calling me a thief. Of what? My own inheritance?"

"He doesn't think it's yours," Brannoc said. "That's rather the point of him."

Tam crouched at the edge of the ruin and did what Tam always did: he picked up a stick and began to draw in the ash. A tower. A chest. An eye above both, watching. Then he scrubbed it out with his palm, hard, as if he'd drawn something that frightened him.

"We can't stay long," Brannoc said. "If Corvane's people burned it, they may come back for the embers. But you should look. Whatever's here, you should see it with your own eyes before we run."

She was right, and I hated her a little for it, and I loved her considerably more, which is how it usually went between us.

* * *

Walking through the wreck of my childhood is not something I can describe in order, because I didn't experience it in order. The mind does strange sorting when everything you loved is in one heap. I stepped over the door — the good oak door Aldous made me oil every spring — and found myself standing in what had been the refectory, except the refectory was now the sky. The long table had survived, half of it, one leg thrust up out of the rubble like a drowning man's arm. I dug along its length without deciding to. My hands knew where they were going before I did.

Third bench from the window. Or where the window used to be.

The carving was scorched but readable. *Y.R., age nine.* And beside it, the lopsided fish.

I sat down on the ash and laughed. It came out of me sideways, ugly, nothing like real laughter, and Brannoc put her hand on my shoulder and let me do it, because she understood that sometimes the body has to get the nonsense out before the heart can get any sense in.

"They burned my fish," I managed.

"A tragedy," she said gravely. "Though between us, it was never much of a fish."

"It was an excellent fish."

"It had legs."

"Fins."

"It had four legs and a frown, Yara."

And that — that stupid, tender argument about a nine-year-old's bad drawing — is what let me stand up and go on into the archive vault, which is where the day stopped being about grief and started being about something sharper.

* * *

The vault door was bronze, and it was open, and it had been opened politely. No battering. No prying. The lock hung intact on its hasp, sprung by a key. Which meant whoever came had known the mechanism, which meant they had come from inside the Guild, which meant Corvane hadn't just robbed the hall — he'd done it with a Ravenworth key in his hand, or worse, with the help of someone who'd carried one.

Inside, the shelves stood in rows like a congregation that had been struck mid-prayer. Empty. Not burned — emptied. Every ledger, every chest, every scroll case: gone, taken with a looter's thoroughness that was almost respectful. On one shelf someone had left a single item behind, squared up neatly against the wall, and I knew before I touched it why they'd left it.

It was the guild seal. The great wax-stamp of Ravensworth, carved with the interlocked rings of the covenant. Someone had snapped the handle clean off.

"You don't burn a thing like this," Brannoc murmured behind me. "You break it. You want the message read."

"I'm reading it."

"He's not erasing the hall, love. He's inheriting it. There's a difference, and it matters. Burners want you gone. Heirs want you to know whose house it is now."

Tam slipped past my knees into the vault. He went straight to the back corner — the corner I'd have gone to, the corner where Aldous kept the oldest things, the records even senior wardens weren't shown — and began pulling down the empty cases one by one, checking, checking. Then he stopped. He made his small urgent sound, the one that wasn't quite a word yet, and pointed.

Behind the last row of shelving, wedged where the wall met the floor, was a gap. A hidden recess. I'd played hide-and-seek in this vault as a child and never once found it; it took a mute boy from the sea two hundred years away to put his hand flat against the stone and feel the draft.

The recess held three things.

A bundle of papers, water-stained and older than any document I'd ever catalogued. A child's shoe, salt-stiffened, far too small to be anyone's who'd lived here in living memory. And a portrait.

* * *

I need to be careful here, because what happened next rearranged me, and rearranged people tell stories crooked.

The portrait was small — a hand's width of painted wood, the kind of thing you'd hang in a bedchamber, not a gallery. The varnish had crazed into a spiderweb of fine lines, and the colors had gone the way colors go after centuries: the blues faded toward gray, the reds toward rust. But the face was clear. Clear as morning.

A woman, perhaps twenty years old. Dark hair pinned back carelessly, the way I pin mine back when I'm working and expect no one to see. A strong jaw. A mouth that looked like it was on the verge of objecting to something. Eyes the color of wet slate, set deep, with the faint permanent furrow between the brows of someone who has spent her whole life squinting into weather she doesn't trust.

Brannoc looked at the portrait. Then she looked at me. Then she looked at the portrait again, and did the bravest and kindest thing anyone did for me that entire journey: she said nothing at all.

Because it was my face.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance you'd squint at over supper. It was mine — the exact cast of brow, the same slight asymmetry where the left eye sits a hair higher than the right, a thing so small I'd only ever seen it in mirrors and had privately decided mirrors exaggerated. The painter had even caught the way I hold my chin when I'm trying to look more certain than I am.

"Two hundred years," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like it belonged to somebody standing further away. "Brannoc. This painting is two hundred years old. The varnish alone—"

"I know."

"The clothes are two hundred years out of fashion. The frame—"

"Yara."

"She has my face."

"I know, love. Breathe."

But breathing wasn't doing what it was supposed to. Because there were only so many explanations, and I could count them on one hand, and every one of them was terrible. That the woman was my ancestor, and blood runs strange and true, and fine — fine — except that faces don't repeat like this, not exactly, not down to the asymmetric eye. That the painting was a fake planted recently, except the craquelure doesn't lie, and neither did the smell of the thing, that deep cellar-smell of genuine age. That—

Or the third explanation. The one my mind kept sliding away from like a foot on wet stone. That the reason a two-hundred-year-old portrait wore my face was that I had worn hers. Before. That I was not the newest branch of the Ravensworth line but some kind of — return. A repetition. Something the Guild had done once and then done again and then kept doing, generation after generation, the way you re-bandage a wound you refuse to let heal.

The child's shoe lay in the recess beside the portrait, salt-stiffened, impossibly small.

I picked it up and my hands were shaking and I could not have told you whether from fear or fury, and honestly I don't think the distinction mattered anymore.

"What did they do?" I whispered. "What did this guild *do*, Brannoc?"

"I don't know. But I know a man who might." Brannoc's jaw set in the way it did when she was about to do something reckless and call it practical. "Aldous didn't burn those records because he's tidy, Yara. He burned them because they were proof. And men who burn proof leave some behind — there's always a page too dear or too dangerous to feed the fire. If there's anything left of the truth in this valley, it's in his ashes, and we're going back for it."

Tam tugged my sleeve. He had taken up his stick again and was drawing in the ash floor of the vault, quick and certain, and I knelt to look.

He drew the woman from the portrait. He drew her well — better than the painter, because he drew her angry. And around her head he drew a ring of salt crystals, and from the ring, lines running outward like rays, to little figures: a village, a crowd, dozens of small upturned faces. Then he drew a chain. From the ring of salt at her throat, down through the picture, into a shape beneath the ground — and he pressed so hard the stick snapped.

He looked up at me. His eyes were wet and furious and very old for a child of eight, and he pointed at the drawn figure, then at me, then laid his small hand flat against my sternum, over my heartbeat, as if confirming something was stored there.

"Anchor," Brannoc said softly, reading the drawing over our heads. "In the old coastal charms, that's the sign for an anchor. The thing that holds the ship steady by suffering the strain." She paused. "Yara. What did Aldous actually tell you about the covenant? About how it works? About why the salt line holds?"

"He said—" I stopped. I made myself say it plainly, because plainness was all I had left. "He said the Guild keeps the terms. He said the salt remembers. He said—" My voice failed and restarted. "He never said what remembered. He never said who paid for the remembering. In twelve years, he never once said what the anchor *was*. He only ever said the anchor was safe."

"And you believed him."

"He was dying," I said. "He was my master. He gave me bread when I was starving and a trade when I had nothing, and yes, Brannoc, I believed him, because the alternative was believing I'd been raised inside a lie with my own name stitched on it."

The ash sifted down through the broken rafters above us, fine and gray and patient. Somewhere outside, the wind turned the notice on the gatepost over, so that for one moment the blank back of the vellum faced the road, and then it flipped again, and the accusation faced outward once more. *Thief.* I almost laughed again. Corvane had it precisely wrong, or precisely right depending on how you counted — someone in my line, in my own skin, had stolen something enormous, and I was only now being handed the bill for it, two hundred years late, with interest.

"Right," I said, and stood, and put the portrait inside my coat, against my ribs, where it lay cold as a second heart. "The shoe comes too. The papers come. We're going back across the mountains, and we're going through whatever Aldous hid in that grave of his, and then, Warden Corvane and all his tidy heirs notwithstanding, we are going to the sea, and I am going to knock on the door of whoever sent that child, and I am going to ask them — very politely — what my face was doing in their covenant."

Tam smiled. It was the first time since the burning that I saw anything like light in him, and it was, I note with some embarrassment, a smile in response to my anger — as if rage, honestly owned, was the first thing he'd seen in me that he recognized.

Brannoc snorted. "Very politely."

"Extremely politely."

"With the shattered seal in your pocket."

"As a conversational aid."

Outside, the evening came down over the ruined hill, and the millpond held the black tooth of the guildhall in its grip, and I took one last look at the place that had made me — at the bench with my fish, at the vault with its congregation of empty shelves — and I found that the grief had changed shape somewhere in the last hour. It was still grief. But it had grown a spine.

We walked down through the ash-fall toward the road, three figures out of a burned story, and behind us the notice flapped on its nail, naming me a thief to an empty hillside.

Neither of us knew — not Brannoc with her hedge-craft, not Tam with his drawings, not me with my stolen face folded against my ribs — that the vault had not been entirely empty after all. That some of the dead of Ravensworth Hall had stayed at their posts, as archivists do, past all reasonable hours.

And that one of them had been watching me since the moment I knelt by the hidden recess, and had recognized my face too.
