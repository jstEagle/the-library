## 9. The Anchor's Name

Brannoc had patched enough wounds in her life to know the difference between a hurt that wanted screaming and a hurt that wanted silence. Yara Ravensworth, sitting cross-legged on the floor of a burned guildhall with a two-hundred-year-old portrait in her lap, was doing both at once — which was to say she wasn't doing anything at all, and it was the loudest thing Brannoc had ever heard.

The fire had taken the roof and most of the second story. What remained of the archive was a scatter of sodden ledgers and scorched scroll-cases, half-buried in ash that still smelled faintly of rosemary, because some fool of a guildsman had burned a bundle of preservation herbs along with everything else. Tam sat nearby, drawing in the ash with one careful finger: circles within circles, a spiral like a shell cut open.

Brannoc crouched beside a collapsed shelf and did what she always did when there was nothing useful to do with her hands: she sorted herbs. Dried yarrow from her pack. Comfrey. A twist of bog-myrtle gone brittle with age. The ritual of it steadied her, and besides, if you were going to be hunted by wardens, you might as well be hunted with a well-organized satchel.

"You're going to want to see this," Yara said. Her voice came out flat as slate.

"I've seen a great many things," Brannoc said. "Some of them I'd have paid not to."

Yara turned the portrait so it caught the grey light falling through the broken rafters. A woman in guild robes of deep blue, silver thread at the cuffs, standing before a salt line rendered in paint so fine it looked like actual crystal. The woman's face —

Brannoc had been traveling with Yara for weeks now. She knew the set of her jaw, the crooked left eyebrow, the small scar through her right brow where a salt-crystal had burst during a lesson gone wrong. Every line of it was on the painted face. Even the scar.

"That's not possible," Brannoc said, because someone had to say it, and Yara clearly wasn't in a state to manage words longer than a knife.

"The frame's oak. Guild-make, pre-Corvane stamp." Yara's thumb moved over the corner of the canvas. "There's a date on the back. Two hundred and eleven years ago."

"Painters flatter their subjects. Sometimes they make them look like other people entirely—"

"She has my scar, Brannoc."

Brannoc shut up. There were arguments worth having and arguments that were just noise, and this was the second kind.

Tam tugged at Brannoc's sleeve. He pointed at the portrait, then at Yara, then pressed his palm flat against his own chest with an expression of such grave urgency that Brannoc felt the hair rise on her arms. Then he drew in the ash again, fast now: the spiral, and inside it a small figure, and lines running out from the figure to every edge of the drawing, like threads from a spider, or roots from a tree, or — Brannoc's stomach turned — like the radiating pattern of a salt line seen from above, with something standing at its center.

"You know what he's saying," she said quietly.

"I think I don't want to." But Yara set the portrait down and looked at the drawing, and Brannoc watched her go very still, the way people do when the ground under them stops being ground.

* * *

The ghost found them an hour later, or perhaps they found the ghost; with archives it was never clear who was haunting whom.

Brannoc had been prying open scroll-cases looking for anything Corvane's looters had missed — a warden's ledger, a map, even a recipe for decent tea would have been welcome — when the air in the reading room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with drafts. It was the particular cold of a place remembering something.

"Oh no," Brannoc said. "No, thank you. I've met your kind before. You're all preamble and riddles and then somebody ends up crying."

The ghost condensed out of the shadow between two shelves: a thin figure in archivist's grey, spectacles that had long since stopped needing to see anything, hands folded with the patience of someone who had spent a lifetime waiting for slow readers. She regarded Brannoc with mild disapproval.

"I am the Keeper of the Deep Stacks," the ghost said. Her voice was dry as vellum. "I have waited two hundred years for someone to ask the correct question."

"Well, I'm a hedge-witch with a criminal record and a bad knee, so I can promise you disappointment."

Behind her, Yara had risen slowly to her feet. "What question?"

The Keeper turned to her, and something in her folded-hands patience cracked — just slightly, just at the edges — into something that looked almost like pity.

"*Who is the anchor?*"

The word *anchor* dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Brannoc heard it fall all the way down.

"That's a guild term," Yara said. "From the old liturgy. 'The anchor holds, the line endures.' We recited it every solstice. It was — ceremony. Metaphor."

"It was inventory," said the Keeper. "Sit down, child. This will take as long as it takes, and you will want to be seated for the parts that break."

Yara didn't sit. Brannoc did, dragging a scorched stool over, mostly because her knees had opinions and also because she had a sudden, crawling suspicion that whatever came next would knock her off her feet anyway.

"The covenant is old," the Keeper began. "Older than the guild, older than the kingdoms, older than the names we give things. In its first form, magic flowed to all who swore the oath — freely given, freely held. But oaths are inconvenient things. They cannot be hoarded. So the founders of what became the Saltbound Guild devised a refinement." The ghost's folded hands tightened. "They bound the covenant's living anchor into a single bloodline. One vessel. One point through which all the covenant's power passed — and one point, therefore, that they could control. Guard. Gatekeep. Tax."

"An anchor holds a ship," Yara said. Her voice was very careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and describing the view.

"An anchor holds a ship," the Keeper agreed. "Or drowns with it. The anchor was never meant to be a person. It was meant to be a *place* — a hearthstone, a boundary-stone, something with no throat to choke and no heart to break. The guild made it a person because a person can be sworn without understanding, bound without consenting, and replaced without mourning."

"And the replacement—" Brannoc started, and then stopped, because she already knew, and knowing arrived in her chest like cold water.

"When the anchor dies," the Keeper said, "the covenant passes to the next born of the line. Always the same face. Always the same name, though the guild changed the records to hide even that. The valley you tended, child — the salt line you walked every morning — you were not maintaining it. You were *emanating* it. The line was not around you. It came from you."

The silence afterward had texture. Brannoc could feel it against her skin, rough as wool.

"My master," Yara said at last, each word placed like a stone in a wall, "told me I was the last apprentice of a dying guild. He told me the magic stayed outside the line because of the covenant. He told me I was ordinary."

"Aldous Winterbourne believed those things himself, by the end. It was easier. The guild taught its own members the lie first — how else would they teach it convincingly?" The Keeper drifted closer, and up close Brannoc could see that the ghost's edges flickered, like a candle in wind. "You were not kept *out* of the magic, Yara Ravensworth. You were kept *as* the magic. Every spell cast beyond the line, every warden's working, every toll collected at every gate — it all passed through you, drawn from you while you slept, while you worked, while you were a child learning your letters. You have been bleeding power into their system since the day you were born."

Brannoc had spent thirty years watching people receive terrible news. She had developed, she thought, a professional detachment. She watched Yara's hands now — how they opened and closed, opened and closed, as if testing whether they still belonged to her.

"The portrait," Yara said. "Two hundred years. How many times?"

"Nine," said the Keeper. "You have lived nine lives, and died eight deaths, and every time you were buried under a different name in a different plot, and every time the guild recorded it as a *succession*. As though you were a chair being reupholstered." The ghost's voice frayed, finally, into something human. "I catalogued the fourth death myself. I was alive then. I helped write the succession record. I told myself it was sacred duty." Her spectacles caught light that wasn't there. "It took me dying to understand what I had helped do. That is why I stayed. Ghosts are only ever the part of us that refuses to stop being ashamed."

Tam made a small sound. Everyone turned. He had come to stand beside Yara, and he was looking up at her with an expression Brannoc had no art to read — except that it held no fear, and no surprise, and something that looked terribly like recognition. He took her hand and pressed it against the spiral he'd drawn in the ash, against the small figure at its center.

"He knew," Brannoc whispered. "That's why he came to the valley. That's why he came to *you*."

"The drowned court sent a plea," the Keeper said. "A child carrying the Old Tongue, because the Old Tongue is the covenant's true language and cannot be silenced by any guild law. The plea was meant to find the anchor and wake her. But the anchor was not supposed to wake for another forty years — not until this life's natural death, when the binding would pass on. The child's arrival tore the schedule. Tam did not merely bring you a message, Yara Ravensworth. His arrival rang you like a bell. Everything since — the salt singing, the line flickering, the covenant failing — that is not the covenant breaking. That is *you*, waking up, and the old bindings cracking under the strain of a soul refusing its cage."

"So the guild's great crime," Yara said, and here her voice did change — it went low and even and terrible, the voice Brannoc imagined the sea used before it decided to come ashore, "isn't that they hid magic. It isn't even that they lied. It's that they took a person — nine times — and made her into a *fixture*. A hinge. A thing that turns so doors can swing. And called it duty."

"Yes."

"And I thanked them." Yara laughed, once, and there was nothing warm in it. "I polished their salt line. I memorized their liturgy. I loved that valley. I loved *them*." Her hand closed around the coral key at her throat, knuckles white. "Every morning I walked the boundary and felt proud of my work, and the whole time I was the work. I was the ox turning their mill and they let me love the harness."

Brannoc got up. She didn't decide to; her legs simply went, and then she was standing in front of Yara with her hands hovering uselessly, because there are moments when a healer's whole craft amounts to being present and not flinching.

"Say it," Brannoc said. "Whatever it is. Say it out loud. Rotten things keep better in air."

"They stole me." The words came out ragged. "Not my labor, not my gift — *me*. My face, my life, my deaths. I was never a person to them. I was infrastructure." Her breath hitched. "And the worst of it — the truly unforgivable part — is that some small, stupid piece of me is *relieved*. Because it means the restlessness was real. All those years I stood at the salt line wanting something I couldn't name — I wasn't ungrateful. I was *caged*. I wasn't broken. I was *used*."

"There it is," Brannoc said softly.

"I could burn this whole ruin down."

"You could. Though I'd note we're standing in it, and I've only the one spare cloak."

Something flickered across Yara's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, dragged up from somewhere deep. "You're very calm for a woman whose companion just announced she's a two-hundred-year-old magical battery."

"I'm a hedge-witch. I was expelled from polite society for healing people without permission forms. Calm in the face of institutional monstrosity is my one professional skill." Brannoc squeezed her shoulder, brief and hard. "Besides. Rage keeps. Better than most herbs, if you store it properly. Don't spend it all standing in ash. Spend it where it buys something."

Tam pulled at Yara's sleeve again. When she looked down, he pointed at the portrait, then shook his head firmly — *no* — and pointed at her, and nodded — *yes* — and then, with the terrible clarity children carry like lanterns, he drew one more thing in the ash: the small figure at the center of the spiral, and beside it, a second figure, walking away, free.

The Keeper drifted toward the ruined shelves. "There is more you must hear, and less time than I would like. Warden Corvane's agents passed through here three days ago. They took the succession records — all but the ones I hid, because I hid them from my own guild two hundred years ago and hiding things from the Saltbound is the only skill I ever mastered. Corvane believes the anchor is a *key* to be claimed. He does not yet understand what you are. When he does—"

"When he does, he'll stop sending agents," Yara said quietly, "and start coming himself."

"He is already coming," said the Keeper. "He has always been coming. Men like Corvane are not chasing you, child. They are chasing the chair they believe you sit in. And they will burn down every village, every road, every kindness between here and the sea to reach it." The ghost's form guttered, thinned. "The Unsworn camp in the river country, past the flooded ford. Find Sefa. Tell her the Keeper of the Deep Stacks sends the debt she is owed. And tell her—" the ghost smiled, and it was a young woman's smile, sudden and startling after two centuries of dust, "tell her the archive finally read to the end of the book."

She came apart into motes of light, like ash rising instead of falling, and was gone.

* * *

They left the guildhall at dusk, because Brannoc insisted that no one makes good decisions in a haunted building, and also because she'd found a jar of pickled plums in the pantry ruins that she refused to abandon on principle.

Yara walked ahead, the portrait wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to her pack. Brannoc had expected her to leave it. She hadn't expected her to take it, and when she asked, Yara was quiet for three full steps before answering.

"It's evidence," she said. Then, softer: "And she looks like someone who deserved to know what was done to her. If I won't remember her, someone should at least carry her face forward."

Tam walked between them, holding a hand of each, and hummed — tuneless, contented, the first sound Brannoc had heard him make that wasn't the Old Tongue. Just a child's humming, going somewhere, in the darkening air.

They made camp in a hollow off the road, no fire, sharing dried fish and the contraband plums. Brannoc was on first watch, which suited her; she thought best in the dark, with her hands busy mending a strap.

It was near midnight when Yara spoke from the shadows, without preamble, the way she'd begun doing since the guildhall — as though silence had become a room she could no longer bear to sit in alone.

"Brannoc. If I'm the anchor — if the covenant runs through me — then everything Corvane wants, everything the drowned court wants, everything the whole rotten system wants... it's all in here." Brannoc heard her tap her own chest, once. "I'm not carrying the key anymore. I *am* the key. Which means there's nowhere I can run where I'm not the prize."

Brannoc considered her answer carefully, the way she'd consider a dosage. "Aye. Probably."

"That's it? *Probably?*"

"You wanted comfort, you should've woken Sefa." Brannoc tied off the strap and looked out at the dark road, where far off — too far to be sure, but not far enough to be comfortable — she could see lights moving. Lanterns. Many of them, moving in formation, sweeping side to side in the methodical way of men searching a ditch for something they've lost.

Or hunting something they've found.

"Because here's my professional opinion," she said, reaching over to shake Tam gently awake, then Yara, "and you can quote me to the court, the guild, or whatever gods are still taking complaints: they can hunt the key all they like. But keys don't get a vote. And you, Yara Ravensworth — ninth of your name, first of your own — you get a vote. So we're going to spend the rest of tonight getting *off this road*, and tomorrow you can start deciding what your vote buys."

The lanterns swung closer along the darkened road. Somewhere behind them, a horn sounded — twice, short, the guild's signal for *quarry sighted*.

"Up," Brannoc said, hauling her pack onto her shoulders. "All of you. The river country starts past that ridge, and I intend to be extremely difficult to catch before sunrise."

Yara rose, and Brannoc saw it happen — saw the rage settle into her spine like ballast, saw the fear get folded away somewhere it could be reached later, saw the girl from the valley become, in the space of a single breath, something older and more dangerous than either.

"Lead on," Yara said. "And Brannoc — when we find these Unsworn?"

"Aye?"

"Tell Sefa the anchor doesn't kneel."

The horn sounded again, closer now, and they ran.
