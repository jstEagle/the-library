## 10. River of the Unsworn

The river found us before we found it.

We had been walking since dawn through country that kept changing its mind about what it wanted to be — meadow, then marsh, then a stretch of birch wood so pale it looked like the trees were holding their breath — when Tam stopped dead in the path and pointed.

At first I thought he was pointing at the water itself, a broad brown ribbon sliding between reed banks. But no. He was pointing at the light on the water, which was wrong in the most beautiful way. It moved against the current. Little motes of gold swam upstream while the water went down, and where they gathered, the reeds straightened as if someone had run a comb through them.

"That's not guild work," Brannoc said quietly. She had gone very still, the way she did whenever magic appeared that she hadn't paid for. "Guild wards don't heal things. They only forbid."

"Then whose is it?"

Brannoc's mouth did something complicated. "Nobody's," she said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The Unsworn found us an hour later, which I suppose should have surprised me and didn't at all. We were being watched; I'd felt it since midmorning, a prickle between my shoulder blades like weather coming. When the reeds finally parted and people stepped out of them — six of them, seven, more behind, dressed in river-mud browns and greens with no badges and no sigils — my first feeling wasn't fear.

It was relief so sharp it embarrassed me.

They carried no warden's iron. They carried fishing spears and one very old crossbow held by a woman with forearms like mooring rope, and they looked at Tam first, always Tam, with an expression I couldn't read until one of them — a young man with a heron feather in his cap — whispered something that made three others sit down right there in the mud, as if their legs had simply resigned.

"You're the child from the salt valley," the woman with the crossbow said. Not to Tam. To me. As if I were his translator, which, in fairness, I was.

"He doesn't speak," I said. "Not in any tongue you'd—"

"We know what he speaks." She lowered the crossbow, which somehow made her more formidable, not less. "I'm Sefa. You're Yara Ravensworth, last apprentice of the Saltbound Guild, currently carrying an object that half the wardens in the marchlands would drown their own mothers for." She looked me over the way Aldous used to look over a suspect batch of salt — thoroughly, and without flattery. "And you're being followed. Badly, but followed."

"How badly?"

"They've lost your trail twice and blamed each other both times. One of them cried." She shrugged. "Wardens are recruited young and trained worse. Come on. You look like you haven't eaten anything that wasn't ashamed of itself."

* * *

The Unsworn camp was not what I expected, though I couldn't have said what I expected. Something grim, probably. Outlaws in every story Aldous told were grim: gaunt faces, bitter songs, justice dispensed from shadows. What we walked into instead was a village built on stilts over a slow backwater — houses of woven reed and driftwood, laundry strung between them, children racing little boats made of bark along channels that had clearly been dug by someone who loved digging. Smoke rose from cookfires. Somewhere a fiddle was being played by someone who was still learning, and nobody seemed to mind.

And everywhere, woven into everything — into the doorframes, the fish-drying racks, the very mud of the paths — small workings. A charm to keep the mosquitoes honest. A knot-spell on a net that made it mend itself while its owner napped beside it. A woman teaching two children to coax warmth out of a cold hearth with nothing but a rhyme and their own two hands, laughing when one of them set the kindling alight too enthusiastically and had to pat it out with his hat.

Magic like bread baking. Magic like sweeping a floor.

Tam had stopped walking entirely. He stood in the middle of the main walkway with his head tipped back and his eyes enormous, and when a charm-lantern drifted past him on some errand of its own, he reached up one finger and touched it, gently, the way you'd touch a bird.

The lantern chimed.

Every head turned. And then — this is the part I will tell my students someday, if I ever have students, if there is ever anything left to teach — every person in that camp smiled. Not hungrily. Not the way people smile at a prize or a weapon. They smiled the way you smile at rain after drought.

"Oh," Brannoc said softly, beside me. Her voice had gone thick. "Oh, they've never seen a speaker before. None of them have. The Old Tongue died out of the free folk centuries ago — the guild saw to that." She wiped her face briskly with her sleeve, pretending she hadn't. "He's not just a child to them, Yara. He's proof. Proof it wasn't all stolen past saving."

* * *

Sefa fed us eel stew, which tasted better than it had any right to, and questioned us with a courtesy that was somehow more thorough than rudeness would have been. She asked about Aldous and listened to my answer all the way through without interrupting, which no one had done yet on this journey. She asked about the tollhouse, and winced at the right places. She asked about Corvane, and her jaw tightened at the name in a way that suggested history.

Then she set down her bowl, folded her hands, and said, "Now. The key."

The fire crackled. Around us, the camp went on with its evening — but quieter now, I noticed. Listening.

"What about it?" I said, which was stalling, and everyone present knew it.

"I want it."

"No," said Brannoc.

"I haven't finished asking," Sefa said pleasantly.

"You don't need to finish. I know how these asks go. 'Lend it to us for safekeeping.' 'Hold it in trust.' 'Just until the danger passes.'" Brannoc leaned forward, firelight catching the gray in her hair. "I patched hedge-charms for thirty years under guild law, Sefa. I've watched every promise of shared power turn into a new lock. The key doesn't care who holds it. It makes whoever holds it *right*."

"And your alternative?" Sefa didn't raise her voice. That was the unnerving thing about her; I got the sense she never needed to. "Your alternative is a girl with no training, no allies, and a bounty on her head walks into the drowned court alone and negotiates with something older than kingdoms. On charm and good intentions." She turned to me. "Is that the plan? Because I've seen charm and good intentions negotiate with the Tide Regent. There's a sandbar in the delta named after the last person who tried."

"The plan," I said, "is that the key opens the gate, and I use it to open the gate, and then—" I faltered. In fairness, I had been faltering on this part since the ashes of the guildhall. "Then we'll see."

"*We'll see,*" Sefa repeated. She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her sigh, deep and tired, the sigh of a woman who had been having this exact conversation with the world for twenty years. "Yara Ravensworth. Do you know why we're called the Unsworn?"

"Because you won't swear guild oaths."

"Because the guild wouldn't let us swear anything else." She spread her hands. "Every oath in the realm runs through the Saltbound. Marriage vows, harvest tithes, grave-blessings — all of it salted, all of it sealed, all of it *theirs*. Folk who refused were cut off from lawful magic, so we came here, to the wet places the wardens find tedious, and we swore oaths to each other instead. To the river. To no one." Her eyes were steady on mine. "We built something free, and it is *small*, Yara. It is small because freedom without strength gets pushed to the margins, and I have spent my whole life watching strong people promise that *this* time they'll share. So yes. I want the key. Not because I'm Corvane with better manners. Because I'm tired of betting my people's lives on strangers' good intentions."

The fire popped. Tam, curled against my side, had gone very still. He was looking at Sefa with an expression I was beginning to learn how to read — the look he gave when the salt sang, when something true was being said near him whether or not anyone meant it kindly.

"And if I say no?" I asked.

Sefa smiled, and it was a real smile, crooked and tired. "Then you sleep here tonight anyway, eat breakfast anyway, and walk to the coast tomorrow anyway, and I'll send guides as far as the delta because I'm not a monster, whatever Brannoc's face says. I make offers, girl. I don't make traps. Traps are guild craft."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat on the edge of the walkway with my feet dangling over the black water and the key in my lap, and it hummed against my palms like a sleeping animal, warm and patient and utterly indifferent to my torment.

It would be so easy. That was the thing nobody warned you about temptation — not that it arrives wearing horns, but that it arrives wearing your own face, saying things you already believe. *Give it to Sefa.* She was fair. She was kind, in her flinty way. Her people practiced magic like breathing and hoarded nothing. Surely the key would be safer in the hands of the free than in the grip of a girl who'd crossed her first salt line three weeks ago and still flinched at thunder.

But.

But I thought of the portrait in the ashes, the woman with my face, and how her eyes had looked even in paint: patient, worn, *used*. Two hundred years of anchors, each one handed over by someone who was sure they knew best. Each one certain that this time the cage would be a home.

"Can't sleep either?"

Brannoc lowered herself onto the walkway beside me with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. She pressed a mug into my hands — chamomile, doctored with something that made my magic-sickness headache recede like a tide going out.

"She's wrong," I said. "And she's right. Both at once. How does that work?"

"Badly. It works badly. That's what freedom is." Brannoc blew on her own mug. "You know what I'd ask myself, if I were you?"

"What?"

"Not *who can be trusted with it.* Everyone's answer to that is themselves." She tapped the rim of my mug with one finger, once, like a teacher making a point. "Ask instead: *what happens to the world if this thing exists at all?* Doesn't matter whose pocket it sits in. As long as it exists, there's a prize. And where there's a prize, there's a war with a queue."

I looked down at the key. Coral and bone, broken at the haft, humming its patient hum. Somewhere beneath the sea, a court was drowning and a Regent was grieving and a child's voice was locked away, and this ugly beautiful fragment was the only doorkey any of them had.

"You think I should break it," I said. "Like the ghost said. Like the original terms."

"I think," Brannoc said, "that you already know what you think, and you're waiting for permission. So here it is, freely given, from one outlaw to another: whatever you decide, decide it because it's *right*, not because it's owed. Debts make terrible foundations. Believe me. I've built on them."

* * *

I found Sefa at dawn, standing at the end of the main walkway, watching mist come off the water. She heard my footsteps and didn't turn.

"My answer is no," I said.

"Mm."

"But not the no you think." My hands were shaking, so I made fists. "I won't give you the key. I won't give it to anyone. But I'll give you what it *knows*. Every working in it, every term of the old covenant, everything the archive ghost showed me — written down, taught, given to your people and any people who want it. Not held by me. Not held by you. Held by everybody, which means owned by nobody." I swallowed. "It's a worse bargain than the one you asked for. It's slower and messier and half of it will fail. But it's the only one I can offer without becoming the next link in a chain that started with a woman who had my face."

For a long moment Sefa said nothing. Mist drifted. Somewhere across the water, the learning fiddler started up again, hit a wrong note, and kept going with tremendous confidence.

"A covenant of consent," Sefa said at last. "No anchor. No gatekeepers. Every hearth its own oath." She turned, and studied me with those mooring-rope arms crossed, and I braced for refusal.

What I got instead was a laugh — short, rusty, startled out of her. "You realize that's the most dangerous idea in the marchlands. More dangerous than the key. Corvane could survive you keeping the key. He cannot survive you *teaching*."

"Then I suppose," I said, "we'd better get me to the sea."

Sefa looked past me then, toward the guest-house where Tam slept, and her expression shifted into something I hadn't seen on her before — something almost gentle, and almost afraid.

"There's a rider coming up the levee road," she said quietly. "Alone. Guild colors, but torn off the shoulder — torn, not removed. He's been sitting his horse at our boundary markers since moonset, not coming closer. Asking, politely, for parley." Her eyes came back to mine, hard as river stones. "He says his name is Corvane. And he says he's brought you an offer you won't refuse."
