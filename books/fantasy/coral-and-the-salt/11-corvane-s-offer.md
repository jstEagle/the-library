## 11. Corvane's Offer

Warden-Commander Corvane had always believed that a man's character revealed itself in how he took his tea.

The innkeeper at the Drowned Lantern — a name that struck him as either very honest or very foolish for an establishment this far inland — had brought him a cup of something gray and bitter, brewed from leaves that had clearly been used twice before. He drank it without complaint. Good order was built on exactly this kind of small endurance. You did not demand the world be pleasant before you did your duty in it. You drank the weak tea, you sat in the drafty room, and you waited, because waiting was nine-tenths of command and the other tenth was paperwork.

He had been waiting three days for Yara Ravensworth to come down out of the hills.

His men had lost her trail at the river, which had annoyed him less than it should have. Brannoc of the hedgerows was with her, and that woman could vanish into a landscape like a rumor dissolving. But people were not rumors. People needed food, fire, sleep, and — this was the thing his younger wardens never understood — people needed *pattern*. Give them long enough and they would return to the shape of their habits like water finding its level. Yara Ravensworth had spent her whole life in a valley with one road in and one road out. She would come to the crossroads town eventually, because crossroads towns were where you bought supplies and asked questions, and she was, by every account he'd gathered, a girl who liked to understand things before she acted on them.

She had come the evening of the third day, soaked to the knees, a child's hand in hers, looking exactly as tired and stubborn as his informants had promised.

Corvane had not sent wardens to take her. That was the first thing she needed to understand about him, and he intended to make sure she understood it before anything else happened between them.

* * *

He found her the next morning at the market well, which told him something encouraging about her judgment. She was haggling over dried apples with the ferocity of someone who had recently learned what food cost when you couldn't grow it yourself, and the child — Tam, the mute one, the one who spoke a language older than kingdoms according to reports Corvane half-believed — was standing very solemnly beside her holding a rope that was attached to nothing.

"Apples are cheaper at Marta's stall," Corvane said, "if you don't mind the ones with the wormholes. The worms, in my experience, have excellent taste."

Yara turned. Up close she was thinner than her file suggested, with salt-cracked hands and the particular set of the jaw that Aldous Winterbourne had worn like a family crest. Her eyes went from his face to the warden's badge on his cloak to the six wardens who had, without any visible signal, drifted into positions around the market square — not surrounding her, not quite, but arranged the way furniture is arranged in a room you cannot leave politely.

"You're Corvane," she said.

"I'm Corvane." He inclined his head. "And you're the woman who burned down my guildhall."

"I didn't burn down anything."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You didn't. But you were there, and the report says you were, and reports are the bones of order." He gestured toward the tavern across the square. "I've taken the back room. It has a table, two chairs, and tea that tastes like regret. I'm asking you to sit with me. Not under guard. Not as a prisoner. As the last apprentice of the Saltbound Guild, speaking with the man who now carries its seal."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll buy your apples for you at full price and wish you a safe journey, and we'll both go on being tired." He smiled, and meant most of it. "But you came here to ask questions, Mistress Ravensworth. Everyone who comes to a crossroads town is asking questions. I am the only person in a hundred miles who has answers worth having."

* * *

The back room smelled of tallow smoke and old river. She sat because sitting let her keep her eyes on the door, and he respected that; he took the chair facing the window so she wouldn't have to wonder what was behind her. Small courtesies. Order was made of them.

Tam sat pressed against Yara's side, drawing in the spilled tea leaves on the table — little spirals, little waves. Corvane watched the boy's fingers move and felt the old cold walk up his spine, the same cold he'd felt in the archive vaults as a young clerk reading things he wasn't cleared to read. He put the feeling away. Feelings about the Old Tongue were a luxury for people who didn't have a realm to hold together.

"You've been to the guildhall," Yara said. "After the fire."

"I arrived four days after. Ashes, looted archives, and a portrait missing from the east gallery." He watched her flinch, just slightly, and knew she'd found it. Good. Better she learned it from the ashes than from him. "I know what you found there, or near enough. A woman with your face, painted two hundred years ago. I imagine it felt like drowning."

"It felt like a lie."

"Yes," Corvane said. "Both things can be true. That's rather the whole difficulty of history." He poured the second cup of tea and pushed it toward her. She didn't drink it. Wise. "Ask me what you came here to ask."

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the market noise washed against the shutters — a cart, a dog, someone singing badly about a miller's daughter. Then: "What is the anchor?"

There it was. Not *who am I*, not *what did they do to me*. What is the anchor. She wanted the mechanism, not the wound. Aldous's training all over, and Corvane felt a pang of something almost like grief for the old man, whom he had respected and reported on for thirty years in roughly equal measure.

"The anchor," he said, "is the living point where the covenant touches the world. Every covenant needs one. A promise is only words until something holds it in place — a stone, a border, a bloodline. Ours held a person. The Saltbound Guild bound the anchor's life to the salt line, generation after generation, so that magic would have a single door instead of a thousand broken windows." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "It was necessary."

"Was it?"

"It kept the wars from happening again." He said it simply, because it was simple. "You weren't alive for the Sundering, and neither was I, but the records are clear enough. When magic belonged to everyone, it belonged to whoever could take it, and what it bought was forty years of burning. The guild ended that. Not perfectly. Not kindly, perhaps. But the valleys slept safely for two centuries, and children grew up without learning the word 'pyre.' That is what gatekeeping bought. I have read the ledgers, Mistress Ravensworth. I know the exact number of lives per century that our monopoly saved, and I will stand in front of any court in the realm and defend them."

"The ledgers don't count the anchor," Yara said quietly. "One life per generation, crossed off the books before the ink dries. Me. And the women before me. Bound without consent, reborn without rest, and none of us ever told." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part of her, he was beginning to see. "That's not order, Warden-Commander. That's theft with good handwriting."

"Perhaps." He let the word sit there, alone, because he had learned that conceding a small point bought you the right to keep the large ones. "Now ask me why I haven't arrested you."

"Why haven't you?"

"Because arresting you solves nothing and costs everything." He leaned forward. "Listen to me carefully, because I am going to offer you the only honest bargain anyone has offered you since the day you were born — and yes, I include your master in that. Aldous lied to you with great tenderness for twenty years. I intend to tell you ugly truths with none at all."

* * *

He laid it out the way he laid out everything: plainly, in order, with the costs named first.

"First. The key. The coral-and-bone key your master gave you — yes, I know he gave it to you, dying men are terrible at secrets. It is the instrument by which the covenant is opened, amended, or sealed. In the wrong hands it is a weapon. In the Tide Regent's hands it is a leash around the neck of every dry land in the realm. In yours—" he paused, deliberately, "—in yours it is a burden you were never trained to carry, no matter whose face you wear.

"Second. The child. Tam is a plea made flesh, sent up from the drowned court to find the anchor and wake her early. The court is dying, the covenant is failing, and everything the guild built for two hundred years is coming apart whether either of us likes it or not. I cannot stop that. Neither can you. But what comes *after* — that is still negotiable.

"Third. My offer." He counted it off on his fingers, unhurried. "Restoration. The Saltbound Guild rechartered under your name — Ravensworth, the true line, the anchor's own — with me as its steward until you're ready. Legitimacy. No more running, no more wardens at your heels, the brand of thief struck from every ledger in the realm. Safety for the boy: wardens' protection, real protection, for as long as he lives. And a seat at the table when the new terms are written — because new terms *will* be written, Yara, the old world is already dead, and the only question left is who holds the pen."

He sat back. "In exchange, you give me the key, and you come home. To the valley. To the salt line. To the work."

* * *

For a long moment she said nothing, and Corvane — who had negotiated with bandit kings and archmages and once, memorably, a river spirit with a grievance — recognized the silence for what it was. Not refusal. Temptation. He had chosen this room, this hour, this weariness of hers, with care. She was tired. She was grieving. She had learned in the space of a fortnight that her entire life was a lie stitched together by people who loved her, and he was offering her the one thing no outlaw, no hedge-witch, no drowned king could give her:

Permission to stop.

"That's it?" she said finally. "I hand you the key and everything goes back to how it was?"

"Not how it was. Better than it was. You'd have what Aldous never gave you — the truth, and a vote." He spread his hands. "I won't pretend the guild was innocent. It wasn't. It stole, it hid, it bound a woman's soul to a fence post for two hundred years and called it duty. I have read those pages too, and I did not enjoy them. But Yara — hear me — the alternative to imperfect order is not justice. It's the Sundering with better manners. Every village lighting its own fires, every hedge-witch and warlord and desperate father reaching for power no one taught them to hold. You've met the Unsworn. Ask Sefa what happens to the ones who reach too deep. Ask her why she has graves."

"Sefa doesn't bind children."

"No," Corvane agreed. "She just loses them at a slower rate and calls it freedom." He softened his voice, and this too was honest, because the last piece of the argument was the truest one he had. "You think I want the key because I hunger for it. I don't. I'm fifty-three years old, I have bad knees, and I would happily hand the whole burden to someone worthier tomorrow. I want it because *someone must hold it*, and the someone who holds it must be answerable to law, to record, to succession — to something sturdier than charisma and kindness. Kindness is a wonderful foundation, Mistress Ravensworth. I've seen what gets built on it. Usually a hospital. Occasionally a pyre. You never know which until the weather turns."

Tam had stopped drawing. He was watching Corvane with eyes far too old for his face, and the tea leaves on the table had begun, very faintly, to tremble — arranging themselves, Corvane realized with that cold walking up his spine again, into letters. Old letters. He looked away from them firmly, the way you look away from a precipice.

"You believe all this," Yara said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation either, which somehow made it worse. "You actually believe you're the reasonable one."

"I *am* the reasonable one," Corvane said. "That's what frightens you. If I were a monster, you could fight me with a clean heart. But I'm a tired man doing arithmetic with lives, and my sums are better than yours, because I've been doing them longer." He rose, straightened his cloak, and set a small pouch on the table between them — dried apples, purchased at Marta's stall, wormholes and all. "You don't need to answer today. Think about the boy's safety. Think about sleeping indoors. Think about how heavy that key gets, night after night, knowing every faction in the realm wants it and only you are standing between them and it.

"But know this, and know it plainly." He paused at the door, and for a moment the courteous steward fell away and she saw the thing underneath, the thing that had climbed thirty years of ledgers and corpses to this room: not cruelty. Conviction, hard as coral. "If you refuse me, I will not stop hunting the key. I will do whatever the preservation of order requires, and I will sleep soundly afterward, because that is what I am *for*. I would genuinely prefer you at my side, Yara Ravensworth. But I built my life to survive losing things I preferred."

He opened the door onto the market noise, the cart and the dog and the bad singer, and stepped through.

At the threshold he stopped, because Tam had tugged his sleeve — the first time the boy had touched him — and held up one small palm. On it, drawn in tea-stain, was a single symbol Corvane knew from forbidden pages: the old sign for *debt*.

The child closed his fist around it and looked at him without fear, and Corvane, Warden-Commander of the Saltbound Guild, keeper of seals and ledgers and two hundred years of necessary lies, found — to his considerable irritation — that he could not meet the boy's eyes.

He walked out into the sunlight and gave the order to his wardens, quietly, the way all the worst orders are given:

"Follow them. And when she refuses — and she will refuse, they always refuse at first — take the child."
