## 12. The Refusal

Corvane arrived the way winter arrives — not all at once, but with a certainty that made everything before him feel like it had only been pretending to be real.

Yara saw him first from the ridge above the Unsworn's camp, where she'd gone to fetch water and, if she was honest with herself, to be alone for a few minutes. She had not been alone in three weeks. Brannoc snored like a landslide. Sefa asked questions that were really knives wearing questions' clothing. And Tam — Tam had taken to following her everywhere, silent as a shadow, watching her with those drowned-deep eyes that made her feel simultaneously chosen and haunted.

So: the ridge. The water skins. Ten minutes of pretending she was a girl on a walk instead of a fugitive with a stolen name.

Then the wardens came up the valley road below her, six of them in grey-and-salt livery, and at their head a tall man on a dun horse who did not need to announce himself because the world seemed to rearrange itself around his importance. Warden Corvane. She knew him the way you know a scar you've never seen: by reputation, by the burned guildhall behind her, by the portrait of her own face two hundred years old that still lived folded in her pack like a wound she couldn't stop touching.

He looked up and found her immediately, which told her he'd known exactly where she'd be.

"Apprentice Ravensworth," he called, and his voice carried the way good sermons carry — warm at the edges, iron underneath. "Or should I say *Anchor*?"

* * *

They let her come down alone, which she understood later was the point. A negotiation required the appearance of choice.

The camp had emptied ahead of the wardens — Sefa's people melted into the treeline like they'd rehearsed it, which of course they had — but Brannoc stood her ground at the fire pit with her arms crossed and her chin up, radiating the particular defiance of a woman who had already lost everything once and found it survivable. Tam stood beside her, small and stubborn.

Corvane dismounted. Up close he was younger than Yara expected — forty perhaps, silver coming in early at his temples, a face built for portraits in guildhalls that no longer burned. He studied her with an expression she recognized, horribly, from Aldous. The look of a man appraising something he believed belonged to him.

"You've had a hard road," he said. It wasn't mockery. That was the worst part. He sounded genuinely sympathetic. "Burned out of your home. Hunted through three counties. Bound to a child who cannot tell you why he came to you." He glanced at Tam with something almost gentle. "You were never meant to carry this, Yara. None of it. The guild failed you long before I ever learned your name."

"The guild burned," Yara said. "That was your learning."

"My *correction*," Corvane said. "There's a difference, though I understand it's difficult to see from inside the smoke." He walked a slow half-circle around the fire pit, hands clasped behind his back, a man giving himself a tour of his own argument. "Let me tell you what I see when I look at this situation. I see a covenant older than kingdoms, fraying. I see a realm where every hedge-witch and river-charmer is about to discover they can do things the law says they can't. I see famine and fire and frightened people burning each other over a drop of power." He stopped. Looked at her directly. "And I see one person standing between all of that and the old order that kept it contained for six hundred years."

"And that person is me."

"That person," Corvane said, "was always going to be you. You are the anchor. The covenant lives in your bones whether you consent or not — that was true the day you were born, and true every day before it. The only question is who stands beside you while you bear it." He spread his hands. "I'm offering the guild restored. Your name cleared — you're branded a thief in nine jurisdictions, incidentally; I can fix that by morning. A place for the boy, protected, fed, taught. And order. Real order, not the Unsworn's charming chaos."

Yara thought about it. That was the shameful thing, the thing she would turn over at night for weeks afterward: she actually thought about it. Her feet ached. Her magic-sickness had come back twice since the tollhouse, leaving her shaking and blind-spotted, and Brannoc's remedies only blunted it. Somewhere behind her ribs the anchor-weight hummed its low constant note, and she was so tired of carrying it without instruction, without help, without anyone who'd chosen to stand beside her rather than behind her or against her.

"Say the terms plainly," she said. "All of them."

Corvane smiled like a man whose fish had taken the hook. "You come to the new guildhall at Greywater. You surrender the coral key. In exchange, the covenant continues as it has always continued — anchored, sealed, maintained. The boy stays under guild protection."

"And the anchor bond itself?"

"Continues," Corvane said simply. "It must. That's what an anchor *is*, Yara. Something that holds because it cannot leave."

"There it is," Brannoc said quietly.

"There what is?" Corvane didn't look at her.

"The comfortable injustice." Brannoc spat into the fire. "Wrapped up so neat you could gift it."

"I'm offering her *safety*—"

"You're offering her the same cage with a fresh coat of paint," Brannoc said. "I've seen your kind of safety, Warden. It looks a lot like a cell with the door painted shut."

Corvane turned to Yara fully then, and here was the thing about him — he didn't get angry. He got *reasonable*. "They will use you," he said. "Sefa and her outlaws. They'll dress it up in talk of freedom, but freedom is expensive and someone always ends up paying, and it will be you. The key, the anchor-bond, the Old Tongue — they want all of it, and you are the delivery mechanism. At least with the guild, you know exactly what you'll be. With them?" He shrugged, eloquent. "You'll find out after it's done to you."

Yara looked at Tam.

Tam looked back at her, and lifted one hand, and pressed it flat against his own chest — over his heart, the way he'd done the first night in the valley, the night Aldous burned his records and everything ended. It had become his wordless vocabulary over the weeks. *Here. This. What matters.*

She had given him bread at the valley door. Before duty. Before prophecy. Before any of it. Just bread, because he was hungry and cold and small.

"Here's my refusal," Yara said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "No."

Corvane's face didn't change. But something behind it did — a door closing, a ledger being balanced. "I was afraid you'd say that."

* * *

Afterward, lying in the wet grass with blood in her mouth, Yara would reconstruct it in fragments, because the whole of it refused to stay whole.

She remembered Corvane saying, softly, "Then I'll take what I need and leave what I don't," and the wardens moving — not charging, just moving, the way weather moves. She remembered grabbing for Tam and getting a fistful of his shirt before a warden's staff took her across the jaw and the world went white and sideways. She remembered Tam screaming — *not* mute, never mute, screaming in the Old Tongue, words that made the grass frost over and the fire gutter blue — and wardens stumbling back with their hands over their ears, and Corvane shouting "The boy! Take the boy, leave the rest!"

She remembered Brannoc's voice rising into words Yara didn't know but felt in her teeth, outlawed craft, forbidden craft, the kind Aldous would have burned a woman for — and the darkness folding around the campsite like a struck match going out.

She remembered Tam's hand tearing free of hers.

She remembered the hoofbeats fading down the valley road, and the silence after, which was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

* * *

She woke because Brannoc was doing something to her face that hurt more than the original injury, which took effort.

"Don't," Yara managed.

"Hold still. You've a crack in your cheekbone I could sail a boat through, and I'm the only healer for forty miles who isn't legally allowed to heal." Brannoc's hands moved over her face, and warmth followed them — wrong warmth, deep warmth, the kind that made the anchor-hum in Yara's chest stutter and lean toward it like a plant toward sun. "This is going to itch. Then it's going to ache. Then you're going to be furious, which will be an improvement, because right now you're doing the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"The thing where you go somewhere inside yourself and don't come back." Brannoc sat back on her heels. Her face was grey with exhaustion — the craft had cost her, Yara realized; it always cost her, and she paid anyway. "Talk to me, girl."

"He took him." Yara's voice came out cracked down the middle. "I had him by the shirt. I had him, and I — I was too slow, I was always too slow, Aldous used to say—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "He's a child. He crossed half the realm to find me. He gave up his voice for— whatever he gave it up for. And I gave him to a man who thinks people are *load-bearing*."

Brannoc said nothing for a moment. She poked the fire back to life with unnecessary violence.

"When my daughter died," she said, "I spent the first year looking for someone to have prevented it. Someone must have known. Someone must have chosen wrongly. Someone must be guilty, because if someone was guilty, then the world was still a place with rules, and rules could be learned, and next time I could—" She shook her head. "There was no someone. There was just a fever and a bad winter. But I understand the arithmetic you're doing right now. *If I had been faster. If I had refused sooner. If.*"

"It's not arithmetic," Yara whispered. "He held my hand."

"Yes," Brannoc said. "And now we get it back."

Yara turned her head — carefully; her cheekbone sang a bright note of protest — and looked at the older woman through the firelight. "We?"

"Well, *I* certainly am. You're welcome to lie here cataloguing your failures, but I've a reputation as a terrible listener." Brannoc hauled herself up, joints cracking like green wood, and began kicking dirt over the fire with the efficiency of a woman who had fled from many camps in a hurry. "Sefa's people will circle back within the hour — they always do; guilt is a homing pigeon. And there's a road south from here that doesn't appear on any warden's map, because the Unsworn keep it that way. It runs to the coast. To the sea caves." She paused. "To the drowned gate."

Yara pushed herself upright. The world tilted, then agreed to hold still. "The Regent's court. You think Tam's been taken *there*?"

"I think Corvane wants the covenant, and the covenant's front door is at the bottom of the sea, and he's got the key now—" She stopped. Frowned. "Wait. Has he? Did you give him the key?"

"No." Yara fumbled at her collar and drew out the cord. The broken key of coral and bone hung there, dull and salt-scoured, humming faintly against her skin. "I never gave it to anyone. It doesn't leave me. I don't know why. It just — won't."

Something moved across Brannoc's face — wonder, quickly buried under practicality. "Then Corvane's taken a hostage for a door he can't open yet. Which means we have time. Not much. But some." She offered her arm, and Yara took it, and together they stood up out of the ashes of another burned camp — Yara's third, now; she was collecting them like a grim hobby.

"Sefa wanted the key as payment for alliance," Yara said.

"Sefa wanted a lot of things. People do, out here." Brannoc squinted south, toward the dark line of hills and, beyond them, a distance Yara could smell — brine and kelp and something older. "But she also lost two people keeping you alive, and she hasn't sold you out yet, and in my experience that's worth at least one conversation."

* * *

Sefa found them an hour later, exactly as Brannoc predicted, materializing out of the treeline with six of her people and an expression like a storm deciding whether to be insulted.

"They took the boy," Yara said, before Sefa could speak. "Corvane. Heading south, I'd guess — heavy horses, wounded pride, one prisoner who frosts the ground when he screams."

"We saw them pass the ridge at dusk." Sefa swung down from her horse. Up close she was thinner than her reputation, all wire and weather, with eyes that did arithmetic constantly. "Nine wardens now. He's pulled from every garrison between here and Greywater. Whatever he plans, he plans it soon."

"Then we go faster."

"*We*." Sefa's mouth twitched — not quite a smile. "An hour ago you wouldn't give me the key. Now it's *we*."

"It was never about the key." Yara's voice surprised her by not shaking. "It was about not trading one cage for another. And I've thought about it, Sefa, on the road, every night — and I still don't entirely trust you, and you don't trust me, and I think maybe that's fine. Maybe trust isn't the price of walking the same direction." She lifted the cord over her head and held the key out on her open palm — not handing it over, just showing it, letting its faint hum speak for itself. "Come with us to the drowned gate. Help me take Tam back. And when the covenant gets rewritten — and it *will* get rewritten — you'll be in the room where it happens. Not outside it. Not beneath it. In it."

Sefa looked at the key for a long time. Then at Yara.

"You sound different," she said finally. "Less like a guild-brat and more like trouble."

"I've had a very educational month."

Sefa laughed — short, rusty, genuine — and turned to her people. "Saddle up. South road. Anyone who's tired can stay and explain to the wardens why they didn't." Nobody stayed.

Brannoc boosted Yara onto a spare horse, and Yara bit down on a cry as her cheekbone flared, and Brannoc muttered, "Heal fast, girl, you owe me a fortune," and Yara thought: *Aldous is dead. The guildhall is ash. My name is a brand and my face is two hundred years old and the boy who chose me is in a cage.*

And beneath all of it, steady as bedrock, a voice she was only now beginning to recognize as her own:

*Good. Now there's nothing left to lose except what matters.*

She turned her horse south, toward the sea she had never seen, and rode.
