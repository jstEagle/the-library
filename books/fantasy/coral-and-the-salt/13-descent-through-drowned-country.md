## 13. Descent Through Drowned Country

Sefa had buried friends before. She had learned the trick of it — you dig, you say the words, you keep walking — but she had never learned the trick of burying two in a single morning and still having something left over for supper conversation.

The sunken road took them down through drowned country, where the old highway had given way to marsh and the marsh gave way to water that lay flat as hammered tin. Willows stood knee-deep and patient. Somewhere ahead, the land would end entirely, and the sea would begin, and everything Sefa had promised these people would be put to the test.

She walked at the rear, as she always did, because a leader who walks at the front is only showing the way, and a leader who walks at the rear is watching who falls behind.

Two had fallen behind forever now. Merrin, who could whistle birds down from the trees and once talked a tax collector into paying *him*. And Osla, who was seventeen and had joined the Unsworn because her village fined her mother for healing a neighbor's fever without guild license. Seventeen. Sefa kept saying the number to herself like a stone she couldn't stop turning over.

"You're doing the face," said Brannoc, falling into step beside her.

"What face?"

"The one where you chew on your guilt like gristle because you think swallowing it whole would be too easy."

"I don't have a face like that."

"You absolutely do. I've seen it on every commander I ever served under, right before they did something stupid and noble." Brannoc adjusted the strap of her herb satchel. "It wasn't your fault."

"Two of mine are dead, hedge-witch."

"And if you'd taken the high road instead of the sunken one, it would have been six. The wardens were waiting at the ford. Corvane doesn't send three riders after a fugitive; he sends thirty and a grudge." Brannoc glanced back at the column. "You got the rest of them out. That's not nothing."

Sefa didn't answer. Ahead, Yara Ravensworth walked near the front with Tam's hand in hers, and the girl moved like someone carrying a lantern through her own burning house — careful, determined, and quietly horrified by what the light showed her.

That was the thing Sefa couldn't stop chewing on, gristle or no. Not grief. Grief was an old companion; she knew its habits. It was *Yara*.

The last anchor of the covenant. The living key. The woman whose bloodline had been stolen and bound and reborn for two hundred years so that a handful of guildmasters could decide who was allowed to touch magic. Sefa believed all of it — she'd watched the archive ghost speak, watched Yara's face when the truth came out. But belief and trust were different animals, and only one of them slept soundly.

Because here was the question that sat in Sefa's chest like a swallowed hook: *what happens to a person who learns she was made to be a ruler?*

Some people broke under it. Some people burned it down and walked away. And some people — the dangerous ones — decided the throne had simply been waiting for the right occupant.

* * *

They camped that night on a levee above the flooded fields, and Sefa made herself do the hard part first: the names.

"Merrin Ashfoot. He stole bread for orphans and lied about everything else. Osla Reed. She was seventeen, and she wanted to see the sea." Her voice carried across the fire. "The Unsworn don't forget their own. We'll carry them to the water, and the water will carry them the rest of the way."

Tam tugged at Yara's sleeve and signed something quick. Yara translated softly: "He says the sea remembers everyone who comes to it. He says it told him so once."

A few of the outlaws shifted uncomfortably. A mute child speaking for the ocean was a long way outside most people's sense of the ordinary, even among people who practiced outlawed craft around campfires.

But Yara just nodded at Tam and said, "Then we'll bring them somewhere worth remembering us."

And there it was again. That instinct toward grace, easy as breathing. Sefa watched her across the fire and hated how much she wanted to believe in it.

Later, when the camp had gone quiet except for the frogs and Fenwick's truly heroic snoring, Sefa found Yara sitting alone at the levee's edge, turning the broken coral key over in her hands.

"Can't sleep either?" Sefa asked.

"The key hums when I hold it. Like it knows where it's going." Yara looked up. "Does that frighten you? It frightens me."

"Everything about you frightens me a little," Sefa said, and then winced, because it had come out more honest than intended.

Yara laughed — short, surprised. "Good. You should say that. Everyone else keeps looking at me like I'm already crowned."

"They look at you like that because they want to hope. Hope makes people stupid. I've built a whole movement on it." Sefa sat down beside her, close enough to see the salt-pale line of the scar on Yara's palm where the key had bitten her. "Can I ask you something ugly?"

"You've been circling it for three days. Ask."

"When this is done — when the covenant's remade — what do you want?"

"For the valley? For my home to be safe, and—"

"No. Not the valley. *You.* If someone handed you the old terms tomorrow, the anchor restored, the guild restored, everything lawful and orderly and yours by right of suffering — would you take it?"

Yara was quiet for a long moment. Out in the dark, the flooded fields held the stars so perfectly it was hard to tell where the world ended.

"I spent my whole life being *used*," she said finally. "Before I even knew it. My whole self was somebody else's lock and key. So no. I don't want a throne. I want—" She stopped, frustrated. "I want to never again be the only one holding something. That's all. Whatever we build, I want it built so that no one person can be the whole wall."

Sefa studied her face in the firelight, looking for the lie, the ambition, the small proud corner where a future tyrant gets started. She found none. She found a tired young woman with ash still in her hair from a burned guildhall.

"That's either the truest thing anyone's ever said to me," Sefa said, "or the best-rehearsed."

"You're delightful company, you know that?"

"I've been told worse." She stood, offering a hand up. "Get some sleep, Ravensworth. Tomorrow we walk into the sea, and I'd rather you were awake for it."

* * *

The next day, the wardens found them anyway.

Sefa heard them before she saw them — the particular wet slap of boots on a causeway, moving fast, disciplined. She put two fingers in her mouth and whistled the falling note, and the column dissolved into the reeds like it had rehearsed it, which it had, forty times, though never with a novelist, a child, and a hedge-witch who complained loudly about her knees.

"There!" A warden's voice, cracking across the water. "The ravens mark! Take the Ravensworth woman alive!"

*Alive.* Of course. Corvane needed his anchor breathing. It was the rest of them who were expendable.

They ran the drowned road — a raised spine of ancient stone barely breaching the black water, running straight as a spear toward a horizon that smelled of salt. Behind them, six wardens in grey-and-silver, crossbows up.

"Bran!" Sefa shouted. "The mist!"

"On it!" Brannoc flung a handful of something from her satchel — crushed fen-orchid and bog-myrtle, outlawed craft, the kind of working that got her disgraced — and the air ahead of the wardens curdled into fog thick as wool.

It bought them a mile. Then a bolt hissed past Sefa's ear and struck the stone, and she understood the mile was all they'd get.

"Fenwick, take the left channel with the twins. Draw them wide. We go straight for the gate."

"Sefa—" Fenwick's face was pale. "The channels flood at midday. The tide tables—"

"I know the tide tables, Fen. Go."

He went. She watched him go, and she knew — the way commanders know things they refuse to write down — that the left channel was a gamble, and gambles cost.

They reached the coast as the light went copper. The sea cave yawned at the base of the cliffs exactly as Tam's drawings had promised, its mouth veiled in spray, and inside it — when Yara lifted the coral key — a low note rolled out through the rock like a bell struck underwater.

The gate was opening. But the wardens were still coming, three of them now, closing along the cliff path, and the tide was rising in the channels, and—

The horns told her before any runner could. Two notes, then silence. Then, distantly, one voice raised in the Unsworn's mourning cry, passed from throat to throat along the water until it reached her.

Fenwick. And one of the twins — Pell, the younger one, who owed Sefa a rematch at knucklebones and would never pay it.

The tide had taken the gamble and its price both.

Sefa stood at the cave mouth with salt wind tearing at her and let the grief come through her like weather, because that was the discipline: feel it fully, once, and then put it down. There would be time to mourn properly later, if there was a later.

Yara appeared beside her, Tam clinging to her back, Brannoc limping up behind. The girl's eyes went to Sefa's face and read it instantly.

"Who?" Yara asked.

"Fenwick. Pell." Sefa's voice came out level, which cost her. "Corvane's toll."

"I'm sorry." And the terrible thing, the thing Sefa turned over and over in the hours after, was that Yara sounded like she meant it — not the polished sympathy of a ruler consoling troops, but the raw, unpracticed apology of someone who felt the deaths landing on her own account.

"Don't be sorry," Sefa said. "Be worth it. That's the only tribute I'll accept."

Yara held her gaze. "I intend to be."

And Sefa — who had doubted her for three days, who had chewed that doubt like gristle — found that the doubt hadn't vanished, exactly. But it had changed shape. It was no longer *will she become another ruler?* It was something sharper and more uncomfortable:

*What if she actually does what she promises, and it works, and the world after is one I have to learn to live in?*

Doubt was easy. Hope was the dangerous trade.

Inside the cave, the coral key sang louder, and the water at the cave's heart began to glow — a road of light descending beneath the sea, down toward a court that had been drowning for two hundred years.

"The gate will hold open only while the tide turns," Brannoc said, checking her satchel with grim efficiency. "Maybe an hour. Maybe less."

"Then I go now," Yara said. "Alone. The key answers to me, and if the court sees an army coming through its door, we'll get war instead of terms."

"Absolutely not," Sefa said. "You're the anchor, the heir, the thief, and the prize, depending on which wanted poster you read. You don't walk into a drowned throne room alone."

"She has to," Tam said.

Everyone stopped. The boy's voice — rusty, small, real — hung in the cave air like a struck bell.

"The court called *me*," Tam said, gripping Yara's hand. "It will call *her* the same way. Alone means honest. Alone means—" he searched for the word, and found it in the Old Tongue, and translated it himself, carefully, like setting down something fragile, "—unarmed."

Sefa looked at the glowing water. At the boy who had spoken his first words in days to defend the woman beside him. At Yara, who was already loosening her pack straps, already becoming the person who walks down.

"One hour," Sefa said. "If you're not back, I'm coming down after you with everyone I have left, terms be damned."

"Fair," said Yara. And she smiled — tired, terrified, certain — and stepped onto the road beneath the sea, and the water closed over her like a door.

Sefa stood watch at the mouth of the drowned gate, counting the tide, counting her dead, counting the minutes.

Behind her, far up the cliff path, torches flickered. Wardens. More than before. Corvane had found the gate too — and he was bringing an army to a negotiation.

"One hour," Sefa muttered to the sea. "Don't make a liar of me."
