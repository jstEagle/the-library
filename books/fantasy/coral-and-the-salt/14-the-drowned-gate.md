## 14. The Drowned Gate

The sea announced itself long before we saw it. It came first as a smell — salt and weed and something colder underneath, something that made the back of my teeth ache — and then as a sound, a vast slow breathing beyond the hills, in and out, in and out, patient as a sleeping animal.

I had never heard the sea. I had never smelled it. And yet some part of me, some part I did not have a name for yet, recognized it the way you recognize a face from a dream you keep having and have stopped mentioning to anyone.

"You've gone quiet," Brannoc said, falling into step beside me. "Quieter. You've gone quieter."

"I'm trying to decide if I'm terrified or excited."

"Ah." She nodded sagely. "The classic dilemma. In my experience the answer is usually yes."

Behind us, Sefa's outlaws made camp-sounds in the lee of the last hill — canvas snapping, a cook-pot clanking, someone laughing at someone else's expense. Tam walked ahead of all of us, as he had for the past two days, drawn forward on an invisible line like a fish on a hook, except the hook was pulling from inside him. He hadn't looked back once. Children are very good at walking toward things; we adults do all our walking away.

The coral key hung against my chest on its cord of braided hair — Aldous's hair, though I hadn't known whose it was when I strung it there, and now I was glad I hadn't. It had been growing warmer as we traveled west. Warmer, heavier, more *aware*. Some nights it pulsed against my skin like a second heartbeat, slightly out of time with mine, and I lay awake listening to the two rhythms argue about which one was real.

"There," Tam said.

He didn't say it aloud, of course. Tam didn't speak — not yet, not in any way I could hear — but lately his meaning arrived in my head anyway, soft as a thought that might have been my own. It had started three days ago, after the wardens' ambush at the ford, when he had pressed both hands flat against my face and stared at me with those gray drowned eyes, and since then his voice came through sometimes like light through fog. *There*, he said again, and pointed.

The hills ended.

They simply gave up, folding down into a shore of dark stone, and beyond it the sea went out to meet a sky the color of a bruise healing. Gulls wheeled and screamed their small grievances. Waves rolled in and unspooled into foam at the tideline, over and over, the world's oldest argument being won and lost and won again.

And set into the cliff-face below us, half-drowned by the tide, was a cave mouth.

It wasn't remarkable, at that distance. A black arch, white-fringed water sucking at its base. But the key went hot against my sternum — hot and singing, a thin pure note I felt rather than heard — and every crystal of salt in my pack-pockets hummed in answer, and Tam turned to look up at me with an expression I could only call *welcome*.

"Well," said Brannoc, "that's ominous."

"It's beautiful," I said.

"I said what I said."

* * *

We made camp above the tideline, out of reach of the spring tide Sefa swore would come tonight. She built the fire herself, which apparently was something leaders among the Unsworn did — no ceremony, no distance, just Sefa kneeling in the sand with flint and steel, cursing cheerfully at damp kindling until it caught.

"So this is it," she said, when we were all gathered around the flames with stew in our hands. "The Drowned Gate. My grandmother sang about it. Thought it was a story." She glanced at me. "Most of what I thought was a story has turned out to be a guild crime, so at least the disappointment is consistent."

"The gate opens for the key," I said. "I think. The songs the Regent's people left in the archive ghost's memory were clear enough about that much. What happens after—" I shrugged. "After is less documented."

"After," Brannoc said, pointing her spoon at me, "is where you walk into a hole in the world full of ocean, alone, because — and correct me if I'm wrong — only one person can enter."

"The key chose—"

"The key didn't choose anyone. The key is a bit of coral and an old man's bone. *Someone* chose, generations ago, and it wasn't you, and I'd thank you to remember the difference." Her voice was gentle even when her words weren't; that was Brannoc's way. She patched people whether they wanted patching or not. "You go down there tonight, and whatever's waiting doesn't have to be friendly just because it's destiny."

"I know."

"And you'll come back up."

I looked at the fire instead of at her. "I intend to."

"*Intend*," Sefa repeated, to the sky. "Gods preserve us from people with intentions. All right. Here's what happens. We hold the beach until dawn. If the tide does something wrong, if your light goes out, if anything comes out of that cave wearing a warden's coat — Wenna's team takes you two north over the headland, and I burn the entrance shut behind you. Agreed?"

"That seems extreme," I said.

"That seems *necessary*," Sefa said. "You're carrying the only thing in the world that can fix what Corvane wants broken. You don't get to be brave and irreplaceable at the same time. Pick one."

Nobody argued with Sefa. People had tried; there was a man named Dorrit who still limped, though honestly that had more to do with the mule.

Tam tugged my sleeve. When I looked down he held up a flattened piece of bread from his ration, solemnly, the way he'd once received bread at my valley door — except reversed now, him giving, me receiving. His meaning drifted up through the fog between us: *for the road. In case.*

"In case," I agreed, my throat tight, and took it.

* * *

The tide turned at moonrise, and the cave opened.

That sounds like poetry but it was mechanics, mostly — the sea withdrawing down the shingle with a long hiss, baring the black arch, leaving it dripping and gleaming like the mouth of some enormous beached leviathan. But it didn't feel like mechanics. It felt like a held breath finally released. The key at my chest stopped pulsing and began instead to *pull*, gently, steadily, a thread of longing tied somewhere behind my ribs.

Inside the arch, where there should have been darkness, there was light. Pale gold-green, moving, the color of sunlit shallows seen from beneath — coming from nowhere, illuminating nothing, simply present, waiting.

The others walked me down to the entrance, which I found touching and also faintly ridiculous, like a funeral procession that kept insisting it wasn't one. Brannoc checked my pack straps twice. Sefa stood with her arms crossed, watching the sea. Tam held my hand and would not let go, and I knelt in the wet sand to be at his eye level.

"You could tell me not to go," I said. "If you know something I don't. You could tell me."

His meaning rose slow and careful, a child choosing words for something too big for them:

*The court is dying. The court is waiting. You are the door and the door-opener both. I can't tell you not to go. Nobody could ever tell you not to go. That's what they made you for — but what you do with it is yours.*

"That's not comforting," I said, "but it's honest, and I find I prefer that lately."

He kissed the top of my head, which is a thing children do when they mean to bless you, and let go of my hand.

Brannoc pressed something into my palm at the threshold: a tiny stoppered vial, warm from her pocket. "Willow-bark, ginger, and three things I'm not licensed to name anymore. For magic-sickness. Chew, don't swallow whole. And Yara—" She caught my shoulder before I could turn. "When you get down there, wherever *there* is, and you're standing in front of whatever passes for a throne — remember that you're not representing the guild. They'll expect you to. Guilds and courts understand each other; it's all doors and tolls and who may pass. Don't negotiate like them. You're representing the woman who shared her bread with a stranger's child. That's the whole of your authority. That's more than any of them ever had."

I hugged her. She pretended to endure it.

Then I stepped through the arch, and the sea closed over the doorway behind me, and I did not drown.

* * *

Here is what nobody had told me, perhaps because nobody knew: the road beneath the water was not underwater. Or rather, I was underwater, and the water was not around me so much as *above* me, curved overhead like the roof of a cathedral, and I was breathing it.

My first breath was panic. Of course it was. Every body is trained by its whole life to know what water means, and mine screamed *surface, surface* while I stood on a road of pale stone that sloped gently down into green-gold deeps. But the breath came anyway, easy as air, tasting of salt and something sweeter underneath — rain, almost, or melon, or the smell of a storm arriving over dry land. Salt and light. I breathed salt and light, and my panic had nothing to stand on, and gradually it sat down and sulked.

Above me, fish moved like weather. Schools of silver turned all at once, wheeling shadows across the lit ceiling of the sea, and where the deeper water began the colors changed — blues going indigo going a darkness that was not empty but *full*, full of lights of its own, drifting constellations, lantern-creatures blinking their slow gossip at one another.

"Oh," I said, and my voice came out strange, rounded by the water-carried sound, and a curious cod the size of my boot regarded me with frank disdain for disturbing the peace.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Everything I'd ever written about the sea, everything I'd imagined in the valley with my nose pressed to the boundary of the only world I was allowed — none of it had prepared me for how *alive* it would be, how unbothered, how busy with its own affairs. I had thought of the deep as a place things drowned. It was a place things lived. Drowning was just our word, the drylanders' word, for arriving here without permission.

The road bore me down. The key pulled. And slowly, as the last daylight failed overhead, the loneliness arrived.

It came quietly, the way it always did. It was an old companion — I'd known it in the valley, in the guildhall with its four remaining members and three of them ill, in every night I'd lain awake wanting a life bigger than the one assigned to me. But this was different. This was loneliness with a horizon to it. Because the road was mine alone — the key allowed no other, Brannoc's warnings echoed behind me, Tam's gray eyes, Sefa's arms crossed against the tide — and the understanding settled over me with the weight of all this water: *some doors only one person can enter.* Whatever waited at the bottom, I would meet it with no hand in mine. Whatever I agreed to down there, I would agree to alone. There was a terrible fairness in that, and a terrible coldness too, and I suspected — with the key humming against my heart like a tuning fork struck years ago and never quite stopping — that the fairness and the coldness had been designed together, by the same people, a long time ago.

"No," I said to the water, to myself, to the dead who'd built this road. "You don't get to make loneliness into law. Not this time."

A lantern-fish drifted close, considered me with one glowing eye, and drifted off, visibly unimpressed by rhetoric.

The road steepened. And then the darkness ahead broke open like a sunrise happening in the wrong direction, and I saw the city.

It spread below me in the deep — spires of coral white and rose and blood-red, towers grown rather than built, streets that followed the old bones of streets some human town had once laid down before the sea took it and improved upon it. Windows glowed. Bridges arched between towers, hung with gardens of swaying anemone. Figures moved along them — tall, pale, graceful, cloaked in kelp and pearl-light — and I saw chariots drawn by nothing visible, and banners that rippled in the current though there was no wind, and at the center of it all, rising from a great ring of white coral like a candle-flame from a holder, a throne.

The Coral Throne. The drowned court. Real, alive, luminous, and — now that I was close enough to see — flickering. The lights in the highest spires guttered like candles in a draft. Whole streets had gone dark, their gardens drooping, bleached bone-white. The city was beautiful the way a feverish face is beautiful: something burning underneath.

The key sang, one clear note, and every window in the city turned its light toward me at once.

At the foot of the road, flanked by guards whose spears were made of living urchin-spine, stood a figure taller than any I had ever seen, robed in the black-green of deep water, crowned with a circlet of nacre that caught and bent the city's light. Ancient eyes. Grieving eyes. Eyes that had been waiting a very long time.

The Tide Regent looked up the road at me, and spoke, and the words arrived inside my skull in the Old Tongue, translated as they came, heavy as anchors:

*"So. The thief returns at last — after two hundred years — to look her debt in the face."*

Behind me, impossibly far above, the surface was a coin of fading light, and my name — whatever my name truly was — sank down through the dark like a stone.
