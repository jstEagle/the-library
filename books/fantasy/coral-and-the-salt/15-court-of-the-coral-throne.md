## 15. Court of the Coral Throne

The drowned road ended where all old roads end: at a door.

It should not have been a door. It was a door of giant clamshell, taller than three men, its edges crusted with pink coral and hung with curtains of living kelp that swayed though there was no current this deep. The light here did not come from above — the surface was a rumor, a pale memory far overhead — but from the water itself, which glowed a soft green-gold, like sunlight remembered rather than seen.

And I was breathing it.

That still caught me sideways every few minutes. I would be walking, thinking my small human thoughts, and then I would remember that I was four hundred feet beneath the sea with no air in my lungs and no distress in my chest, only salt and light moving through me as if I were another kind of fish. My body had accepted it before my mind agreed to the arrangement. Bodies are braver than we give them credit for.

The key — coral and bone, small enough to close in my fist — pulsed against my palm like a second heartbeat.

"Right," I said to no one, because Tam was not with me and Brannoc was not with me and Sefa and her outlaws were a day's climb behind me on the cliffs. I had walked down alone, as the door required. "Hello, sea. I've come about the covenant."

The kelp curtains parted.

* * *

I had imagined a throne room, because I am my master's apprentice and my master's masters built halls to make people feel small. I imagined pillars, banners, rows of courtiers with cold eyes.

What I got was a garden.

The court spread out around me like a city that had decided, over centuries, to become a reef instead. Towers of coral grew in spirals and fans, in colors I did not have names for — colors that existed only down here, where the sun never came to wash them out. Between them lay streets of white sand scattered with shells, and gardens of swaying anemone, and arches of fused pearl through which schools of small silver fish moved like weather. Light pooled everywhere, sourceless and gentle. It was beautiful in the way a sleeping child is beautiful, and like a sleeping child, something about it made my chest ache with worry.

Because it was dying. You could see it if you looked. Whole towers stood bleached bone-white, their color gone. Gardens drooped. In the streets, figures moved slowly — too slowly — and when they turned to look at me, their faces had the translucent quality of things half-faded from an old painting. Some of them were beautiful beyond describing. Some of them had been beautiful once. All of them looked tired the way Aldous had looked tired at the end, tired down in the marrow.

They watched me pass. None spoke. A child with a fish's tail tugged at her mother's sleeve and pointed at me, and the mother pulled her gently back into a doorway of nacre, and I understood that I was the strangest thing these people had seen in a long time, and possibly the most hated, and I kept walking.

At the heart of the court rose the throne.

Not a chair. A tree. A great branching growth of red-and-gold coral, grown — or grown into — the shape of a seat, wide enough for someone very large or very old or both. Kelp draped it like robes. And upon it sat the Tide Regent.

I will try to describe him honestly, though honesty is hard when you are afraid and awed at once. He was old the way the sea is old; not wrinkled and bent, but worn smooth, feature by feature, until what remained was less a face than a coastline. His skin held the shifting gray-green of deep water. His eyes were the eyes of something that had watched continents drift. Around his shoulders lay a cloak that was not cloth but tide, moving in slow pull and release, and in his hands — broad, barnacled, patient hands — he held a piece of broken shell, turning it over and over as if he had been turning it over for centuries and intended to continue.

"Yara Ravensworth," he said. His voice was the sound waves make inside a cave. "Last of the Saltbound. You are smaller than the last one."

"The last one," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, "was me."

"I know." Something moved across the worn-smooth face that might once have been a smile. "I know better than anyone alive or drowned. That is rather the point of you."

* * *

He did not rise. He gestured, and a bench of coral shaped itself from the sand beside the throne — shaped itself *for* me, sized to me, which meant he had been expecting me long enough to measure me, and I did not enjoy thinking about what that implied. I sat anyway. My legs were grateful. Fear is exhausting in ways sleep does not fix.

"Tam," I said. "Where is he?"

"Safe. As safe as anything is, here." The Regent tilted his head, and somewhere in the garden-city a bell made of conch rang twice, soft. "You care for him. Good. He chose well, then, even if choosing was the one thing we denied him."

"He's a child. He didn't choose anything. He washed up on our door half-drowned and mute—"

"He arrived," said the Regent, "because we sent him. The last act of a drowning court is to send out a plea. Do you know what he is, Yara Ravensworth?"

"A plea," I said. Aldous had told me, in his final rattling hours. But saying it aloud in this place made it different, heavier, true in the way things are only true in front of witnesses.

"He is the realm's letter, written in a language your guild forbade anyone to speak. He is Old Tongue given legs and a hunger for bread. Every word he cannot say is a term of the covenant, and every day your salt line holds, another term goes unsaid." The Regent set down the shell fragment. It was coral, I saw now. Broken coral. Its twin sat in my fist. "Come. Before you argue with me — and you will argue, your kind always argues — come and read the letter yourself."

* * *

He led me through the garden-city, and the fading folk withdrew from our path, and I hated every step of it — hated being walked through a dying place like a visiting dignitary while its people watched me with the flat patience of the betrayed.

We came to a low hall built of mother-of-pearl, its walls curved like the inside of a shell, and on those walls, written in lines of glowing script that predated any alphabet I knew—

"There," said the Regent. "Read it."

"I can't read Old Tongue."

"You are the anchor," he said simply. "Yes, you can."

And I could. That was the horror of it. The moment I looked at the script, meaning rose in it like heat off summer stone, as if the words had been waiting two hundred years specifically for my eyes. I read standing up because sitting felt wrong. I read aloud, because some things need to be said in front of witnesses, and my voice shook, and I did not stop.

*I heard myself say:* **The magic of the world is a common fire. No hand may claim the hearth. Every soul may warm itself and tend the flame; the anchor keeps the boundary between the deep and the dry so that neither drowns the other; the anchor serves by consent, for one life, freely given, and is honored, named, and mourned.**

I stopped. The hall echoed with shell-silence.

"Honored, named, and mourned," I repeated. "One life. Freely given."

"Yes," said the Regent.

"And the Saltbound Guild—"

"Took her," he said, with no heat at all, which was somehow worse than fury. "Your guild's founders came to this court in the year your kingdoms call 412, begging aid against the sorcery-wars that were burning the dry lands. We gave them terms. One anchor, consented, to hold the line. Your Aldous's great-great-grandmother's grandmother — a fisherwoman named Maren, who volunteered, who was *honored*, whose name was sung — she anchored the first covenant, and when her one life ended, the covenant asked the deep for another volunteer, as was written."

The Regent picked up his broken coral again. Turned it.

"And your guild decided," he went on, in that cave-voice, level as low tide, "that asking was undignified. That waiting for volunteers was inefficient. That a covenant was safer, tidier, more *manageable*, if the anchor never died — if she was simply reborn, bound before birth, nameless, generation after generation, and told nothing. Not that she served. Not that she existed. They hid you inside your own life, Yara Ravensworth. Two hundred years. Twelve of you. Each one born, used as a keystone, and buried under a false name in a valley that was built around her like a lock around a key."

The hall seemed to tilt. I put my hand against the pearly wall to steady myself, and the wall was cool and faintly pulsing, alive, and I thought: *even the walls consented to be here more than I did.*

"Twelve," I managed.

"You are the twelfth. The first eleven lie in your valley's graveyard under names your guild invented. I have kept count. Someone should." He looked at me then, fully, and the grief in that ancient face was not a wave but a standing ocean. "I do not tell you this to wound you. I tell you because you asked to know, and because knowing is the beginning of repair, and because—" his voice cracked, one hairline fracture in all that stone, "—because we are dying, child, and I have run out of time to be gentle."

* * *

He took me to Tam after that. I think he understood that I could not hear another word until I saw the boy breathing.

They kept him in a chamber of woven light at the top of a coral spire — not a cell, whatever they called it, because a cage is a cage however pretty the bars. He sat cross-legged on a bed of kelp, drawing in the sand floor with one finger: the same drawings, always. Our valley door. Bread. Me. And around the edges of everything, faint as watermark, the outlines were beginning to fade — his lines losing their ink, his edges going soft, a candle burned low.

"Tam!"

He lit up — actually lit up, a faint glow rising in his skin like a lamp behind parchment — and scrambled toward me, and I gathered him up and held on, and he was lighter than he should have been. Noticeably lighter. Like holding a bundle of dried reeds instead of a boy.

"What's happening to him?" I asked over his head.

"He is made of the covenant's terms," the Regent said from the doorway. "The court is made of the covenant's power. The guild starves us both from the same theft. When we fade, he fades; when he fades—" the shrug of a creature who has watched too many things end. "He has perhaps a season. Perhaps less. Your guild's wardens hoard the stolen flow behind your salt line, feeding it to their own petty works, and the interest compounds, and here we grow transparent." He nodded at Tam without judgment, which I found unbearable. "The boy eats nothing because there is nothing here left to eat. The gardens go first, then the young, then the old. We have been dining on ourselves for two centuries, Yara Ravensworth, politely, in order of age."

Tam pulled back and looked at my face and then, with his finger, wrote in the air between us — and the Old Tongue rose off his fingertips in visible script, glowing, and I read it the way I'd read the walls:

**You came.**

"Of course I came," I said, and my voice broke finally, properly, and I let it. "I'm sorry it took so long. I'm sorry about — all of it. Everything. The whole stupid rotten two hundred years of it."

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, then wrote again:

**Bread?**

And despite everything — despite the dying court and the fading child and the twelve graves in my valley — I laughed. A wet, cracked laugh, but a real one. "I'll bake you a loaf the size of your head when we get home. Two loaves. Three."

The Regent watched this exchange with an expression I could not read, and then said, quietly, almost to himself: "That is why he chose you, you know. Out of all the doors in all the dry world, the plea fell to yours. He tasted kindness through the crack before the covenant ever woke you. Children remember bread."

Something cold moved along my spine, and it wasn't the sea. "Woke me?"

"The covenant stirs when its terms are near breach. Tam's arrival strained your salt line. Strain wakes the anchor." He turned the broken coral over one last time and did not set it down. "Which brings us to the matter of repair, and what it will cost, and I warn you now, daughter-of-Maren: you will not like my terms. I barely like them myself."

Behind him, through the chamber's opening, I saw the garden-city dim — actually dim, the sourceless green-gold light guttering like a lamp out of oil — and heard, far below, a sound like a tower of coral sighing.

Tam grabbed my sleeve. His finger was already writing.

**Hurry,** it said.
