## 16. The Price of Repair

I have worn grief longer than I have worn this crown, and the crown was set upon my head before the first keel of the first ship cut the water above us.

Mortals believe the drowned do not keep time. They are wrong. We keep it more carefully than they — each tide a page, each century a chapter in a ledger no living hand has read. I have read all of it. I have had nothing else to do but read, these two hundred years, sitting on my throne of coral while my court dimmed around me like a lamp running out of oil.

And now, at last, the ledger's balance had come due, and the debt stood before me in the flesh.

She called herself Yara Ravensworth. The court knew better. The salt knew better. She was the Anchor — the one stolen, the one bound, the one reborn again and again into bodies that did not know they were instruments. She stood on the floor of my hall with seawater beading on her skin she had no right to breathe, and her chin was up, and her eyes were furious, and I thought: *yes. That is exactly the face the guild made when they took you the first time. Proud. Certain. Certain that they were owed.*

"You want me to be bound," she said. "Forever. Again."

"I want *balance*," I told her, and my voice moved the water the way a storm moves wheat. "Something was taken. Something must be returned."

The coral spires of my court carried my words outward, down the long avenues of the drowned city, to where my people clustered — those who could still cluster, those who had not yet faded into the grey drift at the edges of our realm. I saw them lean in. I saw them divide, even as I spoke, like a current splitting around a stone.

Good. Let them divide. A court that agrees about everything has stopped thinking, and a court that stops thinking drowns twice.

* * *

Let me tell you what was taken, so you may judge whether I am a monster or merely an honest accountant.

Before the guild, magic was a tide. It moved through everything — through the valley grass and the mountain stone, through mortal blood and gill and fin. There were no wardens because there was nothing to guard. There were no gates because there was nothing to gate. My people and the dry-folk traded songs the way they traded fish, freely, and the covenant between us was simple as breathing: *the water shares, the land shares, and neither owns.*

Then the Saltbound Guild was founded by men who looked at a tide and saw, instead, a well. And wells can be fenced.

They could not take magic itself — magic is not the sort of thing that goes quietly into a sack. So they took its anchor instead. Every covenant needs an anchor, you understand, a living point where the pact roots itself into the world, the way a mooring holds a ship to harbor. The anchor is not the source of magic. The anchor is only the *knot*. But if you hold the knot, you hold the whole rope.

So they took her. The woman whose face Yara wears now, or wears now her — the distinction has grown very confusing across six lifetimes, and I confess I have stopped tracking it. They bound the knot to themselves. They built the salt line, which was never protection but *enclosure*, and for two hundred years every drop of magic in the world passed through guild hands, priced and permitted and taxed.

And my court began to die. Because a covenant with one partner bound and the other holding the knife is not a covenant. It is a wound with paperwork.

That is what was stolen. Now you may judge me.

* * *

"Balance," Yara said again, when I had finished. She said it the way one spits a bad taste. "You keep using that word as though it means *revenge*."

"It means what it has always meant." I rose from the throne, and the court flinched — all of them, even the brave ones, even old Vessik who fought beside me when the sea was young. When I stand, the water remembers drowning. "A theft of two hundred years cannot be repaid with an apology and a handshake. You are the knot. Restore the covenant as it was, restore yourself to your place, and the tide returns to all things. That is justice."

"That is *slavery* with older vocabulary."

There were murmurs at that. Some angry. Some — and this interested me — some thoughtful.

She did not flinch under my attention. I will grant her that; none of her six lives had flinched, so far as the records show. She stood in the center of my hall with her ragtag company behind her — the disgraced healer with kind eyes and sharper ones, the outlaw woman Sefa with her arms crossed like a verdict — and the child Tam at her side, fading like sea-foam.

Tam. Yes. Him too. We will come to him.

"You were not there," I said, gentler now, because I remembered being gentle once and saw no reason to entirely abandon the practice. "You do not remember the first binding. But *I* remember. I remember the ambassadors of the guild kneeling on this floor, swearing their order would be a steward, a servant of the tide. I believed them. My credulity killed three thousand of my people in the first century alone, as the magic thinned and the deep places went cold. Do not speak to me of vocabulary, Anchor. I have earned my bitterness. I paid for every grain of it."

For a moment — a small, shameful moment — I wanted her simply to yield. To bow her head and say *yes, Regent, bind me,* and let two centuries of arithmetic come out even. It would have been so clean. Grief loves clean ledgers.

She said: "Who bound you?"

I paused. "...What?"

"You're grieving. Someone died. Someone you loved, or your people, or a version of the world. Somebody bound *you*, Regent, and you've been wearing the knot ever since." Her voice was not cruel. That was the insufferable part of it. It was warm, almost pitying, and pity from the instrument of your humiliation is a particular flavor of maddening. "I'm asking you to do to me what was done to you, and call it repair. You know exactly how that feels from the inside."

"Enough." Vessik surged forward, his frills flared, his old spear — ceremonial now, mostly — half-raised. "You stand in the house of your own rescue and lecture the throne—"

"She stands in the house of her own imprisonment," Yara said, "and asks for a different sentence. There's a difference, and everyone in this hall knows it, because everyone in this hall has been *bound* by someone's idea of balance."

Silence. The good kind and the terrible kind, which underwater are indistinguishable anyway.

* * *

Here is what I did not say aloud, then: she was winning.

Not the argument — arguments are won by whoever tires last, and I am very old and tire never. She was winning the *court*. I watched my people watch her. I saw Nerissa of the Third Shelf, who had lost two daughters to the Fading, nod slowly. I saw young Coraltuck, barely changed, glance from her face to my throne and back with the terrible arithmetic of children who have just realized adults are making it up.

My court was splitting, and both halves wore my face. One half wanted what I wanted: the debt paid, the pain answered, the old terms restored with the anchor at their center whether she consented or not. The other half wanted what she offered: a covenant renewed by choice, power scattered into ten thousand willing hands instead of knotted into one unwilling throat.

Justice, and vengeance. Sisters. Twins. Wearing the same crown, and only I knew which sister I had crowned.

"The old terms," I said at last, because someone had to say it, and I would not make my people do my hard speaking for them. "Are this: the anchor lives, and serves, and does not die until the covenant dies. The anchor's consent is not required, because the anchor is not a party to the covenant — the anchor is the *seal* upon it. Seals do not get opinions." I let the words settle like silt. "Restore them, and the tide returns tonight. Refuse, and my court finishes dying, and I will spend what remains of us hunting the guild to its last warden. Both roads end in payment, Anchor. Choose which coin you'd rather count."

Behind her, the healer — Brannoc, the records call her, though records have been wrong before — made a small sound, quickly swallowed. Sefa's hand had found her knife. Even they understood: this was the moment the drowned court asked its question.

Yara looked at Tam.

I have sat through treaties, funerals, wars, and one genuinely disastrous wedding. I know every species of silence. Hers was the rarest: a person weighing not her own life but whether her life *belonged* to her at all — and discovering, mid-weighing, that she had already decided it did, sometime ago, somewhere she hadn't been watching.

"No," she said.

Just that. No speech. No thunder. The smallest word in any language, and it fell through my hall like a stone through still water, and the ripples have not stopped since.

"The answer is no," she said again, steadier. "Not because I fear the binding. Because you're right that something must balance the theft — but the theft wasn't only mine. It was *everyone's*. Two hundred years of villages afraid of their own gifts. Of hedge-witches branded criminal for healing. Of your people dying while the guild sold tides by the pint. If anyone pays, everyone pays a little — a covenant sworn by all, kept by all, owned by no one. Consent, Regent. Not blood. Not knots. Ask. For once in the history of either of our peoples — *ask*."

"Ask," repeated a voice from the crowd, and it was Nerissa, and then another voice took it up, and another, and I heard my court saying the word as though tasting it, some finding it bitter, some finding it the first sweet thing in a century.

"Enough!" I did not mean to shout. The water darkened with it. "You would gamble the world's last magic on *manners*? On the goodwill of the very species that—"

"On the goodwill you're asking me to prove exists," she said, "while refusing to extend it yourself. Which of us is gambling, Regent? You want certainty. Certainty is just control with better jewelry."

Vessik laughed — one sharp bark, instantly strangled, but I heard it, and worse, half the court heard it.

I descended one step from the throne. Then another. Around me the court held its breath, and I felt the split become a chasm: to my left, those who would see the anchor bound tonight by force if words failed; to my right, those who had begun — gods help me — to hope.

"Consider carefully," I said quietly, "what happens if we cannot agree. There are others who want this power. Others who do not debate terms on a courthouse floor. The guild's warden, Corvane, marches even now, and he does not *ask* anything of anyone—"

Which is when the far gates of the hall rang like a struck bell, and the water turned cold, and a sentry came tumbling down the avenue shouting words I had hoped never to hear in my own court:

*"Wardens! Wardens in the gate-road — they've breached the Gate — they carry iron and salt-fire — the warden-lord Corvane demands the throne—"*

Yara and I turned to look at each other across the wreckage of our negotiation, anchor and regent, debtor and creditor, and I saw my own error written plainly on her face: we had spent our argument's hour arguing, while the man who never argued was already through the door.

"Well," said Yara Ravensworth, into the ringing dark. "That's inconvenient."
