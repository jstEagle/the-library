## 17. Tam Speaks

The voice came back the way water comes back to a dried streambed — first as a trickle he could feel but not use, then as a pooling, then all at once, everywhere, unstoppable.

Tam woke in the drowned court with his own name in his mouth.

*Tam.* Not the name the sea had called him, which was a long silver sound like a bell rung underwater. Not the name the salt had called him, which was more of a hum, patient and grainy. His own name, the small human one, the one a woman with flour on her hands had said to him once across a doorway, and he had not been able to say back.

He opened his mouth and said it.

"Tam."

His voice cracked like a young bird's. It came out rusty and ridiculous and it was the most beautiful sound he had ever made. He said it again, louder, just because he could. "Tam! Tam Tam Tam." He was aware this sounded slightly mad. He did not care. He had been a vessel, a plea, a message wrapped in a child, and messages do not get to chatter. But the court was weak now — he could feel it the way you feel a fever breaking, the great coral halls flickering like a lamp low on oil — and whatever had kept his voice locked away had gone down with it.

He was lying on a ledge of pale stone in a side chamber off the great hall, wrapped in a cloak that smelled of woodsmoke and hedge-magic. Brannoc's cloak. She had tucked him in like he was her own, muttering the whole time about children being sent places, *nobody sends a child anywhere good, in my experience children get sent to chimneys and wells and wars.* He liked her enormously.

He sat up. The water of the court did not wet him here — it moved around him like a courteous crowd, parting and closing, full of small lights. Through the chamber's arch he could see the great hall, and the throne of coral, and the Tide Regent, and — his heart did something complicated — Yara.

She stood before the throne with her spine straight and her fists half-curled, arguing with something older than kingdoms. He had watched her do this for three days now. She was very bad at being afraid. Or perhaps she was very good at being afraid and doing it anyway, which Tam suspected was the same thing wearing different boots.

He should go to her. He had a testimony. He had been sent to deliver it, that was the whole point of him, the entire reason the sea had pulled him under and the salt had pushed him out and the long terrible walk had happened at all.

But first — he patted his pockets, found the heel of bread Brannoc had given him that morning, and ate it slowly, because a testimony delivered on an empty stomach is never as convincing. He had learned this from watching Yara, who argued better after meals, and from Brannoc, who claimed all diplomacy was really just catering.

Then he went to speak.

* * *

The great hall of the Coral Throne was dying beautifully. That was the thing Tam could never explain to anyone who hadn't seen it. The spires still caught the light and scattered it into a thousand drifting coins; the banners of woven kelp still swayed in currents that remembered music. But the colors were draining, the way a face drains. Whole galleries of coral had gone white as bone. Somewhere above, the water pressed down with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world, and the court did not.

The Tide Regent sat upon the throne like a grief given a body. Tam had tried, in his silent weeks, to work out how old the Regent was, and had given up. Old the way a coastline is old. Old the way a wound is old if you never let it close.

"—and you ask me to believe," the Regent was saying, and the voice moved through the water the way cold moves through a wall, "that the guild's heir, raised behind the salt line, raised on their secrecy and their hoarding, will simply *hand back* what her order stole? That she will restore the terms out of the goodness of a heart trained by thieves?"

"I'm not asking you to believe anything," Yara said. "I'm asking you to look at the terms. The original ones. You've had them read out four times."

"Because you keep interrupting the fifth."

"I interrupt because the fifth reading has a clause at the end you keep skipping."

Brannoc, standing off to the side with her arms crossed, caught Tam's eye and rolled hers expressively. *Three days of this,* the roll said. *Three days.*

Tam walked out onto the hall floor, and his bare feet made no sound on the coral, but every head turned anyway. The court had always known where he was; he had been their letter, their hope, their last coin spent. Now the letter had opened its mouth.

"Tam," Yara said, and the relief in her voice made his chest hurt.

"Hello," Tam said.

The word dropped into the hall like a stone into a still pool. The Regent rose from the throne. Courtiers — or the memories of courtiers, pale shapes that might once have been people — stirred along the galleries. Brannoc said, "Well, hallelujah," and then, when everyone looked at her, "What? The boy's been mute since I met him. I'd have settled for *pass the butter.*"

"You speak," the Regent said. The great head tilted, and eyes like deep water fixed on him. "The plea was sent voiceless. Voiceless, so that it could not be refused, argued with, or answered. A plea with a mouth can be told *no.*"

"I know," Tam said. "That's why I waited until the court was too weak to keep me quiet."

Somewhere in the galleries, a shape made a sound that might have been a laugh. The Regent did not laugh. But something in the vast stillness of the Regent shifted, the way a mountain shifts — not moved, exactly. Noticing.

"Then speak," the Regent said. "You were sent with words the salt would not let you carry. Say them. Say what the realm sent you to say."

And here was the trouble, the trouble Tam had been carrying since the drowned gate, since the salt line, since the first terrible night in the storm: what the realm had sent him to say was a formal thing, a legal thing, a thing with the weight of centuries behind it. *The covenant is broken. The anchor was stolen. The debt is owed.* He could say it. It would be true.

But it would not be the truth.

"The realm didn't send me," Tam said. "Not exactly. The realm is a big thing. Big things don't send children. The *covenant* sent me — the broken part of it, the part that's been bleeding for two hundred years. And it didn't send me with a speech. It sent me the way you send a bird with a message tied to its leg. The message was: *find her.*"

"Find the anchor," the Regent said. "Yes. The anchor must be found and restored to her terms, or the terms must be enforced upon her. That is the law."

"No," Tam said. His voice was small in the great hall and it did not matter at all, because everyone leaned in to hear it. "That's what you all think the message was. I thought so too, at first. But I could feel it, the whole time I walked — the pull, the thing dragging me east. And it wasn't pulling me to the anchor. It was pulling me to a door. To a valley. To a specific door, on a specific night, in a storm."

He turned to Yara.

"You gave me bread."

* * *

Later, Tam would understand that he had said the most important sentence of the century, and that it had sounded, in the great drowned hall of the Coral Throne, like the least important sentence ever spoken. Brannoc would tell him this, years after, laughing until she had to sit down. *You stood before the Regent of all the seas, with the fate of magic in the world hanging on your words, and you opened with the bread.*

But in the moment, it was simply the truth, and the truth does not dress up.

"You gave me bread," Tam said again, because the first time had made Yara frown in confusion, and he wanted to get it right. "The night of the storm. I came to your door and I couldn't speak, and I was — " he searched for the word, and settled on honesty — "I was so scared. Everything was wrong. The water had taken me and given me back wrong, and I couldn't remember how to be a boy instead of a message. And you opened the door and you looked at me, and I could feel it, all of it, the whole covenant leaning on me, the realm's whole grief, two hundred years of it, all of it looking at you through my eyes and saying, *this is the one, this is the anchor, judge her.*"

Yara had gone very still.

"And do you know what you did?" Tam said. "You didn't judge me. You didn't test me. You didn't ask what I was or who sent me. You looked at a soaked, shivering, silent child on your step in a storm, and your face did a thing — " he tried to demonstrate the thing with his own face, and it came out as a sort of wobble, and Brannoc made a strangled noise that was definitely not a laugh, definitely — "and then you stepped aside and let the warm out. And you gave me bread. And you sat with me while I ate it, and you didn't ask me a single question, because you could see I couldn't answer, and questions felt like grabbing."

The hall had gone very quiet. Even the water seemed to have stopped moving.

"I didn't know what you were," Yara said quietly. "I'd never seen anything like you. The salt sang when you spoke — sang, Tam, it had never done that — and Aldous went white as a sheet and burned his papers. Every rule I had ever been taught said to be afraid of you. And I was." She smiled, small and crooked. "I was afraid of you the whole first night."

"I know," Tam said. "I could tell. You kept glancing at the salt line."

"I kept glancing at the salt line," Yara agreed.

"But you gave me the bread anyway. Before you knew. Before anything. Before duty, before the covenant, before the whole realm's need — there was just a boy at a door, and you fed him. That's the testimony. That's what I was sent to say." He turned back to the Regent, and felt the message rise in him at last, the true one, the one under the legal one, the one the bleeding broken covenant had tied to his leg. "The realm didn't send me to find an anchor. The realm sent me to find out if there was even one person left behind the salt line who would open the door. Because the covenant was never broken by the theft. The theft was broken by the theft. The covenant broke the day the guild decided that no one would ever again be asked — that magic would be owned, and the anchor bound, and the door kept, and no one would ever get to say yes or no. The covenant is a promise. And you cannot make a promise to someone you never ask."

The Regent had not moved. But the water around the throne had begun, very slowly, to move — a slow turning, like something enormous shifting its weight after a long sleep.

"You are a child," the Regent said at last, and the voice was not cold now, only tired, tired the way the court was tired, tired the way two hundred years is tired. "You are a child sent as a plea, and you stand in my hall and tell me that the smallest act outweighs the law of centuries."

"Yes," Tam said. "Because the law of centuries was made by people who never gave anyone bread. The law of centuries was made by people holding the bread hostage." He thought about this, and decided it was good, and stood a little straighter. "You want the anchor bound again. You want the old terms, the ones with the suffering in them, because suffering balances theft — that's what you said. But the anchor didn't steal anything. The guild stole. And the first anchor didn't consent, and Yara didn't consent, and if you bind her again you're doing exactly what the guild did, with better reasons. The court split over this, I heard them. And I'm just a boy. But I was at a door once, in a storm, and I know what a yes looks like. It looks like warm light spilling out onto a wet step. It doesn't look like chains with good reasons attached."

"Tam," Yara said, and her voice had gone strange.

"And also," Tam added, because he had been holding this in for three days and it was going to come out whether or not it was dignified, "if you bind her again, I will bite you."

The hall went absolutely silent.

"You will *bite* me," the Tide Regent said.

"Yes," Tam said. "On the leg. Brannoc says I have strong jaws. Brannoc, tell him."

"I will not be party to this," Brannoc said, "but I will confirm the jaws."

Something happened then that no one in the court had seen in two hundred years, though the oldest of the memory-shapes stirred as if reaching for a memory of a memory: the Tide Regent laughed. It was a terrible sound, vast and rusted, like a ship's bell raised from the deep, and it went on longer than laughter should, and by the end of it the Regent was leaning on the throne's arm as if the laughter had cost something, and perhaps it had. Perhaps grief always costs something to set down, even for a moment.

"A plea that bites," the Regent said. "The realm spent its last strength on you, child, and you spent it on bread and biting." The great eyes turned to Yara, and the tiredness in them was bottomless. "You hear him. The court hears him. But hear me too, Ravensworth: the debt does not vanish because a child speaks kindly of you. Two hundred years. Two hundred years of a dying court, of a stolen anchor reborn and bound and reborn, of magic hoarded behind a salt line while the world went dark and dumb. The debt wants *paying.*"

"I know," Yara said. "I've been trying to pay it. You keep refusing my currency."

"Because your currency is *change,* and debts of this size are paid in — "

Whatever the Regent meant to say the debt would be paid in, the hall never learned. Because that was the moment the water at the far end of the great hall turned black.

Not dark — Tam had lived in dark; dark was just the absence of light and he'd made his peace with it. This was black the way ink is black, the way a closed door is black, a black with *purpose* in it. The small lights of the court guttered. The courteous water stopped being courteous. And through the black came shapes — many shapes, armored, moving in formation, and at their head a figure whose presence pressed on the hall like a hand pressing on a wound.

Tam did not know the man. But he knew the coat — guild grey, guild silver — and he knew the way Yara's whole body changed beside him, every muscle remembering a name.

"Corvane," she said.

The warden's voice rolled down the drowned hall, amplified by craft, by arrogance, by both at once:

"I have come for the covenant. And I find, conveniently, every party to it gathered in one room."

Tam reached down and found Yara's hand. Her fingers closed around his, cold and steady.

"Get behind me," she whispered.

"I have strong jaws," Tam whispered back.

The black water began to fill the hall.
