## 18. Corvane at the Gate

The first thing I noticed was the light.

The drowned court had settled into something almost peaceful while I stood before the Tide Regent — that green-gold glow filtering down through fathoms of water, catching on coral spires like sunlight through a cathedral window, if the cathedral had been built by patient madmen and then politely asked to sink. Tam sat near the throne, still pale as bleached bone but breathing easier, and Brannoc hovered at my shoulder muttering about how nobody had eaten anything decent in three days.

Then the light changed.

It went from green-gold to a hard, cold white, the kind of light that comes from lanterns carried by people who believe they are right.

"The gate," I said. "I closed it behind me."

"You did," said the Tide Regent. Their voice was the sound of tide going out over gravel, and it did not rise, but every fish in the court seemed to feel it. "Gates made of consent can be opened by it too. Or by force. Force is so much faster. That is rather the tragedy of force."

The water above us — the whole impossible ceiling of it, held back by covenant and courtesy — shivered. And then it *tore*, along a seam no one else could see, and down came the wardens.

Not falling. Marching. Corvane's people had found some way to walk the sea-road without drowning, wrapped in salt-grey charms that hissed where the water touched them, boots finding the sunken road as if it were cobbled stone instead of memory. There were thirty of them. Then fifty. Lanterns on long poles drove spears of white light down into the court, and everywhere the light touched, coral went grey.

"They're burning it," Brannoc said. "The light. It's not light, love, it's *salt* — guild-worked salt, weaponized." She had her satchel off her shoulder and open before she finished the sentence, hands moving with the calm of someone who had patched wounds in worse places than a throne room. Which was, I reflected, probably true.

Corvane came last, and he came the way he did everything: like a man arriving at an appointment he'd scheduled months ago. He walked the drowned road without a charm, bare-faced to the sea, and the water parted for him — or he'd bought something that made it think it had parted. Either way, he reached the floor of the court dry-shod, and he looked up at the Coral Throne, and I watched something move across his face that frightened me more than all his warden steel.

*Reverence.*

He wanted this. Not the power of it — well, yes, the power of it, certainly — but the thing itself. The old order, restored and gleaming, with himself at last standing where the guild had always secretly believed it deserved to stand: at the very hinge of the world.

"Yara Ravensworth," he called, and his voice carried strangely through the water, arriving everywhere at once. "Step away from the throne. This does not concern you anymore. It never truly did — you were only ever the key's keeper, and keys do not get opinions."

"I have *several*," I called back, which was not my finest rhetoric, but my heart was hammering and dignity is a luxury in wartime.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about battles in a drowned court: they are loud in the bones. Every clash of steel rang up through the coral floor and into my teeth. Every shouted order arrived twice — once through air, once through water — slightly out of time with itself, so the whole fight felt like it was happening in a dream you couldn't wake from.

The Regent's court fought. I'll say that for them. The tide-sworn guards rose from niches in the spires, carrying tridents crusted with centuries, and met the wardens' line with a sound like a shipwreck remembering itself. But the grey light kept eating at the coral, and every yard of coral lost was a little less breath in the court's ancient lungs. I saw a guard stagger, her glamour guttering, suddenly looking less like a figure of legend and more like what she was: someone very old, and tired, and afraid.

Tam grabbed my sleeve. He pointed — not at the fighting, but at the throne.

Behind it, half-hidden in a thicket of red coral, was a small door. Carved into it, in the Old Tongue: words I could not read but somehow understood anyway, the way you understand a mother's expression.

*What is kept must be kept safe.*

"The anchor-stone?" I guessed. Tam nodded urgently, then pointed at me, then made a gesture — both hands pressed over his heart.

"Yes, I know I'm the anchor, we've established I'm the anchor, that's rather the problem—"

He stamped his foot. Then, with the terrible clarity of a child dealing with a slow adult, he pointed at the door, at the stone beyond it, at me, and drew a circle in the air: *if the stone breaks, you break.*

Oh.

Well. That explained why I'd felt every strike of grey light against the coral like a hot wire drawn across my skin. I'd thought it was sympathy. Sympathy doesn't bleed.

"We need to get to that door," I told Brannoc.

"We need to *not die*," Brannoc replied, yanking me backward as a warden's halberd came down where my head had been. She answered it with a fistful of something purple and foul-smelling that bloomed into choking smoke; the warden reeled away sneezing, which seemed a gentle fate for someone actively trying to kill us, but Brannoc was Brannoc. "Both would be nice. Door *and* not dying. Aim high."

We ran. The battle churned around us — wardens in their salt-grey, tide-guard in their fading blue-green, and between them, horribly, ordinary folk. I recognized some of the warden faces. One of them belonged to a woman who had once sold me apples at the market below the valley, back when I believed the world ended at the salt line. She swung a weighted chain at a tide-guard and shouted something about *restoring rightful order*, and she meant it. That was the worst part. Everyone out there meant it. The wardens fought for the covenant as they understood it — ordered, gated, owned. The court fought for a grief two hundred years deep. And somewhere beyond the gate, if Sefa had gotten my message, the Unsworn were coming to fight for a future none of us could quite picture yet.

Three armies, each certain it was the hero of the story.

Nobody writes songs about the ones who are merely certain. Songs should require evidence.

* * *

We nearly made the door.

We were ten steps away when the water above us boomed like a struck bell and Sefa's outlaws came through — not marching, not descending, but *pouring*, dropping through the torn seam on ropes and improvised gliders and, in one memorable case, what appeared to be a repurposed fishing net, whooping like the world's most illegal festival. The Unsworn hit the warden flank sideways, and the whole geometry of the battle lurched.

And there, in the sudden opening, stood Corvane.

He had reached the dais of the Coral Throne. He had his hand on its arm. And he was doing something with the broken key — my key, the coral-and-bone thing my dying master had pressed into my palms, which I had carried across a country and been prepared to surrender on my own terms. Except it wasn't in my pack anymore. It hung in Corvane's fist, and it was *singing*, a low grinding note, and around him the stolen magic of the court gathered like iron filings toward a magnet.

"You brought it here," I breathed.

"I took it from you four days ago, outside the Tollhouse," Corvane said, mildly, as if correcting my grammar. "You never checked your pack. You are honest to a fault, Yara. It is your single greatest failing, and I say that with genuine affection."

Four days. Four days I'd been carrying an empty leather wrap and calling myself its guardian. Somewhere behind my ribs, the anchor-stone — wherever it hid behind that little carved door — pulsed with humiliation on my behalf.

"You can't hold it," I said. "The key isn't a tool, it's a *tourniquet*. It's tying the covenant shut around a wound. If you use it—"

"I intend to *untie* it. Properly. Under proper authority." His voice rang out over the battle, pitched now not for me but for everyone — wardens and tide-guard and outlaws alike. "Hear me! The age of hiding ends tonight! The guild's failure will be repaired under my hand, the covenant renewed, magic governed as it must be governed — by law, by warrant, by *order*! Kneel to the restored compact and be forgiven!"

For one heartbeat, the battle actually slowed. Because he sounded right. That was the obscenity of it — he sounded exactly like a man saving the world. Even Brannoc shifted beside me. Even the Tide Regent, watching from the shadow of a spire, wore an expression I couldn't read.

Then Tam stood up on the dais steps, small and stubborn, directly in front of the throne, directly in front of Corvane, and planted his feet.

"Move, child," Corvane said.

Tam didn't move. He looked up at Corvane with the flat, unimpressed stare of a boy who had once stared down the entire Saltbound Guild by refusing to eat his soup, and he opened his mouth, and he spoke — his voice cracked and rusty from disuse, but loud enough that half the court heard:

"That's *my* grandfather's chair."

I don't know who laughed first. I'd like to claim it was me, but honestly, my money's on one of Sefa's net-riders. The laugh rippled outward through the ranks of the Unsworn, and then — gods help us — through some of the wardens too, because even zealots know a child's truth when they hear one, and Corvane's face went the color of a bruise.

Whatever unity that laugh threatened, he killed it. He raised the key, and the singing note became a shriek, and the shriek became a *pull*. I felt it in my marrow — the anchor-stone straining toward the key, the covenant's whole knotted history dragging tight, and every soul in the court gasped at once as something invisible cinched around all of us.

He wasn't renewing anything. He was reeling us in. All of us. Magic, covenant, court, anchor — gathered into one hand, held by one man, forever.

"No," I said. Not loudly. But the anchor heard me, and the anchor was me, and the word moved through the coral like a tremor through a rope.

Corvane turned to face me across the dais, the key blazing in his fist, wardens converging at his back, outlaws surging at his flanks, the tide-guard rallying, the Regent rising from the shadows with the full weight of the drowning sea gathering in their raised hands. Above us the torn sky-water churned. Below us the coral groaned grey. On every side, people who believed themselves righteous crashed together like weather fronts.

And in the middle of all of it, I understood — with a clarity that felt almost like laughter — that the key had never been meant for any of these hands. Not mine, not his, not the Regent's. It had been a mistake the moment it was forged, a way of holding what should have been let go.

There was only one thing left to do with a tourniquet when the healing finally began.

I started walking toward the throne.

Corvane saw me coming. He smiled, and lifted the key higher, ready to receive me — ready, I realized, for me to *fight him for it*, because in his entire architecture of the world, no one ever destroyed the prize. The prize was the point. The prize was always the point.

I kept walking, and I reached for the key with both hands, and I did not intend to take it.

I intended to break it.
