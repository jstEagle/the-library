## 21. The Long Way Home

The road out of the drowned country smelled of rain and green things, and Yara kept stopping to look at trees.

She was aware this was ridiculous. She had walked through a gate at the bottom of the sea. She had shattered a key older than kingdoms. She had argued theology with a Tide Regent the size of a chapel and won, mostly, by refusing to stop talking. And here she was, twenty paces past the drowned gate, standing in the mud with her mouth open because a birch tree had *shimmered* when she touched it.

"It does that," Tam said, tugging her sleeve. His voice was still new to him — he used it the way other children used pebbles, tossing it out to see what sound it made. "Everything does that now. You'll get used to it."

"I don't want to get used to it," Yara said. "I want to keep being ridiculous about it."

Tam considered this with the gravity he gave all important matters, then nodded once, as though she had passed some private examination. He was walking better now. The sea had given him back more than his voice; his step had a bounce in it that hadn't been there when he'd first appeared at the valley door, mute and salt-crusted and singing the Old Tongue without knowing it. Sometimes at night he still woke crying for a city he'd only ever seen from the inside of a dream, and Yara held him until the shaking stopped, and neither of them pretended it was fine. But in the mornings he was a boy again, and the mornings were winning.

Behind them, the drowned gate had closed itself with a sound like a wave being folded in half. Brannoc and Sefa had gone the other way — Brannoc to the river villages with a satchel of outlawed remedies and a head full of everything the archive ghost had taught them, Sefa to ride the terms of the new covenant from hearth to hearth like a herald who'd never been knighted and intended to keep it that way.

"You could have come with us," Yara had said to Sefa at the parting.

"And watch you two walk in circles admiring trees?" Sefa had grinned her crooked grin. "I've got work. Besides — the terms only hold if people *choose* them, and people can't choose what they've never heard. Go home, Ravensworth. Someone has to teach the valley to be afraid of the right things."

So it was the two of them, on the long overland road, in a world that was busy changing without anyone's permission.

* * *

The first hearth-fire they saw was in a village called Marlow's Rest, which wasn't resting at all.

They heard it before they saw it — a great crackle and cheer rolling down the hillside — and came around the bend to find the whole village gathered in the square around a bonfire built so high it leaned, dangerously, toward a thatched roof. An old woman stood on a stool feeding it bundles of dried herbs, and every bundle she threw in made the fire flare a different color: green, then violet, then a deep contented gold.

"Charm-bundles," Tam said, delighted. "She's burning *ward-herbs* in the open. In front of everyone."

At the valley, magic had been a thing you confessed to or died denying. Here, a girl of maybe ten was drawing shapes in the firelight with a charred stick, and the shapes hung in the air after her, glowing faintly, while her mother watched with her arms crossed and an expression Yara recognized from every guild lesson Aldous had ever given: the look of someone supervising something that could go wrong, and deciding, tonight, to let it.

The old woman on the stool spotted them and waved them in like she'd been expecting them for supper. "Travelers! You'll be wanting food. Everyone wants food, that's what the fire's for — warmth and bread and a bit of showing off." She hopped down from the stool, joints crackling louder than the fire. "First night it's been lawful. Well — first night it's been *unlawful*, properly speaking. We're celebrating."

"Celebrating the law being unlawful," Yara said.

"Celebrating that nobody's coming to take Marta's hands for her hedge-craft," the old woman said, nodding at the girl with the charred stick. "That's the whole of it, dear. We heard a rider came through two days past with the terms. Sworn by the court below the sea and witnessed by outlaws and a guild-breaker, whatever that is. We're to swear our own oath, each village for itself, and no warden, no toll, no guild." She squinted at Yara. "You'd know something of it, I'd guess. You've the look of someone the news has been through."

"You could say that."

"Are you the guild-breaker?"

"I'm the guild-breaker," Yara admitted, and braced for it — the fear, the anger, the *what have you done to us* — but the old woman only looked her up and down and said, "Hm. Thought you'd be taller," and went to fetch bread.

They ate by the fire. Marta, emboldened, showed Tam her shapes, and Tam showed her three words of the Old Tongue, and the two of them sat cross-legged making the firelight spell things it had no business spelling, while the village argued cheerfully about what their oath should say. Someone wanted a clause about the well. Someone wanted a clause about the miller, who was widely agreed to be a menace. A man with flour on his sleeves insisted they needed a line about *consent* specifically, because his grandmother had been bound to a river spirit without asking and had complained about it for forty years, and if the realm was doing things properly now, it could start with grandmothers.

Yara sat with her bread and listened, and felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was tight.

This was what the covenant was supposed to have been, she thought. Not a vault. Not a salt line with one family holding the key. This — a village arguing about its own well, badly and at length, while children played with fire and nobody sent for the wardens.

"You're crying," Tam observed, with the tact of the very young.

"I'm not."

"There's a wet thing on your face."

"It's fire smoke."

"It's coming from your eyes, Yara."

She laughed, which settled the matter, and the old woman pressed another heel of bread into her hands and told her she was too thin for someone who'd broken a guild, and Yara held the bread and thought: *this is what it was for. All of it. This exact stupid, wonderful evening.*

* * *

They left Marlow's Rest two days later, loaded with more food than two travelers could carry, because the old woman took rejection of her second helping as a personal insult. The road climbed away from the drowned country, through hills where the magic lay in the grass like morning dew, and Yara taught Tam what she could of the guild's old disciplines — not the gatekeeping, never that, but the *care* of it. How to touch power gently. How to put it down when you were tired. How to know the difference between a thing that wanted helping and a thing that wanted leaving alone.

"That one's not in any book," Tam said, when she explained the last part.

"No. Aldous never wrote it down either. I think he was afraid of what it implied."

"What did it imply?"

Yara watched a hawk turn circles over the valley below. "That some things shouldn't be governed. Only kept company."

Tam chewed on that for a full mile. Then: "Is that why you're taking me home instead of to a guild?"

The question sat in the road between them. Yara had been circling it herself for days, the way the hawk circled, never quite landing.

"I'm not taking you anywhere," she said at last. "I'm walking with you as far as the valley, and after that you choose. School, road, sea — whatever you want. That's the whole point of everything we did, Tam. Nobody chooses for you. Not me, not a guild, not a court under the water."

"Even if I chose wrong?"

"Especially then. That's how you learn you chose wrong."

He nodded, satisfied, and ran ahead to argue with a fence post that was, apparently, slightly magical, and Yara walked on alone with her heart doing complicated things.

Because here was the truth she hadn't said aloud, not even to herself, not quite: she dreaded the valley.

It was a small, shameful dread, and she carried it like a stone in her boot. The valley had made her. The valley had also made the salt line, and the rule, and the silence, and the guild that had stolen a child from the sea and a woman from her own name and called both of them *duty*. When she imagined the valley gate she imagined Aldous's face, and the burned records, and two hundred years of anchors bound without consent, all of it wearing the valley's familiar hills like a disguise. She wanted to go home the way you want to press on a bruise — to know it's still there, to prove you can bear it.

And underneath the dread, shamefully, stubbornly: she *wanted* it. The smell of the salt marsh at dawn. The specific gray of the light on the shingle. The door she'd been born behind, or made behind, or whatever the truth of her beginning was. Home was the wound and home was the salve, and there was no walking to one without the other.

"You're doing the face," Tam said, materializing at her elbow. "The thinking face. You do it when you're sad about the valley."

"I don't have a thinking face."

"You do. Brannoc does it too. It's when your eyebrows go up like they're trying to leave."

Yara laughed despite herself. "Fine. Yes. I'm sad about the valley. Is that allowed, under the new covenant?"

"Everything's allowed," Tam said, with the serene authority of a boy who had recently been to the bottom of the sea. "That's what the covenant *is*."

* * *

They crossed into the high country on a morning so clear the world looked newly made, and that was where they found the second fire.

This one was small — a hearth-fire in the true sense, burning on a stone hearth in the doorway of a shepherd's hut, with no crowd, no celebration, just an old shepherd standing beside it with his hat in his hands, watching it burn with an expression Yara couldn't read until they were close enough to see the tears.

"First one," he said, when he noticed them. "Forty years I've kept a coal hidden in a clay pot under the floor. Buried it every time the wardens came through. Fed it in secret. My wife thought I was mad." He wiped his face with the back of his hand, unembarrassed. "She died three winters back. Never saw it lawful." He looked at the flame, small and ordinary and free. "I lit it this morning and let it burn in the open, and I stood here like a fool, and I thought — if she could see it. Just that. If she could see it."

Yara stood with him in the doorway. She didn't have words that were big enough, so she didn't try. She put her hand on his shoulder, and Tam — who had a genius for the small true thing — picked up a twig and fed it to the fire, the way you'd feed a bird you'd coaxed to your hand.

"Where are you two bound?" the shepherd asked at last, voice steadier.

"Home," Yara said. "A valley, west of the mountains. You'd know it by the salt line."

The shepherd's eyebrows rose. "The silent valley. The guild valley." He studied her, and she watched him arrive at it — the guild-breaker, the anchor, the woman from the rider's tale, standing in his doorway smelling of road dust. "They'll be waiting for you there," he said. "The ones who stayed. They'll have heard everything by now, and half of it wrong, and they'll be standing at that salt line wondering what you've brought back."

"What have I brought back?" Yara asked. It wasn't rhetorical. She genuinely wanted to know.

The shepherd looked at his fire, and at Tam, and at her, and said: "The same thing I lit this morning. A coal somebody kept alive. Now it just needs putting on the hearth."

They walked on. The mountains came up to meet them, and the road bent westward, and on the third evening Yara crested a ridge she'd known all her life from the far side of dreams and duty, and stopped.

There it was. The valley. The salt line a pale ribbon in the dusk, the guildhouse dark on its hill, the marsh silver where the light still touched it. Her whole childhood, laid out below like something in a book she'd read about someone else.

Tam stood beside her and took her hand without being asked.

"Are you afraid?" he asked.

"Yes," Yara said. "Aren't you?"

"A little." He squeezed her fingers. "But the salt's going to sing. I can hear it from here. It's been waiting a long time to sing for someone who'd listen."

And far below, faint as a memory, the salt line began — very softly — to glow.
