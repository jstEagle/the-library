## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The mountain had a voice, and Gideon Okonkwo had spent thirty years learning not to answer it.

He stood at the head of the caravan on the high smuggler road above Thornfell Pass, one hand resting on the cold granite of the switchback wall, listening to the stone the way other men listened for hoofbeats. The rock spoke in slow syllables—*frost-crack, root-push, water-seek*—and beneath that, fainter than breath, the hum of the border wards woven through the mountain's bones like veins of silver. Every child in Thornfell grew up feeling that hum without knowing they felt it: a low, patient note under the world, the sound of being kept.

Gideon heard it because he heard everything. That was the trouble.

"Road's clear," he said over his shoulder, and did not say how he knew. "Two miles to the hollow under Wolfjaw. We'll rest there till moonset."

Behind him, six wagons creaked and groaned, laden with salt wool, river-pearls, and three crates whose contents he'd been paid handsomely never to inventory. The drivers were good people by the standards of their trade—quiet, careful, incurious—and they trusted him the way sailors trust a pilot who has never once run them aground. They believed he read the stars, the wind, the droppings of goats. Let them believe it. The truth was a door he kept shut with both hands.

Because Gideon Okonkwo spoke to beasts. And beasts, unlike men, could not be lied to, bribed, or made to forget what they'd heard.

His rule was older than his beard, older than his first grey hair, older than the night at nine years old when he'd answered a limping fox in its own tongue and watched his mother's face go white as flour. *Never be noticed.* Not by men. Never by men. A man who spoke with animals was a curiosity in the free cities, a witch in the villages, and a resource in the capital—a thing to be owned, leashed, milked. He had seen what happened to the last beast-speaker the crown had found: a comfortable cage, a collar of office, and eyes gone dull as pond ice within two years.

So he listened, and he never answered where anyone could see, and he built a life out of small invisibilities. He walked at the edge of fires. He laughed half a beat after everyone else. He let men think him lucky rather than clever, clever rather than strange. It was a narrow life, but it was his, and he had made it warm.

Tonight the mountain was wrong.

He felt it before he could name it—the way you feel a room change when someone enters behind you. The wards' hum, that constant silver thread, had developed a stutter. A hitch, like a singer drawing breath and not finding the next line. Gideon pressed his palm flatter against the granite and listened harder, sinking into the stone-speech until the cold climbed his arm to the elbow.

*Frost-crack. Root-push.* Then something else, from far below and far away, moving through the rock like a tide: *hollow. hollow. hollow.*

Not silence. Silence would have been kinder. This was the sound of something being *eaten*—a hum swallowed whole, the way a wolf takes a rabbit, quick and total, leaving no blood.

Gideon straightened. His mouth had gone dry.

"Marta," he called softly to the lead driver, an old woman with rope-scarred hands and no patience for omens. "How fast can we make the hollow?"

"Faster if you stop asking stupid questions." She squinted up at him. "Why?"

"Call it weather."

She snorted and turned back to her mules, and Gideon caught himself watching those mules—not as livestock, but as colleagues. The near one, a dun gelding with a scarred lip, was already uneasy. Ears swiveling backward, weight rocking between forefeet, nostrils working at air that smelled, apparently, of nothing at all. Animals always knew first. Animals always knew first, and tonight they were not merely uneasy.

They were beginning to scream.

* * *

It started with the birds.

A nightjar should have been singing from the scree slopes—Gideon had timed this crossing by its song a dozen seasons running—but instead there came a single rising cry from somewhere above the tree line, thin and frantic, cut off mid-note. Then another, from the east ridge. Then a chorus of them, dozens of voices, all climbing toward the same terrible pitch and stopping dead, one after another, like lamps going out down a corridor.

Then the goats in the pens at the abandoned shepherd's station below the road began to bleat—not the ordinary complaint of penned animals, but a flat, continuous, mindless wail that went on and on until Gideon's skin crawled.

And then the wolves.

Thornfell's wolves were old neighbors. He had traded words with their packs since boyhood—warnings about poisoned bait left by trappers, jokes exchanged across ravines, once, memorably, a debt repaid when he'd led a starving winter pack to a valley full of wounded red deer that the king's hunters had missed. The wolves of Thornfell knew his voice. Some of them, he suspected, knew his name, though he had never given it aloud; names were things beasts found out on their own, the way water finds a crack in stone.

Now the whole mountain rang with them. Not hunting cries. Not territorial challenges. Something Gideon had never heard in thirty years of listening: grief. Long, rising, breaking notes, pack answering pack across the dark ridgelines, a sound like funeral keening scaled up to the size of a country.

"They're calling something," Marta said beside him. When she'd come up the road he couldn't say; the woman moved like smoke when she chose to. Her face was turned toward the peaks, and in the starlight he saw the cords of her throat standing taut. "Or warning us."

"Wolves don't warn travelers," Gideon said. "We're beneath their interest."

"Then what are they doing?"

He didn't answer, because the honest answer was: *mourning*. And mourning meant something had died. And whatever had died was big enough that every pack on the massif felt it through their feet.

The dun mule screamed then—a horrible human sound, almost a word—and reared against its harness, and the wagon behind it lurched, and for a moment the road dissolved into shouting and lantern-light and the smell of spilled lamp oil. Gideon moved through it calmly, the way he moved through everything, murmuring nonsense syllables at the mule while actually speaking sense into its laid-back ear: *easy, easy, nothing here will touch you, I won't let anything touch you.* The animal steadied because it understood him, truly understood, and that understanding was the most dangerous secret in the kingdom, and he wielded it now as casually as a man scratching a dog's chest.

But even as the mule calmed, Gideon felt the last of his own calm draining away. Because the mule wasn't afraid of wolves or rockfall or the smell of oil.

The mule was looking up.

* * *

The stars were going out.

Not clouding—there was no cloud; the air above Thornfell was hard and clean as glass. Not setting—the whole sky was still there, black and vast. The stars were simply *ceasing*, in patches, in swathes, like ash falling on embers. One moment the Hunter strode the southern sky with all his jewels burning; the next, his shoulder was a hole in heaven. Then his belt. Then the northern crown of lights that every Thornfell child learned before they learned letters.

Marta made a sound Gideon had never heard from her, small and involuntary, a girl's sound escaping an old woman's body.

"That's not possible," said young Ferro, the wheelwright's son, staring upward with his mouth open. "That's—that's not—"

Around the caravan, the drivers stopped working. Stopped talking. Sixteen grown men and women, hardened by years of illegal roads and bribed checkpoints, stood in the middle of a mountain pass with their heads tipped back like sunflowers, watching the sky be unmade.

Gideon watched too, but he watched differently. He watched the way a man watches a door he knows is about to open.

Because he understood—or thought he understood, in the wordless way of understanding that lives in the gut—the stars and the wards were the same thing. Everyone in the borderlands knew the old rhyme: *As the stars are set, so the stones are strung; what hangs in heaven holds the land.* The ward-stones buried beneath Thornfell and every city of the realm were anchored to the sky, tuned to it, fed by it. The stars were not decoration. They were the counterweight that kept the great wards spinning.

And the wards were being eaten. He had heard it in the stone an hour ago: *hollow, hollow, hollow*, a tide of swallowing moving through the mountain's roots.

Which meant the sky itself was going dark because the wards were dying. Or the wards were dying because—

No. He refused to think the second thought. The second thought was madness.

"Gideon." Marta's hand closed on his forearm, grip like a manacle. "What do we do?"

In thirty years, no one had asked him that question with such naked need. He was the guide. The pilot. The man who knew. And the truth was that he did not know, that no smuggler's trick or hidden path applied to a sky going out like a snuffed lamp, and admitting that—to these people, to anyone—was a kind of death he had spent his whole life avoiding.

"We keep moving," he heard himself say, steady and low. "The hollow under Wolfjaw is walled on three sides. If anything falls from the sky, it falls on open ground, not on us. Move. Now. Quietly."

They moved. Of course they moved; fear makes men obedient. And Gideon walked at the head of the wagons with his heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't felt since childhood, since the night his mother had looked at him with flour-white horror and he had learned that the world punishes the noticed.

Above them, the last constellations guttered. Below them, in the deep stone, the tide of *hollow* rose higher.

* * *

They reached the hollow just past midnight, if midnight still meant anything in a starless dark. It was a fold of the mountain, a natural amphitheater walled in granite, sheltering a spring and a stand of black pines—the kind of camp smugglers had used since before there were borders to smuggle across. Ferro got fires lit, small ones, screened. Wagons were circled. Men checked loads they had already checked twice, because work is a prayer against panic.

Gideon should have slept. He had not properly slept in two days. Instead he slipped away from the firelight, alone, up the goat track at the hollow's rim, to a shelf of rock where he could put his back against the mountain and listen.

This was the part of himself he never showed anyone: the listening. He closed his eyes and opened the other sense, the one with no name he dared use, and let the mountain pour into him.

Stone first: the slow mineral patience, *frost-crack, root-push*, unchanged, indifferent. Stone would outlast whatever was coming; stone always assumed it would.

Then the pines around him, their root-voices uneasy, whispering of soil that tasted wrong.

Then the beasts—and here the night became unbearable. Bats returning to their crags without having fed, chittering the bat-equivalent of prayer. A snow leopard far above, pacing her ledge in tight furious circles, saying over and over, *wrong, wrong, the high places are wrong, my territory smells of nothing, nothing, nothing.* An owl grounded, refusing to fly under a sky with no stars, huddled in a scree crack like a fledgling. And everywhere, everywhere, the wolves, still keening, though their song was fraying now, growing ragged, dropping into something worse than grief.

Confusion. The packs were beginning to forget why they were crying.

That was the detail that finally frightened him properly. Grief fades; he knew that; he had buried a mother and a brother and a life in the river valleys. But beasts did not *forget* grief. A wolf remembered a dead packmate for years, visiting the place where it fell. For the keening to fade so fast, the memory itself had to be fading—as if something were drinking the mourning out of them along with everything else.

*Hollow,* said the stone again, closer now. *Hollow. Hollow.*

Gideon opened his eyes.

The sky was absolutely black. No stars. No moon—the moon had been a waxing crescent tonight, and it was simply gone, and no one had mentioned it because the mind refuses such facts politely, files them among impossible things, moves on. But Gideon made himself look, made himself count the absences, made himself know.

The wards were the counterweight. The stars hung because the wards held. And the wards were being eaten by something that moved through the earth like a tide, and the eating had reached Thornfell, and Thornfell was only the first border. There were seven ward-cities. There were hundreds of stones. And whatever hunger moved beneath the mountain was patient—it had taken him an hour of listening to even hear it—and patience like that belonged to nothing natural.

He sat on the cold rock and did the arithmetic of his own life. He could take the caravan down the western road tomorrow, deliver the cargo, collect his pay, and vanish deeper into the free cities where no one knew his face. That was the rule. That was the life. *Never be noticed.* Whatever was happening to the kingdom would happen whether Gideon Okonkwo spoke of it or not, and speaking of it would cost him everything he had spent thirty years building: the anonymity, the freedom, the warm narrow life.

But the wolves had sung for the wards. The bats had prayed. The leopard had paced her ruined territory, saying *nothing, nothing, nothing* in a voice that cracked.

And Gideon had spent his whole life hearing them and never once answering where it mattered.

Below him, the caravan fires glowed small and orange in the absolute dark, little islands of defiance. Somewhere south, beyond the pass, lay the ward-city of Thornfell proper, its towers and its ten thousand sleepers, all of them trusting in a protection that was, at this moment, being drained like a wineskin.

Something moved on the track below him.

At first he took it for a shadow, a trick of the firelight on pine trunks. Then it resolved: a wolf, descending the goat path toward the hollow, moving with the deliberate, exhausted gait of an old animal. Not stalking. Not fleeing. *Coming.* And behind it, at intervals down the slope, more shapes—five, six, seven—descending in loose procession, silent now, their keening spent.

Wolves did not come to human fires. Wolves especially did not come to human fires on the worst night in the history of the sky.

Gideon stayed perfectly still on his rock shelf, and the lead wolf stopped at the base of it, looked up, and looked directly at him—invisible though he was, unnoticed though he had made himself for three decades—and held his gaze with pale amber eyes that held, unmistakably, deliberately, the expression of someone coming to deliver a message.

The wolf's jaws parted. What came out was not a growl, not a whine, not any sound Gideon had ever catalogued in thirty years of listening.

It was words. Ragged, broken, half-swallowed, as if the speaker were learning language and losing it in the same breath:

*"King. Fire. Sigil. King-burning. Under the—"*

And then the voice failed, and the wolf swayed on its feet, and Gideon was moving down the rock before he had decided to move, rule and life and thirty years of careful invisibility falling away behind him like a cloak dropped on a stair.

The world he navigated by had gone dark. And the dark was speaking to him.
