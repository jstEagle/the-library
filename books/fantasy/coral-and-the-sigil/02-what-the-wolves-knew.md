## 2. What the Wolves Knew

He reached the wolf in three strides and caught it as it folded.

The animal was heavier than it looked — mountain grey wolves always were, all bone and gristle packed under that ragged pelt — but Gideon took its weight across his knees the way he'd taken dying animals his whole life: carefully, as if weight mattered less than witness. Its fur was wrong. That was the first thing his hands told him before his mind caught up. Not matted, not wet, not bloodied. *Cold.* Cold like river stones in thaw, cold like something that had been standing too long in a place where warmth had been drained out of the air itself.

Around them the caravan had stopped. He heard it without turning — the mules shifting, a crate settling, old Ferro's voice dropped to a mutter, the particular quality of silence that meant men were watching him and pretending not to. Gideon Okonkwo crouched at the edge of the firelight holding a wolf, and every one of them would have questions by morning.

*Let them,* said a voice in him that was not usually permitted to speak.

The wolf's eye rolled up toward his face. One eye was ordinary amber, wild and terrified. The other was pale as skimmed milk, filmed over, and when Gideon looked into it he felt the sensation he knew best in all the world — the falling-open of a door between himself and another mind — except this door swung onto darkness. Not emptiness. Emptiness he could have borne. This was a darkness with teeth marks in it, a darkness that had been *chewed.*

"Easy," he murmured, and was distantly amazed that his voice worked. "Easy now. I've got you."

*I've got you.* As if those words could hold anything together tonight. The stars were gone. Not clouded — gone, wiped from the sky like breath from glass, and the border wards of Thornfell, the ancient woven protections his grandmother's grandmother had sung about, had died an hour ago in a wave of silence so total that the beasts along thirty miles of ridge had screamed themselves hoarse and then stopped screaming all at once, like a choir cut off mid-note.

And now this wolf had spoken.

Gideon was a beast-speaker. He had been one since he was six years old and had understood, with a clarity that never left him, that this gift was a death sentence if it was ever noticed. His whole life had been built on the rule his father pressed into him with a hand heavy on his small shoulder: *never be seen doing it, never be known for it, be a smuggler, be dull, be forgettable.* He had kept the rule for thirty years. He was good at keeping rules.

But the rule assumed a world with stars in it.

The wolf's ribs worked under his palm, shallow and fast. Its muzzle hung open, and from somewhere down in the ruined dark of it came the voice again — and now, holding the animal close, Gideon could feel where the voice lived. It wasn't in the throat. It was in the *memory*, in some chamber of the beast's mind that had been cracked open like a geode, and what spilled out was not the wolf's own knowing but something it had carried, something given to it or forced into it, and it came out in shards:

*"Under the crown. Under the— it burns him. It burns and he cannot— we saw, we ran, we ran through the wards and they bent like grass—"*

"Whose?" Gideon asked, low, urgent, forgetting even to keep his voice flat. "Whose chest? Whose crown?"

The amber eye fixed on him. And then the memory came — not in words this time but whole, the way beasts gave things, all at once and sideways — and Gideon was no longer kneeling on a mountain road in the starless dark.

* * *

He was looking up.

That was the first fact of the memory: the angle. Something vast overhead, vaulted and shadowed, banners hanging dead-still in air that should have moved them. A throne room. He knew throne rooms the way he knew most rooms he'd never entered — from maps, from smugglers' gossip, from Sera Voss's kind, cartographers and couriers who drew the world for people who ruled it. This was the great hall at Caer Aldane. He was certain of it the way you are certain of your own name.

He was low to the floor. Stone under paws. He was seeing through the wolf's eyes, and the wolf had been *inside* — inside the palace, inside the ward-perimeter, which should have been impossible, which had been impossible for three hundred years, because the wards did not permit wild things past the outer walls —

Because the wards were *bent like grass.*

In the memory, they bent. Gideon felt it as the wolf had felt it, running through moonlit corridors that smelled of tallow and fear, feeling the old protections part around his body like wheat before a scythe — not broken, not torn, *bent*, as though some enormous hand were holding them aside, and the pressure of that hand filled the memory like a second heartbeat.

Then the throne room. Then the king.

Aldric sat on the Aldane throne, and even in a wolf's borrowed eyes Gideon recognized the set of him — broad, grey-templed, the posture of a man who has carried a kingdom long enough that the carrying shows in the shoulders. There were others in the hall, blurred at the edges of the memory, courtiers or guards, and none of them moved. None of them seemed able to move. Their stillness had the texture of held breath.

And on the king's bare chest, over his heart, something was burning.

Not flame. Flame would have been kinder. It was a sigil — a closed shape, interlocking lines that hurt to follow with the eye, drawn in light the color of a bruise, deep violet shading into black — and it moved. It turned slowly, like a lock turning, like a wheel grinding, and with each turn the king's body jerked once, a puppet's twitch, and Gideon felt through the wolf's senses the thing he would spend the rest of his life unable to unfeel: the *taste* of it. The sigil was feeding. It drank from the man on the throne in long slow swallows, and what it drank was not blood, not breath, not years. It drank *authority*. It drank the binding-oath that every king of Aldric's line swore at coronation, the oath that fed the wards of the realm, the covenant between crown and country that made Thornfell's mountains warded and Merrowgate's harbors charmed and every road in between safe enough for a smuggler to grow old walking them.

It drank the king's kingship. And somewhere far away, in wave after silent wave, the wards went out.

The king's mouth was moving. In the memory the wolf had crept close — why, Gideon couldn't know; perhaps the wards' bending had called every wild thing in the palace to look, the way a wound calls flies — and so the words reached him faintly, slurred, a man speaking through deep water:

*"Stop it. Please. I can feel it eating. Someone — someone stop it—"*

And the courtiers stood in their perfect stillness and did nothing, and one of them — tall, robed, a face the memory refused to hold the way water refuses ink — turned his head, slowly, toward the shadows at the hall's edge.

Toward the wolf.

The last of the memory was that turn of the head, and the smile that came with it — patient, pleased, *hungry* — and then the wolf was running again, out through bending wards, out into the night, out over the fells with the sigil's aftertaste coating its tongue, carrying what it carried because there was no one else to carry it to.

Except there was. There was one person in all Thornfell who might listen.

The memory broke like ice underfoot.

* * *

Gideon came back to himself with the wolf's weight real against his chest, its breath going thin and rattling, and he found that his own arms had tightened around the animal without his permission, that his face was pressed into its cold ruff, that Ferro and two of the others had come near with lanterns and stopped at some distance, saying nothing, because there is a particular silence men keep around a thing they don't understand.

"It showed me," he said, not to them, hardly to the wolf. "Gods below, it showed me."

The wolf's tail moved once against the frozen ground. Not a wag. An acknowledgment. *You received it. Good. Now I can stop.*

"Don't," Gideon said, and hated how young his voice sounded. "Hold on. There's a vet in Harrow's Bend, there's — I know someone who—"

But they both knew. Beast-speakers always know; it is the first lesson and the last, the one gift the gift gives freely: you learn to hear the difference between a body fighting and a body finishing. This body was finishing. Whatever had cracked open in the wolf's mind when the wards died — whatever channel the sigil-binder had opened to broadcast his crime into every living thing within reach of the silence — it had burned through the animal like fire through dry grass. The wolf had run thirty miles on a heart that was already spent.

"I'll carry it," Gideon said. "I'll tell them. Whoever needs to hear it. I swear it on—" and he stopped, because he had never sworn anything on anything in his life; oaths leave marks, marks get noticed. But the wolf's amber eye held his, waiting, and Gideon heard himself say, quietly, "on my mother's name, which I'll not say aloud."

The wolf huffed — almost a laugh, if wolves laughed — and its head sagged against his shoulder, and the last of the tension went out of it, and then, gently, between one breath and the next, the last of everything else went out of it too.

* * *

He stayed there a while. The caravan waited. Ferro, to his credit, eventually waved the others back to the mules and let the road stand empty, and only when Gideon finally rose — stiff-kneed, arms aching, the wolf's body laid with strange tenderness behind a shelf of rock, its nose pointed north toward its own country — did the old smuggler come near.

"That animal talked," Ferro said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation either. Ferro had run the Thornfell roads for forty years and had learned which things not to see. "I'm asking no questions, Gideon. You know that. But I'll say one thing, and then I'll say no more." He looked up at the blank sky, where stars should have been, where for sixty years of his life stars had always been. "Whatever killed the sky tonight — my wife's sister lives in a ward-town east of here. If there's word worth carrying, carry it fast."

"There's word," Gideon said.

"And will you speak it?"

Gideon looked down the dark road. Somewhere south, beyond the passes, beyond the sealed borders he hadn't yet heard about but already felt closing like a fist, a man wearing a king's face was drinking a kingdom. And the memory of that patient, smiling face — the one the wolf's mind couldn't hold, the one Gideon's own mind kept sliding off of — waited behind his eyes every time he blinked.

Here was the arithmetic he ran, fast and cold, the smuggler's arithmetic, the survivor's arithmetic. If he spoke of a sigil, he spoke of sorcery, and sorcery-talk brought inquisitors, and inquisitors brought questions, and questions brought attention, and attention was the one currency Gideon Okonkwo had spent his entire life refusing to earn. Worse: whoever had done this had looked into the shadowed edge of a throne room and *seen a wolf watching.* Such a man would know what a listening beast was. Would know what a listening beast might mean. Would know, perhaps, that some rare souls could open the doors between minds — and would want such a soul found, and bound, or hollowed like the king, or simply eaten the way the wards had been eaten.

To speak of the sigil was to paint a mark on his own chest and stand in a lit window.

To stay silent was to let the dark finish falling.

"The wards didn't fail," he said aloud, testing the truth of it in the open air, hearing how it sounded — like treason, like madness, like exactly the sort of whisper that gets a man a cell. "Everyone will say they failed. Old magic wears out, they'll say, the covenant thinned, bad luck. That's what they'll want to say." He shook his head. "They didn't fail. They were *eaten.* Something is eating them, Ferro. Something wearing the king."

Ferro went very still. Forty years of roads lived in that stillness. "Then you're not asking whether to warn people," the old man said slowly. "You're asking how to do it without dying."

"I'm asking whether I'm already marked just by knowing." Gideon flexed his hands. They were steady. He was mildly surprised. "A dying wolf crossed thirty miles of fells to bring this to *me*, Ferro. Of every soul in Thornfell. It didn't stumble on our fire by chance — beasts don't stumble, not on a night like this. Something chose me. Or something knows what I am." His voice dropped. "Either way, the invisibility's over. I just haven't yet decided what I'll be instead."

Ferro studied him for a long moment, lantern-light catching the seams of his weathered face. Then, practical as ever, he jerked his chin toward the caravan. "Mule train's loaded. Road's dark but I know it blind. We make Harrow's Bend by dawn, and you decide on the way whether you're a smuggler who heard a rumor or the first messenger of a war." He paused. "For what it's worth — and it's worth nothing — I'd bet on the second. You've got the look of a man whose cloak just fell off."

Gideon almost smiled. Almost.

They walked back to the mules together, and the column groaned into motion, wheels and hooves and whispered curses, moving north through a world with no stars, and Gideon rode at the rear as he always did, and for the first time in thirty years he did not check the road behind him. There was no point. The thing hunting him — if it was hunting — needed no road.

What he checked instead was the horizon ahead, and what he thought about, mile after mile, was the king's mouth moving in the memory: *someone stop it.* A hollow man, being devoured alive in front of his own court, begging in a whisper no one was permitted to hear.

Someone had to hear it.

And then, cresting a rise above the valley road, the caravan stopped all at once, mules balking, men swearing softly — because far to the south, where Caer Aldane stood, where no fire should be, the sky glowed a deep and bruised violet, the exact color of a sigil turning slowly over a captured heart.

Gideon looked at it, and made his choice, and did not yet know that the road to keeping it would run straight through a fugitive cartographer with forged maps in her coat — a woman he would collide with in the dark before two more nights had passed, running from the same silence, carrying the same secret from the other end.

The dark, it seemed, had been speaking to more than one of them.
