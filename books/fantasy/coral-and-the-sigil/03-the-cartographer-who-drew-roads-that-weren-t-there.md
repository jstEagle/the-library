## 3. The Cartographer Who Drew Roads That Weren't There

The trouble with drawing a road that does not exist, Sera Voss had learned, is that eventually someone asks you to walk it.

She had known this risk since she was nine years old, crouched beneath her grandmother's drafting table with an ink-stained rag in her fist and a stolen quill behind her ear, copying the old charts that no one was supposed to see. Her grandmother had kept them rolled in oilcloth inside a false-bottomed chest, beneath the good linens and the letters from dead relatives, as though paper could be shameful. As though the truth about the kingdom's shape were something to hide like a scandal or a second wife.

*The roads came first,* her grandmother used to say, tapping one long finger on lines so old they had gone brown as dried blood. *Before Aldric. before his grandfather, before the First Charter. The roads were here when this land had another name entirely.* And then she would cover them again, quickly, the way other women hid love letters.

Sera had spent twenty years forgetting that lesson, and three weeks failing to.

Now she sat in the back room of Halvard & Sons, Chartwrights, in the capital city of Corvane, with a lamp turned low and forty-one forged maps spread around her like the shed skins of a snake. Each one bore the royal cartographic seal—she had cut the die herself, which had taken eleven days and cost her two burned fingers—and each one showed a road that did not exist. Roads through the Greymarsh where the surveyors swore there was only fen. A causeway across the Serpent's Reach that appeared on no naval chart. A tunnel-mouth in the shoulder of Mount Cael, drawn in a hand steadier than her own heartbeat, labeled in careful capitals: **THE OLD WAY**.

They were beautiful maps. That was the worst part. She had made them beautiful because beauty was how you made a lie survive; ugly lies got questioned, but a beautiful lie got framed and hung in a magistrate's office and defended by men who should have known better.

And they had worked. For eight months the forgeries had moved quietly through the kingdom's bureaucracy, redirecting garrison patrols, rerouting grain convoys, opening corridors through the mountains that let certain people move without being counted. She had never asked who was buying. In the capital, you learned not to ask. You took the coin, you drew the road, and you told yourself that maps of things that weren't there were harmless, because everyone knew the world stayed put when you stopped looking at it.

Then the stars went out.

It happened three nights ago, and the whole city had come into the streets to stand in the blackness and feel it—the way you feel a tooth go bad, a low wrong ache at the back of the sky. The priests said eclipse. The astronomers said atmospheric ash. But Sera had stood on the roof of Halvard & Sons with her ink-stained fingers curled over the gutter, and she had felt something older than either explanation. She had felt the wards go out the way you feel a candle die in a far room: not the light itself, but the sudden absence of its warmth on your skin.

Her grandmother had taught her what the wards felt like. *You'll never see them, girl, but you can learn to miss them.*

Three nights of missing them now. Three nights of darkness with no stars, and this morning, boots on the stairs.

Not the ordinary boots of Halvard & Sons' daytime trade—those shuffled, those dragged, those belonged to clerks with ink under their nails and grievances about their lunches. These boots struck the stairs like a verdict. Evenly spaced. Unhurried. Men who did not need to hurry, because they already knew exactly what they would find at the top.

Sera blew out the lamp.

In the dark, forty-one lies surrounded her, and she thought, with a strange floating calm: *I always wondered how it would end. I assumed it would be the Guild, over money.*

"Inquisitors," said a voice below, flat as a planed board. "By authority of the Crown. Open."

There was a sound she recognized from rumor and had hoped never to hear from life: the dry click of a bone needle case unclasping. They said the needles tasted lies off the air itself. They said the last man interrogated in the Salt Market had confessed to crimes he hadn't committed yet.

The door to the back room had a window. The window had a drainpipe. The drainpipe was, according to her own survey of the building—which she had made herself, months ago, because a cartographer trusts no one else's measurements—old enough to be rotten at the third joint and sound everywhere else.

She left the maps. There was no time to burn them, and besides, some animal part of her mind whispered, *let them find the roads. Let them wonder. Let them spend years searching valleys that don't hold what I drew.* It was a petty thought and a dangerous one and she cherished it all the way down the drainpipe, palms burning, the third joint cracking under her heel exactly as predicted, dropping the last six feet into the alley where the night smelled of tallow and horse and the river's cold iron breath.

Then she ran.

She ran the way she had mapped herself running, once, idly, in the margins of a chart she'd titled *Contingencies*. Out of Cartwrights' Row, past the chandler's, down the stepped alley where the cobblestones were loose—she knew which ones, she'd noted them—through the fish market's shuttered stalls, reeking and slick, and out onto the embankment where the river ran wide and black and starless above, so that water and sky became one continuous dark and she might have been running along the seam of the world.

Behind her, the boots. Not following her exact route—but parallel, patient, cutting angles the way good hunters do. They had the city's gates already. She understood that without being told. Whatever the inquisitors wanted with forged roads, they wanted it badly enough to lock down the capital in a single morning, and no gate warden in Corvane was going to argue with men carrying bone needle cases.

Which meant the river.

There was a barge yard two miles downstream, past the tanneries, where freight barges took on cargo for the western towns. Barges paid no tolls at the gates. Barges had masters who asked no questions, provided the questions you didn't ask didn't involve the manifest.

She had coin. She had, sewn into her coat lining, her grandmother's oldest chart—the real one, the true one, brown lines on skin-soft vellum, showing the kingdom before it was a kingdom, threaded with roads that predated every charter. If the inquisitors found that one, no needle-tasting would be needed. It would hang her on sight.

She clutched it tighter as she ran, feeling its small stiff rectangle against her ribs like a second heart.

*The roads came first,* her grandmother had said. Tonight, for the first time, Sera wondered what the roads had been *for*. Because her grandmother had never drawn them as decoration. She had traced them the way you trace a scar—something left behind by a wound. And tonight, with the wards dead and the stars blind and the inquisitors' boots ringing through a city that had suddenly become all teeth, Sera Voss began to understand that she had spent her life forging copies of something real, and the real thing was waking up, and it was hungry.

---

The barge master's name was Doss and he had a face like a closed ledger. He listened to her offer, looked at her coin, looked at the dark river, and named a price triple the going rate, plus one condition.

"You sit below," he said. "Deck passengers get seen. Seen passengers get remembered."

"I'd rather be forgotten."

"Everyone would." He spat over the side. "That's why I charge what I charge."

So she rode west through the starless night in the stinking hold between bolts of wool and crates of salt fish, listening to the water slap the hull and counting the hours by the changing smell of the banks—tannery, pasture, marsh—and telling herself the tightness in her chest was fear of the inquisitors and nothing else. But underneath the fear was the other thing, the thing she'd felt on the rooftop: the silence. Not quiet. Silence. The difference between a room with no fire and a hearth swept cold.

On the second day, the barge grounded at Thornfell's edge, at a landing called Marrow's Stair, where the river narrowed and the hills rose up gray-green and old. Doss refused to go further. "Wards are down," he said, jerking his chin at the mountains. "Folk say the beasts have gone mad in the high passes. Screaming all night, they say, then nothing. Nothing's worse." He took his pay. "Walk if you're walking. But a woman alone on these roads—" he shrugged, a whole sermon in one gesture, "—the roads remember who travels them. Even the ones that aren't there."

She froze. "What did you say?"

"Nothing, mistress. Only talk." But his eyes slid away from hers in a way that told her the talk had been more specific than nothing, and she climbed the Marrow's Stair with her hand pressed flat over the hidden chart, over the second heart, and thought: *they know. Somehow they know about the roads. The inquisitors aren't hunting a forger. They're hunting the original.*

* * *

The mountain road was empty in the wrong way.

Empty roads, Sera could accept—she had walked empty roads before, in war years and plague years, and emptiness had a texture she knew. This was different. This was a road that had been evacuated of something. The birds still sang, but thinly, the way men sing after a funeral. Twice she saw deer at the treeline, standing too still, heads turned toward her—not watching her, she realized with a crawling scalp, but watching *past* her, as though she stood in front of whatever it was they feared.

And the silence followed her. Wherever she walked, it walked. She tested it the second evening, stopping in a meadow and speaking aloud just to hear a voice: "Hello the camp!"—an old traveler's call, absurd, addressed to nobody.

The word fell out of her mouth and died.

Not echoed. Sound should echo in the mountains; she had surveyed these slopes, she knew their acoustics the way she knew her own hands. But her voice dropped into the grass like a stone into deep water, and the meadow swallowed it, and for three full seconds the world held no sound at all—not wind, not water, not her own heart, which she was suddenly unsure was still beating.

Then everything resumed, casually, as though nothing had happened, and Sera Voss stood in a flowering meadow in the last light of a starless dusk with tears running down her face that she had no memory of starting.

"That's new," she said aloud, just to prove she could, and her voice worked, and she hated how grateful she was.

She made no fire that night. She lay in the lee of a granite shelf with her coat buttoned to the chin and the chart against her ribs and she did not sleep so much as fall briefly, repeatedly, into shallow pits of half-dream where her grandmother's finger tapped brown lines and said *you should have asked sooner, girl, you should have asked what ate the stars.*

She woke to hoofbeats.

Many hooves, moving fast along the valley floor below her hiding place, and she flattened against the stone and looked down into a scene she would take out and examine for the rest of her life: a smuggler caravan strung out along the old drover's track like beads on a wire—six wagons, mules, mounted outriders in mismatched leathers, moving with the smooth practiced discipline of people who had done illegal things together for a very long time. And at the head of the column, riding a dun mare, a broad-shouldered man with dark skin and a face that even at distance she could read as careful. Watchful. A man built of not-being-noticed.

She should have stayed hidden. Every instinct honed by eight months of forgery and three days of flight said: *stay hidden, stay alive.* But the caravan was heading up-valley, directly toward the pass she needed—the pass that led west to Merrowgate and the free cities—and the pass, according to her grandmother's chart, had a ward-gate at its throat that had guarded the border for four hundred years.

A ward-gate that was, presumably, as silent as everything else.

She needed to know. Knowledge was the only currency she had ever trusted, and she was broke in every other respect: hunted, gateless, alone in a screaming-silent country with forty-one confiscated lies behind her and one true map against her ribs.

So she did the stupid thing. She stood up on the shelf, in plain view, and raised both hands open and empty, and called down in her best court-trained voice:

"Travelers! I need passage west!"

The column stopped as if she'd fired an arrow into its leading wheel.

For a long moment, nothing moved except dust. Then two outriders peeled off and came up the slope at a walk, unhurried, flanking wide, and she noted with professional admiration that they never once blocked each other's lines of throw. The broad-shouldered man at the head of the column did not approach. He sat his mare and watched her with an expression she couldn't parse at this distance—except that it wasn't surprise. Everyone else in his caravan wore surprise like cheap cloaks. His face had done something else with the news of her: filed it. Measured it.

The outriders reached her. One took her satchel without asking and went through it with quick rough competence, turning out a comb, a pencil, a tin of chalks, a spare shift, and stopping, finally, on the wrapped bundle she had snatched from her coat when she stood up—the false-bottomed pocket, her grandmother's chest recreated in miniature.

"What's this, then?"

"Family papers."

"Funny kind of papers." He weighed it in his hand, frowning. "Heavy for papers."

"Vellum is dense," Sera said. "So is my patience. Return it."

He grinned at her tone, which told her something useful about him, and passed the bundle down the slope to the man on the dun mare without taking his eyes off her.

Gideon Okonkwo—for that was the name she would learn, though not yet—unwrapped the oilcloth right there in his saddle, and Sera watched the brown-lined chart catch the gray morning light and watched his face change.

It wasn't the change she expected. Not greed, not suspicion, not the hungry blankness of a man appraising loot. What crossed his features was recognition—the specific, horrified recognition of a man seeing, in a stranger's hands, evidence of something he has privately feared for days. His thumb moved along the old roads the way hers did. He read the labels. THE OLD WAY. THE KING'S ROAD THAT IS NOT THE KING'S ROAD. And at the chart's western edge, in her grandmother's faded hand, a notation Sera had never been able to translate:

***Where the wards were sung.***

"Where did you get this?" Gideon said. His voice was low and even and completely at odds with his eyes.

"It's a family document."

"Your family draws roads that don't exist?"

"My family," Sera said, "draws roads that existed before yours did. Before anyone's. Before the kingdom had a name or a king or a reason to guard anything." She descended the slope slowly, hands visible, stopping at ten feet—a distance she chose deliberately, close enough to speak without shouting, far enough to make any lunge obvious. "Those lines are real. I've spent eight months forging copies of them for buyers whose names I never learned, and three days ago the stars went out and the inquisitors burned my workshop and I have been running ever since, and I will wager everything I own—which is one map and a comb—that your beasts have been screaming and your passes have gone quiet and you have seen something in the last week that you cannot explain. Am I wrong?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, a bird sang twice and stopped mid-note, as if embarrassed.

One of the outriders shifted. "Boss, we should just—"

"No." Gideon folded the chart along its old creases with exaggerated care and held it out to her, and she saw that his hands were steady and his jaw was not. "You're not wrong."

He swung down from the mare, and up close he was taller than he'd seemed, and tired in the marrow-deep way of a man who had not slept since the stars vanished. Up close, she saw that his eyes kept flicking to the treeline—to the deer, motionless, still staring past them both—as though he were listening to something she couldn't hear. Later she would understand that he was. Later she would understand many things about Gideon Okonkwo, most of them inconvenient.

But that first morning on a gray mountainside, all she knew was this: he looked at her the way she looked at her own forged maps. With a craftsman's respect and a conspirator's caution. Two liars recognizing each other's workmanship.

"You want passage west," he said.

"I want the pass. I'll walk it alone if I must."

"The pass has a toll now. Inquisitors sealed it yesterday. Bone needles and hounds and a captain who smiles too much." He said it flatly, watching her reaction, and she gave him none, and something in his face suggested he approved of giving none. "My caravan moves goods through places that don't officially exist. Your maps draw roads that don't officially exist." A pause. "Seems to me we've been competitors."

"Seems to me we've been colleagues."

Another pause, longer. Behind him the column waited, mules stamping, a wagon creaking, thirty pairs of eyes pretending not to watch. Above them all, the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

"Here's what I know," Gideon said quietly. "Two nights ago I held a dying wolf in my arms, and before it died it gave me a memory that wasn't mine. A sigil burning into a king's chest. Fire under the skin, in the shape of a crown." He watched her absorb that. "The wards didn't fail, cartographer. They were *eaten*. And whoever ate them is wearing the throne in Corvane right now, and his hounds are sealing every pass looking for something—or someone—and here you come walking out of the east carrying a chart that shows roads older than his kingdom. So before I decide whether you ride with us or dig your own grave in this meadow, you're going to tell me why the inquisitors want you."

Sera considered lying. It was her profession; she was very good at it. But she had watched this man's face while he read her grandmother's chart, and she had understood, with the clarity that arrives only at the bottom of exhaustion, that he would taste the lie the way the bone needles tasted lies—some people simply could.

"They don't want me," she said. "They want the original. The chart I'm carrying predates the kingdom. It shows where the first wards were set—where they were *sung*, the notation says, and I've never been able to read the rest. My family kept it for six generations and I never asked why until it was too late. Someone in Corvane knows what these roads lead to. Someone with a crown-shaped brand on his chest and a use for old magic. And I think, Master...?"

"Okonkwo. Gideon Okonkwo."

"Gideon. I think you and I are carrying halves of the same answer, and I think the man who ate the stars wants both of us dead before we put them together." She held out her hand for the chart. He gave it back, and their fingers did not linger, and both of them noticed the other noticing. "So. Passage west. In exchange, I'll draw you routes through the sealed country that no living surveyor knows—roads that aren't there, except that they are, except that I can find them. Mutual blackmail, mutual survival. You keep my secret; I keep yours. Whatever yours is." She glanced, deliberately, at the treeline, at the deer. "And I suspect yours is worth keeping."

Something flickered across his face—alarm, quickly buried—and the two outriders exchanged a glance that said *how much does she know, boss*, and Gideon said, in a voice of perfect blandness, "I don't have secrets. I have a reputation for discretion."

"Of course," said Sera Voss. "So do I."

He laughed then—one short, rusty bark, as if the sound had been stored away and had spoiled slightly—and turned to the column and raised two fingers. The caravan breathed back into motion, wagons groaning, mules grumbling, the whole illegal organism flowing up the valley track toward the sealed pass and the smiling captain and the bone needles waiting somewhere beyond the ridge.

Sera fell in beside the dun mare. Gideon rode in silence for a while, and then, without looking at her:

"The wolf. When it gave me the memory—it knew things. About the roads." A pause. "It said the roads remember."

"So the barge master said."

"Barge masters repeat what beasts tell drunk shepherds." His eyes were on the pass ahead, on the notch of gray sky between the peaks where, on any other night, stars would have been wheeling. "Question is what the roads are remembering *now*. And whether whatever's eating the wards can follow a memory backward to the people who carry it." Finally he looked down at her, and there was no warmth in it and no threat either, only the cold clear arithmetic of a man recalculating his entire route. "Sleep in the third wagon tonight. And Voss—if the hounds catch your scent on this road, I'll cut you loose without a second thought. Don't mistake alliance for affection."

"I wouldn't dream of it," said Sera, and settled the chart against her ribs, against her second heart, and marched west into a country with no stars, alongside a stranger full of secrets, on roads that weren't there—yet.

Behind them, far to the east, faint as a dying ember, something in the direction of the capital began—very softly—to sing.
