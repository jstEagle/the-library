## 4. Inquisitor's Toll

The pass closed on the third morning after the stars went out.

Gideon knew it before he saw it, the way he knew weather in his teeth and trouble in the back of his neck. The mountain had gone quiet—not the hush of snowfall or the held breath of a storm, but the particular silence of a place where every wild thing with sense enough to run had already run. No choughs over the crags. No marmots whistling from the scree. Even the wind seemed to be moving carefully.

"They've chained the gates," Sera said beside him, her voice flat as her maps. She stood at the lip of the switchback with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, watching the road below where it narrowed between two walls of granite. "See the banners? Grey and red. That's not the king's guard. That's the Inquisition."

Gideon looked. Below them, at the mouth of the pass called the Shepherd's Tooth, iron gates had been dragged across the road—old gates, older than the kingdom's paving, dragged out of some forgotten wall and planted like teeth in the throat of the mountain. Torches burned at either side despite the daylight. And through them moved travelers: merchants with their wagons tipped and their goods spilled across the stones, pilgrims kneeling in the frost, a family of dyers standing very still while men in grey robes walked around them with something small and pale held in their gloved hands.

"What are they holding?" he asked, though part of him did not want the answer.

"Bone needles." Sera did not look away. "My father told me about them when I was small, as a story to keep me honest. They say the needles were cut from the femurs of saints, back when there were saints worth cutting. You hold one, and it warms against a lie. Warms until it burns." She paused. "They say the old inquisitors could taste truth on the air itself. These ones need the needle. But a needle is patient."

Below, one of the kneeling pilgrims cried out—a short, high sound, quickly strangled. A grey-robed figure bent close, murmuring, courteous as a physician. Then two soldiers lifted the man by his arms and carried him behind the gatehouse, and the line of waiting travelers pretended very hard not to have seen it.

Bramble shifted under Gideon's hand. The mule's ears were pinned flat, and when he spoke it was in that subvocal rumble only Gideon could hear, a voice like gravel rolled in wool. *We turn back.*

"Back is three days to Thornfell," Gideon murmured aloud, barely moving his lips, "and Thornfell is ash by now, if the rumors hold."

*Back is alive.*

*Maybe.* Gideon watched the gate. He counted guards—twelve visible, likely eight more in the gatehouse—and counted wagons—nine ahead of theirs in the queue that was already forming, because word traveled faster than armies and every soul on the mountain road had decided, this morning, that they needed to be through the Shepherd's Tooth today. Among crowds there was cover. Among crowds, questions got diluted. And among crowds, a man with nothing to declare and no name worth remembering could pass like water through a net.

The problem was the woman beside him.

He glanced at Sera Voss. She had gone very still, in the way of people doing arithmetic they don't like. Her satchel—the fat, strapped, oilcloth-wrapped satchel she slept with and ate with and had nearly knifed him over when he'd once reached for it half-asleep—hung against her hip, and inside that satchel were maps. Maps of roads that predated the kingdom. Maps with ward-stones marked on them like blisters on skin. Maps that, in the hands of any inquisitor with a functioning imagination, would read exactly like what they were: treason drawn in ink.

"You can't go through there with those," he said quietly.

"I'm not leaving them."

"I didn't say leave them. I said you can't go through carrying them." He turned to face her fully, and let himself look the way he almost never let himself look at people—straight on, deliberate, the way you looked at a lock before picking it. "Sera. They'll open your bag because you're alone and you're clever and clever women traveling alone get searched twice. And when they find maps of roads that aren't on any crown chart, roads that lead around their nice new sealed passes, they won't ask where you got them. They'll ask who else has copies. And then they'll ask about everyone you've traveled with. And then—" he nodded down toward the gatehouse, toward the door the pilgrim had been carried behind— "the needle gets warm."

Her jaw tightened. For a moment he thought she would argue; everything about her was built for arguing, all edges and ink stains. But she looked down at the gate too, and whatever she saw there made her swallow it.

"There are hollows in the rock above the tree line," she said instead. "Shepherd caches. My family used them, generations back, when they carried the old charts between ward-keepers." Her eyes came up, sharp. "You knew that, didn't you. You knew these hills had hiding places, because smugglers always know where the land keeps its secrets."

"I knew," Gideon admitted.

"And you didn't tell me."

"I'm telling you now." He shrugged. "Telling people things early costs more than telling them late. Everyone knows that."

"That is the most dishonest piece of folk wisdom I've ever heard."

"It's mine, though. I'm fond of it."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might live, in a person who had been running too long to afford one. Then she unshouldered the satchel, and began, with quick precise fingers, to break it down: oilcloth into her coat lining, folded charts into the false bottom of a flour sack, the true prize—three sheets wrapped in waxed silk—slid flat against her spine beneath her shirt, where no search would press hard enough to find them without undressing her, which even inquisitors hesitated to do on an open road.

Gideon did his own arithmetic. His own cargo was honest—salt fish, tin nails, bolts of undyed wool, the gray porridge of legitimate trade—but his face was not honest, and his hands were not, and most of all his gift was not, coiled tight behind his ribs like a second heartbeat. If a bone needle warmed against lies, what would it do near him? He lied the way other men breathed. Every sentence of his life was a small act of concealment: never noticed, never named, never known. He had built a whole self out of the spaces between truths.

And somewhere below that gate was an instrument designed to find exactly such gaps.

"Bramble carries the flour," he said. "I walk beside him. Sera, you're my wife."

Her head snapped around. "*What?*"

"Wives don't get searched separately. Husbands answer for them—it saves time, and time is what we want to save. Also," he added before she could detonate properly, "you talk too much when you're frightened. A husband can put a hand on your arm and make it look like affection."

"I will *end* you."

"Not before Merrowgate, please. I have cargo to deliver."

*She'll kill you in your sleep,* Bramble observed cheerfully. *Good. Less work for me.*

* *

The queue moved like molasses poured uphill.

They waited four hours in a wind that came off the high snowfields with knives in it. Ahead of them, a wool merchant argued himself hoarse; behind them, the dyer family prayed in whispers; beyond them, the mountain stood with its new silence like a held breath. Gideon spent the hours doing what he did best: becoming nobody. He slouched. He let his shoulders round and his jaw go slack and his eyes settle into the mild bovine patience of a man whose thoughts never rose above freight rates and the price of nails. He rehearsed his story until it wore smooth as a river stone: Gid Okoro, carter out of Millbrook, hauling for Harl & Sons, salt fish and wool and tin, going down-country to Merrowgate for the spring markets. Wife, Sera. Quiet woman. Bad knee.

It was good work, the making of a false man, and he had done it so long it had worn grooves in him, comfortable as old boot-leather. What was not comfortable was how closely the rehearsal brushed against the thing underneath. Because Gid Okoro was a lie, and the needle would taste it, and—

No. He shut that thought away. The needle tasted spoken lies; so said the stories. So he would give it nothing warm to taste. He would answer questions the way water answers stone—around, around, around.

When their turn came, the light had gone long and amber, and the torches had taken over from the sun.

The inquisitor who received them was younger than Gideon expected, with a scholar's soft hands and a soldier's cropped hair, and he smiled too readily, the smile of a man who had learned that courtesy unsettles the guilty more than cruelty does. "Names and business, friend."

"Gid Okoro, master. Carter, out of Millbrook. Hauling for Harl and Sons—fish, wool, tin nails. Down to Merrowgate for the markets, gods willing the roads allow."

"And the wife?"

"Sera." Gideon squeezed her arm, gently, the way a tired husband might. She dropped her eyes and mumbled something about her knee, and it was such a perfect performance of meekness that he nearly admired it. "She's not one for talking, master. Takes after her mother."

The young inquisitor laughed, warm and easy, and gestured them through the outer checkpoint with a flourish—and Gideon allowed himself exactly one breath of relief before the flourish ended in a raised finger. "But you understand, friend. Everyone goes to the table now. New orders from the capital. There's been—" the smile widened a fraction— "unrest. We're checking for unrest."

The table stood just inside the shadow of the gatehouse: a plank across two barrels, a brazier burning low and blue—blue, Gideon noted, wrong color for charcoal, something fed into the fire—and beside the brazier, on a square of black velvet, the needle.

It was longer than he'd imagined. Six inches of yellowed bone, thin as a darning implement, polished to a dull shine by years of hands. It lay there ordinary as a sewing tool, and somehow that ordinariness was worse than any display of menace could have been. Tools like that weren't ornaments. Tools like that were used daily.

"Lay your hand flat on the table," the inquisitor said pleasantly. "This won't hurt unless it needs to."

Gideon laid his hand down. The wood was cold through his palm. Behind him he heard Sera guided to stand aside, heard the soldiers begin on the flour sacks, heard Bramble being led away with a soldier's grunt of effort—the mule weighed as much as sin and cooperated like it, too. All of it registered in the corner of his mind and none of it mattered, because the young inquisitor was lifting the needle, and the firelight slid along its length like oil along a blade.

"Now then. Name?"

"Gid Okoro."

The needle hovered over the back of his hand. Nothing. Warmth would come as heat; the stories were clear on that. He watched it the way a man watches a wasp circling his cup.

"Business?"

"Freight. Fish and wool down, tin back up if the price holds."

Nothing again. The needle hung patient as a plumb line. *Answer around,* he reminded himself. *Water and stone.* Every answer true-shaped: freight was freight, markets were markets, and nowhere in the sentences did he say *I am only a carter*, because that sentence was a lie and lies had weight, and weight was what the bone was listening for.

"You've traveled this pass before, Okoro?"

"Twice a year, ten years. Never seen the gates closed." True. All true.

"Seen anything strange lately? On the road, in the hills?" The inquisitor's eyes came up from the needle to Gideon's face, mild and hungry at once. "Anything about the stars?"

There it was. The real question wearing a traveler's clothes. Gideon felt the trap's shape with the same sense that read weather in his teeth—the pause placed a half-beat too long, the question shaped to catch either denial or eagerness. He let his brow fold, let confusion climb his face honestly, because confusion was true: he *had* seen something strange, and thinking of it hurt.

"Stars went out, master. Whole camp saw it. Beasts went mad that night—wolves screaming down in the valleys, horses tearing loose. Lost two pack animals to panic." All true, every word, and the needle stayed cool as riverbed stone. "Thought it was a judgment. Still think maybe it is."

"Mm." The inquisitor studied him a moment longer. "Judgment on whom, I wonder."

"Wouldn't know, master. I'm just a carter."

And there—the faintest tremor. Not warmth, not yet, but the needle dipped, swayed, as though tasting the air above his knuckles. *Just a carter.* The sentence had a hollow in it, and hollows had echoes.

The inquisitor noticed. Of course he noticed; noticing was his trade. He leaned in, courteous as a confessor. "Just a carter," he repeated softly. "Is that so, Okoro? Only that? Nothing else about you? No other trade? No other... talents?"

The world narrowed to the width of a bone needle.

Later, Gideon could never say precisely what happened in that moment—whether it was instinct, or the gift itself rising to defend its own burial, or simply the oldest law of the wild, which is that a cornered thing goes still. But he found himself looking at the inquisitor, truly looking, past the smile into the tired grey depths beneath it, and he let the smallest thread of his hidden self slip out—not speech, not command, just presence, just the animal fact of him, calm and harmless as a sleeping dog. *Nothing here,* that thread said. *Nothing to fear. Move along.*

And the inquisitor blinked, and yawned—actually yawned, pressing the back of his wrist to his mouth with a flush of embarrassment—and straightened up, waving the needle vaguely. "Clean, then. Go on through, Okoro. Keep your beasts off the verge; there's a column coming up from the south and they take fright at the wagons."

"Yes, master. Thank you, master."

Gideon gathered his hand off the table with fingers that shook only once he'd turned away.

* *

They cleared the gates as the light died.

Sera caught up with him a hundred yards past the checkpoint, walking fast, breath smoking, fury radiating off her like heat off a forge. "The flour sacks," she hissed. "They went through every stitch. One of them had my spare stylus in his hand, turning it over, asking why a carter's wife owned a cartographer's stylus—"

"And you said?"

"I said it was my father's, that I keep it for luck, that I can't draw a straight line to save my soul." She laughed, short and jagged, without humor. "And the needle was on *your* hand, not mine, and the soldier believed me, or was bored, or both. We walked through a sealed pass carrying the oldest maps in the kingdom sewn into my shirt, and the thing that saved us was a bored conscript and a dead man's pen."

"Luck favors the prepared," Gideon said.

"Don't quote proverbs at me. You went white as whey at that table. I saw you."

"Did I?" He kept his eyes on the road. "Cold evening."

"Gideon." She stepped in front of him, forcing him to stop. In the last light her face was all shadows and sharp planes, and her voice dropped to something stripped bare. "That needle stopped. Right at the end. It moved—I saw it move—and then that man yawned like a schoolboy and forgot we existed. What did you do?"

The honest answer sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. *I touched his mind. I did the thing I have hidden since I was six years old, on an open road, in front of the usurper's own priest, because I was afraid.* He looked at her—at this woman who carried pre-kingdom roads against her spine and asked questions like a blade drawn slowly—and understood that the ledger between them had just changed columns. She had seen the mask slip. She could sell him to the Inquisition with a single sentence.

So could he, of course. Maps against gifts. Blackmail had kept stranger marriages warm.

"Wind gust," he said. "Off the snowfield. Plays havoc with a flame that size."

She stared at him for a long moment. Wind gusted, obligingly, right on cue, and neither of them believed it for an instant.

"All right," she said finally. "Keep your secrets, carter. But understand what you bought us back there. Those gates aren't checking travelers anymore—they're hunting someone. Did you hear the soldier talk about a column coming up from the south? Inquisitors don't post columns for smugglers. They posted them because they lost something in these mountains." Her hand drifted, unconsciously, to her spine, to the silk-wrapped secret against it. "Or someone."

Ahead of them the road descended into the gathering dark, switchbacking down toward the valley and the long road to Merrowgate. Somewhere below, a wolf began to sing—one clear, wavering note, the first wild voice Gideon had heard since the stars went out. It did not sound like hunger. It sounded like a signal.

Bramble's ears swiveled toward it. *That's not a wolf song,* the mule said quietly. *That's a call. And it knows our names.*

Gideon went still. "Whose names?"

*Yours,* said Bramble. *It said yours.*
