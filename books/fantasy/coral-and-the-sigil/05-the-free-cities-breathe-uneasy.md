## 5. The Free Cities Breathe Uneasy

Merrowgate announced itself before we saw it, the way a body announces fever.

Gideon felt it first—he always felt things first, though he pretended otherwise—and I learned to read it in the way his shoulders tightened and his chin lifted, nostrils flaring like some animal testing wind. "Smell that?" he said.

I smelled salt, tar, woodsmoke, fish gone soft in the heat. A hundred thousand lives pressed against a harbor. "A city."

"A city *afraid*." He said it without drama, the way he said most true things, as if reporting weather. "Fear has a smell. Sour. Like milk turning. Every city I've ever run cargo through smells of it a little. This one reeks."

We came down off the ridge road at dusk with the caravan strung out behind us—six wagons, nineteen souls who had paid Gideon Okonkwo to move them quietly from Thornfell's shadow into whatever waited beyond, and one cartographer who had paid him nothing at all but had become, through some alchemy neither of us named, part of the freight. Below us Merrowgate spread across its peninsula like spilled coins: lantern-light along the quays, masts thick as winter reeds, the great sea-gates open and torch-lit. The free cities kept no king. They kept ledgers instead, and councils of merchants who governed by weight of gold and weight of grievance, and they had never once, in all their history, closed their gates at night.

The sea-gates stood shut.

Torches burned along their tops. Crossbowmen walked the parapets in pairs. And on the windward tower hung a banner I did not recognize—a black field bearing a white circle, empty, like an eye with nothing behind it.

"That's new," Gideon said quietly.

"It's not a sigil," I said, because my mouth is faster than my sense sometimes. He turned to look at me, and in the last grey light his face was unreadable in the particular way of a man deciding how much you know and whether knowing it makes you dangerous. "The usurper's mark would be different," I went on, because stopping now would be worse. "That's a ward-mark. Or what a ward-mark looks like when someone draws it who doesn't understand it. An empty circle. A ward with nothing inside it. Whoever raised that banner wanted something that looked official."

"And you'd know what official ward-marks look like because—"

"Because my family drew them," I said. "Before the kingdom did. Before there was a kingdom to draw them for."

He was silent for a long moment. Down in the harbor a bell rang the hour, and the sound came up the hill flattened, wrong, as though the air itself were stuffed with wool. I had been hearing that wrongness since Thornfell—the silence where the wards should have sung—but here it was worse. Here it layered under everything, a missing note so vast the whole world seemed tuned around its absence.

"You keep saying things like that," Gideon said at last. "And then not saying anything else. It's a habit I'm starting to hate."

"Get in line. So do I."

* * *

They let us in after an hour of waiting and three questions asked by a gate-serjeant whose hands shook.

Not from cold. From exhaustion, or from fear, or from both wearing each other down to a fine grit. He looked at our papers—Gideon's flawless smuggler documents, which named him a dealer in dyestuffs out of Corvess—and then at me, and then past us at the road we'd come down, as if expecting something to be following us out of the dark.

"Were you on the coast road?" he asked. "South of the salt-marsh?"

"We came the ridge way," Gideon said.

"Then you didn't see." The serjeant's voice had the flatness of a man repeating news until the words wore smooth. "Three days back. The beacon towers south of the marsh went dark. Not burnt out—dark. All of them at once, same hour, same minute by every clock in the district. And the fog that came after." He rubbed his arms. "You could stand on the quay and watch it come up the channel like a tide. Ships rowed into it and didn't come out. Two did. Crews alive. Nobody home behind their eyes. They just sat and smiled."

"Where did the fog stop?" I asked.

He blinked at me as if the question were obscene. "At the old boundary stones. The ones nobody's used since my grandfather's grandfather. It came right up to them and stopped, like water finding a wall." His laugh came out cracked. "Like something was keeping it out. Except everyone knows the old stones don't do anything anymore. Everyone knows the wards are stories."

Everyone knows, I thought. That is precisely the problem with everyone knowing.

Inside the walls, Merrowgate breathed uneasy indeed. The Harbor Council had tripled the watch and doubled the tolls, which told you exactly how frightened they were: frightened enough to spend money, never frightened enough to admit why. Refugees camped in the Fishmarket under canvas scraps, in the colonnades of the money-changers, on the stone steps of the Tide-Temple where priests who had never fed anyone in living memory ladled out barley soup with shaking hands. I counted them as we passed, the way I count everything—it was the first thing my mother taught me, *a mapmaker who cannot count cannot be trusted with a coastline*—and stopped counting at four hundred because four hundred was more than rumor and less than catastrophe, which meant the number was growing faster than anyone would say aloud.

I heard the rumors anyway, because rumors in a frightened city travel faster than carts and carry heavier loads.

*Vellmark's gone silent,* said a fishwife to her neighbor, in the tone of someone discussing weather. *Whole city. No ships in, no birds out. My cousin's husband trades wool there.*

*Harrowsdeep too,* said her neighbor. *Since the stars went out. They say the lighthouse still burns but there's no keeper.*

*It's plague,* insisted a third voice, a man with a merchant's rings and a coward's certainty. *Plague out of the east. It's always plague.*

*Plague that stops at boundary stones?*

Nobody answered him. Nobody needed to. In Merrowgate that night, the truth moved through the crowd like a current under ice: visible everywhere, touchable nowhere, and cold enough to kill.

Gideon got our party lodged above a dyer's warehouse in the Tanners' Quarter—of course he did; a man built of secrets finds lodging among people professionally uninterested in questions—and then stood at the shuttered window a long time, listening to the city with whatever sense it was he wouldn't name.

"Three," he said finally. "Thornfell, Vellmark, Harrowsdeep. Three ward-cities silent. Plus whatever the beacon towers were watching."

"The old records call them anchor-cities," I said. "Cities sited on ward-stones. There were nine once."

He turned. In the lamplight his eyes were very still, the way animals go still. "Nine."

"Seven remain." I heard my own voice go thin. "Assuming the three that have gone silent are the three I think they are."

"And the other six?"

"Scattered. The capital. Two coastal cities. And—" I stopped.

"Sera." He almost never used my name; when he did, it landed like a hand on the shoulder. "Whatever you're about to decide whether to tell me—decide faster. We're standing in a city that's next on somebody's list, and I don't plan to be here when it happens. But I need to know what list you're reading."

So I told him. Or began to.

* * *

My family were mapmakers before the kingdom had borders worth mapping.

That sentence sounds like boasting. It is the opposite. It is a confession, and I have been carrying it the way Gideon carries his gift: hidden, denied, and costing me daily. The Voss family did not draw maps of roads that weren't there because we were forgers in spirit. We drew them because we were the last custodians of roads and boundaries that *predate the kingdom*—the old ways, the old stones, the network the first kings found already humming beneath their feet and claimed as their own protection. Every royal cartographer's office keeps a sealed archive. Every archive contains maps no crown should own. And every Voss who entered that office swore an oath, generation after generation, that the oldest of those maps would never leave our hands, never be copied, and never—*never*—be read aloud.

Because the oldest map has writing on it. And the writing is a name.

I had left the archive behind when I fled the capital. Of course I had; I fled with nothing but my satchel and the forged charts sewn into my coat lining. But my mother taught me the way she taught me counting: *a mapmaker who cannot remember cannot be trusted with anything.* I had spent eleven years in the royal office copying and recopying that map in my head, because memory was the only copy no inquisitor could confiscate.

I took paper from Gideon's cargo—one sheet of good rag stock, worth more than the wagon carrying it—and charcoal, and by dyer's lamp in that warehouse room, with the sour smell of fear drifting up from the streets, I drew it.

I drew the circle of the realm's old wards: nine anchor-points joined by lines that were not roads and not rivers but something older, the leys along which the ward-stones spoke to one another. I drew the three points gone dark and filled them in—Thornfell at the mountain wall, Vellmark on the eastern plain, Harrowsdeep at the northern coast—and saw, as I had known I would see, that they made a line. A slow arc, sweeping west and south.

Toward Merrowgate. Toward the free cities. Toward, eventually, the capital itself—not as a destination but as a table, a place to sit and feed.

"They're not random," I said. My charcoal-hand was steady; the rest of me wasn't. "Whoever is doing this is eating the wards in order. Along the old lines. Each stone feeds the next—or rather, each drained stone weakens the next, the way felling trees on a slope brings the rest down. Thornfell first, then Vellmark, then Harrowsdeep. The next will be—" I touched the fourth point, and my finger stopped, because naming it made it real, and making it real was the thing my family had spent two hundred years refusing to do.

"Say it," Gideon said softly.

"Cauldsholm." A river-city, rich and proud, forty leagues southwest. "Cauldsholm goes silent within the month. Then the two coastal anchors. And then there's nothing between this thing and the capital except the capital's own walls, which were built on the ninth stone, which means the throne room sits directly over the largest reservoir of ward-power in the world."

I watched him understand it. Watched it arrive in him not as thought but as weather—as dread moving across a face built for hiding dread.

"The king," he said. "The king sits on top of it."

"The king sits on top of it," I agreed. "And the stars went out the night Thornfell's wards died, and no one in the capital has said one word about either. Either Aldric doesn't know—which is impossible—or Aldric isn't telling anyone. Or—" I couldn't finish it. I had been unable to finish it since the night I ran.

"Or the king is the reason," Gideon finished for me, in a voice gone very quiet.

I put down the charcoal. Then, because half-truths had gotten us exactly this far and no further, I gave him the rest.

"There's a name on the oldest map. Written in the margin, in the first hand, in the script they used before the kingdom had letters of its own. Every Voss learns to recognize it and swear never to speak it. It isn't the name of a place. It isn't the name of a person. It's the name of a *binding*—the binding the wards were built to hold shut, or hold down, or hold apart; the archives disagree, or pretend to. The founders wrote it once and hid it, and the kings forgot it on purpose, and my family remembered it on purpose, and the entire difference between those two verbs is why I'm sitting in a dye-house instead of a palace." I looked at the map I had drawn, at the three blackened points, at the arc crawling toward the heart of everything. "Someone remembers it too. Someone found the name, and found the king, and is using the crown's own authority to take the wards apart stone by stone. Which means whoever it is doesn't want the realm destroyed. Destruction is loud. This is quiet. This is *harvest.*"

The lamp guttered. Outside, a patrol passed, boots ringing on wet cobbles, and somewhere a child cried and was hushed.

Gideon stood over the map for a long time. When he finally spoke, it was in the flat tone I had learned meant he was more afraid than he would ever admit.

"So we know what's happening, and roughly where, and roughly when. What we don't know is who, or how to stop it, or how to fight something that eats the protections we'd fight it with." He tapped the fourth point on the map, Cauldsholm, gently, the way you'd touch a wound to learn its depth. "But a drained stone is still a stone. If your arc is right, there'll be a husk at Thornfell we can reach before Cauldsholm falls. Whatever's left of it—whatever this binder leaves behind—I want to see it. I want to hear it." Something moved behind his eyes, some gift stirring toward use like a dog waking. "Dead things talk too. If you know how to listen."

"I don't want to know how you know that," I said.

"No," he agreed. "You don't."

* * *

We were woken before dawn by bells—not the hour-bells but the alarm, the harsh doubled peal from the sea-tower that Merrowgate had not rung, the dyer's wife said white-faced on the stairs, since the pirate wars.

We went up to the roof with half the Tanners' Quarter and looked south across the black water, where every eye in the city was looking, and saw it.

Fog. Out on the channel, where no fog should be on a clear starlit night—where no fog *could* be, because the stars were out now, returned two nights past in a flickering, uncertain way that had the whole city arguing whether it was mercy or mockery. The fog stood offshore like a wall, like a held breath, like something patient at a door it had not yet decided to open.

And beneath it, faint as memory, the boundary stones of the old southern road were glowing. Pale blue-white, steady, the light of a ward doing the last work it knew how to do.

Holding.

For now.

Beside me in the crowd's jostle, Gideon went rigid. I saw his lips move—soundlessly, unconsciously, the way a man answers a voice only he can hear—and whatever he heard made him close his eyes as if against a blow.

"What," I whispered. "What is it?"

His eyes opened. They were fixed not on the fog but on the glowing stones, and his face held a look I would come to know well in the months ahead: horror and resolve fused into a single hard grain, like sand pressed into pearl.

"The stones aren't holding it back," he said. "They're calling for help. All of them. Same word, over and over." He swallowed. "They're calling for a ward-keeper. And there hasn't been one in two hundred years."

Below us the alarm bells went on ringing, and the fog at the edge of the lights did not move, and somewhere to the southwest—in Cauldsholm, asleep beside its river—thirty thousand people lay dreaming, unaware that they were next.
