## 6. A Parliament of Strays

The underways of Merrowgate smelled of wet stone and tallow and old secrets, and Gideon Okonkwo walked into them the way he did everything—quietly, sideways, as if he were only passing through.

He had told Sera to wait above. She had refused with a look that said she would follow him into his own grave if it suited her maps, so they descended together through a fishmonger's cellar, past barrels of brine that wept salt down their staves, along a stair worn hollow by three hundred years of feet that preferred not to be counted. The dark came up to meet them like water rising in a hold. Somewhere below, voices.

"Charming," Sera murmured, one hand on the wall. Her fingers trailed the stone the way they trailed everything—reading it. "These aren't smuggler tunnels."

"No."

"They're older than smuggling." She stopped. "Gideon. These are ward-roads. Look at the mortar." She scraped at a seam between stones with her thumbnail and held up a grain of something pale and faintly luminous even here, even in this dark. "Ward-chalk. This was laid by keepers. Before the kingdom drew its borders on top of it."

Gideon had known that for twenty years, the way he knew which taverns watered their beer and which harbormasters took bribes. He had never told anyone. Knowledge was currency, and he spent it carefully, and he had learned young that the old roads beneath the old roads belonged to people who did not want to be found—which meant they belonged to him, or to no one, which amounted to the same thing.

"Keep walking," he said.

The voices grew. Not the furtive mutter of contraband traders—he knew that music, could conduct it in his sleep—but something stranger. A woman's voice rising in argument. A child laughing, high and unguarded, which no child did in a smuggler den. And underneath it all, threaded through like a bass note felt in the ribs rather than heard: animals. The shifting weight of hooves on stone, the soft complaint of feathers, the particular silence of many creatures choosing not to make noise.

His gift woke in him like a second heartbeat. *Careful*, it said, in his mother's voice, though his mother had been dead eleven years and he had spent two memories already just keeping her face whole. *Careful. They will hear you listening.*

That was the trouble with what he was. Listening was not passive. When he opened himself to beast-speech, he opened a door, and doors swung both ways, and things on the other side sometimes noticed the draft.

He kept the door shut. Barely.

The tunnel opened without warning into a cavern that took his breath and held it hostage.

It had been a cistern once—a vast vaulted chamber where some pre-kingdom engineer had taught rainwater to behave—and now it was a city of the unwanted. Lanterns hung from iron rings on chains, dozens of them, swaying slightly in air that moved with no wind, throwing light that pooled and slid across the floor like something alive. Tents of sailcloth and patched velvet clustered against the walls. Cook-fires burned in iron braziers, and the smoke rose and gathered under the vaulting in a low grey ceiling that smelled of onions and woodsmoke and horse. People moved among it all: a dozen, two dozen, more, emerging from side-tunnels, hauling water, mending harness, arguing over a game of knucklebones played with what looked suspiciously like real knuckles.

And beasts. Beasts everywhere. Goats tethered near the fires. A flock of geese parading with the confidence of creditors. Dogs—real dogs, not the starved semi-wolves of the border roads—lying among the tents like citizens. A brown bear dozed against a pillar with a blanket over its haunches, and no one gave it a second glance.

Sera made a small sound beside him. "Gideon."

"I see it."

"The wards fell and everything that could run, ran," she whispered. "They didn't just flee north or east. They fled *down*."

A shadow detached itself from a tent flap and became a man—or mostly a man, broad as a door, with a deserter's cropped hair growing out unevenly and a scar that split one eyebrow into a shrub. He carried a billhook with the ease of long acquaintance.

"You're lost," the man said pleasantly, in the tone of someone who had buried people for saying less.

"We're looking for passage," Gideon said. "South road, then west."

"Passage costs."

"So I've heard."

The man's eyes went over them both—the way Gideon stood with his weight balanced, the ink stains on Sera's fingers, the quality of their boots—and whatever arithmetic he ran, it came out inconclusive. "Names?"

"No," said Gideon.

Something flickered in the man's face—not anger. Recognition. "Right answer," he said. "Wrong question. We don't deal in names here. That's rather the point." He jerked his chin toward the cavern. "You'll want the parliament, then. Everyone does, eventually. Follow me, touch nothing, and if the goose honks at you, apologize. She holds grudges."

* * *

The parliament convened, apparently, wherever there was space, which tonight was a ring of overturned crates near the great dry basin at the chamber's heart. Gideon counted thirty-odd souls arranged around it in no order he could parse—no elders at the head, no armed men flanking the exits, none of the grammar of power he'd spent his life learning to read. A woman with a ward-keeper's faded blue sash worn as a scarf sat next to a boy of perhaps fourteen with a poacher's snare-coil slung over his shoulder. Next to him, a priestess of some faith Gideon didn't recognize, her hands stained with lamp-oil. Next to her—

Next to her sat a mule.

Not tied. Not harnessed. Just sitting, forelegs folded under her like a dowager arranging her skirts, ears at an angle of profound skepticism, watching the proceedings with the weary attention of someone who had heard every argument ever invented and believed none of them.

Gideon's heart performed a maneuver he did not authorize.

Because he knew that mule. Grey-flanked, white-blazed, one ear permanently crumpled from an old rope burn—Bramble, who had carried contraband salt out of Thornfell for six years under a driver named Hesk who beat her on Mondays because it was Monday. Gideon had spoken to her twice, both times in passing, both times telling her which path was safe when Hesk's greed outran his sense. The second time, three winters ago, in the snow, she had looked at him with one rolling eye and said, in the flat gravel voice all mules seemed to have, *You're the one who talks.*

And he had said nothing, and ridden on, and told himself she wouldn't remember, and hated himself a little for how much he hoped she would.

She remembered. He could see it arriving in her face now, the slow turn of her head, the ears swinging toward him like weathervanes finding wind. Her nostrils flared. Her lips peeled back from her teeth in what any horseman would have called a threat display and what Gideon, with a lurch, recognized as a grin.

*Well*, said Bramble, to the entire cavern, in a voice like a millstone rolling uphill, *as I live and breathe. The quiet man. The one who whispers to donkeys in the snow and then pretends he didn't.*

Every head turned. Thirty faces, and behind them the geese went silent, and the bear lifted its head, and somewhere in the dark of a side-tunnel something large breathed in.

Gideon did the thing he had trained himself to do since he was nine years old and his father caught him talking to a dying ewe and called a priest: he reached for invisibility. Shoulders down. Eyes soft. The face of a man who is nobody, who has always been nobody, whose presence is a thing you forget the moment you look away.

It didn't work. It had never once worked on Bramble.

*I know what you're doing*, she said, conversationally, loudly enough that the humans nearest her shifted uneasily—they couldn't hear words, but they could hear *that* a beast was speaking, and the fact rippled outward through the cavern like a stone dropped in still water. *I watched you do it for six years. You wear nothing like a coat. It doesn't fit you anymore, quiet man. Nothing fits you anymore. That's why you're here.*

"I'm not here," Gideon said aloud, before he could stop himself, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it was true in every direction and everyone heard it.

The woman with the ward-keeper's sash stood up.

* * *

Her name was Odessa Vane, though she did not offer it yet; he would learn it later, on a worse night, at a worse stone. Tonight she was simply the one who spoke first, and the others arranged themselves around her voice the way iron filings arrange around a lodestone.

"You heard Bramble," she said. Her accent was capital-city, educated, sandpapered at the edges by exile. "Some of you heard words. Most of you heard a mule talk, which I grant is alarming enough. For those who are new—" her gaze touched Gideon and stayed there a moment too long, weighing him, cataloguing him, "—this is what we are. We are the Unlisted. No census counts us. No guild claims us. No crown protects us, and lately no crown even pretends to. Ward-keepers whose wards died screaming. Deserters who wouldn't burn villages. Smugglers, poachers, priests of outlawed gods, and beasts who felt the wards fall and understood, better than we did, what it meant."

"What did it mean?" Sera asked. She had come forward to stand beside Gideon, and he noticed she did it deliberately, planting herself in his periphery like a witness.

Odessa looked at her. "You tell me. You're Voss. Cartographer. Your family drew the old charts." A murmur went around the crates. "Yes, we know. We know more than you'd like. The wards didn't fail, cartographer. Wards don't fail. They're woven, and weaving doesn't rot—it gets cut, or it gets *eaten*. Three cities gone silent now. Thornfell, Harrowmere, and as of four nights past, Callow's Reach. Whatever is drinking them is working west, toward the capital, and it is patient, and it is *hungry*, and the king's men are sealing passes and hanging mapmakers instead of asking why."

"That's why you're underground," Gideon said. "Hiding."

"Hiding?" Odessa smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a blade being shown its edge. "We're *recruiting*. There's a difference. Hiding is what you were doing, Okonkwo. Six years of whispering to beasts and vanishing. Bramble's told us all about you."

*Bramble*, Gideon said, in beast-speech, sharp as a whipcrack, *we had an understanding.*

*We had nothing*, Bramble replied placidly, chewing on nothing. *Understanding requires two parties willing to admit the other exists. You spent six years pretending you were weather. I don't gossip about weather.* She turned her ruined ear toward him. *But these people needed to know what you are, because these people need what you are, and you were never going to say it yourself. Somebody had to say your name for you. That's all belonging is, quiet man. Someone saying your name when you won't.*

The words went into him deeper than he wanted them to. He stood very still and let them hurt.

"The passes are sealed," Odessa continued, addressing the circle again. "Inquisitors on every road with bone needles and bloodhounds. The Ferryman's Guild still moves cargo, but their price has gone up since the silences started—everything costs more when the world ends, that's economics. We need to reach the first fallen ward-stone at Thornfell before whatever drained it finishes digesting and moves on. We need to learn what unbinding looks like before there's nothing left to unbind." She spread her hands. "Which means we need a guide who knows the old roads. Every road. Including the ones that aren't on any map, including the ones that were never supposed to exist."

Thirty pairs of eyes turned to Gideon. Plus one pair of rolling, cynical, horribly knowing mule eyes.

"I work alone," he said.

"Of course you do," said Odessa. "Everyone here worked alone. That's how we all ended up in a hole under a fishmonger's cellar. Alone is how they win, Okonkwo. Alone is the whole strategy. Divide the realm into people who are safe and people who are noticed, and let the noticed ones hang one at a time." She stepped closer. Up close he could see the exhaustion under her composure, the fine tremor in her hands that she kept folded to hide. "You heard the wards die. Don't lie to me—I can see it in you, you carry it like a stone in the boot. So did we. And you know what killed them wasn't failure. So either you walk out of here and go back to being weather, and in a year there's nothing left to be invisible inside of—or you stop pretending, and you guide us, and you hate every minute of it, and you live."

The cavern waited. The lanterns swayed. The bear sighed and resettled. Somewhere a child was asleep against a goat's flank, rising and falling with its breathing.

Gideon looked at Bramble.

*Say no*, she said. *Go on. Say no and mean it. I'll wait.*

He thought about the wolf dying in his arms on the mountain, the sigil burning behind its eyes, the memory of a king's chest opening like a door. He thought about his rule—*never be noticed*—and how the rule had never once saved anyone but himself, and how thin a life that was, stretched over twenty years, a life spent entirely in the negative space of other people's sight.

He thought about how, standing here, accused and exposed and conscripted, some locked room in his chest had quietly unbolted itself, and he did not know what to do with the draft coming through.

"I'll guide you," he said. "To the first stone. After that we renegotiate."

"Everyone renegotiates," Odessa said. "Welcome to the Unlisted."

* * *

Later—much later, when the parliament had dissolved into watch-rotations and the lanterns had been dimmed to coals—Bramble found him at the edge of the basin, where he sat with his back against cold stone, doing the breathing exercise his mother had taught him for the nights the gift got loud.

*Couldn't sleep?* she asked, folding herself down beside him with a groan of joints and grievance.

"Mules don't ask questions they know the answers to."

*True. Then I'll ask one I don't.* She rested her heavy head briefly against his shoulder, an intimacy that would have cost her dignity anywhere else and cost her nothing here. *Why did you really say yes?*

He was quiet a long time. Water dripped somewhere in the deeps of the cistern, patient as a clock.

"When the wolf died," he said finally, "nobody else saw it. Nobody else will ever see it. It happened to me alone, and I'll carry it alone, and that's—" He stopped. Started again. "I've been carrying things alone my whole life. It turns out I'm tired. That's all. I'm tired, and there were thirty people down here who needed the exact thing I've spent my life hiding, and it felt less like being caught and more like—" He gestured vaguely at the sleeping cavern, the geese, the child, the bear, the whole improbable parliament of the discarded. "*Being useful.*"

Bramble chewed on that, and on nothing, for a while.

*Hesk used to say I was stubborn*, she said at last. *He meant it as a flaw. But stubbornness is just loyalty that hasn't picked anyone yet.* She closed her eyes. *Pick well, quiet man. Or don't pick. Just stop running long enough to notice who's running beside you.*

He might have answered. He never got the chance, because at that moment Sera Voss came down the tunnel at a pace that meant bad news, a scrap of paper in her fist and her face grey in the lantern-light.

"Gideon." She held out the paper—a guild chit, wax-stamped, ferryman's black. "One of Odessa's runners pulled this off a courier an hour ago. The Guild's posted new terms for western passage. Anyone traveling toward Thornfell pays a toll now." Her jaw tightened. "And the toll isn't coin."

She turned the chit over. On the back, in the Guild's neat, mercenary hand, three words:

**THE PRICE IS A NAME.**

Bramble opened one eye.

*Ah*, she said. *There's the hook.*
