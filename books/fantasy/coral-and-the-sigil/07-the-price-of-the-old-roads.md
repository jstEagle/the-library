## 7. The Price of the Old Roads

The river at Merrowgate's back door was called the Lachrymose, and Sera Voss thought the name was honest, which made it rare.

Most names in the free cities were lies wearing good boots. *Merchant's Rest* meant a tollhouse. *The Kindly Ferry* meant you paid twice. But the Lachrymose actually wept — that was the thing about it. It ran down from the high fells where snowmelt gathered in stone basins and dripped through a thousand cracks, so that walking its banks was like walking beside a wall of mourners who never stopped crying. The sound got into your teeth. After three days in Merrowgate listening to refugee rumor and counting silent ward-cities on her fingers, Sera found she didn't mind it. Grief with an honest name was almost restful.

She stood on the shingle bank where the river widened into black glass, watching the ferry come across, and tried to think of her sister without flinching.

Liss. Second-born, quicker than her, dead four years now, and Sera had not said the name aloud since the funeral because saying it felt like picking up a hot coal to show someone. Look. See how it burns me. Isn't that enough for one lifetime.

Apparently it wasn't.

The ferry was a flat barge poled by two figures in oilskin hoods, and it came out of the mist the way things did on this river: all at once, as if the water had been holding it in its mouth and finally spat. On the prow hung a lantern of green glass, and inside the green light sat a woman whose age Sera could not fix — sixty or sixteen or both, with hair the color of wet iron and eyes like coins at the bottom of a well.

"The Ferryman's Guild," Gideon said beside her. He'd been quiet the whole walk down from the city, which Sera had learned was his way of being loud. "They don't take coin."

"I know."

"Then you know what they take."

"A name." She kept her eyes on the water. "I've done my reading. The old ward-maps mark the crossings. Every one of them has a note in the margin — *passage purchased, price a name.* My grandmother wrote it like it was arithmetic."

"It isn't arithmetic." Gideon shifted his weight, and the gravel spoke under his boot, and he stilled again, annoyed at himself. She'd noticed that about him too — how he moved like a man perpetually apologizing to silence. "Names are currency here the way gold is everywhere else. They spend them. I don't know what they do with them. Nobody who's sold one has ever described it well."

"And nobody who hasn't sold one knows anything at all." Sera turned to face him. In the grey light his dark skin looked carved, his scars like seams in old leather. "Gideon. The first ward-stone is nine days' hard march if we go overland, through passes sealed by inquisitors who have your description and mine. Or it's two days by river, under their noses, through country no soldier watches because no soldier can buy passage. We need the ferry."

"I know we need the ferry." His jaw worked. "I'm deciding what we're willing to be afterward."

"That's what I like about you," Sera said. "You decide things slowly enough that everyone gets to watch."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; smugglers didn't waste smiles — and then the ferry touched the shingle and there was no more time for talking, because the woman in the green lantern-light stepped onto the bank without the boat moving at all, which Sera's eyes reported and her mind refused.

"You're the cartographer," the woman said. Her voice had the river in it, layered, several currents at once. "And the beast-speaker. And whatever else is following you around like a smell." Her coin-colored eyes settled on Gideon, and stayed. "You especially. You're loud, for a quiet man."

"We want passage to the Hollow Reach," Sera said. "Two days downriver, landing at the old quarry stair."

"Everyone wants passage somewhere." The woman walked the bank slowly, hood back now, touching nothing and yet seeming to read everything — the gravel, the reeds, the mist. "The Guild runs this crossing the way the kingdom used to run its wards. Quietly. Reliably. For a price that hurts. That last part matters. A price that doesn't hurt buys nothing worth having. Coin? Coin is nothing. Coin is what you give strangers. We ask for what you'd give family." She stopped. Turned. "A name."

"Any name?" Sera asked, though she already knew.

"One of yours. Truly yours. Given to you in love or given to you in blood — but given, not chosen. You'll say it into the lantern, and the river will keep it, and while the river keeps it, you will not be able to say it again. Not to anyone. Not to yourself, inside. Try, and you'll find the word gone, like a tooth pulled clean. You won't remember the shape of losing it after a season. Most people don't. That's the mercy of the trade."

Silence, except the Lachrymose weeping along its stones.

Sera had known, standing on the shingle, that she would do it. She had rehearsed it, in fact, in the small hours of the night — rehearsed the sentence, *my sister's name is* — because rehearsing grief was the only kind she could bear. Liss had been dead four years. The name was a coal in a cold hearth. Let the river have it. Let the river hold something warm for once instead of just weeping about it.

She stepped forward. "My sister—"

"No."

Gideon's voice cracked across the bank like a whip over water, and Sera actually stumbled half a step, she was so unprepared for the force of it. He had gone very still — that terrible stillness of his — and when she looked at him his eyes were bright with something between fury and panic.

"She's offering a dead woman's name," he said to the ferrymistress. "That's not a price. That's a grave-robbery she's doing to herself. You know it. I can see you know it."

"The Guild sets terms, not judgments."

"Then hear counter-terms." He pulled off one glove, finger by finger, and Sera realized what he was going to do about three seconds before he did it, and her stomach dropped through the shingle, through the bedrock, into the cold heart of the mountain.

"Gideon—" she started.

"Take mine instead." He held his bare hand over the green lantern. "Not my name. Worse than my name. My scent."

The ferrymistress went very still, in the way of creatures deciding whether to strike.

"Scent is not a word," she said. "It is not tidy. Names sit in a lantern and stay put. Scent is alive. It drifts. It changes with the wind and the weather and the body that carries it." Her eyes narrowed. "And this scent — I know what rides on this scent. Hounds of the inquisition have been bred seven generations deep to find exactly this. Beast-blood-gift. Old gift. Forbidden gift. If I hold your scent in the river, every hound from here to the capital will pull toward this crossing like a fish on a line whenever you pass near water."

"Yes," Gideon said.

"They will find the place where you bought passage. They will stand on this bank and *howl*."

"Yes."

"And you are asking me to make the Guild's crossing into bait."

"I'm asking you to make it into *mine*." His voice dropped low, and Sera heard beneath it the thing he never let anyone hear — the accent of the Thornfell valleys, the boy he'd been before he learned to sand himself smooth. "You want a price that hurts. Here's one that hurts. Every day for the rest of my life, I will know that any hound, any tracker, any hired nose of Mavros's inquisition can follow me straight to your door. I am handing you the thing I've spent thirty years burying. Not giving it away — *burying* it was the whole point. Do you understand? This isn't charity. This is trust. I don't—" His throat worked. "I don't give people things. Giving gets noticed. So understand what it costs me to set this down in front of a stranger on a riverbank and say, hold this, keep it safe, I'll be back for it."

The mist moved. Somewhere upstream, the river wept on, indifferent and honest.

The ferrymistress studied him for a long time. Then she looked at Sera — a long, measuring look, the look of someone reading a map drawn by an enemy — and something in her face shifted, softened by perhaps a degree.

"He speaks of trust like a foreign language he's learning late in life," she said. "From bad textbooks."

"He does," Sera agreed quietly.

"And you were going to pay with your sister."

"My sister is dead. The name was sitting in me doing nothing but burning." Sera heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for maps and lies. "He had no right to stop me."

"He had every right. That's what prices are *for*, cartographer. Not the paying. The stopping. The moment where someone says, no, not that one, not for this." The ferrymistress turned back to Gideon. "Very well, beast-speaker. The Guild accepts scent as tender. But hear the terms entire, because scent is a living currency and the terms live with it."

She drew from her sleeve a small vial of dark glass, stoppered with wax, and held it out.

"This stays with me. Your scent, gathered true, sealed in river-dark. While I hold it, the crossing is yours — north, south, as often as you need, you and those you name as yours. The Unlisted too, if they travel with you. The river will not sell you, will not whisper you, will not trade you to any crown or council. But the vial can be broken. By me, if the Guild judges you false. By force, if the inquisitors ever take this bank. Understand: the moment it breaks, every hound in seven kingdoms smells you at once, and they will *come*."

"I understand," Gideon said.

"Do you?" For the first time the ferrymistress smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting sight. "Beast-speaker, I've watched men bargain all my long service. Men bargain away their futures easily — futures are abstractions. But you just bargained away your *safety*, which is the only thing you have ever loved, and you did it to keep a dead woman's name out of a lantern." She closed his bare fingers around the empty air where the vial had been, a strange little blessing, and stepped back. "Whoever taught you that trust was possible — even as a language, even badly — I hope they know."

Nobody had taught him. That was the thing Sera realized, watching him pull his glove back on with hands that were not quite steady. Nobody had taught him trust. He had invented it on a riverbank out of fury and stubbornness and some raw material she couldn't name, the way she had invented roads that weren't there until they were.

* * *

The crossing took two days and felt like falling asleep standing up.

The Lachrymose ran through country that maps called empty and the river clearly disagreed with. Sera sat at the bow with her chart-case open on her knees, drawing as they went — not the official survey lines but the true ones, the ones that predated the kingdom, the faint ghost-roads her grandmother's grandmother had inked in a pigment that only showed under certain lights. And the river obeyed them. Where the old charts showed a crossing, there was a crossing. Where they showed a shrine, moss-covered stones still kept a shrine's shape. The land was older than the kingdom and it remembered, and every mile downriver Sera felt less like a forger and more like a translator.

Gideon slept badly. She watched him do it — the shallow, listening sleep of a man who had spent his life as prey. Twice he woke with his whole body rigid, hand finding a knife that wasn't there, and twice he saw her watching and made himself lie back down, deliberately, an apology performed with his spine.

Near dusk on the second day, he came and sat beside her at the bow, and the mist closed around them like a room.

"I owe you an argument," he said.

"You owe me several."

"Not about the name." He looked out at the water. "About what I did. Stepping in front of you like that. It wasn't my right. It was—" He exhaled. "When I was eleven years old, I hid in a root cellar while soldiers questioned my mother about a wolf. And I lay there in the dark learning the lesson of my life: never be noticed, never be owed, never let anyone stand between you and the door. And then yesterday I stood between you and a lantern like some kind of—" he gestured vaguely, "—hero. It felt obscene. I want you to know I knew it felt obscene while I was doing it."

Sera turned the charcoal pencil over in her fingers.

"My sister Liss," she said, "used to finish my sentences. All of them. Drove me mad. And the worst part — the truly unbearable part — was that she finished them correctly. Every time." She watched the mist. "Four years I haven't said her name. I told myself it was mourning. It wasn't. It was hoarding. I was keeping the burn private because private pain is the only property a fugitive gets to own." She glanced at him. "So thank you for the obscenity. Someone should occasionally stand between me and myself."

Gideon was quiet a long moment. Then, in a voice roughened at the edges: "What was her name?"

"No." She smiled, and it hurt, and she let it hurt. "Not yet. Maybe when this is over. When the wards are mended and Mavros is ash and there's a world where names are safe again — maybe I'll say it to the river myself, freely, just to hear it. Until then it's mine. That's the difference between us, Gideon Okonkwo. You gave away your secret to buy trust. I'm keeping mine to prove I still can."

"That sounds lonely."

"It is. It's also load-bearing." She tapped the chart on her knees, where the ghost-road of the pre-kingdom world ran thin and silver toward the Hollow Reach, toward the first drained ward-stone, toward whatever waited there — husk-stone, hollowed beasts, the patient hungry mind she'd seen in Gideon's telling of it and hoped never to see herself. "Everything I am is built on things I refuse to give up. Draw strength accordingly."

Ahead, through the thinning mist, the quarry stair rose out of the riverbank — black steps cut before the kingdom, descending from cliffs where a single pale shape stood waiting at the top.

Sera lifted the glass from her belt case, adjusted it, and felt the bottom drop out of the evening.

The shape was a man. He wore no uniform, carried no visible weapon, and stood with the absolute stillness of something that did not need to breathe. And at his feet, noses to the ground, casting wide and quartering the dark shore with methodical hunger, ranged three lean shapes that could only be hounds — inquisition hounds, white-eyed, silent, working the scent of the bank like a plough works a field.

Behind her, Gideon went so still that the mist itself seemed to flow around him.

"They can't smell us yet," he breathed. "We're mid-channel. Wind's against them." His voice was ice over fast water. "But Sera — they're not searching the shore for travelers. There's no camp, no fire, no tracks. They're standing at the top of the only stair down to the landing."

He turned to her, and in the last light she saw the understanding arrive in his face at the same moment it arrived in hers.

"They know about the crossing," Sera said. "Someone told them where the Guild sells passage."

"No." Gideon's eyes had gone distant, fixed on the hounds, and when he spoke again his voice was barely air. "Worse. Watch them. They're not hunting our trail — they're *waiting*. Quartering, resting, quartering again. That's not a search pattern. That's a stakeout. They're not looking for where we've been."

The largest hound lifted its head, turned, and stared directly across the black water at the ferry — at them — and held the stare, unblinking, white eyes catching the last of the light like coins in a well.

"They're waiting for where we *have to arrive*."
