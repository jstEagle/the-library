## 8. Ward-Stone Hollow

The road to Ward-Stone Hollow did not appear on any map Sera owned, which was how Gideon knew it was a good road. Maps were for people who wanted to be found.

They climbed out of the Merrowgate lowlands through three days of rain that came sideways off the fells, needling through wool and oilskin alike, until the whole company moved in a hunched, dripping silence. The Unlisted had thinned at the edges—deserters slipping back toward the cities, an old ward-keeper's apprentice who wept once, quietly, and was gone by morning—but the core held. Bramble carried the packs and complained about it in Gideon's skull with the constancy of rain.

*Mud,* the mule observed, for the hundredth time that morning. *I am a creature of stable and hay-rack. I have been slandered by circumstance.*

"You volunteered," Gideon murmured.

*I volunteered nothing. You looked at me with your tragic eyes and I lost a bargaining round I did not know had started.* A pause, hooves sucking free of peat. *The birds have stopped.*

Gideon had noticed. He said nothing, because saying things made them heavier, but he had noticed since dawn: no larks over the high pasture, no wren in the drystone walls, not even the carrion birds that should have found their column interesting. The fells around them stood green and wet and utterly empty, like a house from which every stick of furniture has been stolen while the walls remain.

Sera drew level with him, hood back despite the rain, her map case strapped tight across her chest the way other people wore children. "The stone should be over that shoulder," she said. "According to my grandmother's charts. Which are four hundred years old."

"Roads don't move."

"Everything else apparently can." She wiped rain from her face with the heel of her hand. "Gideon. The silence. It's been growing all morning. Like—" She struggled, hating imprecision. "Like walking into a cathedral after market day."

He knew what she meant. His gift had been ringing wrong since they'd crossed the old boundary marker, a leaning slab with Thornfell's sigil worn to a shadow. Beast-speech had always felt to him like standing in a room full of quiet voices; now the room was echoing. Whatever they spoke to here—and Odessa Vane's instructions, memorized and burned, said they must speak—the words would fall a very long way down.

*It gets worse,* Bramble said, uncharacteristically quiet. *There's something ahead that smells of nothing.*

"Nothing?"

*You smell rain, and wet stone, and horse, and fear, and the soup Pell burned last night. Everything smells. This doesn't. There is a hole in the world up ahead shaped like whatever used to live there, and my nose says we're walking into it.*

They crested the shoulder of the fell an hour before dusk, and Ward-Stone Hollow opened below them like a cupped grey hand.

Gideon had imagined the ward-stones as his father's generation described them: pillars of veined white crystal tall as watchtowers, singing a note you felt in your teeth at three leagues' distance, wrapped in the constant soft traffic of beasts who nested in their warmth. Every child in the borderlands learned the stones' names before they learned the king's.

What lay in the hollow was a pillar still, in shape only. It had gone the colour of old bone, of ash, of the inside of long-dead trees. Its surface was crazed with fine cracks, and through the cracks Gideon could see—not darkness, but absence, a depth that his eyes kept sliding off as though the hollow inside it were deeper than the pillar was tall. No song came from it. Instead there was a sound like the memory of sound, the way your ears rang after a bell.

And around it, grazing the dead grass with slow mechanical patience, stood the beasts.

Stag. Does. Foxes. A badger family. Crows walking rather than flying, which was wrong in some way Gideon felt in his spine. Two dozen animals, spread across the hollow floor, moving with the unhurried calm of creatures in a temple where nothing has ever hunted them. Their coats were healthy. Their eyes—

Their eyes were the colour of rainwater over slate. Not empty, exactly. Empty would have been kinder. They were *turned inward*, each animal looking at something far behind its own face.

Nobody spoke. Behind Gideon he heard Sera's breath catch, heard Pell's hand find a weapon and stop, uncertain what it could be aimed at.

*Oh,* Bramble said softly. *Oh, my friend, we should not be here.*

"We have to be here." Gideon's voice came out lower than he intended. "This is what we walked three days in the rain for."

*We walked here for a stone. That—* the mule's ears flattened— *that is what is left when something takes everything and leaves the shape.*

Gideon eased forward down the slope alone, one hand raised to halt the others, because that was his rule and had always been his rule: never send anyone where you will not go first. The dead grass made no sound underfoot. The nearer he came to the pillar, the louder the phantom bell-ring grew, and beneath it he began to feel something else—a pull, faint as current under still water, drawing gently at the place behind his ribs where his gift lived.

The stag lifted its head.

It was a magnificent animal, twelve points on a crown of antlers, winter-coated and heavy-shouldered, and it turned toward Gideon with a movement so fluid and so vacant that his stomach clenched. Rainwater eyes found him. Did not widen. Did not register surprise or threat or curiosity. Registered him the way a scale registers weight.

Gideon did the thing he had spent thirty years perfecting: he opened himself, carefully, and listened.

What came back was not the voice of a beast. He had spoken with stags before—proud, skittish, obsessed with boundaries and browse and the doings of rival herds. What met him now was a voice speaking many voices at once, flattened into one tone, like a choir reduced to sheet music. And underneath it, threaded through it, something else. Something vast and patient that became aware of him the moment he touched the stag's mind, the way a spider becomes aware of the fly not by sight but by the trembling of the web.

*Who wakes the herd?* the stag asked, in a voice like wind through a drained house.

"I'm no one," Gideon said aloud, because his mouth needed to do something. "Old friend. Just passing."

*No one.* The stag stepped closer. Rain slid off its antlers. *No one, who carries an open door where others carry walls. The Warden of the Sigil bids all doors be known.*

The words were not the stag's. Gideon understood that with cold certainty even as they arrived. He should have withdrawn—he told himself to withdraw—but the vast thing beneath the voice had begun to turn its attention fully upon him, and attention like that was gravity, and he was falling into it.

He saw, in fragments that arrived without sequence:

A throne room lit green by wards being drunk like wine, centuries of accumulated protection pouring down a channel of light into a man's chest. A crown, gold, unremarkable, sitting on a brow beaded with pleasant exertion. Hands—scholar's hands, inkstained at the cuff—turning a page of a book whose text repeated, looped, the same line over and over, because the hands belonged to a king who was no longer writing anything. And the mind behind it all, the mind wearing all this like comfortable clothing: hunger, but not wild hunger. Nothing so honest. This was appetite with *patience*, the hunger of a farmer regarding a field, already calculating next year's planting. Three more stones. Four. Then the free cities, which fed themselves. Then everything. There was no malice in it, and that was the most frightening thing Gidon had ever touched. Malice could be goaded. Malice made mistakes. This was a man doing arithmetic.

And beneath the arithmetic, faint as a watermark, something almost like contentment. *At last,* the watermark said. *At last, at last, at last.*

The stag's muzzle was inches from his chest.

*You see him,* it said. *Now he sees you.*

Gideon ripped himself free.

He came back to his body kneeling in wet grass, nose bleeding in two bright threads, Sera's hands on his shoulders shaking him, Bramble's screaming filling the hollow—the mule rearing and lashing out at empty air, at the *wrongness*, while the hollowed beasts around them continued their grazing as though a war band had not just broken over their temple.

"He saw—" Gideon coughed, wiped his nose, made himself say it plainly because plainly was the only way it wouldn't own him. "He saw me. The binder. He knows someone's come to the stones."

"How?" Sera's face was paper-white. "How could he possibly—"

"The hollowed ones are his eyes. Maybe all of them, everywhere, all at once." Gideon got a knee under himself. His hands were steady, which surprised him; somewhere inside, something enormous had shifted its weight and settled, and he recognized it, distantly, as resolve. "He's not hiding anymore. He doesn't think he needs to. We're ants who found his bootprint."

*Good,* said Bramble, who had stopped screaming and now stood blowing hard, ears pinned at the herd of hollow beasts. *Good, then let us be somewhere else when the boot comes down.*

"No." Gideon stood. His legs held. The stag still watched him, rainwater eyes fixed, and Gideon made himself look at the animal—at the magnificent ruined shell of it—and felt the horror in his chest fuse into something harder. Somewhere inside that husk was a stag who had once argued with crows and led his does to secret water. Somewhere inside every one of these silent beasts was everything the binder had eaten around them, leaving only the shape.

Odessa's lessons began with this. He had hoped never to reach them.

"Sera. Everyone back to the tree line. Nothing attacks, nobody runs—if we run, we tell him exactly how afraid we are." He rolled his shoulders, spat red into the grass, and faced the stag again.

"Gideon," Sera said sharply. "What are you doing?"

"What we came for." He reached out with his gift, slow and deliberate this time, letting the great patient awareness feel him coming. If it wanted to look at him, let it look. Let it see an open door and wonder what was walking through it. "One beast at a time. That's the work. Starting with him."

He set his hands flat against the stag's neck—warm, alive, horribly warm, life still burning in the wick though the flame was elsewhere—and reached down into the drained house of its mind, following the thread of the binder's hunger to where it knotted.

Far away, in a green-lit throne room, something patient set down its pen.

And smiled.

* * *

The thread did not want to be pulled.

It was not rope but root, and it was not anchored in the stag's mind but grown through it, fine filaments reaching everywhere the way mould reaches through bread. Gideon took hold of the central knot and the knot took hold of him back, and for a moment they were simply two forces straining, the binder's will against his gift, the hollow's silence against thirty years of listening.

*Small smuggler,* came the impression, not in words—the great awareness didn't bother with words—but in weight, in certainty. *Small thief of roads. I have eaten songs older than your bloodline.*

"Then you've a digestion problem," Gideon said through his teeth, "because you're about to bring this one back up."

He did the thing Odessa had tried to teach in three frantic nights of diagrams and shouted instruction: he did not fight the root. He *spoke* to the stag around it. Past the filaments, down in the deep places where the animal's own self still crouched, starved and dreaming, Gideon found a memory—does in silver mist, a hidden spring, the taste of clean water—and he offered it up like a lamp.

*You are this,* he told the buried stag. *Not the silence. This. Come back to it.*

Something turned over in the dark. Something remembered thirst.

The stag screamed—a real scream, raw and animal and full of everything the hollow voice had lacked—and the root snapped with a sensation Gideon felt in his own chest, a thread tearing loose somewhere behind his navel, taking something with it as it went. He staggered. For a half-second he could not remember the name of the village where he'd been born, and then it came back, and then it didn't matter, because the stag was looking at him.

Brown eyes. Not rainwater. Brown, and wet, and *aware*, and terrified, and alive.

Around the hollow, the other beasts had stopped grazing. Dozens of rainwater gazes had lifted, all together, all fixed on Gideon—and in every one of them, faint as stars at dusk, something flickered. Not freed. But watching. Hoping, maybe, the way prisoners hope when they hear the first bar of a file on the first bar of a window.

*One,* Bramble said quietly behind him. *Several dozen to go, and a mountain between us and the second stone.*

"One," Gideon agreed. His knees were shaking now that it was over, and his nose was bleeding again, and some small warm room in his memory had a scorch mark on the wall that he couldn't quite identify. He put his hand once more on the stag's neck—brown eyes, alive, *his* eyes—and gave the word he'd carried all his life to the wind at last, freely, to one listener who deserved it.

"My name is Gideon Okonkwo," he told the stag. "Remember it. He won't let you forget much longer."

The stag bowed its crowned head.

And far to the south, in a palace above a sleeping capital, Mavros the court sorcerer rose from beside his king's chair, crossed to a window facing north, and began—to the astonishment of every hollowed servant in the hall—to laugh.
