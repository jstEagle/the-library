## 9. Letters from a Hollow King

The letters came to them in a sack of turnips.

That was the thing about Merrowgate that Sera was learning to love and distrust in equal measure: everything arrived by accident, wrapped inside something ordinary, carried by someone who claimed never to have seen the person who handed it to them. The free cities had survived three centuries of kings by perfecting the art of plausible deniability, and nowhere was it more refined than in the merchant quarter's message trade, where a sack of root vegetables could hold more state secrets than the royal archive.

She'd paid for these with two of her mother's rings and a favor owed to a woman named Ivetta who smelled of tallow and ink and asked no questions, which in Sera's experience meant she already knew all the answers.

Now she sat in the back room of a chandler's shop on Tallow Row, where the air hung thick with the sweet rot-smell of rendered fat, and spread eleven letters across a scarred worktable under the light of a single whale-oil lamp.

They were royal correspondence. Sealed with the stag-and-stars of House Aldric. Written, every one of them, in the king's own hand.

* * *

Sera Voss had spent nine years learning to read handwriting the way other people read faces.

Her father had taught her, before the fall of their house, before the maps were confiscated and her sister died and the name Voss became something whispered rather than spoken aloud. A cartographer's daughter learned early that documents lie in their content but confess in their bodies. Ink told you things. Pressure told you things. The way a descender hooked or failed to hook, whether the crossbar of a *t* drifted left when the writer was tired or right when he was afraid—these were fingerprints of the soul, and no forger alive could fake them all at once.

Sera knew King Aldric's hand better than she knew her own.

Not because she had ever met him—she hadn't, not once, though she had stood in the antechamber of his audience hall for six hours on the day her family's maps were seized—but because the royal cartographic office kept exemplars. Every scribe in the capital studied the king's script as a standard, the way choir students studied a perfect pitch. She had copied it hundreds of times herself, in exercises, in forgeries, in the slow obsessive practice of a girl who understood that to draw a road that wasn't there, you first had to learn to write like someone who believed in it.

So she sat with the lamp turned low and read the letters four times through before she let herself understand what she was seeing, because understanding it would change everything, and there was a small cowardly part of her that wanted another hour of not-knowing.

The letters were orders. Execution orders, warrants of attainder, directives sealed in the king's voice and bearing his authority. *Let Ward-Keeper Odessa Vane be taken at the second stone and put to death for abandonment of post.* *Let the ward-keepers of Thornfell be gathered and hanged from their own towers, that their negligence be made an example.* *Let the families of those who flee be questioned until they speak.* Eleven letters. Nine names. Nine deaths ordered in a neat, upright, unmistakably royal hand.

And the same sentences, over and over, like a man walking in circles in the dark.

Sera set down the third letter with fingers that would not quite stay steady, and pressed her palms flat against the worktable, and breathed the chandler's tallow-thick air until the nausea passed.

Because she had seen this before. Not in royal correspondence—in ledgers. In her father's last ledger, near the end, when the wasting sickness had him in its teeth and his mind wandered the corridors of itself. He would write an entry, finish it, and then write it again, word for word, sometimes three times, sometimes five, each repetition a little more slurred than the last, each beginning with the same careful capital letter as if he'd forgotten he'd already started. The clerks called it looping. It was what happened when a mind lost its mooring and drifted on the same current, round and round, unable to remember it had been here before.

King Aldric of Thornfell was looping.

* * *

She worked through the night.

It was not sentiment that drove her, though sentiment was there, coiled tight beneath her sternum like a wire pulled to singing. It was craft. Craft was the one prayer she trusted. When the world went mad, you did what you knew how to do, and you did it precisely, and if you were lucky the precision would carry meaning inside it like a seed inside fruit.

First: authentication. If these letters were forgeries—and they might be; the whole kingdom was drowning in forged papers now, passes and pardons and patents of nobility sold in Merrowgate's back alleys for the price of a good horse—then the loop itself might be the forgery's flaw. Someone imitating the king's hand, running out of invention, copying the same phrases from a master draft.

So she tested them the way her father had taught her to test anything: against time, against pressure, against the body of the writer.

The ink varied correctly. That was the first thing. A forger working from a single sitting uses one batch of ink, one pen, one mood. These letters spanned weeks—she could tell from the references, from the dates in the margins, from the wax seals that darkened subtly with age—and the ink changed with them, thinning in the earlier ones, thickening and re-browning in the later, exactly as a well-used inkwell behaves over days of intermittent use. The pen angle shifted between letters the way a real hand shifts when it writes standing, then seated, then standing again. One letter bore a faint tremor in the long horizontal strokes—the kind of tremor that comes from writing on an uneven surface, a knee, a saddle-cantle, a stone windowsill.

A forger faking compulsion makes the loops too regular. Sera had seen forged madness before, in the confession letters her father had been forced to sign at the end, dictated sentence by sentence by a clerk who wanted the shame legible. Forged derangement is orderly. Real derangement is human. These loops were human: the repetitions drifted, degraded, occasionally broke off mid-sentence only to resume three lines later at the exact same word, as if the writer had surfaced, gasped, and gone under again into the same water.

Second: the hand itself. Here she slowed down, because here was the heart of it.

She took the earliest letter and the latest and laid them side by side under the lamp. Between them, the king's script had changed—not in style but in health. In the first letter, written perhaps five weeks ago, the hand was strong, upright, confident, the *k*s cut with a certain impatient flourish she remembered from the exemplars. In the latest, the same letterforms persisted—the same skeleton—but the flesh of them had wasted. The strokes were thinner. The pressure faltered at the ends of lines. And twice, in the middle of formal legal phrasing, the pen had dragged a short distance sideways, leaving a faint gray streak like a sleeper's foot sliding on ice, before catching and continuing as though nothing had happened.

That was not forgery. That was a body failing while it worked.

Third—and this was the thing that finally broke her, that made her push back from the table and stand in the dark of the chandler's back room with her hand over her mouth—was the content of the loops themselves.

She had assumed the repeated sentences would be the orders. But they weren't. The orders appeared once each, clean and complete. What repeated was something else. Small phrases, tucked into the margins, into the spaces between the legal language, into the postscripts that no clerk would ever transcribe:

*I must remember the wards.*

*I am the keeper of the wards.*

*The wards are my charge. I must remember.*

*I must remember the wards.*

Over and over, in the king's own hand, in letters ordering the deaths of the very people whose whole lives were the keeping of those wards—a mind circling the one fact it could not hold, grasping at it, losing it, grasping again. Like a man drowning who keeps forgetting which way is up and so writes himself instructions on his own skin.

Sera stood there in the smell of tallow and understood.

He wasn't commanding this. He was *writing around* it. Whatever held the king—whatever wore him—needed his hand to sign the orders, needed the authentic seal of royal authority, and so it gave him the pen and let him write. But some buried part of Aldric, some cellar of himself the thing had not yet emptied, used the freedom of the pen to cry out. Not in words anyone would read as a plea—just the same desperate sentence, stamped again and again into the margins of his own death warrants. *I must remember the wards.* As if the wards were the door, and he was trying to leave himself directions to it.

He was not the author of these letters. He was their prisoner. He was dictating nothing and witnessing everything, locked behind his own eyes while his hand moved, and the only rebellion left to him was repetition—the one weapon of the helpless: to keep saying the true thing until someone, somewhere, heard it.

Sera thought of her father at the end, writing his ledger entries three times over, and had to sit down on a crate of candle stubs and press the heels of both hands against her eyes until the burning stopped.

* * *

"You've been at this all night."

Gideon Okonkwo filled the doorway the way he always did—quietly, entirely, as if he'd been standing there for some time deciding whether to exist yet. Behind him the chandler's shop was still dark; dawn was a rumor at the shutter seams. His mule was not with him. His mule was never far, though, and Sera had learned to feel Bramble's cynical attention like weather.

"Come in," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Close the door."

He did. He looked at the table—at the eleven letters fanned across it, at the scraps of her notes, at the cold lantern—and she watched him do the smuggler's arithmetic, cataloguing value and risk without touching anything.

"Those are what I think they are?"

"Royal warrants." She drew breath. "Execution orders. Nine ward-keepers. Odessa Vane among them."

Something moved behind Gideon's eyes—she was learning to read the flickers there, the quick animal assessments—and she saw him arrive at the name. "Vane. That's your name too."

"My father's cousin's widow." Sera smoothed the oldest letter flat. "Or close enough. The old ward-keeping families intermarried for centuries. We're all cousins, distantly. Which is why my family kept the maps we kept." She tapped the parchment. "But that's not why I woke you."

"I wasn't asleep."

"No," she said. "You're never asleep. Look at this."

She showed him. Not the orders—he didn't need the details of the orders, and she watched him choose not to ask, choosing instead to spare her the recitation—but the margins. She walked him through it the way she'd walked herself through it, phrase by phrase, repetition by repetition. *I must remember the wards.* She showed him the ink evidence. The failing pressure. The drag-marks where the sleeping hand slipped. She showed him the earliest letter beside the latest and let him see, even without a forger's training, the difference between a strong hand and a haunted one.

Gideon read in silence for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was low, and stripped of the wry deflection he usually wore like armor.

"He's in there." Not a question. "The king. He's still in there somewhere."

"Yes."

"And the thing wearing him can't write for itself. It needs his hand."

"It needs his authority. The sigil holds his mind, but the kingdom runs on paper, and paper needs the true hand and the true seal, or the ward-keepers' own order would refuse it." Sera heard the bitter shape of it as she said it aloud. "Mavros—or whatever he is—can't simply decree. He has to *be* the king, convincingly, continuously, in ink. Every order costs him. Every document risks exactly this—" she touched the marginal scrawl "—the prisoner leaving notes."

Gideon was quiet a moment. "You pity him."

It wasn't an accusation, quite. But it landed like one, because Sera had been asking herself the same question all night and hadn't liked the answer.

"Of course I pity him," she said. "He's a man buried alive in his own body, forced to dig the graves of everyone still trying to save him. What's left of him spends its nights scratching the same sentence into the margins, hoping—" Her voice caught. She mastered it. "I spent years hating the crown, Gideon. My family's ruin wears the royal seal. It would be easier if this were just a tyrant. Tyrants you can fight cleanly. This is—" She looked down at the looping hand. "This is a hostage situation that has lasted months, and the hostage has been signing our death warrants the whole time, and none of us knew."

Gideon picked up the newest letter—the one with the drag-marks—and held it close to the relit lamp. The light moved in his eyes.

"The wolves knew," he said quietly.

"What?"

"When the wards fell. The beasts screamed before the stars went out. They felt it happen." He set the letter down. "Everything living on the border felt it happen. And the king—" he tapped the looping margin-scrawl "—felt it happen too, from inside. That's what he's trying to remember. Not the wards as law. The wards as *wound*. Something tore them out of him, Sera. The wards ran through the crown, through the king, through the land, and whatever Mavros did, it went through *him* first. He's not just remembering his duty. He's remembering being hurt."

Sera stared at him. Of course. Of course it fit. The old ward-maps her family had guarded showed it plainly enough, once you knew to look: every ward-line in the kingdom traced back, eventually, to the capital, converging on the palace like rivers converging on a sea. The king had never merely ruled the wards. He had *anchored* them. Whoever bound the king had reached into the root system of the realm's protection and gripped it with both hands.

"That's why the letters matter," she said slowly, thinking aloud. "That's why the thing bothers with authenticity at all. It's not just legitimacy. The orders have to come from the anchor. From the node. An order signed by anyone else wouldn't carry the weight—it would be a dead letter. But signed by the king, in the king's hand..." She trailed off.

"...it travels the old channels," Gideon finished. "The same roads the wards used. The wards are dead, but the roads remember." He smiled without humor. "Smuggler's rule. A road doesn't stop existing because nobody walks it. It just gets hungry."

Outside, the bells of Merrowgate began the dawn peal—three bells, then a pause, then three more, the merchants' signal that the exchange houses were opening and the day's careful lies could commence. Gray light crept under the shutters and lay across the worktable, softening the lamp's yellow with something older and colder.

Sera gathered the letters with hands that were steadier now. Purpose does that. Pity alone would have broken her; pity with a use attached was fuel.

"We take these to Odessa Vane," she said.

Gideon's head came up sharply. "She's at the second stone. Half the passes are sealed, and the third letter on that table hangs her."

"Which is why she has to see them." Sera squared the stack and bound it in oilcloth with the efficiency of long habit. "She'll believe her king ordered her death—she may already know; the warrants go out ahead of the soldiers. But she won't believe he *looped*. She won't believe he begged. Not until she sees the hand fail. Odessa Vane taught half the surviving ward-keepers their trade, Gideon. If anyone alive can look at these margins and tell us what *I must remember the wards* means—where the memory goes, how to give it back—it's her." She tied the knot hard. "And if we're going to unbind a king, we should probably start by finding the one teacher who remembers what binding looks like from the outside."

For a moment Gideon said nothing. Dawn strengthened at the shutters. Somewhere below, Bramble made a sound like a hinge complaining about philosophy.

"You understand what you're proposing," Gideon said at last. "Walking to a condemned woman's hiding place carrying the proof of her condemnation. If inquisitors stop us, these letters don't just hang her. They hang everyone who's helped us. They hang the Unlisted. They hang—" he paused, and chose the word with care "—everyone whose names I've finally let myself know."

"I know."

"And you want to go anyway."

"I want to go anyway." Sera met his eyes. "Because a man is buried alive in the capital, digging graves with his own hand and leaving notes for us in the margins, and I am a mapmaker, Gideon. Reading what's written is the only prayer I've ever known how to say."

Gideon looked at the oilcloth bundle for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he did when a decision settled into him like ballast.

"Then we ride tonight," he said. "Through the Hollow Ford, before the toll-boats wake. And Sera—" he paused at the door, half in shadow, and the morning light caught something grim and almost gentle in his face, "—when we find her, you'd best hope the old woman's mercy is bigger than her madness. Because the last ward-keeper who saw a Voss coming got burned for it, and I'd hate for history to repeat itself before breakfast."

He was gone before she could answer, and the door swung softly shut, and Sera stood alone among the letters of a hollow king, listening to the bells count out the hours she had left.

Nine names on the warrants. One of them still breathing, as far as anyone knew.

She meant to keep it that way—if the road, and the king's looping ghost-hand, and whatever waited at the second stone would let her.
