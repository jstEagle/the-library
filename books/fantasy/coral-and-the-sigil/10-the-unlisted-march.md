## 10. The Unlisted March

The rats told him first.

They came streaming through the drainage culverts in a grey river of panic, whiskers flat, tails low, and Gideon felt their terror before he heard a single squeak—the taste of it like copper at the back of his tongue, the way fear always tasted when it belonged to something small and quick and close to the ground. He set down the lantern he had been trimming and stood very still in the lamplit mouth of the Underway market, listening with his whole skin.

*Iron shoes,* the rats said, in that wordless way they said everything. *Iron shoes and fire and a smell like burnt bone.*

"Bramble," he said quietly.

The mule was dozing against a pillar stacked with contraband wool, one ear cocked in permanent skepticism. She opened one eye. "If you're about to ask me to carry something, the answer is no until I hear what it is."

"Inquisitors in the upper city. Coming down."

Both eyes open now. Bramble's ears swiveled like weathervanes finding wind. Somewhere above them—three levels up, past the fish markets and the tanneries and the respectable merchants who paid respectable bribes—the free city of Merrowgate was being opened like a oyster, prised apart joint by joint.

"How long?" Bramble asked.

"Minutes. Maybe less."

"Then I'll say it once." She pushed herself off the pillar with a groan of ancient joints and genuine grievance. "I *told* them. I told Odessa's letter-writer, I told the ferry-brats, I told anyone with two ears and the sense to fill them between. You don't let three hundred runaways sleep in one hole in the ground. You scatter them. You move them nightly. You never—" her voice cracked upward into a bray that carried too far down the tunnel, "—you never build a *parliament*, for all the gods' sakes. Parliaments have addresses."

Gideon was already moving. Around him the Underway market was waking into its particular kind of chaos—lamplighters dousing lamps instead of lighting them, a fence sweeping silver spoons into a sack, two deserters from the Thornfell garrison arguing about which way was out. Three hundred souls, Sera had counted. More if you counted the beasts, and Gideon always counted the beasts: dogs gone strange since the wards fell, a family of foxes that traded news for eggs, the hollow-eyed remnant of a milk cow someone had smuggled down on a barge because she lowed wrong now, in a language no farmer wanted to hear.

And underneath all of it, threaded through the crowd like a wire through wax, the silence the stars had left behind. Six weeks since the night sky went black. Six weeks, and Gideon still caught himself looking up through grates and culvert mouths, expecting light.

He climbed onto a cask of salted herring and did something he had never once done in thirty-one years of careful, unremarkable life.

He raised his voice.

"Listen to me!"

The market stilled. Faces turned—smugglers, runaways, deserters, a child with a fox on either shoulder—and every one of those faces looked at Gideon Okonkwo, who had spent his whole life making sure no face ever turned toward him, and he felt the old rule straining inside him like a rope around a wound: *never be noticed, never be named, never be the one standing on the barrel.* His mother's voice, or the memory of it, or whatever remained of it after the second ward-stone: *A man they can see is a man they can shoot, little star.*

The inquisitors were coming down iron stairs with torches. There was no version of tonight where he stayed unseen.

"The Underways are lost," he said, and his voice carried better than he expected, steady and low, the voice he used on frightened horses. "Inquisitors, top level, moving down. You know what they carry. You know what they ask." A ripple went through the crowd; someone moaned. "So here is what happens now. Everyone who can walk goes west through the Weeper's Cut, single file, no lights after the third turning. Wounded go to Bramble." He pointed at the mule, who glared as though she had been slandered. "Beasts keep to the side channels—they'll hear the soldiers before we do, and I want to know everything they know. Foxes"—the child with the foxes snapped to attention like a soldier—"run ahead of the column and warn every soul in the side tunnels. You don't fight tonight. Nobody fights tonight. Tonight we are smoke, and smoke does not stop to argue."

"And where," said a voice from the back—a deserter with half a sergeant's stripes torn from his sleeve, a man named Coss who'd been drinking Merrowgate dry since the stars went out—"where exactly are we smoking *to*, Okonkwo?"

Gideon had known this question was coming. He had been avoiding it for six weeks, the way he avoided all questions that required him to be somebody. But the rats were pouring past his boots now in a living flood, and somewhere overhead a door burst inward, and there was no more room in the world for a man without a destination.

"Ward-Stone Hollow," he said. "Then the next stone. Then the next, all the way back to Thornfell." He let the silence eat that for a moment. "The wards didn't fail. They were eaten. And whatever ate them has a belly, and bellies can be emptied. But not by three hundred people hiding in a hole. By three hundred people *marching*."

Nobody moved. The foxes' ears flattened. Coss stared at him with the flat contempt of a man who has followed orders into bad places before.

Then Bramble, magnificent, foul-tempered Bramble, threw her head back and brayed—a great ragged bugle of a sound that bounced off the vaulted brick and rang down every tunnel like a war horn, and she said, at a volume calculated to offend the entire assembly:

"Well? You heard the only honest man in Merrowgate. *Move.*"

They moved.

* * *

The Weeper's Cut was an old smuggler road, older than the kingdom, one of dozens Gideon had learned in his years of walking invisible—cut through the city's foundations by hands nobody remembered, seeping groundwater from the walls in slow tears that gave it its name. He took point himself, because that was what a guide did, and because if the passage ahead held death he would rather meet it first than send anyone else to find it.

Behind him, three hundred souls walked in the dark.

That was the part that kept catching in his chest like a fishbone: not the raid, not the iron shoes echoing down through the city's bones, but the sound behind him. The shuffle of feet that trusted his feet. The muffled cough of a woman carrying twins wrapped in sailcloth. The soft, constant conversation of the beasts along the side channels—dogs reporting distances, the foxes' high relay calls fading forward into the dark, the milk cow's wrong-language lowing that even now made the hairs on his arms rise, because he could almost understand it, almost, and what he almost understood was grief.

He had guided caravans through worse dark than this. But a caravan of silk and salt did not care whether it lived. It weighed nothing on his shoulders. This did. This weighed like the sky used to, before the stars went out.

"Gideon." Sera Voss fell into step beside him, breathing hard, her map-case hugged to her chest the way other people held children. In the dark she was mostly a voice and the pale smudge of a face, ink stains ghosting her fingers even now—he suspected she dreamed in cartographer's ink. "The wounded count is eleven. Bramble's carrying seven. Two won't make the Cut at walking pace."

"They'll make it," Gideon said.

"That's not a plan, that's stubbornness wearing a plan's coat."

"I'm a smuggler. Stubbornness is most of my stock-in-trade." He reached out and found her shoulder, steered her around a knee-high sill of ancient masonry without breaking stride. "Get ahead to the third junction. There's a side gallery, dry, hidden behind a false wall—I built the false wall myself four years ago, so I know it works. Put the two worst in there with water and the fox-child's mother to nurse them. We collect them on the way back."

"*On the way back,*" Sera repeated. "You say that like it's certain."

"It isn't. Say it anyway. Armies march on sentences like that."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "That's the first time you've called us an army."

"It's the first time we've been one." He heard rather than saw her smile—heard it in the changed shape of her breathing, the small exhale of a woman who had spent months drawing roads that weren't there and had just watched one fill up with soldiers.

At the junction she peeled away into the dark, and Gideon led on.

* * *

The inquisitors found the Underway market empty.

Gideon knew it the moment it happened—not because he saw it, but because the rats told him, breathless and vindicated, and because the screaming started three levels up: torch-fire and fury and the sound of a city's secret heart being put to the question. They would find cold lamps and warm ashes and a fence's abandoned spoons, and they would find, chalked on the central pillar in a hand the inquisitors would spend a fortnight trying to trace, a message Sera had left in the last minutes before departure, grinning like a girl as she wrote it:

*THE ROADS REMEMBER WHAT YOU BURN.*

"You're going to get us all flayed," Gideon had told her.

"Good," she'd said. "Let them lie awake wondering which road."

Now he listened to the pursuit organize itself above—the peculiar rhythm of it, whistles answering whistles, boots dividing into search parties—and did the arithmetic of the dark. The Weeper's Cut ran west for two miles beneath the tanners' district, surfaced the canal, and died there unless you knew the trick of the sluice-gate that opened into the reed-flats. The inquisitors knew the Cut existed. Every constable in Merrowgate knew. What none of them knew—what only a lifetime of invisibility had taught him—was that the Cut forked at the half-mile mark behind a curtain of roots, and the lesser branch dropped ninety feet by way of a broken stair into the Old Tide Gallery, and the Old Tide Gallery connected to the drowned chapel of a god whose name the kingdom had forbidden, and from the drowned chapel there was a passage the eels kept, and eels kept secrets better than any guild.

"Dogs," said the fox-child, skidding out of a side channel, eyes huge. "Down in the Cut behind us. Big ones. They smell—" the fox paused, choosing, "—they smell *him*. Not us. Him. Like he's lit up."

Gideon's stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like a body rolling in surf. The Ferryman's price. His scent, given away at the toll four nights ago, sold to whichever master paid in the coin the Guild loved best. The hounds were not tracking the Unlisted.

They were tracking *him*.

Bramble's head swung round, ears flat, and he realized she had heard the fox too—that everyone within twenty feet had heard it, and that three hundred people were very quietly recalculating the value of the man leading them.

"Then he stops being at the front," Bramble announced, in the tone of a general court-martial. "Gideon Okonkwo, you will remove yourself to the middle of this column, surrounded by persons of no interest to hounds, and you will stay there, and you will let the rest of us be anonymous, which frankly we were better at until you started giving speeches."

"The front is where I can hear the dogs' handlers," Gideon began.

"And the middle is where you can't get killed, which is worth more to this army than your ears." She stomped once, a hoof-strike that echoed like a judge's gavel. "I carried your mother's grain sacks, boy. I have known you since you were knees and elbows. Do not make me bite you in front of your constituents."

Somewhere behind them, closer now, a hound gave tongue—a deep bay that rolled down the Cut like a landslide finding its voice.

Coss appeared at Gideon's elbow, sergeant's ghost-stripes and all, holding a bill-hook he must have looted from the market. "There's a sluice-gate control forty yards back," he said. "Tanner's runoff channel. Open it and the Cut fills to the waist with lime-water and dead hides. Kills scent. Kills dogs' noses for days." He shrugged, elaborately casual. "Assuming the man who knows these roads orders it."

Gideon looked at the deserter. The deserter looked at him. Something passed between them that had never passed between Gideon and any lawful man: the specific, weightless recognition of people who had both decided, separately and for their own reasons, that the law was somebody else's story.

"Do it," Gideon said. "Then everyone runs."

* * *

The lime-water saved them, and cost them.

It killed the hounds' scent-trail as promised, and it soaked three hundred people to the thigh in freezing chemical filth, and by the time they came up through the sluice-gate onto the reed-flats under a sky black as a closed eye, two of the wounded had stopped shivering in the way that meant the nursing women had done all they could. They buried them in the reeds without coffins, in the hour before dawn, and the milk cow stood over the graves and lowed her wrong-language grief until Gideon, kneeling in the wet grass with his hand on her enormous skull, finally understood the word she was saying.

*Names,* she was saying. *You die twice, and the second death is when no one says your name.*

He stood up from that grave a different man, though he couldn't have said how, precisely, except that the old rule—*never be noticed, never be named*—sat differently in him now. Lighter. Or emptier. Like a coat outgrown.

Sera found him there as the column formed up on the flats, three hundred damp and grieving souls sorting themselves into marching order with a competence that surprised everyone, most of all themselves. Bramble took the wounded as if she'd been born to triage, which, knowing Bramble, perhaps she had. The foxes ranged ahead. The dogs flanked. Coss had somehow acquired a command voice overnight and was using it on men who'd outranked him a week ago.

"They're going to call you something," Sera said, watching them. "Crowds always do. The Unlisted need a list, and it starts with whoever walks first."

"Don't," Gideon said. It came out harder than he meant. "Sera. I spent thirty-one years learning how to be nobody. It kept me alive when nothing else would have. Being *somebody*—" he looked down the long ragged line of them, refugees and runaways and talking beasts, his, somehow, all of them his, "—being somebody is how my mother died. Named. Found. Burned out of her own valley for the gift I've got hiding under my tongue."

Sera was quiet for a while. Out on the black water, something dove and did not surface.

"Here's what I know about maps," she said finally. "A road that's never drawn can't lead anywhere. It also can't bring anyone home." She adjusted the strap of her map-case, a gesture he'd come to read as her version of wringing hands. "Ward-Stone Hollow is nine days' march if we cut across the Thornfell highlands instead of following the river. Faster, and the inquisitors won't expect it, because no army crosses the highlands without maps." A thin, dangerous smile. "I have maps. I drew them. Some of them are even true."

"And some of them aren't yet."

"Give them a week," said Sera Voss, cartographer of roads that weren't there, and turned to give the order to march.

They went north and west across the reed-flats as the sky stayed dark, three hundred strong, and Gideon walked at the front of them—openly, visibly, where anyone could see—and the strangest thing was not the hounds far behind or the graves in the reeds or even the weight of all those eyes on his back.

The strangest thing was that the beasts kept saying his name as they passed him. The foxes said it like a password. The dogs said it like a promise. Even the milk cow, falling in near the rear with her terrible gentle language, said it once, softly, the way you say the name of the dead to keep them from dying twice.

*Gideon. Gideon. Gideon.*

Nine days to Ward-Stone Hollow. Nine days of highlands no army should have crossed.

On the second morning, the eastern horizon over Thornfell turned the color of an old bruise, and then the color of a burn, and the wind brought them the smell of smoke, and Gideon stopped walking and could not, for a long moment, remember how to start again—because the wind was blowing from the valley where he had been born, where a woman who is now only a name had taught him the speech of beasts, and everything the wind carried said that Mavros had gotten there first.
