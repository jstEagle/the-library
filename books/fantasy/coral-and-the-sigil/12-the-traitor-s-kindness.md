## 12. The Traitor's Kindness

The valley was still burning when they buried the ones who hadn't run.

Gideon dug with his hands more than the spade, because the ground was stony and because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Ash came down soft as grey snow, settling on his shoulders, on the shrouds of sacking they'd wrapped the dead in, on Bramble's dun back where the mule stood guard over the graves like a mourner who refused to leave the churchyard. The smoke tasted of pine and of wool and of things Gideon would not let himself name.

Six graves. He had known some of these people by their footsteps. Old Marta who kept goats that gossiped worse than market wives. The twins from the upper croft whose dog had once translated, in the blunt grammar of hounds, exactly what it thought of Gideon's beard. He had drunk their cider. He had sat at their fire not three winters past and said nothing, been nothing, a shadow passing through — because that was the rule, that was always the rule: never be noticed, never be owed, never be known.

He had been known now for months, and look what it cost.

"You'll wear your fingers to the bone," Sera said behind him. She held out a water skin. Her voice was scraped hollow; she had organized this burial, drawn the map of graves in the dirt with her cartographer's precision, and she had not slept.

"Bone grows back." He took the skin, drank, handed it back. Below them in the blackened croft-rows, the Unlisted were striking camp with the quiet efficiency of people who had learned that grief does not excuse you from moving. Odessa was three days dead at the ford. The column was smaller now, harder, and it moved like a wound animal moves: low to the ground, watching everywhere at once.

That was the thing Gideon could not stop chewing on, the stone in the boot of his mind. *Watching everywhere at once.* The raid on Merrowgate's underways had come within an hour of the Guild's warning. The trap at the ford had been laid precisely where Odessa's route crossed the water — no other crossing, no better place for an ambush in twenty miles. And this letter, this lovely courteous threat naming Sera's sister, delivered into a camp that no road led to.

Mavros knew where they were. Mavros kept knowing where they were.

"We have an ear," Gideon said.

Sera went very still, the way she did over a map when a coastline refused to match the chart. "Pell says the raiders tracked us from the Guild."

"Pell says a great many helpful things." Gideon wiped ash and grave-dirt on his thighs. "Pell found the lost child in Merrowgate. Pell scouted the ford before Odessa chose it. Pell volunteered to ride back for the wounded we couldn't carry." He made himself say the next part plainly, without heat, because heat would make her argue instead of listen. "And last night, when I lay awake listening, I heard the crows above our camp talking to each other. Crows talk about everything, but mostly they talk about food and fear. They were talking about fear of a man who walks soft. There's one man in this company who walks soft enough to make crows nervous, and he isn't me. Crows like me."

Sera looked at the graves. Then north, toward the smoke-hazed horizon. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain enough not to say his name twice."

* * *

They did not take him at night, which would have been easier, or in front of the others, which would have been simpler. They took him at dawn on the scout's own path, the narrow goat-track above the camp where Pell went every morning to watch the ridgelines, alone, conscientious, kind.

Kind. That was the poison in it. Gideon had spent his whole life reading creatures, and he knew what kindness looked like in the wild — the lure-lamp of the anglerfish, the cuckoo's egg laid soft in another bird's nest. But Pell's kindness had seemed so unremarkable, so genuinely worn, that Gideon had catalogued it and moved on. A man who remembered you liked your tea sweet. A man who carried Bramble's extra weight up the mountain passes without being asked, and sang to the mule badly enough that even Bramble's complaints sounded fond.

Pell was sitting on a flat rock at the track's crest, sharing dried apples with a fox that had followed the column for two days. That was its own small indictment; wild things trusted him, and wild things only ever trusted what smelled safe. When Gideon and Sera stepped out of the pines, the fox poured itself over the rock lip and vanished, and Pell turned with that open, pleasant face and said, "Morning. Ridge is clear, far as I—"

"Your family's in the capital," Gideon said.

It was not a question, because he had decided, lying awake through the smallest hours, that it could not be a question. A question gave a liar room to arrange himself. A statement just watched to see what broke.

What broke was small and terrible to watch. Pell's hand stopped halfway to the apple bag. His face didn't collapse; it simply emptied, like a house whose tenant has left in the night, and beneath the emptiness Gideon saw the thing that had lived there all along: exhaustion. The specific exhaustion of a man who has been carrying two lives and has just been permitted, finally, to set one down.

"How long," Sera said quietly.

"Since Merrowgate." Pell's voice was flat. He looked at his hands. "Since before. They came to my mother's house in Candlewick Row eight months ago. Not inquisitors — polite men, well-fed, very apologetic. They moved my mother and my sisters into a fine townhouse with a garden and servants and guards on the door, and my brother-in-law lost his debts, all of them, every debt, forgiven. It's a lovely house." He laughed, and the laugh had glass in it. "There's a walled garden. My mother can't get out. None of them can get out. They're fed, they're warm, they're *kept*, like — like a gardener keeps things he means to use later."

"Mavros," Gideon said.

"He never gave a name. Letters come in a courtly hand, looping, the same phrases turning over and—" Pell stopped. His eyes came up, and there was real fear in them now, but not for himself. "You've seen the letters. The king's chancery script. You know how it repeats."

"The same sentences," Sera murmured. "Like a man writing under water."

"That's how mine read too. *Tell me where they sleep. Tell me which roads. Your mother tends roses.* Over and over, like the hand that wrote it had forgotten it had already written it and loved the sentence too much to stop." Pell stood slowly, keeping his palms visible, an old habit of men who deal with soldiers. "I want you to understand that I knew what I was. Every night I told myself I'd find a way to warn you sideways, pass something so small it wouldn't count as treason, keep everyone alive including them. And every morning I'd write down where the pickets were, because the alternative was imagining my little niece asking why the garden gate doesn't open."

The wind moved through the pines, carrying ash. Below, the camp was waking; someone had started the flat, wordless humming that the Unlisted used for the dead instead of bells.

Gideon had rehearsed this part in the dark. He had imagined rage — his own, righteous, clean, the rage of a man betrayed by shared tea and songs for mules. He had imagined the simple arithmetic that the law would do, that any court would do: a traitor feeds the usurper, the usurper kills children, therefore the traitor's throat is a debt owed to order. Odessa was dead at the ford because of this man's neat reports. Six graves on the hillside below were fed, in part, by this man's careful hand.

And yet.

He thought of the fox taking apples from a traitor's fingers. Animals were not fooled often, and they were never fooled about the things that mattered. Whatever else Pell was, the kindness was not a costume — it was the rope he was hanging from, pulled taut between two sets of people he could not bear to lose. Mavros had chosen him precisely because of that. The sigil-binder did not recruit the cruel; the cruel needed no leverage. He collected people who loved too widely for their own safety, and he built his ears out of their love.

"The law says I should cut your throat and be done," Gideon said. "One verdict, clean, no appeals. The Unlisted don't hold courts, but they hold grudges, and half of them would help me dig."

"Yes," Pell agreed, steady. "They should. I won't argue."

"But the law also says Aldric ordered ward-keepers executed in his own hand, and we both know whose hand that really was. The law is a map somebody else drew." Gideon came closer, close enough to see the pulse jumping in Pell's throat. "Maps can lie, or they can tell the truth badly. Either way, you don't follow them off a cliff. You draw new lines."

Sera understood first — she always understood structure fastest. She said it aloud for all three of them, laying the plan out like contour lines: "You keep reporting. You report what we choose. He trusts his ear; we become his mouth."

Pell stared at her, then at Gideon, and something moved across his ruined face that might have been hope arriving late and ashamed. "You'd trust me to lie to him? After—"

"No," Gideon said. "I wouldn't trust you. Trust is for people who aren't holding knives to their own families." He let a breath out, long and slow, and made the choice with his eyes open, feeling its full weight settle across his shoulders like the ash that would not stop falling. "But I'd *use* you, and I'd protect what you're protecting, because that's the difference between justice and the needle-and-rope version of it. The inquisitors would hang you and call the ledger balanced. We're not balancing ledgers. We're ending the man who writes in loops."

"There's a condition," Sera said. "From now on, nothing leaves your mouth that hasn't passed through ours. Nothing. If Mavros asks the color of the sky, you ask us what color he wants it."

"And if he asks something I can't fake?" Pell asked. "If he asks something true that hurts you?"

"Then we decide together what truth costs, and who pays it," Gideon said. "That's the arrangement. Nobody pays alone anymore. That's rather the point of the whole enterprise."

Pell sat back down on the rock, abruptly, as if his legs had resigned without notice. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes for a moment, and when he lowered them he was, absurdly, crying a little and smiling at the same time, and Gideon understood that he was watching eight months of solitary terror end — not into safety, but into company. Sometimes that was the same gift.

"My mother," Pell said. "Her name is Ilsette. She grows roses she can't reach. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong the way hanging goes wrong," Gideon said. "That's all I can promise. But I'll tell you something I've never told anyone, and you can weigh my promise against it." He crouched, so their eyes were level, and let the mask drop for one breath — the smuggler's blankness, the careful nothing, all of it set aside. What looked out at Pell was the thing Gideon had hidden since childhood, the gift that heard the speech in everything. "The beasts know me. The crows, the foxes, the wolves in the high passes. When the time comes, I will send word to every creature in Candlewick Row, and there are rats in that walled garden, and sparrows in the eaves, and a cat on the wall next door who owes nobody anything. Your family will not be alone in that house. Not for one day. That I can promise, and the mountains can witness it."

Pell made a sound that was not quite a word and nodded, and nodded again.

* * *

They walked back into camp together, side by side, which was itself a message the Unlisted would read by noon. Gideon let them wonder. By evening he gathered the senior ones — Sera, Bramble standing in for the beasts who would not be summoned, old Ferren the deserter with his missing fingers, Wren of the ferry-flats — and told them as much of the truth as an army could carry without breaking.

"Pell is ours now," he finished. "Not because we forgave him. Forgiveness is a private matter and none of your business, nor mine. He's ours because he's more dangerous pointed at Mavros than buried under the hill, and because his family is hostage to the same crown that hollowed our king. Anybody who needs to hate him today, go ahead. Hate him quietly, and work beside him tomorrow."

Ferren chewed on that with his remaining teeth. "Mercy," he said at last, tasting the word like meat gone slightly off. "In my experience the gallows is cheaper."

"The gallows is cheaper," Gideon agreed. "So is burning a valley to deny your enemy shelter. Cheap things are what got us here." He looked around the fire, at faces lit orange and hard-edged, at the empty places where Odessa should have sat, where the Thornfell dead should have sat. "We're going to do the expensive thing. Once. As an experiment."

Bramble snorted, a long wet sound of profound equine skepticism. *"An experiment,"* the mule said. *"Wonderful. I shall note it in my diary, directly beneath the chapter where I carry your entire revolution uphill on my spine."*

A few people laughed — the first laughter since the graves, thin and startled, like birds flushed from cover. Gideon let it live a moment before he killed it, because there was work.

"Pell," he said. "Your next report. What does Mavros most want to believe about us?"

Pell had washed his face. He looked thinner and older and steadier, a man reassembled along new seams. "That you're desperate. That you're scattered. He wants reports of desperation the way a man wants confirmation he's winning." He paused, and Gideon watched the first act of the new life take shape behind his eyes. "He also wants one thing above all. He's asked three times, in three letters. Where the beast-speaker sleeps."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, a wolf began to sing — one of the mountain wolves, the ones who had carried their warning across the passes, the ones who knew Gideon's name now and used it freely among themselves, a low recurring note woven through the song like a signature.

"Then that," Gideon said softly, "is what we'll sell him. Sera — fetch the maps. If Mavros wants to know where I sleep, we're going to build him a beautiful bed, and we're going to invite him to tuck us in."

North, beyond the ridgelines, the sky was still starless. But Pell was looking up at it with narrowed eyes, calculating distances to a walled garden in Candlewick Row, and for the first time since the stars went out, the darkness had a shape they could map — and somewhere inside it, a locked gate that had just, very quietly, acquired a key.
