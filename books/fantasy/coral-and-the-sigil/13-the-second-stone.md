## 13. The Second Stone

The map said the valley should not exist.

Sera Voss had drawn a thousand false roads in her life—roads that bent where rivers didn't run, roads through mountains that had never been tunneled—and she knew their grammar the way a forger knows ink. But this valley had not been drawn by anyone. It simply was: a green cleft folded into the shoulder of the Cindervails, hidden so completely that the ridgelines around it seemed to lean together like conspirators. The old ward-road ran down its throat, and at the bottom of it, half a day's ride from the hollow-eyed silence of the first stone, stood the second ward-stone.

She felt it before she saw it, the way you feel a lit oven behind a closed door.

"Stop," she said, and raised her hand, and to her mild astonishment the whole ragged column stopped—Bramble with his ears back, Pell tightening his grip on his bow, Gideon reining in beside her without question. That was new. That was three weeks old and still strange: Gideon Okonkwo, who had spent his whole life making himself invisible, letting her stand beside him in the open like a person worth standing next to.

"You feel it too," he said quietly.

"It hums." She pressed her palm flat against the air, absurdly, as if she could touch the vibration. "The first stone was dead silent. This one isn't."

"That's either very good," Bramble said, "or it means whatever lives down there is still awake." The mule's voice was dry as road dust. He had been predicting their deaths since Merrowgate with the reliability of a tide-table, and so far he had been wrong every time, which he took as a personal affront.

Gideon swung down from the saddle. "We walk from here. Sera, with me. Everyone else—" he glanced at Pell, at the two deserters from the Thornfell garrison, at the gaunt woman called Marsh who had fled the falling wards with nothing but her ward-keeper's iron ring and the clothes she wore—"hold at the treeline. If I raise my hand, fall back to the horses."

"And if something raises *its* hand?" Bramble asked.

"Then run," Gideon said, "and I will be extremely annoyed about it afterward."

They went down into the valley on foot, Sera with her satchel of maps against her spine and her sister's name folded up small inside her chest where the Ferryman's Guild could never collect it again. The grass grew wrong here—too even, too green, like a carpet no one had walked on—and there were no birds, but neither was there the terrible mute emptiness of Ward-Stone Hollow. There was *attention*. The whole valley was paying attention to them.

"Gideon," she murmured, "the traps—"

"I know."

He had stopped walking. Ahead of them the meadow sloped toward a circle of standing stones, and in its center rose the second ward-stone: a pillar of grey rock taller than two men, carved over every surface with script older than the kingdom, older than the roads it guarded. It was cracked down one face, and from the crack seeped a faint amber light, pulsing slowly, like breath. Like a heart that refused, out of sheer stubbornness, to stop.

Around it, arranged with a madwoman's precision, were the traps.

Sera counted without meaning to; counting was how she thought. Nine snare-lines strung between stones, camouflaged with woven grass. Four pits under lattices of green boughs, their lips dusted with what looked like ash but probably wasn't. A bell hung from an alder branch with no clapper—which meant the clapper was somewhere else, waiting to be pulled by wire. And hanging in the branches of the alder itself, turning gently in the wind, a dozen wooden plaques, each painted with the same symbol: a circle bisected by a line that did not quite touch either side.

Her stomach dropped. She had seen that mark once before, in the oldest book of her family's collection, on the page her grandmother had burned rather than let be copied.

"The sigil," she whispered. "Those are warnings. *Against* the sigil."

"She knows," Gideon said. "Whoever she is, she knows what ate the wards."

A voice came out of the standing stones like a thrown knife.

"One more step, smuggler, and the ground takes your legs at the knee."

* * *

Odessa Vane did not look like a legend. She looked like a scarecrow that had been left out through seven winters and had opinions about it. Her hair was white going grey going white again, hacked off at the jaw with what might have been a knife or might have been teeth. She wore a ward-keeper's coat so old its insignia had been unpicked, thread by careful thread—the way exiles unpick things, so no one can prove what they were. In her hands was a crossbow, aimed at Gideon's chest with the steady patience of someone who had been aiming at things for thirty years.

Behind her, half-hidden in the shadow of the ward-stone, moved shapes: goats, six of them, each painted on the forehead with that same broken-circle sign, each watching the strangers with eyes that were not goat-eyes. They were aware. They were *listening*.

"Odessa Vane," Sera said, and the crossbow snapped toward her voice.

"Nobody's called me that in twenty years." The old woman's eyes were pale as river ice, and they went from Sera's face to her satchel and stayed there. "You're carrying charts. Real ones. I can smell the vellum."

"Sera Voss. My family kept the old maps. I'm—"

"I know what you are." Odessa spat, neatly, to the side. "Cartographer. The capital's little drawing rooms full of people who copy the wards' outlines onto silk and call it scholarship while the actual keepers rot in exile for asking why nobody's *renewed* anything in a hundred years." The crossbow came back to Gideon. "And you. You smell like beasts and lying. Talk fast."

Gideon did something Sera had learned to recognize: he put on the smuggler's mask, the easy slouch, the smile that gave away nothing. "We're looking for—"

"Don't." Odessa's finger tightened on the trigger guard. "Don't do the voice with me. I've been alone with goats for two decades; I've got nothing but time and I will spend all of it watching you decide whether to tell the truth." Her head tilted. "There. Feel that? Your beasts talk to you. Don't insult us both by pretending."

The silence stretched. Beside Sera, Gideon had gone very still, and she watched the mask fail—not dramatically, not all at once, but like frost giving way under a boot heel. His shoulders dropped a quarter inch. His hands, which had been loose and casual, curled slightly inward, the way hands curl around something being taken.

"How long have you known?" he asked. His real voice. Sera had heard it maybe ten times.

"Since you crossed my snare-line at the treeline and the hares told me you apologized to them." Odessa lowered the crossbow by a single degree. "Now. Say what you came to say, beast-speaker, and say it plain, because the stone is dying and I have been guarding a corpse for three years and I am *tired*."

So Gideon told her. All of it—the night the stars vanished, the wolf that died in his arms with a king's burning chest behind its eyes, the hollowed stag, the drained husk of the first stone, Mavros's patient mind wearing a crown like a garden hat. He spoke plainly, as commanded, and Sera watched Odessa Vane listen, and watched the madness in the old woman's face rearrange itself into something worse: understanding.

When he finished, Odessa laughed. It was a terrible sound, thin and rusted, the laugh of someone whose sanity had been rationed for years and had just been handed an invoice.

"*Eaten*," she said. "I told them. Twenty-two years ago I stood in the Wardens' Hall and told them the tribute calculations were wrong—that the stones were drawing more than the realm fed back, year over year, a slow deficit, a *leak*—and they stripped my rank and put me out past the border for alarmism." She turned and looked up at the ward-stone, at its amber guttering pulse. "It wasn't a leak. It was a mouth. Someone found the tap and has been drinking from the well for longer than any of us knew, drop by drop by drop, and now he's thirsty enough to drink the kingdom dry."

"Mavros," Sera said. "The court sorcerer."

"The court sorcerer." Odessa nodded slowly. "Of course. Who else gets to stand next to a king for forty years, unremarked, tending him like a prize rose?" Her ice-pale eyes cut back to them. "You want something from me. Nobody climbs into a madwoman's valley for the conversation."

Sera unshouldered her satchel. "We need to learn unbinding. How to pull the sigil's threads loose—to free the beasts, and eventually the king. We heard a keeper survived exile in the Cindervails. We hoped—"

"You hoped I'd teach you." Odessa walked to the ward-stone and laid one spread hand against its cracked face, and Sera saw the light pulse brighten under her palm, recognizing her, the way a dog recognizes its own. "Unbinding isn't a trick. It isn't knots and countersigns, whatever the Hall taught before they forgot everything. To unbind a sigil-thread you have to *hear* it—you have to speak to the thing it's bound into, find the loop of compulsion, and convince it, thread by thread, to let go. Convincing requires speech." She turned. "Speech requires a gift. And the only gift-bearer I've met in twenty years is standing in my meadow apologizing to hares."

The valley went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath among the alder branches, and the wooden plaques knocked softly against each other like teeth.

"No," Gideon said.

"You haven't heard the terms yet."

"There's only one term you could want, and the answer is no."

"My terms," Odessa continued, inexorable, walking toward him with the unhurried gait of a woman who owned every inch of this ground, "are these. I will teach you unbinding—all of it, the true craft, not the Hall's dried-out liturgy. I will give you my traps, my stores, my knowledge of the stones, and if I die before the last lesson is done, my notes, which are extensive and mostly legible. In exchange—" she stopped an arm's length from Gideon, close enough that Sera could see her hands trembling, and understand it was not fear— "in exchange, you let me study you. Every day. Your gift, its limits, its grammar, its *costs*. No one alive knows how beast-speech works, beast-speaker. No one has ever written it down. When this war eats us all, I intend for there to be a book."

"That's it?" Bramble would have said it with more sarcasm, Sera thought. Gideon said it with something rawer.

"That's it," Odessa said. "That's the whole price. Let yourself be *seen*, boy. Thoroughly. By someone taking notes."

* * *

They made camp that evening in the lee of the standing stones, because Odessa decreed it—"You'll not march anywhere until I've mapped what's in his head"—and Sera sat apart with her charts, ostensibly cataloguing, actually watching.

She had known Gideon Okonkwo for less than a season, and in that time she had pieced him together the way she pieced together coastlines: from fragments, inference, the shape of the gaps. She knew he flinched from gratitude worse than from blades. She knew he slept facing the exits. She knew that when the Unlisted children in Merrowgate's underways had clamored to touch Bramble's ears, Gideon had translated the mule's complaints into elaborate compliments, and had looked, for exactly four seconds, like a man standing in warm water—before catching himself and closing the door on it.

His whole life was a door held shut. And now an old madwoman with a crossbow and a lifetime of loneliness was asking him to take it off its hinges.

Odessa had him seated on a flat stone near the ward-pillar, her notebook open on her knees, firing questions like a customs officer. "First word you ever heard from an animal?"

"A horse. I was six. She told me my mother was crying in the next room and asked me to bring her the comb."

"Were you frightened?"

"I was six. I brought her the comb." A pause. "The fright came later. When I understood that no one else had heard it. That no one else ever would."

"And your first instinct, at six, having been given a miracle?"

Gideon was quiet for a long moment. The ward-stone pulsed its stubborn amber light across his face, and Sera saw him look at it instead of at them, the way people look at fires when they're deciding what truth to spend.

"To hide it," he said. "My mother told me—she was terrified, actually, the moment I told her—the old stories about gift-bearers. Taken for the army. Taken for the temples. Taken apart by scholars. So we made a rule, her and me. Never be noticed. Not for this. Never for this." His jaw worked. "I've kept that rule for thirty years. I have walked past drowning men and kept it. I have—there are things I did to keep it that I don't—"

He stopped. Odessa waited, pen poised, with the patience of the very old.

"Ask your questions," Gideon said finally, and his voice was level, and Sera understood that he had just stepped through the door, and hated it, and was doing it anyway. Because the king was hollow and the wards were dying and three cities were silent, and the only craft that could save any of it required a teacher, and the teacher required payment, and the payment was himself.

Odessa wrote for a while. Then, without looking up: "Your mother's rule kept you alive. It also kept every beast in this realm deaf to the one man who could have warned them. Do you understand that I'm not punishing you? I'm offering you the chance to make the hiding have been worth something."

"I understand," Gideon said, "that you're enjoying this far too much for a hermit."

"I've talked to goats for twenty years," Odessa said. "Yes. I'm enjoying it."

Sera closed her chart case and walked over, because some moments you don't get to observe from outside without becoming smaller. She sat down on the grass beside the flat stone, and Odessa glanced at her, and something passed across the old woman's face—recognition, maybe, of what Sera was doing, or permission for it.

"The first lesson," Odessa said, "starts tomorrow at dawn. Unbinding is done through the beast, so we begin with the goats. They're bound—lightly, my own work, testing what I learned before exile. You'll talk them loose, thread by thread, and I'll watch how you do it, and then we'll write it down, and then we'll do it harder." She snapped the notebook shut. "But tonight, one more term, and this one's mine alone, not part of the bargain."

"Name it," Gideon said, sounding tired and young.

"When you unbind the king—if you live that long—you'll be spending memories to do it. I've read the accounts of the old binding-wars. I know what the craft costs." Her pale eyes fixed on him, and for a moment the madness fell away entirely and Sera saw the warden she must have been: granite, exact, unbending. "So before we start, you'll tell me the things you can't afford to lose. Your mother's face. The sound of her voice. Whatever else is load-bearing in that skull of yours. And you'll write them down, here, in this book, in your own hand—so that when the craft strips them out of you, someone who loves you can read them back."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere in the dark above the valley, impossibly, faintly, a star appeared—one only, flickering like a candle in a draught, and Sera felt the whole camp turn toward it, hungry, hopeful, afraid to believe.

Gideon reached out and took the notebook. His hand was shaking. He opened it to the first page.

"Her name," he said quietly, "was Amara. She sang off-key on purpose, to make us laugh. She smelled like cedar smoke. Start writing, Sera—you spell better than I do."

And that was when the goats began to scream.

All six at once, a sound no goat should make, high and human and terrified—and Odessa was on her feet with the crossbow up, and Gideon was already running toward them, and Sera saw the animals' painted foreheads glowing, the broken-circle sigil kindling red in the dark like a brand being pressed to flesh.

Something had noticed them. Something patient, something hungry, wearing a crown—

And down the valley's throat, from the direction they had come, came the sound Sera had been dreading since Merrowgate: hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast. With hounds.
