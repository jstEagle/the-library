## 14. Lessons in Unbinding

The hollowed beasts came to the hollow at dusk, as if Odessa had called them—which, Gideon realized later, she had.

She stood at the lip of the ravine above the second ward-stone, a lean and weathered woman with frost in her braid and madness braided somewhere beneath it, and she rang a small iron bell that made no sound Gideon's ears could catch. But the beasts heard it. They came down the scree in single file: the stag with its drained and milken eyes, two foxes moving stiff as clockwork, a she-bear whose fur had gone dull as old rope, and behind them a scatter of crows that did not caw, did not call, did not do anything a crow ought to do.

"They still come when the stone calls," Odessa said, watching them gather around the pale husk of the ward-stone like mourners around a grave that wouldn't stay shut. "That's the mercy of it. The sigil eats the beast's will but leaves the shape of obedience. It doesn't know how to make anything new. It only knows how to wear what was already there."

"Like its master," Sera said quietly, from where she sat with her maps spread on a flat rock, pretending to work while she listened to everything.

"Like its master," Odessa agreed, and her mouth did something that had once been a smile.

Gideon kept his eyes on the stag. He had looked into those eyes before, at Ward-Stone Hollow, and what he'd seen looking back out of them still lived behind his eyelids at night: patience vast enough to fill a kingdom, hunger patient enough to wait out a dynasty, a mind wearing a crown of borrowed light. Mavros. The court sorcerer who had hollowed a king the way a man hollows a gourd to carry water.

"You said you could teach me," Gideon said. His voice came out flatter than he intended. "To unbind them."

"I said I would teach you *if*." Odessa turned from the ravine and looked at him, and her eyes were not mad at all in that moment; they were old and clear and terribly kind. "If you let me study the gift. If you show me how a smuggler boy from Thornfell learned to speak the tongue of every furred and feathered thing between the passes without a single lesson, without a single keeper noticing him, for thirty years."

"That's the price," Gideon said. "Not memory. Not blood. Just—to be seen."

"Just the worst thing you can imagine," Odessa said. "I know. I hid too, once. Different hiding. Same dark."

* * *

The first lesson began an hour after moonrise, in the cold amphitheater of the ravine, with the drained ward-stone rising between them like a tooth pulled from the world's jaw. Sera watched from the rim with Bramble, the mule's ears swiveled forward, and Pell—still half-suspected, still carefully forgiven—kept the fire low.

"Touch the stag," Odessa said.

"He'll feel me."

"He feels everything. That is the horror of his condition. He is a passenger in his own body, awake behind glass, watching his hooves move without him." Odessa's voice hardened. "You have spent your whole life speaking to beasts who wanted to speak back. Now you must speak to one who cannot answer. It is a different skill. It is closer to surgery than conversation."

Gideon climbed down into the ravine. The stone underfoot was wrong—the way all stone near a dead ward was wrong now, faintly sour, like milk gone off, like a room where someone had died. The stag stood facing the husk, motionless except for the shallow bellows of its breath.

He put out his hand and rested it on the animal's neck.

The silence hit him first. Every beast he had ever touched had been noisy inside—a churn of appetite and wariness and curiosity, a weather of small wants. The stag was a held breath. A house with all the furniture covered in white sheets. And beneath the sheets, faint as a heartbeat through a wall: something pressing. Something *there*, trying to turn its face toward him the way a drowning man turns toward light.

*I'm here,* Gideon said, in the wordless tongue he had never had a name for because he'd never dared give it one. *I see you.*

The response came not as thought but as pressure, a moan shaped like meaning. And threaded through it, wound around it the way bindweed winds around a living stem: the sigil. He could feel it now that he knew to feel—thin black filaments of compulsion, anchored somewhere far away, drinking. Not killing. Feeding slowly, the way a farmer milks a cow he intends to keep through winter.

"Can you feel the threads?" Odessa asked, from somewhere above him. Her voice seemed to come through water.

"Yes."

"Then listen well, because this is the part no one survives learning twice." She came down the slope, and even maddened and exiled she moved like a woman who had tended stones for decades, sure-footed on the sour ground. "A sigil-thread is not cut. Cutting leaves a stump, and stumps regrow toward the heart. You must *unpick* it. Follow it to its knot. Loose the knot. Draw the whole thread backward through everything it has woven into, gently, gently, the way you'd draw a splinter from a child's foot. And while you hold that thread in your hands, Gideon Okonkwo, you are holding one end of a line that runs all the way to a crowned thing in the capital. Do you understand? For as long as the unbinding lasts, he can feel you touching his work."

Gideon's mouth went dry. "And when he feels it?"

"Then he knows exactly where you are, and exactly what you are, and exactly which of his beasts you're stealing back. Which is why we work at night. Which is why we work one at a time. And which is why—" her grip closed on his shoulder, hard as a trap "—you must never, ever pull faster than the thread wants to go."

"And the memory," Gideon said. "You mentioned payment."

Odessa was quiet for a moment. Above them the vanished stars were only a darkness deeper than dark; it had been eleven nights since anyone had seen a star, and the sky had begun to feel less like night and more like a lid.

"The gift draws on what you are," she said at last. "Speaking with beasts costs most folk nothing because they have so little of it—it trickles. But you are a flood, boy, and floods carve channels. To force that much of yourself down a sigil-thread, to burn hot enough to loosen a knot tied by Mavros—you will pay in the coin of the self. The gift takes memories. Small ones first, if you're lucky. It takes what lies loosest." Her voice gentled into something almost apologetic. "It will take what you can least afford, eventually. They always do."

"How do you know?" Gideon asked. "How unbinding works, what it costs?"

And Odessa Vane, half-mad keeper of a dead stone, smiled with all the cracks showing. "Because I paid for my knowledge the same way you're about to pay for yours. Ask me the name of my mother sometime. Ask me the name of my *own* daughter." She let go of his shoulder. "Begin."

* * *

The fox went first, because it was smallest.

Gideon knelt in the frost with the little vixen's head cradled in both hands, and Odessa talked him through it in a low dry voice, like a woman teaching needlework: find the thread, follow it, don't yank. He found it almost at once—a filament of cold running down through the vixen's spine into the earth, thin as spider silk and strong as bridge cable. He followed it down. Down past the roots of the dead stone, down through sour rock, down along a line that stretched impossibly far, southward, toward a throne room he had never seen.

Then he burned.

There was no other word for it. The gift he had dammed and hidden and starved for thirty years broke loose all at once and poured itself down the thread, and the knot at the far end of it—he felt it, a hard black whorl of Mavros's will, patient and neat—began to soften. Not fast. Never fast. He worked it loose strand by strand the way Odessa had said, and the whole time he could feel the attention at the other end sharpening, a vast drowsy thing stirring, one finger of its awareness curling toward the itch in its web.

*Let go,* something whispered up the thread, in a voice like a king granting audience. *You are wasting yourself on vermin, little speaker. Come and be counted. There is a place for your kind at my court. There has always been a place—*

Gideon did not answer. Answering, he understood instinctively, was a thread too, and threads ran both ways.

The knot gave. The vixen shuddered once, whole-bodied, and then her eyes came back—not bright, not yet, but *hers*, cloudy with confusion and terror and the awful backlog of everything she had done while not herself. She screamed then, high and thin, and Gideon held her while she screamed, because there was nothing else to do.

It wasn't until the screaming stopped and she curled, exhausted, against his chest, that he noticed the gap.

He reached for the name of the bend in the Thornwillow River where he'd caught his first trout—his father's deep laugh teaching him the lure—and found the bend, and the trout, and the cold water. But the laugh was gone. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from the gum: a smooth absence, healed over already, as though the laugh had never existed at all.

"There," Odessa said softly, watching his face. "Now you know the price."

"It took my father's laugh."

"It took the loosest coin in your purse. Tonight. Tomorrow it will dig deeper." She crouched beside him, and for a moment her madness and her clarity were indistinguishable. "You can stop. One fox unbound is one fox. No one would blame—"

"No," Gideon said. He looked down at the sleeping vixen, and at the stag waiting beyond her, patient behind its glass. "Thirty years I stayed hidden so nothing I did would cost anybody anything. Including me. This is the first time the hiding's been worth breaking." He swallowed. "Teach me the stag next."

* * *

They fell into a rhythm across the following days, and the rhythm was a slow hemorrhage.

Each dawn they chose one beast. Each dusk Gideon went down into the ravine with his hands steady and his heart a fist, and each night he came back up missing something.

The second unbinding took the smell of woodsmoke, which he discovered only when Pell lit the evening fire and Gideon realized he was breathing in nothing at all—that whole warm vocabulary of pine and birch and peat, gone like a language he'd never studied. The third took the color of his mother's kitchen, leaving him with a room he knew was yellow but could no longer see. The fourth, crueler, took a whole afternoon: a market day in Merrowgate when he was nineteen, a girl with flour on her wrists, three hours of his youth scooped out entire. He knew it had happened. He could see the edges of it, the way you see the edges of a hole in fog. The middle was simply not there anymore.

Sera noticed on the fifth day.

She was up at dawn, as always, correcting charts by the fire, and she looked up when he came out of the ravine with the she-bear walking free and dazed behind him, and she looked at him too long.

"What did it take?" she asked.

"A winter," he said. "The whole winter I was fourteen, snowed in at the high camp with just the wolves for company. Best winter of my life." He heard his own voice and was vaguely proud of how level it came out. "Apparently it was also the loosest coin in my purse."

"Gideon." Sera set down her pen. She had ink on two fingers and shadows under her eyes and the particular fierce stillness of a woman deciding whether to say something dangerous. "You should keep a record. Write things down. Names, places, faces—everything that matters to you. Before it matters too late."

"So I can relearn myself from notes." He almost laughed. "Like a stranger reading a traveler's account of some country he's never visited."

"Better than losing the country entirely." Her jaw tightened. "I've seen what forgetting does to people, Gideon. I forged roads onto maps because my family remembered roads the crown wanted forgotten. Memory is the only weapon some of us get. Don't surrender yours without keeping receipts."

So that evening he sat by the fire with one of Sera's spare charts and charcoal, and wrote, in the careful hand of a man who'd taught himself letters late: *My mother's face—I have not lost this yet. Brown skin, lines at the corners of her eyes, she laughed with her whole shoulders. Check often.* Below it: *Bramble hates the word 'nag.' Find out why, while finding out is still possible.* Below that, hesitating, then pressing hard: *Odessa had a daughter. Someone should remember that Odessa was someone's whole sky once.*

Odessa read the list over his shoulder without asking. When she reached her own name, she made a sound that was not quite laughter.

"Clever cartographer," she said to Sera. Then, to Gideon, quieter: "Tomorrow, the bear's mate. The big male. He's the deepest bound of any of them—Mavros spent real strength on him, he's meant to be a weapon, not a watcher. It will cost you more than an afternoon." She looked at the fire. "I want you to choose, tonight, what you're willing to pay. Hold it in your mind. Name it to yourself. Sometimes—only sometimes—if you offer the gift something freely, it takes what's given instead of what's nearest. It prefers to be paid honestly. Most old powers do."

* * *

He chose badly, or he chose well; he would never be certain which.

The great bear came down into the ravine at dusk like a landslide given fur, and Gideon felt the sigil-threads in him before he touched the beast—thick as ship's cable, woven deep, anchored not in the spine but somewhere behind the eyes. Following them down was like descending a rope into a well. The knot at the bottom was not a knot. It was a *grip*. He felt Mavros notice him almost immediately, felt that vast patient attention swing toward him like a lighthouse beam, and this time the voice on the thread did not whisper courtesies.

*Mine,* it said. *All of it. Every beast, every ward, every starless mile. And you, little thief—you I will keep. Your gift is wasted on animals. Kneel, and I will teach you what it was made for.*

Gideon burned.

He burned hotter than he had ever let himself burn, thirty years of dammed flood tearing loose at once, and the grip fought him the whole way down, strand by shrieking strand, and somewhere in the middle of it—when the bear was bellowing and the ravine walls were ringing and his own vision had gone white at the edges—something in him tore free that he had not offered. He felt it go like a hand unclasping from his own chest, and he grabbed, blindly, and offered instead the only thing he had ready: the list. The chart by the fire. *Check often.*

The gift took the words themselves. He felt them dissolve—*brown skin, lines at the corners of her eyes, laughed with her whole shoulders*—felt them pour down the cable and vanish into the burning, and the last thing he knew of his mother's face was that he was losing it, and then he didn't know it, and the absence where it had been was smooth as river stone.

When he surfaced, the bear lay free and gasping in the frost, and Odessa had both his arms, hauling him upright, shouting his name—his name, and then, when he didn't answer, another sound, a word in a language he didn't recognize, repeated like an anchor-line. Later he would realize it was the ward-keepers' word for *alive*.

He got up. His legs held. Across the ravine, Sera was standing very still with the charcoal list pressed to her chest, and her eyes were wet, and he understood with a strange floating clarity that she had watched him lose it. That she had read the words as they left him. That she was now the only person alive who knew what his mother had looked like.

"Sera," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Tell me about her. Please. While you still can."

And she came to him across the frost and told him—lines at the corners of the eyes, laughter in the shoulders, brown hands quick with work—telling him back his own inheritance like a cartographer redrawing a coast from memory, getting every detail right, knowing she had only one copy of the map and he had none.

Behind them, unnoticed, Pell slipped away from the firelight toward the ravine's southern rim, where the ground rose toward the pass road and the dark, and stood a long moment looking south, toward the capital, toward the thing that paid for his family's breath.

Then he climbed back down to the fire, and warmed his hands, and said nothing.

But Odessa, mad Odessa, keeper of a dead stone, watched him settle, and watched the direction his eyes drifted when he thought no one looked, and said nothing either—only added, silently, to a tally she had been keeping since the first night.

Three more beasts waited in the ravine. And far to the south, under a starless sky, a crowned thing had felt five knots come undone, and was no longer merely curious.
