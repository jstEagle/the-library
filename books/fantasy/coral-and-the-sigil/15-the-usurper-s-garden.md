## 15. The Usurper's Garden

The letter came folded inside a hollowed reed, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and it smelled faintly of the capital's incense—myrrh and something sweeter underneath, something like fruit left too long in the sun. Pell had written it in a hand so small and neat it looked like the work of a clerk, not a scout with sawdust in his boots. Sera read it three times by candlelight in the back room of the Ferryman's safehouse in Merrowgate, and by the third reading her hands had gone cold enough that she had to set the page down against the table to keep it from trembling.

*Mavros,* the letter said. *It was Mavros all along. Court sorcerer. Keeper of the King's Health, they call him now. He tends the king the way a gardener tends a rose he cannot bear to cut.*

She had known, of course. Some part of her had known since the ward-stone at Hollow, since Gideon had come back from speaking with the hollowed stag with frost in his voice and a name half-shaped on his lips. But knowing a thing in the abstract and reading it in a dead clerk's careful script were two different hungers. She read the letter a fourth time, and this time she did not flinch.

*The king lives in the east tower. They have moved his apartments there—three rooms, kept warm, kept quiet. He is fed. He is washed. He is dressed every morning in the full regalia, crown and all, and seated by the window where the light is best. Mavros comes to him twice a day. I have seen it through the servants' stair, from the landing below. The sorcerer brings a watering can.*

*A watering can, Lady Voss. I swear this on my children's names. A silver watering can, and he waters the king as if the king were a plant.*

Sera closed her eyes. She could see it—could not help seeing it—the great gilded chamber, the vacant king in his crown of Thornfell iron and Merrow gold, and the old sorcerer moving between him and the window with the unhurried tenderness of a man pruning something he loved. It was worse than cruelty. Cruelty she could have mapped, could have predicted, could have turned against itself. This was devotion. This was a man who had hollowed out his sovereign and kept the shell alive because the shell was *useful*, and who had somehow, in the years of it, come to cherish the usefulness the way a miser cherishes the weight of his hoard.

*The sigil is not on the king's chest anymore,* Pell wrote. *Or not only. It has grown roots. I saw the king's collar loosened once, for his bathing. There are lines down his ribs like the veins of a leaf. They move when he breathes.*

She read the rest standing up, because sitting had become unbearable. The letter gave her numbers, and numbers were the language she trusted when words failed. Three guards at the tower stair, rotated at dawn and dusk. A fourth posted inside, a woman named Corla who had been hollowed so gently she did not know it—she smiled, she joked, she carried her post with perfect devotion, and there was nothing behind her eyes but the sigil's patient green light. The king's physicians had all been dismissed two years ago. The king's food was prepared by Mavros's own hands. The king had not spoken a word in nineteen months, and the court had learned to call this *dignity*.

*They say he is at prayer,* Pell wrote. *They say the king has withdrawn from the world to commune with the stars. And the terrible thing, Lady Voss, the thing I cannot put down, is that they believe it. The whole court believes it. I believed it. I carried his orders. I executed ward-keepers in his name and went home to my wife and slept soundly.*

*My family is still in the capital. I am still his ear. I will send what I can. But the roads close around me like a hand, and I think he knows something is listening. Be quick.*

There was no signature. There never was.

* * *

Gideon found her an hour before dawn, the way he always did—without sound, without warning, simply present one moment where he had not been the moment before. He stood in the doorway of the back room with his cloak still wet from the river fog, and he looked at her face and then at the letter, and he did not ask. He never asked. He waited, the way he waited for beasts, with a patience that had once infuriated her and now felt like the only kind of company she could bear.

"Mavros," she said. "It's Mavros. The court sorcerer. He's been there the whole time—thirty years at Aldric's side. He hollowed him slowly, Gideon. Years. Maybe decades. The first forged orders in the king's hand are dated eleven years back."

Gideon came to the table and stood over the letter, reading. His face did the thing it always did when he read human cruelty: it went very still, the way a forest goes still before a storm that has not yet decided to break. "A watering can," he said at last.

"A watering can."

"He waters him." Gideon's voice was flat, but Sera had learned to hear the currents underneath it, the way she had learned to hear the old roads underneath the new ones. There was fury in the flatness, and under the fury something colder and more useful: calculation. "He keeps the sigil fed. The king is the battery—the wards were the great reservoirs, and the king was the well they all drew from, and Mavros is drinking through him. Every ward-stone we've found drained, every city gone silent—it all runs through that one hollowed man in a tower."

"And the court adores him for it." Sera pulled the letter back and smoothed it flat, an old habit, the cartographer's reflex—every document was a map, every map could be mended. "That's what Pell doesn't understand, or understands too well. Mavros hasn't hidden the king. He's *displayed* him. A king at prayer. A king in contemplation. The realm has spent two years watching its sovereign sit in a window and calling it holiness."

Gideon pulled out the chair across from her and sat, which he almost never did; he was a man who seemed to regard chairs as a temporary condition. "Then we don't just have to kill a sorcerer," he said. "We have to convince a kingdom that its saint is a leech."

"We don't have to convince them." Sera met his eyes. In the candlelight they were the color of river water over dark stone. "We have to show them the king. Alive. Speaking. That's all. One hour of Aldric's own voice, and every order Mavros ever forged turns to ash in the court's hands."

"That's if there's anything left of Aldric to speak." Gideon said it gently, which was somehow worse. "You've read the letters. The looping. The same sentences, over and over, like a wheel turning in mud. Sera—how much of a man survives eleven years of that?"

She had no answer, and he had known she would have no answer, and that was why he had asked: not to wound her but to make her carry the question, because he believed—and she had come to believe it too—that a plan built on hope was a road built on fog. She turned instead to what she could do. What she had always been able to do.

"I need the charts," she said. "The old ones. My family's."

* * *

The Voss charts had traveled with them out of the capital in a oilcloth roll no longer than her forearm, sewn into the lining of a dead mule's pack-saddle, and Sera had not unrolled them since Merrowgate. They were her inheritance and her indictment both: six generations of cartographers who had drawn the roads that predated the kingdom, the ways that ran under mountains and beneath rivers, the paths the first ward-keepers had walked when the stars themselves were young and the wards were being set into the world like stitches into living flesh. Her family had kept the maps. Her family had also, in the end, been the ones the inquisitors came for, because a map of the kingdom's foundations was a map of the kingdom's weaknesses, and the sigil-binder could not abide weaknesses he did not own.

She unrolled them on the long table in the Ferryman's maproom, weighting the corners with inkpots and a candlestick and Bramble's abandoned iron shoe, and she began—methodically, lovingly, with the terrible precision of her trade—to build the palace.

Not the palace as it was. The palace as it had been, and therefore, in all the ways that mattered, as it still was. Kings rebuilt facades; they did not rebuild bones. The Citadel of Aldric's line had been raised on the foundations of something far older—she had proven that once, in ink, and it had cost her family everything—and its bones ran deep. Servant ways cut into living rock. Cistern tunnels from the days before the aqueducts. The old ward-keepers' galleries, sealed for centuries, that ran beneath the throne room like the veins beneath a wrist.

She drew from memory first, because memory was the chart no inquisitor could burn. She had walked those halls as a girl, her father's hand on her shoulder, her father's voice in her ear: *Count the doors, Sera. Count the windows. A map is a promise you make to someone you will never meet, and you do not break promises.* She had counted. She had never stopped counting. Even in the inquisitors' cells, even on the road with a smuggler's caravan, some part of her mind had gone on measuring the world, door by door, window by window, because it was the only inheritance they had left her that could not be confiscated.

The candle burned down. She did not notice. She laid the old charts over her memory-work and let them argue with each other—the girl who had counted doors against the ancestors who had measured galleries—and where they disagreed she trusted the older hand, because stone outlasts memory, and memory outlasts paper, and the oldest paper in the realm was older than the crown.

It was nearly full light when she saw it.

She sat back. She looked at what her own hands had made, and she felt the particular cold that cartographers knew, the cold of a map that has just told you something you did not want to know.

The east tower. The king's tower, where Pell's letter said the hollowed king sat in his window like a flower in its pot. On her chart, in her great-great-grandmother's faded ink, the east tower was not a tower at all. It was a *root*. The oldest structure in the citadel, raised over the junction of three ward-galleries, planted—there was no other word for it—directly above the place where the first ward-stone of the capital had been set into the earth. The kings had built their bedchamber over the wellspring of the realm's protection, generation after generation, because that was what kings were *for*. The crown and the wellspring, bound together, each guarding the other.

Mavros had not moved the king to the east tower for the light.

He had moved him home to his roots.

"He's *gardening*," Sera said aloud, to the empty room, and her voice sounded strange in it. "He planted the sigil where it could drink from the source. The king isn't just a battery. He's a cutting. He's a graft. Mavros took the royal line—the bloodline the first wards were sworn to—and he's grown the sigil through it, down into the old stone, and everything the wards have left is being drawn up through a dying man's ribs."

Behind her, a floorboard spoke. She did not startle; she had stopped startling at Gideon's arrivals weeks ago, the way one stops startling at weather. He stood in the doorway with two cups of something hot and the shadows of the underways still clinging to his shoulders, and he looked at the chart for a long time.

"Show me the way in," he said.

* * *

She showed him. That was the morning's work, and the morning after that: Sera Voss, fugitive, forger, last of the Voss line, laying out on a smuggler's table the anatomy of the palace she had been born in the shadow of, while the man who would walk into it listened the way he listened to everything—completely, and without once interrupting.

"The servants' stair on the north face is the old way," she said, her finger tracing a line that existed in three centuries of ink and one living memory. "It was cut for the ward-keepers, before the palace was the palace. It surfaces in the undercroft beneath the great hall, and from the undercroft there are two galleries. One runs east, under the throne room, to the root of the east tower. The other runs south and dies at a collapse—here—" she tapped it, "—or it did, two hundred years ago. I can't promise what's past the collapse. No one has mapped it since my family stopped being allowed to."

"And the galleries themselves?"

"Sealed. Officially. But seals are made by men, and men forget why the doors were shut." She looked up at him. "Gideon, I have to say this plainly, because it's the one thing I know that you don't. Those galleries were built by ward-keepers, and they will remember ward-keepers' hands. Yours. When you walk them, the stone will know you. That may open doors. It may also—" she chose the word with a cartographer's care, "—*announce* you. Everything in that palace that still remembers the old wards will feel you coming. Including, I think, the sigil."

Gideon was quiet for a while. Outside, the underways of Merrowgate were waking—the Unlisted's morning noises, Bramble's complaining, the clatter of the wounded being washed and fed—and the sounds came down the corridor like a tide that belonged to no king.

"Then we go in the way he won't expect," Gideon said at last, "which is the way that tells him exactly what we are." He almost smiled. It was a thin thing, that smile, worn down to the wire by everything they had lost—Thornfell in ashes, Odessa's traps still unsprung somewhere in the hills, the hollowed stag's dead eyes that Gideon did not talk about and Sera did not ask about. "Mavros offered me a place, you know. In the stag's mind, I heard the edge of it. He's been watching me for years, Sera. A beast-speaker, unlisted, unclaimed. He wants the gift in his garden too."

"Then he's been watering you as well."

That startled a real laugh out of him, short and rough, and it was such a rare sound that Sera found herself memorizing it, the way she memorized everything: door by door, window by window, a promise made to someone she might never meet again.

* * *

The second letter came nine days later, and it was shorter, and the hand was not steady.

*He knows,* Pell wrote. *I don't know how, or how much. Yesterday he stopped in the east tower on his morning rounds—me on the landing below, holding my breath—and he stood at the king's window and he said, to the king, to no one, in that gentle voice he uses: "Someone is walking the old roads again. Isn't that wonderful. We haven't had visitors in so long."*

*And the king turned his head. Lady Voss, the king has not moved in nineteen months. He turned his head toward the window, and he smiled, and it was not the king's smile. It was the garden's smile. The sigil is so deep in him now that I think when Mavros speaks, the sigil hears, and when the sigil hears, the king's face answers.*

*I am being watched. Corla asked me yesterday where I sleep. I told her the barracks and she smiled at me with the green light behind her eyes and said, "What a small life. Wouldn't you like a larger one?"*

*I will send what I can. If I stop sending, tell my wife—*

The letter stopped there. No ink failure, no torn edge. Simply a line left unfinished, the way a sentence is left unfinished when the hand that writes it remembers, suddenly, that sentences can be evidence.

Sera sat with it for a long time. Then she rose, and rolled the charts, and went to find Gideon, because the map was finished, and the map said what maps always say in the end: *here is the way, and here is the cost, and you do not have forever.*

"We go at the new moon," she told him. "Through the galleries. And Gideon—" she held up Pell's unfinished sentence, "—we go *fast*. He knows we're coming. He's setting the table."

Gideon looked at the letter, and then north, toward the capital, toward the tower where a hollowed king sat smiling in a window with roots growing through his ribs, and his face went still the way the forest goes still.

"Then we'd best not keep the gardener waiting," he said.
