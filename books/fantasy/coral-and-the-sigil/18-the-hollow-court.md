## 18. The Hollow Court

The tunnel ended in a wall of stone that was not a wall.

Gideon felt it before he saw it—the way he felt everything now, after Odessa's lessons and the road under the mountain: a pressure against his skin like the moment before a storm breaks, a hum in the teeth, a taste of copper at the back of the throat. Ward-work. Old ward-work, older than the kingdom, older than the roads Sera had drawn into being with ink and stubbornness. It lay across the tunnel's mouth like a spiderweb of light, and it was singing.

*It knows us,* said Bramble beside him, her breath warm and hay-sour against his ear. The mule had not left his shoulder since the ford, since Odessa died holding the crossing with her hands full of fire and her mouth full of curses. *The stone remembers who built it. It's asking if we're theirs.*

"Tell it we're nobody," Gideon murmured.

*You've never been nobody,* Bramble said. *That was always your problem.*

Behind them, forty-one souls waited in the dark—outlaws and deserters and ward-keepers' orphans, the Unlisted, packed shoulder to shoulder in a tunnel that had no business existing beneath a mountain that had no business hiding it. Sera stood at the front with her charts rolled tight in one fist, her face lit grey by the seam of light bleeding through the false wall. Pell hung back among the rearguard, and Gideon did not look at him. He had made his peace—or something near it—with the boy who had sold their route to Mavros for the sake of two sisters held hostage in this very city. Peace, but not forgiveness. Forgiveness would have to be earned in what came next.

Sera pressed her palm flat to the stone. "Odessa's notes said the old roads were keyed to names," she said quietly. "Not passwords. Names. The ward-keepers wrote themselves into the foundations."

"Then we have a problem," Gideon said. "None of us were alive when they poured these stones."

"No." Her eyes came up to his, and there was something in them he had learned to dread on this long road: hope. "But you speak. And the stone is listening."

He understood then what she was asking, and every instinct he had honed across thirty years of careful invisibility rose up screaming. *Never be noticed.* His mother's rule before it was his, back when his mother still had a face he could remember. Speak to the world and the world speaks back, and some things answer too loudly.

But Odessa was ash on a riverbank. Thornfell was smoke. The wolves who had given him their dying memory were bones somewhere behind him in the dark, and the king sat hollow in a palace above their heads, wearing a sigil like a leash.

Gideon laid his hand beside Sera's and opened the door he had kept shut all his life.

*We come in need,* he said—not aloud, not in words, but in the way of beasts and stones and all the listening things of the world. *We come to unbind what was bound wrongly.*

The web of light shivered. The hum climbed through his arm, his chest, his skull—and for one vertiginous instant Gideon felt the whole mountain around him, felt the weight of the palace above and the city beyond and the thousand small lives going about their morning unaware that anything beneath their feet was awake. Then the stone sighed, the way a guard sighs and steps aside, and the wall became a door, and the door became stairs, and light came pouring down them like water finding its level.

They climbed toward it in silence.

* * *

The throne room of King Aldric had been described to Gideon a hundred times by a hundred smugglers, always with the same mixture of contempt and awe: a hundred feet of white marble, banners of nine provinces hanging from gilded beams, windows of colored glass that turned the morning into a garden of jeweled light. He had imagined it as smugglers imagine palaces—a place to rob, never a place to be seen.

What he surfaced into, blinking, was none of that.

The banners were gone. In their place hung tapestries of a single repeating design: a crowned figure seated beneath a tree whose roots spread down and down and down, into darkness, into stone, into—Gideon's stomach turned as he recognized it—the shapes of ward-stones, drained and hollow, rendered in thread with loving precision. The colored glass remained, but the figures in it had been replaced: where saints and kings had stood, there were now only crowns. Dozens of them. Crowns floating in amber light, crown upon crown upon crown, and beneath each one, small and dark, a kneeling shape.

And the court.

The court was there. Lords and ladies in silk, guards in the king's blue and silver, clerks with ledgers, petitioners with their hats in their hands—all of it exactly as an audience morning should look, except that no one moved. No one breathed, so far as Gideon could tell. They stood in attitudes of attention, faces turned toward the throne, and their eyes were the eyes he had seen in the hollowed stag at Ward-Stone Hollow: polished, patient, empty as wells.

"They're not dead," Sera whispered at his shoulder. Her knuckles were white around her chart case. "Look—the lords from the letters. Lord Cassel. Lady Imre. They're *here*, they're—" She stopped, because saying it made it worse. *They're furniture,* she didn't say. *He keeps them the way a man keeps a garden.*

At the far end of the hall, beneath a canopy of black silk, the throne of Thornfell stood empty.

Beside it, on a plain chair of pale wood—as though the throne itself were merely decoration, a relic he tolerated—sat a thin man in grey robes, reading a ledger.

Mavros did not look up. "Fourteen minutes late," he said, in the mild tone of a steward noting a delivery. "The tunnel adds eleven. I'd estimated the mountain road myself, years ago, but I never found the entrance. You must thank Voss for me. Her family always drew what wasn't there yet. It's a kind of prophecy, cartography. Most people don't realize."

Gideon's hand found his knife out of pure habit, and the absurdity of it almost made him laugh—forty-one armed souls and a company of beasts pouring out of a hole in the floor, and the man at the end of the hall did not even mark his place in his ledger.

"You knew," Sera said. Her voice shook, but she planted herself like a surveyor driving a stake. "You let us come."

"I *invited* you," Mavros said. He closed the ledger at last and looked up, and Gideon got his first true sight of the enemy's face, and hated how ordinary it was. A long, thoughtful face. Tired eyes. The eyes of a scholar who has been reading too long by bad light. "Though invitation implies you might have refused, and we both know better. The wards are dying, Okonkwo. Three cities silent, a fourth failing as we speak. Did you feel it in the tunnel? The realm is a body losing blood. I needed you here before the last of it went out, because when it goes out, everything changes, and I wanted—" he smiled, thin and genuine, which was somehow the worst thing yet— "*witnesses.*"

"The king," Gideon said.

"Yes," Mavros said softly. "The king."

And Gideon looked past him, at last, to the figure he had been refusing to see.

Aldric sat in a chair beside his own empty throne, and he was not what Gideon had braced himself for. Not a corpse, not a puppet slumped on strings. He was *tended*. Clean-shaven, hair combed, dressed in a plain wool robe with a blanket over his knees. His hands rested open on his thighs, palms up, the way a man sits in a bathhouse. His eyes were open too, fixed on the middle distance, and they were not empty like the court's. They were worse. They were *full*—full of everything behind them, all the horror and knowledge pressed down under glass, drowning without being allowed to drown.

As Gideon watched, the king's lips moved. No sound came out. But Gideon had spent his life learning to read mouths across noisy markets and wind-scoured passes, and he read this one, and wished to every god he didn't believe in that he hadn't.

*Run,* Aldric mouthed, to no one, forever. *Run. Run. Run.*

* * *

"Gideon Okonkwo," Mavros said, rising, and the name in the air of that hollow room landed on Gideon like a brand. Behind him he heard the Unlisted shift, heard Bramble stamp, heard forty-one people learn his name in the same breath. "Beast-speaker. Smuggler of the Thornfell roads. Son of Adaeze, who kept the valley inn and never once told the ward-keepers' census about her son." He descended the dais steps slowly, hands folded, a lecturer approaching his favorite diagram. "Do you know how long I have looked for you? Not you specifically. Your *kind*. The gift runs in veins, Okonkwo—it pools, like water in low country. I mapped the pools decades ago. Your valley was on my map. I simply arrived too late; someone had taught you to hide."

"My mother taught me to hide," Gideon said.

"And she was right, for her time." Mavros stopped ten paces away, close enough that Gideon could smell him—ink, lamp oil, and underneath it something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit rotting in a locked room. "For a kingdom at peace, hiding was survival. But the peace is over. The wards are dying because I am eating them, yes—I won't insult you with denials; you've spoken to my stag, you've seen my stones. Ask yourself *why*, Okonkwo. Ask yourself what the wards were *for*."

"They kept the border," Sera said sharply. "They kept the old things out."

"The wards kept the old things *down*," Mavros corrected, with the gentle patience of a tutor. "There is a difference. Your kingdom was built on a lid, Voss, and your family drew maps of the pot. I have read the oldest of them—the ones your grandmother hid inside the binding of a psalter. I know what sleeps under the ward-lines. And I know the wards don't hold it forever. They were never meant to. They were meant to buy time while the realm grew strong enough to face it. Instead the realm grew fat and frightened and forgot. So I made a choice. I took the strength the wards were hoarding"—his gaze flicked, briefly, tenderly, to the vacant king—"*and I am spending it. Preparing. When the lid comes off—and it will come off, within a generation, wards or no wards—there will be something ready to meet what crawls out. Me. Or nothing.*"

"Lies taste like copper," Gideon said quietly. "I've fed enough hounds enough lies to know. You don't taste of copper, Mavros. That's the worst part. You believe it."

"I do," Mavros agreed pleasantly. "Now."

He gestured, and the hollowed court moved—every lord, every lady, every guard turning as one, parting down the center of the marble floor like a field of wheat before a scythe, opening a corridor from the broken floor where the Unlisted stood to the foot of the dais. An invitation made of other people's bodies.

"So here is my offer," Mavros said. "No tricks. Tricks are for people who fear each other, and I do not fear you, Okonkwo—I *honor* you. You walked into my hall through a door that has been sealed for six hundred years because you asked it politely in a language only you and the stones remember. Do you understand what you are? There were thousands like you once. Speakers. The ward-keepers bred them like hounds, used them, burned them out, and the gift thinned with every generation until men like you learned to pass as deaf. But you—you're a well, not a trickle. Odessa Vane saw it, didn't she? That's why she trained you. That's why she spent herself at the ford buying you time." Something moved behind the tired eyes—regret, perhaps, or its excellent imitation. "She was worth ten of my inquisitors. I mourn her."

"Say the offer," Gideon said, "or stop saying her name."

"The offer." Mavros spread his hands. "Stand beside me. Yours openly. No more masks, no more smuggler's silence, no more pretending to be less than you are. I will give you the hollows—all of them, every beast and man I have emptied—to *unmake*. Every one you free, freely given. You could spend years pulling threads loose one at a time, paying in memories, and save perhaps a hundred souls before the lid breaks. Or you could stand where I stand, and save them all at once, and keep saving them, when the real enemy comes." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and the terrible thing was that it was kind. "You have spent your whole life unseen, Okonkwo. I am offering you the one thing you have never had. To be *known*. To walk into a room and have it rise for you. Belonging—not stolen, not smuggled, not hidden in the underways of a free city among strays. Given. Honored. A place."

And there it was. The knife slid in past every defense Gideon had, because it was not a lie, or not entirely, or not in the way that mattered. He thought of the underways of Merrowgate, the lamplight and the shared bread, the first time Bramble had said his name and the world had not ended. He thought of how he had spent thirty years being a shadow, and how even now, leading the Unlisted, some cold cellar-room in his heart kept waiting for the moment they would see him clearly and turn away.

Mavros watched him think it. That was the horror of the man—he *watched*, patiently, the way he tended his hollowed garden, and he knew exactly which weed he was watering.

"You could say yes," Mavros murmured. "No one would ever blame you. Least of all me."

Above them, the amber crowns drifted in the colored glass. Below them, the hollowed court stood breathing in unison. On his chair, the king mouthed *run, run, run* at a world that no longer listened.

Gideon thought of his mother's face—which he no longer had, having spent it at the ford to unbind a hollowed wolf that would otherwise have torn Sera's throat out. He could not picture her. He could remember that he had loved her, the way you remember a sum you once did correctly. And he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that this was what Mavros was really offering. Not belonging. Permission. Permission to stop paying. To keep what was left of himself instead of spending it on strangers and strays and a hollow king who mouthed *run* at the walls.

Every memory he had spent had bought something. He would not stop the account now.

"No," Gideon said.

Mavros sighed, and it was almost fond. "I truly thought you might surprise me."

"You misread the offer," Gideon said, and took one step forward, into the corridor of hollowed bodies, and let the last door in himself swing wide. "You offered me a place at your side. I'm not here for a place, Mavros. I'm here for *him*." He lifted his chin toward the vacant king. "And for every soul you emptied to fill your garden. You wanted witnesses? Look around. You have forty-one of them, and every beast under this mountain, and one very good mapmaker. We've seen what you tend here."

He opened his gift all the way, for the first time in his life, in a room full of enemies—and reached, not for Mavros, but past him, into the hollowed court, into the wells and the polish and the patience, and began, gently, terribly, to *speak*.

The nearest hollowed lord blinked. Once. Twice. Somewhere deep behind those empty eyes, something that had been drowned a long time turned over in its sleep.

Mavros's tired face went very still.

"That," he said quietly, "was a mistake."

The lights in the amber glass went out, all at once, and in the dark the king's voice—rusty, cracked, real—said: "*Run.*"
