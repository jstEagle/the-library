## 19. Unbinding the King

The tunnel opened into silence.

Not the silence of empty places—Gideon had known those all his life, the hush of high passes and deep caves where nothing lived and nothing listened. This was a *made* silence, thick as wool packed into his ears, and it pressed down on the vaulted dark beneath the palace like a hand pressing down on a mouth.

He knew that silence. He had heard it once before, on the night the stars went out, rolling off Thornfell in a wave that killed every sound the mountain made. The sound of wards being eaten.

"Here," he breathed, though breath was barely needed; the air tasted of cold stone and old candle smoke and something sweeter underneath, like flowers rotting in honey. "We're under the throne room. I can feel them walking."

Above them, through forty feet of granite, he felt footsteps—not with his ears but with the gift, the old beast-sense stretched past anything Odessa had ever taught him until it brushed against human minds the way it had always brushed against wolves and stags. Dozens of minds up there, moving in patterns. Guards. Courtiers. And at the center of the pattern, two minds that did not move at all: one vast and slow and *hungry*, coiled around its meal like a serpent digesting, and one small and dim and flickering, guttering like a candle in a draft.

Aldric. What was left of Aldric.

Sera crouched beside him in the dark, her map-case open across her knees, the last of Odessa's chalk dust smeared on her fingers. She had drawn the palace from memory three times now, correcting against the pre-kingdom charts, and she traced the final line with a nail bitten to the quick. "The audience chamber sits directly above. There's an old venting shaft here—" her finger tapped, tapped again, "—wide enough for a man if he's willing to be narrow about it. Pell?"

Pell stood apart from them, as he had stood apart since the ford, since the moment Gideon had put his hands on the man's shoulders and said *I know why you did it* instead of cutting his throat. He looked worse than he had at the ford. He had not slept. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady anyway.

"The guards change at the ninth bell," Pell said. "Mavros holds open court every night now. He likes them to see him with the king. It's how he keeps them loyal—makes the hollow thing on the throne look like a blessing instead of a wound." He swallowed. "There are twelve in the chamber. Four more at the doors. And Mavros doesn't sleep, so don't plan around him sleeping."

"And when we come up through the floor," Sera said, "twelve armed men will be very interested in us."

"Not all of them." Pell's voice cracked and steadied. "The garrison captain, Dorn—he has a brother in Thornfell. A village that burned. He's been asking questions the others don't ask. If someone shows him proof—if someone stands up first—the rest will hesitate. They're not bound, most of them. They're just afraid." He laughed, a dry broken sound. "Fear is easier to turn than loyalty. That's what Mavros never understood about me either. He held my family over my head, but he never bought me. He only frightened me. There's a difference, and tonight I'm going to spend it."

Gideon looked at him for a long moment. In the faint grey seep of light from the shaft above, Pell's face was gaunt and ordinary and brave in the way that ordinary men were brave, which was to say badly, tremblingly, and anyway.

"You could still run," Gideon said. "Take your family's names and go north. No one would follow."

"They'd follow." Pell shook his head. "And besides." He glanced at Bramble, who stood in the shadows behind them with the last of the unbound beasts—a fox with one white ear, a raven that had learned to say *Gideon* in a voice like tearing silk. "Besides, the mule called me a coward this morning. In front of everyone. I have a reputation to repair."

"I called you worse than a coward," Bramble said. "I called you a *donkey*. But I stand by both."

Gideon almost smiled. Almost. Then he closed his eyes and reached upward with the gift one more time, and the smile died where it was born.

Because now, this close, he could feel the sigil.

It was not a mind. It was older than minds, hungrier than hunger, a knot of will tied into the king's chest like a fist tied into a net, and it was *feeding*—he could feel the slow pull of it, drawing strength out of Aldric the way the drained ward-stones had been drawn dry, the way the border wards had died, the way three cities had gone silent one after another after another. Every scrap of power the kingdom had ever woven into its protections, funneling down through stone and oath and crown into one man's chest, into one parasite's keeping.

It felt him feeling it.

The silence shivered. Far above, something turned over in its sleep—or in its waking—and Gideon felt attention settle on him through forty feet of rock, vast and patient and amused, the way a spider is amused by the fly that walks willingly into the web.

*Ah,* said the sigil-binder's mind, without words, without sound, in the language of pressure and appetite that Gideon had learned in Ward-Stone Hollow. *The little speaker. The one who talks to beasts. Come up, then. I have wanted to meet you. I have wanted you for a long time.*

Gideon wrenched his senses back and found himself on one knee, blood running warm from his nose, Sera gripping his arm.

"It knows we're here," he said. "It always knew. It's been waiting."

"Then we stop waiting too." Sera's voice was low and hard, the voice of a woman who had forged maps of roads that weren't there and then walked one of them into being. She pressed the chalk into his hand. "Odessa's marks. You remember the sequence?"

He remembered everything. That was the horror of what he was about to do—he remembered everything, and he was going to feed it away piece by piece until there wasn't enough of him left to remember why any of it had mattered.

"Yes," he said.

"Then go up," Sera Voss said, "and I'll hold the door."

* * *

The venting shaft was exactly as narrow as Sera had promised, which was to say it flayed a layer of skin from Gideon's shoulders and ribs and left him breathing like a bellows by the time he hauled himself over the lip into the dark behind the throne room's eastern pillar.

Light everywhere. A hundred candles, white and steady, burning without flicker because the air itself was held still by will. The audience chamber of King Aldric rose around him in pillars of black marble veined with silver, and the court stood assembled in ranks—lords and ladies and officers in their finery, faces lit gold from below, all of them staring toward the throne with expressions Gideon recognized from funerals.

On the dais sat the king.

Gideon had expected a corpse wearing a crown. What he saw was worse. Aldric sat upright, dignified, hands resting on the arms of the throne like a portrait of kingship—and his eyes were open, and they were *empty*, and yet behind the emptiness something flickered, faint and frantic, like a man trapped under ice watching the world through six inches of frozen water. His chest rose and fell. His lips were slightly parted. And beneath the collar of his shirt, glowing through the linen like a coal wrapped in gauze, burned the sigil.

Beside the throne, standing with one hand resting affectionately on the king's shoulder, stood Mavros.

The court sorcerer was smaller than Gideon had imagined—slight, silver-haired, with the mild face of a beloved tutor. He wore no armor and carried no weapon. He looked like a gardener. He looked like a grandfather. He looked at the pillar behind which Gideon crouched, and smiled, and spoke in a voice pitched to carry gently through the whole silent hall.

"There you are," Mavros said. "Come out, Gideon Okonkwo. You've traveled so far. Let's not be rude."

The court turned as one. Twelve guards leveled spears. And Gideon Okonkwo, who had spent thirty years perfecting the art of being unnoticed, stepped out from behind the pillar into full view of the entire hollow court, and stopped trying to be invisible at all.

Below, muffled through stone, the first screams began. Sera and the Unlisted had reached the guard posts.

"You came through my mountain road," Mavros said, tilting his head. "Through *her* road—the cartographer's forgery. Delightful. Do you know, I've been aware of that tunnel for two hundred years? I let it exist the way a man leaves a window unlatched. One always wants the mice to believe they have a way in." He sighed, fond and terrible. "But none of that matters now. Look at you. All this way, all this noise, all these little friends dying below for the privilege—and for what? To speak to him?" He patted Aldric's shoulder. "He can't hear you. I ate his hearing years ago, along with most of the rest. But you—you I can hear. That gift of yours. Beasts whisper to you, don't they? They told me about you long before you knew yourself. The wolves sang of a boy who spoke their tongue. I have wanted that tongue in my collection for so very long."

"Then come take it," Gideon said.

His voice sounded strange to him. Steady. He had thought he would be afraid, and he was—but the fear had gone quiet, banked down to coals, and what burned above it was something cleaner. He walked forward across the marble, alone, while somewhere below him steel rang against steel and Sera Voss shouted orders in a voice cracked raw, and the guards at the dais hesitated, and hesitated again, because through the floor came the sound of the Unlisted holding the doors and holding them *well*, and because fear, as Pell had said, turns easier than loyalty.

One guard moved first. A broad man with a grey streak in his beard—Dorn, it must be Dorn—he drove his spear butt down on the marble with a crack like a breaking bone and roared, "THORNFELL BURNED! I have a brother's ash and no grave! Who gave that order? WHO?" And the hesitation spread like fire through dry grass, and four guards lowered their spears, and then seven, and the court began to murmur, and Mavros's smile thinned by a single degree.

"You mistake the shape of tonight," Mavros said softly. "You think you've brought an army. Child, I have eaten armies. I have eaten *wards*—the great web your grandmothers' grandmothers spun over this whole foolish kingdom. Do you know what I am? I am the reason the stars went out. I drank them. Every ward in this realm was anchored to the heavens, and I pulled, and they came loose like rotten teeth." He stepped forward, off the dais, and the candles dimmed as his shadow lengthened impossibly. "And you propose to fight me with a parlour trick and a handful of beggars."

"No," Gideon said. "I propose to do what I've done my whole life. Talk to something that isn't human, and find out what it wants."

He closed his eyes. He opened the gift all the way.

The world went wide. The hall vanished and he stood in the space behind things, the place where Odessa had trained him, the country of threads—and there it was, the sigil, vast beyond reckoning, a knot of black will driven through the king's heart and anchored into the crown, into the stones, into the dead stars themselves. Threads ran from it in every direction, into the guards' oaths, into the walls' foundations, into the hollowed beasts pacing the palace gardens, into everything the kingdom had ever promised or protected. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and it was the ugliest, because beauty built on feeding was only appetite wearing a mask.

*You cannot untie me,* the sigil said, and its voice filled the thread-country like a tide filling a cave. *I am older than your kingdom. I was old when your gods were young. Kings fed me willingly, once, in the years before the wards—do you think protection is free? Everything your realm has ever been, it has been on my sufferance.*

"That's not true," Gideon said aloud, in the hall, in the thread-country, in both places at once. "The wards were given. Freely. By people who chose it. That's the difference between you and them, and it's the only difference, and it's enough."

*Words.* Amusement rolled through the dark. *You'll need more than words.*

"I know." Gideon knelt, there on the marble before the throne, before the vacant king, and took Aldric's cold hand in both of his own. "I brought more than words."

He reached into himself, into the storehouse of everything he was, and he began to pay.

The first memory he spent was small, the way you spend small coins first: the smell of rain on the smuggler roads, the particular green smell of wet pine on the Thornfell passes. He gave it to the sigil deliberately, wrapped in the shape of a thread, and the knot *took* it, greedy, gulping—and in the instant of swallowing, its grip loosened. Just a hair. Just enough.

"Oh," Mavros said, and his mild face changed. "Oh, you clever, *terrible* boy. You're paying it. You're buying your king back with *yourself*."

The second memory was larger. His mother's voice, singing—no. Not that one. He flinched from it, saved it, and spent instead the taste of the first honest meal he'd ever eaten in Merrowgate's underways, bread and salt shared with strangers who became the Unlisted. The sigil swallowed. Loosened.

The third. The fourth. The name of the first wolf who ever answered him. The layout of every smuggler road in the western ranges. The color of the sky over the free cities at dawn. Each one a coin, each one a piece of Gideon Okonkwo handed over to the hungry dark, and with each one the sigil's knot slackened and the threads running out of it grew thin, and Aldric's hand twitched in his grip, and the flicker behind the king's eyes burned a little brighter, a little more frantic, a drowning man seeing surface.

*Stop,* the sigil said, and for the first time there was something other than amusement in it. *Stop. You will unmake yourself. There will be nothing left.*

"Then there'll be nothing left," Gideon gasped. Blood ran freely from his nose now, from his ears, warm on his collar. His knees screamed against the marble. Somewhere far away Sera was shouting his name. "That's the difference between us too. You take. I give. Take *this*—"

He spent the memory of his father's face.

He spent the memory of learning to whistle the hawk down from the wind.

He spent the memory of Bramble's real name, the long complicated mule-name that meant *stubborn friend* in the old speech, and felt it go, and wept without knowing what he wept for, because the grief remained even after the memory that caused it, grief being the one coin the sigil couldn't swallow.

*Enough—ENOUGH—*

The knot was fraying. Threads snapping like harp strings. The sigil thrashed, and the whole hall shuddered, candles guttering at last, and Mavros screamed—a real scream, shrill, the gardener's mask torn away—and raised his hand, and shadow poured off him like smoke off a pyre—

—and the doors burst open, and Sera Voss came through them with half the Unlisted at her back and a stolen guard's shield on her arm, and behind her, stepping out of the ranks of the king's own soldiers, Pell threw back his hood and shouted in a voice that broke on every word: "IT'S TRUE! ALL OF IT—I WAS HIS EAR AND IT'S TRUE, THE KING IS ALIVE UNDER THERE—HOLD THE SORCERER!" And Dorn's spear came down, and the garrison turned, and the court's fear curdled into fury, and they surged toward Mavros like a tide toward a sandcastle.

Gideon didn't see the end of it. He was deep in the dark, both hands fisted in the sigil's last great thread, spending the final coins of himself—the name of the valley where he was born, the sound of his own laughter, the memory of the night the stars went out and a wolf died in his arms—giving, giving, giving, until the knot was bare thread and bare thread pulls free.

"Now," he whispered—to Aldric, to the flickering man under the ice. "Now. Push."

And Aldric—the hollow king, the garden Mavros tended, the battery and the prisoner—pushed.

The sigil tore loose with a sound like the sky cracking, a shriek of released power that blew every candle out and every courtier to their knees. Gideon felt it rip through him as it went, taking the last of what he'd offered and more, taking pieces he hadn't chosen to give, and then it was gone—unraveling, screaming, coming apart into nothing in the air above the throne like smoke unwriting itself.

Silence. Real silence this time, clean and empty.

Then, through the high windows of the audience chamber, light.

Faint. Silver. Flickering—once, twice, three times, like eyes opening after a long sleep—and then steady. Stars. Stars pouring back into the black sky over the capital, star by star by star, the old anchors refinding their moorings, the dead web of the wards shivering awake across the whole breadth of the kingdom. Gideon heard it happen, heard the wards sing, heard beasts howl in the hills outside the city in voices that were no longer hollow, and he lay on the cold marble with blood on his face and holes in his memory where his life used to be, and he laughed.

"Gideon." Sera's face above him, tear-streaked, filthy, alive. "Gideon, stay with me. What did it cost? What's gone?"

He searched. Carefully, the way you search a house after a flood.

A lot was gone. Most of his childhood. His mother's face—spent at the ford, and her voice gone now too. The roads, half of them. His own birthday. But some things remained, stubborn as burrs: Sera's face. Bramble's ears, crooked from an old injury. The feeling—wordless, sourceless, but *true*—that he had once been loved, and had loved, and that love had made him who he was even if he could no longer say how.

"Enough," he rasped. "It left enough."

On the throne, King Aldric drew a breath that was entirely his own, and his eyes—his own eyes, terrified and grieving and awake—filled with tears as he looked out at the wreckage of his court, at the guards who had obeyed his hollow orders, at the sorcerer being dragged down beneath twenty pairs of hands, screaming a name no one answered to anymore.

"What," the king whispered, in a voice rusted from disuse, "have I *done*?"

Gideon closed his eyes. That question, he thought, would take longer than one night to answer—and the court, pale and assembled and beginning to understand what it had applauded for three years, was already turning to demand it.
