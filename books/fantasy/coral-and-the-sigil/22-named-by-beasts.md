## 22. Named by Beasts

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

Gideon knew this because Bramble told him so, in the flat, unimpressed tone of a mule who had lived through three wars and considered celestial events a personal inconvenience. *The sky's fixed,* she said, as they crested the last ridge above the Ashwater valley. *You can stop looking at your feet now.*

He hadn't realized he'd been looking at his feet. He hadn't realized how long he'd been walking with his head bowed, as though the darkness overhead were a debt he still owed. But there they were—the stars, all of them, spilled across the black like salt from a torn sack, and among them the constellations the old ward-keepers had named: the Antlered One, the Ferryman's Lantern, the Long Wolf pacing the horizon with her muzzle lifted toward the north.

North. That was where he was going. He wasn't entirely sure why, except that the road went that way, and the beasts wanted it, and for the first time in his life he was not traveling alone and not traveling hidden, and both of those facts still sat strangely in his chest, like coins of a currency he hadn't learned to spend.

*You're doing it again,* Bramble said. *The brooding. You have a face for it. Very dramatic. The wolves are taking bets.*

"I'm not brooding."

*You're sitting motionless on a hilltop at midnight staring at the sky. In my experience that is either brooding or prayer, and you don't pray.*

He laughed—a rough, unused sound, like a door opening after a long winter—and swung up into the saddle. Below them, the column moved through the moonless dark without torches, because they didn't need them. Forty-odd riders and walkers, most of them Unlisted, some of them something older: the stag with the healed hollow where his eyes had once been eaten away, walking now with his head high; the wolves of Thornfell, eleven of them, moving along the flanks like outriders who had never been sworn to any banner; a bear called Sorrel who had been mute for two years and had begun, in the last week, to hum.

They were going north because the north was where the wards had first fallen, and the wards would need rebuilding, and the charter Aldric had signed—ink still fresh, seal still warm—said the Unlisted would be the ones to do it. Answerable to no crown. Funded by no treasury that could be starved or seized. Bound only to the land itself, and to each other, which Gideon privately thought was the more binding of the two.

Behind him, Sera Voss rode with her maps rolled tight in oilcloth, already muttering about survey lines and the proper way to chart a road that existed in one season and not in others. She'd kissed him once, at the ford where Odessa died, and then said, "Don't make it strange," and they hadn't spoken of it since, and somehow that was its own kind of promise. She was three lengths back and to the left, always three lengths back and to the left, because she said a cartographer's place was slightly outside the picture, where you could see its edges.

The valley opened below them, silvered with returned starlight, and Gideon reined in and let the column pass him, because there was something he wanted to hear.

* * *

The stag reached him first.

Gideon had never learned his name—that memory had gone into the unbinding at the second stone, spent like a coin to pull a thread of Mavros's sigil out of the beast's marrow. He remembered the shape of knowing it and not the knowing itself, the way you remember a room you slept in as a child: the window here, the door there, and nothing of what happened inside. It was an odd grief, mourning a name instead of a person. He'd stopped trying to explain it to anyone except Sera, who had drawn him a map of his own missing memories once, blank spaces marked in careful ink, and said, "There. Now they're somewhere."

The stag stopped in front of him and breathed on his hand, warm air smelling of pine and crushed grass, and then he spoke—not in the half-formed impressions beasts used with strangers, but plainly, deliberately, the way one man speaks to another across a table.

*Gideon Okonkwo.*

His own name, in a voice like wind through antlers. Not *smuggler.* Not *the speaker.* Not *you, the one who took my pain out and left a hole shaped like himself.* His name, whole, offered freely, the way you offer bread.

"You know me," Gideon said, and heard how young his voice sounded.

*I have always known you,* the stag said. *What was taken from me was taken from the part that could say it. The knowing stayed. It waited under the silence like water under ice.* He turned his ruined-healed eyes toward the north. *We will need names, where we're going. Yours. Ours. The stones must be woken by someone they trust, and trust begins with naming.*

"Then we'll start tomorrow," Gideon said. "Tonight I just want to ride."

*That,* said the stag, with something that might have been amusement, *is the first sensible thing a two-leg has said in this valley in a hundred years.*

The wolves came next. They did not speak as the stag spoke—they were wolves, and their speech was weather and scent and posture—but when the pack flowed past him on the trail, the great gray bitch who had carried the dying messenger down off Thornfell paused, pressed her shoulder against his stirrup, and breathed his name into the space between them. Not the word. The weight of it. *Yours. Known. Kept.* And behind her, one by one, each wolf touched him the same way as they passed, until he was laughing again, helplessly, wiping his face with the heel of his hand and pretending it was the cold wind.

Bramble watched all of this with the expression of a mule who found sentiment fattening but not nourishing.

*Don't let it go to your head,* she said. *I've known your name for years. Where's my ceremony?*

"You bit me the first time we met."

*And I apologized. Once. Which for a mule is practically a coronation.*

* * *

They made camp at dawn in the ruins of a ward-stone shrine, one of the small ones, the roadside kind that travelers had fed with offerings for centuries before anyone thought to write anything down. The stone was cold and gray, drained like all the others, but when Gideon laid his palm against it he felt something faint beneath the deadness—a pulse, slow as a sleeping heart. Not gone. Starved. Waiting.

Odessa would have known what to feed it. The thought arrived without warning and sat down heavily beside him, the way grief does when you stop running long enough to let it catch up. Odessa Vane, half-mad and magnificent, who had taught him unbinding with a switch in her hand and curses in her mouth, who had died holding a river crossing so that strangers could live, whose last words to him had been *don't waste it on pretty memories, boy, spend the ugly ones, nobody misses those* —and he had disobeyed her, in the end, spending his mother's face and his father's laugh and the smell of the valley where he'd learned to listen, because the ugly ones were all he had left and he'd been a coward about it, and he was glad, every day, that he'd been a coward about it.

Sera found him there, of course. She always knew where he was standing, which he suspected was less magic than attention—the rarest kind, the kind almost nobody practiced.

"The stone's alive," she said. It wasn't a question. She crouched, put her ear against the granite like a physician listening to a chest, and frowned. "Faint. But the rhythm's wrong for a ward-stone. It's—" She looked up. "It's learning the beat from you. From your hand. Like a heart finding a drum to follow."

Gideon pulled his hand back and looked at it. It was just a hand. Scarred, rope-burned, the knuckles too big, a smuggler's hand, a listener's hand. For thirty years he had kept it closed around his gift like a coal in a fist, feeding it nothing, letting it burn itself small so no one would see the light. Then one year of war had forced it open, and everything it touched had changed, and everything it cost had been paid in the only coin he couldn't mint more of.

But the fist was open now. There was no closing it again. And standing in the starlight with a waking stone under his palm and forty people and beasts depending on him, he found—to his genuine surprise—that he didn't want to.

"Sera," he said. "When we rebuild the first ward, I want to do it different. Not the old way. Not blood-price, not memory-tithe, none of what Odessa knew. Something new. Something that doesn't eat the keeper."

She studied him for a long moment, and he watched her mind work the way he'd watched it work a thousand times—turning the problem over, finding its edges, refusing to accept the edges as final. "Wards are contracts," she said slowly. "Old ones. Written in payment because the people who wrote them believed everything true costs something." A pause. Her mouth did the thing it did when she was about to say something dangerous. "But I've forged documents that became real roads, Gideon. I've seen words written in good faith hold better than words written in blood. Maybe the price was never the point. Maybe the *keeping* was."

"Maybe," Gideon said, "we find out."

Above the shrine, the first light came up gray and then gold, and the stars faded one by one, politely, like guests who know the hour. Gideon watched the Long Wolf go last, her constellation dissolving into morning, and felt no dread at all in the losing of them. They'd be back tonight. They'd be back every night, for the rest of his life, and for the first time he could remember, the rest of his life felt like a country rather than a hiding place.

* * *

They rode north for six days before the mountains closed around them, and on the sixth night, in a camp ringed by pines with snow coming down soft as ash, the wolves sang his name.

Not to him. About him. To the dark, to the stones, to whatever old things still listened in the high places—the way packs had once sung the names of ward-keepers, back when the borders were young, back when the covenant between two-legs and beasts was a living thing and not a ruin. Gideon lay awake in his bedroll and listened to his own name rise and fall in voices that had once been hollow, that he had personally pulled back from the edge of emptiness one thread at a time, paying for each rescue with a piece of himself, and he understood suddenly that the pieces hadn't vanished. They'd gone into the beasts. Into the stones. Into the space between his name and theirs, which was no longer a distance at all.

Some memories were gone. His mother's face. The taste of the plum wine he'd stolen the night he first ran away. The sound of his own childhood laughter, which he mourned more than he'd expected. But new ones were being written over the blanks, in ink he hadn't chosen and couldn't have predicted: Bramble's dry commentary at every funeral and every festival alike. Sera's maps spread on the ground, her finger tracing roads that didn't exist yet with the certainty of someone inventing the world on purpose. Pell, forgiven and useful, sending letters south in a code of his own devising, each one signed with a small drawing of a fox. The stag teaching the younger wolves the old paths through the high passes. Sorrel the bear humming, tunelessly, endlessly, a song nobody recognized and everybody loved.

Aldric had offered him a wardenship, a title, a place at court, and Gideon had stood in the rebuilt hall with the whole realm watching and said no, and negotiated instead a charter for people who would never be welcome anywhere, and ridden out the same evening, and had not regretted it for a single hour. Justice, he'd learned, wasn't a verdict handed down from a throne. It was a road you walked with whoever was willing to walk it with you, and you repaired it as you went, and you named it honestly, and you left it better for the ones behind you.

The singing faded. Snow kept falling. Somewhere north, past the passes, the first great ward-stone waited under its shroud of silence, hungry and patient, waiting to be woken by someone it trusted.

Gideon closed his eyes. Around him, breathing in the dark, were wolves and mules and bears and outlaws and one impossible cartographer, all of them knowing his name, all of them keeping it.

*Bramble,* he said silently, the way he'd said a thousand things to her over the years. *Are you awake?*

*Unfortunately.*

"When we wake the northern stone," he said, "what should we call it?"

A long pause. Snow ticked softly against the canvas. Then, in a tone of profound suffering:

*Something dignified. Unlike yourself.*

He smiled into the dark, and slept, and dreamed of roads.
