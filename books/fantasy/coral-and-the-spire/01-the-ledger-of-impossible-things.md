## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Cyrus Eastwick finished the monsoon entry.

She dipped her pen, checked the figure twice, and wrote it in the small, exact hand that six years of practice had worn into her fingers: *One (1) bottled monsoon, seasonal, intact. Sold to the Province of Ashkarra, drought-bound, price as negotiated. Delivered by guild courier, third bell.* Beneath it she recorded the weight—four pounds, three ounces, heavier than most weather, because this one had been gathered in anger—and the seal number of the jar, and the name of the clerk who had corked it, and the name of the courier who had carried it, and the name of the farmer in Ashkarra who had wept when the first rain in eleven years fell out of a jar the size of a wine bottle and went on falling for three days.

That last name was not required. Cyrus wrote it anyway.

The Meridian Guild's ledgers were famous, in the way that things are famous among the dozen people permitted to know they exist. Every impossible thing the guild bought, sold, pawned, or lent passed through these pages, and every page passed through Cyrus's hands. She was the reconciler. When a bottled storm's seal number failed to match its manifest, it was Cyrus who found the discrepancy—usually a junior clerk who had swapped two jars, once a courier who had drunk a thunderhead on a dare and spent a week coughing lightning. When a debt of borrowed years came due and the debtor claimed he'd already paid, it was Cyrus who turned back through the volumes and found the date, the witness, the wax, and the truth.

She liked the work. That was the dangerous thing about it, though she did not yet know how dangerous. She liked that numbers did not lie to flatter you, and that ink dried into permanence, and that somewhere in the columns a grieving widow's sorrow had been given a value and a place and could be found again, always, exactly where she'd left it.

The widow's entry was three lines below the monsoon. Cyrus had taken it that morning, and it still sat wrong in her chest, the way certain entries did.

*One (1) pledge of memory, three (3) years, pawned by Marta Vell of Cooper's Row. Years: the marriage of Edda Vell, deceased. Terms: redeemable at any time within the borrower's natural life. Interest: none, by guildmaster's discretion.*

The woman had come in at midmorning with rain in her collar and a shawl the color of old dishwater, and she had not haggled. That was the first bad sign. People who haggled still believed they would want the thing back. Marta Vell had set three years of her marriage on the counter—the wedding, the winter after it, the last spring of her wife's life—and asked only how much the guild would give, and when the appraiser named a figure she had said *yes* in a voice like a door closing, and walked out into the street lighter by three years and by everything those years had weighed.

Cyrus had watched from her alcove behind the counting floor, invisible in the way she had made herself an art. Not the hedge-witch vanishing her mother's mother had taught her—she never used that here, never—but the ordinary kind. The kind made of stillness and plain clothes and a face that gave nothing back. She had stood at her high desk with her pen moving and no one had looked at her, and Marta Vell had passed within an arm's reach without turning her head, and Cyrus had felt the old twin sensations that were as close as she came to feeling watched: pride, and loneliness.

Pride, because the ledger would hold this honestly. Somewhere in the vault below, in a stoppered vial with a ribbon of the widow's own hair tied round its neck, those three years would wait, intact, redeemable. The guild was a den of thieves and miracle-mongers, but its books were clean, because Cyrus kept them so. In a city where the powerful traded in weather and time and other people's pasts, she had built herself one small country of accuracy, and she was its unseen queen, and no one knew her name.

Loneliness, because no one knew her name.

She blew out the candle stub, and the counting house went dark except for the lamp over the vault door, which was never allowed to go out, and the green-gray light of the harbor coming through the high windows like something patient at the glass.

* * *

She should have gone home. She had a room above a chandler's on Lantern Street, narrow and clean and hers, with a lock she had improved herself and a window that looked at nothing. She should have gone home and slept, because tomorrow the quarterly reconciliation began, and the reconciliation was three days of her pen moving without stopping while clerks sweated and the guildmaster's steward hovered.

Instead she found herself standing in the counting house with her cloak half-fastened, looking at the door to the master's stair.

The private collection was kept on the level below the vaults, behind a door that opened to Vess's ring and to no other key in the building. Cyrus had catalogued it exactly once, six years ago, her first month, when the old reconciler before her had died—quietly, in his sleep, of nothing anyone could name—and she had been handed his duties and his alcove and his silence in the same week. The collection inventory had taken her four nights. She remembered a jar of last words. A feather that fell upward. A mirror that showed the room as it would be if no one had ever loved in it. She remembered the founding shelf, and the book on it, and she remembered that she had catalogued it without opening it, because the clasps were sealed with wax stamped by the first guildmaster a hundred years ago, and because opening it had not been her work.

Her work was numbers. Numbers did not ask her to look at things.

She stood in the dark counting house and listened to her own heart do something unledgered, and then she took the master's stair down, because the wax on the founding book's clasps, she had realized somewhere between the third stair and the seventh, had been broken. Fresh-broken. She had seen it tonight, passing the shelf with the lamp, the way you see a thing only after six years of knowing where everything else in a room belongs. The seal of Aldous Meridian himself, cracked clean through, and no entry in any inventory to say who had done it.

The guildmaster's ring would not open the door for her. But the door, she knew—because she had catalogued it, because she catalogued everything—had a flaw. A hairline gap at the hinge-side, where a century of settling had pulled the frame a quarter-inch out of true. The old reconciler had known it. He had shown her once, wordlessly, with a thin blade of whalebone, and she had understood that this too was part of the work: the guild's secrets had a maintenance schedule, and someone had to keep them oiled.

The whalebone was in her sleeve, because it was always in her sleeve. It took her four breaths to have the door open.

* * *

The private collection smelled of cold stone and the sea, which it should not have, being four levels below the harbor line. Cyrus stood in the doorway and let her eyes adjust and did not touch the lamp by the door, because a lamp by a door was a lamp meant to be seen by, and she had spent six years learning never to be seen.

The founding shelf was where she had left it in the inventory, third from the floor, and the book was where she had left it, and the wax was broken the way she had remembered it broken—both clasps, cleanly, recently, the wax still sharp-edged enough that she could read the impression of the first guildmaster's sigil in the broken halves: a spire above a wave, and beneath them, in letters worn nearly smooth, the guild's oldest motto, the one no clerk ever spoke aloud because it had stopped being true and everyone preferred not to notice:

*All Debts Honored.*

Cyrus looked at the broken seal for a long time. Then she looked over her shoulder at the empty stair, and listened, and heard the building do its nighttime breathing—water in pipes, timber settling, far off the bell of the harbor watch—and she took the book down and opened it.

It was not a ledger. She understood that within the first page, and understanding it made her hands go cold, because she was the guild's reconciler and she knew every kind of book this building held, and this was none of them. The pages were vellum gone the color of old honey. The hand that filled them was the first guildmaster's—she knew it from the charter in the vault, from the signatures on the oldest contracts, a hand like iron nails driven in a row—and it did not record transactions. It recorded a conversation. Questions, and answers beneath them, in a different ink, an ink that even now, a century later, looked less like ink than like seawater that had learned to hold its shape.

The covenant. The founding of the guild. She had always been told it was a trade charter, a license, a piece of paper. It was not. It was a treaty, and the other signatory had not been any duke or magistracy of the city.

The answers were signed with a single character, repeated down a century of pages, a glyph like a hook, like a wave about to break.

Cyrus turned the pages faster. The conversation moved through the decades—the guild's obligations, the city's obligations, the terms of what was owed and to whom and when—and the answers grew shorter as the years went on, and colder, and then, near the end, they stopped being answers at all and became something else. Warnings. She could not read the drowned tongue, but she could read a ledger, and a ledger's grammar is universal: these were entries in arrears. These were debts come due and unpaid.

And then the last written page, and her own breath stopped in her throat.

A date. Circled twice, the circle pressed hard enough to score the vellum beneath. The month, the day, the year.

Tonight.

And beside it, in that iron-nail hand, in the first guildmaster's ink, in a book sealed for a hundred years behind wax that had been broken only recently, by someone, for someone, two words:

*The unseen scribe.*

The ink was wet.

Cyrus stood in the cold dark of the private collection with a century-old prophecy open in her hands and its ink transferring, faintly, darkly, onto the pad of her thumb, and somewhere above her—three levels up, in a counting house where she had just blown out her candle—a door closed, softly, with the particular care of someone who did not wish to be heard.

She had spent six years becoming invisible.

Someone had known she would.
