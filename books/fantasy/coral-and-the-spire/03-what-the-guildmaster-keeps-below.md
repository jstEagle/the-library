## 3. What the Guildmaster Keeps Below

The vaults of the Meridian Guild had no lock that Cyrus could find.

She had spent six years learning the building's arithmetic—the way its corridors added up to more than their floor plan allowed, how a stairwell could subtract a landing between one visit and the next. The guild traded in impossible things; it stood to reason its foundations would keep a few secrets of their own. But she had never found a door to the deep levels, never seen a key on Vess's ring that didn't correspond to any room above ground, never heard so much as an echo from below when she pressed her ear to the cellar floor at midnight.

Which meant, she reasoned now, standing in the counting room with the prophecy book's wet date still ghosting behind her eyes, that the way down was not a way down at all. It was a ledger entry.

Everything in this place was a ledger entry.

She went back to her desk and pulled the master index—the great chained volume that catalogued every item the guild had ever held, bought, sold, or destroyed. She did not look for *vault*. She looked for what would have to be true if the guildmaster kept something beneath the building: an asset too valuable to shelve, too dangerous to display, entered under a category no clerk was meant to question.

There were only three categories marked with a black asterisk instead of a number. Two were empty, their entries struck through in a hand older than the ink around them. The third read simply:

*COVENANT — SEE FOUNDER'S TERMS.*

And beside it, in the column where a location should have been, a single character she had never learned to read. It looked like water written by someone who had only ever seen it frozen.

Cyrus sat very still for a long moment, listening to the candle eat its wick.

Then she took out her own pen—a hedge-witch's pen, goose-quill, unremarkable—and wrote the strange character onto a scrap of foolscap. When she lifted it, the ink did not sit on the paper. It sank, the way a stone sinks in still water, and where it vanished the paper turned briefly translucent, like ice over a deep hole.

She understood then that she was not looking for a door. She was holding one.

* * *

The cellar stairs ended at a wall of shelving that Cyrus had dusted every quarter-day for six years. She pressed the scrap of foolscap against the mortar between two bricks, feeling faintly ridiculous, feeling entirely certain, and the wall exhaled.

Not opened—*exhaled*. A breath of cold salt air rolled over her, smelling of low tide and old iron, and the shelves swung inward on hinges she had never once noticed, revealing a stair that descended farther than the building had depth to allow. Lamplight burned along it at intervals, each flame burning without flicker, as if the air down there were too well-behaved to move.

Cyrus told herself she was being careful. She told herself she was gathering evidence, doing what any good clerk would do before presenting an irregularity to a superior. She told herself these things the way one tells oneself anything while walking down into the dark of a building that trades in bottled storms.

The stairs went down past the point where her counting said they should end. They went down past the point where the city's cellars ended, past the water table, past sense. And all the while the salt smell thickened, until it wasn't a smell anymore but a pressure, gentle and enormous, like the palm of a very large hand resting against her sternum.

At the bottom was a chamber that no surveyor had ever drawn.

It was round, and its walls were not stone but something darker and smoother, veined with lines of pale luminescence that pulsed in slow rhythm—slower than a heartbeat, slower than a tide, but in time with both, somehow, the way two clocks in the same house will drift into agreement. In the center of the chamber stood a lectern of black coral, and on the lectern lay a document.

Cyrus approached it the way one approaches a sleeping animal.

The document was long—longer than any contract she had ever balanced—and written in two hands. The first was human, formal, the looping script of the city's founding generation, browned with age. The second was not human at all. It looked less like writing than like the record of water's movement: swells and troughs, a script you had to read with your peripheral vision, meaning arriving in waves rather than lines.

A treaty. She knew it within three sentences, because she had spent six years reading treaties, and this one had the same architecture as all the rest—terms, considerations, durations, penalties—scaled up until the parties named were not merchants but *realms*. The City of Meridian, above. The Drowned Court, below. The harbor as the seam between them.

She read fast, her lips moving, her clerk's mind sorting clauses even as dread climbed her spine like cold water filling a boot.

The city would have the impossible made ordinary: storms bottled, years borrowed, names unspoken preserved against loss. The drowned realm would have—she slowed here, reading twice—*remembrance*. Witness. A standing record, kept fairly, of everything the realm beneath had been and done, so that it could not be forgotten by the world above, because to be forgotten, the treaty said plainly, in the drowned hand's rolling swell, *is to dissolve.*

That was the trade. Not miracles for gold. Miracles for memory. The guild hadn't been founded to exploit the impossible. It had been founded to *record* it.

Her eyes reached the final clause and stopped.

There was no final clause. The page ended mid-sentence, the vellum ragged where it had been torn—not cut, torn, hurriedly, by someone strong and frightened—and the missing piece was nowhere in the chamber. She checked. She lifted the lectern's cloth, ran her fingers along the coral's seams, searched the pale-veined walls for any niche that might hold a scrap of parchment six inches wide.

Gone. The last clause of the founding covenant, the terms of renewal or ending, the thing that would tell her why tonight's date was circled in a book sealed for a century—

Gone, and the tear was fresh enough that the fibers still curled.

Cyrus stood in the breathing dark and felt the vertigo come again, worse than before, because this was no longer the vertigo of a safe life revealed as a cage. This was the vertigo of understanding who had built the cage, and when, and why the bars had been arranged exactly so. Someone had torn out the covenant's ending. Someone had let a century pass with the treaty incomplete. Someone had let tonight arrive.

And someone, she realized, with a dread so complete it had begun to feel almost like calm, might be watching her right now.

* * *

Vess had watched her for eleven minutes.

He knew the number precisely because he had taught himself, long ago, to count everything—every coin, every breath, every second of another person's ignorance. Eleven minutes since the wall exhaled. Eleven minutes since his clerk, his quiet, invisible, indispensable clerk, had walked down into the dark carrying a scrap of foolscap with a founder's glyph copied in a hedge-witch's hand.

He stood in the shadowed mouth of the upper stair, where the lamplight gave out, and he did not announce himself.

This was not cruelty, whatever the clerks whispered about him. It was method. A man learns more from watching what people do unobserved than from anything they say to his face, and Vess had built the Meridian Guild's modern fortune on precisely that principle. He watched Cyrus Eastwick read the covenant with her lips moving. He watched her understand it—he saw the exact moment, her shoulders dropping half an inch, the small involuntary sound she made, grief-shaped. He watched her search for the torn clause with the thoroughness of a woman balancing a ledger, checking every entry twice.

And he watched her stop searching, straighten, and stand very still in the pulsing light, and he recognized what he was seeing, because he had seen it once before, in a cottage by the marshes, on the face of a dying hedge-witch who had refused him with her last breath.

Resolve. Dread finishing its fermentation and turning to resolve.

Vess's jaw tightened. He had planned for discovery, eventually—he always planned—but not like this, not yet, not with the tide turning tonight and the envoys already restless in the harbor's deep channels. He had needed another day. One day to move the remaining assets, to burn the correspondence, to be standing innocently at his desk when his clerk came running up from nowhere with wild eyes and questions he could deflect until after midnight.

Instead she was down here now, alone with the truth, assembling it in that meticulous head of hers, and there was no longer any version of events in which Cyrus Eastwick remained merely a clerk.

He considered the arithmetic. It was what he was best at. One witness, dead, versus one witness, managed. A body in the harbor versus a name on a new covenant, signed willingly, in blood, by the unseen scribe the prophecy demanded—signed, if he played it correctly, to terms of *his* drafting, renewing nothing, restoring nothing, transferring everything. The drowned realm wanted remembrance? Let them remember who held the pen.

But the prophecy was specific, and prophecies always were, in the ways that mattered. *Willingly given.* *Freely witnessed.* The old founders had built those words into the covenant's spine like rebar, and even torn out, even hidden, they held. He could not force her. That was the whole infuriating architecture of the thing. He could only make her choose, and shape the choosing.

So he did not step out of the shadows. He did not speak. He turned, silent as the ledgers he loved, and climbed back up toward the light, already composing the summons he would send her before dawn—an audit, a private commission, something that would put her in his office with the door closed, where the conversation could begin.

Below, in the chamber with the breathing walls, Cyrus stood a moment longer over the torn covenant. Then she did the thing that six years of invisibility had trained her to do better than anyone alive: she memorized everything. Every clause, every wave-script line, the exact shape of the tear, the direction of the fibers. She committed the treaty to the one vault no guildmaster could enter.

Then she took out her quill, and on the back of the foolscap, in her small precise hand, she began to copy it out—for evidence, she told herself, for leverage, for the reckoning she could feel approaching like weather.

She did not see Vess in the dark above the stairs.

But as she climbed, copying as she went, she passed the place where he had stood, and stopped, and frowned—at the floor, where the dust lay undisturbed except for the faint print of a boot, facing downward, recently placed.

Someone had been here. Someone had watched her find it, and had let her leave.

Cyrus blew out her candle, folded the covenant's ghost into her sleeve, and went up into the guild's ordinary night—to her desk, to her ink, to the long hours before dawn—with her heart hammering a single clear thought:

Whatever the final clause had said, she would need to learn it before the guildmaster decided she had learned too much. And somewhere beneath the harbor, something ancient was waiting for an answer that was already a century late.
