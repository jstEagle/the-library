## 4. The Drowned Envoy

The well in the guild's lower court had never, in six years of Cyrus's careful observation, produced so much as a ripple without cause.

She was crossing the flagstones at dusk with a lantern and a stack of returned ledgers when the water went still—not the ordinary stillness of a deep cistern but a held stillness, the stillness of something drawing breath. The lantern flame leaned toward the well like a compass needle finding north. Then the surface bulged upward, smooth as glass over a rising fist, and a hand broke through it.

The hand was wrong in ways her eyes refused to summarize. Too many joints. Skin the grey-green of deep harbor water, webbed between fingers that ended not in nails but in something like polished shell. Water poured off it in ropes that fell *upward* for a heartbeat before remembering which way was down.

Cyrus did not run. That was the first thing she noticed about herself afterward, examining the memory like an entry she'd have to reconcile: she stood there, ledger clutched to her chest, because some part of her had already read this page. She had found tonight's date circled in century-old ink. She had seen the torn clause of the covenant. The books had been telling her all week what was coming to collect.

The envoy rose out of the well the way a thought rises into language—suddenly, entirely, without transition. He stood taller than any man in the guild, draped in kelp-dark robes that moved as if still underwater, and his face was long and serene and terrible, crowned with a circlet of white coral that glowed faintly, like moonlight kept in reserve. Where his eyes should have been there were only calm, luminous hollows, and yet she felt *seen*—seen down to the entries in her, catalogued, balanced.

"You are the keeper of accounts," he said. His voice came with the smell of low tide and old stone, and it resonated in her ribs more than her ears. "I require the signatory."

Cyrus's mouth was dry. "The—the guildmaster is Vess Alderyn. If you'll come up to the—"

"I do not require the guildmaster." The hollow eyes fixed on her. "I require the one who sealed the covenant. The hand that signed. It is the hour of renewal. I have crossed the long dark beneath your harbor carrying the terms, and I will not return without them honored."

Her mind raced through the guild's hierarchy the way she'd race through a ledger looking for a misposted sum. Signatories were things she filed. Seals were things she shelved. In six years she had never once seen a name attached to the founding covenant—only the seal itself, wax gone black as tar, in the vault below.

"The covenant," she said carefully, "is... under review."

Something shifted in the envoy's face. Not anger. Something worse: the slow grief of a clerk finding the books cooked.

"Under *review*." He repeated the word as though tasting spoiled fish. "For one hundred years, at every turn of the tide-tithe, an envoy has surfaced and been received, and the terms have been renewed, and the waters have kept their patience. Tonight the chamber stands empty. Tonight the clause is torn out. Tonight no hand waits to sign." He descended from the well's rim, and where his feet touched stone, seawater bloomed outward in a spreading stain. "Do you know what your city was before the bargain, keeper of accounts? Do you know what sleeps beneath your harbor, patient because it was *paid*?"

"No," Cyrus said honestly. "But I've read the interest terms."

For a moment—one strange, suspended moment—she could have sworn the envoy almost laughed. Then the coral crown flared, and the serenity in his face closed like a door.

"Then you will understand me precisely. Bring me the signatory by moonrise, or be counted among the debtors." He raised that too-many-jointed hand, and from the water streaming off his sleeve he drew a shape—a sigil of wet light—and pressed it against the air between them. It hung there, glowing, patient. A mark. She knew marks; she stamped them daily. This one was a lien.

"When the moon clears the spire," he said, "the mark collects. There is no vault deep enough, no ledger clean enough, no shadow thick enough to hide a debtor from the drowned court. We taught your founders that lesson in blood, and they wrote it down, and then they tore the page out." His hollow gaze lingered on her face. "You read. You will find what they hid. And you will learn that reading does not make you safe. It makes you *responsible*."

Then he stepped backward into the well and sank without splash, without ripple, without even the courtesy of displacement, and the water lay flat and innocent as if nothing had ever climbed out of it.

* * *

Cyrus ran.

Not immediately—first she stood in the lower court for three full breaths doing what she always did, which was assess, itemize, post the facts to their proper columns. Fact: the covenant was broken. Fact: the envoy believed someone in the guild could renew it. Fact: the mark on the air between them had followed her when she moved, drifting after her like a loyal dog made of light. Fact: moonrise was less than two hours off, and the spire's shadow already stretched across the court toward her feet.

Then she ran, because assessment had delivered its verdict.

She took the servants' stair two steps at a time, past the candle-vault, past the memory-pawn room where widows' years slept in little glass drawers, up into the cataloguing hall where she spent her days being invisible among the shelves. Her plan was formless and desperate: find Vess, put the problem in front of him, watch his face. Vess had watched her in the vaults without speaking. Vess had let her see the torn clause. Either the guildmaster was a fool—which six years of ledgers said he was not—or he had known the envoy was coming, and had wanted *her* standing here when it arrived.

The hall was empty. The lamps were lit. On her own desk, square in the middle of her tidy workspace, sat a book she had not left there.

Her hands shook as she opened it. It was the prophecy volume from the private collection—the one with tonight's date circled, the ink still wet. But now beneath the circling, in the same impossible fresh ink, new words had surfaced like bodies in a harbor:

*The unseen scribe sees both shores. Let her choose which shore to stand upon.*

Below that, a third line, smaller, almost gentle:

*Run, child. You were taught how.*

Cyrus stared at the last line until the letters blurred. *Taught.* Her mind went back—back past the guild, past the city walls, to a crooked cottage at the fen's edge and an old woman with ink-stained fingers who had drilled her, patiently, mercilessly, in the small vanishing arts. *Stillness is a door, Ulyana—no, Cyrus—stillness is a door and attention is the key.* She had thought it hedge-craft. Tricks for avoiding tax collectors and worse men. She had built six years of safety on the assumption that what Maren taught her was small.

The mark drifted through the doorway behind her, patient as a creditor.

Moonlight was beginning to pool through the high windows. She could hear, far above, the guild hunters' boots—Vess would know by now; Vess perhaps had always known—and she understood with cold clarity that the guildmaster would not save her. The guildmaster had engineered this. The broken covenant was not a failure. It was a *product*. And the only person in the building who could renew it was the one person the guild had never bothered to see.

Fine, she thought, with a ferocity that surprised her. Then let them go on not seeing me.

She did what Maren taught her. She stopped. Not froze—*stopped*, the way water stops when it becomes ice, the way a sentence stops when it becomes true. She gathered her attention the way Maren had shown her a hundred times over a hundred dull evenings, folding it inward, narrowing it to a single bright point, and she let the rest of herself—the part that people look *through*, the clerk-shape, the gray-mouse shape she'd worn for six years—fall away like a coat.

It had always worked before. It had been enough to slip past drunk dockhands and bored inspectors.

This time, the world *changed*.

The lamplight bent around her. The shadows received her the way the harbor receives rain—not hiding her but *claiming* her, acknowledging her, and she felt a pressure on her skin like a second signature being written. Because this was not a trick. She saw it now, saw it with the terrible clarity the envoy had promised her. The vanishing arts worked because they were *meant* to work—because somewhere in a torn-out clause, a covenant had set aside a place for a witness no one could see, a scribe who could stand between two realms and record them fairly, unseen by either. Maren hadn't taught her magic. Maren had *trained her for a post*.

The mark hovered at the edge of the lamplight, searching, its wet glow sweeping the shelves like a lighthouse beam. It passed over the space where she stood. It hesitated. And then—it could not hold her. The light slid off her the way water off oiled cloth, and the sigil dimmed, frustrated, and began to drift back toward the stairwell to report its failure to the sea.

Cyrus stood in the shadows of the cataloguing hall, invisible even to the drowning court, and shook.

Not from fear—or not only. Underneath the terror, steady as a keel, was something she had no column for. Purpose. Six years she had believed her invisibility was a wound, a deficiency, the price of survival. It was neither. It was an office. She had been appointed—to what, by whom, she didn't yet know—but the appointment was real, it was ancient, and it was now marked for death by one realm while a man in a high tower planned to spend her like coin to cheat the other.

Maren had hidden her here the way you hide a deed in a safe house. *Run, child. You were taught how.*

"Yes," Cyrus whispered to the empty hall, to the dead woman, to herself. "I was."

She took the prophecy book. She took her own ledger, because a scribe travels with her records. And instead of running for the gates—instead of running anywhere the guild or the sea expected—she turned toward the one exit no hunter watches because no honest member uses it: the old trade-well shaft that connected the guild's cellars to the harbor district, disused for decades, unlisted in every index except the one she kept in her head.

The smugglers used it. Which meant Tam Voss's kind used it. Which meant that by morning, half the docks would know the guild's quiet clerk had vanished on the very night the sea came calling.

Let them know, she thought, slipping into the dark with the ink-still-wet book pressed against her heart. Let the whole city know.

An unseen scribe, it turned out, was only invisible until she picked up her pen.
