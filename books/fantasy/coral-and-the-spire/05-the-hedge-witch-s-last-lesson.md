## 5. The Hedge-Witch's Last Lesson

The cottage at the end of the saltmarsh road had always looked like something the tide forgot. Low-roofed, turf-sodded, its chimney leaning like an old man against the wind, it sat in a bowl of gorse and sea-thrift where no one went unless they were lost or invited. Cyrus had been both, once, six years ago—a girl with soot on her fingers and nothing to her name but a talent for not being seen.

Now she stood at the gate again, and the cottage was broken.

The door hung off one hinge, splintered inward. The herb garden was churned to mud by boots that had come and gone in numbers—she counted the prints without meaning to, the way she counted everything: eleven distinct sets, some doubled back, some pacing. Inside, through the ruined doorway, her teacher's life lay scattered like a spilled drawer of type. Dried yarrow crushed underfoot. Jars of stormwater shattered, their contents already evaporating into the morning fog. The great cedar chest that had held Maren's books stood open and empty as a pulled tooth.

Cyrus did not go in right away. She stood at the threshold with the wet wind pulling at her coat and let herself feel the shape of what had happened here. *They came for her.* Not for the prophecy—the guildmaster's hunters would have gone straight to the spire archives if it were only paper they wanted. They came for Maren Thistle, hedge-witch of the marshes, the woman who had taught a foundling girl how to walk through a crowded room like smoke.

And Maren was gone. No body. No blood. Only absence, total and deliberate, the way she had taught Cyrus that absence could be made.

* * *

Six years ago, on the last night Cyrus ever spent under that leaning roof, Maren had made tea from bog-myrtle and told her a story she swore was nothing at all.

"You'll think this is a lesson," the old woman said, "and then you'll be angry when you find out it wasn't."

"I'm listening," Cyrus said, sixteen and certain she already knew everything worth knowing.

Maren turned her cup three times widdershins on the saucer, a habit she had whenever she was about to lie or about to tell the truth—Cyrus could never tell which, which was perhaps the point.

"There was a woman once who could make herself invisible. Not vanish—not the cheap trick, the flash-and-gone that street conjurers sell. I mean *unseen*. Present in a room, breathing its air, and no one's eyes would land on her. Kings walked past her. Guards looked through her. And do you know why?"

"Because she was very good at it?"

"Because she was *supposed* to be unseen." Maren set the cup down. "There's a difference, child, between hiding and being hidden. Hiding is something you do. Being hidden is something done *to* you, long before you were born, by people who needed someone exactly like you to exist and exactly where no one would look."

Cyrus remembered laughing. "That's not a lesson. That's a riddle with no answer."

"It's the only kind worth teaching." Maren touched her cheek, one dry finger, brief as a bird landing. "When you get to the city—and you will get to the city—find the Meridian Guild. Ask for work in the ledgers. Don't ask why. Don't ask for anything else. And don't come back here, whatever happens, until the day you can't help it."

"The day I can't help it," Cyrus repeated, and thought it was nonsense, and packed it away with the dried lavender and the bone needle case Maren pressed into her hands.

She had obeyed. Six years of obedience disguised as ambition: the apprenticeship, the ledgers, the careful construction of a self so unremarkable that even the guildmasters' clerks forgot her name between paydays. She had believed she built that invisibility herself. Tonight, standing in the wreckage of the only home she'd ever known, she understood at last that she had been *placed* there—set into the guild's machinery like a gear cut to secret specifications, by a woman who knew what was coming and loved her enough to aim her at it.

* * *

The ransacking had been thorough, but thoroughness has blind spots, and Maren had spent seventy years learning where they fell.

Cyrus knelt in the ruins of the kitchen, lifting the broken flagstone beside the cold hearth—the loose one, the one Maren had never let her scrub because "a floor should keep one secret, or it stops being yours." Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, a bundle of letters tied with tarred twine.

She carried them to the doorway where the light was better, and her hands shook, and she hated them for shaking, and she untied the knot anyway.

The first letter was addressed to her. Not *To whom it may concern*, not a codicil, not a spell. To her, by name, in Maren's crabbed hand: *For Cyrus Eastwick, on the day she cannot help but come home.*

"My girl," it began.

"If you are reading this, then the ink in the old book has found you, and you are frightened, and you are angry at me, and you have every right to be both. Sit down. Have something to eat. Grief is heavy labor and you shouldn't do it fasting."

Cyrus laughed out loud, alone in a ruined doorway, and the laugh cracked in the middle and became something else.

"I knew your mother," the letter went on. "Not well. Well enough. She came to me before you were born, the way women used to come to hedge-witches before the guild made even birth a transaction. She asked me two questions. The first was whether her child would be safe. The second was whether her child would matter. I told her the truth, which is that those are often the same question asked badly.

"She left you to me because she had seen the covenant-marks on her own family going back four generations—did you never wonder why your grandmother could quiet a room just by entering it? Why your mother could stand in a market and pickpockets' hands would slide off her as if she were oiled glass? That is not luck. That is lineage. That is the covenant, written in blood instead of ink, waiting for a scribe to read it aloud.

"You are the unseen scribe, Cyrus. You were the unseen scribe before you could hold a pen. All I ever did was teach you the grammar."

Cyrus read that line four times. Her eyes kept sliding off it, the way eyes slid off her. *All I ever did was teach you the grammar.*

"I hid you in the guild," the letter continued, "because the guild is where the covenant is kept, and because Vess cannot break what he cannot find. He has been hunting the signatory line for thirty years—I watched him buy up every record of births along the coast, every parish ledger, every midwife's memory he could pawn. He thinks the covenant is a document. It isn't. It's a person. It has always been a person, and the drowned realm knows it, which is why their envoy comes now, and why you must be faster than grief.

"In the cedar chest you'll find nothing—they took it, or they will. But look beneath the hearthstone, and look in the henhouse, third nest box, under the false bottom. There is a thing there you will need. I won't say what it is. If these letters fall into the wrong hands, let the wrong hands dig in a chicken coop.

"One more thing, and then I'll let you be angry in peace. You will want to believe I abandoned you. Six years of silence is a long silence, and silence reads like desertion. So hear me: I wrote to you every season. Every letter came back unopened, because I paid the postal clerk to return them—I couldn't risk Vess tracing the road from my door to your desk. Burning forty letters over six years, my girl, is its own kind of invisible work. You of all people should understand.

"You were chosen. Not by me—I was only the courier. Chosen by a bargain older than the city, older than the guild, older than the names we give things. Your mother chose to trust it. I chose to serve it. What you choose is the only part of this story still unwritten.

"Don't be careful. Be exact.

"—M."

* * *

Cyrus sat in the doorway until the fog burned off and the sun found the marsh, and she read the letter again, and again, and let the anger drain out of her the way water finds its level.

She had spent six years believing her invisibility was her own achievement—a fortress she built stone by stone out of diligence and dullness. It was a comfort, that belief. Fortresses belong to their builders. But the truth was stranger and, in the end, kinder: she had been hidden the way a seed is hidden, planted in the dark on purpose, by someone who expected her to grow toward a specific light.

Not abandoned. *Assigned.*

The grief didn't leave. It changed shape. It stopped being the ache of a loss and became the weight of an inheritance—less comfortable, more demanding, the difference between mourning a woman and owing her. Maren had burned forty letters so that Cyrus could sit here, untraceable, holding the forty-first. Maren had endured six years of a silence she designed herself, trusting a girl she'd raised to eventually be clever enough to come home.

*The day you can't help it.* Tonight's date, circled in century-old ink. The envoy rising through the guild's well. Vess watching from the dark of his own vaults. She hadn't been able to help it. She'd run from the guild with a torn clause burning in her satchel and nowhere in the world to go except here.

Except here had never been a refuge. It had been a starting line.

She got up, knees stiff from the cold stones, and went to the henhouse.

The coop leaned at the back of the property, half-swallowed by gorse, its wire rusted to lace. Third nest box from the left. She pried up the false bottom Maren's knife had fitted so precisely that six years of weather hadn't swelled it shut, and drew out a flat packet wrapped in waxed linen.

Inside: a pen. Not a quill—an instrument, a nib of grey metal that was neither iron nor silver nor anything she could name, warm to the touch the way a held hand is warm. Beside it, a small stoppered vial of ink so black it seemed to drink the daylight, and a single sheet of paper that felt like skin and smelled faintly of the harbor at low tide.

And beneath these, folded twice, a scrap of map—not drawn, but *written*: a passage in Maren's hand describing the spire's covenant chamber in obsessive detail. The stair behind the archive shelf. The count of steps. The draft that moved upward through the shaft, and the hour it reversed.

At the bottom of the page, one final line, underlined twice:

*Vess will offer you a partnership. He offers everyone a partnership; it is cheaper than murder. Refuse him in writing. Words bind him more than blades—he is a creature of contracts, and contracts cut both ways.*

Cyrus folded the map into her satchel beside the torn clause, tucked the pen into the inner pocket where her heart beat against it, and stood a moment in the doorway of the ruined cottage, looking out at the marsh road winding toward the city and the spire rising above it like a needle through the world's fabric.

Behind her, the cottage. Ahead of her, the water climbing the harbor stairs.

She had one ally left in the city, and he was a smuggler who'd sold the guild a stolen tide and cheated them honestly while doing it. Tam Voss—no relation, he'd sworn, to the guildmaster, though nothing in this city was unrelated to anything else. If Maren's letters were right, she had until the next high tide to reach the docks.

She blew out the candle she'd lit out of habit in a house with no roof left to darken, and started walking.

The marsh grass bent around her ankles, closing behind her like water, and for the first time in six years Cyrus Eastwick did not bother to be unseen. Let them see her coming. She was done being hidden by other people's hands.

It was time to write something of her own.
