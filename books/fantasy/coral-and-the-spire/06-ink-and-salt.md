## 6. Ink and Salt

The city smelled different when you came to it from the marshes instead of through its gates. From the east it smelled of rot and rope and fish guts left in the sun—honest smells, the smell of things that had been used up and thrown away. From the gates it smelled of ink and candlewax and money changing hands. Cyrus had spent six years breathing the second smell. It took only half a night of walking through the first to understand which one she'd been hiding in.

She kept to the drainage channel where the seawall crumbled into mudflats, because even a woman done with being unseen did not need to be *found* yet. The guild's hunters would be awake by now. Vess would have found her cot cold, her ledger entries immaculate, her resignation written in her own hand and dated three days past—the first lie she had ever entered into guild paper, backdated so carefully that even she almost believed she'd decided this long ago.

*Let them read it,* she thought. *Let him read it twice.*

Ahead, the harbor opened like a dark mouth, and beyond it the Spire stood against the stars, pale as a bone someone had stood upright in the earth. The drowned realm was beneath that water. She could feel it now, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue—a pressure, an attention, something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the tide line and beginning, slowly, to wake.

She heard the boat before she saw it: the creak of a hull being bailed, the mutter of a man arguing with himself about knots.

Cyrus stopped at the edge of the mudflat and considered. There were a hundred boats along this stretch of shore, but only one kind of man who beached his boat in the drainage shallows instead of the licensed docks, and only one smuggler foolish enough—or honest enough—to have once sold the Meridian Guild a stolen tide and lived to complain about the price they paid him.

"Tam Voss," she said, "or whoever's wearing his voice tonight."

The bailing stopped. A lantern came up unshuttered, and behind its slatted light a broad face squinted out at her, weathered brown, one eyebrow split by an old scar. Tam looked her over from muddy boots to wind-scoured hair.

"Guild," he said flatly. "You've got guild hands."

"I've got guild hands and no guild anymore." She stepped closer, letting the lantern light find her face. "You sold the guild a stolen tide four years ago. They paid you a quarter of what it was worth and made you sign a paper saying you were glad of it. I'm the scribe who balanced that entry."

Something moved behind his eyes—not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The particular look of a man meeting the person who wrote down the arithmetic of his ruin.

"You're the one who rounds in my favor," he said slowly. "Half-crowns, odd farthings. I always wondered if that was a mistake."

"It wasn't."

"Huh." He considered the mud, the sky, the Spire standing white and patient across the black water. Then he jerked his chin toward the boat. "Get in before I come to my senses. And mind the bail bucket. She takes on water faster than the guild takes offense."

*

Tam Voss ran contraband the way other men ran errands: steadily, without ambition, and with a private conviction that everyone above him in any chain was a thief with better stationery. He had sold the guild a stolen tide once—one whole high tide, lifted from a bay two hundred miles north and carried south in a cask of salt-glass—and the guild had used it to flood a rival's warehouse district, drowning six streets' worth of goods and one watchman, and paid Tam enough to fix his boat's keel and not one coin more. He had hated them cleanly ever since. Hatred, in Tam's experience, was the most reliable cargo a man could carry; it never spoiled, never leaked, and always fetched its weight.

But the woman climbing into his boat was not asking him to carry hatred. She was asking for passage to the Spire, tonight, with guild hunters already moving through the dockside wards—he'd seen their lamps, three green points swinging along the seaward road like will-o'-the-wisps with expense accounts.

"So let me get the shape of it," he said, shipping oars and letting the current take them out along the breakwater. "The covenant under the city is failing. The drowned folk are waking up angry. The guildmaster broke it on purpose so nobody gets miracles except through his till. And the prophecy says some unseen scribe has to write a new one, and—" he squinted at her "—that's you."

"That's the short version."

"What's the long version?"

"The long version has a torn-out page, a dead hedge-witch, and an envoy who wants me dead because I couldn't produce a signature that predates my grandmother."

Tam nodded as though this were all perfectly reasonable. "And what do I get? Because I'll tell you plain, mistress Eastwick: I don't row people toward disasters for free. That's charity, and charity sinks boats."

Cyrus reached into her coat and drew out a slim book bound in grey leather—the little ledger she'd kept for six years, the one with every rounding in every dockside seller's favor marked in her neat hand. She set it on the thwart between them.

"This is every favor I ever did you and forty others like you. Half-crowns rounded up, fines recorded lower than Vess ordered, a widow's pawned years logged as three months instead of three winters. If I die tonight, this book proves the guild cheated you all. If I live, I intend to put every one of those debts right—publicly, in a registry anyone can read."

Tam didn't touch it. He looked at it the way he looked at weather coming over the horizon.

"And if I say no and row you home?"

"Then keep the book anyway. It's yours whether I sink or swim. That's the bargain. No conditions, no clauses hidden in the margins." She met his eyes. "I'm told that's unusual, in trade."

He was quiet a long moment. Somewhere behind them, faint over the water, a bell began to ring in the ward-towers—the hunters had reached the docks.

"Well," Tam said finally, and pulled the oars in. "First honest offer anybody's made me since I learned to swim. Which makes me suspicious as hell, but suspicious isn't going to outrun those bells." He set the blade in the black water. "Hold on. We're going to cut the breakwater gap at speed, and the Spire stairs are flooded to the third landing, so don't scream until we're actually sinking."

*

They cut the gap at speed. Cyrus did not scream, though the spray hit her like a slap and the Spire rose up out of the water close enough that she could see the old covenant-marks carved into its base—spirals and open hands, worn nearly smooth, older than the guild, older than the city's name.

Tam brought the boat alongside the drowned stair with the ease of a man who had been doing illegal things in this harbor since before he had chin hair, and held it steady against the stone while she gathered herself to climb.

"Eastwick." His voice was lower now, stripped of its banter. "That envoy. The drowned one. When it marked you—it touched your hand?"

"Yes."

"Then look at it."

She looked. In the lantern light, across her palm, faint as a watermark, lay a spiral—the same spiral carved into the spire's foundation, drawn in a silver that hadn't been there yesterday.

"That's not a death-mark," Tam said quietly. "My gran was harbor-born, back when the old families still remembered things. She said the drowned court marks its *scribes*, not its enemies. Says the mark means: *this one writes fairly, or this one doesn't write at all.*"

Cyrus stared at her own hand. Six years of vanishing spells, six years of believing she'd built her invisibility herself, stitch by careful stitch—and underneath it all, this. A covenant's signature, waiting in her skin for her to notice it.

Above them, on the flooded stair, lamplight bloomed. Three green flames, descending fast. Guild hunters, and moving like they knew exactly where she'd land.

Tam followed her gaze, then sighed with the deep weariness of a man whose night had gone entirely according to his fears.

"Stairs are out," he said. "So we do it my way. Ever been up the outside of a building, mistress scribe?"

"No."

"Lucky you. First time's the easiest—you're too terrified to make mistakes." He threw her a coil of rope, and grinned, and it was not entirely a kind grin. "Trust me. It's the last honest thing either of us does tonight."

Above them, at the top of the Spire, a window lit gold—and in the light of it, briefly, a silhouette leaned out and looked down: narrow shoulders, still posture, the unmistakable patience of Vess watching his property come home.
