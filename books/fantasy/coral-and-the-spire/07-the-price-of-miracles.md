## 7. The Price of Miracles

They took Tam Okafor in the space between one wave and the next, which was fitting, because he had spent his whole life living in that space.

One moment he was cinching the *Salt-Luck*'s mooring line at the Tidewrack stairs, the boat still rocking with the ghost-weight of the woman he'd just ferried up the harbor. The next, four figures in gray oilskins stepped out of the fog the way fog steps out of nothing, and a fifth voice behind him said, pleasantly, "Don't run. It's raining, and I hate chasing people when it's raining."

Tam ran anyway. He made it eleven steps.

The blow to the back of his knees was professional—no anger in it, no malice, just the efficient geometry of men who had done this many times. The cobbles came up and met his face, tasting of salt and old iron, and then there were hands under his arms and a hood over his head that smelled of lamp oil and something sweeter underneath, something like flowers left too long in a closed room.

*Preserved things*, he thought, as they hauled him into a waiting carriage. *That's what the guild smells like. Everything kept past its season.*

He had sold them a stolen tide once. He'd known even then, pocketing the guild's heavy coin, that he was selling to people who would not forgive a debt or forget a face. Six years he'd told himself the ledger had closed. Tonight he learned that ledgers, in Meridian, never close. They only wait.

* * *

The room they put him in had no windows, but it did have a table, two chairs, and a decanter of amber wine, and somehow that combination frightened Tam more than chains would have. Chains meant you were worth taking by force. Wine meant you were worth persuading.

He tested the door twice—locked—and then sat, because standing made him feel like livestock at auction. The chair was comfortable. That frightened him too.

When the door opened, the man who entered was not what Tam expected. He had built a picture of Vess over six years: a monster in fine cloth, some gilded shark with rings on every finger. Instead, here was a spare, silvering man in a clerk's plain coat, spectacles pushed up on his forehead, carrying a folio under one arm like a schoolmaster late for lessons. Only his hands gave him away—long, precise fingers, ink-stained at the nails, the hands of a man who wrote things down so that other things could be done to them later.

"Tam Okafor," Vess said, settling across the table. "Smuggler, salvage-man, occasional thief of tides. You stole the spring tide of 1189 from the harbor mouth and sold it back to us for forty crowns." He opened the folio. "You undersold yourself."

"Didn't know it was worth more."

"You didn't know what it was *for*." Vess poured two glasses without asking. "Neither, frankly, did we, not entirely. That's the trouble with miracles, Mr. Okafor. Nobody reads the terms until the interest comes due." He slid one glass across. "Drink. It's not drugged. Poisoning you would be an expense, and I am, above all things, frugal."

Tam didn't touch it. "Where's the woman?"

"Cyrus Eastwick." Vess said the name the way a man sets a piece on a board. "Yes. Let's talk about her. But first, let me tell you a story, because you've earned context—you're in it, after all, though you didn't know it. Do you know what the covenant is? Not the story the clerks are told. The real shape of it."

"I know the city doesn't flood."

"The city doesn't flood," Vess agreed. "A hundred years ago, our founders went down beneath the harbor and bargained with the realm down there—the drowned court, the deep folk, call them what you like. The terms were simple. The sea keeps its distance; the city pays a price. And do you know what the founders did, clever clerks all?" He smiled thinly. "They made the payment someone else's problem. They bound the covenant to a witness—a life freely given, renewed each century—and then they hid the mechanism inside their own guild, where it could be managed. Managed. Delayed. Negotiated downward, year after year, generation after generation. Every guildmaster before me kicked the debt along like a bad coin."

"And you?"

Vess removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried, a man enjoying the one audience that could appreciate him. "I stopped paying altogether."

Tam's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "You *broke* it."

"I *completed* it." For the first time, something moved behind the clerk's mildness—hunger, old and patient as barnacles. "Do you know what happens when the covenant fails, Mr. Okafor? Nothing. At first. The impossible simply stops being rationed. No treaty deciding who gets bottled storms and who gets borrowed years. A free market of miracles—and the guild holds the entire inventory. I have spent thirty years acquiring it. When the drowned realm rises—and it will rise, soon, that's rather why we're having this conversation—I will sell this city its survival at whatever price I name. Every prayer will pass through my counter. There will be no miracle in Meridian that does not carry my watermark."

"That's madness. The water's already climbing. People will die."

"People die every day, Mr. Okafor, of perfectly ordinary causes, and nobody calls it madness. I'm merely changing whose fault it is." Vess set the spectacles back on his nose and regarded him with something almost like kindness. "But here is the difficulty. The covenant has a renewal clause. If someone renews it—if a new signatory writes herself in—the whole architecture comes back. Rationing. Scarcity. My inventory becomes worthless paper overnight. And your friend Miss Eastwick is climbing toward exactly that as we speak."

"She won't sign anything of yours."

"No. She'll sign her *own*. That's what makes her dangerous." Vess laced his fingers. "Which brings us to the offer. Renewal requires a willing life, given freely—that's the old term, and the old terms are the only ones that bind. She believes she must give her own. Noble girl. Terrible bookkeeping. But she can't renew what she can't reach, and she can't reach the spire without a boat, and the only boatman fool enough to take her is currently sitting in my wine cellar."

Understanding arrived cold. "You want me to tell you her route."

"I want you to tell me where you're taking her. The channel, the landing, the hour. In exchange: your freedom, your boat, and forty thousand crowns—which, I note, is a thousand times what you asked for the tide. You see? I told you that you undersold yourself. I pay fairly. It's the guild's one virtue." He turned the folio around and slid it across with a pen resting on it. "Or refuse, and I send my hunters to the obvious channels anyway, and they will find her, because there are only three ways up the harbor and I own all three. The difference, Mr. Okafor, is that if they find her *because of you*, she dies knowing your name. If they find her anyway—" he shrugged, "—your betrayal buys nothing, and costs everything. It is, purely arithmetically, better to sign."

Tam looked at the pen. At the wine, sweating gently in its glass. At the pleasant, patient face of a man who had broken a hundred-year peace the way other men break seals on letters, and who called it completion.

He thought about Cyrus in the stern of the *Salt-Luck*, gripping the gunwale with white knuckles while the drowned envoy's mark burned cold on her skin, and how she hadn't asked what the crossing would cost him. She'd said, *name your price honestly and I'll pay it honestly,* and he had named it—passage to the spire, nothing else, no questions—and she'd paid it to the copper without blinking. First honest bargain he'd ever struck with anyone wearing guild colors. Maybe the first honest bargain of his life.

Forty thousand crowns.

His father had drowned for less. Half the Tidewrack had.

"No," Tam said.

Vess sighed—not angry, almost fond, the sigh of a teacher watching a student fail an exam he'd failed himself long ago. "Sentiment. The most expensive commodity in Meridian, and everyone insists on hoarding it." He rose, collected the folio, and paused at the door. "For the record, I sent the hunters out five minutes ago. Your answer changed nothing except your price for the next buyer. Someone always sells, Mr. Okafor. It's the one law older than the covenant."

The door locked behind him.

Tam sat alone in the lamplit room, heart hammering, doing the arithmetic of treachery against the tide tables in his head. Three channels up the harbor, Vess had said. He owned all three.

Except he didn't. Because the route Tam had given Cyrus wasn't any of the three—it was the drowned smuggler's cut beneath the old customs house, the passage he'd never sold anyone, never written down, never spoken aloud since the night his father showed it to him and swore him silent. Vess's hunters would sweep the open water while Cyrus slipped under their keels through a channel that existed only in the memory of a dead man and the head of a liar sitting in a locked room.

Tam picked up the untouched wine at last and drank it slowly, letting the fear settle into something harder.

*Run true, little scribe,* he thought, watching the door. *I've got maybe an hour before they work out what I am—and then, one way or another, I'm coming up that harbor after you.*
