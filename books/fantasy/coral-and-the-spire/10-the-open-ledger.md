## 10. The Open Ledger

The registry hall had once been the guild's counting room, and some mornings Cyrus still caught herself reaching for the shutters, wanting to close them against the world.

She never did. That was the point.

Morning light came through the tall windows in long amber bars now, falling across rows of open shelves where the ledgers of the Meridian Guild sat with their spines turned outward—every one of them. Bottled monsoons and borrowed years, pawned memories and unspoken names, six centuries of miracles traded in the dark, all of it catalogued in plain script anyone who could read was welcome to read. A cooper from Saltrow stood at the second shelf just then, sounding out the entry for a storm his grandmother had supposedly bought in 1189, his lips moving, his finger tracking the lines. Behind him, a widow waited her turn to petition for the return of three years of memory she had pawned to pay for her husband's funeral.

Cyrus would find those years. She had promised herself that much.

"Mistress Scribe." The widow's voice pulled her back. She was small and gray and held her petition folded so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth.

"Cyrus is fine," Cyrus said, for perhaps the ten-thousandth time. "Let's see what we can do."

* * *

It had taken four months to dissolve six hundred years of institutional secrecy, and it had not been done gently. The city council had wanted the guild's assets seized outright; the harbor temples had wanted them burned; three separate factions had wanted Cyrus herself appointed, deposed, or drowned, depending on the hour. In the end she had drafted the terms herself, sitting up three nights in the old covenant chamber with ink on her fingers and salt still crusted faintly in the grain of the desk—the desk where she had signed her own blood into law.

*One ledger,* she had written. *Open to all. No trade without record. No miracle without price named in advance. And no scribe above the record.*

Vess had taught her that last part, in a way. Every cruelty of the guild had grown from the same root: the belief that whoever holds the ledger holds the power. So Cyrus had written the scribe out of power entirely—a servant of the record, nothing more. Neutral as water. Visible as glass.

The irony did not escape her. Six years spent learning to be unseen, and now half the city knew her face. Children pointed at her in the fish market. An innkeeper refused her coin. It should have been unbearable. Some evenings it still was, a little—when the hall emptied and the silence came back and she felt the old instinct rise, the pull toward shadow and anonymity like a tide going out.

But then morning came, and the doors opened, and people walked in carrying their impossible griefs and their small hopes, and they looked *at* her, not through her, and asked for help. Belonging, it turned out, could be earned the same way anything else could: paid for, witnessed, recorded.

Her vanishing arts still worked. She checked sometimes, alone, late at night—let the covenant-marks take her, stand invisible between the shelves. But she used them less and less. There was less and less need to hide.

* * *

Tam found her at midday, as he always did, arriving by the side door with the particular gait of a man who had spent his life walking decks and had never fully forgiven solid ground.

"Docks are honest," he announced, dropping onto the bench across from her desk. "Disgusting, frankly. I don't know how you people stand it."

"You people?"

"Scribes. Honest folk." He helped himself to an apple from her bowl. "A barque came in this morning flying the new registry colors and the customs master didn't even try to shake him down. Where's the sport in that."

"You're complaining about the absence of corruption."

"I'm observing it. Professionally." He bit the apple. His eyes, quick as ever, went past her shoulder to the window, to the harbor beyond, and softened in the way they only ever did when he looked at water. "Tide's high. Fair wind. Envoy's due today, isn't he?"

Cyrus felt something loosen in her chest. "Noon."

"Then I'll stay," Tam said, which was his way of saying he'd been worried, and wouldn't admit it.

* * *

The envoy surfaced at noon precisely, because the drowned kept their appointments the way they kept everything else—with the patience of pressure itself.

He came up through the registry well, which Cyrus had converted from a vault-access into an official gate: brackish water rising in the shaft, then the long gleaming head breaking the surface, kelp braided through a crown of pale coral, eyes like coins at the bottom of clear water. He filled the well room with the smell of deep harbor and the sound of water finding its level.

"Neutral Scribe." His voice was the sound of a hull settling at anchor.

"Envoy Nereus." She bowed—not deeply; they had negotiated the correct depth over months, and settled on this. "You're expected. There's a chair, if you'd like—"

"I will stand in the pool. It is more comfortable than your chairs." He lifted one great webbed hand, and two of his retinue passed up objects wrapped in eelgrass: gifts, as there had been gifts every month since the flood receded. This time it was a bell of greened bronze that rang notes underwater and above alike—"for calling aid across both realms," Nereus said—and a jar of pearls the color of dawn, each one grown around a word the drowned had wished to say and could not, in air.

Cyrus logged them in the open ledger while he watched, and he liked watching, he had told her once, because under the old covenant nothing had ever been written down where his people could read it. They had given their storms and their tides and their dead in secret, generation after generation, and called it peace. Now every term of the new pact sat on an open shelf in daylight, in two languages, and any drowned child who learned letters could climb the well and check the arithmetic.

"The third clause is holding?" Nereus asked. It was his ritual question.

"The witness clause holds. I saw the drowned quarter yesterday—the new spawning terraces. I'll file my account by week's end."

"And the city's account?"

"Honest as I can make it. Which is very."

Nereus made a sound like water over stones, which after eight months of practice she had learned was laughter. "Under Vess," he said, "the signatory lied to us for forty years and called it diplomacy. You write us the truth and call it paperwork. The drowned prefer paperwork." His coin-bright eyes turned toward the window, toward the harbor, and his voice went quieter, the way it always did when he spoke of the old days. "My grandmother remembered the first covenant being signed. She said the scribe wept as she wrote it, and everyone assumed it was grief." A pause. "It was not grief, I think now. It was the weight of being the only one who saw both worlds whole."

Cyrus looked down at her own hands—ink-stained, salt-scarred, steady. "Yes," she said. "Something like that."

* * *

Evening came slow and gold. The hall emptied by degrees—the cooper leaving with a certified copy of his grandmother's storm, the widow leaving with hope and an appointment, Tam lingering until the last petitioner was gone because he was incapable of leaving anywhere first.

They walked out together into the warm dusk, down the wide steps into the square where the fountain now ran with ordinary, unremarkable, gloriously mundane water. Somewhere beyond the rooftops the harbor lay flat and calm, and beneath it, lit with cold lanterns, an entire realm went about its evening business, bound to the city above by ink and salt and a promise kept in public.

"Supper?" Tam asked. "There's a place on the docks. Terrible wine. Honest books."

"You mean honest ledgers."

"I mean the cook keeps accounts you can trust, which in my experience is rarer." He glanced at her sideways, and for a moment the smuggler's mask slipped and there was just Tam, who had sold a stolen tide to buy his crew out of debt-bondage and had never quite forgiven himself for every other deal he'd made before he knew better. "Come on. One meal. I'll even let you pay, since apparently that's legal now."

Cyrus laughed—an easy thing these days, though it still surprised her when it happened—and they turned toward the water together.

That was when she saw the boy.

He stood at the edge of the square, maybe twelve years old, dressed in the rough wool of the hill country, mud to his knees, clutching a book to his chest the way she had once clutched hers. Not a ledger. Not a registry copy. A handmade thing, sewn crooked, pages crowded with a child's careful handwriting—and around him, faintly, shimmering, the air refused to settle. Passersby looked through him without meaning to. Their eyes slid off like rain off glass.

Hedge-witch marks. Covenant-marks. Untrained, ungoverned, burning themselves out of him.

The boy met her gaze—and only she could see him do it, only she and perhaps whatever watched from the well of the registry behind her—and held out the crooked little book like an offering, or a summons.

"Scribe," he said, in a voice that carried strangely, as if more than one throat spoke it. "I need someone to teach me what I am. Before the wrong people notice me first."

Behind her, Cyrus heard the well-water stir.
