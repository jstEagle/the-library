## 1. The Weight of Small Charms

Rain came to Greyhollow the way gossip did—sideways, and never quite stopping. It slicked the cobbles of Tanner's Row to the color of old pewter, beaded on the canvas awnings, and turned the market into a chorus of dripping eaves and huddled voices. Casper Ashgrove had learned, over seven years, to love the rain a little. Rain meant hoods up. Rain meant wardens walked the streets with their collars turned and their sniffing-stones wrapped in oilcloth to keep them dry. Rain meant the city forgot to watch.

They had a stall no wider than a door: a plank table, a striped awning patched in three places, and a tray of charms arranged with the careful disorder of a person who wanted to seem harmless. Copper hares for luck in travel. Knots of grey thread against nightmares. Little glass beads that caught the lamplight and held it, so that a child staring into one might believe, for a moment, that the sun still lived somewhere behind all this weather.

Casper sold smallness. That was the whole trick of surviving in Greyhollow—sell nothing the wardens could feel. Their stones hummed when they passed real magic, a low wasp-drone that turned heads, and so Casper's charms were honest work and no more: knots tied tight, copper polished bright, glass warmed in an oven. If a charm worked, it worked because people believed in it, and belief, Casper had learned, was the only magic a city like this permitted.

They were re-threading a hare's ear when the child arrived.

She came down the Row the way stray cats did—pressed close to the walls, quick, expecting to be shooed. Eight years old, maybe nine. A coat too big for her, sleeves rolled four times. She stopped at the edge of the awning's shelter, just where the rain began again, and stared at the tray of charms with an expression Casper recognized the way one recognized one's own face in a dark window. Want, and the certainty of not being allowed it.

"You're letting the rain in," Casper said, not looking up. "Come under. It's free."

The child crept forward like the invitation might be a trap. Up close she was thinner than she'd seemed, with a bruise going yellow-green along her jaw and a smell of smoke about her that wasn't hearth-smoke. Casper had lived in Greyhollow long enough to know what burned in the poor quarters, and how often, and how little anyone came running.

"Looking for something?" Casper asked.

The child's eyes went to the glass beads. Of course. They always did.

"Those are a copper each," Casper said. "But the one with the blue flaw in it—" they lifted it, turned it so the light found the swirl at its heart like smoke caught mid-sigh— "that one's a ha'penny. It's got a crack, see. Nobody wants the flawed ones."

It was not, in fact, a ha'penny bead. It was not, in fact, cracked. But the child dug in her coat and produced a ha'penny worn nearly smooth, and Casper took it with the gravity of a merchant closing a great trade, and pressed the bead into her small cold hand.

"What's it do?" the girl whispered.

"Nothing, if you don't need it," Casper said. "If you do, it holds light. You hold it up to a lamp, fill it, and then when it's dark—when it's very dark—you look into it, and you remember the light was real. That's all a luck-charm is. A memory that refuses to leave."

The girl closed her fist around it. For one moment her face did the thing that made seven years of small charms worth the smallness: it unknotted. She looked, briefly, like a child and not like a survivor.

"Thank you," she said, and then, bolting before gratitude could cost her anything more, she was gone down the Row, into the rain, a scrap of oversized coat swallowed by the grey.

Casper watched her go and felt the old ache, the one they'd gotten good at folding up small and putting away. *You could give her a real charm,* whispered a voice they did not let speak aloud anymore. *You could give her a bead that actually burns. You could—*

*No.* Seven years. Seven years of quiet. The storm they'd called at seventeen had taken a harvest, a bridge, and three lives, and the exile-oath stitched into their skin still itched at the wrist when they so much as hummed with power. They had meant only to shout at the sky. The sky had answered. They had learned, since, to keep their voice at a whisper.

The wardens came at midday, as they always did, two of them in oilskin yellow as old bile, walking the Row with their sniffing-stones cradled like infants. Casper kept their hands moving—thread, knot, thread—and kept their breathing slow, and kept the door in their chest, the one with the storm behind it, shut and shuttered and painted over. The stones passed within arm's reach. One of them gave a faint tick, the sound of a cooling kettle, and Casper's heart performed a small controlled collapse before they realized the stone was only reacting to the warden's own damp charm against fever. The wardens moved on, their boots slapping the wet cobbles, and the market exhaled around them.

*Invisible,* Casper reminded themselves. *Small. Safe.*

The stranger came an hour later, and the rain, which had held itself to a drizzle all morning, chose that moment to fall in earnest, as if the sky had been waiting for its cue.

He came from the direction of the river gate, and he came wrong—staggering, one hand pressed to his side, his cloak dark with more than rain. The market saw him and performed its favorite trick: everyone looked very hard at their own goods. A fishwife turned her back. A tinker suddenly found urgent business in the bottom of his cart. Greyhollow had learned that trouble was a contagion, and the only cure was to be somewhere else when it arrived.

The stranger did not go anywhere else. He came straight down the Row, weaving, and his eyes—grey as the weather, wide with something worse than pain—found Casper's stall and fixed on it as though it were the only lit window in a dark house.

"You," he rasped.

"Friend, I think you want the physicker, two streets—"

He collapsed against the table. The tray of charms jumped; a copper hare fell and rolled into a puddle. His hand shot out and caught Casper's wrist with a strength that had no business being in a dying man, and Casper felt, with a lurch, the heat of him—too much heat, fever and blood-loss and something else, something that prickled along Casper's skin like the air before lightning.

And under that prickling, faint as a heartbeat, the exile-oath on their wrist *itched*.

The man's other hand came up from beneath his cloak. He pressed two things into Casper's palms and folded their fingers shut over them, hard, as if sealing a wound. The first was soft and crackling: a folded map, oilskin-wrapped, damp with blood. The second was metal, cold even through the stranger's fever-heat—a ring, heavy, with a broken face. Casper knew the shape of it without looking. Every soul in Veyland knew the shape of the royal signet, even snapped clean in half.

"Listen," the man said. Blood at his lips, breath whistling. His eyes were not fever-mad; that was the worst of it. They were clear, and terrified, and certain. "The king is dead. Whatever wears his face now—it's counting storm-callers. It has a list. It has *names*." His grip tightened, nails biting Casper's skin. "It found the last of the Thornwater line last month. It's found others. And three days ago it found *you*."

"You're mistaken," Casper said, and their voice came out thin. "I'm a charm-seller. I tie knots. I—"

"He knows your name now, Ashgrove." The stranger smiled, and it was a terrible thing, that smile—pity and apology in equal measure. "Casper Ashgrove, of the Ashgrove storm-line. He knows. And his wardens don't carry sniffing-stones anymore. They carry *your name*, written down, and the stones only tell them where to look."

Sirens, then—no, not sirens. Horns. Three notes from the river gate, the warden-call, the one Casper had heard only twice in seven years and never this close. Down the Row, the yellow oilskins turned. One of them unslung a stone from its oilcloth, and even at forty paces Casper could hear it begin to hum.

The stranger sagged. His hand fell away from Casper's wrist.

"The map," he whispered. "It remembers the way. And Ashgrove—your sister's writing is on it. She's alive. She's in the capital, and she is not free."

Then his eyes rolled back, and he slid from the table into the rain, and Casper was left standing with a dead man's blood on their hands and a broken crown in their fist while the wardens came on through the water, unhurried, certain, like the tide.

Seven years of smallness. Seven years of knots and glass and careful, careful quiet.

It had just ended, and the storm behind the shuttered door in Casper's chest turned over in its sleep, and *smiled*.
