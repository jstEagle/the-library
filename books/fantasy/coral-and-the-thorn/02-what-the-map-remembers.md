## 2. What the Map Remembers

The rain had followed Casper out of Greyhollow, as if the sky itself had decided to finish what the wardens started.

They ran through the orchard lanes behind the market, boots slick with mud, the map crushed against their ribs beneath a soaked coat. Behind them, lantern-light bobbed and split—three wardens, maybe four, their sniffing-stones clicking like beetle jaws as they cast for the taste of magic. Casper had nothing left to hide behind. The charm-stall was ash in their wake, the awning slashed, the little luck-knots scattered in the gutters like dead moths.

*He knows your name now.* The courier's blood was still tacky on Casper's palm. They could not seem to wipe it off.

They cut left at the crooked pear tree, the one they'd climbed as a child in another life, another name, and nearly ran into her.

She stood at the lane's end with a drawn sword and a guard's cloak plastered to her shoulders, and Casper knew her at once—not from Greyhollow, but from seven years of nightmares. The royal livery. The Warden's Office badge. The woman who had been sent to bring them in.

"Storm-caller Ashgrove." Her voice was flat as slate. "By order of the Crown, you're under arrest."

"Then arrest me." Casper's breath came ragged. "But you might want to hurry. Your colleagues are behind me, and I don't think they've been told to take anyone alive."

Something flickered in her eyes—doubt, quick as a fish. She looked past Casper's shoulder at the bobbing lights, then at Casper's empty, rain-washed hands.

"The order said *detain for questioning*," she said slowly.

"The order changed an hour ago. Ask the courier who bled out at my stall."

The lanterns rounded the pear tree. The woman made her choice in the space of a single breath—Casper watched it happen, watched duty and doubt wrestle behind her face and doubt win. She sheathed her sword, seized Casper's collar, and hauled them both into the hedgerow's black throat just as the wardens swept past, their sniffing-stones clicking, clicking, finding nothing in the rain-washed dark.

They didn't speak until the lights had faded toward the river road.

"Wren," she said at last, as if the word cost her. "Formerly of the King's Guard. Currently of nowhere." She pulled a flask from her cloak and drank, then offered it. "I was sent to find you three days ago. I've been deciding what to do about it ever since."

"And you decided on hedgerows."

"I decided on *not* dragging a man to a death he doesn't deserve." She wiped her mouth. "The courier you mention—Tamsin Vale. She carried dispatches from the capital. She was mine. I trained her. When she didn't report in, I came looking." Her jaw tightened. "So. The map. Show me."

* * *

They sheltered in a shepherd's hut on the high pasture above Greyhollow, wind worrying the thatch, and by the light of a shuttered lamp Casper unrolled the map at last.

It was not paper. Or it was not *only* paper. The vellum was warm, faintly, like a sleeping animal, and the ink moved—slowly, the way frost creeps across a window. Roads shifted. Rivers breathed. And through the heart of it ran lines of silver, fine as spider silk, threading from the mountains down through the valleys, converging on the capital like veins feeding a heart.

"Ley-lines," Wren said quietly. "I've seen charts of them. They never looked like this. They never looked *alive*."

"It's remembering," Casper said. They didn't know how they knew; the knowledge arrived the way the storm had arrived seven years ago, uninvited and total. "The courier didn't carry a map. She carried a witness."

As if the words were a key, the vellum shivered—and showed them.

The ink pooled, gathered, rose into a shape: a throne room rendered in miniature, all shadow and gold. A king sat upon the throne, and Casper leaned close, and wished they hadn't. The face was King Aldric's face—the man who had signed Casper's exile with a look of genuine sorrow—but the eyes were wrong. Hollow. Lamps with the fire scraped out. And the mouth moved, though no figure stood near it, and though the map made no sound, Casper *heard* it anyway, in the marrow of their teeth: two voices, braided, one the king's own and one that was old and dry and patient as rot.

*"Bring me the callers. Bring me the storms. The binding is at solstice, and I will never be empty again."*

Casper jerked back. The vision collapsed into ink and roads.

Wren had gone the color of tallow. "That's the king."

"That's the thing wearing the king," Casper said. "Tamsin died getting this to me. She must have known what it was. She must have known *I'd* know."

"Why you? Why a charm-seller in a rain-soaked backwater?"

"Because of who I am. What I am." Casper's hands had begun, traitorously, to tremble. "It's hunting storm-callers. All of us. And there's only one other left that I know of, and she's the reason I learned to hide."

They turned the map over.

And there, in the margins, in a hand Casper would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea—small, slanted letters, pressed hard enough to score the vellum:

*I bought us three days. No more. He's using me to hold the crown together, Casper. I can feel him through it, like a hook behind my ribs. If you're reading this, don't come for me. Come for the storm. — L.*

Lark.

Casper's sister. Alive. And bound.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the wind changed, and somewhere far off, thunder answered it—a low, rolling call, like something vast turning over in its sleep. Casper's skin prickled with answering static, the old gift waking in their blood after seven years of being smothered, starved, denied.

They hadn't called a storm since the one that ruined them.

* * *

Wren stood watch at the hut's door while Casper slept—or lay with eyes closed, which amounted to the same thing. She turned the courier's broken signet ring over in her fingers. The royal seal, snapped clean through. Tamsin had carried it as a badge of office; she'd given it away as a warning.

*He knows your name now.*

Wren had served the Crown for eleven years. She had believed in the Crown the way other people believed in rain—that it fell on everyone equally, that it was simply the weather of the world. Three days ago she'd been handed a warrant and told the target was a traitor. She'd read the warrant twice and noticed what was missing: no charges. No crime named. Only a description, and a name, and the instruction that the arrest be *quiet*.

Quiet orders were never innocent orders.

Behind her, the map rustled on the table like a thing dreaming.

She should turn back. Report her failure, take the demotion, live small and dutiful until duty killed her the way it had killed Tamsin. It would be easy. It would be *safe*, in the way a cell is safe.

Instead she said, to the dark, "The solstice is nine days off. The capital's four days' ride if we take the river road and don't sleep."

A pause. Then Casper's voice, rough with sleeplessness: "You're coming, then."

"I'm deserting, actually. There's a difference, and the difference is that deserting means they'll hang me too." She turned. In the lamplight, the disgraced guard looked younger and more tired than her sword arm suggested. "Tamsin was mine to protect. I failed. I don't intend to fail twice."

Casper studied her for a long moment. Trust, Casper had learned, was a charm that never sold and always cost. But the map had shown a hollow king, and the margins held a sister's handwriting, and the thunder was still muttering on the horizon like a debt come due.

"The map," Casper said. "It showed me the king. I think it shows different things to different people. What did it show you?"

Wren's hand closed around the broken signet. For a moment she didn't answer, and the wind filled the silence with the smell of coming rain.

"It showed me Tamsin," she said finally. "Walking the palace corridors. Alive. Being marched somewhere by men in the king's livery—only it wasn't the king's livery anymore. The crest had been changed." Her voice dropped. "It showed me the solstice altar. And it showed me every ley-line in Veyland bending toward the capital, the way rivers bend toward a drain."

She met Casper's eyes.

"Whatever sits on that throne is drinking the kingdom, Ashgrove. And in nine days, it intends to drink it *forever*."

Far to the south, beyond the hills, the storm that had followed Casper from Greyhollow finally broke—thunder cracking open the sky like a struck bell. And on the table, the map flared silver, every ley-line kindling at once, all of them converging, all of them pointing home.

From the margins, in ink that had not been there a moment before, new letters surfaced, slanted and small and desperate:

*He felt you, Casper. He knows you're coming. Run.*
