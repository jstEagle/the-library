## 3. The Solstice Crown

The capital of Veyland smelled of honey-wine and burning cedar, and beneath both, faint as a bruise, of ozone.

Casper felt it the moment they passed the city gates—a taste on the back of the tongue like the air before lightning. Seven years in Greyhollow had taught them to read weather in a hundred small ways: the ache in old bones, the way gulls went silent, the green-black bruising of clouds over the fells. This was different. This was *held* weather, gathered and caged and made to wait, and it sat over the palace district like a second sky.

"Stop staring," Wren murmured beside them. She wore her old guard's cloak with the crest torn out, leaving only a pale ghost of thread where the sun-and-spear had been. In the festival crowd—everyone masked, everyone laughing, everyone carrying paper lanterns that turned the dusk to drifting embers—she was just another face. "You look like a woman reading her own funeral notice."

"I'm reading the sky."

"The sky's fine."

"The sky is *full*, Wren." Casper pulled their own mask up—plain bone, unadorned, the kind worn by servants and no one worth robbing. Around them, the solstice crowd surged toward the great square where the king would appear at moonrise to light the First Flame. Children ran between legs with sparklers; a fiddler played something old and sweet on a corner; a woman sold candied plums from a tray that steamed in the cold. All of it lovely. All of it a held breath.

The map against Casper's ribs was warm. It had been warm since they crossed the river, and now it pulsed faintly, like a heart counting down. When Casper pressed a hand flat to their chest, they could almost feel it pulling—not north or south, but *up*. Toward the palace on its hill of black stone, where every lantern in the city seemed to lean.

*He knows your name now.* The courier's words, seven days cold. Casper had run from them across three provinces, and here at the end of the road the words had simply been waiting, patient as winter.

They moved with the crowd through streets that climbed in switchbacks, past shrines where people left offerings of salt and ribbon for the turning year. At each shrine, Casper noticed, the salt had gone grey. The ribbons hung dead still, though the night wind moved everything else. Whatever slept in the palace was drinking even the luck out of the holy places.

Wren stopped at the last bend before the servants' gate, where the crowd thinned and the shadows thickened under an aqueduct. "Once we're inside, we don't split up. We find your sister, we find the ritual chamber, and if it goes wrong—" she touched the knife at her hip, the one she'd carried since the day she'd chosen treason over orders, "—we go loud."

"It won't go wrong."

"You said that about the ferry."

"The ferry *mostly* didn't go wrong."

Wren's mouth twitched—the ghost of the smile Casper had spent two weeks learning to read, there and gone like fish under ice. Then her hand found their shoulder, brief and hard, and she said quietly, "Whatever you see in there. Whatever it shows you. It lies with true things. Remember that."

They went through the servants' gate behind a laundry cart, Wren's forged chit trembling only a little in the porter's lantern-light, and the palace swallowed them.

* * *

Inside, the ozone was a living thing.

It crawled along the corridors, sweet and wrong, threading through the smell of beeswax and stone. The walls were hung with solstice garlands—holly, gold thread, dried rowan berries—and every garland within sight of the throne floor had withered to brown. Servants moved through the halls with their eyes down and their steps quick, and none of them sang, though outside the festival noise rose like a tide.

The map led them. Casper had stopped questioning how a piece of bloodstained vellum could know the way; it tugged at their ribs like a hooked fish, left at the third corridor, down the stair that smelled of wet slate, and always, always upward. Twice they hid in alcoves as patrols passed—real guards, royal guards, men and women whose faces Wren knew and who did not look well. Their skin had a waxy cast. Their eyes, when the lantern-light caught them, were the flat grey of rain about to fall and never falling.

"Hollowed," Wren breathed after the second patrol. "All of them. The whole garrison."

"Not all. You're not."

"I was already hollow. Easier to fill an empty thing." She said it lightly, and it wasn't light at all, and Casper reached through the dark and squeezed her wrist once, and she let them.

The map pulled them to a door of black oak banded in silver, and the silver was shaped into chains, and the chains were shaped into words too old to read. Beyond it, Casper heard something that stopped their heart mid-beat.

Humming.

A lullaby. Off-key, the way it had always been off-key, because Marisol could never hold a tune and never once cared. Their sister used to hum it over the wash-basin, over the hearth, over Casper's fevered childhood bed, her voice wandering the melody like a sheep wandering a hillside.

Casper's hands were shaking so badly they could barely work the lock-picks Wren pressed into them. The tumblers gave. The door opened onto starlight.

* * *

The chamber at the top of the tower had no roof. Snow fell gently through it, melting before it landed, and the stars wheeled overhead in their slow solstice turn. In the center of the room stood a pillar of pale stone, and bound to the pillar—

"*Mari.*"

She was alive. That was the first thing, the only thing, a shout inside Casper's chest that drowned out everything else. Alive, and older, hair cropped short and a scar along her jaw that Casper didn't know, and her eyes—her eyes opened at the sound of their voice, and they were *aware*, and they filled with tears.

"Cas." Her voice was rust and honey. "Oh, you stupid, brave, *idiot* boy. You shouldn't have come."

"What did they do to you—"

Chains of silver light ran from her wrists and throat into the pillar, into the floor, into the mountain itself. Where they touched her skin, the light pulsed in time with the ozone-taste, in time with the held storm above the city. Casper understood then, with a sick lurch, what an anchor was. What it cost. Every ward, every hollowed guard, every dead ribbon on every shrine—all of it drew through her.

"I've kept it hungry," Marisol whispered. "Seven years, I've kept it starving. Don't let it feed, Casper. Whatever it offers—"

"*Whatever I offer,*" said a voice from everywhere, "*I keep.*"

The king stepped out of the shadow of the stairwell—or the thing that wore the king. He looked, at first glance, like the man from the coins and the murals: broad, golden-bearded, crowned. But the crown sat slightly wrong, the way a hat sits on a scarecrow, and when he moved, his joints moved a half-second after his body decided to move them. His eyes were the eyes from the map's visions. Hollow-eyed. And his mouth, when it smiled, smiled a half-second late.

Behind him, guards poured onto the roof—six, eight, ten—and among them, wrestled down with a blade knocked skittering across the stone, Wren, blood at her temple, snarling like a caught hawk.

"Wren—"

"Bought you time," she gasped. "Didn't say how much."

The king-thing raised one hand and the guards froze like puppets with cut strings. It regarded Casper with those empty eyes, and when it spoke again, both voices came—one the king's warm baritone, one beneath it, vast and dry, like wind through a dead forest.

"Casper Ashgrove. Stormcaller. Exiled by oath, seven years quiet." It tilted its head, and the crown gleamed. "Do you know why I let the courier live long enough to reach you? Why the map found its way home? A binding needs a willing vessel, little storm. Your sister has fed me scraps for seven years, and I am *so* hungry, and the solstice turns tonight. So here is my bargain, plain as bread."

It gestured, and the silver chains around Marisol dimmed—not released, merely loosened, a mercy shown precisely to be taken away.

"Give me the storm you carry. Give it freely, gladly, and she walks free. Refuse, and I take it anyway at moonrise—along with her life, spent as kindling. One gift buys a life. One theft buys nothing but ash." The late smile widened. "You were exiled for a storm you never meant to call. Here is your chance to mean it."

The whole sky leaned in. Above the open roof, the held weather churned, and Casper felt their own magic answer it—the storm they'd swallowed and starved for seven years, pressing against the walls of their exile-oath like water against a dam.

Marisol shook her head, tears freezing on her cheeks. "Cas. *Cas.* Look at me. It lies with true things. Everything it says will be true except the part that matters."

And it was true—that was the horror of it. The bargain was real. The chains would loosen. She would walk out of this tower alive, and Casper would finally, *finally* pay the debt of the storm they'd never meant to call, and it would cost nothing but the last secret thing they owned. Seven years of invisibility, of charms too small to hang them, of watching the world through other people's fires—one trade, and their sister lived, and they could be empty together, ordinary together, safe.

Love had never offered anything so reasonable.

"Moonrise is in a quarter hour," the king-thing said softly. Both voices, braided. "Choose."

Casper looked at their sister—at the scar they didn't know, the seven years they hadn't been there for, the chains drinking her life drop by drop. They looked at Wren, kneeling bloody in the snow-melt, watching them with eyes that said *remember what it does* and also, impossibly, *whatever you choose, I chose you first.*

Then Casper Ashgrove lifted their head toward the caged and waiting sky, and began, very quietly, to unbind the wrong vow.

The ozone screamed.
