## 4. Stormcaller's Oath

The ritual chamber had been a throne room once. Casper could still see the ghost of it beneath the corruption—the long sweep of marble worn pale by a century of petitioners' feet, the tall windows where stained-glass kings had watched over the court in colors like spilled wine. Now the glass was black. The marble breathed.

That was the worst of it, Casper thought, kneeling in the circle of salt and iron with their sister's name caught like a fishhook behind their ribs. The floor *breathed*. Slow, wet contractions of stone, as if the whole palace were a lung and the thing on the dais its beating heart.

The crown-thing wore King Aldous the way a puppeteer wears gloves. It sat slumped and magnificent on the throne, robes pooling, the crown sunk into its skull to the brow, roots of black gold threading down through the king's temples, his cheeks, his throat. Its eyes were open and empty as two wells.

*You could still say yes,* it said, in the king's voice and in another voice beneath it, a voice like wet gravel turning over, like the sound a riverbed makes when something old shifts in the silt. *One word, stormcaller. Give me the storm, and the girl walks free. You could go back to your little grey town. Your little grey life. No one would ever—*

"No," Casper said.

The word was small. It fell into the vast breathing dark of the chamber and vanished, and for one terrible heartbeat Casper thought it had not been heard at all. Then the crown-thing's head tilted, slow as a ship coming about, and both of those voices laughed together, and the sound made the salt in the circle shiver.

*No?* it said. *You crossed a kingdom for her. You let them take your Wren for her. And now you say no?*

Casper's hands were shaking. They let them shake. Seven years of hiding had taught them that trembling was not the same as weakness—sometimes it was just the body telling the truth the mouth hadn't found yet.

"You want a storm given to you," Casper said. "Willing. Bound. Fed into that crown like wine into a mouth. That's the ritual. That's what all of this was for—the wardens, the hunting, my sister strung up on your ley-lines like a bell to be rung." They looked at her then—Maren, suspended in the web of light above the altar-stone, her eyes closed, her wrists ringed in the same black gold as the crown. Her breathing was slow and even, and Casper held onto the sight of it like a handhold on a cliff. Alive. Still alive. "You want a storm that belongs to someone."

*Yes,* the thing said, and beneath the king's voice the old voice went hungry and soft. *Yes. That is the only kind that lasts.*

"Then I have one to give you," Casper said. "Just not the way you think."

They reached for the exile-oath.

It had lived in them for seven years, a scar of cold iron wrapped around the storm in their chest, the king's own magic spoken by the king's own mouth: *never call, never gather, never be what you are, on pain of unmaking.* They had carried it the way you carry a splinter worked too deep to cut out—around it, carefully, always around it. It had kept them small. It had kept them safe. It had kept them *alive*, which they had told themselves was the same thing.

It was not the same thing.

Casper found the oath's edge the way you find the edge of a scab with your tongue, and they spoke the unbinding words—not the ones the crown-thing wanted, not the ritual of offering, but the older grammar underneath, the one every storm-caller learned first and was made to swear away: *what I called, I recall. What I bound, I unbind. What I am, I am.*

The oath came out of them like a hook coming out of flesh.

They screamed. Everyone screams; no one tells you that part, all the ballads and the fire-side stories, no one tells you that unbinding is not a door opening but a wound, that the magic does not rise gently like dawn but *floods*, seven years of dammed weather breaking all at once through a body that had learned to be a wall. Casper felt their ribs flex around it. Felt their teeth ache with pressure like a coming change in the air. Felt, distantly, the salt circle scorch to glass around their knees.

The crown-thing stood up.

For the first time, it moved like something afraid.

*Stop,* it said, in the king's voice, in the gravel voice, in a dozen voices it stole from the dead wardens and the dead guards and all the storm-callers it had hollowed before Casper—*stop, stop, you don't know what you—*

"I know exactly," Casper said, "what I'm doing."

And they called the storm.

* * *

It was the storm they had been exiled for. That was the cruelty and the rightness of it, both at once. Not a new storm, not a weapon forged fresh for this hour, but the same one—Harrowmere's storm, the one that had come when they were nineteen and grieving and had reached for the sky without meaning to, the one that had torn the roofs off three villages and drowned the king's own orchards and put their name on a banishment scroll written in a hand that had, even then, been going hollow at the edges.

It had never left. It had only been waiting.

It came down through the black glass of the windows like judgment and like mercy, which Casper had learned were often the same thing wearing different coats. Lightning stood in the chamber like pillars. The breathing floor convulsed and cracked, and through the cracks Casper could see the ley-lines beneath the city, the great golden veins of it, and the storm went into them the way blood goes into a starving body.

The crown-thing unraveled.

That was the only word for it. It did not die; it came *undone*, thread by black-gold thread, the stolen voices peeling away from it one by one—each warden, each guard, each caller crying out once, briefly, like notes released from a chord—and the king's voice was the last to go, and it did not scream. It said, in a small and terribly human voice, *oh*, as if waking from a long illness into a room full of light.

Then the crown fell. Just a crown, ringing on the marble, rolling in a slow, drunken circle, and stopping.

The web of light around Maren went out all at once, and she dropped, and Casper caught her—arms full of their sister, their sister, her heart hammering against their own like two birds in one cage—and above them, through the shattered windows, the storm was already gentling, already going soft and ordinary, rain on a ruined city, rain on the festival fires below, rain that smelled like the sea and like iron and like the end of a very long winter.

The magic went out of Casper the way the tide goes out. Not torn. *Drawn.* The storm took its payment as it left, and Casper felt it go—felt the place in their chest where the weather had lived become simply a place, quiet and hollow and clean, a room with the windows finally open.

They sat on the scorched marble, holding Maren, and laughed, or wept, or both, and could not have said which.

* * *

The king woke three days later, in a bed, in a room with ordinary sunlight in it, and the first thing he asked for was a list of every name on every banishment scroll his hand had signed.

Casper was on it. Of course they were.

They expected the old fear when the summons came. They had practiced it for seven years, the flinch, the duck, the small charm slid across a market table with the eyes down. But when they stood in the rebuilt throne room—new glass in the windows, kings in spilled-wine colors watching from the panes—and the king, thin and grey and unmistakably *himself*, said "Casper Ashgrove" aloud, in front of the whole court, in a voice that meant it as a name and not a charge—

—nothing flinched. Nothing hid. Something in them simply stood up straight.

The pardon was read. The storm-caller trials were promised, and reforms, and Wren—standing at Casper's shoulder in a guard's uniform with the disgrace struck from her record and a new scar along her jaw—made a sound at that which might have been a laugh or might have been her swallowing something she'd been carrying for years.

Afterward, they walked out together through the palace gardens, where the rain had left everything shining, and the city below was ringing its bells for the solstice, late and off-rhythm and completely unbothered about it.

"So," Wren said. "No more magic. No more storms. No more charms too small to hang you." She glanced sideways, that crooked half-smile Casper had begun, without noticing, to build their days around. "What exactly is it you plan to be, Ashgrove?"

Casper thought about it. Thought about Greyhollow, and the market, and the frightened child with the luck-charm, and the seven years of smallness that had somehow, without their permission, taught them the shape of the life they'd actually wanted all along.

"Ordinary," they said. And found, to their enormous surprise, that the word fit like a coat that had always been theirs.

Wren laughed—really laughed, head back, bells and rain and all—and took their hand, and they walked down into the city together, where everyone, at last, knew their name.
