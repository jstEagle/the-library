## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The wine was laced with arsenic again.

Edmund knew it the way other men knew rain was coming — a faint metallic whisper at the back of the throat, a ghost of bitter almond that no one else at the table could taste because no one else had spent eleven years teaching his tongue to read poison like scripture. He let the sip sit on his palate for three full seconds. Long enough to be convincing. Short enough to be survivable.

Then he swallowed, set the goblet down with the soft click of crystal on mahogany, and said, "The King's Burgundy is sound tonight."

"Excellent," said Lord Chamberlain Hessler, who never looked directly at Edmund when he spoke to him, as though gratitude were something you aimed slightly off-target. "You may withdraw until the fourth course."

Edmund withdrew. He always withdrew. It was part of the choreography — the taster sipping, the court exhaling, the taster stepping back into the shadow behind the dais where he became furniture again. Eleven years of being furniture that drank.

He stood in the alcove behind the great hall's eastern pillar and did what he did every night of every service: he counted.

Three hundred and six dishes tasted since he'd begun. Three hundred and six measured doses, each one a little heavier than the last, each one a little less likely to kill him. The royal physician — a dry stick named Corvane who smelled of vinegar and disapproval — kept the ledger, and by Corvane's arithmetic Edmund needed thirty-one more tastings before his blood would hold enough immunity to walk out from under the palace roof without an antidote kit strapped to his ribs.

Thirty-one dishes between him and the door.

Because that was the bargain nobody wrote down but everybody understood. A food-taster served until his body was proof against everything a poisoned cup might carry, and only then — *only then* — was he permitted to leave the valley. Amberdale Valley. Sixty miles of river and orchard and limestone hills ringed so tight around the capital that the maps called it a bowl. The old folk had another name for it. They called it the Still Place, and they said it in the hushed voice people used for graveyards, because magic could not follow you across its borders. Wards older than the kingdom itself turned the valley into a dead zone for anything uncanny. Nothing enchanted could enter. Nothing cursed could pursue.

It was, officially, the safest ground in the world.

Unofficially, it was the most elegant cage ever built, because the same wards that kept sorcery out also kept the poisons in — or rather, kept them *quiet*. Every toxin sleeping in Edmund's marrow lay docile inside the valley's borders, held still by whatever ancient law hummed beneath the limestone. Corvane had explained it once, in the flat tone of a man reciting weather. *Your blood is a library of every poison we've fed you. Inside the valley, the books stay shut. Cross the border, and they all open at once.*

So: thirty-one more dishes. Thirty-one more nights of arsenic wine and hemlock broth and the cyanide glaze on the winter pears. And then Edmund Amberdale could pack one bag, walk out through the western pass, and see whether the world beyond the bowl was worth the eleven years he'd spent dying slowly in order to live in it.

"The fourth course," Hessler said, materializing at his elbow. "The quail. Corvane wants a double measure tonight."

*Double measure.* Which meant someone upstairs had heard a rumor, received a threat, dreamed a bad dream. Edmund nodded and rolled up his sleeve for the needle.

Routine. All of it routine. He'd gotten very good at routine. It was the only language longing knew how to speak in this place.

* * *

The riverside tavern was called the Drowned Bell, which was either a joke or a warning, depending on how much ale you'd had. It sat crooked at the water's edge where the Amble bent slow and brown around the pilings, and its floorboards groaned like an old ship in calm weather. Edmund arrived seven minutes early, because arriving early meant he could choose the table by the window and arrange his hands somewhere natural before anyone saw that his hands didn't know how to be natural anymore.

Eleven years of court service had taught him how to eat, drink, bow, and die gracefully if required. It had taught him nothing whatsoever about sitting across from a woman who wasn't serving him dinner.

Coral was already there.

That was the first wrong note — she'd beaten him to his own early arrival, seated at the window table with her fingers ink-stained to the second knuckle, reading a book that had clearly been dead for a long time and was being lovingly resurrected under her hands. She'd split its spine and was sewing it back together right there at the table, needle flashing, thread pulling, a little wooden clamp holding the thing open like a surgeon's retractor. She worked the way Edmund imagined people played music — without watching her own hands.

"You're the taster," she said, without looking up. "You smell like candle smoke and silver polish. Palace silver polish, not the cheap kind."

"That's — yes." Brilliant. "Edmund. Edmund Amberdale."

"I know your name. I bound the guest ledger for the Chamberlain's office two years ago; your name's been in my head ever since. It's a good name. Sounds like honey and regret." Now she looked up, and her eyes were the color of shallow water over coral — which, he would learn much later, was exactly why she'd been given the name, and not the other way around. "Coral Vess. Sit down before you vibrate apart."

He sat. His hands found the table and pretended to belong there.

She closed the book — gently, the way you close a coffin — and he saw the title stamped in flaking gold: *Observations Upon the Covenant of Realms, Anno 1189.* Old. Older than the kingdom's current dynasty. The kind of book that belonged in a locked case, not on a tavern table next to a bread basket.

"Work?" he asked, because it was safer than any of the forty questions actually crowding his tongue.

"My whole life. I mend things other people gave up on." She slid the book aside and poured them both wine from the jug the barmaid had left, unbidden, and Edmund felt the small cold reflex flicker at the back of his throat — *check it first* — before he strangled it. Not here. Not tonight. Tonight he was just a man drinking bad house red with a stranger, and the arsenic in him was asleep, and the valley was quiet, and thirty-one dishes still stood between him and anything resembling freedom.

"To mending things," Coral said, lifting her cup.

"To mending things." He touched his cup to hers and drank, and for one absurd moment he catalogued the vintage out of pure habit — *watered, vinegary, harmless* — and then made himself stop, and discovered he didn't quite know what to do with his mouth when it wasn't analyzing. So he smiled. It came out crooked, and she laughed anyway.

They talked. That was the miracle of it, the thing he kept waiting to collapse and it never did. She told him about the bindery on Tanner's Row, about the widow who paid in pies, about the century-old ledgers that crossed her bench smelling of mildew and secrets. He told her — carefully, in the flattened version he told everyone — that he tasted food for the crown. She looked at him for a long moment with those tide-pool eyes and said, "That's the flattest version of that story I've ever heard," and he laughed, really laughed, for the first time in longer than he wanted to count.

Outside the window, the river moved dark and unhurried. Somewhere upstream, past the last lamplit bridge, the valley walls rose into hills that hid the border passes, and beyond those passes a world he had never once seen was going on without him. He thought about it the way he thought about it every night: not as grief, exactly. Grief would have been simpler. It was more like hunger with nowhere to put itself.

"Can I ask you something strange?" Coral said.

"I collect strange."

She turned her wine cup slowly on the table, watching the candlelight swim in it. "Do you ever feel like this valley is holding its breath? Like the whole bowl of it is waiting for something to happen somewhere else, and we're all just... standing in the pause?"

Edmund opened his mouth to give some easy answer — *it's safe, that's all safety feels like* — and stopped. Because she had just described, precisely, the sensation he'd carried in his chest for eleven years and had never once managed to name. The held breath. The pause. The sense that the Still Place wasn't still because it was peaceful, but because it was *stunned*.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Every day."

Something passed across her face then — not fear, exactly. Recognition, maybe. Or the beginning of it. She reached into her satchel and drew out the old book again, the covenant one, laying it flat under the candle. "I found something today that I keep telling myself is a coincidence," she said. "I've been laughing at it all afternoon. But you have an honest face, so I'm going to show you, and you're going to tell me I'm not mad."

"Coral—"

"Just look."

She opened it to a page near the middle, dense with faded iron-gall script, and turned it toward him. Her finger came down on a single line, and Edmund read it, and the tavern noise fell away from him like a dropped cloth.

*And in the hundredth year of the sundering, when the flame gutters low, a daughter of the shore-rose shall cross the veil unbidden — Coral her name, born under no sign — and with her shall come one whose blood has drunk every death and lived.*

His eyes went to her face. Her name. Her actual name, in ink a century old, in a hand long dead.

"And the date," Coral whispered. She traced lower, to a marginal notation in smaller script. "Look at the date, Edmund."

He looked. The old script recorded it plainly, as prophecy always does — flat, certain, indifferent:

*The appointed meeting: the Drowned Bell, by the river, at evening.*

Tonight.

The candle flames bent sideways. Both of them, at once, as though the room had exhaled — bending toward the window, toward the river, toward something on the far side of the glass. Above their table the air went thin and shimmered, like heat off summer stone, except the season was autumn and the light coming through was wrong — greenish, deep, the color of the underside of leaves.

Coral's hand found his across the table and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

"Edmund," she said, "I don't think it's a coincidence."

Above the river, the veil began to tear.
