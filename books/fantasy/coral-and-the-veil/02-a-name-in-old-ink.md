## 2. A Name in Old Ink

The book arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of someone else's attic.

Coral Vane had repaired four thousand books in her life, and she could read a volume's history the way other people read letters. This one had been stored too dry, shelved too near a chimney, and loved badly — its spine cracked down the center, boards warped, the leather gone the color of old tea. Someone had written *Prophecies* on the first flyleaf in pencil, which was like labeling a cathedral "church."

She cleared her bench, laid out her knives and paste and bone folder, and opened it under the good lamp.

The text inside was hand-inked, a century old at least, in that slanted ecclesiastical script she privately called monk-scream. She worked by touch more than reading, lifting pages free of their broken binding one at a time, and so it wasn't until the third gathering that she actually looked at what she was holding.

*And when the veil begins to fail,* the ink said, *a daughter of neither realm shall stand at the seam. Her name is written in the book itself.*

Below it, in the same hand, as if the scribe had been transcribing rather than inventing:

*Coral Vane. The ninth of October. The riverside tavern, where two candles burn.*

Coral sat very still.

Her name. Her actual name, spelled correctly, which strangers almost never managed — there was always an extra *l*, always a *K*. And beneath it, tonight's date. And beneath *that* — she checked her phone, stupidly, as if the tavern might have changed since this morning — the Ferryman's Rest, table by the window, where Marta set two candles on every table because electricity flickered in that corner and she'd stopped pretending otherwise.

"Okay," Coral said aloud to the empty shop. "Okay, this is a prank."

Except the book was a hundred years old. She'd be able to prove that by Friday; she could date paper in her sleep. And pranksters didn't forge century-old iron gall ink with period-accurate degradation bloom just to mess with a bookbinder who fixed hymnals for the parish.

She laughed anyway. It came out thin.

There were explanations. Coincidence, first and most comforting — somewhere in the world another Coral Vane existed, or had existed, and the scribe had simply recorded her evening plans, and the universe had a sense of humor with terrible timing. Or forgery. Or — and here her mind did the thing it did when she was alone too long with damaged books, the thing that made her friends say *you read too much fantasy* — or the book was exactly what it claimed.

She turned the page.

*A man of the safe valley will cross for her. His body will hold what should kill him. Together they are the hinge; apart they are only names.*

Coral closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a room where something is breathing.

She had a date tonight. First one in eight months, with the quiet royal food-taster who came into the shop every few weeks buying nothing, lingering over her repair shelf, and who had finally — after a year of her dropping increasingly unsubtle hints about the coffee place across the street — asked her to dinner in the tone of a man defusing something.

Edmund Amberdale. A man from the valley. Where, everyone knew, magic couldn't follow, though nobody in the city could explain what that meant, because nobody talked about magic except in the way you talk about weather in a country you've never visited.

She looked at the prophecy again. *His body will hold what should kill him.*

"That's not romantic," she told the book. "That's a cry for help."

But her hands were shaking slightly as she washed the bench, and she caught herself checking the clock four times before five, and when she locked up the shop she took the prophecy with her, wrapped back in its oilcloth, tucked under her arm like a shield she hadn't decided whether to believe in yet.

* * *

The Ferryman's Rest at dusk was all amber glass and river-smell, the water sliding dark and patient past the windows. Edmund was already there when she arrived, sitting straight-backed at the window table with his hands folded, looking — she thought, hanging her coat — like a man at his own trial.

Two candles burned between them. Of course they did.

"You came," he said, and then winced. "I mean — thank you. For coming."

"You sound surprised."

"I budgeted for disappointment." He said it lightly, but she saw the truth underneath it, the way she saw water damage under fresh gilt: this was a man accustomed to things not working out, who had built his whole posture around expecting it.

They ordered. He tasted the wine before she touched hers — a small reflexive motion, lips to glass, a pause — and she remembered, suddenly and vividly, what his job actually was. What went into a royal food-taster, year after year, dish after dish.

"How much poison is in you right now?" she asked, because apparently she was the kind of person who led with that.

Edmund considered the question with more seriousness than most people gave their taxes. "Currently? Arsenic, belladonna derivative, three compounds I'm not cleared to name, and whatever was in the shellfish course yesterday, which I suspect was a test."

"And you're fine."

"I'm becoming fine. That's the point. Forty-one dishes to go." Something moved through his face when he said it — not pride. Longing. "Full immunity. And then I can leave."

"Leave the valley?"

"The valley can't be left. Not really. Magic doesn't follow past the ridge line — that's why the court keeps tasters there, keeps everything there. Whatever's in your blood stays stable inside those borders." He turned his glass a quarter-turn. "Outside, everything I've swallowed becomes what it always should have been. So I drink arsenic for six years toward a freedom I can't use until the last drop finishes curing me."

The candle flames bent sideways.

Not guttered — bent. Both of them, together, leaning toward the window as if the river had exhaled, though the air in the room was still and warm and smelled of bread. Marta, passing with a tray, frowned at them without stopping.

"Huh," Coral said.

"Coral." Edmund's voice had changed. Lower. Careful. "Your candle—"

"I see it."

The flames leaned further. Then further than fire should be able to lean, stretching like taffy, like fingers, both tongues of light straining toward a point above the table where the air had begun to look wrong — not torn, not yet, but *thin*, the way old vellum thins before it splits, and Coral thought absurdly: *I could stabilize that. If it were paper. If it were anything I understood.*

She reached into her bag instead and pulled out the oilcloth bundle, because some part of her had already done the arithmetic, had already matched tonight against the flyleaf, and her hands wanted proof even while the rest of her wanted to run.

She unwrapped the book. Laid it open on the table between the plates, between the bending candles.

*The ninth of October. The riverside tavern, where two candles burn.*

Edmund read it. She watched him read it — watched the color leave his face in stages, watched him glance from the page to her face to the page again, doing his own arithmetic, arriving at the same sum.

"When did you find this?" he whispered.

"Today. It came in for rebinding." Her voice sounded far away to her. "It's a hundred years old, Edmund. It's a hundred years old and it has my name in it and tonight's date and—" She stopped. *A man of the safe valley.* "And possibly yours."

Above the table, the thin place in the air split.

There was no sound. That was the worst part — no thunderclap, no scream, nothing the ear could hold onto. Just a seam opening like an eye, a vertical darkness edged in light that was not any light the tavern's lamps could make, and through it came a smell she would remember for the rest of her life: cold stone and smoke and something vegetal and vast, a forest breathing through a keyhole.

Every candle in the Ferryman's Rest went out except theirs, and theirs stood up straight now, tall and rigid, burning blue.

"Coral." Edmund was on his feet. He had her wrist. His grip was stronger than she'd expected and trembling harder than she'd expected, and she realized with strange clarity that he knew what this was — or half-knew — that the valley boy had grown up on stories the city never told. "Don't look at it. Don't—"

"It's looking at me," she said.

And it was. The seam had no eyes, and it was looking at her, and the attention pressing down on her felt like a hand on the back of the neck, gentle and absolute, the way a librarian holds a book she intends to keep.

The pull came next. Not violent — worse than violent, *patient*, a gravity with intent behind it. Her chair scraped. Her feet left the floor an inch, two inches, and Edmund hauled her back down and held on and shouted something — her name, probably, or Marta's, or a word from the valley — and the whole tavern was screaming and running and none of it mattered, none of it touched them, because the seam had chosen its table and its two candles and its name written in old ink.

"The book," Coral gasped. She didn't know why it mattered. She knew exactly why it mattered. You don't abandon a text mid-sentence. "Take the—"

The pull became a verdict.

The world folded along a crease she couldn't see, and the tavern — the amber glass, the bread smell, Edmund's hand crushing her wrist, Edmund's face, white and furious and *afraid for her* — tore away like a page.

The last thing she heard was not a shout but a promise, low and wrecked:

"I'll find you. I swear it — I'll cross."

Then cold stone, and smoke, and a forest breathing in the dark, and a voice — many voices, hollow and reverent and wrong — saying words she already knew by heart:

*"The Reforger. The Reforger has come."*
