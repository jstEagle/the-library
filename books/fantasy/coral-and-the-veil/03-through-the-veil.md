## 3. Through the Veil

Falling, Coral decided, was a great deal like being unmade.

Not the falling of dreams — that slow stomach-drop with a soft landing waiting at the bottom. This was falling in every direction at once, as if the world had been folded like one of her book signatures and she'd been slipped between the pages. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, a pale gold that had no source. Sound arrived before its cause. She could taste her own name.

She thought, absurdly, of the glue pot she'd left warming on her workbench. Of the Aldermoor folio due Friday. Of Edmund Amberdale's face across the table, caught mid-word when the candles bent sideways, his expression flickering from confusion to alarm to something worse — recognition — right before the air above their table split like a seam under a too-hot iron.

*Don't,* he'd said. Or maybe she'd said it. Maybe they'd both said it, reaching across the little tavern table for each other's hands like two people who had known each other longer than one dinner, and then the light had taken her and the world closed up like water over a stone.

Now there was only the fall, and the strange conviction — calm, clinical, terrifying — that whatever was pulling her had been pulling for a very long time. A hundred years, said a voice that wasn't a voice, threaded through the light. *A hundred years we have waited, Reforger.*

*I'm a bookbinder,* she wanted to say. *I mend broken spines. That's all I mend.*

The light didn't answer. The light just kept falling with her, and somewhere below — below having no meaning here, but she felt it anyway, directional as hunger — something vast was breathing out warmth that smelled of woodsmoke and rain on hot stone.

Then ground. Abrupt, ungentle, real.

* * *

She landed badly, shoulder-first into wet moss, and lay there a moment taking inventory the way you do after a fall: fingers, toes, ribs, skull. All present. All complaining. Above her stood trees unlike any she'd catalogued in the valley's modest woods — trunks wide as cottages, bark silvered and peeling like old vellum, and every leaf on every branch gone the color of ash. Not autumn-ash. Dead-ash. This forest hadn't turned; it had given up.

The silence was wrong too. No birdsong. No insects. Just a low, arrhythmic pulse beneath the soil, slow as a failing heart, which she felt through her palms before she heard it at all.

Coral got to her knees. Got to her feet. Checked her pockets by reflex — needle case, bone folder, the little brass awl she'd carried since apprenticeship. Her hands needed something to do, and if there was nothing to fix yet, there was at least something to hold.

"Right," she said aloud, because her voice worked, and hearing it steadied her. "You've fallen through a hole in the world. Scream now or scream later."

She chose later. Later seemed safer.

Ahead, through the dead trees, light flickered — orange, guttering, alive. She walked toward it because the alternative was standing still in a dying forest listening to the ground breathe, and she had never once in her life chosen standing still.

The braziers appeared first: great stone bowls taller than she was, spaced along what might once have been a road, each cradling a tongue of flame so thin it barely qualified as fire. They burned without fuel. They burned the way a candle burns after the wax is gone — on memory, on stubbornness, on last breaths. And hunched around the nearest brazier stood three figures in grey robes, watching the flame as if it were the only thing left worth watching.

They turned when she crunched onto the gravel path. Their faces stopped her cold.

Not monstrous. Worse than monstrous: hollow. Eyes like rooms long vacated, skin stretched over cheekbones the way parchment stretches over a frame — taut, deliberate, maintained. They looked at her with an intensity that had nothing warm in it. Hunger, maybe. Or hope. With faces like theirs, the two were hard to tell apart.

"You came," said the tallest, and his voice cracked like a spine bending past its limit. "Through the failing place. You came."

"I fell," Coral corrected. "There's a difference, and I'd thank you to note it."

The three wardens — she knew somehow they were wardens, the word arriving in her head the way names do, unbidden and sticky — exchanged a look that carried whole paragraphs.

"She doesn't know," whispered the small one.

"Then we tell her." The tall one stepped forward and bowed, shallow and stiff, a gesture practiced so long ago it had worn smooth of meaning. "Welcome, Coral of the valley. You are the Reforger. Your coming was written. We have waited a hundred years for your name to wake."

"My name," Coral said slowly, "was asleep?"

"In a manner of speaking." He gestured, and one of the others produced a book.

Everything in her lurched at once — professional recognition first (calfskin, sewn boards, a binding style three centuries out of date, done by someone who *loved* this work), then personal horror, because she knew the hand inside it. She'd know it anywhere. It was hers. Her handwriting, down to the way her 'r's kicked. Except she hadn't written it. Couldn't have. The ink was older than her grandmother.

The warden opened it to a marked page and read: "*When dragon-fire gutters and the veil wears thin as old cloth, the binder shall come whose name is written in the waking world. Coral, mender of broken things. She shall reforge the covenant and bind the realms anew.*"

The forest breathed its slow, sick pulse. The brazier-flame leaned toward her like a listener.

"That book," Coral said, keeping her voice level with effort, "is bound in my style. Written in my hand. And it predates me by a century. So either someone is playing an extraordinarily elaborate joke, or—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Who wrote it?"

"We did not write it," the tall warden said. "It was found. In the ruins of the covenant-stone, the day it shattered. The prophecy came *through*, along with everything else that has leaked between the realms since. It waited for you the way the veil waits to fail."

"The veil." She latched onto the term like a handhold. "The thing I fell through."

"The covenant held the two realms apart but joined — separate worlds, one border, sealed in harmony. A hundred years ago it broke. Not wore. Broke." His hollow eyes didn't blink. "Since then the border has decayed. Thin places. Tears. Leaks. Tonight it failed badly enough to reach across and take you, exactly as written. And when it fails entirely—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. She looked at the ash-leaved trees, the starved fire, the breathing ground, and understood that she wasn't looking at a dying forest. She was looking at a dying *world*, and through the thinning veil, another one dying with it.

"How long?" she asked.

The smallest warden held up her hand. Between thumb and forefinger: a gap the width of a coin.

* * *

They walked her through the ruin while the light died — not sunset-dying, just dying, the sky dimming like an ember going out. She saw where the covenant-stone had stood: a crater ringed with fused glass, a hundred years old and still too wounded for moss. She saw villages of empty houses, doors left open, tables set for meals a century cold. She saw the braziers, dozens of them, strung across the landscape like stitches in a wound, each holding its scrap of stolen fire against the dark.

And at the center of everything, half-buried in the hillside above the crater, she saw the dragon.

Or heard it first — that warmth-breath she'd felt during the fall, magnified now into something seismic. Ashveil, the wardens called it, and would say little else. Vast beyond sense, coiled around the mountain's roots, scales the color of cooled lava. From its nostrils rose a single thread of flame, thin as the brazier-fires, feeding them all. The last fire. The guttering heart of a whole realm.

"Its flame sustained the veil," the tall warden said quietly. "Now it sustains only itself. When it goes out—"

"Both realms collapse together. Yes. You mentioned." Coral hugged her arms around herself. The cold coming off that hillside wasn't weather-cold. It was absence-cold. "And you want me to reforge your covenant. Fix your stone. Mend your world."

"We ask," the warden said, with the careful gentleness of a man handling something fragile. "We do not command. The prophecy chose you."

Something in that sentence — the smoothness of it, the *we ask, we do not command* delivered by three starving wardens with hollow eyes and a hundred years of patience — snagged on her like a burr.

"No," Coral said.

The word dropped into the clearing like a stone into a well.

"No?" echoed the small warden.

"No. I'll think about it. Those are different things." She turned to face all three of them, and found, to her surprise, that she wasn't afraid anymore — or rather, that the fear had compacted into something denser and more useful. "I've been *chosen* before. Apprenticed at eleven because a master said I had the gift. Promised to the guild at nineteen because the guild said I owed them. Everyone always so certain about what Coral is for." She jabbed a finger toward the buried dragon, the crater, the book. "I don't reforge anything until I know who broke that stone. Covenants don't shatter on their own. Someone broke it. I want to know who, and why, and what they got out of it — before I hand my life over to fixing their mess."

The wardens went very still. Stillness, she was learning, was what they did instead of flinching.

"The records are sealed," the tall one said at last. "The breaking is... not spoken of."

"Then unseal them. Un-speak them. Because I promise you—" and here she stepped closer, close enough to see that even hollow eyes could widen, "—if you think I'll bind two worlds together on faith and a book with my handwriting in it, you've pulled through the wrong girl. I mend things for a living. First rule of the craft: find what actually tore, or your repair won't hold. Something tore that stone. I'll know what before I touch it."

For a long moment, nobody spoke. The brazier-fire leaned. Far up the mountainside, Ashveil exhaled, and the thread of flame above its nostrils trembled like a candle in a doorway.

Then the small warden laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge remembering movement.

"Oh," she said, to no one in particular, to the forest, perhaps to a hundred years of waiting. "Oh, she's going to be *trouble*."

Behind them, back along the road through the dead trees, in the direction of the thinnest place in the veil — the direction, though Coral couldn't have said how she knew it, of a riverside tavern and a dinner interrupted — the air shimmered.

Someone, it seemed, had followed her through.
