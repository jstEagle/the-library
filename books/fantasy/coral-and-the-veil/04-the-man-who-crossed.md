## 4. The Man Who Crossed

The valley had a smell, and Edmund had never noticed it until the night he decided to leave.

Bread and river water and woodsmoke and the green rot of the millpond — the ordinary perfume of a place that kept its people like a cellar keeps wine: cool, dark, and sealed. He'd breathed it for thirty-one years without once thinking of it as a wall. Now, standing at the northern ford with a satchel on his shoulder and his heart going like a rabbit's, he could think of nothing else.

The valley was where magic couldn't follow. That was the whole of its gift and the whole of its joke. Nothing enchanted crossed the ridgeline — no curses, no blessings, no prophecy, no veil. And nothing of Edmund's strange half-life worked beyond it either, because his immunity wasn't immunity at all. It was chemistry. It was a hundred poisons metabolized into something like armor, an equilibrium so delicate that a physician had once described it to him as *a house of cards in a windless room*.

Outside the valley, the wind picked up.

He knew what happened to tasters who left. There had been one, before him — Master Corvin, who'd grown old and homesick and convinced himself that twenty years of small doses had made him safe anywhere. He'd walked out through the southern pass on a Tuesday. The mule drivers found him Wednesday, three miles down the road, dead with no mark on him, every poison in his blood having remembered all at once what it was made to do.

Edmund had read the report twice. He'd been fourteen. It was the first time he understood that his profession came with a horizon he was never meant to cross.

And now here he stood at the edge of it, because a woman he'd known for one evening had fallen out of the world through a hole above their dinner table.

"Coral," he said aloud, just to hear the name in open air. It sounded smaller out here. Everything did.

"You're the taster."

The voice came from the alder thicket at the ford's edge, and Edmund's hand went to the knife he carried more from habit than hope. A man unfolded from the shadows — big through the shoulders, wrong through the middle, the way men get when they've been hungry in a way food doesn't fix. Grey stubble. Eyes the color of pond ice. A warden's grey cloak, but the sigil on the clasp had been scratched out with something sharp and patient.

"I'm Brack," the man said. "I was told you'd be here. I was told you'd be stupid enough to come." He looked Edmund over the way a farrier looks at a horse he's being asked to save. "You don't look stupid."

"Appearances," Edmund said, "can be laced with arsenic."

Something that might have been respect moved across Brack's face and was gone. "You know what crossing does to you."

"I know what it did to Corvin."

"Then you know you have perhaps an hour past the ridge before your blood turns on you. Less if you run. Fear speeds it — the heart pumps faster, everything moves quicker." Brack said it flatly, a man reciting mileage. "So we won't run. We'll walk. And when we reach the other side, there is a thing I can give you that will hold you together long enough to matter. After that—" He shrugged. "After that you're a dead man walking, and how long the walk takes depends on why you're walking."

"Why are you helping me?" Edmund asked. "You're a deserter. You scratched off your own badge. Why care whether a food-taster lives or dies?"

Brack was quiet for a moment. Downstream, the ford muttered over its stones.

"Because I helped break it," he said finally. "The covenant. A hundred years ago, my order shattered the bond between the realms, and they've been drinking the leak ever since — the wardens feed on the decay of the veil, do you understand? Every year it thins, they live longer. Every year it thins, their realm dies faster. And now it's failing altogether, and the girl your king's cooks never fed you news about has been named to fix what we broke." His jaw worked. "I put her name in that book, taster. A century ago, I wrote the ink. So yes. I'll walk you across. It's the least cowardly thing I've done in a hundred years, which is a low bar, but a man takes what's offered."

* * *

They crossed the ford at moonrise.

Edmund had imagined this moment his whole life — the far bank, the ridge, the world beyond — and in every imagining he'd been free. Nobody had ever imagined it like this: stepping over the boundary not toward freedom but away from it, carrying his own death in his veins like coins in a thief's pocket, each one waiting for the chance to spend itself.

The water went over his boots. Cold. Ordinary. On the far bank, Brack stopped and turned.

"Last chance to be sensible," the old warden said. "Go home. Drink your little doses. Live to eighty in a cage with a very educated tongue."

"The woman I'm following," Edmund said, "laughed at my jokes for two hours and then got pulled out of reality by a hole in the air. I had one date with her. One. And I've spent every hour since understanding that I would rather die on the wrong side of that ridge than live to eighty wondering." He heard his own voice and was surprised by it — steady, unpoisoned, true. "That's not courage. I want to be clear about that. Courage implies options."

Brack snorted. "Come on, then."

They climbed. The ridge path switchbacked through pine and scree, and with every hundred feet of altitude Edmund felt it begin — faint at first, like a tuning fork touched to his teeth. A hum in the blood. The poisons waking up. Not attacking yet; the valley's quiet still clung to him like warmth from a fire. But they knew. Somewhere below his skin, a hundred lethal guests stirred in their sleep and began, slowly, to remember their names.

His hands started shaking at the treeline. By the saddle of the pass, sweat had soaked his shirt despite the cold, and his vision had gone soft at the edges, the stars smearing like wet ink.

"How long?" he managed.

"Twenty minutes to the crossing point. You'll make it." Brack took his arm without asking — a grip like a manacle — and steered him up the last stretch of scree. "Talk to me. Talking keeps the heart even. Tell me about her."

"She binds books," Edmund said. His teeth wanted to chatter; he wouldn't let them. "Antiquarian work. She re-sews broken spines. She said — God, she said most people think restoration is making a book look new, but it isn't, it's making it able to be read again. She holds a four-hundred-year-old binding like it's a sleeping animal." He laughed, and the laugh cost him something. "I sat there across the table thinking, *this is the most interesting person I have ever met*, and then the candles bent sideways and she was gone, and I realized I hadn't even learned how she takes her tea."

"What is it?"

"Honey and too much cinnamon. She puts cinnamon in everything. She had it in the wine. I tasted it under the—"

He stopped. Under the arsenic. Under the nightshade. Under the six other things he'd swallowed that evening out of thirty years of habit, tasting each one, naming each one, while she talked about bookbinder's glue and laughed with her whole face.

Brack said nothing. They crested the pass.

Beyond it, the world was wrong.

Not dramatically — that was the horror of it. The same moon, the same kind of pines. But the dark between the trees had depth to it, a listening quality, and far off across the black country something glowed: a dull red pulse beneath the horizon, regular as breathing. Dragon-fire, guttering. Edmund could feel it through the soles of his boots, a heartbeat that wasn't his, slowing.

"There," Brack said, and pointed to a seam of light hanging in the air between two boulders — a vertical shimmer, thin as a scar. The tear. The wound in the veil that Coral had been pulled through, still unhealed, still leaking.

"If I step through that," Edmund said, "everything in me wakes up."

"Yes."

"And the thing you can give me?"

"A warden's draught. It won't save you. It will suspend you — hold the poisons in suspension the way frost holds a river. Hours, maybe a day. Enough time to reach the forest. Enough time to matter." Brack's eyes were on the seam, and there was something raw in his face, some old grief worn smooth by handling. "I crossed this line myself, once, running the other way. I left a daughter behind in that forest. The veil took the road home with it when it broke. I have spent a hundred years being unable to go back, and now the way is open for exactly as long as it takes a dying dragon to finish dying." He exhaled. "So. Taster. Last words?"

Edmund thought about it. Genuinely considered his last words, standing at the border of the only world he'd ever known, shaking with cold and poison and the specific terror of a man about to do the one thing his entire life had trained him never to do.

"Tell them I'm coming," he said. "Tell her I'm coming. Not to save her — she doesn't need saving, she needs help, there's a difference and she'd want me to know it. Tell her the tea order can wait."

Brack held out a small iron flask. "Drink. All of it. It tastes like a churchyard."

It tasted worse than a churchyard. It tasted like the inside of a lock, and Edmund drank it to the dregs anyway, and felt the storm in his blood go suddenly, unnaturally still — a held breath, a suspended sentence.

Then he stepped through the seam.

The crossing took less than a second and lasted a year. Light without color. Pressure without weight. And underneath it all, faint and vast, a voice that was not a voice — the covenant itself, broken and bleeding, recognizing him the way a body recognizes a splinter:

*What are you?*

"Nobody," Edmund said, into the roar. "I'm nobody. I taste things for a living. And I'm coming through."

On the far side, he fell to his knees in ash-grey grass beneath trees whose leaves were the wrong color for any season, in a forest that smelled of smoke and old sorrow. Above him, stone braziers burned with flames that guttered like candle-ends in a draft, and figures in grey were already moving out of the dark toward him — hollow-eyed, robed, wrong.

And rising over the treetops, vast against the bruised sky, a shape of ember and shadow lifted its head and looked at him with eyes like the last coals of a fire that had once warmed two worlds.

Brack landed beside him, drew a breath that shook, and said, quietly, to the darkness and the dragon and the dying realm both:

"We're here. And I've brought you the anchor's man."
