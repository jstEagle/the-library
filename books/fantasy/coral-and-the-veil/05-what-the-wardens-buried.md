## 5. What the Wardens Buried

Coral had been in the dying forest for six days when she stopped being polite about it.

"You're lying," she said to Warden Ilyen, the one with the hollow eyes who brought her bread and water and assurances that everything was being handled. "Or you're wrong. One of those. Which?"

Ilyen set down the tray on the stone ledge of her cell — not a dungeon, they kept insisting, a *guesthouse*, though it had no door and the windows were carved with wards that made her teeth ache — and folded his hands the way a man does when he has rehearsed patience.

"The covenant broke," he said. "A century ago. It happens."

"Things don't just happen," Coral said. "I'm a bookbinder. I know what things look like when they break and what they look like when they're broken." She crossed the small room and put her finger against the ward-carved window frame, where the stone had been scored deliberately, deeply, with lines that did not match the rest of the carving. "This is the difference between a spine that cracks from use and one that's been cut with a blade. Someone did this."

Ilyen's hands went very still.

"And here's what else I know," she continued, because six days of politeness had bought her exactly nothing and she was done spending it. "The book that named me is a hundred years old. The veil started failing a hundred years ago. Your wardens have been *waiting* a hundred years — you knew I was coming, or someone like me, and you built a guesthouse with no doors and taught yourselves to say 'everything is being handled' without flinching." She stepped back. "So either tell me the truth, or find someone who will, because I am not going to stand in this room and be managed like a shipment of rare paper."

For a long moment Ilyen said nothing. The guttering light of a brazier outside moved across his face, and Coral saw something shift there — not guilt exactly, but its exhausted cousin.

"Ask Ashveil," he said finally. "If you want truth. But understand what you're asking for. Truth is the one thing we buried deeper than the covenant itself."

He left the tray. He did not look back.

* * *

Edmund had never been cold before.

Not truly. The valley kept a mild climate the way a good housekeeper keeps linens — everything temperate, everything tended — and Edmund had lived his whole life inside that temperature-controlled lie. Now he sat shivering against a fallen tree at the forest's edge while Brack built a fire that burned green-gold and gave off almost no heat, and understood with a lurch that the valley hadn't just been safe. It had been *kept*. A terrarium. And he was out of it now, and every poison in his blood knew it.

"It gets worse before it doesn't," Brack said, crouching by the fire. "Your body's been holding all that poison quiet because the valley holds it quiet. Out here, everything wakes up. The arsenic first, usually. Then the heavier metals. You'll feel them looking for somewhere to go."

"Wonderful," Edmund managed. His teeth chattered around it.

"I told you what crossing would cost."

"You told me. I crossed anyway. That was the whole point." He pulled his coat tighter. "Where is she? Coral."

"Close. The wardens have her in the old hall — they'll call it protective custody because wardens never call anything what it is." Brack fed a stick into the fire and didn't watch it burn; he watched the darkness instead, the way a man watches a room where he used to live. "You should know something before we go further. Before you meet her again. Something I've carried forty years."

Edmund waited.

"I was on the council once. Junior seat. I was there the night they decided." Brack's voice came out flat, sanded smooth by decades of saying nothing. "The covenant wasn't failing, taster. Not then. It was fraying — a loose thread here and there, the kind of thing a Reforger could have mended with a season's work. And the council looked at that fraying thread and saw what it could become. The veil decays, and as it decays, it *leaks*. Life leaks through it. Years. Vigor. The council learned to drink what came through, and they liked the taste, and so instead of mending the thread—" He turned his hand over, palm up, an old man's gesture of helplessness. "They cut it."

The cold in Edmund's bones found a new register entirely. "They broke it. On purpose."

"They voted nine to two. I raised my hand with the seven cowards and the two fools, and I have been walking away from that table ever since, and I have never once arrived anywhere." Brack looked at him at last, and his eyes were terrible and clear. "You asked me why a deserter would guide a stranger across the veil. That's why. I'm done arriving nowhere."

Above the trees, distant, something vast exhaled a plume of ember-light, and the night briefly turned the color of cooling iron.

* * *

They let her out on the seventh day. Or perhaps simply stopped pretending to keep her — the distinction felt important to Coral, and she suspected the wardens understood it too.

Brack came for her himself, which surprised her until she saw Edmund behind him, gray-faced and shaking, wrapped in a coat far too heavy for him, leaning on a staff cut from valley-wood like a man leaning on the last argument he had left.

"You came through," she said. Stupid thing to say. Her voice cracked on it anyway.

"You left through," Edmund said. "Seemed fair to return the favor."

She crossed the distance between them fast enough that dignity lost the race entirely, and held onto him, and felt how much he was trembling, and held on harder. "You're poisoned. Outside the valley—"

"Everything's waking up. Brack says I'm a walking apothecary's nightmare now." He laughed, and it was a bad laugh, thin as old paper. "Turns out my life's work was also a countdown. Who knew."

"I knew," Brack said dryly. "It's why I needed him. Come on. There's someone who has to speak with both of you, and she's tired of waiting, and she is considerably older than either of us and entitled to her impatience."

They walked into the heart of the dying forest, and the dying forest walked them deeper, and at the center of it stood a ring of stones the size of houses, and within the ring, coiled around a brazier the size of a chapel, lay the last dragon in either world.

Ashveil was smaller than Coral had somehow expected and larger than anything she had ever seen — the way a candle flame is small and fire is not. Her scales were the gray of ash over embers, and beneath them, faintly, light moved, like breath under a blanket. When she lifted her head, the air pressure changed. When she spoke, it was in a voice like the last heat leaving a hearth at dawn: low, patient, and unbearably sad.

"So," said Ashveil. "The Reforger. And the anchor's man. I wondered if the book had gotten it right. Books usually do. It's people who get them wrong."

"You knew my name," Coral said. "A hundred years ago. Before I existed."

"I wrote part of it. The binding was mine — the covenant, I mean. My fire sealed it, once. Two realms, one promise, each keeping the other honest." The great head lowered, eye level with her, an eye like a coal going gently out. "And then the council decided honesty was expensive."

"They broke it," Edmund said. "Deliberately. To feed on it."

"To farm it." Ashveil's exhale stirred the dead leaves across half the clearing. "Do you understand what the veil's decay gives them, little binder? Every year the barrier thins, and every year a little more of what lies between worlds bleeds through — vitality, duration, the raw stuff of continuing. Mortal creatures were never meant to hold it. The wardens drink it anyway. They have not aged a day in a hundred years. Their hall is warm in winter and cool in summer and nothing in it ever dies, and every year they renew their little pact and call themselves guardians of the balance they murdered."

The fury came up through Coral like water rising in a well — not hot, but vast, and very cold at the bottom. She thought of the prophecy book, her own name in old ink, a girl chosen before her birth to clean up a crime. She thought of Ilyen's rehearsed patience. *Everything is being handled.* Yes. It had been. For a hundred years, beautifully.

"Why keep me alive?" she asked. "If the rite needs an anchor, and the reforging ends the leak—"

"Because they don't intend for you to reforge anything," Ashveil said. "They intend for you to *renew*. The rite can be turned, little binder. Spoken backward. Instead of sealing the covenant anew, it can bind the veil open — a wound kept deliberately unhealed, with a mortal anchor holding it wide. You would stand in their hall for eternity, keeping their meal flowing. They've been preparing the ceremony since before your grandmother was born."

The clearing was silent except for the gutter of the great brazier and, very faintly, Edmund's breathing — quick, shallow, the sound of poison looking for purchase.

"They need my consent," Coral said slowly. "The rite needs willingness. That's why the guesthouse, the courtesy, the *handling*. They can't force me."

"They cannot," Ashveil agreed. "But they have a hundred years of practice at persuasion, and you have been here eight days, and your anchor's man is dying by inches now that he's left his valley. Desperate people consent to remarkable things." The dragon's gaze slid to Edmund, and something ancient and gentle moved in it. "As do loyal ones."

"Then we don't consent," Edmund said. "To any of it. Either version."

"You misunderstand me, taster. I am not warning you away from the rite. I am telling you the shape of the board." Ashveil rose, unfolding, and the light beneath her scales brightened like a fire remembering itself. "There are days left. Perhaps three. When my flame gutters out, the veil fails entirely, and both realms come down like badly joined books — spines split, pages scattering, everything that was bound falling apart at once. The council means to be anchored and fed when that hour comes. You mean to stop them. And between you and stopping them stands a hall full of undying liars who have already killed for less than this."

She lowered her great head until her breath warmed Coral's face — breath that smelled of woodsmoke and, underneath it, incredibly, of some flowering tree from a spring a thousand years gone.

"So decide quickly," said the last dragon, in a voice like cooling embers, "what you are willing to burn."

Far off, across the black trees, lights were moving. Torches. Many torches, converging on the ring of stones, and ahead of them, faint on the wind, came the sound of the council's horns — calling everyone home.
