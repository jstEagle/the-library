## 6. The Guttering Flame

The rite was supposed to be simple. That was the first lie.

The wardens' oldest texts — the ones Coral had pried out of Brack's reluctant hands, the ones the council didn't know she'd copied — described it in language so plain it felt like an insult. *Stand at the heartfire. Speak the names of both realms. Open your hands.* A hundred years of collapse, two worlds sliding toward each other like continental plates, and the fix fit on a recipe card.

She'd been at the heartfire since before dawn, if dawn meant anything in a forest where the light came down gray and thin as dishwater. The stone basin sat in a clearing at the forest's center, wide as a millpond, and in it burned what remained of Ashveil's fire — not flames anymore, exactly, but a slow amber breathing, like coals remembering they'd once been a bonfire. Three days ago, Coral had been able to feel its heat from thirty feet away. Now she could stand at the rim and only the faintest warmth touched her face, the way you might feel a fever through a door.

*Speak the names of both realms.*

That part she knew. She'd spent three days learning them from a warden named Ilenne who spoke them like apologies — the valley's name, which had no translation because it needed none, and the other realm's name, which was less a word than a chord, and which made Coral's back teeth ache every time she tried to shape it. She'd practiced until her mouth bled a little. She'd stood in her rented room above the warden barracks whispering the chord into her pillow like a prayer, like a spell, like a girl practicing saying something true.

*Open your hands.*

She opened them now, over the guttering heartfire, and spoke both names into the gray morning.

Nothing happened.

Not nothing-nothing. The coals stirred, lifted, reached for her — she felt that reach like a hand brushing hers across a crowded room, desperate and brief — and then they fell back, guttering, and the silence closed over the clearing again, thick as river mud.

Coral stood there with her arms out over a dying fire and felt something in her chest crack along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"No," she said. Out loud. To the fire, to the forest, to whatever cosmic clerk had written her name in a book a century ago and then neglected to include instructions. "No, you don't get to do this. You chose me. You don't get to choose me and then have me be wrong."

The fire breathed its small amber breath and did not answer. It had never answered. That was the thing about being chosen, she was learning: nobody asked you anything back.

She tried again. And again. By the fourth attempt her voice had gone raw on the chord-name and the coals barely flickered, and by the sixth she was kneeling at the basin's rim with her forehead against cold stone, saying the names over and over in a cracked whisper, the way Edmund must have sipped poison all those years — not because it worked, but because stopping would mean admitting what it cost.

It was Ilenne who finally pulled her back. The old warden's hands were cool on Coral's shoulders, and when Coral looked up, Ilenne wasn't looking at her with pity, which somehow made it worse. She was looking at her with recognition.

"It needs more than a name," Ilenne said quietly. "I told the council it would. They never listen. They stopped listening a hundred years ago."

"Then tell me what it needs." Coral heard her own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "Tell me, or I'll start assuming you're all lying about everything, and honestly? The evidence is stacking up."

Ilenne didn't flinch. She crouched beside the basin, and for a long moment she just watched the coals breathe, and Coral watched her watch them, and understood that the old woman was deciding how much truth a stranger could carry.

"The covenant isn't a wall," Ilenne said at last. "People think of it that way — a veil, a barrier, something you build. But it was never built. It was *given*. When the realms were young, someone stood where you're standing and offered themselves as the hinge between worlds. Freely. That's the word that matters. *Freely.* The fire doesn't take a sacrifice. It accepts a gift." Her eyes came up, gray as the light. "You were chosen, Reforger. But chosen isn't willing. The fire knows the difference. It has to. Anything less and it would just be another cage — and the whole point, once, was that no one should hold the door shut unless they've chosen to stand in it."

Coral stared at her. "So the prophecy picked me, and the prophecy is useless."

"The prophecy picked a door," Ilenne said. "Doors don't open themselves."

Coral laughed, and it came out ugly, and she didn't care. "A door. Right. So somewhere out there is a person who wants to be the hinge between two collapsing worlds, freely, gladly, and I'm supposed to find them in—" she did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do all morning "—three days. Maybe four. Before that fire goes out and everything on both sides comes through the tear whether anyone likes it or not."

"Two days," Ilenne said. "Maybe three."

The laugh died in Coral's throat.

She walked back toward the barracks alone, past braziers where dragon-fire clung to life in shrinking orange islands, past hollow-eyed wardens who watched her pass and whispered *Reforger* like a title and an accusation in the same breath. She let them look. Let them pin their hope on her like a medal she couldn't return. Because the alternative was telling them the truth: that their chosen one had knelt at the heartfire six times and been refused six times, that the ancient rite wanted something she couldn't give, that she was a bookbinder with ink-stained fingers and a talent for mending broken spines, standing in front of a break too big for any spine she'd ever held.

That night she sat on her narrow bed with the prophecy book open on her knees — her own handwriting in the margins now, notes and failed translations, the scholar's version of screaming — and read her own name in century-old ink for maybe the thousandth time.

*Coral, the binder, shall stand where the flame gutters.*

Well. She'd stood. The flame had guttered. Nobody mentioned the part where standing wasn't enough.

She thought, absurdly, of dinner. Of candle flames bending sideways over the bread basket. Of a man across the table counting dishes under his breath like a rosary, who had looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a page you're afraid to turn — and said, *I've never left the valley,* with a smile that was mostly apology. She'd thought he was boring, for about four minutes. Then she'd realized the boredom was armor, and that she recognized it, because she wore the same kind over her own loneliness, stitched a little differently.

He was probably still in the valley. Safe behind whatever invisible border kept magic out and kept him in. Drinking wine that wasn't poisoned tonight, maybe, or poisoned on purpose, or — no. No, she corrected herself, pressing the heel of her hand against her eyes. He wouldn't stay. She'd seen his face when the veil tore open above the table and she went up into it like smoke drawn through a chimney. He hadn't looked frightened for himself. He'd looked like a man watching a door close on the only room he'd ever wanted to enter.

Which was ridiculous. They'd known each other four hours.

She fell asleep with the book open and dreamed of a fire that wouldn't take her gift, and woke before light with the certain, sick knowledge that two days was not enough time to find a willing stranger, and that she had never in her life hated being right about anything so much.

* * *

Edmund reached the forest at dusk on the second day, though reaching was a generous word for it.

Brack half-carried him the last mile, one arm hooked under Edmund's shoulder, and Edmund let him, because the alternative was the ground, and the ground had started looking less like a place to walk on and more like a place to lie down. Every poison he'd ever swallowed had woken up the moment he crossed the valley's border — that was the joke, the beautiful stupid joke of his whole life: outside the valley, his immunity didn't protect him, it *accused* him. His blood was a ledger of a hundred toxins, and leaving home had torn the ledger open, and now everything he'd ever neutralized was trying to reassert itself at once, arguing its case in fevers and tremors and a taste like burnt copper at the back of his throat.

He'd known the risk. Brack had laid it out plainly at the valley's edge, in the dark, with the wardens' patrol lights bobbing behind them: *Cross, and every drop of poison in you turns feral. Stay, and she dies alone in a world you can't imagine.* Edmund had stood there for perhaps nine seconds. He remembered counting. Counting was what he did.

Then he'd stepped over the line, and the world had lit up with pain like a struck match, and he had not stopped walking.

"Almost there," Brack grunted. The deserter was built like a drystone wall and moved like one toppling slowly downhill, but his grip was sure. "Heartfire's close. She'll be—"

"I know where it is." Edmund's voice came out scraped. "I've been able to feel it since noon. Like a toothache in my sternum." He almost laughed at Brack's expression. "Don't ask. Everything's strange out here. I've stopped being surprised."

What he didn't say — what he'd been not-saying for two days, through fevers that bent the trees sideways and nights when his own heartbeat sounded like someone else's — was that the pull toward the heartfire wasn't strange at all. It was the most familiar thing he'd ever felt. It felt like service. Like the tasting chair, like the ritual sip, like a life spent putting his body between other people and harm — except this time the harm was everything, and the person he was standing in front of was a woman whose name was written in a book older than his family's oath.

They came through the last screen of trees and the clearing opened up, and there it was: the great stone basin, and the fire inside it breathing its small dying breaths, amber going gray at the edges.

And kneeling at the rim, sleeves pushed up, hair escaping its braid, forearms smudged with ash — Coral.

She turned at the sound of them. Her eyes found Edmund's across the clearing, and he watched her face do three things in quick succession: disbelief, relief, and then a kind of horror, because he knew what he looked like. Gray-lipped. Shaking. Sweat-soaked despite the cold, swaying like a man on a ship.

"You crossed," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing wonder's clothes. "You absolute idiot. You crossed."

"You left," Edmund said. "Seemed rude."

She was already moving toward him, fast, and Brack wisely released his grip, and then her hands were on his face — cool, ink-smudged, real — tilting his head so she could study him the way she studied damaged books, cataloguing the water damage and the broken binding and deciding whether anything could be saved.

"You're poisoned," she said. "Badly. Since the border?"

"Since the border."

"And you kept walking."

"It seemed rude," Edmund said again, and this time the smile got through, crooked and feverish, and Coral made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and pressed her forehead against his.

Behind them, in the basin, the heartfire flared — sudden, startling, a tongue of true flame rising off the coals for the first time in days — and both of them turned to look at it, and neither of them yet understood why it had reached for Edmund like a hand recognizing a hand.

But Ashveil, coiled in the dark beyond the trees, felt it, and opened one ember-bright eye.
