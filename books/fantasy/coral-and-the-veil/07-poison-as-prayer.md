## 7. Poison as Prayer

Edmund had spent eleven years learning what death tasted like.

Hemlock was green and sweet at the edges, like a meadow pretending to be kind. Arsenic had no taste at all, which was the worst of it — you only knew it by the way your tongue went numb in rings, from the tip inward. Nightshade bloomed bitter and then warm, like a lie you wanted to believe. He had catalogued them all the way other men catalogued wines: vintage, region, finish. The finish was always the same. It was him, still breathing.

So when Brack pressed his cracked palm against the heartfire's stone basin and said, *the rite needs an anchor that can hold poison without dying*, Edmund did not need anyone to explain it to him.

He understood immediately, completely, with the terrible clarity of a man who has spent his whole life preparing for exactly one thing.

The forest around them was dying in slow motion. The great trees — silver-barked, tall as cathedral spires — stood bare in what should have been high summer, their leaves curled and brown at the crown, falling one by one into air that shimmered wrong, like heat haze in winter. The braziers along the warden road held flames that guttered blue at the base, starved of whatever fed them. And above everything, through gaps in the canopy, the sky showed seams — thin bright cracks where the veil was wearing through, and beyond them, faintly, the lights of another world leaking in. His world. Streetlamps. Window-glow. The riverside tavern where he'd sat across from Coral with a candle between them, ninety minutes into the best evening of his life.

That had been four days ago. It felt like a different man's memory.

"The anchor stands in the flame," Brack was saying. He was a big man gone hollow, a deserter's stoop in his shoulders, and he spoke about the rite the way soldiers speak about old battles — flatly, precisely, with nothing wasted. "It takes the covenant's fire into itself. All of it. Every wound in the veil passes through the anchor's body on its way to being mended." He looked up, and his eyes were the color of ash. "No living thing survives that. Not here. Nothing in this realm can carry poison and live."

"But something in mine can," Edmund said.

Brack went still.

Edmund turned his hands over and looked at them — ordinary hands, a taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips where he'd pricked himself testing for contact toxins in the early years. Under the skin, invisible, a hundred poisons lay layered like sediment in stone. Arsenic from the first year, taken in grains so small they'd seemed like nothing. Hemlock. Foxglove. The distilled essence of a river fish whose name he'd never learned, served to the Duke of Marrow's table on a night of political tension. Antimony, belladonna, white lead, the slow mineral patience of mercury. Eleven years of deliberate, methodical self-destruction, each dose calibrated to kill him a little and teach his blood a little more, until his body had become something no apothecary could explain and no physician would believe.

A body saturated with every poison the mortal world could produce. Alive when nothing should live.

"I'm already the experiment," he said quietly. "I'm the proof it works. You want an anchor that can hold poison without dying? I've been holding poison without dying since I was nineteen years old."

"You don't know what the rite asks," Brack said. "You're guessing."

"I'm a professional guesser. It's my entire trade." Edmund smiled, and found the smile held. "Every meal I've ever tasted, I've guessed whether it would kill me. My guesses have kept a king alive for a decade. Trust my guesses."

Brack stared at him for a long moment. Then the big man sat down heavily on a root, the way men sit when they've been carrying something too long, and put his face in his hands.

"There was an anchor once before," he said, muffled. "A hundred years ago. When the council broke the covenant — they told us it snapped, they told us it was age, they told us *nothing we did* — but I was young then, and I read the old rite myself, before they burned the copies. It needs a willing mortal. Willing is not a word the council understands. They'd have to ask someone to die, and asking is the one thing power never learns." He lifted his head. "If you stand in that fire, taster, you will burn through. Your poisons will hold the covenant's wounds, and the covenant's wounds will eat your poisons, and when there's nothing left in you but you—" He shrugged, a terrible small gesture. "I don't know what's left after that. Nobody knows. That's the price."

*When there's nothing left in you but you.*

Edmund thought about the valley. He thought about the walls of Amberdale House, the tasting table with its three silver spoons, the physician who checked his pulse every morning with the detached interest of a man monitoring livestock. He thought about the gate at the valley's edge that he had walked past ten thousand times and never once through, because magic could not follow him there and neither, therefore, could anything else — any future, any risk, any life worth the name. He had built his cage out of his own survival, dose by dose, and called it a career.

And then he thought about Coral laughing at him across a tavern table, candlelight in her hair, saying, *you taste poison for a living and you ordered the mushroom pie?* — and the way she'd looked when the candles bent sideways, terror and wonder fighting for her face, and how even terrified she had reached back for him as the veil took her. She had reached for *him*. A food-taster. A man made entirely of other people's attempts at murder.

He had crossed a dying world for her. He had walked out of the only safe ground he'd ever known with his blood full of volatile death, shaking so hard the first day that Brack had to hold his arm at every stream crossing — and he would do it again before breakfast. So the arithmetic here was not difficult.

"Then that's the price," Edmund said.

* * *

Finding Coral was easier than he expected, because she had stopped running.

He came over a ridge of blackened granite near dusk and saw her below, standing in the ruins of a warden waystation with Ashveil coiled around it — the last dragon, vast and dim, scales the color of banked coals, wings folded like a closed cathedral. Coral was talking to the dragon. Of course she was. Four days in an impossible realm and she had already decided the dragon was a person, because Coral decided things about books and beasts and broken men the way other people breathed: constantly, instinctively, without asking permission.

She heard his boots on the scree and turned, and the relief on her face hit him harder than any toxin ever had.

"You're alive." She crossed to him fast and gripped his arms, checking him the way he checked dishes — thorough, unsentimental, real. "You look terrible."

"I always look terrible. It's the pallor. Very fashionable among poisoners' victims."

"Don't joke." But she was almost smiling, and her hands didn't leave his arms. "We tried the rite. Yesterday. Ashveil opened the heartfire and I stood in it and it—" Her jaw tightened. "It refused me. I'm the Reforger, the book says so, ink doesn't lie, and it *refused me*, Edmund. Because the rite doesn't need a chosen one. It needs a willing mortal anchor. Someone to stand in the fire and hold it." Her voice dropped. "And there isn't anyone. There's no one in this whole realm willing, and no one from mine who could survive the crossing, let alone—"

"Coral."

"—and the flame dies in six days, and when it does both realms come down like—"

"*Coral.*" He took her hands. Held them still. Looked at her — really looked, memorizing her the way he memorized flavors, completely, against the possibility of never tasting this again. "Let me tell you something about my work, and then let me tell you something about yours."

She waited.

"My work is poison," he said. "Eleven years of it. Not surviving it — *becoming* it. I am the most thoroughly poisoned living creature in either of your worlds, Coral. I have arsenic in my bones and hemlock in my blood and enough antimony in me to fell a regiment, and I am standing here talking to you, which means my body learned to hold what should have killed it." He watched her face change — watched understanding arrive like weather. "Your rite needs an anchor that can hold poison without dying. You just haven't met one until now."

"No." The word came out fast and flat. "No, Edmund, the anchor burns through, Brack told me what the old rite—"

"Brack told you what happens to an ordinary body. Mine hasn't been ordinary since I was nineteen." He kept his voice gentle, which was easy; gentleness was the one honest thing he had left. "I'm not asking your permission. I'm telling you what I've decided, the way you'd tell me a book was rare. It's simply true."

"You don't get to decide this alone—"

"I crossed a world alone. I drank from streams I couldn't test. I ate berries off a tree while Brack screamed at me, because I needed to know if my immunity held outside the valley, and it did, Coral, it *held* — I am the only man alive who can do this, and I will not stand in a dying forest and watch both realms end because the one qualified volunteer was busy being modest."

Her eyes were wet and furious. "And what happens to you?"

And there it was. The question, standing in the road between them, patient as death.

Edmund had lied to kings. He had lied with his face, his pulse, his breath, while arsenic worked coldly through his veins and courtiers watched him for the smallest flinch. He was very good at lying. It would be the simplest thing in the world to say *I'll survive, I always survive* and let her believe it, and take her hand, and walk to the fire.

Instead he said, "Something changes. Something I can't predict. That's all I know."

It wasn't all he knew. But it was all he could say and still mean it, and some part of him — the part that had spent eleven years swallowing truth in thirty-second doses — understood that this, too, was a kind of honesty. He would give her the whole of it later. Or he wouldn't, and it would never matter.

Coral searched his face for a long time. Whatever she found there, she didn't call it a lie. Maybe she recognized the technique — a bookbinder knows paper, and paper knows what's written under the writing.

"Dawn after next," she said finally. "Ashveil says the heartfire peaks then. If you're wrong about yourself, Edmund—"

"I'm never wrong about poison."

"That's what terrifies me."

* * *

He found Brack at the edge of the camp that night, sitting alone with a whetstone and a short sword that had seen better centuries.

"She agreed?" Brack asked without looking up.

"She agreed to the rite. She doesn't know the whole price." Edmund sat beside him. "Neither did you, once. You told me. You helped break the covenant — young, stupid, believing the council's lies. You've been paying for it for a hundred years."

Brack's whetstone paused. "Your point, taster."

"My point is that you keep calling yourself a deserter. But deserters run away from things. You've been walking toward them since the day you met me at the veil's edge." Edmund looked at the old man's ruined hands. "The council will come for us before dawn after next. You know that. They let us reach the forest because they wanted to see if the Reforger would fail on her own. Now she won't fail. Now she has an anchor. Which means they'll come with iron and numbers and a hundred years of practice at burying inconvenient truths."

"They'll come with more than iron. They'll come with the pact-fire. What feeds them." Brack set down the whetstone. "Against that, one old coward with a short sword is a rounding error."

"One old coward with a short sword who knows the council hall's cell gates, its patrol rotations, and which of its wardens still remembers what honor tastes like." Edmund allowed himself a thin smile. "I taste things, Brack. I tasted you the first hour we met. Underneath the rot and the shame, there's a man who broke the covenant and has hated himself cleanly ever since. That's not a deserter. That's a witness waiting for his case."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, through the canopy, the seams in the sky flickered — and in one of them, briefly, Edmund saw the tavern. The candle. The two cups of wine going warm.

"When they come," Brack said at last, slowly, like a man stepping off a cliff he'd been circling for a century, "someone has to open the cells. Someone has to stand in the council hall's doorway and say *no, this far, no further* — and mean it, and be believed." He picked up the sword. Tested its weight like an apology. "I'll stand witness, taster. At the rite and at the hall both. Let the council choke on it."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank yourself, when you're on the other side of that fire." Brack looked at him then, ash eyes steady. "Because you will be, understand? I buried an anchor once — the last one the council murdered rather than allow. I will not bury the man who fixes what I broke. You get out of that flame, or you haunt me specifically. Those are your options."

Edmund laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, the first in days. "I'll consider it a contract."

Above them, the seams in the sky widened a fraction, and far to the south — too far to see, close enough to feel — something with many teeth began to move toward the forest.

Dawn after next. Two dawns. Edmund lay back against the cold granite, folded his hands over his poisoned heart, and slept without dreaming, the way men sleep when they have finally finished deciding.
