## 8. The Council's Teeth

They came for us at the edge of the forest, where the trees thinned into a meadow of silver grass that sang when the wind moved through it. I heard them before I saw them — a sound like wind chimes made of bone — and by the time I turned, they had us ringed.

Six wardens in grey robes, faces hidden behind veils of woven smoke. The one at their center carried a staff tipped with a shard of veil-glass, and it hummed against my teeth the way a tuning fork hums against bone.

"The Reforger," it said. Its voice was layered, three voices speaking at once, none of them quite in agreement. "And the anchor."

Edmund stepped in front of me. I'll remember that always — the way he did it, without hesitation, without calculation, a man who had spent his whole life tasting danger in careful sips suddenly throwing his whole body across the cup.

"He's sick," I said. "He crossed the veil poisoned. If you touch him—"

"We know what he is." The staff tilted toward Edmund, and I watched the smoke-veil shift, the way a face shifts when it smiles. "A hundred toxins in a living body. Do you know what that is, Reforger? It is a key. And keys are wasted on doors their owners choose."

I didn't understand. Not then. I thought they'd come to kill us, and I braced for it — the flash of veil-glass, the cold. What came instead was worse.

They took us alive.

* * *

The cells beneath the council hall were not dungeons. That was the first thing I noticed, and the thing that frightened me most. They were comfortable. Warm stone, soft light, a bench wide enough to sleep on. A pitcher of water that never emptied — I tested it, drank three times, watched it refill. Everything here was designed to keep something alive for a very long time.

The door had no lock. It didn't need one. The threshold shimmered with the same veil-glass hum, and when Edmund reached toward it, his hand stopped an inch from the air as if it had struck glass.

"Edmund." I caught his wrist. His skin was clammy, and there was a fine tremor running through him that I'd been pretending not to see since the meadow. "Your poisons. Being here, on this side—"

"Brack warned me." He sat down heavily on the bench, and I sat beside him, because there was nowhere else, and because I wanted to be near him more than I wanted to pretend I didn't. "Every toxin in me went volatile the moment I crossed. My body's been fighting a war on two fronts since the forest. I have days. Maybe less."

"Days." The word came out flat. "And they know that. That's why they took us alive. That's why the comfortable cell." I stood up, paced the six steps the room allowed, turned, paced back. "They're going to let your poison do their killing for them, Edmund. Slowly. While they—"

"While they negotiate with you." He said it quietly, and the quiet was worse than shouting. "I heard the warden at the meadow. *Keys are wasted on doors their owners choose.* They don't want me dead, Coral. They want me *dying*. On a schedule. So that every hour you refuse them is an hour you can watch me spend."

I stopped pacing. The soft light made everything gentle — his face, the warm stone, the impossible water — and I hated it. Cruelty dressed as comfort. A cage built like a guest room.

"Then we refuse them every hour," I said. "We refuse them until there are no hours left."

Edmund looked up at me, and something moved through his expression that I couldn't name — grief, maybe, or its close cousin, gratitude. "You should know what they'll offer you," he said. "Brack told me what the old pact was. What they'll want to renew. They feed on the veil's decay, Coral. They'll offer you the same bargain they gave the council a century ago. Your life stretched thin and long, in exchange for letting the realms keep bleeding into each other at the edges. In exchange for *me*, probably. They'll offer him to you the way you'd bargain over a horse."

"Then they've misread me."

"They've misread everyone, always. That's how they've lasted a hundred years." He laughed, and it turned into a cough, and I sat back down and put my hand flat against his back and felt the tremor there, the war still being fought under his skin.

* * *

We talked for a long time after that. Or maybe it wasn't long — time moved strangely in that soft-lit room, the way it does in sickrooms and churches.

He told me about the valley. About growing up in a place where magic couldn't follow, where the air was just air and the water was just water, and how a child learns to call a cage *safety* if the cage is all he's ever seen. He told me about the first time he tasted poison on purpose — arsenic in the royal wine, a boy of sixteen, counting the dishes between himself and freedom. "I thought immunity was a door," he said. "I thought if I could survive enough, I could go anywhere. And then I got close to the end of the count, and I realized the valley had become part of the immunity. That the cage had grown into my body. That leaving would kill me as surely as staying would starve me."

"And then you left anyway."

"And then I met a woman at a riverside tavern," he said, "who laughed at my jokes and had candle-smoke in her hair, and then the sky over her head tore open, and I understood that I'd been counting the wrong things. That freedom was never a number of dishes. It was—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "It was a person. It was choosing a person and walking toward them through the one door that might kill you."

I told him about the book. About the afternoon in my workshop, the century-old prophecy open on my bench, and my own name in the old ink, and *the riverside tavern* beneath it, and the date. I told him how I'd laughed, how I'd dressed for dinner half in spite — *if fate wants to write my evenings, it can at least buy me a decent glass of wine* — and how the candles had bent sideways and the world above our table had split like old vellum.

"Everyone keeps saying I was *chosen*," I said. "The wardens. Ashveil. Even the book. Chosen, chosen, chosen. Like I was picked out of a bin. But you know what I felt when I read my name? Not chosen. *Accused.* Like the ink had been waiting a hundred years to hand me a bill."

"And now?"

"Now I think being chosen is just being seen," I said. "The realm didn't pick me. It *looked* at me. And the wardens looked away. That's the whole difference, Edmund. That's the whole war. Some things look at you and want to use what they see. And some things—" I touched his face, turned it toward mine. "Some things look at you and stay."

His eyes were wet. He didn't hide it. "I crossed a dying veil for a woman I'd known for one dinner," he said. "Brack asked me why, on the road. I didn't have an answer then. I have it now. It's because in that tavern, for one hour, I wasn't a taster or a tool or a body full of counted poisons. I was a man having dinner with Coral. And I would rather have one more hour of that and die than a hundred years of the valley without it."

"Then don't die," I said. My voice cracked on it. I let it. "Don't you dare die, Edmund Amberdale. We are going to get out of this room, and we are going to reach the heartfire, and you are going to stand beside me when I reforge the covenant, and you are going to *live* — do you hear me? Whatever it costs, whatever they—"

"Coral."

"Whatever they—"

"*Coral.*" He took my hands. His grip was still strong. That mattered to me more than I can say. "Whatever happens at the heartfire, I need you to promise me one thing. Not that I'll live. Not that we'll win. Just this: that whatever they offer you in that council hall — long life, my life, the veil itself — you'll remember that a bargain made from a cage is just a cage with better furniture."

I laughed. It came out wet and broken and real. "A cage with better furniture," I repeated. "You tasters. Everything's a metaphor you can swallow."

"Everything's a poison," he said, "if you take enough of it. And everything's a medicine, if you take it for the right reason."

* * *

The door opened at what might have been dawn.

The lead warden stood in the threshold, smoke-veil stirring, staff humming. Behind it, I could hear the council hall waking above us — voices, footsteps, the dry rustle of old robes.

"The Reforger," it said. "The council will receive you now."

"And him?" I asked.

"The anchor will remain. He is safest here." The veil-smile again, I was sure of it. "We would not want him to spend himself before the bargaining is done."

I looked at Edmund. He looked back at me, and in that look we finished a conversation we'd been having all night without words. *A bargain made from a cage is just a cage with better furniture.* Whatever they offered, we'd refuse it together. Whatever they took, we'd take back.

I stood. I smoothed my ruined dress. I walked to the door, and just before the threshold's hum closed behind me, Edmund caught my hand and pressed something into it — small, hard, folded.

I didn't look at it. Not until the great doors of the council hall had swallowed me, not until I stood before the twelve veiled figures of the council and heard them begin to speak the terms of a century-old lie.

Then, in my closed fist, I unfolded it.

A strip of vellum, torn from the prophecy book itself. My own name in the old ink. And beneath it, in Edmund's careful taster's hand, added sometime in the night while I slept:

*The ink chose her. The poison chooses me. Let the council choke on both.*

Above us, through the high windows of the council hall, the sky began to burn orange — and it was not dawn.

It was Ashveil.
