## 10. Two Names, One Covenant

The fire took Edmund's hand first.

I felt it happen — not as heat but as recognition, the way you feel a word settle into a sentence and know it was always meant to be there. The heartfire poured through his skin, through the hundred poisons sleeping in his blood like a congregation waiting for a hymn, and instead of burning him it *read* him. Every toxin he'd swallowed for seven years. Every dose he'd taken so he could one day walk out of a valley that had never once let him go.

The rite wanted a life freely given. What it got was a body already halfway between death and something else — a man who had made himself unkillable by small increments, in secret, for no reason he could name until tonight.

Then I grabbed his hand.

"You idiot," I said, which is not what poets would have chosen, standing at the center of a reforging covenant while a dragon burned a council hall above us. "You absolute *idiot*. You didn't tell me the price."

"There wasn't time—"

"There's always time to tell someone you're dying for them!"

The fire turned toward me. I felt it consider me, this second hand latched onto the anchor, this woman with no poison in her blood and nothing to offer except the fact that she refused to let go. And then Ashveil's voice came down through the collapsing hall like cooling embers given grammar:

"*Two names. One covenant. It was always meant to be witnessed, not endured alone.*"

The old book had said one name. Mine. But books are written by people, and people get things wrong, or leave things out, or are edited by councils who feed on broken things. The prophecy hadn't been incomplete. It had been *trimmed*.

So we gave it both names.

I won't pretend I remember all of it. There was light that wasn't light, a sound like the veil exhaling after holding its breath for a hundred years, and a sensation of being *stitched* — pulled thread-tight through two worlds at once until I couldn't tell which side of the seam I'd been born on. Edmund's grip on my hand never loosened. That's the part I kept. Whatever else happened in that white roar, I kept his grip.

* * *

When it ended, we were kneeling in the ruins of the council hall, and the fire around us had gone out — not extinguished but *settled*, banked into the stone floor like coals into a hearth. Above us, dawn was coming through a hole in the roof, and it fell across both realms equally, because there were no longer two skies to divide it.

Edmund looked at his hands. Turned them over. The faint silver tracery of veins beneath his skin — the map of everything he'd survived — now glowed softly, like rivers seen from very high up.

"I can feel both," he said wonderingly. "The valley. This place. Like rooms in the same house."

"That's what we are now," I said. "Doors."

He laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound — and then Brack was there, hauling us both upright with hands that shook. The old deserter looked at the two of us, at the covenant-light still fading from our joined hands, and did something I hadn't seen him do: he bowed. Not to us. To the space between us, where the new seam of the world ran.

"The council?" Edmund asked.

"Gone." Brack spat. "What Ashveil didn't burn, the wardens' own servants abandoned. You can't keep feeding a lie when the truth is standing in the room." He jerked his chin upward, toward the high perch where the last dragon settled her wings, her flame — guttering for a century — now burning steady and low and *patient*, the way a hearth burns rather than a pyre. "I'll carry word to the valley settlements. Someone has to tell them the veil isn't a wall anymore. It's a hallway."

"A hallway," Edmund repeated.

"Walkways need walking," Brack said, and left before either of us could thank him, which I suspect was the point.

Ashveil descended last. Her head came down level with ours, eyes like banked furnaces, and she studied us for a long moment with the attention of a creature who had waited a hundred years for a repair job and intended to inspect the work.

"*Two anchors,*" she rumbled. "*The covenant will hold as long as you hold. And you will hold longer than you wish, and less than you fear.*"

"That's very comforting," Edmund said.

"*It is not comfort. It is terms.*" A curl of smoke, almost wry. "*But you are not alone in your terms. That is the difference. Go home, taster. Go home, binder. The book is yours to finish.*"

* * *

Crossing back was nothing like crossing over. No tearing, no terror — just a step, and the world folded politely around us, and suddenly we stood on the near bank of the river, in the valley where magic cannot follow, except that magic followed us home like a dog that had decided where it lives.

Everything looked the same. That was the strangest part. The tavern with its crooked sign. The river doing its patient silver arithmetic over the stones. My own hands, ink-stained as ever, holding a man's hand that glowed faintly if you knew where to look.

We went back inside. Our table was exactly as we'd left it — two plates gone cold, two candles burned to stubs, and the prophecy book lying open where the veil had torn it from my grasp a lifetime ago.

Edmund sat. I sat. And then, because we are who we are, we finished the date.

He ordered wine. He tasted it first, out of seven years of habit, and then he laughed — really laughed — and pushed the glass across the table to me. "No arsenic tonight," he said. "First time in my adult life I can say that."

"What's it taste like?"

"Safe." He considered. "Strange. I don't think I like it."

"You'll adjust."

"Will I?" He looked at me across the candle stubs, and his voice went quieter, the way it does when a man says something he's been rehearsing without knowing it. "Coral. I don't fit here anymore. I can feel the whole valley like a coat that nearly fits. And out there—" he nodded east, toward the folded seam only we could see, "—out there I'm a curiosity. An anchor. A story wardens will tell their children about the fool who volunteered."

"Same," I said. "Neither side gets all of us."

"No." He turned my hand over in his, traced the covenant-line that ran pale along my palm like a binder's crease. "Neither side gets all of us. But—" and here he smiled, tired and certain, "—we get each other. Whole. That's the whole treaty, as far as I'm concerned. Two names. One covenant. Signed."

"Signed," I agreed, and drank his safe wine, and found it tasted like beginnings.

Outside, the river kept on. Somewhere east, a dragon's flame burned steady. Somewhere beyond the veil, a world was learning to be a neighbor instead of a rumor. And somewhere in my chest, in the quiet place where belonging used to ache like an empty room, there was simply — him. Not a place. A person.

Home, it turns out, is not where the veil lets you stay.

It's who stays when the veil finally lets you go.

* * *

**Edmund**

I still count dishes. Old habit. But the count means nothing now — I could eat anything in either realm and live, and some mornings that feels like freedom and some mornings it feels like losing the shape of the man I was.

On those mornings, Coral finds me.

Today she's at the workbench in our little shop that straddles nothing — built half in the valley, half in the other realm, the doorframe itself a seam you step through like stepping through a thought. She's rebinding the prophecy book. She cut away the trimmed pages weeks ago, found the hidden margin where a second name had been scraped out a century ago, and she's restoring it letter by letter in gold leaf: not just hers now, but mine beside it. Two names. The way it was always written, before someone decided one was cheaper than two.

"Coral," I say, "the warden envoy crossed over this morning. First official traffic since the reforging. They're asking for us at the border-stone."

She doesn't look up from her brush. "Asking, or summoning?"

"Asking. Politely." I lean against the doorpost, one shoulder in each world, which is apparently my permanent condition now. "They want help drafting the new accord. Something about wanting it 'bound by hands that understand binding.'"

Now she looks up, and she's smiling the smile she saves for finished work and impossible requests. "They want us to write the treaty."

"They want us to *be* the treaty. Apparently there's a difference."

She sets down her brush. She takes my hand — the poisoned one, the blessed one, the one that held the fire — and presses her thumb along the covenant-line like sealing wax.

"Well then," she says. "Let's go give them terms."

And we step through the doorway together, into the country that is neither and both, where every road is open and every road leads back to each other — two names on one covenant, walking home the long way, which is now the only way there is.
