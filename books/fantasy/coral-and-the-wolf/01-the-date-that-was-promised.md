## 1. The Date That Was Promised

There are three rules to selling charms in a city where magic is a hanging offense, and I have followed all of them for twelve years without exception.

One: never sell anything that works.

Two: never let them see you hope it might.

Three: never, under any circumstances, open the door behind the wall.

I keep the rules the way other men keep their teeth — through daily, unglamorous attention, and with a low hum of dread about what happens when you stop. The charms themselves are honest frauds. Copper acorns for safe travel. Knotted red thread for mending a broken heart. Little glass beads I call "storm-calming stones," which calm storms exactly as well as any other rock would, which is to say not at all, but which give the buyer something to hold while the weather does what weather does. People don't come to me for miracles. They come because the world has been frightening lately and they want to hold something warm while they wait it out. I can provide that. I have built an entire respectable life on providing that.

The wall at the back of my shop is another matter entirely.

Behind the shelf of dried lavender and the curtain of grey felt, there is a door. It is old iron, older than the city's name, and it does not belong to me no matter what the deed says. My family has kept it shut for longer than anyone remembers keeping anything, and I intend to be the last Amberdale who ever touches it, which is easy, since I also intend to be the last Amberdale, full stop. The lock is sound. The hinges are oiled twice a year on the first frost, quietly, before dawn, so that they never squeak and never seize. A gate that seizes is a gate someone will want to pry open. A gate that opens smoothly is a gate nobody thinks about. I learned that from my mother, who learned it from hers, all the way back down a line of people whose entire contribution to history was making sure nothing happened.

Nothing is a full-time occupation. I recommend it to no one.

* * *

It was the third of Frostwane, and I remember the date precisely because the paper told me to.

I had gone into the back room to look for more red thread — I was down to two spools and heartbreak, like winter, always comes in quantity — when I noticed the box. Not a new box. An old one, cedar, iron-banded, sitting on the highest shelf where I had looked a thousand times and seen nothing but shadow. But the light came in differently that morning. Snow was falling out of season, fat and slow past the window, and snow-light is a strange light; it comes from everywhere and nowhere and it finds things.

The box had my surname burned into its lid. AMBERDALE, in letters that had gone brown-black with age, in a hand that was not mine and not my mother's and not, I thought, anyone's who had died within living memory.

Here is what you should understand about me: I am a coward by profession. Cowardice, done properly, looks identical to prudence. So the correct response to a mysterious century-old box bearing my name would have been to put it back on the shelf, dust my hands, and go sell a widow a copper acorn. That is what the careful man does. That is what I have trained myself, over twelve years and three thousand unremarkable days, to do.

I took the box down.

I tell myself now that it was because leaving it out in the open light was riskier than opening it, and there is even some truth in that — if a warden ever saw that lid, we would both hang, the box and I, though only one of us would kick. But the truth underneath the true thing is simpler: my hands wanted to know. Twelve years of nothing makes a man hungry for anything, even the thing he fears most, even the thing his mother died rather than let him touch. My mother. Who walked through the forbidden door when I was nineteen and did not come back, and left me a ring of keys and a set of instructions and no explanation whatsoever, because explanations, she said, were how gates got opened.

I sat on the floor with the box between my knees and worked the old clasp until it gave, which took longer than it should have, because my hands were shaking and I pretended they weren't.

Inside: paper. One sheet, folded in thirds, soft as cloth with age, smelling faintly of cedar and something else — something cold, something that smelled the way the air smells just before the first hard frost, that mineral emptiness. And beneath the paper, a second object I did not examine too closely, because examining things closely is how this whole trouble starts: a strip of vellum covered in cramped script, wax-sealed, the seal stamped with a wolf's head split down the middle.

I unfolded the paper first. It seemed the lesser danger. Paper burns; everyone knows that. Paper is the most honest material in the world because it stays destroyed.

The hand was old-fashioned, formal, the kind of script they taught scribes a hundred years ago when prophecy was still a discipline instead of a crime. I read it standing up, because I am superstitious about bad news — you take it standing, like a blow, or it knocks you down.

*On the third day of Frostwane, in the year the snows come early,* it began, *the last keeper shall find what was hidden above his own ceiling, and shall know the date of his ending.*

Well. Chilling, but generic. Prophecies are like horoscopes that way; they say *last keeper* and every lonely shopkeeper in the realm feels a shiver. I almost laughed. I actually did laugh, a little, the short ugly laugh of a man reassuring himself, and then I read the next line.

*His name is written in the keeping of the gate. His house is Amberdale.*

I stopped laughing.

I stood very still in my own back room, among the lavender bundles and the unsold thread, holding a dead person's handwriting that knew my surname, and I did the arithmetic I did not want to do. The year the snows come early. Snow, out of season, falling past my window at that exact moment, fat and slow and wrong, the first snow I had seen in Frostwane in twelve years of keeping careful records of unremarkable things. And today's date — the third of Frostwane — sitting in that first line like a signature on a confession.

A century ago, someone wrote down today. Someone wrote down my house. Someone wrote down *last*, and meant me, and sealed it in cedar, and hid it above the room where I sleep, and waited.

Prophecy is a hanging offense too, in case you're wondering. Reading it, owning it, repeating it — the wardens don't distinguish. The law says the future belongs to the crown alone, and the crown says the future is whatever the crown says it is. I had spent twelve years being so carefully innocent that I'd nearly forgotten innocence was a thing you could lose. I was holding the losing of it in both hands.

So I did the sensible thing. I did the thing the careful man does. I lit the lamp, crumpled the page, and fed it to the flame, and watched it curl and blacken and go to ash in the dish where I burn my lavender stems, and I felt, for approximately four seconds, enormously better. Destroyed is destroyed. Ash keeps no dates. Whatever a dead scribe had known a hundred years ago, it was smoke now, and smoke doesn't testify.

Then I swept up the ash, because I am thorough even in cowardice, especially in cowardice, and as I swept — as the little grey pile came together under the broom — I saw the writing again.

Not in the ash. In the dust. On the floorboards, in the fine pale dust that accumulates in a back room nobody sweeps often enough, letters had appeared, thin and precise, as though a finger had written them from underneath the wood:

*You cannot burn what was written in you.*

I stood there holding a broom, in my shop, in my careful life, and I understood that I was not holding a broom. I was holding the last ordinary object in the room.

* * *

I want to be clear about what kind of frightened I was, because there are kinds.

There is the fear of wardens, which I know intimately, the way a fish knows water — the low constant vigilance of a man whose trade is technically capital treason performed so badly it passes for commerce. That fear is manageable. That fear has routines. You pay your bribes, you keep your ledger boring, you smile at inspections, and you survive.

This was not that fear. This was older. This was the fear of a door you've kept shut your whole life suddenly clearing its throat.

I closed the shop early. I turned the sign, drew the bolt, and told myself I was doing it because of the snow — because foot traffic would be poor anyway, because there was no sense standing around selling nothing to nobody. Dread is very good at dressing itself in practicality. It had been dressing itself in practicality my entire adult life. Every careful choice I had ever made, every rule I followed, every charm that did nothing — all of it, I saw suddenly, looking at that sentence in the dust, was dread wearing an apron, sweeping a floor, oiling hinges before dawn. My mother hadn't given me instructions. She'd given me her fear, laundered into habit, and I had worn it so long it fit like skin.

But knowing that didn't help. Knowing the shape of a trap is not the same as being out of it.

I cleaned the floorboards. Scrubbed the writing out with lye and hot water, working fast, telling myself the whole time that dust-script was a trick of the light, that grief and cold and a century-old letter will make a man see words in woodgrain the way tired travelers see faces in clouds. The letters came away. The floor was only floor again. I dried it, and stood over it, and watched it, the way you watch a wound, waiting to see whether it bleeds.

While I watched, the snow outside thickened. By late afternoon it lay three fingers deep on the cobbles of Charm Row, and Charm Row has not held snow in my lifetime — the city sits low, the river keeps it temperate, the winters here are wet and grey and grudging with their frost. Old men were saying so in the street, stamping their feet, delighted and unsettled in equal measure. *First snow in forty years, boy. First snow since the old king's time.* They said it the way people say things that are wonderful and terrible at once, with laughter in the front of the voice and something colder behind it.

The old king. I grew up on stories of the old king, King Aldric, the Lawgiver, the one who outlawed magic after the Sundering Wars, who put the wardens in their black coats, who made my family's whole inheritance a death sentence and then, mercifully, mostly forgot we existed. He'd been old when my mother was young. He'd be impossibly old now, a king preserved past all reason, still issuing proclamations from a palace I'd never seen, still the fixed star everything in this city orbited. Fear of the king was the other water I swam in. Everyone's water. You did not speak against Aldric. You did not speak of magic except to condemn it. You lived small and you lived quiet and once a year you watched his Winter Address from a respectful distance and applauded the way you applaud weather.

I thought about him, that afternoon, because the snow made everyone think about him. And I thought: a century-old prophecy, naming today, naming my house, and the first snow in a generation arriving to mark the occasion. Coincidence is a load-bearing wall in my life. I have leaned on it many times. But walls creak when you lean too hard, and somewhere in me, below the part that was scrubbing floors and banking the fire and being sensible, something had begun — quietly, without permission — to count.

* * *

Evening came early, the way it does under snowcloud, and I did what I always do on the first frost: I oiled the hinges of the forbidden door.

I want to defend this ritual, because it must look mad from the outside — the magic-outlawing maintenance of a magical door, performed by the least magical man in the city. But the logic is sound and it is the only inheritance I trust. Oiled hinges mean no squeal means no curiosity means no questions means no gallows. The ritual is not devotion. It is camouflage. It is the lie I tell the door twice a year: *nothing here, nothing to see, move along.* I have performed it since I was nineteen, kneeling in the dark with the felt curtain pushed aside, and in twelve years the door has never once answered. It is iron. It is heavy. It is shut. Those are the facts, and I have built my life on facts of that particular size.

I knelt. I pulled the curtain aside. I set the oil flask down on the flagstones.

And the corridor beyond the door was cold in a way it had never been cold. I know that corridor's temperature the way I know my own pulse. It holds the chill of stone, constant, patient, indifferent — the temperature of a thing that has been ignored for a hundred years. This was different. This was the cold off a glacier, the cold that has wind in it somewhere, the cold that gets into the joints of your fingers before you decide to notice. Breath of it came through the seam under the door and moved the dust on the flagstones, a thin white breath, and the dust swirled and settled into lines.

I am going to be honest, since honesty costs me nothing tonight that I wasn't already going to lose: the lines were letters. Small ones, neat ones, the same hand as the burned page. And I did not kneel there reading them, because I am a coward, and cowards survive, and men who kneel in dark corridors reading messages out of the dust do not.

I snuffed the oil flask's cap on tight, dropped the curtain, and got out of that back room at a speed I reserve for fires.

In the front of the shop I lit every lamp I owned, which is a stupid response and I recommend it anyway; light is a kind of company. I made tea I didn't drink. I sat behind my counter among the copper acorns and the knotwork thread and the storm-calming stones, surrounded by the honest frauds of my careful life, and I listened to the snow come down on Charm Row, and I waited to feel ridiculous.

That's the trick of dread, you see. Half of surviving it is waiting for the moment it reveals itself as overreaction — the moment the floor stays floor, the night stays night, the world declines to confirm your worst suspicion and lets you feel foolish instead. Foolish is fine. Foolish is cheap. I would have paid a great deal, right then, to feel foolish.

Midnight came, and the bells rang it in across the river, nine strokes and the great slow tenth from the palace tower that means the king sleeps safe. I counted them out of habit. Under the last stroke, in the back room, faintly, through two walls and a curtain and a locked iron door, something shifted its weight.

Not a voice. Not yet. Just a weight, settling, the way a house settles, the way ice settles on a pond before it speaks. Then silence, total, the specific silence of something listening back.

I did not sleep. I sat up with the lamp trimmed low and my mother's key-ring in my fist — six keys, five of them for locks that no longer exist, and the sixth, the smallest, warm as a held coin, for a door I have never once used. She left it to me with the instruction I have obeyed every day since: *Keep it shut, Cyrus. Keep it shut, and keep yourself small, and outlive it.*

Outlive it. As if it were a season. As if it were a man.

As the sky went grey toward a snowbound dawn, I got up, crossed the shop, and swept the ashes of the burned page out onto the fire one final time — ceremony, superstition, spite, I couldn't have said which. The ash went up the flue in a single grey breath.

And on the hearthstone, where the ash had lain, in letters of soot too fine to smudge, the page wrote itself out one line at a time, patient as frost forming on glass, until the whole prophecy stood complete in my fireplace, dated, signed, and addressed — and the last line, the one that had been missing from the folded sheet, the one it had saved for a man driven to check, read:

*What the gate wants, it already knows the way to ask.*
