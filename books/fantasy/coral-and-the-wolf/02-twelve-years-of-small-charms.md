## 2. Twelve Years of Small Charms

I did not sleep again that night, which was becoming a habit I could not afford.

Instead I opened the shop.

This is a thing you should understand about me: when my life cracks, I sweep. When it splits down the middle, I dust the shelves and straighten the jars and count the till twice. There is a kind of prayer in inventory, if your god is small enough — and mine has always been small, domestic, content to live in a drawer of labeled twine and dried lavender. The Ashfall gods were hung from their own gates centuries ago. What's left for a man like me is counting.

So at half past six on the morning after the prophecy wrote itself into my fireplace, I unlocked the front door of Amberdale Charms & Sundries, flipped the sign to OPEN, and began selling lies to the good people of Corrow Lane.

* * *

Let me tell you what I sell, because it matters more than you'd think.

The little blue sachets against nightmares: nothing but chamomile and a stitch worked in red thread. The iron nail charms for doorways: iron nails, purchased honest from Ferro's yard, bent slightly so they look "worked." The luck-knots for fishermen: knots, tied well, which is genuinely most of luck anyway. The love tokens: honey, rose oil, and the profound human willingness to believe that someone who bought you a gift must have feelings about you. Everything in my shop does exactly what its mundane contents would do and no more. Chamomile aids sleep because chamomile aids sleep. A well-tied knot holds because knots hold.

There is not one drop of magic in Amberdale Charms & Sundries. There never has been.

People come in anyway. They came in before the edicts, back when magic was merely unfashionable, and they come in now, when it's a hanging offense, and they buy their sachets and their nails and they leave feeling *kept*, which is the entire product. I am not selling charms. I am selling the sensation of having done something, which is the rarest commodity in a city ruled by fear, and I supply it cheaply and honestly, in the sense that I never claim it works by anything but chamomile.

If a warden ever asked — and wardens had asked, early on, doing their rounds with their little brass ledgers — I would show them the chamomile. I would show them the receipts from Ferro's yard. I would say, with the flat pride of a craftsman, *sir, there is nothing here*, and mean it as an oath, and be telling the truth, and be lying through my teeth all at once, because there is something in that shop that is not merchandise.

It is behind the north wall, behind the shelf of preserved lemons, behind a door that takes a key I wear under my shirt.

But we'll come to the wall. First, the woman who taught me to lie with chamomile.

* * *

My mother, Maren Amberdale, sold real charms.

Understand the difference. Before the edicts, before the Wolf — though we didn't know to call it that then; before the king's voice changed on the winter proclamations, before the snow stopped melting in the high quarters even in June — before all of it, she kept this same shop and she sold workings that *worked*. Fever-charms that broke a fever by dusk. A finding-stone that pulled toward whatever was lost, warm as a bird in your palm. She once mended a shattered jug by singing to it, badly, while it knitted itself together in her lap like a cat deciding to forgive you.

And behind the north wall, behind the lemons, was the gate, and she kept that too.

I didn't know what the gate was, at nineteen. That sounds impossible now. It sounds like the sort of thing a son should have noticed — the way she went through that door every night at moonrise with the key-ring at her belt, the way she came out an hour later looking wrung-out and quiet, the way the air near the north wall tasted faintly of cold iron no matter the season. But children are excellent scholars of everything except their own households. I thought it was a cellar. I thought maybe she kept wine.

On the last night of autumn twelve years ago, she called me down to the shop after closing, and she was wearing her grey traveling cloak indoors, which I had never seen her do, and the key-ring was already on her belt.

"Sit," she said.

"I'm standing fine."

"Cyrus." My name, in her mouth, had always been a full sentence. I sat.

She laid three things on the counter between us. The key-ring. A folded paper. And a cup of tea she'd made me without asking, which should have been my first warning, because my mother never made tea without asking unless she wanted my hands occupied.

"You're going to want to argue," she said. "Drink first. Arguing with cold hands is just flailing."

I drank. She watched me with an expression I have spent twelve years re-examining and still can't file — grief and resolve in equal measure, worn smooth already, like she'd been carrying it for years and had finally gotten used to the weight.

"The edicts will get worse," she said. "You've heard the proclamations. The king is afraid of something, and frightened kings make hungry laws. One day soon they'll hang a charm-seller in Coronation Square to make a point, and they'll want it to be someone whose charms actually work, to make the point properly."

"So we stop selling the ones that work."

"We," she said, and smiled, and the smile cracked something in me even then, "are going to have a conversation about pronouns. Listen to me. The gate behind that wall cannot stay unkept. It doesn't matter whether anyone remembers why — it was built with a reason, and the reason is still in there, and it is patient in the way that only old hungry things are patient. Our family keeps it. That's the whole of the Amberdale inheritance. No land, no gold, no title. A door and an obligation."

"Maren—"

"—Amberdale, keeper of the Amberdale Gate, yes, it's a very original arrangement, my grandmother thought so too." She pushed the key-ring across the counter. It made a sound I had heard my whole life and never listened to: a low, dense clatter, like keys underwater. "The paper tells you what I can't tonight. Read it. Burn it after, or don't, it won't matter — it isn't really paper. And Cyrus: sell nothing that works. Not ever. If they come, and they will come, let them find a shop full of chamomile and bent nails and a man bored enough to be harmless. Fear looks for magic. Starve it."

"And where will you be?"

She looked at the north wall. For a moment — I have replayed this moment perhaps ten thousand times and I will play it ten thousand more — for a moment her face did something I can only describe as *homesick*, there in her own shop, in her own city, looking at her own wall.

"Nearby," she said. "Doing the work. Keeping it shut from the other side for a while, until things settle." She kissed the top of my head like I was nine instead of nineteen. "Moonrise, love. Don't follow me. Whatever you hear through the wall — however it sounds — don't follow me."

"What will it sound like?"

She was at the door to the antechamber. She paused with her hand on it, and she almost turned around, and I have wondered since whether everything would have gone differently if she had — whether she would have told me some truer thing, or whether the truest thing was the one she chose.

"It'll sound like me," she said. "That's rather the point. Goodnight, Cyrus."

She went through. The door closed. At moonrise — I counted, I sat behind the counter with the folded paper untouched and the tea cold and I counted every strike of the cathedral bells — at moonrise, something on the other side of the north wall sighed, long and slow, like a house settling, like a held breath released.

She did not come out.

I waited until dawn. I read the paper at dawn, because I couldn't read it before — my eyes slid off it the way eyes slide off a stranger mid-crowd — and at first light it consented to be read, and I will not reproduce it here. Some documents you keep folded. It named our family. It named the gate. It used a word I hadn't learned yet, a word Wren would later translate for me with shaking hands: *the Wolf*. It gave instructions. The instructions amounted to: lock it, feed it nothing, answer nothing, open it for no voice.

And at the bottom, in my mother's handwriting, added recently enough that the ink still shone:

*He will try to sound like me. He will get better at it every year. Love him anyway, from a distance, through a locked door.*

*He.*

I burned the paper. It rewrote itself. I burned it eleven more times over the following month, and on the twelfth attempt I simply couldn't lift the tongs anymore, and I put it in the iron box at the bottom of the till where it has lived ever since, being patient, being right.

* * *

Here is what twelve years of small charms looks like, day by day, so you understand what I was protecting when I burned that page a second time last night.

It looks like opening at half past six. It looks like Mrs. Hallow buying her monthly nightmare-sachet and telling me, as she tells me every month, that her dreams have been "quiet as church" since she started using them, and my saying "chamomile's a wonder," and both of us knowing — in the way of two people who have agreed on a fiction so long it has become furniture — that chamomile is not the wonder. It looks like Ferro's boy delivering nails and asking, once a year, slyly, whether business is "still boring," and my saying yes, gloriously boring, boring as bread. It looks like winter proclamations nailed to the corner post, each one longer than the last, each one promising that the cold was temporary, that the plague quarantines were temporary, that the curfew was temporary, while the ice on the eaves got fat as hams and stopped caring about spring.

It looks like answering nothing.

Because the gate talks. I should be plain about that, since I've been dancing around it. Three, four times a year — more in deep winter, less in the brief false summers — the wall speaks. Not in words, at first. In suggestions. A smell of woodsmoke like my mother's hearth. A warmth along the seam of the hidden door, body-warm, hand-shaped. And then, as the years went on and it got — as she said it would — *better*: her voice.

The first time, I nearly opened it. I was twenty-one, alone, feverish, and the wall said *Cyrus, love, I've caught my cloak on something, bring the ring,* and my hand was on the shelf of lemons before my brain caught up, and what stopped me wasn't courage. It was the tea. Her rule about occupying my hands. I made tea instead, absurdly, furiously, and drank two cups, and by the third the voice had gone, offended, like a guest refused at the door.

After that I set rules, because rules are how cowards build armor plate by plate. Rule one: never answer aloud. Rule two: never go behind the wall after dark. Rule three: on any night the wall speaks, make tea, drink it, sleep in the shop, check the seal at dawn. Twelve years, and I have broken none of them. You may think that makes me strong. It makes me a man who knows exactly how weak he is and has arranged his life accordingly.

The city, meanwhile, got colder and quieter and more obedient. Wardens' rounds doubled. A tinker on Vetch Street was taken in autumn — people said charms, people said sedition, people said nothing at all, which was safest. His stall stood empty for a week and then a warden's notice stood there instead, and after that, customers started coming to my shop in a new spirit. Not hoping. *Needing.* A sachet against nightmares became a sachet against everything. Old Mrs. Hallow gripped my wrist across the counter and said, "Whatever's in these, don't ever stop making them," and I said "chamomile's a wonder," and she pressed a coin into my palm far too large and left quickly, and I stood there holding it like it might go off.

That's the trade, then. That's the whole ledger. They give me coin for comfort; I give them chamomile and call it mercy; and behind the north wall something old and hungry gives me my mother's voice and calls it payment, and everyone in the arrangement pretends, because pretending is the only currency that never devalues.

* * *

Which brings me back to yesterday morning, and the fireplace, and the line of soot.

*What the gate wants, it already knows the way to ask.*

I stood in my shop with the morning crowd beginning to murmur past the windows — market-day noise, carts, gulls arguing over the fish-cries — and I understood, with the calm that arrives after the panic has spent itself, that the careful architecture of my life had just been read aloud by someone who built it.

Twelve years of small charms. Twelve years of teaching the gate that I would not answer, that I would not open, that the son was harder than the mother. And the prophecy, dated yesterday, says: he already knows the way to ask.

Not *will learn*. Not *may find*. Knows. Present tense. The way is known. Which means either the gate has found a question I haven't armored myself against —

— or someone is coming up Corrow Lane right now who carries the other half of the paper I burned.

I looked up. Through the frost-feathered window, between the passing carts, a figure had stopped across the lane. Young, hooded despite the hour, ink-stained fingers curled around the strap of a satchel too heavy for one shoulder — the specific, unmistakable posture of a scholar traveling light and leaving fast. She was looking at my sign. Then at my window. Then, directly, deliberately, at me.

Snow had begun to fall. It was the last week of harvest, and the sky over Corrow Lane was letting go of white.

She crossed the road.
