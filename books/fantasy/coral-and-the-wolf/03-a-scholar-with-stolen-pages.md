## 3. A Scholar with Stolen Pages

The trouble with stealing a prophecy from the Royal Archive is that you can't exactly report it stolen. I learned this the hard way, standing in the Archive's great reading hall three years ago while Magister Corvane read my name aloud from a list of irregularities and two wardens-in-training escorted me past shelves I had known better than my own heartbeat.

"You were found," Corvane said, "with restricted materials in your possession."

"I was found *reading* them," I said. "In the building built for reading. The scandal of it."

They took my credentials, my key, my desk — third row, north wall, by the window that fogged in winter so I could draw on it — and they did not take the pages, because the pages they knew about were decoys. Every scholar worth her chalk keeps a decoy. Mine was a treatise on irrigation law in the southern provinces, dry enough to bore a moth to tears, and it worked exactly as designed. What they never found was folded against my ribs: four leaves of vellum in a hand two centuries old, describing an event that had not happened yet.

Or so I believed, right up until the snow started falling on the ninth day of Harvestmonth, when the air should have smelled of rotting apples and woodsmoke, and instead the sky over Vellamarch opened like a purse and shook out winter as if it had been waiting all year for permission.

* * *

Let me back up, because prophecies deserve context, even the ones that ruin you.

The Amberdale Fragment — I named it that myself, which is the closest thing to immortality a disgraced archivist gets — is four leaves long and reads like a drunk man arguing with a clock. The script loops, the meter stumbles, and whoever wrote it had a positive genius for ambiguity. But the middle verses are clear enough if you've spent ten years learning to read around censorship:

*When the crown is hollow and the throne a throat,*
*when snow falls out of season on the keeper's coat,*
*the gate that has no keeper shall remember its own name,*
*and what was bound in amber shall come to feed on flame.*

And then, the verse that ended my career, because I made the catastrophic error of writing it down in a marginal note where Corvane could see it:

*On the eve the almanac cannot hold, seek the shop beneath the door.*

I showed it to him because I thought he'd be pleased. I was twenty-six and still believed institutions rewarded accuracy. Corvane read my note twice, went very pale in the way powerful men do when they intend to make their paleness someone else's problem, and said, "Where did you get this?"

"The Archive."

"From *where* in the Archive?"

"The sealed stacks. Sub-level four." I should have lied. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone: Wren Halloway, at the moment of her professional death, told the truth with her whole chest. "The catalog entry says the collection was destroyed in the Aldric Reforms."

"It *was* destroyed," Corvane said, "which means either you're lying, or it wasn't, and both of those are problems I don't intend to have."

So. Expelled, blacklisted from every library in the capital that mattered, and quietly — very quietly; there were no formal charges, because formal charges would require acknowledging the fragment existed — warned never to speak of it again. Which is how I came to spend three years as a private researcher of no particular reputation, copying deeds and tracing land disputes for lawyers who didn't ask questions, all while I hunted the one question that mattered.

*What shop sits beneath what door?*

* * *

The answer, when it finally came, arrived the way most answers do: through paperwork.

A land-registry dispute, of all things. A client wanted to know whether a certain property on Candlemaker's Row had ever changed hands since the Reforms, and whether any covenants attached to it survived the previous owner's death. I pulled the deed history expecting nothing and found instead a covenant older than the Reforms themselves, written in that same looping, stumbling hand:

*The property known as Amberdale Gate shall pass to the blood of its keepers in perpetuity, together with all duties pertaining, and no duty shall be recorded herein save the keeping itself.*

Beneath the door. The shop beneath the gate. It had been sitting in a registry drawer for a hundred years, filed between a tannery lease and a complaint about drainage, waiting for someone bored enough to read the appendices.

I sat in my rented room above a dyer's shop — everything I've ever lived above has been a shop; it's a whole character flaw at this point — and I looked at the covenant, and then I looked out the window at the sky, which had gone the color of dirty pewter three weeks too early, and I did the arithmetic every scholar dreads.

If the prophecy was real, and the date was coming, and the shop was real —

Then the keeper was real. And whatever was coming for the city would arrive at his door before it arrived anywhere else, and he would have no idea why, because nobody tells keepers anything. That's practically the job description.

I packed a bag that night. Two shirts, my notes, a knife I mostly used for sharpening pencils, and the four stolen leaves, sewn into the lining of my coat where they'd been living since the day Corvane read my name. Then I bought passage on a grain barge downriver to Vellamarch, because the capital's wardens knew my face and Vellamarch's, I hoped, did not.

The barge took six days. On the fourth, snow fell on the water and did not melt.

* * *

Vellamarch smells of candle wax and river mud and, underneath both, something older and mineral, like the stone itself is thinking about something. I walked up from the docks with my collar up and my hood down — hoods mark you as either criminal or clergy in a town this size, and I couldn't afford either reputation — and found Candlemaker's Row without difficulty, because the map in my head had been drawing itself for three years.

The shop sat low in the row, wedged between a chandler and a house that appeared to be losing an argument with ivy. Its sign was small and honest: **AMBERDALE CHARMS**, and beneath it, in smaller letters, as if apologizing for the ambition of the first line, *charms, wards, tokens &c.* The window displayed a modest array: knotted cords, painted stones, little glass bottles the color of pond water.

I stood across the street and watched it the way you watch a wild animal you've only seen in books.

Because here's the thing I hadn't let myself say out loud, not even in my notes, not even in the privacy of my own skull: the fragment didn't just describe a gate. It described a *keeper*. Singular. Specific. And every record I'd chased suggested the Amberdale line had thinned to a single thread — one person, alive, in this city, selling charms under a forbidden gate in a kingdom where magic got you hanged.

Which meant one of two things. Either he was a collaborator, knowingly sitting on the most dangerous secret in the realm —

— or he was exactly what the fragment implied: a lock who didn't know he was a lock. A man who had been handed a burden wrapped so carefully in ordinary life that he'd mistaken it for a career.

I was betting on the second. I was betting my life on it, actually, though I tried not to phrase it that way even internally, because phrasing matters. Scholars know that. Words are load-bearing.

The snow thickened. Out-of-season, wrong-month, impossible snow, falling soft and businesslike over the rooftops of Vellamarch while children in the street caught flakes on their tongues and their mothers called them inside with the particular sharpness of parents who have decided not to think about something. I watched a warden at the corner look up at the sky, frown, and write something in a notebook. Good. Let them write. A regime built on fear always mistakes weather for conspiracy, and every hour they spent investigating clouds was an hour they weren't investigating me.

I crossed the street.

The bell over the shop door was small and tin, the kind that announces rather than welcomes. Inside, the shop smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and, faintly, of cold stone — the smell of a cellar that isn't quite a cellar. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, crowded but tidy, each item tagged in a small, careful hand. And behind the counter stood a man perhaps thirty, dark-haired, sleeves rolled, sorting a tray of painted stones with the concentration of someone who finds peace in small tasks precisely because large ones are unthinkable.

Cyrus Amberdale. He looked like a man who had arranged his entire existence to avoid being looked at, and I was about to walk in and stare directly at him. No wonder he flinched.

He flinched.

"Closed in an hour," he said, not looking up. "If it's a love charm, I'll sell you a candle and tell you to talk to her yourself. Works better. Cheaper, too."

"Noted for future reference." I set my satchel on the counter and began, deliberately, to unpack it: ink, notebook, the registry copy of the covenant. "Actually, I'm here about a property matter. A covenant on this address. Older than the Reforms."

Something happened to his hands. Not much. A pause in the sorting, half a second, stones held a fraction too tight. If I hadn't spent ten years watching archivists pretend not to care about documents, I'd have missed it entirely.

"There's no covenant," he said.

"There's a covenant dated one hundred and eleven years ago, witnessed by two names that appear nowhere else in the registry, binding this property to 'the blood of its keepers.'" I turned the copy toward him. "It's a fascinating document. Mostly because of what it doesn't say. It mentions duties, plural, and then declines to record a single one. Whoever wrote it wanted it legible enough to find and illegible enough to obey. That's a very particular kind of drafting."

"That's a very particular kind of trespassing," he said, and now he did look up, and his eyes were the exact color of the river in November, gray going green going gray again, and afraid. Not of me. Of the sentence I'd just finished. "Who sent you?"

"The weather," I said.

That landed. I watched it land — watched him glance, involuntarily, at the window, where the snow kept falling with its terrible punctuality, out of season, on the ninth of Harvestmonth, and I saw the moment the careful architecture of his ordinary life developed its first visible crack.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Cyrus." I hadn't learned his name yet — I'd guessed it, from the deed and the register of births, a guess I'd been turning over for six days on a grain barge — and using it now was cruel, and necessary. "Wren Halloway. Formerly of the Royal Archive, until I read something I shouldn't and made the mistake of telling the truth about it. I've spent three years finding this shop. I'm not here to hurt you, or rob you, or arrest you, though I grant that's what all three of those people would also say. I'm here because snow is falling in Harvestmonth, and there is a prophecy in my coat lining that says when it does, something comes to a gate, and you are the last person in this kingdom whose surname matches the door behind your wall."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere upstairs, or behind, or below — I couldn't tell — a pipe knocked once, like a knuckle on wood.

Then Cyrus Amberdale did the single most informative thing available to him.

He didn't deny it. He didn't laugh, or reach for a weapon, or call for wardens, or perform the elaborate bafflement of an innocent man. He simply closed his eyes, exhaled through his nose, and said, with the weariness of someone setting down a load he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it was heavy:

"How much of it have you read?"

"All of it. Except the parts that haven't happened yet."

"And those?"

"Those," I said, "are what I've come to help you survive."

* * *

We talked for two hours while the light died and the snow kept its appointment. He gave me tea that tasted mostly of honey defending itself against cheap leaves, and I gave him the fragment, page by page, and watched him read with the expression of a man confirming a diagnosis he'd already suspected.

He told me almost nothing. That was the remarkable part. Twelve years in this shop, he admitted. His mother before him. A gate behind the wall that he maintained, oiled, and never opened. Charms that did nothing, sold honestly, priced fairly. When I asked what his mother told him about the gate, he looked at his tea for a long time and said, "She told me to keep it shut."

"And when she left?"

"She went through it."

I set down my cup. "Through the gate."

"She asked me to open it. I was nineteen. She said—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "She said it was time, and that I'd understand someday, and that the charms in the blue box were for after. Then she walked through and the gate shut and it hasn't opened since, and I have spent twelve years telling myself that's obedience instead of cowardice, and I would thank you not to look at me like that."

"I wasn't looking at you like anything."

"You were looking at me like a footnote."

Fair. I have been told I read people the way I read manuscripts — thoroughly, hungrily, and with an eye for what can be cut. I adjusted my expression and asked the question that mattered:

"Do you believe me? About the snow. About the date."

He was quiet long enough that the fire popped. "My mother left a list of dates," he said finally. "Important dates. One per year, in her hand, in a book I'm not showing a stranger. Tomorrow's date is on it. Underlined. In ink darker than the rest, like she pressed harder writing it, like her hand knew something her mouth didn't."

"So you believe."

"I believe my dead mother pressed harder." He looked at me, and the fear in him had settled into something steadier and more dangerous: decision. "What I don't believe is that you crossed half the country and burned your career on a hunch. Nobody does that. So here's my price for listening, Scholar. You tell me what *you* get out of this. The true version. Not the noble version."

And there it was — the bill, presented without malice, by a shopkeeper. I have faced wardens, magisters, and one genuinely terrifying land-dispute lawyer, and none of them unnerved me the way this man did with a teacup and a direct question.

"The true version," I said, "is guilt."

He waited.

"Three years ago I found the fragment, and I was so proud of finding it that I told the Archive. I thought they'd protect it. Study it. Instead they buried it, expelled me, and forgot it — but not before word moved the way word does in that building. Down through corridors. Sideways, into rooms I've never seen." I turned my cup slowly on its saucer. "Whatever's coming knew where this shop was because the Archive knew, and the Archive knew because of me. I didn't open your gate, Cyrus. But I may as well have mailed the Wolf a map."

He stared at me. Outside, the snow erased the street, the rooftops, the ordinary world, one soft white lie at a time.

"The what?" he said.

And that was when we both heard it — faint, from behind the wall, from the room he had never shown me and I had never needed to see:

A lock, turning.
