## 4. What the Gate Wants

The lock turned at moonrise. I know because I was watching it, which is not the same as saying I wanted to watch it.

I'd spent the evening doing what I always do when the world threatens to become interesting: inventory. Three jars of river-salt that does nothing but taste like regret. A drawer of knotted red thread sold as "protection against ill dreams," which protects exclusively against the buyer's spare coin. Forty-one luck-charms carved from driftwood, each one sanded smooth and utterly inert, each one guaranteed — by me, aloud, with a straight face — to "help where help is needed." The lie is in the guarantee, not the charm. The charms are honest. It's the commerce that's corrupt.

Wren had taken my cot, such as it is, and fallen asleep mid-argument about whether prophecy is a science or a wound. She'd said *wound*. I'd said *fraud*. Neither of us had budged, and she'd fallen asleep wearing the particular smugness of a scholar who knows she'll win tomorrow.

I sat at my workbench with a lamp turned low and watched the wall behind the shelves.

You have to understand what twelve years of watching teaches you. The Amberdale Gate doesn't look like much. A door of grey wood gone silver at the edges, iron-banded, set into the back wall of my cellar as if some architect had started a corridor and then lost his nerve. No keyhole on this side. No handle. Just a seam in the world that my family has spent four generations pretending is furniture.

It had never once moved.

At moonrise — I knew it was moonrise because the light through the cellar grating changed from lamplight-grey to something colder, bluer, the color of the snow that shouldn't be falling in harvest month — the iron bands gave a sound like a man clearing his throat before bad news.

Then the lock, which lives on the far side, which has no business being reachable by anything, clicked open.

I did not scream. I want credit for that. I set down my sanding cloth, folded it, placed it on the bench, and then screamed internally in a way that would have alarmed a physician. Wren slept through it, which told me either the sound hadn't carried or her conscience was quieter than mine.

The door didn't open. Not yet. It just stood there, unlocked, like a held breath.

I should wake her, I thought. I should wake her and we should leave, now, tonight, out the front, down the street, onto any road going anywhere the name Amberdale means nothing.

Instead I picked up the lamp and walked toward it. Because that's the thing about careful men: all our care is scaffolding around a structure we never chose, and when the structure finally groans, we find out the scaffolding isn't holding it up. It's holding us up against it.

* * *

"Mother used to say the gate dreamed," I told Wren an hour later. She'd woken — eventually, after the third click from inside the wall, a sound like a lock testing its own teeth — and now she sat on an upturned crate with a candle stub, hair like a crow that had lost an argument with weather, watching the door with the expression of a woman reading a very bad manuscript.

"Dreamed how?"

"Loudly. On certain nights. My father said it was the river under the foundations. He was a liar about most things but he was a good liar, so maybe that was true too."

"And your mother?"

"My mother said the gate remembered." I kept my eyes on the door. "She said everything that goes through leaves a shadow on the other side of the wood, and if you're quiet, and you're hers, you can hear the shadows talking."

"Is that true?"

"It's true that she said it."

Wren accepted that the way scholars accept things — with a small nod that files it under *unverifiable, keep*. Then the door breathed.

Not opened. Breathed. The bands flexed, the whole door expanding and contracting like a chest, and cold came off it in a slow exhalation that guttered both candles and left only my lamp, and the moonlight through the grating laying bars across the floor like the cell I'd been living in for twelve years without noticing.

And then, through the wood, in a voice I would have known drowned, burned, buried, or divine:

"Cyrus."

I'm going to tell you exactly what happened inside me, because it matters to what happened next. Twelve years is a long time to grieve someone whose body was never found. Grief without a grave doesn't close; it just learns to walk quietly. And my grief had learned to walk so quietly that I'd genuinely believed, most days, that it had left. That I'd paid it off. That the shop and the nothing-charms and the careful life were installments on a debt fully settled.

Her voice walked in and every quiet step it had ever taken became loud again at once.

"Cyrus, love. It's cold out here. Open the door."

"Mama." I said it before I decided to. My hand was already rising toward the wood. I don't remember deciding that either. There's a version of me that simply went to her, the nineteen-year-old version, the one who still waited up on the nights she traveled, and he took my arm and steered it forward while the rest of me watched like a man watching his own house catch fire.

"Cyrus." Wren's voice, sharp, from behind and to the left. "Cyrus, look at the frost."

"I hear her. Wren, I *hear*—"

"*Look at the frost.*"

I looked. Because her tone had the specific authority of someone citing a source, and even drowning men respect citations.

Frost was climbing the cellar walls. Climbing *from the door outward*, in patterns — and here is where the longing in my chest curdled into something colder than the frost itself — in patterns too regular to be natural. Spirals. Rings. The kind of geometry you find in snowflakes and nowhere else in weather. The frost wasn't creeping the way damp creeps. It was *arriving*, the way an army arrives, in formation.

"Mother," I said carefully, and my hand stopped a finger's width from the wood, trembling with the effort of stopping. "When did you go through the gate?"

"You know when, love. You were there."

"When?" I made it a demand. "Say the day. Say the season. Say what I was wearing."

A pause. The pause of something consulting notes. "Autumn," said my mother's voice, gently, warmly, wrongly. "You wore your blue coat. You cried. You begged me not to go, and I went anyway, because it was my duty. Let me in, Cyrus. I've done my duty too. I've been so patient out here in the dark."

Everything she said was true. That was the horror of it, the part I'd understand later and be sick over later: every word was accurate, drawn from some ledger of my worst night, recited back with perfect fidelity. And none of it was her. My mother — the real one, the one who raised me on stolen plums and invented constellations — would never have described that day in the second person like a court record. She'd have said something useless and warm. She'd have lied about the plums.

"The frost," Wren said quietly, beside me now, though I didn't recall her crossing the room. "Prophecies mention it. The hollow things can't generate warmth, so they steal the shape of it. Cold arranged in rings is a signature. Cyrus — that isn't a ghost. Ghosts are echoes. Echoes repeat. This thing is *answering you.* It's listening and composing. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Something over there heard your grief and is building a key out of it."

My hand was still raised. My fingers were numb, and not entirely from cold.

"Open the door, love." Her voice frayed at the edges now, and I realized with a fresh wave of sickness that the fraying was also composed — a calculation that a son responds to fragility, so *supply fragility*. "I've been alone so long. Twelve years, Cyrus. You counted them. So did I."

"Don't listen to the numbers," Wren said. "Numbers are bait. It's offering you the exact arithmetic of your loss because arithmetic feels like proof."

"It *is* proof—" I heard my own voice crack. "Twelve years, Wren. She's been gone twelve years and something knows that, and whatever knows it—"

"—has read the same prophecy you burned. Has read *you*, Cyrus. That's what I'm saying." Wren stepped between me and the door, small and stubborn, a hedgehog of a woman facing down a winter. "It's not remembering her. It's reading your memory and performing it back. Every word is true and every word is false, and the only way through is to refuse the comfort even though refusing feels like killing her again."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. In it, the frost reached my boots and climbed them, delicate spirals lacing the leather, beautiful the way traps are beautiful.

"Cyrus." My mother's voice had changed. Colder. Not angrier — emptier, like a room after the furniture's been sold. "You will let me in. Your family owes a debt at this gate, and debts are collected."

"There it is," Wren whispered. "There's the landlord."

I lowered my hand. It was the hardest physical act of my life, harder than lifting my father's coffin, harder than opening the shop the morning after she vanished, harder than smiling at forty-one customers a week while selling them nothing. I lowered my hand and stepped back, and the nineteen-year-old inside me screamed the whole way down, and I let him, because he was right to scream, and because someone in this cellar had to mourn honestly since the thing at the door could only mourn theatrically.

"No," I said.

The frost stopped growing.

"No," I said again, louder, to the door, to the thing, to the boy in my chest. "You don't get her voice. You can wear it, you can quote it, you can count the years at me until midwinter — but you don't get it. Her voice is mine. I earned it. Find your own."

For a long moment, nothing. Then the door exhaled one last time, and the frost retreated — not melted, *withdrew*, rank by rank, a disciplined retreat — and the lock clicked shut again with the unmistakable sound of a merchant closing early, satisfied with the day's reconnaissance.

The silence afterward rang. Wren sank onto the crate. I sat down on the floor, because my legs had resigned without notice, and put my head in my hands, and discovered I was shaking, and let that happen too.

"You did the right thing," Wren said, after a while.

"I know."

"You're allowed to not feel like it was right."

"I know that too." I lifted my head. The lamp had recovered; it threw our shadows long across the frost-scoured floor, where faint spiral traces remained like the footprints of something that had tried the lock with a key cut from my own heart. "Here's what I can't stop thinking, Wren. It said my family owes a debt at this gate. Not the city. Not the realm. *My family.* It wasn't threatening me. It was invoicing me."

Wren was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, she had her scholar's voice on, the careful one, the one that means she's about to ruin someone's year and knows it. "Cyrus, when I was in the Archive — before they expelled me — there was a sealed folio. Amberdale provenance records. I never got past the seal, but the catalog entry survived, and catalog entries are written by clerks who gossip in ink." She looked at me the way you look at someone before telling them the surgery is necessary. "The entry called your family line 'the Lock.' Capital L. As in: not keepers of the gate. Part of it."

Outside, above the shop, above the street, the unnatural snow fell on and on, and somewhere across the sleeping city a king who was not a king prepared another address about mercy.

And behind the wall, patient as debt, the gate waited to see what kind of lock I'd turn out to be.
