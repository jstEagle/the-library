## 5. The King's Winter Address

I had a theory about coronations, executions, and royal addresses, which was that they were all the same event wearing different hats. A crowd gathers. Somebody important stands above the crowd. The crowd is told what to feel, and feels it, more or less, and then everyone goes home and tells their neighbors they were there, closer to history than they actually were.

I'd been to four royal addresses in my life. The first three I attended as a scholar of the Royal Archive, standing in the reserved section with my colleagues, taking notes nobody would ever read, whispering commentary nobody should have heard. The fourth I attended pressed between a fishmonger and a woman who smelled overwhelmingly of lamp oil, in the general crush of Coronation Square, because the man I'd come to warn about an apocalypse insisted we needed to "see it ourselves," and because I had no better plan.

You will notice the archive did not invite me this year. That's what reading the wrong pages gets you. Well — reading the right pages, technically. The Archive has strong opinions on the difference.

"Stop fidgeting," Cyrus said. He had his hood up and his chin down, doing a remarkable impression of a man with nothing whatsoever hidden under his shop. Twelve years of practice, presumably. He looked like a fence post pretending to be a slightly guiltier fence post.

"I'm not fidgeting. I'm scanning."

"You're vibrating."

"Scholars vibrate. It's how we think." I elbowed a path through the crowd toward a spot with a view of the dais. Snow was falling again — third day of Frostmonth, which even in a bad year meant weeks early, and this year the snow came down fat and unhurried, like it owned the place and had already redrawn the deed. The square had been swept for the address, but the drifts were rebuilding at the edges, climbing the statues, softening the corners of the world. Somewhere behind us a child was crying because her mittens were wet, and honestly, child, same.

"They moved it up," I said. "The address. It was supposed to be at the solstice."

"Maybe he couldn't wait," Cyrus said. His voice was flat in a way I was learning to distrust. Flat the way ice is flat right before you find out there's water under it.

The bells rang noon, all nine of them, slightly out of step with each other as always, and the crowd noise dropped to that particular hush — the sound of several thousand people deciding to be afraid in unison. I've stood in reading rooms quieter than that hush, but never one so large.

Then the king came out onto the balcony.

Now. Let me tell you what I knew about King Aldric before that morning, because I want to be fair to him, and fairness is a discipline like any other. Aldric IV took the throne thirty-one years ago. In his ninth year he issued the Edict of Ash, outlawing the working of magic in all forms, and since then somewhere north of four hundred people had gone to the gallows for charms, cantrips, hedge-healing, weather-sense, and — in one case that still made me furious to think about — a lullaby with an unusually good rhyme scheme. He was not beloved. He was endured, which in a king is a kind of success.

He was also, according to every record I could find, sixty-one years old, gouty, half-blind in his left eye, and famously afraid of heights.

What stood on the balcony that morning was tall. Straight-backed. It wore the king's face the way a glove wears a hand — correctly, completely, with attention to every crease — and none of it fit. The left eye was sharp. The posture was a soldier's. And when it raised its hand for silence, the hand didn't tremble, and I thought: *there's our plague announcement, delivered by something wearing Aldric like a coat,* and then I thought: *don't be ridiculous, Wren, you've been sleeping in a flooded archive,* and then the shadow moved wrong and both thoughts died at once.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. First came the speech.

"My people," said the thing in the king's voice, and the voice carried across the whole square without effort, which no sixty-one-year-old lungs should manage. "For three months, a sickness has crept through our southern provinces. You have heard its name whispered. You have buried its dead."

It paused. The snow fell into the pause and filled it up.

"I will tell you now what my physicians would not: there is no cure coming. The colleges have tried. The remedies fail. Prayer fails." Another pause, longer, and I watched the crowd lean into it, hungry and horrified, the way crowds do. "But I am your king. And I have found another way."

Beside me, Cyrus had gone very still. Not fidgeting anymore. Not anything.

"Winter stops the sickness," the king said. "Cold kills contagion. Cold purifies. So I have asked the realm for patience, and I have asked the season for help, and the season has agreed." He spread his arms — a gesture that should have been grand and instead looked like something unfolding. "There will be no spring this year. There will be winter upon winter, until the sickness is starved from our borders. This is not punishment. This is mercy. I give you the long winter so that you may live to see the short spring after it. Rejoice, my people. You are being saved."

And here is the part I will never put in a formal citation, though I've written it three times in my private notes and burned it twice: the crowd *cheered.*

Not all of them. Not loudly. But there was applause — scattered, numb, obedient applause, hands coming together because hands must do something when terror has nowhere else to go. The fishmonger beside me clapped twice and stopped, looking embarrassed. The lamp-oil woman wiped her eyes. A man near the front shouted "Long live the king!" with the desperate volume of someone who needed very much to have been the first to say it.

I didn't clap. Neither did Cyrus. We stood in the middle of all that fear dressed up as gratitude, and I felt something curdle in my chest that had been, until that moment, merely dread.

Because I had read the speech. Or rather, I had read *a* speech — the draft of it, three years ago, in a folder in the Archive's restricted annex labeled ALDRIC: ADDRESSES (DRAFTS, REJECTED), because I was twenty-four and bored and believed rules about folders were suggestions. The rejected drafts were all ordinary: plague measures, grain tariffs, an apology for a bridge collapse. There was no eternal winter in any rejected draft. Which meant either the king had written this one recently, in secret, alone —

— or the king hadn't written it at all.

"He's lying," Cyrus said. Low, under the fading applause. "Or — no. That's the problem. Listen to the words. Every word is almost true. Cold does slow sickness. There is a plague; I've sold fever-charms to southbound merchants all autumn. Whoever wrote this knows exactly how to make a lie out of true things."

"That's what I keep trying to tell you," I said. "That's what prophecy *is.* True things arranged into a trap."

But he wasn't listening to me anymore, because the king stepped back from the balcony rail, and that's when I saw it too.

The sun was high and thin behind the snow-clouds — a pale coin of light, enough to throw shadows, and the balcony threw a proper one, straight down the palace facade. Everyone on the balcony cast a shadow that behaved. The guards. The herald. The two courtiers flanking the doors.

The king's shadow did not.

It lay on the stone beneath him, black and crisp-edged, while the others' shadows blurred grey in the diffused light. And as the king turned — just turning, the small pivot a man makes to walk back inside — the shadow turned the *other way.* Briefly. Like a door swinging against its hinge. Then it snapped back into obedience so fast I doubted my own eyes, and the king walked inside, and the doors closed, and the crowd began to move all at once, a slow cold river of people going home to tell each other they'd been saved.

Cyrus grabbed my sleeve. His grip was hard enough to hurt and his face had gone the color of old candle wax.

"Tell me you saw that," he said.

"The shadow."

"The shadow." He exhaled, and the exhale shook. "My mother used to say — she used to say you can bind a thing to a shape, but the binding shows in the shadow. Shadow doesn't lie. Shadow doesn't know how." He was quoting, I realized. Reciting something learned young, worn smooth like a river stone from handling. "Wren. That is not Aldric."

"It's wearing Aldric."

"Yes." He said it the way you confirm a diagnosis. "Yes, that's exactly it. Wearing."

We let the crowd carry us — arguing in a stationary knot in Coronation Square is how you meet the wardens — and for a hundred yards we moved in silence, shoulder to shoulder with the saved and rejoicing, past a stall where someone was selling roasted chestnuts nobody wanted and a shrine where someone had already hung a ribbon of white cloth, mourning-wear for spring itself.

At the corner of Threnody Street, where the crowd thinned enough to breathe, Cyrus stopped and turned to face me, and I saw that whatever had been cracking in him since the night the prophecy named his surname had cracked clean through.

"I need to tell you something and you're going to be scholarly at me, and I need you to not be scholarly at me."

"I make no promises."

"My gate opened three nights ago." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "On its own. At moonrise. The lock turned itself. And something on the other side spoke in my mother's voice and asked to be let in, and I didn't open it, because you told me not to, and I have spent three nights telling myself you were right." His jaw worked. "Today I watched a dead-tall lie stand on a balcony and promise an endless winter, and my gate opens the same week, and I am done pretending those are different problems."

I should note, for the historical record, that I did not say *I told you so.* Scholars are trained to resist these impulses. Mostly.

Instead I said: "How far under the palace are the old cisterns?"

Cyrus blinked. "...Why?"

"Because everything in the prophecy happens at the gate, but the Wolf that the prophecy describes isn't at the gate. It's *wearing a king.* And if I've learned one thing from twelve years of archives, it's that you cannot understand a thing from only one end." I pulled my collar up against the snow. "Also, the cisterns connect to the sub-archive where I've been sleeping, and I would like my notes, and possibly my boots."

Something that was almost a laugh crossed his face and didn't stay. "You sleep in a flooded basement."

"It's dry-ish. Scholarly. Very atmospheric." I started walking, because if I stopped walking I would think about what I'd seen on that balcony, and what I'd seen in the annex folder three years ago, and the order in which I'd seen them, and a question I had been keeping in a locked drawer at the back of my mind ever since: *how did the Wolf learn there was still an Amberdale to worry about?*

Who reads forbidden prophecies? Scholars read forbidden prophecies. Who reports findings to superiors? Good scholars. Ambitious scholars. Scholars hoping to be reinstated.

I kept the drawer locked and walked faster.

"Cyrus," I said. "Before we do anything foolish — and we are going to do something foolish, that much is settled — I need to see your gate. Properly. With a scholar's eye. There may be verses that map to it."

"There's a warden post on my street."

"There's a warden post everywhere. That's rather the point of the regime."

He looked at me for a long moment. Snow collected on his hood and on my shoulders and in the space between us where an argument should have been. Behind him, Coronation Square was emptying, and the snow was already filling in the crowd's footprints, erasing the evidence that thousands of people had stood together and agreed to be afraid.

"All right," he said finally. "Come at dusk. Use the cellar stair, not the shop door. And Wren —" He hesitated, and for a second I saw the nineteen-year-old who'd inherited a locked door and a dead woman's instructions instead of a mother. "If my mother's voice comes again while you're there, don't let me answer it. No matter what I say. No matter what *she* says."

"No matter what," I agreed.

He nodded once and turned down Threnody Street, and I watched him go — a charm-seller with a hollowed-out face, walking away from a square where he'd just seen his family's ancient enemy standing on a balcony wearing the realm's crown — and I thought, with the strange clarity that sometimes visits me in bad weather: *this is the part of the story where it stops being about pages.*

Then I went to get my boots.

* * *

The snow kept falling all afternoon. By dusk, Coronation Square's proclamation had been painted onto boards and posted at every crossing: THE LONG WINTER IS THE KING'S MERCY. REPORT HOARDERS. REPORT SEDITION. Underneath, in smaller letters, someone had added in charcoal: *report what?* — and the wardens had scrubbed at it and failed, because charcoal in snow-melt is forever.

I passed the posting outside the Archive itself — my Archive, ex-Archive, the building I'd been dragged out of two years ago with my satchel confiscated and my name struck from the rolls. The lights were on in the upper windows. My former colleagues were working late, no doubt drafting the official commentary on the king's address: *His Majesty's seasonal initiative, unprecedented in scope, consistent with established royal prerogative…*

They'd write it beautifully. That was the thing about the Archive. It could make anything sound reasonable if you gave it enough footnotes. It had made my expulsion sound reasonable. *Persistent unauthorized access to restricted materials, disregard for classification protocols, and — final entry, the one that ended me — unsubstantiated claims regarding the authenticity of Her Majesty Queen Isolde's death records.*

Unsubstantiated. I had the substantiation in my head and it had teeth marks in it, but you can't cite memory, and they'd taken my copies.

I let myself into the sub-archive through the flooded stairwell, boots in hand, hem soaked to the knee, and gathered my notes into oilcloth while a meter of dark water sat perfectly still around the shelving legs like it was also waiting to hear what happened next. Verse by verse, I checked the prophecy against the day's events, and verse by verse it matched, and matching is a terrible feeling when what's matching is a cage being built around you.

*When frost comes early and the crown lies twice* — check.

*And mercy is the mask the hunger wears* — I touched today's proclamation board in my memory. Check.

*Then look to the shadow, for the shadow keeps the shape the thief forgot.*

I sat in the dark with my wet boots and understood, finally and completely, that I had not stumbled into this story. I had *started* it. Three years ago, a girl with something to prove had carried a folder of forbidden pages up four flights of stairs and laid it on her superior's desk and said, look, look what the king really is.

And whatever wore the king had looked back.

I blew out my lamp, put on my boots, and went to keep my promise about dusk — about gates, and mothers' voices, and the things I would not let Cyrus answer.

I was two streets from the shop when I saw the wardens.

Six of them, lanterns bobbing, moving on Amberdale Lane with the unhurried purpose of men who had been given an address and a reason. And at their head, consulting a paper in the lantern-light, was a woman I knew — grey-haired, upright, precise as a footnote — carrying the Archive's authority in her pocket and, I would have bet my last copper, a tip about a charm-shop keeper whose name had surfaced somewhere it shouldn't.

Someone had reported him.

I stood in the falling snow, halfway between the Archive I'd lost and the friend I was about to lose, and did the arithmetic of what I could say, where I could run, and which of us the prophecy could spare.

The wardens reached his door.
