## 6. Charm-Seller, Arrested

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was the first wrong thing about it.

Wardens don't knock at half past nine. Wardens knock before dawn, when you're stupid with sleep and your hands are nowhere near anything useful. Or they come at midnight, boots first, door second. Half past nine is the hour of respectable commerce, when a man might legitimately have customers — and a man with customers is a man who cannot run.

So when the three knocks landed on my shop door, even and official as a metronome, I was already moving toward the back room while my mouth said, "One moment, please," in the pleasant voice I use for people who buy love charms for their neighbors' wives.

I had maybe forty seconds. Here is what forty seconds buys you: time to take down the key-ring from its hook behind the dried lavender, time to feel how cold the brass is against your palm even in summer-warm October, time to look once around the shop that has been your whole careful life and understand that looking is all you get.

The bell over the door didn't ring. That was the second wrong thing. Someone had muffled it — packed wool into the mechanism, quick and practiced — which meant this wasn't a raid. It was an execution with paperwork.

"Cyrus Amberdale." The voice came through the wood, flat as slate. "By order of the Crown Wardens, open your premises."

I have spent twelve years being unremarkable on purpose. I want that understood, because everything that happened next only makes sense if you understand what I was losing. I wore gray. I priced things fairly. I laughed at jokes about the weather and agreed that magic was a filthy business, best stamped out, yes, quite right, terrible what it did to people. My charms were knots and beads and dried flowers that did precisely nothing, sold to people who needed to *do* something about their dying mothers or failing harvests, and doing nothing is sometimes the kindest thing you can sell a frightened person. I knew every one of them by name. Marta who bought a birthing charm for each of her daughters. Old Pell, who came every month for a luck-knot he swore kept his boat afloat and which I refilled free because his boat stayed afloat through stubbornness alone. The little Ferro girl who bought a courage bead before her first day at the dye-works and paid in buttons.

That shop was not a business. It was a disguise so complete I had stopped knowing where the costume ended.

I went down the cellar stairs two at a time, key-ring clenched so hard the teeth bit crescents into my hand, and behind me the front door gave way with a crack like green wood splitting.

* * *

Let me tell you what the cellar looked like, because I will never see it again, and someone ought to record it. Shelves of jarred honey from the market, because a keeper should seem to eat like other men. The pickle crock. My mother's chair, still pushed under the table after twelve years, because moving it felt like admitting something. And against the far wall, beneath a rack of drying sage, a door.

Not a hidden door. A *door*. Oak, iron-banded, with a lock older than the city's name. Anyone who found it would find a wall of damp stone beyond, and most people who found it assumed it led to a collapsed cistern and left it alone. The wardens had searched the shop twice in twelve years — routine sweeps, a bored lieutenant poking at jars — and both times they'd stood in this cellar and looked at the sage rack and seen nothing worth their trouble.

The Amberdale gate doesn't hide itself. It just waits for people to stop asking questions. My mother taught me that. She taught me most of what I know, in the four years between my father's death and her own disappearance, and she taught it fast, like a woman writing a letter she knows will arrive late.

"Locks are honest," she used to say, turning the key-ring in the lamplight. Brass keys, seven of them, graduated small to large, worn smooth as river stones. "It's the doors that lie. Remember that, Cyrus. When everything else goes wrong, trust the thing that only does one job."

She vanished through that door when I was nineteen, on a night in late autumn much like this one, and I have hated the door and loved her in equal measure ever since, which I've learned is the standard rate of exchange in families like mine.

Boots on the floor above. Voices. Something heavy going over — the counter, probably, and Marta's last order with it. I fitted the smallest key, the one shaped like a fishhook, turned it, and heard the lock answer with a sound like a sigh held for a century.

The door opened onto the antechamber, and I stepped through, and pulled it shut behind me just as the cellar door at the top of the stairs banged open and light poured down the steps like spilled milk.

* * *

The antechamber is a long hall of grey stone that exists, as far as I can determine, slightly to the side of the world. There are no windows, but there is light, sourceless and pale, the color of the sky an hour before snow. The walls are carved with names — hundreds of them, thousands, spiraling down through centuries — and every name is Amberdale. At the far end stands the gate itself: black stone, tall as two men, carved all over with coral branching up from the base and wolves running down from the crown until the two patterns meet in the middle and become, if you squint, the same pattern.

Twelve years I've come here to oil the hinges and sweep the floor and talk to the gate like a madman, telling it the news — Marta's granddaughter born, Pell's roof fixed, another winter survived. Twelve years of keeping it locked, oiled, swept, and shut, per instructions I received from a dead woman through the medium of a key-ring.

And now, standing inside with the door sealed behind me and the sound of my own life being dismantled overhead, I noticed the third wrong thing of the morning.

The gate was warm.

I put my palm flat against the black stone and it was warm the way a cheek is warm. Not sun-warmth. Blood-warmth. Somewhere behind that stone, something was awake and paying attention, and it had been paying attention for days — since the prophecy burned and rewrote itself on my worktable, since the snow started falling out of season in a city that doesn't see snow before midwinter, since Wren stood in my shop with stolen pages shaking in her hands and told me the date named in a century-old verse was *today*, and my surname, and the word *opens*.

Overhead, muffled through two floors and a lifetime, the wardens were finding things. I heard a shelf go. I heard Lieutenant Voss — I'd know his voice anywhere; he bought a headache charm off me once and I charged him double out of spite — shouting orders. And then, clearer than the rest, a sound that made my stomach drop through the floor and keep falling: the scrape of the sage rack being dragged aside, and then fists on the oak door I'd come through.

They'd found it. Of course they'd found it. An anonymous tip, Wren had said, and tips that precise don't come from neighbors annoyed at your chimney smoke. Someone had told them exactly what to look for. Maybe someone who'd read the same pages Wren stole. Maybe someone wearing a crown.

"Cyrus Amberdale!" Voss's voice, right against the wood now, closer than a voice should be able to travel. "Open this door in the King's name!"

There's a certain dark comedy in being ordered to open a forbidden magical gate in the name of a king whose laws forbid exactly that. I almost said so. Twelve years of practice kept my mouth shut even with no one to hear it.

Instead I did what my mother would have done, which is think about keys.

Seven keys. Six of them fit locks in this chamber — the door behind me, the maintenance hatches, the old lamp-boxes, nothing that mattered. But the seventh, the largest, the one shaped like a wolf's head with the teeth filed down so it fits nothing visible, had never fit anything in twelve years of trying. I used to think it was ceremonial. Sentimental. My mother's one indulgence.

I took it off the ring and walked toward the gate, and as I crossed the threshold-stones the warmth coming off the black stone increased until I could feel it on my face like standing near an oven, and the carvings — I swear this, and I know how it sounds — the carvings had moved. The wolves ran lower. The coral grew higher. They were closer together than they'd been yesterday, closer than they'd been in twelve years, and in the narrow space between the two patterns the stone had gone thin. Translucent. Like ice over water.

Through it, faintly, I could hear something breathing.

"Amberdale!" The door behind me shuddered. Iron bands sang. They'd brought a ram, or a mage, or both, and either way I had minutes at best. "We know you're in there! Open in the King's name and the court may show mercy!"

Mercy. From a crown that hangs children for lighting candles without permits. From a king whose shadow, Wren and I had watched it together from the crowd in Coronation Square, moves wrong — slides across the cobbles a half-second behind its owner like a reflection in delayed water. I thought of the frost on every window in the city, thick as a thumb even indoors. I thought of the proclamation: eternal winter as mercy against plague, the plague no one had seen, the mercy no one had asked for. I thought of the little Ferro girl buying courage with buttons, and wondered if anyone had told her yet that her charm-seller was a criminal.

They would tell her today. They'd post my name on the square boards, AMBERDALE, CYRUS — CHARM-SELLER — ARREST BY ORDER OF THE CROWN WARDENS, and by nightfall the whole city would know there had been a real keeper under the harmless little shop all along, and every person who ever bought a knot or a bead from me would be terrified, interrogated, made to remember my face. Marta. Pell. The girl with the buttons. Every kindness I'd done converted, at a stroke, into evidence against the people I'd done it for.

That's the arithmetic of survival nobody warns you about. You can save yourself, but you can't save yourself *clean*. Everything you carry out of a burning house has smoke on it.

The door behind me cracked along one iron band. Dust sifted from the lintel.

I pressed the wolf's-head key against the thin place in the stone, mostly because I had nowhere else to put it, and it sank in.

Not slid. Sank. The stone closed around it like flesh around a splinter, and the breathing on the other side stopped, and for one long moment the whole antechamber was so silent I could hear my own heart doing its stupid animal work. Then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the walls, from the names carved in them, from the warm stone under my hand — a voice.

Not my mother's. I want that recorded too, because later, very soon, I would hear my mother's voice come out of that gate and nearly lose everything to it. This was different. This was older and flatter, a voice like wind through a keyhole, like winter reading aloud.

*"Keeper."*

"I'm not—" I started, and stopped, because the word had landed somewhere true and I both wanted and didn't want to examine why.

*"You kept it well,"* the voice said, and I couldn't tell if it meant the gate, the oath, or the lie of my whole adult life. *"Kept things are so much sweeter to open."*

The door behind me burst. I heard it rather than saw it — the crash, the shout, boots flooding into the antechamber — and I did the only thing left, the thing the seventh key had apparently been waiting twelve years for me to be desperate enough to do. I turned the key.

The gate opened.

Not all the way. A seam of light split down the middle of the black stone, thin as a blade, wide enough for a man sideways, and cold poured out of it — real cold, absolute cold, the cold of a place that has never once heard of summer — and I went through it because staying meant the hangman's rope and going through meant whatever this was, and I have always, God help me, chosen the unknown door.

Behind me I heard Voss screaming orders, and boots skidding on frost that bloomed across the antechamber floor faster than any winter I'd ever known, and then the seam swallowed me and the light snapped shut, and the last thing I heard from the world I grew up in was my own name, shouted by strangers, posted already on every board in the city:

Cyrus Amberdale. Charm-seller. Arrested.

* * *

On the other side, there was snow.

A courtyard — no. The bones of one. Pillars of black stone under a sky the color of dirty wool, and snow falling upward, gently, from the ground to the clouds, as though the world here had gotten tired of the usual arrangement. The air tasted of metal and old grief.

And in the middle distance, between two pillars, someone was standing. A woman. Small, dark-haired, wearing the shawl I'd buried empty in the family plot twelve years ago.

"Cyrus," said my mother's voice, warm as bread, warm as blood, warm as the gate had been. "Oh, my love. You came. Let me in — let me come home—"

My feet moved before my heart finished voting. Two steps. Three.

Then a hand caught my arm from the shadows of the pillar, and Wren — furious, frost-burned, holding stolen pages against her chest like armor — put her mouth against my ear and said the words that saved my life:

"That isn't her. Whatever it is — it knows what she sounded like. It's been listening to this gate for a hundred years."

The figure between the pillars tilted its head, all at once, too far, like a bird considering something edible.

And behind us, faint but unmistakable, through the seam in the black stone, came the sound of the wardens finding the seventh key still turning in its socket — and beginning, patiently, with iron bars and cold hands, to pry the gate open from the outside.
