## 8. An Oath in Amber

The antechamber did not want me there.

I don't mean it was hostile. Hostility would have been almost comforting — a thing that hates you has at least noticed you exist. No, the antechamber regarded me the way an old housekeeper regards a grandchild who has turned up muddy and unexpected at a funeral: with a kind of weary, disapproving patience, as if to say *so you've finally come, and look at the state of you.*

I had come through the cellar door with my mother's key-ring clenched in one fist and soot from my own burning shop in my lungs. Behind me, above me, twelve years of careful life were going up in warden fire — the counter where I'd sold ten thousand charms that did nothing, the shelf of dried lavender arranged just so to suggest apothecary rather than heresy, the little brass bell over the door I had polished every morning because routine was the only prayer I knew. All of it ash now, or soon to be. My name was already nailed to the post outside the Wardenhouse: CYRUS AMBERDALE, CHARM-SELLER, ARREST ON SIGHT.

Arrest on sight. As if sight were the dangerous part. As if looking at me too long might catch something.

I stood in the dark of the antechamber and coughed, and the cough echoed far longer than the room should have allowed.

* * *

The room itself was not large. That was the first lie it told. From the cellar stairs it looked like a modest stone chamber, maybe ten paces square, lit by nothing I could name — a grey luminescence like moonlight remembered rather than seen. But when I took three steps forward, the walls did not come closer. When I spoke aloud — "Hello?" — the word went out of me and kept going, diminishing, until I lost it somewhere behind the far stone the way you lose a coin down a well.

The gate itself dominated the wall opposite the stairs. I had known about it my whole life, the way you know about a birthmark — always there, never discussed. My mother had shown it to me once when I was small, lifted the false shelving in the shop's back room and let me look at the great slab of black wood bound in iron bands gone green with age. She had said only: "It opens for no one, Cy. Not even us." Then she'd made me promise to sell only harmless charms, and I'd spent nineteen years thinking that promise was about commerce.

Now the gate stood open perhaps a hand's width, and cold came through the gap like water through a cracked hull. Not winter cold. Something older and hungrier than that. And threaded through the cold, faint as a voice heard through several walls, something that might have been singing.

I did not go closer. Some part of me — the part that had spent twelve years selling lavender and lies to frightened customers — understood instinctively that the gate was like a sleeping animal's eye: approachable only if you pretended not to care about it.

Instead I looked around the antechamber properly, keeper's eyes opening for the first time, and saw what I had been too terrified to see on the night of the raid.

The walls were covered in names.

* * *

Not graffiti. Not prayers, though some were written prayer-shaped. Names — hundreds of them, thousands, cut into the stone in every century of handwriting: sharp Roman chisel-strokes, looping court hands, a whole column in a cramped script I recognized from my mother's ledgers. They spiraled up from the floor and vanished into the gloom overhead, and the longer I looked the more certain I became that the list continued past what light could reach, into stone that had no business being there.

Amberdale. Amberdale. Amberdale. Over and over, generation after generation, sometimes two or three to a line, sometimes alone with dates beside them. Near the bottom of the visible spiral, freshly cut — the stone dust still glittering in the seams — I found:

**MARA AMBERDALE. ENTERED WILLINGLY.**

And beneath it, in older letters that seemed less carved than grown, like frost forming:

**CYRUS AMBERDALE. PENDING.**

"Pending," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

There is a particular quality to discovering your own name in a place you've lived above all your life without knowing. It is not surprise. Surprise is quick; this was slow, a cold spreading outward from the sternum, because some buried part of you has always known and is only now being formally introduced to the knowledge. I had thought myself a shopkeeper. A man who inherited a failing business and a superstition and had the good sense to keep both small. And here the wall said otherwise. Here the wall had been keeping my place.

"I'm not doing it," I told the antechamber. "Whatever it is. I sell charms that don't work. That's my contribution to the magical arts. You've got the wrong Amberdale."

The antechamber, characteristically, declined to argue. It simply grew brighter by one degree, the way a host relights a lamp when a guest stops shouting, and I saw for the first time the object standing in the center of the floor.

A table. Low, plain, oak — the most ordinary thing imaginable, which after the names and the gate felt almost obscene. On it lay a single object the size of a plum: a lump of amber, honey-colored, warm-looking despite the cold, with something caught inside.

I picked it up before I could decide whether picking things up in haunted rooms was wise. My mother raised a fool as well as a coward, apparently.

Inside the amber, suspended mid-gesture, was a figure. Tiny — smaller than my thumbnail — a man in robes that might have been fine once, rendered in miniature with a precision no jeweler would bother with. One arm raised. Mouth open. And though he was smaller than a beetle, I could feel him the way you feel a storm over the horizon: pressure, intention, weight.

The moment my skin touched the amber, the memory began.

* * *

I did not see it so much as remember it, though the remembering belonged to someone else. This is the best description I can manage, and I am a liar by trade, so trust that it costs me something to say.

Firelight. A round tower room, or a room built to feel like one, its walls hung with chains that were not decoration — each chain ran into the floor and hummed faintly, tuned, like instrument strings under terrible tension. Snow beat against windows that were barred from the inside. And in the center of the room, held down by those humming chains, was the Wolf.

It wore no shape I can honestly report. My eyes insisted on giving it one — first a wolf, then a man, then a king, then a hole in the world with ambitions — and each shape lasted exactly as long as it took to be believed. What stayed constant was the hunger. Even filtered through amber and a hundred years, it came off the thing like heat off a forge. It wanted. It wanted so specifically, so patiently, that wanting seemed less like something it did and more like what it was made of.

And before it, arm raised, mouth open in the middle of words I somehow already knew, stood the first Amberdale.

He was younger than I expected. That undid me a little. In the family stories — the few, the deliberately boring ones my mother permitted — the first keeper was a patriarch, gray-bearded, monumental. This man was maybe thirty, hollow-cheeked, with ink stains on his fingers and the exhausted posture of someone who had been awake for a very long time because sleep had stopped being safe. His robes were scorched at the hem. His left hand was bandaged badly.

He was speaking. The amber held his voice the way it held his body, and it poured into me whole:

"—and I will not pretend this is mercy, because I know what it is. It is arithmetic." His voice was hoarse, a scholar's voice pushed past its limits. "The Wolf cannot be killed. We have tried everything killable. It eats crowns because crowns concentrate obedience, and obedience is the richest food there is. It ate the King of the Winter Court in a single night and wore him out to dinner. So we do not kill it. We starve it. We bind it behind the gate and we starve it, and the binding requires a lock."

The Wolf moved against its chains. The sound it made was worse than roaring. It sounded reasonable.

"And the lock," the young man continued, and here his voice changed, dropped into something rawer, "is not a mechanism. Iron fails. Spells fray — I have watched them fray tonight, all of them, everything the colleges promised me. The only lock that holds is a person. A will, renewed. An oath, spoken by blood, re-sworn each generation, so that the gate is never unwatched, never unfed, never forgotten." He laughed then, short and awful. "My masters wanted a dynasty of soldiers. Instead they got me. A translator. Third-rate. The only one of seven who survived the night, and only because I ran, and only because running gave me an idea."

He lowered his arm. The memory shivered, jumped — hours passing inside a heartbeat — and resumed with him kneeling, drawing something on the floor in chalk and what I feared was not chalk. The Wolf watched him with whatever it used instead of eyes.

"The oath passes to my children, and their children, down the line, until the Wolf dies of starvation or the world ends, whichever comes first. They will not know what they carry. That is also part of the working — a keeper who knows what he is becomes a target; a keeper who believes himself a shopkeeper is invisible. They will keep the gate shut. They will sell their little charms and mind their little fires and never once understand that they are the pin holding the avalanche." His voice cracked. "I am sorry. Whoever you are, descendant, whoever reads this in whatever amber I can afford to make — I am sorry. I chose you before you existed. There was no other way, and I have learned tonight that 'there was no other way' is the sentence monsters use, and I am using it anyway."

The Wolf spoke. Its voice was — I need to write this down even though writing it makes my hand shake — its voice was my mother's. Not similar. Hers, exactly, the exact grain of it, saying: *"They will hate you, little translator. Every one of them, down all your generations, hating a burden they cannot name. I can wait inside that hatred. Hatred keeps."*

"I know," the first Amberdale said quietly. "That's why I'm making the oath out of love instead. Watch."

He pressed his bandaged hand to the floor, to the circle, and began the words — and here the amber did something cruel and merciful at once, blurring the actual syllables so I received only their shape, their weight, the sensation of a vow settling into bone like a splinter that would work its way down through a hundred years of descendants. I felt it enter me across time. Felt it find the place in my chest where I had always, secretly, kept my fear of the gate, and fit itself there with an almost audible click.

The last thing the memory showed me was his face as the working took hold — relief and grief in equal measure, the face of a man setting down a weight by fastening it to everyone he would ever love — and his final murmur, so soft the amber barely caught it:

"There. Now you're not a prison, old hungry thing. Now you're a debt. And debts are patient, but so are families."

* * *

I came back to myself sitting on the antechamber floor with the amber in my lap and no memory of sitting down. The grey light had dimmed to embers. Somewhere beyond the gate, the singing had stopped, and the silence it left behind was worse — attentive silence, listening silence.

"So," I said to the empty room, because saying it aloud made it real and I have always needed things real before I could survive them. "I was never a shopkeeper."

Twelve years. Twelve years of measuring out rosemary and candle-wax, of charging three coppers for protection against headaches and bad luck, of telling myself that the harmless charm trade was a kindness, a quiet rebellion, a way to keep a little magic alive in a city that hanged people for it. And the truth was meaner and stranger: the charms were harmless because harmlessness was camouflage. The shop was not a shop. It was a sentry post disguised as a failure. My mother hadn't taught me caution; she had drilled me into it, day after day, *sell nothing that works, learn nothing that shows, be small, be dull, be forgettable* — because a memorable Amberdale is a dead Amberdale, and a dead Amberdale is an unlocked gate.

She hadn't been hiding me from the wardens.

She had been hiding the gate from everyone else, using me as the lock's disguise, and she had done it so well that I'd mistaken my own cage for my personality.

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, more cough than laughter, and the smoke still in my lungs made me pay for it. I sat on the cold floor of a room that had known my name since before my birth and laughed until my ribs ached, and the antechamber waited me out with that same housekeeperly patience.

Then I got angry, which was better. Anger has more uses.

"He chose us," I said. "Some third-rate translator with burnt sleeves got one good idea on the worst night of his life, and he nailed it to every descendant forever, and none of us ever got a vote. My mother died — *vanished* — for this. Entered willingly, says the wall. Willingly! Into that." I pointed at the gate and my hand was steady, which surprised me. "And me? Pending? I didn't pending anything. I opened a shop."

The gate said nothing. But the gap in it — that hand's width of dark — seemed, in the ember-light, to widen by a hair's breadth. Or perhaps the light was failing. Or perhaps it was breathing.

Because here is the thing I had not let myself think since the raid, since I came down the cellar stairs with the key-ring burning a hole in my palm: the gate had opened on its own. Before any prophecy, before Wren and her stolen pages, before the wardens and the fire — the date on the paper had come due, and the gate had begun to move. And a lock that opens itself is either broken or *picked*, and neither possibility said anything good about the strength of a hundred years of half-understood oaths.

The first Amberdale had made the binding out of love, he'd claimed. Love and arithmetic. But love thins, spread across six generations. Love turns into habit turns into lavender and candle-wax turns into a man polishing a brass bell every morning without knowing why. Maybe the oath was starving along with the Wolf. Maybe that's why the gate sang. Maybe it wasn't calling me.

Maybe it was forgetting me.

I stood up. My knees complained — I was thirty-one years old and having the worst fortnight of my life, and my body had opinions. I put the amber in my coat pocket, next to my heart, because it seemed to want to be there and because I was done pretending I didn't take orders from objects in this room.

At the top of the cellar stairs, behind the ruined false shelving, I heard footsteps.

Not warden boots — wardens march like they're billing you for it. These steps were quick, uneven, trying hard to be quiet and failing at it in the specific way of someone who has read about stealth but never practiced it under pressure. Someone was coming down into the smoking ruin of my shop, moving toward the back room, toward the shelf, toward the stair.

Toward me.

I killed the last of the light — how, I couldn't say; I pressed my palm flat against the antechamber wall and it simply obeyed, which was a question for another, more terrifying hour — and stood in the dark with my mother's key-ring open in my fist, one key between each finger like a gambler's knuckle-duster, and listened to a stranger descend into the one secret left in my life.

The footsteps reached the false shelving. Stopped.

And a voice I had heard exactly twice before, low and urgent, breathed into the dark:

"Keeper? Keeper, are you down there? It's Wren. I read the middle verses. You're going to want to sit down."
