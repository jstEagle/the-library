## 9. Loyalty, Tested Twice

The flooded sub-archive had a smell I can only describe as *old argument* — vellum and mildew and the ghost of every scholar who ever lost their temper at a footnote. I'd been down there three days, wrapped in two coats and one increasingly threadbare blanket, deciphering the middle verses of the Amberdale prophecy by candlelight while the water lapped politely at the ankles of my boots.

I want to be clear about something: I did not cry when I read what the Wolf was. Scholars don't cry over revelations. We take notes. My hand shook so badly that my notes looked like they'd been written during an earthquake in a moving carriage, but that's a different thing entirely, and I will defend the distinction with my life.

*The crown is hollow. The crown eats. What wears the king wears nothing.*

Three years of my life I'd spent chasing this text, and it took a flooded basement and a charm-seller with a burned shop to make me actually understand it. There's a lesson there about the difference between knowing and comprehension, and I'm sure it would be very edifying if I had the energy to learn it.

It was on the fourth day — or possibly the fifth; time gets soft when your calendar is damp — that I heard footsteps on the stairs above.

Not Cyrus. Cyrus moved like a man who'd spent twelve years listening for wardens: careful, heel-to-toe, apologetic about existing. These footsteps were unhurried. Certain. The footsteps of someone who had never once been told no in a room they considered theirs.

I knew who it was before I saw her.

Magistra Corvane stood at the top of the stairs holding a lantern, and she looked exactly as she always had: silver-streaked hair pinned with mathematical precision, robes pressed despite the apocalypse outside, expression arranged into the particular kindness of a woman preparing to do you harm for your own good.

"Wren," she said. "You look terrible."

"Thank you, Magistra. Three days in a sewer will do that." I didn't stand. Standing felt like surrender, though I couldn't have articulated why. "How did you find me?"

"You were always sentimental about the sub-archives." She began descending, lifting her hem above the waterline with the practiced ease of a woman who'd toured worse. "You used to sneak down here as a first-year. You told me once it was the only place in the Archive where the books hadn't been *corrected* yet."

"I said that out loud?"

"Frequently." She stopped six feet away, which was either courtesy or tactics. With Corvane it was always both. "The Archive remembers its scholars, Wren. Even the expelled ones. Especially the useful ones."

I turned my candle toward her, mostly so I could see her hands. In my experience, Magistra Corvane's intentions lived in her hands. Her face lied fluently; her fingers told the truth.

They were empty. No writ, no wardens waiting behind her. Just the lantern, and patience.

"You're not under arrest," she said.

"That's almost disappointing. It would simplify my evening."

"The city is under curfew. The frost has taken the east quarters. Two hundred people are in the charity houses with lungs full of ice, and His Majesty announced yesterday that spring will come when the plague of unlicensed magic is purged from the realm." She recited it flatly, the way she'd recite any official summary, but I caught the pause before *purged*. Corvane had been reading between lines for forty years. She knew what the sentence cost to say. "And somewhere in all of it, a charm-seller named Cyrus Amberdale vanished from a burned shop on Tallow Row, along with a woman who was expelled from this institution for theft."

"For *reading*," I corrected. "The theft was rhetorical."

"The charge was theft. I wrote it myself." Something flickered across her face — not guilt, exactly. Corvane didn't traffic in guilt. Regret, perhaps, in the way a surgeon regrets an incision. "I've had three years to think about that report."

"Has it kept you up nights? Because I have recommendations for the pillow situation down here."

"Wren." She set the lantern on a dry shelf, and the light climbed her face and made her look, briefly, like the woman who had taught me paleography when I was nineteen and desperate and certain that old texts were doors rather than walls. That was the cruelest thing about Corvane: she had genuinely been good to me. Kindness makes better leverage than threats. Every blackmailer knows it. So does every mentor. "*Why* do you think you were expelled?"

I blinked water off my eyelashes — the cellar dripped, that was all — and considered lying. Then I considered how pointless lying to Corvane was. It was like lying to an index.

"Because I found the prophecy," I said. "Because I brought it upstairs and put it on your desk and told you it named a date and a family, and you read it, and then it disappeared into the restricted vaults and I was expelled within the month for 'unauthorized handling of sealed materials.'"

"Yes."

"And now the date has arrived, the family has been burned out of house and name, and the entire city is freezing because whatever that prophecy described has been sitting in your vault for three years getting *catalogued.*"

"It was copied, cross-referenced, and submitted to the Crown for review," Corvane said, with the faint defensiveness of a bureaucrat whose procedure has just been accused of ending the world. "Which is precisely what should happen with anomalous prophetic material."

"And what did the Crown say?"

She was quiet for a moment. The water dripped. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone and snow, a bell rang the hour — the wrong hour; the frost had been confusing the clock-wards for weeks.

"The Crown said nothing," she admitted. "The Crown sent back a receipt."

"A receipt."

"*Received and filed. No action required at this time.*" Her mouth thinned. "I assumed it was bureaucratic indifference. I have since revised that assumption. I have since done a great deal of revising, Wren, which brings me to why I am standing in a flooded basement talking to the most wanted scholar in the capital instead of reporting her."

I sat up straighter despite myself. This was the part where the trap showed its teeth. Traps always show their teeth eventually; it's practically a courtesy.

"Reinstatement," Corvane said.

The word landed in the dark between us like a dropped coin.

"Full restoration. Your position, your credentials, your rooms in the scholars' wing — the ones with the window, since you complained about them constantly. Access to the restricted vaults. And more than that." She drew a folded paper from inside her coat and held it, unoffered, in the lantern light. "A commission. Royal authority to reconstruct the Archive's prophetic division under your direction. You'd be the youngest magistra in the institution's history. You'd have everything you wanted when you were nineteen and hungry and hauling stolen pages around like they were scripture."

"And in exchange," I said, "you'd like to know where Cyrus Amberdale is."

"I'd like to know what he *knows,*" she corrected, and there it was — the distinction she needed to make to keep her own hands clean. I recognized it intimately. I'd made the same distinction three years ago, when I'd carried the prophecy upstairs instead of burning it. *I'm not betraying anyone,* I'd told myself. *I'm submitting findings.* "The Crown believes he's connected to the winter. The wardens believe he's a fugitive. I believe—" she paused, and her fingers finally betrayed her, tightening on the paper until it creased, "—I believe he's the only person in this city who might understand what we're actually facing. And I believe that whatever he intends to do, he cannot do it alone, and he cannot do it safely, and a girl in a sewer with a candle is going to get herself killed for nothing."

"There it is," I said softly.

"Wren—"

"No, no, it's well constructed. I mean that sincerely. 'You'll die for nothing' is *much* better than 'betray him,' because it lets both of us pretend the second isn't hiding inside the first." I got to my feet, finally, joints cracking like green wood. "Here's what I know, Magistra. Three years ago I found a prophecy and I gave it to the institution, because I believed — I genuinely, stupidly believed — that the system worked. That knowledge given to the right hands becomes protection. And the right hands filed it. Filed it and forgot it, while whatever it warned about crawled out of its hole and put on a crown."

"The Crown's review process—"

"Sent back a *receipt.*"

Her jaw tightened. She wasn't angry; that was the terrible thing. She was *listening*, cataloguing, doing what the Archive trained us all to do — treating my fury as data.

"You think I don't know what that filing cost?" she said quietly. "You think I haven't spent three years watching the frost creep up the river and wondering? I taught you, Wren. I know exactly how your mind works. Which is why I came myself instead of sending wardens, and why I'm offering you the commission instead of the cell. Because I need the best reader I ever trained pointed at this problem with institutional power behind her — not drowned in a basement playing revolutionary with a shopkeeper."

"Cyrus is not *a shopkeeper* the way you're not *just* a bureaucrat, and we're both lying when we use those words."

"Then tell me what he is."

I hesitated.

That was the moment, looking back. Not the offer. Not the paper. The question. Because the honest answer — the answer I'd only admitted to myself somewhere around day two, huddled over the Wolf verses with my teeth chattering — was that I didn't fully know. He was a keeper of a gate I'd only read about. He was a man who sold charms that did nothing because doing nothing was the safest magic left. He was terrified, and stubborn, and he'd heard his mother's voice through a forbidden door and let me talk him out of opening it, which took a strength I'm not sure I possess. And he trusted me. Badly, grudgingly, with constant footnotes — but he trusted me, because I was the only other person alive carrying his half of the truth.

And Corvane was asking me to convert that trust into information. Into *institutional power*. Into a receipt.

"He's the lock," I said. "That's all you get. The prophecy names an Amberdale as the binding on something very old and very hungry, and if the Crown's wardens drag him in chains to the palace, they won't be arresting a fugitive. They'll be delivering the key directly to the thing that wants it."

Something shifted in Corvane's face. Not belief — belief takes longer — but the flicker of a scholar encountering evidence that contradicts her working theory. I watched her weigh it. Watched her decide, visibly, whether I was mad or whether the world was.

"You sound certain."

"I've read the middle verses. Have you?"

"They're damaged. Fragmentary."

"They're *flooded*, Magistra. There's a difference. Damage loses words. Water moves them." I gestured at my spread of drying pages, pinned to shelves with bent nails. "Come look. Bring your lantern. Read what I've reconstructed and then ask me again where Cyrus Amberdale is, and see if the answer changes."

For a long moment I thought she would. I wanted her to, absurdly — some starved corner of me still craved her approval like a plant craves a window. If Corvane read the Wolf verses and went pale, if she said *oh* in that small voice she reserved for genuine discovery, then maybe I wouldn't have to lose her. Maybe there was a version of this where the institution woke up and fought beside us instead of hunting us.

Then she folded the paper back into her coat, and I understood.

"The offer stands for six days," she said. "Midwinter eve. After that, the commission goes to someone else, and my report on your whereabouts goes where reports go." She retrieved her lantern. At the bottom of the stairs she paused, her back to me, silver hair catching the light. "I taught you to read dead languages, Wren. Teach yourself this one too: institutions survive by being survived *in*. You can't fix the Archive from outside it. You can't fix anything from a sewer."

"You'd be surprised what grows in sewers," I said. "Given enough warmth."

She climbed the stairs without answering, and her footsteps faded, unhurried and certain, and then the door at the top closed, and I was alone with the water and the candles and the enormous ringing silence where my future used to be.

* * *

I gave myself one hour to fall apart. I recommend this practice highly; scheduled collapse is far more efficient than the unscheduled kind, which tends to strike at tactically inconvenient moments.

I sat on my crate and thought about the room with the window. I thought about being nineteen, hauling the Amberdale pages up four flights of stairs with my heart hammering, thinking *this is it, this is the discovery, this is the moment my life turns real* — and it *had* turned real, just not in any direction I'd have chosen. I thought about how Corvane had been kind to me, and how kindness from powerful people is always, structurally, an investment. I thought about Cyrus saying, in the antechamber, with terrible simplicity: *You could still walk away. Go north. Take the boat. Nobody would blame you.*

Nobody would blame me. That was the whole horror of it. The offer was fair. The terms were generous. Six days from now, if I stayed in this sewer, I would be a wanted woman with no credentials, no archive access, no institutional power, and exactly one ally: a charm-seller who was himself walking toward something the prophecy implied would cost him everything.

I thought about the Wolf verses. *What wears the king wears nothing.* I thought about the courtiers with frosted eyes, about the crowd in Coronation Square applauding eternal winter because applause was warmer than dissent. About how surrender looks like peace right up until it doesn't.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest: I thought about the fact that when Corvane offered me everything, my first feeling wasn't temptation.

It was insult. As if the last three years had been a test I'd already failed once, and the universe — or the prophecy, or whatever writes these things — was checking whether I'd fail it twice.

Well.

I stood, and took down my credentials.

They were beautiful things, honestly. Heavy cream stock, the Archive seal stamped in ink mixed with silver so it caught the light — a detail I'd always found vain and now found obscene. *Wren Halloway, Journeyman Reader, Second Chair of Paleography.* A document certifying that I was permitted to know things. Folded twice, softened at the creases from three years of carrying them anyway, because even expelled, I hadn't been able to throw them away. Sentimental. Corvane had said so herself.

I lit them from my candle.

Paper burns slowly when it's cold. The seal caught first — silver ink flaring a strange brief color — and then the flame walked up the page, and I stood there in the flooded dark holding my entire former life as it turned to light and ash, and I will confess that my eyes were watering, because the smoke was acrid. That's my story and I'm keeping it.

The ashes fell into the water and spread in a gray bloom, and the water took them, and the Archive — the real Archive, the one made of people who read what shouldn't be read and told the truth about it — didn't burn at all. It never needed credentials. I'd known that for three years and been afraid to act on it.

"Right," I said aloud, to the mildew, because someone should mark the occasion. "No reinstatement. No window. No cell either, ideally, if I'm fast."

Because here was the thing I hadn't said to Corvane, the thing I'd realized only as her footsteps faded: she'd given me a deadline, and deadlines are gifts if you read them correctly. Six days. Midwinter eve. Which meant the Crown knew the date mattered — the receipt, the filing, the *no action required* — and then the sudden urgency, the sweep of the sub-archives, the personal visit from the Magistra herself. They weren't searching randomly. Someone upstairs had re-read the prophecy after three years and finally understood what midwinter meant, and now they needed the Amberdale before the night did.

And if the Crown wanted him delivered before midwinter eve — then whatever happened at midwinter, they intended to be standing next to him when it did. Holding the key.

I started gathering my pages, folding them into oilcloth with hands that had stopped shaking somewhere around the third verse and hadn't started again. I blew out all but one candle. I checked my boots, my knife, the stolen archive-magic coiled in the copper band on my wrist — three charges, no more, each one borrowed against a future I was increasingly unsure I'd owe.

The loneliness arrived right on schedule, the way it always does after a refusal. It didn't feel dramatic. It felt like the cold: total, patient, everywhere. No Archive. No mentor. No room with a window. One ally, one knife, three charges, and six days.

I climbed the stairs, pushed open the door, and stepped out into streets gone white and silent under a sky the color of a bruise.

Snow was falling out of season again — thick, deliberate snow, snow with intent behind it — and far off across the frozen city, in the direction of the palace, I could see the frost-light burning in the high tower windows like eyes.

Six days. And I had to reach a burned shop on Tallow Row before someone else did — because I'd left one thing out of my accounting, one small cold fact I'd been avoiding since Corvane walked in:

If the Crown had re-read the prophecy, then they knew about the keeper.

And if they knew about the keeper, they didn't need me to betray his location.

They only needed to watch the gate.
