## 10. The Court of Hollow Men

The trouble with stealing a dead man's coat is that it never quite fits you, in the shoulders or anywhere else.

I want to be clear that I didn't kill the clerk whose uniform I was wearing. Wren acquired it — she has her methods, and I have learned not to ask questions I don't want the answers to — from a laundry line behind the Warden's Annex, where a young man named Pell Harrow had left his entire professional identity drying next to his smalls. The coat was good wool, grey, cut for a man two fingers broader than me through the chest. The ledger he'd left in the pocket was better: an official writ of requisition for the palace stores, signed by a captain with handwriting like a spider drowned in ink.

"You're not going to read it," Wren had said, watching me study the thing by candlelight in the cellar that wasn't my cellar anymore, because my cellar was ash and my shop was ash and my name was chalked on three separate notice boards with the word *SEDITION* under it.

"I'm going to carry it," I said. "Reading it feels unlucky."

"Cyrus." She said my name the way other people say *stop that*. "The palace clerks don't read their own documents. That's the whole point of clerks. You walk in, you look bored, you hold paper, and everyone will believe you belong there, because no one voluntarily belongs there."

She was right, as she so often was, and as I so often resented. Which is how, on the ninth morning of winter out of season, I walked through the servants' gate of the royal palace holding a document I hadn't read, wearing a dead-souled coat, with charcoal rubbed into my collar to suggest a man who worked too close to a stove, and a expression I had practiced in a cracked mirror: pleasant, vacant, and faintly put-upon. The face of a man who has been sent somewhere and would very much like to be sent back.

The guards at the gate barely looked at me. One of them glanced at the writ, saw the word *REQUISITION* at the top, and waved me through with the profound weariness of a man who had processed nine hundred such papers since the frost came and could no longer remember what summer paperwork looked like.

That was the first wrongness, though I didn't know it yet. No one checks. In a city where magic is a hanging offense, where wardens once searched my shop twice a year with the thoroughness of men looking for a reason, no one checks the king's own gate. Fear makes some people careful. It made these people *smooth*.

* * *

Inside, the palace was warm.

I want you to understand what that meant. Outside those walls, the city was in its third month of hard frost. Ice had climbed the Fountain of the Nine Sisters up past the sisters' chins. The river was a road now; children skated on it while their parents sold roasted chestnuts that went cold before they cooled enough to eat. My breath had been visible indoors for weeks. Half of Copperlane heated its rooms with books, because paper was cheaper than coal.

And here, in the long corridor between the servants' gate and the great hall, the air was warm as late spring. Not fire-warm. There were hearths along the wall, yes, but they were cold, swept clean, decorative. The warmth came from everywhere and nowhere, even as a smell — the smell of the last day of autumn, cut grass and warm stone, a day that had ended twelve years ago and was being replayed here, quietly, on a loop nobody had asked for.

I knew what made that smell. I had spent twelve years selling charms against it, harmless charms, charms that did nothing, because the one thing in this world that did anything lived behind a locked door in my cellar and I had promised my mother's ghost I'd keep it there.

The Wolf. Wearing the palace like a coat that fit it perfectly.

I held my paper and I walked, and I watched, and what I saw I will be describing badly for the rest of my life, because English doesn't have the words. But let me try.

The courtiers moved through the corridor the way water moves through a channel. That's the nearest image. They flowed. There were perhaps forty of them in the great hall when I entered — lords and ladies, secretaries, a bishop, a general, women whose jewels caught the impossible springtime light — and every one of them wore the same expression. Not identical faces. Identical *expression*: a soft, settled smile, the smile of someone who has just remembered something lovely and can't quite recall what.

And their eyes.

Frosted. That is the only honest word. Not white, not blind — they saw me, all of them, their heads turning together with a soft rustle like a field of wheat deciding to lean the same direction — but there was a pale rime over the color, the way ice furs the surface of a well. You could tell there was still water down there somewhere. You just wouldn't want to drink it.

A woman near the dais laughed at something. The sound rang false in that hall, a laugh with no one inside it, a bell rung for the shape of the sound. Three courtiers nearby laughed too, half a beat later, in exactly the same pitch.

I am not a brave man. I have never claimed otherwise; cowardice, honestly priced, is the foundation of my entire career. Every instinct I owned told me to drop the paper, walk out, keep walking until the sea. But beneath the fear was something worse, something that kept my feet moving down that hall among the smiling frozen people:

It looked *peaceful*.

That's the horror I need you to sit with, the thing I sat with for the length of that hall. These people were not suffering. Nothing about them suggested torment. They had surrendered whatever it is that makes a person a person — the friction, the wanting, the small daily war between what you are and what you wish you were — and in exchange they had been given this: warmth, smiles, the end of every argument they'd ever have with themselves. The Wolf wasn't torturing them. The Wolf had *finished* them, the way you finish a sentence, the way you finish a meal. And they glowed with the contentment of the completed.

I thought of my mother, and the gate, and the choice she'd made, and for one black moment I understood the appeal so completely that I had to dig my nails into my palm to stay myself.

"Requisition?" said a voice at my elbow.

* * *

He was a steward, grey-liveried, frosted-eyed like all the rest, and he stood closer than strangers stand. This close, I could see the frost properly: it moved. Slowly. Like weather seen from very high up.

"Stores," I said, and my voice came out blessedly dull. "Cellar inventory. Captain's orders." I offered the writ. He took it and looked at it for four full seconds without appearing to read it, and I understood that reading was optional here, that the paper was a ritual object, that meaning itself had been gently confiscated from this building along with everything else.

"The deep cellars are closed," he said.

"Captain says count them anyway." I shrugged, the shrug of a man whose opinions live elsewhere. "Not my crown."

Something flickered across his face at the word *crown* — a twitch, a ripple, like a reflection disturbed by a thrown stone — and then smoothed away into the settled smile again.

"Third landing," he said. "Past the cistern stair. Don't disturb the floor."

"Don't disturb the—"

But he had already turned away, flowing back into the current of the court, and I was left holding my useless paper and a phrase that lodged under my skin like a splinter: *don't disturb the floor.*

Wren's voice in my head, dry as archive dust: *Cyrus, when a frosted man tells you not to disturb the floor, the correct scholarly response is to go disturb the floor immediately.* Except Wren wasn't in my head. Wren was across the city in a flooded sub-archive surrounded by stolen pages, and if I died down here she would probably write a monograph about it, and I found, oddly, that this thought steadied me more than any prayer would have.

Third landing. Past the cistern stair.

I walked.

* * *

The corridors emptied as I went down, which surprised me until I thought about it. Courtiers are drawn upward, toward the throne, toward the warmth, toward the source. Nobody flows downward. The walls changed as I descended — gilded plaster giving way to old granite, then to granite so old it had gone glassy, worn smooth in places as if by hands, or by centuries of something passing along it.

And the warmth faded. By the second landing the false springtime had drained out of the air entirely, and the cold I met coming up the stairs was real cold, stone cold, honest cold, and I have never in my life been so relieved to be freezing. Cold means gaps in the armor. Warmth like that upstairs is total. I stood on the third landing shivering in a dead man's coat and thought: *this is what hope feels like now. Teeth chattering. Good to know.*

The cistern stair was there, exactly where the steward said, a black mouth in the wall breathing up damp. But I made myself pass it first, because the deep cellars were my story and stories get checked, eventually, even here. I counted doors. I muttered numbers. I wrote nothing down, because clerks who write things down get read, and I had no interest in being read by whatever read things in this place.

Then, done performing, I turned back toward the stair —

— and heard the screaming.

* * *

I want to tell you it was muffled. It wasn't. It came up through the floor itself, through the stone, as if the stone had been made to carry it, and it was not the screaming of a man in pain, or not only that. It was the screaming of a man who has been in pain for so long that pain has stopped being the point and become merely the medium, the ink, the thing he writes in because there is nothing else left to write with.

It was a king's scream. Even ruined, even starved down to pure sound, it had authority in it. Twelve years of authority, screaming into granite.

Every hair on my body stood up. My mother's key-ring — I carried it always now, three iron keys and one of amber, warm against my chest — seemed to pull toward the sound, the way a compass needle pulls, and I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *that's him. That's Aldric. He's not missing. He's not dead. He's under the throne room. He's been under the throne room, screaming, while something wearing his face proclaimed eternal mercy to a frozen city.*

Don't disturb the floor.

I stood on that landing for a long moment, listening to a king scream beneath my feet, and I understood — finally, fully, in my body and not just my head — what we were doing. Not politics. Not prophecy. Somewhere under this palace was a living man being eaten alive in slow motion by a thing that had borrowed his smile, and above him forty hollow people drifted through a fake spring, perfectly content, perfectly finished, and the whole rotten arrangement rested on one simple cruelty: that no one downstairs could hear him.

The Wolf had built a palace on top of its meal. Of course it had. That's what you do with a meal you intend to keep.

I pressed my palm flat against the wall, over the screaming, a stupid gesture, a child's gesture, and the stone was vibrating faintly, or I imagined it was, and I said — aloud, softly, like a fool, like a keeper, like an Amberdale —

"Hold on. We're coming. Both of us. I promise."

The screaming paused.

Just for a heartbeat. Just long enough. And in that gap, beneath the pain, I heard — or dreamed I heard, and I have decided to live in the version where I heard it — a single word ground out of twelve years of darkness:

"*Amberdale.*"

Then the screaming resumed, and somewhere far above us, in the warm false spring of the great hall, every frosted eye in the palace turned at once toward the third landing — and the steward with the moving weather in his gaze began, unhurried, to descend the stairs.

I ran for the cistern, keys hot against my heart, already knowing that the easy part of this war was over — and that the thing upstairs knew my name.
