## 11. Midnight with the Wolf

I want it noted, for whatever record survives this winter, that I did not go looking for the Wolf. The Wolf came looking for me.

That's the part no one tells you about monsters. In the charm-shop version — the one I sold to children for two coppers apiece — you have to summon them. You draw the circle wrong on purpose, or you say the name three times into a well, and then, and only then, does the thing come slouching up out of the dark to collect its fee. It puts the burden on you. *You* asked. *You* opened the door.

Nobody asked anything on my behalf. I had simply been standing in my mother's antechamber for six days, sleeping against a wall that hummed like a struck bell, eating dried plums and arguing with Wren through the crack in the floor about whether the third verse of the prophecy was a warning or an instruction, when midnight arrived without asking my permission either — and the gate, which had been shut and silent since the night it let me in, said my name.

Not *Cyrus*. Not even *Amberdale*. My whole name. The one my mother gave me at the font and never used again because she said names were handles and handles got pulled. She'd whispered it once, the night before she walked through, so that someone in the world would know it besides her.

The gate knew it now.

I stood up off the wall the way you stand up when a floorboard groans in a house where you live alone. Every hair on my arms stood at attention. The candle I'd been rationing guttered sideways, though there was no draft, and the light went strange — not dimmer, just *tilted*, as if the flame were leaning toward the door to listen.

"Cyrus Amberdale," said the gate again, and this time the voice had texture. It was a voice like frost forming on glass — slow, crystalline, patient — and underneath the frost, something else was moving. Something with appetite.

I should have stayed quiet. Wren had told me twice: *it can't use what you don't give it.* But twelve years of talking to customers had built certain habits into my spine, and one of them is that when a voice says your name, you say good evening.

"Good evening," I said, to a door.

* * *

The frost spread from the seam of the gate outward across the flagstones in fingers, delicate as fern-work, beautiful in the way that only things which intend to kill you are beautiful. Where each finger touched, the stone went white and the humming of the wall went silent, as though the room itself were holding its breath.

"I have been meaning to make your acquaintance," the voice said. Closer now. It was coming from everywhere the frost was, which by then was most of the floor. "Twelve years you've lived above me. Twelve years of selling little lies to frightened people. Charms that do nothing." A sound that might have been laughter, if laughter could freeze mid-throat. "I admired that, actually. The honesty of the dishonesty."

"You've been watching my shop?" I kept my back to the wall, near the memory-amber, near the key-ring in my pocket. "Flattered. Most kings don't bother with the small merchants."

"Kings." The frost crept another hand-span. "Yes. Let us speak of kings. You've seen mine. You stood in Coronation Square and watched him promise the people warmth forever, and you saw his shadow move before he did." The voice softened, the way a butcher softens toward the animal. "Tell me, keeper's son — what did you feel? When you saw it?"

"Fear," I said honestly. "And a headache."

"Fear is the honest answer. Fear is *my* honest answer too. Do you know what I am made of?" The frost reached my boots. Cold climbed through the leather, through the wool sock, into the bone of my heel. "I am made of every winter people didn't survive. Every plague-pit. Every famine year. Every child's fever that broke the wrong way. I am the sum of everything they were afraid of, and I have been hungry for a very long time."

"And the king?"

"The king is a coat," it said pleasantly. "A warm one. He keeps the shape of things while I do the work."

I thought of Aldric on his balcony, his face gray as old tallow, and the shadow sliding across the marble a half-second ahead of him like a dog testing ice ahead of its master. And beneath the throne room floor, faint but unmistakable, screaming. I hadn't told Wren about the screaming yet. Some things you carry alone until you understand them.

"What do you want with me?" I asked. "You have a kingdom. You have a crown. You have a whole square full of people clapping on command. Why talk to a charm-seller in a cellar?"

"Because of a debt," said the Wolf.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about my family, if you haven't gathered it already: we are liars of a very specific kind. We lie by omission, generationally, the way some families pass down chins or tempers. Nobody ever told me what the oath meant. Nobody ever told me what the gate was for. I inherited a shop, a wall, a ring of keys, and a set of instructions written in my great-great-grandmother's hand that read, in their entirety: *Feed nothing. Open nothing. Answer nothing after dark.*

I'd broken all three rules within a week of turning nineteen. So perhaps I shouldn't have been surprised that the Wolf knew more about my family than I did.

"The first Amberdale made a bargain," the voice said. The frost had stopped advancing; it lay around my feet like a tide gone out, waiting. "Do you know what she traded? No. Of course not. They never tell the children anymore. She traded a debt. A debt of *presence*. One soul, held in trust, against the day the seal weakens. That is how binding-magic works, keeper's son. Nothing is ever locked. Things are only *owed*."

My mouth had gone dry. "What does that mean?"

"It means," the Wolf said gently, "that I have been holding collateral."

The candle flame leaned so far sideways it nearly lay down flat.

"Your mother," it said, "is alive."

* * *

I'm going to be precise here, because precision is the last thing I owned. I did not fall to my knees. I did not cry out. What I did was worse: I went perfectly, completely still, the way small animals do when the hawk's shadow crosses them, and every part of me that wasn't listening stopped existing.

"She walked through the gate twelve years ago," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "She closed it behind her. I watched her do it."

"You watched her *leave*, yes. And I caught her on the other side. Or rather—" the frost rippled, adjusting itself, a gesture I realized with a lurch was a shrug— "she caught herself. She offered herself in place of the debt. A substitution. Very old magic. Very legal." Another frozen laugh. "I honor my contracts, Cyrus. Ask anyone I haven't eaten."

"If she's alive, prove it."

"Ah." The voice warmed by a single degree, which was somehow more frightening than the cold. "There it is. The merchant's instinct. Proof of goods before payment." The frost withdrew from my feet — actually withdrew, drawing back toward the gate in a slow, courteous tide — and in the space it left behind, the air began to change. To thicken. To smell of rosemary and lamp oil and bread rising, which had been the smell of my childhood kitchen, and which I had not smelled in twelve years and would have known blindfolded, underwater, dead.

"No," I said.

The candle flame stood upright again. And in the corner of the antechamber, where the shadows pooled deepest, a woman took shape the way a figure takes shape in fog. Gray at first. Then less gray.

She was thinner than I remembered. Her hair was longer, shot through with white I hadn't given her time to earn. She wore the same blue shawl she'd worn the morning she left, frayed at the same elbow. She looked at me the way she used to look at me over the top of her mending — half fondness, half exasperation, entirely my mother.

"Cyrus," she said. "You've let the shop get dusty."

Every word I knew abandoned me at once. I had prepared speeches for this moment for twelve years — in the bath, on the stairs, walking home through snow — speeches that began with fury (*you left me*) and speeches that began with grief (*I missed you*) and one truly humiliating speech that began with an apology for the state of the ledgers. None of them survived contact with her actual voice.

"Ma," I said. Which was not any of the speeches.

"There's my boy." She smiled, and her eyes crinkled at the corners exactly right, exactly the way they always had, and something in my chest tore cleanly down the middle. "Look how tall. Look how tired. Are you eating?"

"You're dead," I said. "Or you're not real. Or—" I looked past her at the frost, at the gate, at the patient dark between them. "Or you're bait."

The smile flickered. Just at one corner. Just for a moment — and in that moment her face was a mask worn slightly crooked, and underneath it something vast and cold and amused was watching me watch it.

"Would it matter?" my mother asked softly. "If it were true? Would knowing change what you want?"

"Yes," I said.

"Liar," she said fondly. "You're an Amberdale. Wanting and knowing have never shared a house."

* * *

I made myself look away from her. It cost me more than anything in my life has ever cost, and I include the night I watched her close a door on herself, and the morning wardens burned my shop, and the afternoon I learned the king under the floor screams. I made myself look at the frost instead, at the gate, at the thing wearing my mother's voice like a stolen coat.

"What do you want?" I asked it.

"To settle accounts," said the Wolf. "The seal weakens. Midwinter comes. The prophecy you've been so diligently assembling says what happens next — you've read the verses, haven't you? The unbinding. An Amberdale pours himself into the lock and ends the line forever. A noble death. A tidy one. The scholars love those."

"And you'd rather I didn't."

"Oh, I'd *love* you to. Please. Proceed." The frost spread wide, mockingly hospitable. "But consider the alternative. Walk away. Lock the shop, keep your head down, let midwinter pass unmarked. The realm grows colder, yes. The people grow quieter. But you—" and here the voice dropped to the register it must have used on the real king, all those years ago, the register of a friend explaining a kindness— "you get her back. Alive. Whole. Warm. I release her from the debt the moment the prophecy dies unread. One soul against a realm. Even your own mother thought that trade was fair — she made a version of it herself."

My mother watched me from her corner. Her expression was gentle. Encouraging, even.

"That's the offer?" I said. "Silence for Ma?"

"Silence for Ma," the Wolf agreed. "It's a better price than you'll get from the truth. The truth wants everything you are. I only want a little cowardice. Everyone can afford a little cowardice. It's the cheapest coin there is."

I looked at my mother. Really looked. And I noticed — finally, horribly — the details. The way her shadow fell toward the gate instead of away from the candle. The way the frayed spot on her shawl was frayed in exactly the pattern it had been twelve years ago — no older, no newer, a memory of cloth rather than cloth. The way she never blinked quite in rhythm with her words.

She saw me noticing. And for one flicker — one heartbeat — her face changed. The mask slipped further than the Wolf intended, and what looked out through my mother's eyes was urgent and terrified and *hers*, and it mouthed three words at me, fast, before the smooth fondness flooded back:

*Don't. Trust. It.*

Then she smiled again, warm as bread, and said: "You were always such a careful boy, Cyrus. Careful boys live long lives."

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I did in the four seconds that followed, and I present it to you without shame:

A realm is an abstraction. I have never met a realm. I have met bakers and clerks and a warden's clerk whose hands shook when he counted my change. Realms don't catch fevers. People do. And my mother is a specific person with a specific laugh and a specific scar on her thumb from a carving knife, and she is being held in a cold place by a cold thing because my family signed a contract nobody read carefully enough, and every fiber of the animal I am wanted to say *yes*. Wanted it the way drowning men want air. Twelve years of wanting it, stockpiled, compounding.

Which is exactly why I couldn't trust it. Because the wanting was the point. Because a trap that offers you exactly what you've dreamed of for twelve years isn't offering — it's measuring. It wanted to know how much I was worth. It wanted to know what price would bend me, so it could pay that price to Wren, to the king under the floor, to whoever came next.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said.

The frost went very still.

"You keep her," I said. "Warm, fed, alive — you swore that's what you're holding, and apparently you honor your contracts. Keep holding her. Because the moment I walk out of this antechamber, I stop being the collateral on anyone's debt. The moment I stop being an Amberdale, there's no line for you to collect from. No lock for you to weaken against. You want the prophecy dead? Fine. But the price isn't my silence. The price is her. Free her now, freely, no conditions — and maybe, *maybe*, I lose the verses somewhere."

Silence. Long enough that I heard my own heart, three times.

Then my mother laughed — a bright, easy, familiar laugh, the one she used when I'd haggled a vendor down properly. And the laugh slid upward in pitch as it went, and kept sliding, until it was no longer a sound a human throat could hold, and the shape of her came apart into threads of gray light, and the light funneled back into the gate like water down a drain.

When the frost had finished withdrawing, when the candle stood straight and the walls resumed their humming, the Wolf spoke one last time. Quietly. Almost respectfully.

"Your mother bargained better than you," it said. "But then, she had something to protect. You only have yourself." A pause, long and cold. "We will meet at midwinter, keeper's son. Bring your scholar. Bring your verses. I will bring the winter."

And the gate sealed, and the dark went ordinary, and I sat down on the cold floor among the fading fern-work of frost and discovered that my hands were shaking so badly I had to fold them together to make them stop.

It had shown me her. It had let me hear her voice. And the worst part — the part I will carry past the end of this story — is that I still don't know which parts were the mask and which parts were my mother. Maybe none. Maybe *don't trust it* was the trap, and the fondness was real, and I just refused my mother's rescue to spite a monster. There is no way to check. There is no ledger for this.

I sat there until the candle burned low, doing sums that wouldn't close.

* * *

Wren found me at dawn, dropping down through the floor-crack with frost in her hair and triumph and terror fighting openly on her face. She'd deciphered the middle verses, she said, breathless — the Wolf, the hollow, the crown — and then she saw my face and stopped mid-word.

"It talked to you," she said.

"It offered me a trade."

"What trade?"

I looked at her — at the frost melting in her hair, at the ink stains on her fingers, at the one person in this frozen city who'd chosen loyalty over safety and burned her own credentials to prove it — and I lied to her. Smoothly. The way my family lies.

"It offered to let me go," I said. "If I abandoned the rite. I refused."

Wren studied me a moment. She is many things — exiled, obsessive, the best reader of dead languages I've ever met — but she is not stupid, and I watched her decide, deliberately, to accept the lie. For now.

"Good," she said. "Because there's something in the final verse. Something about the sacrifice. And Cyrus —" she knelt, spreading the copied pages on the frost-scarred stones between us, her voice dropping as if the walls might be listening, which, I now knew, they might — "the sacrifice isn't what we thought. It's not your death the rite requires."

The candle flame leaned toward the pages.

"It's your *everything*."
