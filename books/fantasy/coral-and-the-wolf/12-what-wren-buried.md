## 12. What Wren Buried

There are three ways to confess a sin, my mother used to say. Quickly, like ripping a bandage. Slowly, like paying a debt in installments. Or never, and let it rot you from the inside out until people mistake the smell for character.

I had been practicing option three for three years, and I was very good at it.

We were in the antechamber of the Amberdale Gate, which is not a sentence I ever expected to construct, sitting among Cyrus's mother's things like trespassers at a wake. He had lit every candle he could find — the man hoarded candles the way other refugees hoard coins — and the small room glowed amber and gold, warm in defiance of everything outside it. Through the wall, the gate itself hummed. Not opened. Not shut. *Waiting*, the way a dog waits by a door when it can hear footsteps that might belong to someone it loves.

Cyrus had spent an hour cataloguing his mother's key-ring. Forty keys, some older than the city's walls, each tagged in her handwriting with names that were either places or people or jokes only she had understood. He held them the way a drowning man holds driftwood: gratefully, and with no illusions about what driftwood can actually do.

"We should sleep," he said.

"Yes," I said.

Neither of us moved.

The candle nearest me guttered, steadied. Somewhere above us, snow fell on the ashes of his shop, on the posted warrant with his name, on the whole white lie of Aldric's winter. And somewhere beneath all of that, in the flooded dark where I'd done my reading, sat the truth I had been carrying like a stone in my boot since the night the Archive expelled me.

"Wren." He didn't look up from the keys. "You've been rehearsing something for two days. You do this thing with your hands."

"I do not have a thing with my hands."

"You fold your left thumb under your fingers and press until the knuckle goes white. You did it in the square when the king spoke. You did it when we found the memory of the first Amberdale. You're doing it now."

I looked down. My thumb was folded under my fingers, pressed white as frost.

Damn him. Damn careful men who notice things.

* * *

Let me tell you what I actually buried, because if I'm going to dig it up, I want to do it properly, in order, the way you exhume anything — carefully, with gloves, hoping the thing underneath is smaller than you remember.

Three years ago I was twenty-four and hungry in the way that clever poor girls are hungry: not for food, but for *consequence*. The Royal Archive is a cathedral of dust where ambition goes to be embalmed. I had been there six years by then, promoted twice past better-connected scholars because I could read four dead scripts and they could read one living patron. My mentor, Magister Corvane — yes, *that* Corvane, hold your gasp — used to say I had "a mind that reads too far ahead of its permissions." It was meant as a compliment. In the Archive, it always was, right up until it became an indictment.

In the autumn of that year, cataloguing a seizure of materials from a suppressed monastery in the Grayreach hills, I found a folio that had been misfiled under agricultural records. A century old. Written in a hand that shook, by someone who clearly expected to die soon and wanted the record to survive them.

It was a prophecy. Not the vague, market-fortune kind. The specific kind. Dated. Naming the gate. Naming the family sworn to keep it. Naming a day — and I stood in the archive stacks with my finger on that date and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic came back *three years from this autumn*. Midwinter eve. Full dark.

You understand what that is to a person like me. It isn't dread. Dread is for people with something to lose. It's *leverage*. It was the answer to the question every archivist asks themselves in the small hours: *what do I know that no one else knows?*

So I made my choice. I copied the relevant verses — not all of them, I wasn't stupid enough to steal the original — and I brought them to Magister Corvane, and I laid them on his desk like a girl laying flowers, and I said: *Look what I found.*

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he said, "Wren. This is exactly the kind of initiative the Archive values." And he took my pages, and he thanked me, and for three weeks I glowed.

Here is what I learned afterward, here is what I pieced together from sealed requisitions and a clerk who owed me a favor and the particular way Corvane stopped meeting my eyes: my pages went upward. Past the Archivist Primus. Into channels that don't officially exist. To advisors of the crown, who passed them to the king's private secretaries, who served whatever it was that wore King Aldric's face by then and had worn it for years.

The Wolf knew the prophecy said the gate would open on midwinter eve. The Wolf already knew that; it had eaten the prophecy-writer's certainty along with everything else. But what the Wolf *didn't* know — couldn't know, sealed behind its gate, blind in its long siege — was whether there was still an Amberdale. Whether the lock still had a keeper. Whether, on the appointed night, there would be anyone standing at that door worth consuming first.

My pages answered that question. My pages said: *yes. One left. Surname Amberdale. Occupation: charm-seller. Address included.*

I gave it his address. I wrote it out myself, in my neat archival hand, because I thought thoroughness was a virtue.

That is what I buried. That's the shape and weight of it. Now you know why my thumb goes white.

* * *

"Before you sleep," I said, "there's something you need to hear. And I'd ask you to let me finish before you decide anything, because I've had three years to decide things about myself and I'll spare you nothing."

He set the key-ring down. That was the first mercy — he gave it his whole attention, and Cyrus's whole attention is a heavy thing to stand under.

"Three years ago I found the prophecy. The real one, the full one, in a misfiled monastic folio. I copied the verses naming the gate and the date, and I brought them to Corvane, and I told him they pointed to a surviving keeper of the Amberdale line. He asked for verification details." I heard my own voice go flat, clerical, the voice I use for inventory. "I gave them. Your name. Your trade. The location of your shop."

The candle between us burned straight up, perfectly still, as if even the flame was holding its breath.

"The raid on your shop," I said, "the warrant, the fire, all of it — that machinery started turning the moment Corvane carried my pages upstairs. Maybe they moved slowly because the Wolf likes to savor. Maybe it needed the date to come due. But the reason it knew where to look, the reason there was ever a warden's boot on your doorstep—" I unfolded my thumb and pressed both hands flat on my knees, palms down, like a child showing they held nothing. "Was me. I did that. I traded your existence for a chance at a promotion, and the promotion didn't even come through. They expelled me anyway, eight months later, when I started asking questions about where my pages went. Which means I sold you and got robbed into the bargain, which is somehow worse."

Silence. The gate hummed behind the wall, low and patient.

Cyrus looked at me. Not at my hands, not at the door, not at the middle distance where people store their escapes. At me. Twelve years of selling harmless charms had given him a shopkeeper's face, smooth and pleasant and closed, but underneath it something was moving — and I want to be honest, since honesty was apparently the theme of the evening: what moved there first was not forgiveness.

"You told them where I lived," he said quietly.

"I told them where you lived."

"For twelve years I kept that door shut. Charms that do nothing. Never a spell, never a whisper of working, nothing the wardens could hang me for. I did everything my mother asked and everything she didn't live to ask, and I was so *careful*, Wren. I was careful the way you're careful when you know you're the last of something." His voice didn't rise. That was the terrible part. It went *lower*, down into some cellar of him. "And you walked in off the street with stolen pages and a scholar's arrogance and handed my life to the people hunting it, because you wanted—"

"A title," I said. "A chair with my name on it. To matter. Pick whichever sounds least pathetic."

"—because you were ambitious," he finished. "Say the true ugly word. Ambition isn't shameful. What you did with it was."

"Yes."

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"What would you like instead?" I asked, and I confess my temper arrived, late but punctual as always. "Would you like me to explain? Would that help? I can give you the whole architecture of it — the poverty, the Archive's politics, the fact that a girl who reads too far ahead of her permissions gets taught early that knowledge is currency and hoarding it is theft. I can make you *understand* me, Cyrus. Understanding is the trick I do best. It's also how I talked myself into that copy desk in the first place. So no. No explanations. Yes. I did it. I was wrong. There is no version of me three years ago who deserves what you're deciding right now, and I am not going to perform contrition well enough to earn absolution, because I've seen how the Archive rewards performance and I'm done auditioning."

The words rang in the little room and died against the stone.

Cyrus stood. Walked to the wall. Put his palm flat against the gate's seam the way he must have done ten thousand times over twelve years, the way you touch a scar to confirm it's yours. For a long time he said nothing at all, and I sat in the candlelight doing sums I couldn't balance: twelve years of caution against one page of mine; his mother's forty keys against my one act of carelessness; the exact weight of a life against the exact weight of a career.

Then, without turning around:

"When the wardens came," he said, "you could have run. The tip was anonymous. You'd already been expelled — you owed that shop nothing. Instead you came back through the smoke and got me into the cellar and held the hatch while they broke the floor above us. You bled on my stairs."

"You were more useful to the prophecy than I was."

"Don't." He turned. "Don't do that. Don't convert it into strategy. I've watched you do it since the square — every decent thing you do, you immediately file under tactics so you never have to call it what it is." He crossed back and crouched in front of my chair, eye level, close enough that I could see the exhaustion under his eyes and the stubbornness under that. "Why did you come back?"

"Because—" And here is the humiliating truth, the one I'd buried deeper than the pages, the one I'd have gone to my grave protecting: "Because when I found your name in that folio three years ago, before I ever met you, before the shop, I sat in the archives and thought, *whoever this keeper is, I hope they're braver than me.* You were the person in the story I wished I was. And then I met you, and you were, and coming back for you wasn't strategy. It was the closest I've ever come to being the person I'd hoped would exist."

His expression did something complicated. Grief and anger sharing a narrow doorway, neither quite willing to yield to the other.

"You should have told me at the bridge," he said finally. "The night we met. Before I trusted you with the gate."

"Yes. I should have. I told myself I'd wait until you trusted me enough to survive hearing it, and then I discovered that trust doesn't work like that — it's not a lock you pick until it opens, it's a fire you keep feeding or it dies." I exhaled. "So feed it or put it out. Those are your choices. I'll abide either. But I won't carry it buried anymore. Not with midwinter coming. If we're going to do whatever it is the rite requires, I need you to be able to look at me without wondering what else I haven't said. So: ask me anything. Anything at all. Tonight, while I'm apparently constitutionally incapable of lying."

* * *

He thought about it. Actually thought, head tilted, the way he did over a customer claiming a charm had cured their rheumatism.

"Why did you really get expelled?" he said at last. "Not the official reason. Eight months after you handed over the pages — what did you actually do?"

Ah. That one. Of course that one.

"I broke into the sealed annex and stole back my own copies," I said. "Or tried to. Corvane caught me at the door with them in my coat. Because by then I'd traced where my report had gone — to offices that don't appear on any chart — and I understood what I'd done, and I wanted to unsend it the only way paper can be unsent." I shrugged. "Corvane offered me a choice. Recant, apologize, forget what I'd learned, and keep my post. Or lose everything. I chose everything. I told myself it was conscience. Some nights I still believe that."

"And other nights?"

"Other nights I think I just couldn't stand being the kind of person who stays quiet after learning what I'd fed. Either way, Cyrus — the expulsion, the ruin, the exile, all of it — that part I don't regret. It's the only payment I've made so far, and it cost me almost nothing compared to what it cost you."

He sat back on his heels. Outside, the wind changed its mind about something and threw snow against the high slit of a window; the candle flames leaned and recovered.

"The wardens will find this place eventually," he said. It wasn't forgiveness. I knew that. It was something being built next to where forgiveness would eventually go, the way you build the house before the family arrives. "Corvane has your habits. He'll know the sub-archive, and the sub-archive knows this cellar. We have days. Maybe fewer."

"Then we shouldn't waste them on my penance."

"No." He rose, and gathered the key-ring again, and paused. "One more question."

"Anything."

"The prophecy. The full one — the one you stole back and I burned and it rewrote itself. The middle verses you decoded in the flooded archive. You told me what the Wolf is. You didn't tell me everything the verses said about the *keeper*." His eyes came up to mine, steady and unflinching. "What do they say happens to the last Amberdale?"

And there it was. The second burial. Smaller than the first, but buried deeper, because I'd told myself it was mercy — that he'd learn it when he needed to, that a man carrying a warrant and a dead shop didn't need one more weight.

But I had promised. Anything at all. While constitutionally incapable of lying.

I reached into my coat and drew out the folded page — my transcription, the one thing I'd saved from the fire, hidden these two days in the lining where my body heat had kept the ink safe — and I unfolded it, and I turned it toward him, and I showed him the final verse.

He read it once. Then again, slower. I watched the color leave his face in the order it leaves everyone's: cheeks first, then lips, then the steadiness around the eyes.

"'The gate opens,'" he read aloud, barely voicing it, "'for the willing blood. The lock is a life. The keeper pays in full.'"

"Cyrus—"

"It says *in full*," he said. "Wren. What does *in full* mean?"

Outside, far off across the frozen city, a bell began to ring — not the hour, not the alarm. The palace bell, the one that hadn't sounded in twelve years, ringing now at midnight for no reason the living had been given.

The candles all bent toward the wall at once.

Behind the stone, the gate began, very softly, to laugh.
