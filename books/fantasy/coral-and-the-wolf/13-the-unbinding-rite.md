## 13. The Unbinding Rite

### Cyrus

The antechamber had a table. That was the first miracle of the day, and I want you to appreciate how low the bar had fallen, because the second miracle was that Wren set her stolen pages on it without them bursting into flame, dissolving, or filing a formal complaint with the Royal Archive.

"Right," she said, spreading three fragments of parchment like a gambler laying out a losing hand. "Let's do this properly. We have—" she glanced at the crack of grey light under the gate's door, which was not a window and did not care about weather, but she consulted it anyway, the way scholars consult anything, "—two days until midwinter eve. Which means we have one night to build a rite from a prophecy that was written by someone who clearly hated punctuation."

"It rhymes," I offered.

"Everything rhymes when you're a century dead and no one can argue."

I pulled up the second stool — the antechamber had two stools, which suggested our founding ancestor expected company, or at least expected his descendants to fail alone and need witnesses. I sat. Wren sat across from me, ink-stained fingers pinning down the oldest fragment, the one she'd lifted from the Archive's flooded lower stacks with the calm efficiency of a woman who considered theft a form of citation.

"Read me the middle verses again," she said. "The ones in your mother's hand. I want to hear how they sound coming from an Amberdale."

"They sound like homework," I said, but I read them.

*What was bound in amber must be loosed in dark.*
*At full black midnight, on the year's last eve,*
*An Amberdale's own blood, an Amberdale's own spark,*
*Shall call the Wolf from off the crown it grieves.*

Silence. The kind of silence a room makes when everyone has understood something and nobody wants to say it first.

"'Blood and spark,'" Wren said finally. "That's not poetry. That's specification. Blood for the rite itself. Spark for—" She stopped. Looked at me. Looked away, which for Wren was the equivalent of weeping openly.

"For everything I've got," I said. "Yes. I did the arithmetic too. I'm just better at pretending I didn't."

"Cyrus—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "We'll get to that. We have to get to that, because you've been circling it since the cisterns like a moth around a lamp it finds personally offensive. But if we stop to fight about my inevitable doom now, we'll still be fighting at midwinter, and then we'll all be wolves' breakfast. Sequence of operations, Wren. Shopkeeper's discipline."

Something crossed her face that was almost a smile. "You were a terrible shopkeeper."

"I was an *excellent* shopkeeper. Nobody ever suspected the man selling love charms that did nothing."

"Because they didn't work."

"*Exactly.* Perfect cover." I tapped the parchment. "Go on. What does your half say?"

She smoothed the Archive fragment flat. Her voice shifted when she read the old verses — dropped its edge, took on the careful weight of someone handling something that might still be alive.

*Not by force, nor fire, nor blade drawn in hate,*
*But at the Gate where first it was sealed,*
*Where the coral remembers and the stone keeps the date,*
*Shall the hollow be called to yield.*

"And beneath that, in the margin — later hand, maybe fifty years after the original —" she turned the page toward me. Someone had written, in cramped, frightened script: *the gate must stand open. the keeper must stand inside. there is no outside to this working.*

I read it twice. "Charming. Our anonymous annotator really knew how to end on an uplifting note."

"He knew how to end on an *accurate* note," Wren said quietly. "Cyrus. The gate has to be open while you do this. You'll be standing in the doorway. Between the Wolf and everywhere else."

"I know."

"You don't flinch when you say things like that anymore. It worries me."

"I flinch internally. There's a whole lot of internal flinching. You just can't see it because I've had twelve years of practice looking bored while terrifying things happen behind a wall in my cellar."

She laughed — short, surprised at herself, gone almost immediately. Then she pulled a fourth paper from her coat, and I stopped joking, because I recognized the handwriting on it before I recognized the words.

It was my mother's.

### Wren

He went very still when he saw it. Not the stillness of shock — the stillness of a man standing in a river who has just felt the current change. I'd seen it before, in the Archive, when readers found their own names in records that should have forgotten them. Names are heavier than people think.

"Where did you get that?" Cyrus asked.

"The flooded sub-archive. Third shelf, east wall, under a false binding labeled *Treatises on Municipal Drainage.* Whoever hid it wanted it boring." I slid it across the table to him. "It's a letter. Or the start of one. Unsent."

He read it. I watched him read it, which felt like trespassing, but some things you have to witness or you haven't done your job.

*To whoever keeps the gate after me,* it began. *If you are reading this, then the prophecy has found you too, and I am sorry, because I promised myself I would burn every copy and spare you the knowing. Here is what matters: the Wolf cannot be killed. I looked for a way to kill it for nine years. There isn't one. It isn't alive the way we're alive. It is an appetite wearing the shape of a creature, and you cannot execute a hunger. You can only feed it something else, or close the door on it, or both.*

"But she wrote 'loosed,' " Cyrus said. His voice was steady. His hands weren't. "The middle verse says *loosed.* And the prophecy names midwinter eve. If there's no way to kill it—"

"Then 'loosed' doesn't mean freed," I said. "It means *removed.* Pulled off the king. Off Aldric. The rite doesn't destroy the Wolf, Cyrus. It evicts it. And then the gate—" I stopped, because saying it aloud made it real in a way that thinking it had somehow failed to do. "Then the gate takes it back. With whatever's holding the door."

He looked at the letter again. Read the rest of it. I gave him the time. Outside the antechamber, beyond the gate's closed door, something breathed — slow, vast, patient as frost. It had been breathing like that for days now, ever since we'd learned its name. Knowing a thing's name, I've found, rarely makes it quieter.

"There's more," he said at last. He turned the letter so I could see the final lines.

*I have left you the key-ring. Not because you will need it to lock the gate. Because locking is easy. Anyone can lock a thing. What you will need it for is the choice, and I cannot make that choice for you, and I am sorry, and I love you, and the tea in the blue tin is still good.*

He put the letter down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when you'd rather throw it.

"She's been dead twelve years," he said. "And she's still leaving me instructions."

"Is that so terrible?"

"It's — " He exhaled through his nose. "No. No, it's the opposite. It's the only thing in this entire miserable winter that feels like her." He looked up. "All right. Fragments on the table. Let's assemble the beast. What do we actually have?"

I pulled the pages into a line. This was the part I was good at — the part they'd expelled me for being good at. Collation. The humble, unglamorous work of putting broken things next to each other until they admit they belong together.

"One," I said. "The rite happens at full dark on midwinter eve. Both sources agree. The original prophecy specifies it; your mother's letter calls it 'the year's last true night.' Two: it happens at the gate. The Amberdale gate specifically — the coral in the stone isn't decorative, it's the anchor, the oldest binding-point in the city. Three: it must be performed by an Amberdale. By blood right. Which means you."

"Four," Cyrus said, "I pour in everything. All of it. Whatever magic the family line has been sitting on for six generations, all of it goes into the working, and none of it comes back." He said it flatly, the way you read a bill. "Five: the gate stands open while I do it, and I stand in the opening, and the Wolf gets pulled off Aldric and through me — or past me, the texts disagree — and back behind the seal."

"Six," I said, and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized. It wavered. "There's no keeper afterward. Your mother's letter implies it and the marginal note confirms it. A seal that holds without a keeper means the keeping-magic is spent. Entirely. The line ends with you, Cyrus. Not just your magic. The *oath.* The name. All of it, poured into one night."

The breathing beyond the door continued. Patient. The Wolf had waited a hundred years once already. It could afford to let two humans sit at a table and do sums about its destruction.

"That's the price list," Cyrus said. "Line ended. Magic gone. Standing in an open doorway at midnight with an ancient hunger on the other side of me and a starving king on this side. And in exchange — spring. Eventually. And Aldric gets his body back, and the city gets its fear back — I mean stops getting fed on fear — and nobody ever has to be afraid of a charm shop again." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd ever seen on him, bright and cracked like ice over deep water. "Honestly? Still cheaper than rent in this district."

"Cyrus."

"Wren."

"You don't have to decide tonight."

"Yes, I rather do." He gathered the pages together — mine, his mother's, the prophecy, the frightened margin-note — and squared their edges with a precision that was entirely for his own benefit. "Because there's a seventh item you haven't written down yet, scholar. We still need to get the king out of the palace and to this gate by tomorrow night. Past the wardens. Past the Frost Fair. Past the thing wearing his face that knows exactly what we're planning, because it's been listening through every frosted window in this city since the snow started."

I hadn't written that down because I'd been hoping it would solve itself. Hope, I've learned, is just scholarship with the citations removed.

"The Frost Fair," I said slowly. "The whole court will be there. The Wolf *wants* the fair — it feeds best in crowds, all that forced cheer, all that terror underneath. It won't be able to resist being seen."

"So we move the king during the fair," Cyrus said, "when every eye in the palace is pointed at the river."

"Or we die on a frozen river in front of several thousand spectators. One of those two things will happen. I'd put money on it but I recently burned my credentials, so technically I'm no longer qualified to have opinions."

"Technically," Cyrus said, "you're the most qualified person alive. You're the only scholar in the kingdom studying the correct subject." He stood, and tucked his mother's letter inside his shirt, over his heart, which was such an obviously sentimental gesture that neither of us mentioned it. "One night to prepare. One river to cross. One king to steal."

"One rite," I said. "One sacrifice you keep insisting on making without letting anyone negotiate."

"We can negotiate after midwinter."

"That's not how negotiation works."

"It's how *mine* works." He picked up the lamp. Behind the gate's door, the breathing paused — just for a heartbeat, just long enough to notice — and then resumed, and I swear on every book I ever stole that it sounded amused. "Come on, Wren. We've got a wolf to unbind and only a day and a night to learn the words."

I followed him toward the stairs, and behind us the gate breathed on in the dark — patient, hungry, and, for one more night, still shut.

Outside, the snow was falling harder. Tomorrow, the river would freeze solid enough to hold a fair.

And the day after that, at full dark, it would have to hold everything else.
