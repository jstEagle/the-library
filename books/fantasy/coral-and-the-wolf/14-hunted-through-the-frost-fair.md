## 14. Hunted Through the Frost Fair

The river froze the week the king declared winter eternal, and the regime — being what it was — decided to celebrate.

That's the thing about fear. It doesn't just sit in you like a stone. It wants a festival. It wants roasted chestnuts and paper lanterns and children shrieking on ice-skates, because a crowd that's laughing is a crowd that isn't counting wardens. The Frost Fair opened on the frozen Vell three days after we fled the shop, stretching from the Old Bridge to the palace cisterns like a carnival laid over a coffin lid. Banners of white and silver. A snow-carved effigy of King Aldric that some artisan had given a smile the real one would never manage. Stalls selling mulled wine, frost-charms — *frost-charms*, sold openly, by licensed vendors, while men still hung for the unlicensed kind — and little tin wolves on strings for the children to wear around their necks.

I stopped walking when I saw the wolves.

"Cyrus." Wren's hand closed on my sleeve. "Don't."

"I wasn't going to do anything."

"You were going to buy one and burn it, and then we'd both be arrested, and I'd have to write 'he died as he lived: petty' in my final account of events."

She was right, which was becoming an annoying pattern. We joined the current of bodies moving along the ice road, two more gray faces in a gray crowd, Wren in a borrowed fishwife's shawl and me in a carter's coat three sizes too big at the shoulders. The cistern entrance we needed sat at the river's far end, beneath the old customs house, behind a door my mother had once shown me with the offhand air of a woman pointing out where she kept the good scissors. Twelve years I'd assumed she was being whimsical. Twelve years I'd been wrong about nearly everything.

The plan, such as it was: get under the palace, find the real king, do whatever the rite required before midwinter eve. The plan did not account for the Frost Fair. The plan especially did not account for what stood at the fair's midpoint, planted on the ice like a nail through the map of our escape.

A checkpoint. Six wardens in their slate-gray coats, pikes grounded, breath steaming. And behind them, checking papers with the bored efficiency of a man who'd done this ten thousand times, a warden-captain whose face I knew.

Sergeant Hallow. He'd bought a headache charm from me every month for four years. He'd told me about his daughter's wedding. He'd once shaken my hand.

"Small city," Wren murmured.

"Small world," I said. "Bad odds."

We could go around. That was the obvious move — skirt wide along the north bank, lose twenty minutes, maybe lose our nerve. But the customs house clock said we didn't have twenty minutes. The rite needed full dark, and full dark came early now, pressed down by that bruised permanent cloud, and if we missed the window we missed everything until the gate sealed forever and the winter won by forfeit.

So we went through.

Here is what nobody tells you about walking toward danger: your body knows before you do. My hands went cold in a way the weather couldn't explain. My heartbeat moved up into my throat and set up housekeeping there. I fixed my eyes on a point six feet ahead — the back of a fat merchant's fur collar, a lantern swinging on a stall-pole, anything — and I let the crowd carry me like driftwood.

Wren drifted ahead of me, half a step, playing her part: a tired woman dragging her tired man to the far stalls because the near ones charged too much. She was good at this. Three years in the Royal Archive teaches you how to be furniture.

The checkpoint grew. Ten feet. Five.

"Hold." Hallow didn't look up from his ledger. "Papers."

"We're going to the far stalls," Wren said, in exactly the voice of a woman who resented being delayed by officialdom, which was true, so it came out perfect.

"Papers," Hallow repeated.

And here is where I confess something. We had no papers. We had a stolen key-ring, half a prophecy memorized between us, and a fishwife's shawl. What I had instead was twelve years of practice at being harmless, and I deployed it now the way other men deploy swords.

"Problem with the papers?" I asked Hallow, mildly, the way a man asks about the weather. And I watched him look up. Watched his eyes pass over my face. Watched the flicker — the tiny hitch — of recognition, because four years of monthly purchases buys a certain familiarity, and familiarity is a coin you can't take back once spent.

His brow creased. "Have we—"

"You bought the willow-bark blend," I said quietly. "For your head. Tuesdays, usually. How is it?"

The crease deepened. Somewhere behind him, another warden shifted, pike scraping ice. Hallow looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him weigh something — not suspicion, exactly. Something sadder. He knew. I think he'd known since the raid, since my name went up on the posts, since the shop burned. He'd bought charms from a criminal for four years and the arithmetic of that was sitting on his chest now like a cat he couldn't shoo.

"The far stalls," he said finally, stepping aside. "Mind the ice past the bridge. It's thin where the current runs."

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, and hated meaning it, because he was going to pay for this. Men like Hallow always paid first.

We made it forty feet.

Then the bell started.

* * *

It wasn't the checkpoint bell. It was the deeper one, the palace bell, the one they rang for fire and plague and — apparently — for us. Its sound rolled across the frozen river and every head on the ice turned at once, a thousand faces tilting like sunflowers toward the wrong sun, and in that moment of collective attention I felt the crowd change. Fear has a texture. You learn it, running a shop in a city where magic hangs people. The Frost Fair's fear had been festive, decorative, worn like the tin wolves. This was different. This was the fear underneath, suddenly uncovered, and it turned a thousand neighbors into a thousand pairs of eyes.

"There," someone shouted. "The clerk — the one from the posters!"

So much for the carter's coat.

"Run," Wren said, and shoved me, and we ran.

The ice road dissolved into chaos around us. Skaters scattered. A chestnut cart went over, coals skittering across the frozen surface in orange arcs, and the smell of burning sugar mixed with screaming. Behind us the wardens broke from the checkpoint — all six, plus more pouring from the customs house, a dozen, fifteen, boots hammering the ice in that disciplined cadence that says *we have done this before and enjoyed it*.

"North bank!" I gasped.

"No time — the cistern door—"

"The cistern door is behind fifteen wardens, Wren!"

"Then we make them regret their careers." She cut left, hard, toward the stall row, and I followed because following Wren had become, without my ever agreeing to it, my default setting.

What she did next, I want to describe carefully, because I've thought about it many times since, usually at night.

She stopped at a stall selling frost-lanterns — paper globes on wire frames, glowing with that cold blue light the regime favored — and she put her bare hand flat against the stall's iron post, and she *read* it. That's the only word. Her lips moved, fast, silent, the way she read the prophecy pages, the way she read everything, because Wren's magic wasn't fire or lightning or any of the showy kinds. Wren's magic was the Archive itself: three hundred years of catalogued spells, indexed, shelved, and — after her expulsion — partially stolen, carried out in her head the way other scholars carry quotations.

The lanterns flared. All of them, every globe on the stall and then every globe down the row, blooming from blue to blinding white, and the light hit the ice.

Frost-lanterns are cold light. Everyone knows that; it's their whole selling point. But cold light focused through archive-glass becomes something else — becomes a lens, a burning point, a seam. The ice beneath the nearest warden squad groaned, cracked, split in a black zipper thirty feet long. Two wardens went down into the shallows with a splash and a curse. The rest stopped dead, because men will charge a sword but nobody charges open water in eternal winter.

"That'll hold them ninety seconds," Wren said, already moving. "Maybe sixty. Go."

"You're coming with me."

"I'm *holding the line*, Cyrus, that was always the shape of this — go!"

"I'm not leaving you on the ice with fifteen wardens and a trick you clearly haven't tested!"

"Nothing's tested anymore! Everything's first draft!" She grabbed my coat and kissed me — fast, furious, tasting of cold and ink — and then pushed me away hard enough to stagger. "Cisterns. Now. If you die under the palace I will never forgive either of us."

There was no time to argue, which I suspect she knew. There was never any time with Wren; she budgeted seconds the way misers budget coins. So I ran — along the stall row, past the toppled chestnut cart, past the snow-effigy of the smiling king with its paper crown already sagging in the heat of burning lanterns — toward the customs house and the door my mother had shown me with her whimsical scissors voice.

Behind me, Wren turned to face the wardens alone, backlit by her own stolen light, and raised her arms, and began to recite.

I heard the first line only. It was in Old Archive cant, the language they used for indexing truths too dangerous to file plainly, and it sounded like a door opening in the middle of the sky. Then the wind rose, and the screaming started, and I ran faster.

* * *

The customs house door was exactly as she'd described it: oak, iron-banded, painted the dull green of bureaucratic neglect, with a lock that hadn't been oiled since before the war. My mother's key-ring sang softly against my hip as I knelt — the keys had begun doing that lately, a faint harmonic hum, as if they knew we were getting close to whatever they'd been made for. I found the key by feel. Third from the oldest end. Of course.

The lock turned. The door opened onto darkness and the smell of wet stone and old water, and I paused on the threshold, one foot in the dark, because that's what you do at thresholds. You hesitate. Even when hesitation costs.

Behind me, across the ice, the light of Wren's working flickered — white, then dimmer, then white again, a candle in a gale. I could see shapes moving against it. Wardens, closing. More than fifteen now. I counted at least thirty, and beyond them, at the far edge of the fair, something else: a palanquin, silver-roped, borne on the shoulders of men who walked too smoothly.

The Wolf had come to the fair.

I don't know how I knew it was the Wolf. The palanquin was just a palanquin, curtained, royal. But the cold bent toward it. Every breath of wind on that river leaned in its direction like a congregation, and the nearer it came to Wren's flickering light, the harder the light fought.

Go down, said the sensible part of me, the part that had kept a shop alive for twelve years by knowing which risks weren't mine. Go down. Find the king. Do the rite. That's how you save her — the only way you save anyone.

But my feet wouldn't move. Because here's the thing about loyalty: it isn't a decision. Decisions happen in the head, and my head was perfectly clear, giving excellent directions. Loyalty happens lower down, in the marrow, in the place where my mother's oath had been written into me before I could read, and it was saying something very simple:

*You don't leave people on the ice.*

Wren's light guttered. Went dark. Came back weaker, shaped wrong, and through the wind I heard her voice crack on the third line of the cant — she was running out of stolen pages, running out of herself, and the wardens' boots had stopped retreating and started advancing.

My mother had gone through a gate to slow a monster. Alone. On purpose. Leaving her son a ring of keys and no explanation, because explanation would have been a cruelty, or a temptation, or simply more than one life could carry.

I understood her better in that moment than I had in all my years of missing her.

"Not today," I said — to the dark doorway, to the Wolf, to the whole rotten architecture of fate that kept asking ordinary people to be extraordinary. "Not her. Not like this."

I pulled the key from the lock, slammed the customs house door, and ran back onto the ice.

* * *

The wind hit me like a wall of knives. The fair had emptied around the fight — crowds flee fear the way water finds drains — and so what remained was stark and simple: Wren, kneeling on the ice inside a circle of guttering blue script, blood on her lip, her hands shaking as she fed the last of her stolen archive into the working. And around her, closing like a fist, thirty wardens with pikes leveled, and behind them the palanquin, closer now, its curtains stirring though the curtains faced away from the wind.

I had nothing. No magic — well, none I'd ever been allowed to know I had. No weapon. A carter's coat and a key-ring that hummed.

But I had grown up in a city that feared magic, and fear leaves traces everywhere, and one of the traces it leaves is this: wardens train for sorcerers. They drill against fire and lightning and summoned things. They have protocols. What they do not train for, because no one in twelve years had been stupid enough, is a charm-seller with a sack of worthless trinkets and a lifetime of knowing exactly what harmless objects do to frightened minds.

The stall I wanted was ten feet away. The one selling tin wolves.

I tore the display down by its guy-rope and swung the whole tangled mass — fifty tin wolves on their strings, spinning, catching the lantern-light — out over the ice in a glittering arc that landed among the front rank of wardens like a shower of small mad bells.

They flinched. All of them. Thirty trained men, facing a girl and a circle of dying light, flinched at *tin wolves*, because the regime had spent a year teaching them that wolves meant the king's displeasure and the king's displeasure meant disappearing, and superstition will beat training every time if you give it half a chance.

In the flinch, I reached Wren.

"Idiot," she breathed.

"Probably."

"They'll kill you."

"Get in line." I hauled her up. The circle died behind us, its last script guttering out like a snuffed wick, and the wardens recovered — recovered fast, anger replacing superstition, boots pounding ice. We ran. Not toward the customs house; that door was shut and locked and thirty men deep in pursuit. Toward the bridge, toward the thin ice Hallow had warned me about, toward the only card left on the table.

"Where?" Wren gasped.

"Trust me."

"I *hate* that phrase."

The ice past the bridge pilings was gray and webbed, the current talking underneath in a low hungry voice. I'd fished this stretch as a boy. I knew where the channel ran, knew the spots where the freeze was an inch thick and the spots where it was honest, and I led us along that knife-edge knowledge while boots thundered behind us and the palanquin's bearers — impossibly, horribly — began to *glide*, the curtains streaming, the cold bending around them like water around a keel.

Halfway across the thin stretch, I stopped, turned, and put my heel down on the ice with everything I had.

The river opened.

Not dramatically. Just a long black seam, widening, the current shouldering up through it, and between us and the wardens spread thirty feet of open winter water steaming in the cold. Pikes clattered. Boots skidded. A sergeant screamed orders nobody obeyed, because even the Wolf's own men would not cross open water in eternal winter, and the palanquin stopped at the seam's edge like a beast at a fence it couldn't smell its way over.

For a moment, across the black water, the curtain twitched. And I felt it look at me. Not see — *look*, the way a hook looks at a fish, patient, certain, amused. Then a voice, not loud, carrying anyway, sliding under the wind and straight into the marrow where oaths live:

"Soon, keeper. Bring the keys."

Then Wren's hand in mine, pulling, and we were running again — off the thin ice, up the far bank, into the tangle of warehouses below the customs bluff where the cistern stairs ran down into the dark. My side was bleeding where a pike's butt had caught me in the crush. Wren's lip was still red. Between us we had one key-ring, half a rite, and perhaps two hours until full dark.

"Next time," Wren said, leaning on me as we stumbled down the stairs, "we plan for the festival."

"Next time," I agreed, "we stay home."

Below us, the dark opened its mouth, and somewhere far beneath the palace, something that had once been a king heard footsteps coming, and began — I swear it — to sing.
