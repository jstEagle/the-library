## 15. The King Under the Floor

The cisterns beneath the palace were older than the palace itself, which is the sort of sentence that sounds like a scholarly flourish until you're standing in one.

Cyrus went first. I followed, holding a lantern I had stolen from a Frost Fair stall along with three candied oranges and a hat I did not need but could not leave behind. Theft, I have found, is mostly a matter of momentum. Once you've taken the oranges, the lantern hardly counts.

The water came to our shins and then our knees, black as spilled ink and cold enough to make my teeth ache in their roots. Above us, muffled through feet of stone and ice, the Frost Fair was still screaming its joy — fiddles, fireworks, the wail of some child who had lost her mother near the roast-chestnut cart. Down here there was only dripping. Drip, and drip, and the slow slap of our own movement, and Cyrus's breathing, which had gone very controlled in the way of a man who has decided that if he panics he will do it quietly.

"You feel that?" he said.

I felt it. Cold, yes, but a different cold than the water's cold. The water's cold sat on the skin. This came from underneath, from ahead of us, like standing near an open door in winter and feeling the draft of a room you cannot see. Archive-cold. I had spent six years among the sealed stacks of the Royal Archive, in rooms where nothing living was permitted to decay because nothing living was permitted at all, and this was that same temperature. The temperature of held breath.

"The prophecy said 'beneath the crown,'" I said. "I assumed that meant politically."

"Scholars assume," Cyrus said, without heat, "and then they get wet."

He was right, which annoyed me less than it should have, because being right while knee-deep in a dead king's plumbing is a humbling sort of correctness for both of us.

* * *

We had come down through the cistern mouth behind the fair — a grate Cyrus picked with two of his mother's keys, which fit locks they had no business fitting, which is a thing about the key-ring I have stopped asking questions about. The plan, such as it was, had been assembled in the eleven minutes we'd had between the wardens spotting us at the fair and Wren setting fire to a fortune-teller's tent with archive-magic she was probably going to regret using. The plan was: down, under, find the man under the floor, and pray the Wolf was too busy enjoying its festival to notice two rats in its cellar.

Plans made in eleven minutes are not plans. They are prayers with footnotes.

But here is what I knew, and why I had insisted on coming despite every sensible argument Cyrus made against it: the middle verses of the prophecy — the ones I had deciphered in the flooded sub-archive, coughing up mildew water, my notes dissolving even as I read them — spoke of the king twice. *The crown shall eat the king*, the verse said, *and wear him as a coat*. And then, further down, in a hand so cramped I had initially taken it for watermark damage: *the king remains beneath, as a voice remains beneath a bell after it has been struck.*

A bell rings, and you think the sound is the bell. But the sound passes. The bronze stays. Somewhere under that ringing voice in Coronation Square, under the frost-white face and the shadow that moved wrong, there was supposed to be bronze. A man. Aldric, if the histories were honest, though histories written by courts are honest the way tax ledgers are honest — technically, selectively, in the places where honesty costs nothing.

Twelve years ago, King Aldric the Just — and he had genuinely been called that, before; there are plaques — outlawed magic after the Ember Plague swept the river cities. He burned the archives. He hanged the hedge-witches and the charm-sellers and, eventually, the scholars who objected, of whom I was one of the last and least important. And then, according to everything I had pieced together, something had come for him. Something that had waited a century behind the Amberdale Gate for exactly this: a kingdom scrubbed clean of anyone who might recognize it. It had eaten him from the inside and walked out wearing his grief like a coat, and it had ruled ever since, and it had called the endless winter *mercy*, and forty thousand people had nodded along because the alternative was to look too closely at the thing on the throne.

I had helped start this clock. Three years ago, in a moment of ambition I will be ashamed of until I die, I had brought the prophecy to the Archive's attention, hoping favor would save my career, and the Wolf had learned of the keeper because of me. Cyrus knew. He had forgiven me, or something adjacent to forgiveness, in a conversation where neither of us used any word harder than "anyway." But forgiveness is not the same as absolution, and I had come down into this freezing dark carrying mine like a stone in my coat pocket, next to the stolen oranges.

If the king was down here — if some crumb of Aldric remained — then perhaps the rite could work. Perhaps the crown-oath could be unmade from the inside. Or perhaps we would find nothing but water and old bones, and I would have dragged the last Amberdale into a hole in the world for nothing.

Both possibilities were live. That's prophecy for you. It never closes a door without opening two more.

* * *

The passage narrowed. The ceiling came down to meet the water until we were wading bent nearly double, shoulders brushing stone, and the cold-from-underneath grew teeth. Frost feathered the walls in patterns that were not frost patterns. Real frost makes ferns and feathers, fractals, the mathematics of freezing. This made other things. Letters, almost. A script I half-recognized from the oldest stratum of the Archive — pre-dynastic, the catalog called it, meaning *we found it and gave up*. Words crawling up out of the waterline like things surfacing.

Cyrus touched one with his fingertips and snatched them back. "It's warm," he said, bewildered. "The wall is warm where the writing is."

Everything else in this place was trying to kill us with cold, and the writing was warm. I filed that away with the key-ring and the self-rewriting prophecy and the shadow that moved independently of the king: another entry in the growing ledger of *things in this city that are wrong in ways I don't have vocabulary for yet.* My old mentor, Archivist Corvane, used to say that scholarship begins where vocabulary ends. Corvane also sold me out to the wardens last week, so I try to take his aphorisms with appropriate salt.

Then the passage opened, all at once, into a chamber.

I wish I could describe it as vast. It wasn't. It was small — smaller than my cell at the Archive had been, and I had complained about that cell for six years. Maybe ten paces across, domed, entirely dry, which after the wading felt like a miracle until I understood that dryness down here was not natural. Water does not simply decline to enter a room beneath a river. Something had told it to stay out.

And against the far wall, in a niche carved deliberately — deliberately, with tools, with intention, by someone who had known this day would come — sat a man.

Or what was left arranged in the shape of one.

* * *

I want to be careful here, because what I saw first was not the truth, and I think the order in which you see a thing matters.

What I saw first was a king. Robes, faded from crimson to the pink of old blood. A circlet of iron, tarnished almost to rust, sitting on hair gone entirely white. Hands folded in his lap like a man waiting patiently for an appointment. Posture upright. Composed. Everything about the arrangement said *dignity*, said someone had dressed this figure carefully and set him here to wait.

Then the lantern light moved, and dignity fell off him like a coat off a nail.

He was starved. Not thin — starved, in the way that has passed through thin and out the other side into architecture, cheekbones and collarbone and the cage of his ribs all visible, skin stretched over them like parchment over a frame. His eyes were open. That was the worst part. They were open, and they tracked the lantern, and they were clear — horribly, unbearably clear, the pale gray-green of winter light on shallow water — and they were *aware*.

"He's alive," Cyrus said. His voice cracked on it. Twelve years he had believed his mother was the only person on the other side of anything; twelve years of assuming the gate's prisoners were past prisoner-hood. "Wren. He's alive."

"I can see that," I said, and my voice cracked too, and I decided we would agree never to mention either crack again.

Because here was the thing I had deduced from verses and fragments, sitting in front of us, breathing. Shallowly. Slowly. But breathing. The king under the floor was not a corpse and not a ghost. He was a man who had been kept — kept the way you keep a coat, the way you keep a hostage, the way you keep the original of a document so the forgery stays legal. The Wolf had not destroyed Aldric. Destroying him would have been mercy, and the Wolf does not deal in mercy; it deals in *use*. It had hollowed him and worn him, and it had left this remainder here, beneath the throne room floor, close enough to hear every proclamation, every speech, every *merciful* decree issued in his stolen voice. Close enough to know. For twelve years.

I put the lantern down. I knelt in the dust at the edge of the niche, because whatever else he was — enemy of my profession, burner of my life's work, author of the laws that had gotten friends of mine hanged — he was a starving man in a hole, and kneeling cost me nothing.

"Aldric?" I said.

His eyes moved to me. Then to Cyrus. And then he did the thing that undid me more thoroughly than anything the Wolf had ever shown me in its theater above.

He flinched.

Not from fear. From *shame*. I watched it move across his face like weather — the tightening around the mouth, the small attempt to turn his head away, the failure of the attempt because turning required muscles he had spent twelve years rationing. He looked at us the way a debtor looks at the door when the creditor knocks. He looked at us the way I had looked at Corvane's desk the morning after I realized what I'd done.

"Who sent you," he said. His voice was a ruin. Dust and disuse and something underneath the dust that sounded, faintly, terribly, like the voice from the coronation broadcasts — the real one, the one the Wolf had learned from, the way a forger learns from the true signature. "The court? Is this a test?"

"No one sent us," I said. "We came for you."

That was worse. I watched worse land on his face. If we had been sent by the court, by the Wolf, then we were part of the system, and systems could be endured. But we had come for *him*, voluntarily, which meant we thought he was worth something, which meant we might intend to *restore* him, and restoration was the one outcome he seemed to fear more than the dark.

"Why," he said. Not a question. An objection shaped like one.

* * *

Cyrus crouched beside me. He had his mother's manner in moments like this — a stillness that wasn't calm, exactly, but a decision to let the other person set the terms. He didn't reach for the king. He didn't offer food, though he had a heel of bread in his coat that I knew he'd been saving since morning. He just crouched there, at the edge of the niche, hands visible, and waited.

It took a long time. Long enough that my knees began to inform me of their opinions. Long enough that the dripping resumed its rhythm in my ears, drip and drip, and the frost-script on the walls glowed faintly warmer, and somewhere above us the fair played on, oblivious, a kingdom dancing on the lid of its own grave.

"We know what you are," Cyrus finally said. Quietly. "We know what happened. We know the thing wearing your face isn't you."

"Do you." The king's gaze slid to me. "You're the scholar. The expelled one. Wren." He knew my name. Of course he knew my name; the Wolf had fed him my name, I'd wager, the way you feed a prisoner news of the outside specifically chosen to hurt. "They said I hanged you. I remember—" his breath caught, and I understood with a lurch that he was remembering *in the Wolf's voice*, remembering the announcement of my arrest delivered from his own stolen mouth, delivered to him, down here, as entertainment or punishment or simple bookkeeping. "I remember ordering it. I remember hearing myself order it. I screamed and what came out was the decree."

I had prepared, on the walk down, a whole speech about the laws, about the burnings, about the colleagues. I had composed it in the bath of my own resentment, polished it, rehearsed it. Standing — kneeling — in front of him, I found I couldn't deliver it. Not because he didn't deserve it. Because he had already served the sentence. Twelve years of it. In the dark, listening to himself commit his crimes forever, unable to look away, unable to die. There is no accusation I could have made that his captivity had not already made, hourly, with better diction.

So instead I said: "The prophecy gives a way to pull it off you. At the gate, at midwinter, at full dark. An Amberdale holds the lock." I gestured at Cyrus without looking at him, because looking felt intrusive. "We need your oath. The crown-oath you swore at your coronation — the true one, before it took you. Broken freely, given to the working, it's the counterweight. It's how the unbinding holds."

And Aldric said: "Don't."

Just that. *Don't.* In a voice scraped down to the grain.

"Don't unbind me." His hands unfolded from his lap — slowly, joints protesting audibly, twelve years of stillness cracking like old glue — and pressed flat against the stone floor, and he pushed himself up to something like sitting, and he looked at us with those clear winter-water eyes, and he said:

"You think I want to come back? You think I want that face returned to me? I wore it while it hanged children. I wore it while it froze the river and called the freeze mercy and forty thousand people cheered, and every cheer went through me like a nail." His voice was rising now, cracking, the ruin of it tearing. "I signed the laws. Whatever wears me now signs them with my hand, but I *signed* them first. I lit the pyres. I built the thing it needed — a kingdom blind and frightened and grateful for it. It didn't conquer me. It *harvested* me. There's a difference, scholar. Look it up."

"I've looked it up," I said. "The difference is a verse long. *The crown shall eat the king, and wear him as a coat.* The eating is not the king's crime."

"The planting was." He was shaking now, the whole ruined architecture of him, and tears cut clean tracks down the grime of his face, which meant his body still worked, which meant twelve years of weeping had happened here, alone, unheard, and the salt tracks of all of it were layered in that grime like tree rings. "I was afraid. That's all it ever needed. I was afraid of the plague, afraid of the old powers, afraid of being the king who did nothing while the cities burned — and I chose fear, I chose it every time, I chose it so consistently that when the Wolf arrived there was nothing left of me that knew how to choose anything else. It didn't have to break the door. I had already unlocked it. I had oiled the hinges for years."

Cyrus made a sound. Small. I glanced at him and saw him looking at the king with an expression I recognized from mirrors and from the night the gate opened — the expression of a man hearing his own inheritance described by a stranger.

"My family bound that thing," Cyrus said. "A hundred years ago. Chose a descendant as its lock. I never agreed to any of it. I've spent twelve years telling myself the oath wasn't mine." He paused. "It was a lie. It was always mine. Being born into a debt doesn't make the debt less yours. It just makes the paying slower."

"Don't," Aldric said again, gentler now, and gentleness from him was somehow more terrible than the shouting. "You seem like good people. Foolish, but good. Leave me here. Let it wear me. As long as it's busy wearing me, it's not hunting the rest of you. I'm the coat. Let it keep its coat. What's one man against a kingdom's peace?"

And there it was. The theme of my entire miserable career, laid out on a stone floor in a dead king's voice: *price*. Everyone in this story has been asked to pay one. Cyrus's mother paid hers by walking through the gate. Cyrus pays his daily, in charms that do nothing and a life half-lived. I paid mine in exile and was about to pay it again in something larger. And here was the man who had tried to pay with an entire kingdom's freedom — who had confused surrender for sacrifice, who had stood in front of us offering to go on being devoured forever and called it duty.

"It isn't peace," I said. "It's digestion. Ask the frost-dead. Ask the hanged. Ask the girl at the fair who cried because she couldn't remember summer." I leaned forward. "You think staying eaten protects anyone. It doesn't. It's just the shape your guilt prefers. Guilt loves a locked room. It's very comfortable in here."

His breath went out of him. For a moment he only looked at me, and I watched something move behind the winter of his eyes — not agreement, not yet, but the place where agreement might eventually be built, if the weather held.

"You sound like my mother," he said, finally, absurdly. "She was a fisherman's daughter from the delta. She used to say guilt was a room you rented forever at rates you couldn't afford." A sound that might once have been a laugh. "She'd have liked the prophecy. She liked anything that ended a debt."

"Then help us end yours," Cyrus said. "Midwinter eve. Full dark. The gate. Your oath, broken freely — *freely*, Aldric, that's the term, freely means you choose it, not that you deserve it. Nobody deserves anything down here. We're all just choosing."

The king closed his eyes. Above us, faint through the stone, the fair's music swelled toward midnight, and the cold-from-underneath deepened, and the frost-script on the walls brightened like a held breath.

And that was when we heard it — not from above, but from the tunnel behind us. Boots. Many boots, moving fast through standing water, and a voice I knew, calling down into the dark with the crisp authority of a man reading from an approved list:

"Wren! I know you're down there. Corvane sends his regards — and the Wolf knows about the gate!"

The lantern guttered. Aldric opened his eyes.
