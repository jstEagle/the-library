## 16. Betrayal at the Gatehouse

The voice in the tunnel belonged to Magister Corvane, and I had never met the man, and I hated him completely.

That's the thing about betrayal — you don't need to know the betrayer. You only need to know what he's carrying. And Corvane was carrying boots. Dozens of them. The sound came through the flooded tunnel with a horrible clarity, water slapping against leather in rhythm, an army of men who had decided that wading was worth it.

Wren grabbed my sleeve. "Back way. There's a back way."

"Is there?"

"I'm improvising. Help me improvise."

Aldric was already moving. Twelve years under the floor of his own throne room had not, apparently, dulled whatever instinct lets a king count exits. He went up the cistern steps ahead of us, thin as a bundle of kindling, one hand trailing the wall, and I remember thinking — absurdly, at the worst possible moment — that he climbed like a man who'd done a great deal of stairs in the dark and had stopped minding.

We came out through a grate into the alley behind the frost court's stables, where the snow was falling upward. That's not poetry. That's not me embellishing for the reader. The snow was falling *upward*, slow and wrong, because something beneath the palace wanted it to, and nobody standing in that alley thought it was beautiful. A stable boy stared at it, then at Aldric's ruined face, then very hard at the ground.

"The gatehouse," Wren said. She'd gone pale in the way scholars go pale — not from fear but from arithmetic. "If they know about the gate, they're already there. If Corvane's here, the wardens are already—"

"Then we run," I said.

"Your legs are—"

"My legs," I said, "have spent twelve years walking down stairs to a shop. They'll manage."

I want it noted that they did not, in fact, manage, and that Wren took my arm somewhere around Tanner's Row and simply pretended she wasn't holding me up, and I pretended she was, and between the two of our pretenses we made it.

* * *

You could see the gatehouse fire from three streets away.

My shop — the shop above the gate, the shop that had been my whole careful life — had burned two nights ago on the wardens' torches. But this was different. This was the old tollhouse beside the gate itself, the one with the amber lamp that had burned in its window since before my grandmother was born, and it was burning now, and around it stood perhaps thirty wardens in their grey winter cloaks, and before them, on the steps of the gatehouse, stood a tall man with white streaks in his dark hair reading aloud from a sheaf of pages.

"That's him," Wren said. Her voice had gone very flat. "Corvane."

"You're sure? You said he was—"

"Fat and kind and half-blind. People change when you ruin them." She watched him turn a page. "He kept my research. After I was expelled. He filed it under *confiscated*, which is Archive for *mine now*."

"He's reading your prophecy."

"He's reading my *marginalia*." Something in her face I couldn't name. "The verses he can't parse. He's reading my guesses."

We crouched behind a snow-heap at the mouth of the street, which is exactly as dignified as it sounds, and I got my first good look at the gate.

I had seen it every day of my life. I had dusted its frame, checked its seals, told customers it was a decorative arch from some lord's demolished manor. But I had never seen it from the *outside* at night, in winter, with the Wolf awake behind it. It didn't look like stone anymore. It looked like a held breath. The darkness inside it had a texture, like deep water seen from above, and the frost on its frame grew even as I watched — not spreading, *reaching*, fingers of ice curling outward toward the crowd of wardens as if tasting them.

None of the wardens seemed to notice. That frightened me more than anything else that night. Thirty men and women standing close enough to touch a thing that was tasting them, and not one of them looked at it twice.

Corvane finished reading. He folded his pages with the care of a man handling state documents, and then he raised his voice in the tone Wren must have heard a hundred times across a lecture hall:

"Open it."

Nobody moved.

"His Majesty's command," Corvane said, "delivered under seal. The gate is to be opened from without, in preparation for the royal progress at midwinter. Sergeant, the key."

"There is no key, Magister." A sergeant's voice, young and unhappy. "We've searched the tollhouse. There's no lock. There's no mechanism. It's a wall, sir, if you ask me, it's—"

"It is not asking you." Corvane descended the steps. "It doesn't want a key. Keys are for doors that consent to be doors. This—" he laid his palm flat against the frozen darkness, and the frost around his hand bloomed into patterns I recognized, because I'd been reading them all week in prophecy-script, "—this wants a *name*. Amberdale! Front and center!"

And a warden stepped out of the ranks, and my heart did something complicated and painful, because it was a woman I knew. Not well. A customer. She bought luck-charms every quarter-day, always the same ones, always apologetic about it, and her name was Hess and her grandfather had been an Amberdale before the family name scattered like dropped coins. Distant kin. The last distant kin I had.

"Magister," Hess said, "I don't—"

"You have the blood. The Archive confirms it. Approach the gate and put your hand upon it."

"They said the family was ended," Hess said. Her voice shook. "Everyone says the family was ended years ago."

"The Archive," Corvane said, with terrible patience, "was wrong about a great many things. Your hand, sergeant's clerk. Or I will hold it there myself, and I promise you will enjoy that considerably less."

Hess walked to the gate. I watched her do it. I watched her plant her feet the way people do when they're trying to be brave in front of colleagues, and she reached out, and the moment her fingertips touched the frame her whole body jerked like a hooked fish, and the frost-script lit up amber along the entire arch, and Corvane made a sound like a scholar finding a footnote he'd hunted for twenty years.

"Partial resonance," he breathed. "Diluted, but sufficient. Again. Hold it longer—"

And that is when Wren stood up out of the snow.

I grabbed for her and got her cloak and missed. She was already walking down the middle of the street with her hands open at her sides and her chin up, and she called out in a clear, carrying, infuriatingly calm voice:

"Magister Corvane! Your methodology is embarrassing and your sources are forged, and I would know, because I forged better ones."

Every warden in the square turned. Every crossbow came up. Wren kept walking, and I pressed myself deeper into the snow-heap and understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, exactly what she was doing. She was making herself the most interesting thing in the square. She was buying me the one commodity I had left, which was seconds.

"Well," Corvane said, and his voice changed — softened, went warm and awful. "Wren. You came. I hoped you would. I told them you would."

"Of course you did. You always could predict me; it's why you were never threatened by me." She stopped ten paces from him. Close enough to talk. Far enough that the crossbows shifted uncertainly. "The gate can't be opened by force or fraud, Magister. The verses are explicit. Blood alone won't do it — it wants the blood *willing*. Your sergeant's clerk just gave you a jolt, nothing more. If you want the gate, you need the keeper, alive and unharmed and *cooperative*, and everything you do tonight makes that less likely, so I suggest—"

"Willing," Corvane repeated. He said it softly, tasting it. Then he turned to the wardens. "Take her."

They took her. Of course they took her. She'd known they would; she'd walked into it with her arms practically out. Two wardens caught her elbows and she didn't struggle, didn't give them anything, just looked over her shoulder once — once, directly at my snow-heap, at the exact spot where I was hiding, though I don't see how she could have known precisely where — and her expression said, with perfect scholarly precision: *the final verse, Cyrus. Read the final verse.*

And then they dragged her up the steps, and Corvane turned back to the gate, and I lay in a snowdrift with my heart hammering and my mind tearing itself apart between two commands: *go after her* and *she died to give you time, so use the time.*

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about the final verse. We had it. We'd had it for days. And we hadn't been able to read it, because it was written in a script that predates the Archive, predates the kingdom, and neither of us could parse more than fragments.

But I had spent that evening in the cisterns with a starving king, and Aldric — who had been crowned with the old rites before the Wolf came — had read it over my shoulder in a voice that got quieter with every line.

I remembered it now. Lying in the snow, watching Wren's captors reach the door, I remembered it whole, and I wished with everything in me that I didn't.

*When the hollow comes hungering to the hinge of the world,*
*and the crown serves the hunger, and the law serves the crown,*
*then let the last of the line come willing to the locking,*
*and pour out the inheritance entire —*
*not the gift alone, but the giver.*
*The gate does not take payment. The gate takes the payer.*

"Not the gift alone," I whispered, into the snow, "but the giver."

Twelve years I'd thought the oath meant guarding. Standing watch. Keeping the shop, keeping the secret, keeping the gate shut — a life of small dutiful nothings. It had never once occurred to me that *keeper* was a job description with an end date.

The magic my mother left me. The warmth under my skin I'd spent my whole life pretending wasn't there, feeding it nothing, starving it politely the way you starve a stray cat you refuse to name. All of it. All at once. Into the gate. And then—

And then nothing, presumably. An ordinary man. An empty man. Alive, maybe. The verse didn't say. Prophecies never say the part you actually need to know.

I lay there and I did the coward's arithmetic. Wren captured. King half-dead in the tunnels. Wardens everywhere. The Wolf ordering its own door opened, and the only thing between that door and forever being opened was me, lying in a drift, wishing the math came out differently.

It didn't. It never had. My mother's key-ring sat cold against my chest, twelve keys for one door, and I finally understood what she'd been telling me all those years: *somebody has to pay, love. Better it be someone who chose to.*

I got up out of the snow.

And the gate saw me.

I don't know how else to put it. Across the square, past thirty wardens and one traitor scholar and one captive woman, the darkness inside the arch turned — the way a head turns — and the frost-script flared white, and out of that textured dark came a shape. It unfolded from the gate like smoke deciding to be a man, and it wore the king's face and the king's crown and the king's grey ceremonial cloak, and it was taller than Aldric by half a head, the way a lie is always slightly larger than the truth it's wearing.

"Keeper," the Wolf said.

Its voice was everywhere. It was in the falling-upward snow. It was in the frost on my eyelashes. Every warden in the square went still — not asleep, not dead, just *stilled*, like furniture, like things waiting to be needed again.

"You've come at last," it said, and it smiled with Aldric's mouth, and the smile was warm and reasonable and hungry, and I understood in my bones what Wren had tried to warn me about on the first night: it doesn't threaten. It offers.

"I have your mother," it said. "She asked me to tell you she forgives you. For taking so long."

Behind it, held motionless between two stilled wardens, Wren's eyes found mine. And even frozen in place, even caught, she was shaking her head — slowly, deliberately, *don't listen* — and her lips moved, shaping words with no breath behind them:

*It needs you willing. So be willing — but for the right door.*

The Wolf turned its borrowed head and saw her looking at me, and something flickered across Aldric's face that was almost respect.

"Ah," it said. "Yes. Come to the gate, keeper. Bring the girl if you like. Open it with me, and everyone stands up again, and everyone goes home, and your mother waits for you on the other side, and midwinter passes gently, and no one ever has to be brave ever again."

It extended Aldric's hand, palm up. An invitation. A bargain. The oldest bargain there is.

And I walked toward it through the stillness, because I was always going to walk toward it — but I walked with my hand closed around my mother's key-ring, and in my mind I laid the final verse over its offer like a sheet of glass, and I counted, silently, the distance to the threshold, and the distance from the threshold to Wren, and the exact number of seconds between moonset and full dark on midwinter eve.

The gate does not take payment, the verse said.

The gate takes the payer.

Three paces away, the Wolf smiled wider, and the dark inside the arch began, very quietly, to laugh.
