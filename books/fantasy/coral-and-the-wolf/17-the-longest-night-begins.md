## 17. The Longest Night Begins

The frost-court kept its prisoners in the old banquet hall of the winter palace, which told you everything you needed to know about the Wolf's sense of humor. Gilded chairs pushed against the walls. A long table polished to mirror-brightness and scrubbed of food. Chandeliers hung dead overhead, their candles replaced with icicles that grew downward like teeth. And along the far wall, a row of iron cages that had clearly once held wine — because nothing said *mercy* like locking people where the vintage used to sleep.

I had been in worse rooms. I had, in fact, been expelled from better ones.

My cellmate was a bread-seller named Marta who had been arrested for warming her hands over her own oven without a permit, and who had spent the last six hours telling me about it in the tone of a woman composing a legal treatise. I let her talk. It kept both of us from listening to the other sound in the hall — the slow creak of ice thickening on the windows, and beneath it, faint as a pulse through a wrist, something breathing.

The Wolf did not visit its prisoners. It didn't need to. The cold was its visiting room, and every hour the frost climbed another finger's width up the walls, and every hour the frosted courtiers outside the bars grew more serene. That was the part I kept coming back to, the part that sat in my stomach like swallowed river-stone: they weren't unhappy. Serenity like theirs had to be taken from somewhere. It was being taken from us.

"You're not crying," Marta observed, breaking off her treatise to study me with professional interest. "Most of them cry by the third hour."

"I'm a scholar," I said. "We cry internally. It's in the regulations."

"What regulations?"

"The ones they expel you for following."

She laughed — one sharp bark, quickly strangled, because even laughter felt like the kind of thing that got permits revoked now — and I went back to what I had actually been doing for six hours, which was inventorying my captors.

Here is what three years in the Royal Archive teaches you that no warden's academy can: institutions are made entirely of habits, and habits are made entirely of people, and people will tell you everything if you know which habit to pull. The wardens outside my cage rotated on a four-hour watch. The senior one — the one with the frost creeping into the corners of his eyes like spilled salt — checked a ledger at each changeover. He wrote in it. He was proud of writing in it. And when he wrote, he licked the tip of his thumb to turn the page.

An archivist's trick, that: watch for the page-lickers. Men who love paper will trade anything for a better relationship with it.

I had no paper. They had taken my satchel, my notes, my stolen pages, and my boots — the boots being, I think, pure spite, since the floor was ice. But I had spent eleven days in the flooded sub-archive memorizing things I never thought I'd need, and one of them was this: Warden-Captain Ellery Vance, third rotation, had a sealed reprimand sitting in the Archive's disciplinary vault with his name on it. I knew because I had catalogued the disciplinary vault during my final month, back when I still believed that reading everything was the same as being trusted with anything.

The reprimand concerned a shipment of confiscated charm-books that had reached the palace instead of the incinerator. Someone had been paid. Vance's signature was on the manifest. His frost-grey superior either didn't know or didn't care, and there was my lever: a man whose secret sat in a vault he couldn't reach, held by an institution that had disowned me.

You cannot blackmail your way out of a cage with information alone. You need delivery. Fortunately, I had spent two hours of my imprisonment establishing that the youngest warden on rotation — a boy of perhaps nineteen with chapped hands and no frost in his eyes yet — could not stand Marta's treatise any better than I could, and had begun lingering near the bars in the hope that conversation would be directed at him instead.

"Corporal," I said, when he drifted close enough. "A question of protocol."

"We don't answer prisoner questions."

"It's not really a question. It's more of an offer." I kept my voice low, bored, the voice of a woman discussing the weather. "There's a document in the Archive's disciplinary vault, fourth shelf, grey box, seal of the old Provost. It concerns your captain. If it were to reach the wrong desk — say, the desk of anyone in the frost-court who wanted leverage over him — his career would end before the thaw. If it were to reach *no* desk, because a corporal with good handwriting copied out a prisoner's note and delivered it to the captain personally as an act of loyalty, then the captain owes that corporal a debt he'll spend the rest of a very short winter repaying."

The boy's face did several things in sequence, none of them subtle. "That's treason."

"That's patronage. Treason is what happens to people who don't understand patronage." I smiled the smile I had once used on Archive examiners, the one that said *I am merely the messenger for ideas far older than either of us.* "You've noticed, haven't you, that the courtiers with frost in their eyes all seem happy? Ask yourself where the happiness comes from. Then ask yourself whether Captain Vance would rather owe you a favor or owe the Wolf an explanation of that manifest."

He looked at Vance. Vance, at that moment, licked his thumb and turned a page with the tenderness of a man greeting a lover.

The boy came back within the hour with scrap paper and a stylus. I dictated three lines — enough to prove I knew the box number, the seal, and the date; not enough to hang anyone, including me. He copied it in careful clerk's hand, and when the watch turned, he hesitated only slightly before carrying it to his captain like a man delivering a live coal.

Marta leaned against the bars beside me. "What was that?"

"Insurance," I said.

"Against what? You gave him your way out."

"No," I said. "I gave the captain a reason to move me to a softer cage and the boy a reason to leave a door unbarred. Both of them think they're the clever one. That's how you get two exits for the price of one." I had learned that from Cyrus, actually, though he'd have denied it — twelve years of running a shop in a hanging offense taught a person to build doors out of other people's assumptions. I missed him with a suddenness that felt like cold water, and put the feeling away where I kept everything else I didn't have time for.

* * *

Vance moved me before midnight. Not to a softer cage — to an audience chamber one floor up, where the throne of the winter court stood draped in white furs that had never known a sheep, and where a woman waited whom I had hoped never to see again in this life.

Archivist-Superior Odile Hark. My mentor. The woman who had taught me to read dead languages by candlelight and to fear living ones; who had signed my expulsion with a steady hand and, I had always suspected, genuine grief; who now wore the frost the way other women wore pearls — delicately, at the temples, in fine white filaments that might have been age if age ever looked that contented.

"Wren," she said, warm as a hearth. That was always the obscenity of Odile: she was genuinely kind, in the way a well-run library is kind, in the way that has nothing to do with what happens to the books it burns.

"Superior." I dropped the old honorific into a curtsy shallow enough to register as commentary.

"Look at you." She circled me once, appraising, and I hated how much of me stood straighter under it, some starved student reflex twelve years deep. "In a cell. Without boots. Reading the wardens like ledger entries — yes, I heard about Vance. You always did collect other people's secrets the way other girls collected ribbon."

"Someone had to keep the skill alive. The Archive stopped rewarding it."

"The Archive stopped rewarding it because secrets in the wrong hands start wars." She settled onto the throne's armrest rather than the throne itself — a courtesy, or a taunt, or both. "You found a prophecy, Wren. Three years ago. You brought it to me. Do you remember what I told you?"

"You told me to recatalogue it under agricultural almanacs."

"I told you it was dangerous. You read further anyway. And here we are." She gestured at the hall, the frost, the serene guards with their salt-white eyes. "So I find myself wondering what else you read down there in the wet dark. What you know about the rite."

And there it was. Not an interrogation — an invitation. The frost-court wanted to know what its enemy knew, and it had correctly calculated that the fastest route ran through whatever remained between me and my teacher.

I could have lied. Lying to Odile was a graduate exercise and I'd have passed with distinction. But lies are perishable goods, and I might need her to believe me later, so I gave her something true instead: "I know the prophecy's final verse isn't about the Wolf at all. It's about the gate. And I know you're afraid of it, because the Wolf doesn't send Archivist-Superiors to interrogate shopgirls unless it's afraid."

"Shopgirl," Odile repeated, amused. "You mean the Amberdale boy." Something moved behind the frost-filaments — not anger. Recognition, the particular recognition of a scholar who has just confirmed a citation. "You care for him."

"I am professionally associated with him."

"Wren." She said my name the way she used to when I'd brought her a wrong attribution: gently, and with the full weight of an institution behind it. "Come inside. Truly inside. There is a place for you here — a *real* archive, not a drowned cellar. The Wolf preserves, child. Everything the old world burned, it keeps. Every book. Every voice. Even—" and her voice did something almost human, almost soft, "—even your mother's field notes. All nine years of them. Catalogued. Intact. You could hold them again."

My mother died in a survey expedition when I was eleven, in the white country north of the map's edge, and I had spent twenty years pretending that the hole her absence left was simply the shape of the world. Odile knew that. Odile had held me at the funeral. And now she stood in a stolen palace offering me my mother's handwriting like a merchant offering sugar, and the worst part — the part I would have to live with — was that for three full seconds, I wanted it.

Three seconds. Then I saw the frost on her temples for what it was: interest paid. Serenity purchased. Somewhere in this palace, someone's grief was being eaten to make her calm, and she had stopped asking where the coin came from, and that was the whole architecture of the thing, wasn't it — evil as immaculate bookkeeping.

"You archived her notes," I said, "because the Wolf archives everything it intends to digest later. Thank you, but I decline. I've seen what's on the menu."

Odile sighed, and for just a moment the kindness in her face was real, and that was somehow worse than everything else. "Then you'll go back to the cage, and midwinter will come, and the gate will seal, and none of it will matter. Unless." She rose from the armrest. "Unless you tell me where the Amberdale boy is keeping himself tonight."

"The frost-court already knows," I said. "You wouldn't be asking if it didn't. Which means your master wants confirmation from *me*, and I would sooner teach a fish to catalogue than give it."

"Take her back," Odile told the guards, wearily. Then, at the door, without turning: "For what it's worth, Wren — I'm sorry about the boots."

They were the most sincere words she'd said all night. That was Odile exactly. Sorry about the small cruelties; committed to the enormous one.

* * *

The corporal came for me at the black hour before dawn, the hour the old texts call *the hinge*, when the night swings on its pin. He had keys and a terrified expression and a story about the captain needing the prisoner moved to holding cells near the gatehouse — which was either my bribe bearing fruit or a trap wearing its skin, and I had perhaps ten steps to decide which.

I decided on the basis of his hands. They were shaking. Wardens walking you into a trap shake with excitement or frost; only wardens committing their first treason shake like that. I went with him.

He took me down servants' stairs, past a laundry frozen mid-task — sheets stiff as sails on lines of ice — and out through a coal door into the alley behind the stables, where he stopped and thrust something at me. Boots. My boots, recovered from stores, smelling of someone else's cedar.

"The captain says you're to be questioned at the gatehouse," the boy whispered. "But the questioning detail doesn't muster till the fourth bell, and the gatehouse is through the frost fair, and nobody checks papers at the fair because everyone there is already too cold to run." He swallowed. "The manifest. Grey box. You'll send word to the Archive that it stays buried?"

"On my life," I said, and meant it in the specific way that I intended to go on living.

"Which way is the Amberdale man?" he asked, because everyone always eventually asked, and I looked at him — nineteen, chapped hands, first treason still fresh enough to taste of — and told the truth.

"Wherever he is, he's doing the hardest thing anyone can do tonight. Waiting." I pulled on the boots. "Thank you, Corporal. When this winter ends — and it ends tonight — get out of the wardens before they ask you why your captain's enemies keep surviving."

* * *

The frost fair was Midwinter Eve's own cruelty: the regime had frozen the River Marrow solid and pitched a carnival on its back — stalls of tallow candles, spitted chestnuts gone cold in their pans, a carousel whose painted horses had been replaced by carved wolves at some point nobody discussed. Families moved through it in silence, spending ration-tokens on warmth they couldn't afford because not attending would be noticed. Above the fair, lanterns burned with a white flame that gave off light and no heat whatsoever, and everyone pretended, in the universal grammar of terror, that they were perfectly normal lanterns.

I crossed the ice with my collar up and my scholar's walk suppressed — head down, shoulders dull, the gait of a woman going somewhere tedious for reasons involving paperwork. Halfway across, a warden detail fell in behind me.

Not after me. *Behind* me, at parade distance, escorting a prisoner wagon toward the palace. And in the wagon, hair loose and hands bound, sat a young man I'd seen once through a cellar grate and twice in nightmares: not Cyrus. The other one. King Aldric — the real one, hollow-cheeked, twelve years starved inside his own echo, being transported to the gatehouse ahead of schedule.

Ahead of schedule. The words rang alarm-bells in the archive of my skull. The rite required full dark of midwinter eve — tonight, yes — but the prophecy's third verse specified the *convergence*: keeper, king, and gate, together, at the turning of the hour. If the Wolf moved Aldric to the gate early, it wasn't reacting to us. It was *positioning*. It had read the same prophecy I had, and unlike me, it had an army.

And Cyrus — Cyrus was waiting, because I'd told him to wait, because we'd agreed the plan, because he trusted me to come back with the last piece of the working translated from the drowned verses.

Which was when the second piece of knowledge, the piece I had carried out of the sub-archive and had been carrying ever since like a stone in my boot, finally made me stop walking. Right there on the ice, among the wolf-carousel and the cold chestnuts, in front of the lanterns that lied about fire.

The drowned verses. The ones I'd translated by candle-grease in the flooded dark while Cyrus stood guard above. I had given him the assembly order, the timing, the geometry of the circle. I had not given him the cost, because I hadn't finished translating the cost until yesterday, and I had spent the day since not saying it out loud, in the superstitious belief that words spoken become words true.

Verse forty-one, in the old tongue, which translates more honestly than I would like:

*The gate drinks the giver. Not a measure. Not a portion. The well entire.*

The rite didn't need Cyrus's magic poured into the gate. It needed Cyrus's magic *gone* — every thread of the Amberdale inheritance, permanently, irrevocably, the birthright and the bloodline's burden surrendered whole. And since the magic *was* the line and the line *was* the magic, what it truly required was the end of the Amberdales. Not his death. Something the old text considered worse, and had written in a hand that shook: an ending while still alive. Twelve years of charms that do nothing becoming twelve hundred years of charms that do nothing, forever, because there would never be another keeper, another key-ring, another heir.

He didn't know. He thought he was pouring out a cup. He was pouring out the well.

I ran.

Not the dignified trot of a scholar but the flat-out, boots-skidding-on-ice sprint of a woman who has just understood that her silence has a body count. A chestnut vendor shouted. A wolf on the carousel seemed to turn its carved head. The warden detail behind me yelled something about papers, and I did the single least scholarly thing available: I grabbed the hem of my coat and bellowed, in my best Archive Latin, *"Plague-cart inspection! Make way!"* — and in a city where plague was the official reason for eternal winter, wardens parted like I was made of knives.

Behind the palace, down the coal-road, past the laundry with its frozen sails. My lungs burned with air like swallowed glass. Somewhere ahead, in a gatehouse antechamber or a cistern shadow, Cyrus Amberdale was checking his mother's key-ring for the hundredth time and preparing, with that terrible gentle diligence of his, to give what he thought was a portion.

The sky overhead had gone the color of a closed eye. Full dark was coming. The longest night was beginning, and I had exactly one thing left to offer the man I loved and would die before naming: the truth, delivered before the hour turned.

I ran faster.

"*Cyrus!*" I shouted at the dark ahead of me, at the sleeping city, at the gate I could feel under my feet like a heartbeat in the stone. "It takes *all of it!* Do you hear me — it takes *all*—"

The gatehouse loomed out of the snow, and every door in it opened at once.
