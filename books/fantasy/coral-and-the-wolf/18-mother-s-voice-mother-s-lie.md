## 18. Mother's Voice, Mother's Lie

The gate had never looked like much from the front. That was the joke of it — twelve years of my life spent beneath a door that a carpenter would have called shoddy work. Old oak gone grey as driftwood, iron hinges furred with rust, and carved into the lintel in letters worn nearly smooth: *AMBERDALE*. My name. My family's whole history, reduced to a word people stopped reading before I was born.

Now it stood open an inch, and it was the most frightening thing I had ever seen.

Wren was behind me. I could hear her breathing — quick, controlled, the breathing of someone who has decided to be brave and is holding the decision together with both hands. She'd said her piece on the stairs. *It isn't her, Cyrus. Whatever you hear through there, it isn't her.* And then she'd done the harder thing, which was to stop saying it.

I put my palm flat against the oak.

Cold came through like the wood was a window onto midwinter itself. Not the cold of stone or snow — the cold of somewhere that had never once been warm. And beneath it, faint as a voice heard through a wall, my mother said: *"Cyrus."*

Just my name. That was all it took. Twelve years of careful living, twelve years of charms that did nothing and doors that stayed shut and grief folded up small enough to fit in a shopkeeper's apron pocket — and one word in her voice undid all of it.

"I know," I said. "I'm here."

*Don't,* Wren breathed behind me.

But the gate was already opening. Not swinging wide — the way water opens around a sinking thing. The dark between the door and its frame deepened, thickened, and became a threshold, and the threshold became a room, and the room was our kitchen on Coriander Lane, the one from before the shop, before everything. Copper pots on their hooks. The dent in the table where I'd dropped a hammer at nine years old. Bread smell. My mother at the stove with her back to me, sleeves rolled, humming the tune she always hummed when she thought no one was listening.

"Cyrus," she said again, turning. Her face was exactly right. That was the horror of it — not some approximation, not a mask with the seams showing. It was her, down to the flour smudge on her cheekbone she never seemed to notice.

"You're dead," I said. Or maybe: "You're gone." I don't remember which. Both were true, and neither mattered to the thing wearing her.

*"Am I?"* She smiled, and it was her smile, tired and crooked, the one that meant she'd won an argument without telling me we'd been having one. *"You've kept the shop so well, love. All these years. Come inside and tell me about them."*

Behind me, Wren said my name. Once. Quietly. She didn't grab my arm. Later I would understand what that restraint cost her, and what it meant.

I took one step forward.

And the kitchen flickered.

It was small — a stutter, like a candle in wind — but I saw it. For half a heartbeat the copper pots were frost-white, the bread smell was the smell of old ice, and my mother's eyes were the flat grey of a frozen pond. Then it smoothed over, warm and golden again, and she held out her hand.

Something in me went very still and very cold, colder than the gate.

Because I'd seen this trick before. Three nights ago, in the throne room, the Wolf had worn King Aldric's face and offered me the same bargain in the same loving voice. And I'd learned something in that room that I hadn't understood until this exact moment: the Wolf is not good at love. It is magnificent at longing. It knows precisely what you ache for and can build it perfectly, plank by plank, out of your own memory. But it builds from the outside in. It gets the face right and the hands right and the voice right, and it forgets that love lives in the flaws — in the things the beloved got wrong, the habits that made no sense, the small stubborn wrongnesses you'd never trade away.

My mother hummed when she cooked. True. But she hummed off-key, deliberately, because she claimed songs sounded better slightly broken. This woman hummed true.

My mother never turned her back on a door. Ever. She'd grown up in a border town that had been raided twice, and she'd sooner burn the bread than stand with her back to a room's entrance. This woman had her back to the threshold.

And the flour smudge — the flour smudge was on the wrong cheek. I knew because I'd kissed that cheek ten thousand times, and you do not misremember where you put your mouth for twenty years of goodbyes.

"Say something only she'd say," I said aloud, to the kitchen, to the thing in it.

*"Cyrus, darling, come inside—"*

"Wrong answer."

The kitchen shattered like a dropped plate.

* * *

What was left wasn't a room at all. It was the space behind the gate as it truly was — vast, dark, and full of winter. Snow hung in the air without falling. Ice grew in shapes that weren't natural: spirals, whorls, patterns like ferns pressed in white glass. Coral, I thought, absurdly. It looks like coral. White coral, a reef at the bottom of a frozen sea.

And everywhere, threaded through the frost, images moved. Memories, caught in the ice like flies in amber. A young man in a crown, screaming. Wardens marching. A city under white. And nearer, just past the threshold, a single figure standing in the deep cold with her back to me.

Her back to me.

I almost laughed. Even now. Even here. My mother, refusing to turn her back on a door.

"Mum," I said.

She turned.

This time it was her. I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — not by checking, but because the knowing is older than checking. Older than sense. She was thinner than I remembered, greyer, dressed in the travelling coat she'd vanished in twelve years ago, and her face was lined in ways the Wolf had never bothered to learn. The Wolf gave me the mother I remembered. Reality gave me the mother who had aged.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you're taller."

That's what she said. Twelve years. A gate between us. An apocalypse on the other side of the wall. And my mother looked at me and said *you're taller*, and I stood in the freezing dark of a prison between worlds and cried like the nineteen-year-old she'd left behind.

She didn't come to me. I noticed that. She stayed exactly where she was, three paces inside the threshold, hands visible, the way you stand with a horse you've spooked or a man you've hurt.

"You're not coming home," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd known it since the kitchen shattered, maybe since the day she left. Knowing a thing and surviving the knowledge are different skills, and I have never been good at the second one.

"No," she said gently. "I'm not."

"The Wolf took you."

"Cyrus." She said my name the way she used to when I was about to make a mistake in arithmetic — patient, braced. "Look at my hands."

I looked. They were bare, chapped, ordinary. And around the left wrist, faded almost to nothing, was a line of ink. A binding-mark. The same mark I'd seen in the antechamber memory, on the arm of the first Amberdale, the one who'd made the Wolf a lock and made his blood the key.

"You went in," I said. The words came out of me like splinters. "You walked in. On purpose."

"On purpose," she agreed. "Twelve years ago, the seal was failing. Your grandmother's work had frayed — she stretched it thin keeping the shop and the gate both, and it cost her, and by the end there wasn't enough of her left to renew it. Someone had to go in and hold it from the inside. Feed it slowly, so it wouldn't wake hungry. So it wouldn't come looking." She smiled, and it was the tired crooked smile, the real one. "There was only one Amberdale left with the strength for it who wasn't a child."

"You could have told me."

"And you'd have let me go?" She raised an eyebrow. In the frozen dark, lit by cold light, it was such a familiar expression that my chest hurt. "You were nineteen and you'd have insisted on coming with me, and then there'd be two of us in here and nobody outside, and the shop would be empty, and the gate would have no keeper, and the Wolf would have won by spring. No. I did what Amberdales do. I lied to the people I love, neatly, and paid for it alone."

"That's a terrible family tradition."

"It's the family tradition," she said. "Along with the key-ring."

I had it on my belt. I'd carried it out of the burning shop without thinking — twelve keys on a ring of dull brass, and for twelve years I'd assumed they opened the shop's various locks, cellar, back door, strongbox. I'd never once tried them on the gate, because the gate had never needed a key. It needed a keeper.

"Count them," my mother said.

I counted. Eleven. There had always been eleven. But when I looked closer, really looked, each key was different — and each one was shaped like a year. No. Shaped like a decade. No —

"They're not keys to locks," my mother said. "They're keys to choices. Every keeper who faced this threshold held the ring, and every keeper saw what they needed to see in it. Your grandmother saw eleven doors. I saw eleven winters. You'll see what you see." She stepped closer — finally, finally close enough to touch, though she still didn't. "The Wolf will offer you a bargain tonight. It may already have. It keeps me, Cyrus. That part is true. It holds what's left of me the way a miser holds coin, and it will spend me gladly to get what it wants. What it wants is for the gate to open wide from the inside. If an Amberdale walks out willingly, the lock breaks forever, and it comes through into a world that has forgotten how to fight it."

"So I leave you here."

"So you leave me here," she said, "or so you finish what I started. Those are your eleven keys, love. Everything else is the Wolf talking."

"It said you were payment on a debt."

"It would." Her jaw tightened — and there, for a moment, I saw something fierce move behind her face, something that had spent twelve years in the dark refusing to become what surrounded her. "Listen to me. The Wolf cannot lie outright. It's bound too tightly for that; the first Amberdale saw to it. So it tells truths bent double. Yes, I am its prisoner. Yes, I am its payment. But it omits the part where I *volunteered* to be both, and it omits the part where every year I hold it here is a year it can't feed freely on the city above. I am not its treasure, Cyrus. I am its tapeworm."

Despite everything — despite the frost and the grief and the apocalypse waiting upstairs — I laughed. It came out cracked and wet, but it was a laugh.

"There he is," my mother said softly. "Your father's laugh. He'd have liked the joke."

"He'd have hated everything else about this."

"Fiercely. He'd have burned the whole gate down with everyone standing in front of it." She reached out then, and touched my face, and her hand was cold but it was *her* hand, chapped and certain. "Cyrus. I need to tell you the last thing, and then you need to go, because the Wolf is learning this conversation even as we have it, and everything I say that it hears, it can use."

"Then don't say it."

"I have to." She took a breath. "The rite Wren found — the unbinding. It's real. It will work. But it doesn't take some of an Amberdale's magic. It takes all of it. Every thread, down to the last knot. You'll live — the prophecy is clear on that, cruelly clear — but you'll live *empty*. No craft, no gate-sense, no inheritance. Just a man in a shop selling charms that do nothing." Her eyes searched mine. "I am so sorry. I am sorry we built a life for you out of other people's sacrifices and then handed you the bill. If there had been another way —"

"Mum." I covered her hand with mine. "There wasn't. There isn't. And I've been a charm-seller for twelve years. Turns out I'm overqualified for a life of doing nothing."

She laughed — a short, startled, real laugh — and then pressed her forehead to mine, and for one moment the cold went out of the world entirely, and I was eight years old with a skinned knee and everything was simple and nothing was lost.

Then she stepped back, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"The key-ring," she said. "Take the eleventh key. The small one, the one that looks like it fits nothing. When the time comes, you'll know. It was always going to be yours. It was always going to be this night." She was already fading — not vanishing, but receding, the dark folding itself politely between us. "One more thing. The Wolf showed you a kitchen tonight. Remember how you knew. That's how you'll beat it. It can copy everything we ever loved except the reasons we loved it. Hold on to the wrongnesses, Cyrus. Hold on to the flour on the wrong cheek. That's where the truth lives."

"Mum—"

"And Cyrus — the girl with the stolen pages. Keep her. People like us find a hundred reasons to be alone, and they're all lies."

And she was gone, and the gate stood open an inch, and the cold came through like a promise.

* * *

Wren was sitting on the cellar stairs with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, and she had clearly been crying and was clearly going to deny it. I decided to let her.

"Well?" she said.

"She went in on purpose." I sat down beside her. The stairs were cold but the cold felt honest now, just stone and winter, no voices in it. "Twelve years ago. To slow it down. She left me the shop and the oath and a lie about being taken, because if she'd asked, I'd have followed her in."

Wren was quiet for a while. Somewhere above us, muffled by floors and walls, the city groaned under the weight of the longest winter anyone had ever recorded. Midwinter eve was tomorrow. Full dark was coming.

"I told the Archive about the prophecy," she said suddenly. "Three years ago. I wanted them to value me, so I handed them the one dangerous page I'd found, and the Archive reported upward, and the Wolf learned a keeper named Amberdale existed, and sent wardens to watch your shop, and posted your name, and burned your home." Her voice was steady in the way of someone gripping a rail. "I've been trying to find a version of that story where I'm not the reason you're standing in a cellar with nothing. There isn't one."

"Wren."

"My mentor offered me reinstatement yesterday. All I had to do was tell him where you sleep."

"I know. You burned your credentials. I was there."

"I need you to know that I would do it again. The burning. All of it."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm telling you what my mother told me." I took out the key-ring and turned it over in my fingers, eleven keys catching what little light there was. "She said the rite takes everything. All of it. Permanently. After tomorrow I'll be nobody. No magic, no line, no gate. Just a man."

Wren looked at the keys, and then at me, and I watched her arrive at the same place I had arrived at in the dark, standing in a memory of my mother's hands.

"Will you hate me," she said quietly, "if I admit I'm relieved? That it's a price and not a death? I've been preparing myself for worse since the Frost Fair."

"Don't be relieved yet." I stood, and held up the smallest key, the one that fit nothing, the one that had always been waiting. "The Wolf still has my mother. And the king. And the whole city. And tomorrow at full dark, I intend to take every one of them back — starting with the thing that thought a flour smudge on the wrong cheek would fool me."

Above us, faint through the floorboards, the first bells of midwinter eve began to ring.
