## 19. Unbinding

### Cyrus

Full dark came at half past four in the afternoon, which was the kind of joke the winter liked to play.

Wren and I stood in the gatehouse antechamber with the key-ring in my hand and the rite chalked on the floor in a circle of symbols I could neither read nor erase. My mother's handwriting, mostly. Some of it older. The oldest lines were scored into the stone itself, which meant the first Amberdale had not trusted ink to hold his work, and I supposed I couldn't blame him.

"Say it back to me," Wren said.

"The gate opens only for an Amberdale." I turned the ring over. Twelve keys, one for each year I'd kept the shop, though I'd only ever known what three of them did. "The Wolf can be pulled off the king only at the threshold. Only by my blood's magic, given freely and completely. At full dark of midwinter eve."

"And the part you keep skipping."

I looked at her. She looked at me with that scholar's patience she wore like armor — the look of a woman who has read every ending of a story and is waiting to see if reality is brave enough to contradict her.

"'The keeper gives all,'" I said. "'Nothing held back. Nothing left over.'"

"Good," she said softly. "Now stop skipping it."

Above us, through the ceiling of stone, the palace shook. We'd come up through the cisterns with Aldric between us — the real king, starved thin as a winter branch, walking on legs that hadn't carried him in twelve years because the thing wearing his shape had never once let him out of the echo of his own body. He had begged us, in the dark under the floor, to leave him bound. *I deserve it,* he'd said. *I outlawed magic out of fear, and fear is what it eats. I built its table.*

"You didn't build it," Wren had told him. "You just set the places."

And now here we were, at the top of the world's worst staircase, while somewhere above us the Wolf tore itself loose from the shape it had worn and came down the mountain of the palace like an avalanche with opinions.

The door at the top of the antechamber groaned. Frost bloomed across it from the inside — our side — spreading in ferns and feathers, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful.

"It's early," Wren said. "It wasn't supposed to reach the gate until the rite began."

"Maybe it read the prophecy too."

"Cyrus."

"I know." I knelt at the circle's edge and pressed my palm flat against the chalk. It was cold. Everything was cold; cold was the water we swam in now. "I'm not holding anything back. I just — I want it noted, for the record, that I had a very nice life planned. Quiet shop. Bad tea. A customer base of maybe nine people who all believed nonsense I sold them honestly."

"Noted," she said. Her voice cracked on the last word, and she covered it by kneeling across from me, mirroring my posture, her hands flat on the opposite edge of the circle. "For the record, I had a career. Tenure. A window office in the Archive with a view of the courtyard."

"A view of a courtyard."

"It was a very good courtyard."

The door burst inward in a soundless bloom of white.

* * *

### Wren

There is a moment in every text, when you are translating something old and hostile, when you understand that the author wanted you to feel exactly what you are feeling. The Wolf's arrival was like that. It did not roar. It poured.

It came through the ruined doorway as winter itself — a flood of white and hunger shaped vaguely like a man, wearing the king's silhouette the way a glove wears a hand. Behind it the frost-court's light died room by room, and the temperature dropped so fast my breath cracked in my throat. Cyrus stood up inside the circle. So did Aldric, swaying, one hand braced against the wall, and I saw the Wolf see him — see its own discarded coat — and make a sound like a glacier calving.

"*Thief,*" it said, in a voice made of a hundred stolen voices, and one of them, underneath, was a woman's, and I watched Cyrus hear it too.

"That's not her," I said, loud, fast, before longing could do what longing does. "Cyrus. That is not your mother. That's a hook with her voice tied to it."

"I know." His jaw was tight. His eyes were wet. "I know. But it's rude not to answer."

He raised the key-ring, and the oldest keys began to sing.

I don't have another word for it. The Archive had terms — *resonance, harmonic binding, sympathetic architecture* — and every one of them was a scholar's way of saying: the stone woke up. The scored lines in the floor filled with amber light, honey-colored, warm, the first warmth I had felt in weeks, and the circle rose around us like a wall of late summer. The Wolf recoiled from it with something I can only call offense.

"*Keeper,*" it said. "*Little lock. You were made to hold me. Hold me, then.*"

It struck the circle.

I felt it through my hands, through my knees, through my teeth — a pressure like the whole sky leaning on one point. The amber wall bowed inward but held, and inside it Cyrus began the rite, voice steady, reciting verses he'd memorized in two days that his family had bled into the stone over centuries. And I did my part, which was to stand at the circle's edge with stolen archive-magic burning down my arms and keep the geometry true — feeding the lines, patching them where the Wolf's cold gnawed at them, being, in the oldest sense of the word, a keeper's assistant.

Between us, forgotten by both monsters, King Aldric pushed himself off the wall.

"No," I said. "Stay inside the—"

But he was already walking toward the Wolf, this starved ghost of a king, and when he spoke his voice was wrecked and small and absolutely certain.

"You wore me," he said. "Twelve years. You ate my crown-oath out of my mouth and used it to feed. But an oath isn't yours until it's given, and I never gave it to *you*. I gave it to them." He turned, and looked at the circle, at Cyrus, at me. "To the realm. All of it is still theirs. Take it back."

And he broke his own crown-oath — snapped it like bread — and threw the pieces into the working.

* * *

### Cyrus

The power that came off him nearly knocked the rite apart.

An oath is a structure, Wren told me once — she talks like that even while bleeding, it's unbearable — and a king's oath is a cathedral. When Aldric shattered his, twelve years of stored vow came loose all at once, and the circle drank it, and the amber light went from honey to white fire, and the Wolf screamed with a hundred throats as the thing it had been digesting for a decade turned to acid in its belly.

"Now," Wren shouted. "Cyrus, *now*, it's loose from him — pull!"

I pulled.

I don't know how else to describe it. There was no gesture, no word I understood. I reached into the gate behind me — the true gate, the one my mother had walked through, the one my line had been forged into the hinge of — and I took hold of the Wolf the way you take hold of a splinter, knowing the flesh comes with it.

It fought. Of course it fought. It showed me everything it had: my mother's face, my shop intact and warm, a life where none of this happened, a life where I was nineteen and she was alive and the oath had gone to someone else. It offered me the ordinary life I'd spent twelve years pretending I already had.

"That's the trick," I said, and my voice sounded far away to me, calm, almost bored, which was strange because inside I was screaming. "You keep offering people their own lives back. But they're not for sale. They never were. You just rent them out and charge fear as interest."

I gave.

All of it. Every thread. The charm-sense I'd inherited and pretended was imagination. The warmth in my palms when I touched the good wood of the counter. The way the shop always knew when a storm was coming before I did. The deep, dumb, patient strength at the bottom of me that I'd never once named out loud — the Amberdale inheritance, twelve generations of it, banked and hoarded and passed down like a debt no one had ever asked me whether I wanted.

I poured it into the gate, and the gate opened, and the Wolf — starving, shrieking, stripped of its king and its winter and its borrowed voices — was drawn through like smoke up a chimney, like water down a drain, like everything hungry finally getting what hunger deserves.

The last thing it did was stop fighting.

That's the part I'll remember. At the very end, at the threshold, it turned its stolen face back toward me and said, in my mother's voice, gently, sincerely: "*Thank you, little lock.*"

And then it was through, and the seal closed, and the quiet afterward was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

* * *

### Wren

The winter broke like a fever breaks — all at once, in sweat and shivering, leaving the world pale and wrung-out and astonished.

Snow slid from the rafters of the gatehouse in slabs. Somewhere above us, bells began — real bells, human bells, the city waking to a midwinter night that was simply a midwinter night, cold and finite and ending. I sat down hard on the chalked stones, which were just stones now, and laughed, or cried, or performed some undignified combination of the two that I will deny to my grave.

Cyrus was still standing in the center of the circle. Standing very still.

"Cyrus?"

"I'm fine," he said. Then, after a moment, with the small wondering tone of a man testing a floor that might not hold: "I'm — empty. It's strange. It doesn't hurt. I thought it would hurt."

"What do you feel?"

He considered. "Cold," he said. "Tired. Like the morning after a fever." He flexed his fingers, turned his palms up, and looked at them the way you'd look at a house you'd moved out of. "Ordinary. Completely, entirely ordinary."

Through the broken doorway, King Aldric lay on the stones where the unbinding had dropped him — breathing, alive, aged decades in minutes, his face for the first time in twelve years entirely his own. He was weeping without any attempt at dignity. I decided to let him have it. Kings get so few honest moments; it seemed cruel to interrupt the first one.

Then I heard it.

A voice, from the sealed gate — faint, fading, and this time there was no frost in it, no hook, no hunger. Just a woman's voice, worn smooth with love and long patience, saying goodbye the way you say it when you've been waiting years for someone to be ready to hear it.

Cyrus went to the gate. Put his hand flat against the stone. Listened.

I didn't try to hear the words. Some pages aren't meant to be translated.

When he turned around, his eyes were red and his face was open in a way I had never seen it, and he said, "She chose it. Same as me. She's telling me it gets easier." A breath. "She says thank you. To you. For reading the whole thing."

"Tell her—" I started, and stopped, because the gate had already gone quiet, and some messages arrive too late by exactly the right amount.

Cyrus looked at his empty hands again. Then at me. Then he smiled — tired, crooked, entirely ordinary, and worth more than every charm he'd ever sold.

"So," he said. "Any idea how a man with no magic, no shop, and a burned-down house goes about starting over?"

I got up off the floor. My legs complained. I ignored them.

"As it happens," I said, "I know a scholar who's very good with beginnings."

Above us, through the shattered roof, the first thaw-drip struck the stones — one bright drop, then another, then a thousand, the whole frozen palace beginning, at last, to melt.
