## 21. Thaw

The ice on the Coronation Square fountain cracked at dawn on the first day of spring, and by noon the whole city had come out to watch it die.

I stood in the crowd with my hands in my coat pockets, feeling like a fraud in the best possible way. Three months ago I'd stood in this same square watching frost crawl up the king's banners while a thing wearing his face promised us an endless winter as a mercy. Now the same square was full of people selling roasted chestnuts and paper windmills, and a child near me was stomping puddles with the focused violence of someone who had never once seen snow before and already hated it.

"You're staring," said the child's mother, not unkindly.

"I'm appreciating," I said. "It's different."

It was different. That was the strange part. You would think a city that had spent twelve years under a false king and one terrible winter would come out of it changed beyond recognition — rebuilt, renamed, torn down to the cobbles and started over. But cities are like scholars that way. They keep their old notes. They reshelve the same mistakes and call the shelf new. The bakeries had reopened under the same names. The wardens' barracks still stood, though half-empty now and flying no crest at all, because nobody could agree what crest a warden should fly when the law he'd enforced turned out to have been written by a hunger wearing a crown.

And Aldric — the real Aldric, the one we'd pulled out of his own body's echo like a splinter from a wound — sat on the throne he had technically never left, aged twelve years in a season, and did the one thing no one expected.

He told the truth.

* * *

I was there for the repeal. Of course I was there; I'd have crawled there on broken glass if they'd only let me through the doors without a title, which, as it happened, they wouldn't, which is how I ended up standing in the throne room of a palace I had once infiltrated disguised as a clerk's assistant, wearing a dress borrowed from a merchant's widow who owed me a favor, feeling like a cat that had been invited into the fishmonger's as a consultant.

Aldric looked smaller than he had from the square. Twelve years inside himself, starved of everything but his own guilt, will do that. His hair had gone white at the temples in patches, like frost that hadn't gotten the news. But his eyes were his own — brown, tired, human — and when he spoke you could see him checking every word against something internal before releasing it, the way a man checks thin ice with each step.

"The law known as the Edict of Purity," he read, "is repealed."

The room made a sound I've only heard twice since: not cheering, exactly. Something more careful. A few hundred people all exhaling at slightly different times.

"Further," he continued, and here his voice caught on something, "the Crown acknowledges that magic was never the plague upon this realm. Fear was. And fear, it turns out, can be legislated far more easily than cured." He set down the scroll. Looked up. "That is the last lie this crown will tell. There will be others — smaller ones, I'm sure, because governments are made of smaller ones the way walls are made of bricks — but none spoken knowingly, and none about this."

Then he did the second unexpected thing. He looked directly at me.

"Wren of the Royal Archive," he said. "Come forward."

I want it noted, for the historical record I am apparently going to be responsible for keeping, that I considered running. The thought lasted approximately one and a half heartbeats, during which I calculated the distance to the servants' door, remembered that I had burned my credentials in a brazier nine months ago and therefore technically did not exist, and concluded that fleeing the throne room would create paperwork problems for other people. So I walked forward instead, which is the braver choice only in retrospect.

"Your Majesty."

"You were expelled from my Archive," he said.

"Fired, technically. The expulsion came later, after I broke back in. Twice." A ripple went through the courtiers. I kept my eyes on the king, because if I looked at them I would start cataloguing which ones had frosted eyes last winter, and that was a list I didn't need to be holding in public.

"And then you broke into it a third time to save my life," Aldric said. "Along with a charm-seller, a stolen prophecy, and — I'm told — a great deal of archive-magic that no living person should have been able to hold."

"It held me more than I held it, sire."

"That is usually how real knowledge works." He descended one step, so we were nearly level, and the whole court leaned in like tide. "The Archive is a ruin. Its masters were complicit or cowardly or both, its sub-levels flooded, its collections scattered or burned or eaten — literally eaten, I'm told, by the thing that wore my face, which consumed knowledge the way it consumed everything else. I am told you know those sub-levels better than anyone alive. I am asking you to rebuild it. Openly. As its keeper."

Keeper. The word went through me like a needle finding an old scar. I glanced sideways, involuntarily, toward the tall windows and the grey city beyond them — south, toward a certain street where a certain shop stood shuttered, where a certain man had poured out the last of his family's magic and gone home to sweep up.

"No," I said.

The silence had texture. Somewhere a courtier dropped a fan.

"I mean — yes, obviously, to the Archive. To all of it. Open shelves, open doors, open reading rooms where a girl can be fired for reading the wrong page and *not* have it end her life. Someone has to do it and it turns out I'm insufferable enough to survive the job." I heard my own voice doing the thing it does when I'm terrified: getting precise. "But no title. No keeper's oath. Nothing inherited, nothing sworn, nothing passed down like a debt. Your Majesty has just watched what oaths become when things go wrong. I'll rebuild your Archive as a hired scholar with a bad reputation. If I do it well, fire me someday and let someone else improve on it. Institutions shouldn't belong to people. People should belong to institutions briefly, and then leave them better."

Aldric studied me for a long moment. Then — and I will treasure this until I die — he almost smiled.

"A hired scholar with a bad reputation," he repeated. "Very well. You'll find the pay insulting. That's traditional."

"Sire, I'd have done it for free, but don't tell the treasurer."

* * *

The Archive, when I first walked back into it, smelled of river water and ash and, underneath both, of dust that had been settling patiently for years, waiting for someone to disturb it. Which is, when you think about it, the most hopeful smell in the world.

We started with the lower floors. The floodwater had taken the periodicals and most of the legal codes — no great loss; I'd spent the winter drafting a proposal to burn the legal codes anyway — but the deep stacks had survived, sealed behind doors that the Wolf had never bothered with. It ate knowledge that people feared. It had never learned to fear footnotes.

My first hires were the disgraced and the dismissed. A cartographer who'd been expelled for mapping the flooded levels against orders. Two copyists who'd been arrested for circulating banned histories of the succession. An old archivist named Pellam who had spent the Wolf's reign quietly misfiling everything important so the frost-court couldn't find it, and who greeted me on my first morning by handing me a ledger and saying, "You'll want to know what survived, and I'll warn you now: the answer is 'less than you hoped, more than you deserve.'"

"Pellam," I said, "you are describing my entire life."

"Good. Then you'll feel at home."

We worked through the thaw and into true spring. I learned things about rebuilding that no book taught me. That an institution heals the way a person does: unevenly, with setbacks, with days when you find a sealed room full of confiscated grimoires and have to sit down on the steps outside it for a while. That trust isn't restored by decree any more than it's destroyed by one. Every week a few more citizens came to the reading room door, hovered, and left. Then some stayed. Then some brought their children, and pointed at the shelves, and said words I couldn't hear from my desk but could guess: *this used to be forbidden.*

One gray afternoon I found a girl of perhaps ten in the prophecy section, reading a text upside-down and getting most of it right.

"You know those are dangerous," I said, from the doorway.

She looked up with the flat, unimpressed stare of her age. "Are you going to arrest me?"

"No," I said. "Never again. Nobody is. Read whatever you want. That's rather the entire point of the building."

She nodded slowly, filed this away as obviously correct, and went back to her book. I stood there longer than I meant to. Somewhere in the middle of the worst winter of my life, I had burned my credentials in a warden's brazier and chosen a man over a career, and I had thought of it then as a loss — the last door closing behind me. Standing in the doorway of the reopened Archive watching a ten-year-old read prophecies legally, I understood that I had actually opened one. It had just been too dark, at the time, to see what was on the other side.

Institutions mended by people, not crowns. I wrote that sentence in the front of the new accession ledger, then crossed it out as too pleased with itself, then wrote it back in, because it was true and I have never once let modesty interfere with accuracy.

* * *

Cyrus came to the Archive in late spring, on a Tuesday, carrying a box.

I knew he was coming, of course. He'd sent a note three days earlier — Cyrus communicates entirely through short notes, as though postage were priced by the word — which read: *Found things in the cellar. Some yours? Come see the shop when you can. The sign is up.* The sign being up was, I had gathered from other channels, a matter of some local interest; the shop had reopened under a fresh-painted board that read AMBERDALE CHARMES & CURIOSITYES, HONESTLY LABELED, and the neighborhood had adopted it immediately, the way neighborhoods adopt anything honest, which is to say rarely and then fiercely.

He was sweeping when I arrived. Of course he was. Cyrus sweeps the way other men pray.

"Your sign has two extra E's in it," I said from the doorway.

"I know. I'm testing whether honesty attracts customers or repels them. Early results are encouraging. Yesterday I sold a woman a luck-charm and told her it was a painted button, and she bought three."

"For herself?"

"For her sister. She said her sister needs to learn to make her own luck, and an honest shopkeeper is cheaper than a dishonest priest." He leaned the broom against the counter. Behind him, past the bead curtain at the back of the storeroom, the wall stood quiet. The gate slept behind it — unlocked, unneeded, a dark doorway that led nowhere anymore except onward, which is the best thing a doorway can do. I felt it the way you feel a sleeping animal in the next room: a warm absence of threat.

"What's in the box?" I asked.

"Cellar things." He set it on the counter between us and lifted the lid, and my breath did something embarrassing. Papers. Dozens of them, water-stained and foxed and wonderful — pages I recognized, because I had spent three years of my life chasing fragments of them across half the archives of the realm. Prophecy leaves. Marginalia in the hand of the first Amberdale. Notes in my own handwriting from before the winter, pages I'd copied and hidden and assumed burned when the wardens torched the shop.

"You hid these," I said. "Under the floorboards. Before the raid."

"After you visited the first time. I figured if trouble followed you, trouble follows paper." He shrugged, in the manner of a man who had casually performed the single most archival act of my acquaintance. "Some of it's yours. Some of it's the prophecy — the whole thing, I think, including the verses the gate showed me that night. The parts about the price." He said it evenly. We had made our peace with the price, he and I, in the way of people who have stood together at the bottom of something and helped each other climb out. You don't discuss the climbing much afterward. You just keep the rope.

"There's more," he added, and pulled out a slim bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string. "This one wasn't mine to read. Mother's hand. I found it behind the loose brick where she kept the key-ring." He set it down but didn't untie it. "I've read the first page. Only the first. She wrote it for whoever was still standing after — she says so on page one — and I think that might mean you as much as me. You're the one who reads things for a living. And you're..." He stopped, and did the thing he does instead of finishing sentences about us, which is look at the shelf of honestly-labeled buttons as though it required inventorying.

"We could read it together," I said. "Tonight. Tea, the good lamp, the whole scholarly apparatus. Footnotes as foreplay."

"That is the least romantic sentence ever spoken in this shop," Cyrus said, "and I am including the sign."

But he smiled when he said it, and untied the string, and put the bundle in my hands — because some inheritances are burdens, and some, it turns out, if you're stubborn and lucky and surrounded by the right stubborn people, get to be work instead. Work you choose. Work you share.

Outside, the thaw ran loud in the gutters, and somewhere down the street a child was stomping puddles, and behind the wall the gate slept on, dreaming, if gates dream, of nothing at all.

I opened my mother-in-all-but-law's first page, and read the opening line aloud:

*"If you are reading this, then someone loved the world enough to pay for it — and someone else must love it enough to write down what happens next."*

I got out my pen.
