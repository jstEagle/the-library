## 22. Coral and the Wolf

The new sign went up on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays are for beginnings nobody planned.

I'd carved it myself over three evenings in the back room, which is to say I ruined two boards and set fire to my sleeve before the third attempt came out legible. **AMBERDALE CHARMS — THEY DO NOTHING.** Below that, smaller, because honesty deserves a fine print: *No luck. No love. No protection from wolves. Sold as wood, string, and good intentions only.*

Wren said it was commercial suicide. Wren also said it while eating my bread and drinking my tea, so I took her opinion with the appropriate grain of salt.

"You'll starve," she said.

"I've been starving for twelve years," I said. "I'm just going to do it honestly now."

She had no answer for that, which was rare enough that I savored it.

* * *

Here is what I learned about truth in commerce: people don't want it, and then, once they've had it, they can't live without it.

The first week, foot traffic dropped to nothing. The frost-bitten regulars who used to buy my mother's knot-charms against bad dreams crossed the street when they saw me sweeping the step, as though honesty were contagious. Old Marta from the chandlery stood in my doorway for a full minute reading the sign, her lips moving, and then she looked at me with something close to pity.

"Cyrus," she said gently, "you don't have to tell them."

"I know," I said. "That's rather the point."

But then came the girl. Eleven, maybe, gap-toothed, dragging a sled through slush with a cat riding on it like a figurehead. She marched into the shop, planted both hands on my counter, and demanded a charm so her brother would stop pulling her hair.

"They don't work," I told her. "None of them. That's what the sign says."

She read the sign. She looked at me. She looked at the wall of little wooden knots and braided cords and painted river-stones, all of them beautiful and all of them useless, and she said, "Then why do you make them?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dodging since midwinter, asked by a child in four seconds flat.

"Because they're pretty," I said. "Because making things with your hands is one of the few honest pleasures left. And because sometimes a person needs to hold something small and believe, just for a moment, that the world might be kind — and there's no shame in wanting that. There's only shame in charging silver for it." I slid a braided cord across the counter. "So this one's free. It won't stop your brother. But you could hit him with it."

She laughed like a door coming off its hinges, took the cord, and left. Two days later she was back with her brother, who wanted a charm *against his sister*, and by the end of the month half the children in the ward had useless Amberdale originals hanging off their belts, and their parents started coming too, sheepish, asking in low voices whether I had anything for grief.

"No," I always said. "Nothing works for grief. But this one's carved from applewood, and it fits nicely in a pocket, and holding it gives your hands something to do while your heart does its work."

They bought them. Not because they believed. Because they didn't have to anymore, and it turned out that was a relief. Twelve years of a city terrified of magic, of wardens and whispers and charms sold under the counter like contraband — and now the laws were repealed, the winter broken, the king himself walking the streets looking twenty years older and apologizing to everyone he met — and what the city wanted most, it turned out, wasn't power. It was permission to stop pretending.

I gave them pretty lies labeled as pretty lies. Business was, if not booming, at least breathing.

* * *

Behind the back wall, past the cellar where I'd once escaped the wardens through a door I'd sworn never to open, the gate sleeps.

Unlocked. That's the part that still wakes me some nights, lying in the narrow bed above the shop, listening to the building settle. For a hundred years the Amberdales kept that gate locked, and the lock was us — our oath, our blood, our magic poured into the hinge of the world like solder. My mother walked through it to hold the Wolf slow. I emptied every thread of inherited power I had into it to seal the thing away forever. And when it was done, when the winter broke and the true king stumbled out into the snow weeping like a child, the gate simply... stopped needing to be shut.

There's no lock on it now. There's nothing behind it but a corridor of grey stone that goes farther than it should and ends at a wall of quiet dark. Sometimes I stand in front of it with a lamp, the way you check on a sleeping animal, and the dark doesn't stir. The Wolf is beyond it, starving, sealed behind a working made of a dead line's last magic and a broken king's crown-oath and a scholar's stubborn circle-holding — and the seal doesn't need a keeper. That's what the rite promised, and the rite kept its word.

It doesn't need a keeper. But I check anyway. Habit, or love, or the simple fact that some doors you spend your whole life minding, and the minding outlasts the reason.

My mother's voice hasn't come back since that night. Her real voice, I mean — the goodbye, the one that was finally hers and not the Wolf's bait. I've decided not to grieve that. She said what she needed to say. *You were never the lock, Cyrus. You were the key. Locks stay. Keys get to leave.* And then the gate closed between us, gentle as a book shutting, and I have been leaving ever since.

Most days it works.

* * *

Wren comes on Thursdays. She has never once called it a schedule, because Wren does not call anything anything; she simply appears, usually wet, usually carrying books, usually already talking.

"Your sign's crooked," she announced today, shouldering through the door with a satchel that clinked in a way satchels shouldn't. "Also, your tea is terrible, also, I brought better tea, also—" she dropped the satchel on my counter hard enough to make the charm-rack jump "—I found pages I can't read."

"Can't or won't?"

"There's a difference?"

"With you? Constantly."

She unpacked the tea with the ceremony of a surgeon laying out instruments, then the pages: six of them, ragged-edged, salvaged — she admitted with elaborate casualness — from the flooded sub-archive beneath the snow, the one we'd hidden in during the worst weeks, the one the thaw had finally drained enough for the Archive's new recovery teams to enter. Her teams. The king had offered her a title. Archivist Royal, Keeper of the Reborn Collection, something with rings and a stipend. She'd declined the title and accepted the work, which is the most Wren sentence in existence: refuse the crown, keep the labor.

"These predate the binding," she said, tapping the oldest page. "Predate the first Amberdale. Maybe predate the gate itself. And I cannot read them. Me. I've tried every cipher in the collection, every dead script, every—"

"Wren."

"—every trick I know, and they just sit there being smug at me, and I thought, well, who do I know whose family has been smugly entangled with this gate for a century—"

"Wren."

"What."

"Drink your tea."

She drank her tea. She glared at me over the rim of the cup, which is how Wren rests. Outside, the thaw was doing its loud spring work — meltwater chuckling down the gutters, the river running brown and free again, somebody's laundry strung across the alley like signal flags celebrating a war that was over. A year ago this city was silent under snow, afraid of its own shadow because shadows here moved wrong. Now the loudest danger was the icicles coming off my eaves.

I picked up the oldest page. The paper felt warm, which paper shouldn't, and the writing on it was in a hand I knew.

Not recognized. *Knew.* The way you know your own signature, your own mother's handwriting on a shopping list, the letters you've traced a thousand times in shop ledgers without ever thinking about where the shapes came from.

"It's Amberdale script," I said slowly. "Older than old. This is — Wren, this is the hand from the antechamber memory. The first keeper's hand. I've seen it once, in a preserved memory inside the gate, and I somehow know it like my own name."

Her eyes did the thing they do when scholarship and hunger shake hands. "Can you read it?"

I looked at the page. The characters swam, refused, settled — and then, like a word remembered on the tip of the tongue, the opening line surfaced:

*To whoever comes after: the gate does not need guarding. It needs visiting.*

My hands went cold around warm paper.

"That's not a warning," I heard myself say. "That's an instruction."

Wren set down her cup very carefully, the way she does when she's excited and pretending not to be. "Read me the rest."

* * *

I want to be clear about what I did not do. I did not run to the back wall. I did not light a lamp and stand before the quiet dark with my heart hammering like a boy's. I finished my tea first, because whatever else the last year taught me, it taught me that dread moves faster than you and you should never let it set the pace.

Then I went back, and Wren followed with the pages, and the gate lay open and asleep as it always does, and I read the first keeper's letter aloud in a corridor of grey stone while a disgraced scholar hung on every word like it was scripture, because to her it practically was.

Most of it was housekeeping, absurdly. *Feed the hinge-oak twice a year. Do not let ivy take the north face. If the dark hums, hum back; it is only lonely.* A hundred years of keepers' wisdom, written by a man who had bound a hungry god and then apparently spent his retirement gardening.

But the last page was different. The last page was newer — same hand, older ink, written late, the letters pressed deep like the writer was afraid of being forgotten.

*I lied in the earlier pages,* it said. *The gate does sleep, and the seal holds, and none of it needs you. But I am old, and I have watched the dark a long time, and I will tell you the truth I told no one: the Wolf is not the only thing beyond it. Something else came through with it, long ago, before the binding. Small. Quiet. Coral-pale. It hid in the gate's bones while we fought the Wolf, and we let it stay, because it asked so politely.*

*It is not a hunger. It is a memory. It remembers every person the gate has ever taken, and it keeps them the way amber keeps flies — whole, and shining, and waiting.*

*If you ever hear humming behind the wall, descendant, go in. Go in and listen. Some doors are locked to keep things out. This one was locked to keep something in that has been waiting, patiently, for someone from our line to come and say hello.*

The corridor was very quiet. Somewhere far behind the dark — or maybe in my own chest, I genuinely could not tell — something began to hum.

Low. Coral-pale. Patient as a century.

Wren looked at me. I looked at Wren. And I swear on my mother's key-ring, the first thing either of us said was:

"Don't you dare—"

We said it together. Then we laughed, badly, in the dark, and went to see who was waiting.
