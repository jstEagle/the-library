## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The widow Anselm arrived at the workshop on a morning so mild and golden that Marguerite Nakamura felt briefly, foolishly certain that nothing in the world had ever gone wrong anywhere.

She came up the path with a basket of plums balanced on one hip and her grief folded neatly under the other arm, the way people in the Hollow Valley carried it — politely, like a borrowed shawl. "Master Nakamura," she said. "I've brought payment in advance. The tree's been generous this year, and Tomas always said your carvings deserved better than coin."

"Tomas was a flatterer," Marguerite said, taking the basket. "Best kind of man. Come in, Widow Anselm. Mind the shavings."

His workshop sat at the crook of the Millbrook road, a low stone building with a roof that sagged in the middle like an old dog settling down to sleep. Inside, the smell of bone dust and beeswax hung sweet and constant, and every surface held something half-finished: a comb with wrens caught mid-flight along its spine, a button carved from elk ivory, a small round locket still waiting for its hinge. The Hollow Valley had no smiths worth the name and no jewelers at all, but it had Marguerite, and Marguerite had his hands, and between them the valley's dead were remembered in materials that would outlast the mourners.

Because that was the thing about bone, though he never said it aloud to customers. Bone remembered. Not in the way wood remembered grain or stone remembered pressure — but truly, stubbornly, the way a song remembers being sung. When he carved a piece from the bones of the beloved dead, something lingered in the marrow channels, some last sediment of the person, and as his knife worked through it, he could hear them. A word here. A phrase there. Once, memorably, an entire argument about the correct way to salt soup, delivered with such force that he'd put down his tools and apologized to the widow for eavesdropping.

It was not magic. He'd been careful all his life to think of it as *not* magic, because the Hollow Valley did not permit magic, and whatever lived in his hands had been born here, raised here, and knew the rules. It was closer to listening. The way you might catch the shape of a room through a wall — muffled, incomplete, but unmistakably *there*.

He set Widow Anselm in the good chair by the window and unwrapped the bundle she'd brought: a femur, clean and pale, already boiled and scraped by the undertaker's careful hands. Tomas Anselm's own leg, offered up so that his widow might carry a piece of him against her heart.

"He'd have laughed himself sick about this," she said, watching Marguerite turn the bone in the light. "He hated fuss."

"Then I'll keep the fussing to a minimum." Marguerite settled the bone into his carving cradle and picked up his finest knife. "Tell me about him while I work. It helps the piece come out true."

This was also not entirely true, but it was kind, and kindness in the Hollow Valley was currency enough.

So she talked. She talked about how Tomas had courted her by losing to her at cards on purpose and then being terrible at hiding it; about the forty years of mornings he'd burned the porridge and eaten it anyway rather than admit defeat; about the last week, when his lungs had begun their slow betrayal and he'd spent his hours dictating instructions for the orchard as though the trees might take notes. And beneath her voice, soft as silt settling, Marguerite began to hear the other voice — the one in the bone.

*Tell her the east rows,* it murmured as his knife skimmed the shaft. *She'll forget the east rows.*

"The east orchard rows," Marguerite said gently, without looking up. "He wants you to know they'll need grafting come spring. Scions from the Miller stock."

Widow Anselm went very still. Then she laughed, wet and startled, and pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Forty years," she managed, "and he never once told me what he wanted without making me guess first."

*That's because guessing is half the marriage,* said the voice in the bone, warm and dry as August. *Write that down for her.*

Marguerite smiled and kept carving. This was the work, and he loved it the way a ferryman loves the river — not without fear, but with a deep and daily gratitude. For thirty-one years he had lived in the valley his mother built, and for thirty-one years the dead had whispered to him through his knives, and never once — not once — had anything here frightened him.

The locket took shape under his hands: a small oval case, hinged, lined with felt, the outer face worked into a pattern of plum branches because Marguerite believed even grief deserved a little sweetness. By late afternoon it was finished. He polished it with wax and lambswool until it glowed like a peeled egg, fitted the tiny clasp, and held it up to the window light.

"There," he said. "Tomas Anselm, portable."

And the voice said, clear as a bell rung inside his own skull:

*Don't stay in the valley, boy.*

Marguerite's hands stopped.

The carving was finished. Finished meant silent — that was the whole order of things, the tide and rhythm of his gift. When the knife stopped, the voice faded, always, like footprints washing off sand. Thirty-one years of certainty.

But the voice did not fade. It hung there in the finished, polished, clasp-secured quiet of the locket, patient as a man waiting at a door.

*You hear me. Good. I wasn't sure the border would let me through, even secondhand.* A pause, and when it spoke again there was something underneath the warmth now, something urgent worn smooth to seem calm. *Listen carefully. I don't have long and I don't understand it myself, only that I woke up on the other side of whatever this is, and I can see the valley from out here, boy, and it is the only dark place left.*

"The only—" Marguerite said aloud, and then caught himself, because Widow Anselm was watching him with her red-rimmed eyes, and he turned it into a cough. "Dust," he explained. "Bone dust. Gets everywhere."

He carried the locket to her with steady hands and a smile he'd practiced since childhood, and she paid him in more plums than any locket deserved, and she held the little case to her ear before she left — everyone in the valley did that, a folk habit, listening for a word from their dead — and her face when she lowered it was puzzled, faintly hurt.

"It's quiet," she said. "They're usually so chatty, aren't they? In the pieces."

"Sometimes they rest," Marguerite said. "Grief is tiring work for them too."

What he did not say: the voice hadn't gone quiet. It had simply stopped speaking to her, and turned back to him, and followed him around the workshop all evening like a dog that had decided he was its business now.

*You've never crossed the border, have you,* it said while he banked the fire. *Never once. Your mother saw to that, didn't she. Kept you close. Kept everything close.*

"My mother died thirteen years ago," Marguerite told the empty room, and heard how young and stubborn his own voice sounded.

*Did she,* said Tomas Anselm, who had known her, who had drunk cider with her at harvest fairs, whose opinion on the matter should have been no better informed than anyone else's in the valley — and yet somehow was. *Ask your aunt Serah what year the bells stopped ringing, boy. Ask her why nobody remembers the thirteenth bell.*

"I'm going to bed," Marguerite announced to the dead man, "and you are going to be tired bone-dust by morning, like every other voice I've ever heard."

The voice said nothing more. But it did not leave either. Marguerite lay awake in his loft above the workshop and felt it there, faint and persistent, like a draft under a door — and told himself, with the practiced ease of a man who has spent three decades not thinking about certain things, that it was fatigue. Fatigue, and too much sun, and the natural melancholy of a day spent with a widow's fresh grief.

He almost believed it. He was good at almost.

* * *

He woke past midnight to a sound like rain falling upward.

Marguerite sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and for one merciful moment thought it was weather. Then he understood. The whispering was coming from below — from the workshop — and it was not one voice.

It was all of them.

Every finished piece he had ever carved. Every locket and comb and button and ring sold or unsold, given or buried or kept in drawers — they were all there, all sounding at once, dozens upon dozens of voices layered over each other like a congregation mumbling through a prayer none of them had agreed on. He could pick out old Marta Hale's comb muttering about frost. The Kettering twins' buttons arguing, as they had argued in life, about which of them had been born first. The memorial ring he'd made for the ferryman, repeating one phrase over and over in a tone of rising alarm:

*The moon. The moon. Count the turns of the moon.*

Marguerite came down the stairs slowly, candle in hand, feeling like a man walking into a church where the congregation has all turned to look at him. The workshop glowed amber in the candlelight, utterly ordinary — tools racked, shavings swept, the day's work done. Nothing moved. But the air itself hummed with voices, and as he stood in the doorway, they began, slowly, to fall silent — not fading the way voices fade, but stopping, one by one, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters.

Until only one remained.

Not Tomas Anselm. Older. Rougher. A voice like wind through a dry field, and it came from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls themselves, from the valley outside, from the ground under his feet.

*Thirteen years the border has held,* it said. *Thirteen years the gods have gone unanswered. Do you know what happens to a covenant when it is broken, little carver? It does not die. It waits. And the moon is coming around to its thirteenth turn, and the waiting is nearly done.*

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

*Your mother did not die, Marguerite Nakamura. Your mother is the reason the world has gone quiet. And the quiet is ending.*

Then silence — real silence, total, the silence of a valley that has forgotten how to listen — and Marguerite stood alone in his workshop with his candle guttering, looking at the row of finished lockets on their shelf, every one of them shut tight as a closed eye.

Above the hills to the east, through the little window, the moon hung low and full and just slightly wrong — a hair too large, a shade too pale, like a face trying to remember whose it was.

Thirteen turns, it had been whole. Thirteen turns, the valley had kept the world out.

Marguerite blew out his candle, sat down heavily on his work stool in the dark, and began, very quietly, to count.
