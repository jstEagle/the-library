## 2. Thirteen Bells

The morning the god answered, Marguerite was arguing with a goose.

"You have no claim on that rib," he told her. She had stolen it from his scrap basket — a cow's rib, sanded smooth, destined to become nothing grander than a button — and she stood in the doorway of his workshop with it clamped in her beak like a scepter, radiating the smugness peculiar to geese who have won something.

"Give it back, Honoria."

Honoria did not give it back. Honoria had never once given anything back in six years of residency behind the workshop, and Marguerite had long ago accepted that she was less a goose than a landlord who occasionally honked.

He was still negotiating when the bells began.

Not the valley's bells — those rang on the hour, dull and dependable as bread. These came from the shrine at the top of the hill, and they were ringing wrong: fast, arrhythmic, the way bells ring when someone is ringing them rather than letting them ring. Then, beneath the bronze, there was another sound. Marguerite felt it in his teeth before he heard it with his ears, the way he felt everything that mattered — a low, sweet pressure, like a note held under water.

The whole village stopped. Honoria dropped the rib. Even the wind seemed to pause out of politeness.

Later, everyone would tell the story the same way, because everyone had seen it: little Tam Wren, the cooper's boy, seven years old and missing two front teeth, standing in the shrine square with his hands cupped around a cricket in a matchbox. The cricket had been dying — Tam said so, everyone agreed the box barely rattled — and Tam had done what children do when adults are useless. He had prayed. Not to anyone in particular. Just *please*, aimed at the sky like a thrown stone.

And the sky had caught it.

Vessia came down the way frost comes across a window: gradually, then all at once. Witnesses spoke of a woman made of first-snow light, of a voice like a harp string being drawn through water, of the moment the matchbox began to rattle again — furiously, joyously, a cricket singing its whole heart out. Vessia had knelt in the dust before a seven-year-old, and the dust where she knelt had bloomed with small white flowers that no one could name and no one would ever find growing anywhere else again.

*It is good to be asked,* she had said. *I had begun to think no one remembered how.*

Then she was gone, and the flowers stayed, and Tam Wren walked home holding a live cricket above his head like a lantern, and the Hollow Valley — the Hollow Valley, where magic could not follow, where miracles went to die at the border like moths against glass — had witnessed its first answered prayer in living memory.

Marguerite watched from the edge of the crowd, and the unease that had been living in his chest since the night his workshop whispered grew roots.

* * *

By evening, the road into the valley looked like a river deciding to flood.

Pilgrims. Dozens, then scores, then more than Marguerite could count from his doorstep, streaming down the switchback path with bundles and children and caged hens, all of them fleeing toward the one place in the world that had been built to keep wonders out. He understood the logic of it even as it turned his stomach inside out: if gods were answering prayers again, then the only safe place left was the place where prayers went unheard.

They came seeking sanctuary from miracles. It was the saddest sentence Marguerite had ever thought, and he thought it while handing out barley soup to a family whose farm had been swallowed by a vineyard that sang hymns.

"The grapes won't stop," the mother told him, hollow-eyed, cradling a toddler who kept humming in her sleep — the same three notes, over and over, the notes the vines sang. "We didn't plant them. They just *came*. And when my husband tried to cut them back—" She stopped. She didn't finish. Marguerite carved bones for the dead; he knew all the ways sentences could end without ending.

"Here," he said, ladling her another bowl. "In the valley, your daughter will just be a child who hums."

The woman wept into her soup with gratitude, and Marguerite stood in the doorway of the common house watching the pilgrim fires dot the hillsides like fallen stars, and felt the whispering in his workshop grow louder. Every finished piece — every memorial bone, every keepsake, thirteen years of the dead pressed into ivory and antler — murmuring at once now, not words anymore but insistence, a tide pulling one direction:

*Toward the border. Toward the border. Toward the border.*

"It can wait until morning," he told them, the way you tell a dog to stop barking at weather.

The dead, in his experience, rarely listened. But they had never been this loud, either.

* * *

His aunt Serah found him at midnight, or perhaps he found her; afterward neither could say. She was sitting in his workshop among the whispering bones with a lamp turned low, looking smaller than a woman who had once wrestled a river into a new bed had any right to look.

"They're loud tonight," she said.

"You can hear them?"

"I can hear *you* hearing them." She patted the stool beside her. "Sit down, Margo. We need to talk about your mother."

The nickname landed soft; what followed did not. Marguerite sat. Honoria, sensing drama, wedged herself between them and refused to move.

"Everyone says she died," Marguerite said carefully. "Thirteen years ago. There's a stone in the churchyard. I've carved flowers on it every spring since I was old enough to hold a chisel."

"I know. I'm the one who told you to." Serah turned the lamp wick up, and the light caught the lines of her face the way light catches the grain of wood. "Your mother didn't die, Margo. She *left*. And the stone — the stone was for us, not for her. A grave needs somewhere to put its grief, even when there's no body in it."

The workshop's whispering rose, just slightly, like a held breath.

"She walked out," Serah said. "On the eve of the moon's twelfth turn. She kissed your sleeping head — you were four, you don't remember, don't pretend you do — and she walked to the border, and she did something there that I will never fully understand, though I've had thirteen years to try. When she finished, the valley was sealed. Magic-dead. Miracle-proof. Nothing holy could cross the line she drew." Serah's voice frayed. "And she couldn't come back inside it. Whatever she built, it needed a door that only opened outward."

Marguerite discovered he was gripping the edge of his workbench hard enough to whiten his knuckles. "You let me carve her gravestone. You let me mourn her every year. You let me—"

"Yes." Serah didn't flinch, which was its own kind of cruelty and its own kind of mercy. "Because she asked me to. She said a boy who grows up mourning a mother learns tenderness, and a boy who grows up waiting for one learns nothing but waiting. She chose the story she wanted you to live in. I hated her for it. I love her for it. Both things are true and I've stopped trying to reconcile them."

"Why?" His voice came out strange, flat, a stranger's voice. "Why seal the valley? Why leave? Why—"

"I don't know all of it." Serah held up a hand before he could sharpen the question into a blade. "But I know this much: she wasn't running *from* something, Margo. She was building a cage. Out there, beyond the border, something is locked away, and your mother is the lock, and the valley—" she gestured at the walls, the shelves, the thirteen years of quiet bones, "—the valley isn't a sanctuary. It's the inside of the lock."

The whispering stopped. All at once, completely, the way a choir stops when the conductor drops his hands.

Into that silence, Marguerite heard — clearly, unmistakably, in a voice he had spent thirteen years forgetting on purpose — his mother. Not from any carving. From everywhere. From the direction of the border, thin as smoke through a keyhole:

*Margo. Don't restore what I broke. And whatever you do, don't come looking for me before the moon finishes turning.*

Then nothing. The ordinary night sounds returned: wind, Honoria's disgruntled breathing, the far-off murmur of pilgrims dreaming of a world without gods in it.

Serah was watching him. "You heard her."

"She said not to come looking for her."

"And you've always done exactly what your mother told you?"

Marguerite looked at his hands — the carver's hands, the hands that heard the dead, the hands his mother had kissed on the night she walked out of the world. Thirteen years of graves and grief, and she had been alive on the other side of the line the whole time. The betrayal didn't crack open in him like thunder. It cracked open quietly, the way ice cracks on a pond: one hairline thread, spreading.

"No," he said. "I haven't."

Outside, above the hills, the moon hung swollen and pale, beginning its thirteenth turn. And far away, at the valley's edge, something knocked — softly, twice, like a traveler asking at a door — and then fell silent, waiting to be let in.
