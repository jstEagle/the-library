## 3. What the Border Keeps Out

The knocking came again at dawn, and this time it was not polite.

Marguerite had slept badly — which was to say, he had lain awake in his workshop with a blanket over his head like a boy hiding from thunder, listening to forty finished bones whisper in the dark. They had stopped by morning, all but one: the widow Ansel's memorial, which murmured her husband's name over and over, *Tomas, Tomas*, like a man calling for someone lost in fog.

He had decided, somewhere around the third bell of night, that he would not think about his mother. This resolution lasted until he picked up his carving knife and found his hands shaking, at which point he decided instead that he would think about her while doing something useful, because grief sat better in him when his hands were busy.

So he went out into the grey morning to see who was knocking on the valley's edge.

The Hollow Valley had no gate. That was rather the point of it. The border was not a wall or a fence or even a line you could point to — Serah had once described it as "the place where the air stops holding its breath," which Marguerite had always found unhelpful until he'd stood at it himself and felt the difference, subtle as stepping from shade into sun. Inside, the world was quiet. Outside, the world hummed with everything magic had ever touched.

The path there wound up through birch trees whose leaves never turned gold, because turning was change and change was magic's first cousin. Marguerite walked it with his satchel of tools out of habit, though what use a bone-carver's kit would be against whatever waited at the border, he couldn't say.

The stranger lay half across the line itself, as if he'd fallen mid-stride and hadn't the strength to finish falling.

He was young — younger than Marguerite, certainly, though god-touched folk aged strangely and he might have been older than the birches. His coat had been fine once; now it was scorched down one side, and beneath the scorching his skin glowed faintly, the way coals glow under ash. Not burning. *Lit*. As if something inside him were trying to get out through his ribs.

"Hello," Marguerite said, because his mother had raised him to be polite even to things that might eat him. Especially, she'd said, to those.

The stranger's eyes opened. They were the pale grey of river ice, and they focused on Marguerite with an effort that seemed physical. "You're real," he said. His voice was rough as a file. "You're — I can't hear you humming."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Everyone hums." The stranger laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that shook sparks loose from his collar. "Mortals. You all hum. Little prayers, little fears, little songs you don't know you're singing. It's been driving me mad for thirteen years. But you—" He reached up a glowing hand and let it fall short of Marguerite's boot. "You're silent. Like water. Like sleep."

Marguerite crouched, keeping just inside the border, where the air held its breath. "You're hurt."

"I'm *pursued*," the stranger corrected. "Hurt is incidental." He tried to prop himself up and failed, sliding another hand's width across the line, and where his body crossed, the glow dimmed and he gasped — not in pain, Marguerite noticed, but in relief. "Oh. Oh, that's better. What is this place?"

"A valley," Marguerite said. "Where magic doesn't follow."

"Where magic doesn't—" The stranger stared at him. Then he began, weakly, to laugh again. "Do you know how long I've looked for a place like this? Do you know what I'd give—"

Hooves. Far off, but coming closer, up the eastern road where the land dipped toward the lowlands. The stranger heard them too; Marguerite watched the relief drain out of his face like water from a cracked bowl.

"They found me," he said. "They always find me. I shine too bright."

"Who?"

"Men who believe prayers should go unanswered." The stranger's mouth twisted. "They call themselves the Unbroken. They think the old gods answering prayers is an invasion. And they may be right — I'm hardly a fair judge, being what I am — but their method of diplomacy involves fire and a great deal of shouting about purity." His eyes flicked to Marguerite's face. "You're going to leave me here."

It wasn't quite a question, and it wasn't quite an accusation. It was simply a man stating the arithmetic of strangers: one wounded god-touched fugitive does not outweigh one quiet life.

Marguerite thought about the workshop full of whispering bones. About Serah's face when she'd said *she left, she chose to leave*. About thirteen years of hearing the dead say last words and nothing after, silence stacked on silence like stones on a grave.

"No," he said. "I'm going to carry you."

The stranger blinked. "That's — no, listen, they'll follow you, they'll—"

"Yes," Marguerite said, hauling the man's arm over his shoulders. "Probably. Less talking. More walking."

The moment they crossed fully inside the border, the stranger made a sound Marguerite had only heard from dying men and new mothers: the sound of pain ending. The glow under his skin guttered and sank, banked like a fire in a stove, and his weight went honest and heavy against Marguerite's shoulder. Whatever he was, inside the valley he was only flesh.

Which meant, Marguerite realized with a lurch, that whoever hunted him could not follow. Not without giving up whatever made them dangerous.

He allowed himself one small, petty smile and kept walking.

* * *

The stranger slept for two days in the back room of Marguerite's workshop, on the cot usually reserved for clients too grief-stricken to walk home. Serah brought broth and opinions in equal measure.

"You carried a god-touched man across the border," she said, setting down the bowl hard enough to slosh. "Into the valley. Our valley. The whole point of which is—"

"He was dying on our doorstep."

"Our doorstep has been perfectly safe for thirteen years precisely because we don't open it!"

"And yet," Marguerite said mildly, "something knocked."

Serah pressed her lips together. She had their mother's stubborn jaw and none of her softness — or perhaps she had the softness buried so deep it only came out sideways, as worry. "What did he say he was?"

"He didn't. He said everyone hums and I don't." Marguerite turned his knife over in his hands. "Serah. When I crossed the line carrying him — for a moment I was outside it. Both feet on the far side before I pulled us through."

She went very still. She knew. Of course she knew. Everyone in the valley knew what the border did to the bone-carvers, why the Nakamura family had stayed inside it for three generations, why Marguerite had never once in his life stepped beyond the hills.

"And?" she asked, though her voice said *don't tell me*.

"It was like standing next to a waterfall," Marguerite said. "After a lifetime of hearing streams. Every dead thing within fifty miles, talking at once. Prayers. Echoes. Old gods muttering in their sleep." He looked down at his hands. "And my mother."

Serah set down the ladle.

"She wasn't among the dead," Marguerite said carefully. "That's the thing. The dead speak *backward* — last words, old memories, echoes wearing down. She spoke forward. She said, 'Not yet, little wren. Wait for me at the ruin.' Like she was talking to me right then. Like she knew I'd be standing there."

"Little wren." Serah's voice came out strange. "She called you that when you were small. Before your gift came in. Before—" She stopped herself, and Marguerite watched her decide, visibly, how much truth to spend. "There are ruins past the western ridge. Where the old shrine stood, before the valley was founded. If she left a message there, it's because she wants you to come out. And if she wants you to come out—"

"Then it's a trap, or a confession, or both."

"Or both," Serah agreed grimly. "Your mother never did anything by halves. Except leaving. She managed to do that completely."

* * *

On the third evening, the stranger woke properly, ate two bowls of stew, and introduced himself as Ilex, "formerly of everywhere, currently of your cot."

"You're a god," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question. He'd spent three days listening to the man sleep, and sleeping men can't guard their voices — Ilex muttered in a language that predated the valley, that predated perhaps language, and the bones on Marguerite's shelves had leaned toward it in their stands like flowers toward sun.

Ilex considered the stew. "I'm what's left when a god is demoted. Demoted, not deposed — there's a difference. Deposed means someone took your throne. Demoted means the throne itself was broken, and you fell with it." He glanced up, and for a moment the coal-glow showed behind his eyes. "Thirteen years ago, something shattered the covenant between gods and mortals. Every minor god went tumbling. Some landed soft. Some landed hungry."

"And you?"

"I landed curious." He smiled, and it was a good smile, tired around the edges. "I've spent thirteen years walking the lowlands watching what happens when prayers start getting answered again. Mostly I watch small gods discover appetite. A rain-goddess who floods a village because they thanked her *insufficiently*. A hearth-spirit who won't let a fire die, even when the house around it has burned. Power without terms, Marguerite. That's what a broken covenant is. Not freedom. Hunger with no table manners."

"The men chasing you. The Unbroken."

"Zealots. Led by a prelate named Ossian, who is either the sanest man alive or the most frightened, and those are often the same thing." Ilex set down his spoon. "They want the covenant to stay broken forever. No answered prayers, no miracles, mortals governing themselves even if they govern themselves badly. There's a certain honor in it. And a certain blindness." He studied Marguerite with those river-ice eyes. "You've heard them, haven't you? Since the moon started turning. The dead, the gods, all of it bleeding through."

"Every finished piece in my shop whispered at once," Marguerite said. "Two nights ago."

Ilex nodded slowly. "Then you already know what I came here to tell you, or something like it. The covenant isn't just broken. It's *scattered*. Shattered into fragments, and the fragments are still singing — counter-melodies, holding the old terms together by a thread. Find enough of them, and the covenant could be reforged. Whole. Or rewritten." He leaned forward. "Or left broken, and the world gets to find out what unbound gods do when they're starving."

Outside, the swollen moon cleared the birch ridge, one night brighter than before.

"And my mother?" Marguerite asked quietly. "Where does she fit in your theory?"

Ilex was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone gentle, the way one speaks near a wound. "I think, Marguerite Nakamura, that your mother didn't break the covenant despite loving a god. I think she broke it *because* of it. And I think the reason the border of this valley holds so strong — stronger than any working I've felt in a thousand years — is that it isn't powered by magic at all."

"What else is there?"

"Sealing," Ilex said. "Something is trapped at the heart of what she built. Something she wanted kept out. Or kept in." He rose, unsteady, and stood at the workshop window looking west, toward the ridge, toward the ruins Serah had named. "The moon completes its thirteenth turn in nine days. Whatever she sealed, whatever she broke, it ends then. And I think she's been waiting for you at that ruin since the day she walked out of the world."

Marguerite looked at his hands. Carver's hands. Seal's hands, maybe. The hairline crack in him spread another inch, quiet as ice on a pond.

"Then I suppose," he said, "I'd better pack my tools."
