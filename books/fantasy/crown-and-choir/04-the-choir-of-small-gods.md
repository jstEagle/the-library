## 4. The Choir of Small Gods

Ilex had forgotten how loud the world was.

Not the world of men — he'd spent thirteen years among them, and men were mostly quiet, mostly tired, mostly muttering about the price of flour. He meant the other choir. The one that had been silent for a generation and had now, apparently, woken up starving and drunk on its own voice.

Three days past the valley's border, and he could hear it everywhere. In the rustle of wheat that wanted thanks for growing. In the well-water that murmured *remember me* when you drew a bucket. In the wind off the hills, which used to be just wind and now had opinions.

"Your gods are chatty," Marguerite said.

Ilex glanced back at him. The bone-carver walked with his satchel hugged to his chest like a sleeping child, and every few hundred paces his face went tight, the way it did when something spoke through the relics he carried. "You can hear them?"

"I can hear *something*. The bell fragment won't stop humming. Neither will this." He pulled out the little carved charm — a sparrow, half-finished — that Ilex had watched him whittle by firelight. "The sparrow is asking me whether I believe in it. Which is a strange question from a thing I made myself."

"That's because you didn't make it alone," Ilex said, before he could stop himself.

Marguerite stopped walking. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning nothing. Meaning keep walking." *Meaning that once, a very long time ago, I sat in a circle of my peers and breathed intention into small things, and small things remember who breathed on them.* But saying that aloud would require explanations he wasn't ready to give, so he pointed instead at the smoke rising over the next ridge. "Village ahead. We need food, and you need to stop talking to your carvings in public."

The village was called Bracken Hollow, though Ilex learned quickly that this was now a contested fact.

* * *

They found the shrine in the village square where the well used to be. The well was still there, technically, but a roof had been built over it, and the roof was hung with ribbons, and the ribbons were hung with little wooden plaques, each carved with a name.

An old woman sat beside it, shelling peas into a bowl, watching them with the flat assessment of someone who had decided not to be impressed by anything ever again.

"Travelers," she said. It wasn't a greeting.

"Pilgrims," Ilex corrected smoothly. "We've come about the singing."

"Hmph." She snapped a pod. "Everyone comes about the singing now. Used to be people came about water. You want water, you put a name on the roof and you thank her properly. Her name's Maren. She was the midwife here sixty years, saved my mother's life, died quiet in her bed. Now she's the Well-Mother and if you forget to thank her, your bucket comes up full of eels."

"Eels," Marguerite repeated.

"Flooded fields, three miles of river nearby. It's not hard for her." The old woman shrugged. "It's not even cruel, that's the thing. She's not mean. She's just—" she searched for the word, snapping another pod, "—*hungry*. For being remembered. My grandmother said the gods used to be like weather. Distant. Now they're like neighbors. Neighbors who stand in your doorway until you say something nice about their garden."

Ilex felt something twist behind his ribs. He knew exactly what hunger that was. He had felt it himself, once, dim and distant, in the years after his demotion — the ache of fading, of being forgotten, of pouring yourself out for mortals who no longer poured anything back. He had been one of the lucky ones. Fading gently, in a valley that couldn't hear him, among people who loved him anyway without knowing why they felt safe.

Maren the midwife had not faded gently. Maren had woken up into a world where prayers were answered again, and power had pooled in her like rain in a dry creek bed, and she had discovered she could make eels.

"What happens," Marguerite asked carefully, "if you don't put a name on the roof?"

"Nothing. That's the worst part." The old woman looked up at him, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "She doesn't smite us. She doesn't curse us. She just gets *quieter*, and sadder, and then the whole village feels it — this heaviness, like a debt nobody remembers taking out. Last winter the Hedley boy refused to thank her on principle. Said thanking a dead woman for water was superstition." She paused. "He drowned in two inches of ditch water in spring. Accident, probably. Probably."

"And the other villages?" Ilex asked.

"Worse, some. The mill god at Corren's Ford takes a tithe of flour or the grain rots. There's a hedge-saint down south who'll give you visions if you fast at her shrine, and if you don't fast, she gives them to you anyway, at night, whether you want them or not." She dumped a handful of shelled peas into a cloth bag and held it out. "Take these. You look hungry. And take some advice: whatever you're really doing here — and nobody walks toward the singing, only away from it — do it quick. The big ones are waking too. The ones with real names. And small gods gone greedy are bad enough. I don't want to find out what big ones are."

They thanked her. Marguerite hesitated a moment — Ilex saw him almost say something about the widow's memorial bone, about hearing the actual Maren, about how the woman under the well might prefer a kind word freely given to a ribbon nailed to a roof — but he swallowed it and just nodded. Wise. Mostly.

That night, camped in an orchard at the edge of Bracken Hollow, Ilex lay awake listening to the countryside sing.

It wasn't a metaphor. That was the horror of it. The minor gods had always sung — faintly, harmonically, a bass note under the world that kept soil rich and rivers running and hearts beating in the dark. He'd helped write parts of that song. But a chorus needs a score. A chorus needs *agreement*. And the covenant had been the agreement, and the covenant was in pieces, and now every small power was improvising.

The wheat sang for gratitude. The well sang for Maren. A fox-thing in the hills sang for fear, high and thin, and somewhere a mile off a stone-circle god sang for blood — old-fashioned, that one, embarrassed by its own appetite but unwilling to stop. And underneath all of it, threading through like a silver wire, something else sang back. A counter-melody. Steady. Patient. Coming from the direction of the town they'd reach tomorrow: Saint Aldric's Crossing, where there was a church with a bell.

Marguerite sat up across the embers. "You hear it too."

"The counter-song? Yes."

"It's coming out of my satchel. Out of everything. It's—" He fumbled, drew out the sparrow carving, held it to his ear like a seashell. His face did something complicated. "It's a name. Someone's name, set to music. It sounds like... it sounds like a contract being read aloud. Terms and conditions, but beautiful."

"That's because it is one." Ilex fed a stick to the fire. Here it was, then. The moment where he either lied again or didn't. "Fragments of the covenant survived the breaking. They hid themselves in things that carry sound. Bells, mostly. Church bells, temple gongs, a few singing wells — things mortals made to praise, back when praise was a shared language. The fragments sing the covenant's original terms. Where the chorus is chaos, they're..." He searched. "Grammar."

"Grammar," Marguerite said flatly.

"The world runs on grammar, carver. Every miracle, every prayer, every god's hunger — it's all sentences. The covenant was the rules of the sentence. Break it, and everyone starts shouting at once." He met Marguerite's eyes across the fire. "Tomorrow we get to Saint Aldric's before dark. If the fragment there is intact enough, it may be able to teach us the rest. Where the other pieces fell. Maybe even who broke it, and why."

"You know who broke it."

Ilex put another stick on the fire and watched it catch. "I know what everyone beyond the valley knows. Thirteen years ago the covenant shattered. Thirteen years ago the founder of the Hollow Valley disappeared. Draw your own conclusions — you clearly already have."

Marguerite was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was careful, the way a man's voice is careful when he's carrying something breakable. "My aunt says my mother left. Not died. Left. And that the valley's silence — the reason no magic can follow anyone inside — was her doing. Her last work." He turned the sparrow over in his fingers. "If she broke the covenant to build that wall..."

"Then every drunk god in this countryside is standing on the rubble of something she knocked down," Ilex said. Gently, because it needed saying and gentleness was all he could offer. "Or guarding something. Or both. The terms will tell us which. That's what terms are *for*."

Above them, the sky was cloudless, and the moon hung fat and pale, one turn short of complete. As Ilex watched, a cloud that hadn't existed drifted across it anyway — thin, deliberate, shaped almost like a hand. The wheat-field god, flexing. Showing off. Asking the moon itself for acknowledgment.

*Small powers,* Ilex thought, with an ache he hadn't permitted himself in years. *Oh, what worship withheld does to the small.*

* * *

Saint Aldric's Crossing earned its name from a bridge, and the bridge was why the church still stood — pilgrims crossing east to west had always paid a coin to ring the bell for safe passage, and coins kept roofs mended. The bell tower rose gray and patient above the rooftops, and as they came down the lane toward it, Ilex heard the counter-melody swell from a thread into a voice.

So, apparently, did everything else.

The fields around the town had gone strange. Wheat leaned *toward* the tower like grass bending in wind that wasn't blowing. In a pasture, a herd of cattle stood in a perfect ring, heads bowed — not grazing, just facing the bell, the way men face a king. A crow perched on a fence post opened its beak and produced, instead of a caw, three clear notes of the counter-melody, then looked embarrassed about it.

"They're listening," Marguerite whispered. "All of them. Even the animals. Even the—" He stopped. His hand had gone to the satchel. "Even my carvings. They're all turned toward the tower. Ilex. Everything I'm carrying is *facing* it."

"The fragments call to each other. Sound calls to sound."

"No." Marguerite's voice dropped lower. "No, it's more than calling. The sparrow — it's trying to *sing along*. All of them are. Like they're remembering words they never learned." He looked up at Ilex, and there was wonder in his face, and under the wonder, dread arriving right on schedule. "What does power do to the small, Ilex? What's it doing to *my work*?"

Ilex didn't have a good answer. He had a true one, and true answers were heavier. *Power doesn't change what a thing wants,* he thought. *It just removes every reason not to want it out loud.* But before he could decide whether to say it, the town solved the problem for him.

The bell rang.

Once — deep, bronze, and underneath the ordinary clang of metal, unmistakable, a word. Not a word in any living language, but a *term*. A clause. Ilex felt it go through him like cold water through cloth, and around the square he saw farmers stop mid-stride, children turn from their games, a dog sit down abruptly and tilt its head. The whole town listened without knowing why.

Then the bell rang a second time — and cut off halfway through, strangled, as though a great wet cloth had been thrown over it. The silence afterward had weight. Shape. And moving up the street toward the church, unhurried, certain, came a column of figures in gray-and-white vestments, led by a tall man whose shadow fell the wrong way in the afternoon light.

At the column's head, borne on a pole like a banner, was a ledger bound in black leather, its pages fluttering open in a wind that touched nothing else.

Marguerite went very still beside him. "Those are zealots," he said. "The ones who were chasing you."

"Yes," Ilex said. "And they're going to hear exactly what we came to hear. The only question is whether they let us live long enough to understand it."

The tall man stopped at the foot of the bell tower, looked directly across the square at the two travelers by the fence — looked, Ilex was quite sure, straight at *him*, at the faded divine ember still guttering in his chest — and smiled.

"Ring it again," the man said to his followers. "Let's see who comes to answer."
