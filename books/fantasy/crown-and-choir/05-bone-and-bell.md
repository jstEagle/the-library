## 5. Bone and Bell

They came at dawn, when the mist still clung to the hedgerows like a second skin, and they came singing.

That was the worst part, Marguerite thought later. Not the arrows thudding into the cart's side, not Ilex hauling him off the road and into a drainage ditch that smelled of old cabbage — but the singing. Forty voices, maybe fifty, raised in a hymn so flat and joyless it could have curdled milk. The god-chorus that had plagued them since the valley was a wild, drunken improvisation by comparison. This was worship as military drill.

"Down," Ilex hissed, pressing Marguerite's face into the mud. "Down, down — they're Ossian's. Prelate Ossian's. The Unbroken."

"The Unbroken what?"

"The Unbroken. It's the name. He thinks it's very clever."

Arrows whispered overhead. Somewhere ahead, their poor cart horse — a placid creature named Duchess who had tolerated three days of god-haunted countryside with more grace than either of them — screamed and bolted, cart and all, into the fog.

"Well," Marguerite said into the mud. "There goes the cheese."

"Marguerite."

"I'm just noting it for the record."

Hands found them before the fog lifted. Rough hands, many hands, and then Marguerite was hauled upright into a circle of grey-robed figures with ash rubbed into their brows and iron chains wrapped around their staves — iron, he noted with a professional interest, cold-forged, unadorned, the kind of iron that remembered nothing and told no stories. The dead could not ride in it. He had carved a rosary from such iron-bone once, for a woman who wanted her husband's voice gone rather than kept. She had wept the whole time he worked.

Their leader sat a grey mare at the circle's edge, and he was not at all what Marguerite had expected. He was small. That was the first shock. Prelate Ossian, terror of the god-touched countryside, burner of shrines, was a compact, silver-templed man with laugh lines around his eyes and the air of someone who had once been very funny and had decided, on principle, to stop.

"Bone-carver," Ossian said, in a voice like a warm hearth. "And a god. Or nearly. You've been shedding divinity across three parishes, friend; my people can smell it like rain coming." His gaze moved to Ilex, who had gone very still. "Hello, little brother."

"I'm not your brother," Ilex said.

"You were. Before the demotion. Before any of it." Ossian dismounted with the ease of a man who had done ten thousand times. "You're the one who used to tend the orchard gods. Vesper, wasn't it? The little god of first frost. You'd walk the rows and touch each tree."

Marguerite looked at Ilex. Ilex looked at the ground.

"First frost," Marguerite said. "You never told me that."

"It didn't come up."

"It's a lovely name."

"It's a *dead* name," Ilex snapped, and the grey-robed men flinched, and Marguerite understood that even diminished, even mortal-adjacent, there was still something in Ilex that made the air pay attention.

Ossian, to his credit, only smiled. "Bring them," he said. "Gently. We're not savages."

* * *

The Unbroken kept their camp in the bones of an old amphitheater, and Marguerite had to admire the choice. Stone seats rising in a half-circle, moss-eaten, acoustically perfect — and dead as a doornail. Not one ghost, not one whisper, not one god. Whatever the zealots had done to this place, they had scrubbed it clean of every voice but the living.

For the first time in weeks, Marguerite's head was quiet.

He hated how much he enjoyed it.

They were given a tent, a meal of barley stew, and a guard of four men who kept iron chains coiled at their feet like sleeping snakes. Ossian came at dusk, alone, carrying two cups of something that smelled like cider and a ledger bound in grey leather.

"I knew your mother," he said, without preamble, and handed Marguerite a cup.

Marguerite did not drink it. "Everyone knew my mother. She founded a valley. People wrote songs."

"I knew her before the valley." Ossian sat on the tent's low bench, unhurried, and for a moment the laugh lines did their old work and he looked, briefly, like a man Marguerite might have liked. "We were young together, she and I. She was going to be a great theologian. I was going to be a great heretic. We compromised: she became both." He sipped. "Do you know what the covenant was, bone-carver? Truly?"

"I know it's broken. I know the gods have gone mad without it. I know my mother had something to do with it."

"She had *everything* to do with it. And she was right." Ossian set down his cup. "You've walked out there. You've seen what the small gods have become — drunk, grasping, demanding worship through fear. You think that's the covenant failing? No. That's what gods *are*, Marguerite. That's what they were before the covenant, and what they'll be after. The covenant was never a blessing. It was a leash we put on our own necks and called it piety. It traded every mortal's memory, every mortal's choice, for the gods' good behavior. Your mother saw it. She paid for it. Thirteen years of silence, thirteen years of a world where a widow's grief is her own and a child's prayer goes unanswered and *no one kneels*."

He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle.

"Come with us. You have her gift — or the seed of it. You could be the one who keeps the world free. Not a seal. Not an instrument. An author."

Marguerite's hands were shaking. He made himself look at the ledger on Ossian's knee. Grey leather, worn soft at the corners, and — his heart stuttered — a bone clasp, carved with a pattern he knew better than his own name. His mother's knotwork. Her hand had held that clasp. Her knife had cut it.

"You said she paid for it," Marguerite said. "Paid how?"

Ossian's smile flickered — just at the corner, just for a breath.

"That," he said, "is not my story to tell."

"Then whose is it?"

"Ask her." He stood, tucked the ledger under his arm, and paused at the tent's mouth. "Think on my offer, bone-carver. The world out there is loud and getting louder. The moon is nearly full. When it turns, everything changes — for everyone, one way or another. I'd rather it changed with your mother's son standing beside me than against me."

He left. The guards stayed. The stew went cold.

* * *

Marguerite did not sleep. He sat in the dark of the tent and listened to the silence — the true, scoured, iron-clean silence — and found it was not peaceful at all. It was the silence of a room with the furniture taken out. It was the silence of something *removed*.

Ilex slept, or pretended to. Marguerite watched the moon through the tent's gap and thought about his mother's knotwork on a heretic's ledger.

Near midnight, the guard changed.

Marguerite had been watching the new man for an hour before he understood what he was seeing: the fellow was young, maybe nineteen, with a chapped neck and the fidgeting hands of someone who had signed on for righteousness and received boredom. And around his wrist, half-hidden under his sleeve — a cord. And on the cord, a little carved charm.

Bone. Cheap bone, badly carved, but bone.

Marguerite closed his eyes and reached, gently, the way he'd reach for a whisper in a finished piece.

The charm had no voice — dead bone, iron-camp, nothing to hear — but it didn't need one. It needed only to be *seen*. Marguerite let his own hands tremble, let his breath catch, and murmured, just loudly enough: "That carving. Who made it? My sister makes things like that."

The guard's fidgeting stopped. "My mum," he said, wary. "Before the border. She said the valley carvers were the only ones worth the wood."

"Valley carvers," Marguerite said, "are worth more than wood." And he told the boy — softly, in the dark, the way you tell a homesick child a story — about his mother's valley. About bone that remembered. About how a good carving didn't trap the dead, it *kept* them, the way you keep a letter, the way you keep a song. He watched the boy's iron-hard certainty soften the way wax softens, not melting, just loosening at the edge.

"I'm not supposed to talk to prisoners," the boy said, near the end, but he said it the way people say things they've already stopped meaning.

"No," Marguerite agreed. "You're not."

* * *

They went out through the amphitheater's old vomitoria — Ilex's word, and Marguerite had laughed until the guard threatened them both — under a moon three nights from full. Ossian's tent stood at the camp's heart, and it was there, in the last mad minute of the plan, that Marguerite did the thing he would think about for the rest of his life.

He went in.

Not for the ledger — that was Ilex's idea, Ilex the tactician, Ilex who could smell divinity and presumably paperwork. But Marguerite saw the ledger on the camp table, grey leather and bone clasp, his mother's knotwork under the lamplight, and his hands moved before his conscience caught up.

The clasp was his mother's work. And his mother's work answered to his mother's blood.

It opened under his thumb like a hand unclenching.

He had time for one look — one line, one entry, in a cramped familiar script that made his eyes burn — before Ilex hissed his name from the dark like a curse and a blessing both. Marguerite tucked the ledger into his shirt, against his skin, where it lay cold as a stolen relic, and ran.

They ran through the fog and the sleeping camp and out onto the moonlit road, and no one sang after them, and no arrows came, and Marguerite did not decide until the camp was a mile behind them that he was shaking.

"You went into his *tent*," Ilex said, when they finally stopped to breathe.

"He had my mother's handwriting."

"You could have woken the whole— what line? What did you see?"

Marguerite stood in the road with the ledger cold against his chest. Behind them, the Unbroken camp. Ahead, the ruin where the covenant had died, three days' walk, two if the moon hurried them.

"One line," he said. "*The seal must be living, and it must be loved.*"

Ilex went very still. Somewhere in the dark fields, a drunk little god was singing to its scarecrows.

"Marguerite," Ilex said carefully, "what did your mother seal?"

Marguerite looked up at the moon, nearly full, nearly turning, and felt for the first time in his life like an instrument that had finally been told what it was built to play.

"I think," he said, "she sealed me."
