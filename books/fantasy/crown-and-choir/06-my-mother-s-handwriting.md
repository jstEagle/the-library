## 6. My Mother's Handwriting

The stolen ledger weighed less than a hymnal, and Marguerite resented it for that.

He had expected something heavier from the man who wanted to keep the world broken — vellum thick as hide, iron clasps, maybe a lock that screamed when opened. Instead Prelate Ossian's ledger was a plain thing of gray boards and stitched spine, smelling of tallow and road dust, the kind of book a merchant might use to track grain. It had ridden against Marguerite's ribs all night as they fled the zealot camp, Ilex limping beside him through the pinewhine dark, and every jolt of his horse had pressed it into him like a second heartbeat.

Now they were holed up in an abandoned waystation at the crossroads of two dead roads, and Marguerite sat with the ledger on his knees while Ilex boiled pine needles in a dented kettle and pretended not to watch him.

"You could eat first," Ilex offered.

"I've been carrying this thing against my heart for eight hours. If I don't open it soon it'll start making decisions for me."

"That's not how books work."

"You haven't met my family's books." Marguerite untied the cord. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He'd spent his whole life handling other people's grief — widows' bones, orphans' keepsakes — and he knew the trick of it: you went in gently, like entering a sickroom. But this wasn't a widow's grief. This was his mother's handwriting, and there was no gentleness in the world equal to that task.

The first page stopped his breath.

Not because of what it said. Because of the hand itself. He knew that slanting script, that little hook she put on her *d*s like the letters were about to walk away. He'd seen it on recipe cards, on the margin notes of his childhood primers, on one birthday letter he still kept folded in a box under his bed. *For my magpie,* it had said. *Who collects everything.*

Here, the same hand wrote:

*Day 1 of the Sundering. The covenant is cut. Let no one say it was carelessness.*

Marguerite read it three times. The words didn't change. Outside, the wind moved through the pines with a sound like distant applause, and somewhere far off — too far off to be real, surely — a bell fragment sang its counter-melody from the bottom of his pack.

"Marguerite." Ilex set a cup of pine tea by his elbow. "Whatever it says—"

"It says she did it on purpose." His voice came out flat as slate. "Ossian thinks she broke it to free us. Free mortals from the gods' thumbs. That was his whole sermon — *the covenant was chains dressed as promises.*" He turned the ledger so Ilex could see. "*Let no one say it was carelessness.* She wrote it like a mason signing her work."

Ilex sat down slowly across from him. In the firelight, the demoted god's face looked older than it had any right to — tired in a way that mortality was only beginning to teach him. "Then why break it?"

"I'm reading to find out."

* * *

He read through the night, and the night read back.

The ledger was part journal, part confession, part engineering report, if an engineer's material were divinity instead of stone. His mother — Serah had called her Wren, and the ledger confirmed it, *Wren Nakamura, bone-carver, fourth of that name* — had spent years before the Sundering studying the covenant the way a physician studies a body. She'd mapped its terms. There were pages of them, columns of careful script:

*Clause III: The gods shall hear prayer, and answer according to measure.*
*Clause VII: No god shall take memory as payment.*
*Clause XI: What dies shall pass wholly across; nothing shall linger half-remembered.*
*Clause XIV: The gods shall forget what mortals forgive.*

And in the margins, in a tighter, angrier hand that emerged over the years like a bruise surfacing: her objections. Her questions. Her fear.

Because here was the thing the ledger made undeniable, page after page: the covenant had been failing long before she broke it. Clause VII had been violated — quietly, repeatedly, for decades. Small gods, hungry gods, taking memories as tolls. Villages that forgot their own names. A whole valley in the north that woke one morning having forgotten its dead so thoroughly that the graves stood nameless, and the gods' chorus sang a little louder for it.

"The Choir," Marguerite whispered. "The small gods we heard. They're not drunk on new power. They're starving. They've been eating memories for years, and breaking the covenant just—" He stopped. Rethought. "No. Wait."

He flipped back. Read again. And felt the floor of his understanding tilt.

Breaking the covenant hadn't fed the small gods. Breaking the covenant had *cut them off.* The Sundering hadn't been a door flung open — it had been a trap sprung shut. When his mother shattered the old terms, every clause collapsed at once, and anything bound inside the covenant's architecture was bound inside the collapse.

Something was in there. Something she had wanted caught.

He found the entry eleven days before the Sundering:

*I have found where it hides. It wears the covenant like a coat. It has worn it for centuries, slipping between clauses, feeding along the seams — never enough to be noticed, always enough to stay alive. The others call it nothing. The others are wrong. I have heard it. Gods help me, I have heard it singing in the spaces between their voices, and it is so hungry, and it is so lonely, and I love it.*

Marguerite set the ledger down.

His hands were no longer steady.

"Ilex," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "My mother loved a god."

Ilex went very still. It was, Marguerite would think later, the stillness of a man hearing his own name spoken in a room he thought empty.

"Not worshipped," Marguerite went on, because now the words were coming and would not stop. "Loved. The way you love something that sings to you in the dark. She found it hiding in the covenant — some starving thing living in the gaps between clauses — and everyone else wanted it cast out or destroyed, and she—" He laughed, and hated the sound. "She broke the whole covenant around it. Built a prison out of the wreckage. Sealed it in the Hollow Valley, where magic can't follow, where it couldn't feed, couldn't sing, couldn't do anything but *wait.*"

"The valley," Ilex said quietly. "The border isn't keeping the gods out."

"It's keeping one god *in.* Or it was. Thirteen years, and the seal is thinning, and Vessia answered a child's prayer because the walls are coming down, and my mother—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Where is my mother, Ilex? Serah said she left. Ossian said she died. The ledger says—"

He turned to the last written page. Dated the day of the Sundering. The hand was shakier now, the hooks on the *d*s drooping like tired birds.

*It is done. The prison holds. But a prison of clauses needs a clause-keeper, and I cannot stay — if I remain within the seal, it will know me, and it will plead, and I am not strong enough to refuse it twice. So I have made the seal self-renewing. It will hold without me.*

*It will hold through blood. Through gift. Through inheritance.*

*I am sorry. I am so sorry. I used the only material strong enough: a soul not yet born, woven into the covenant's new root before it could ever choose otherwise. My child will carry the hearing. Every relic he carves will be a thread of the seal, every voice he hears a knot tied tighter. He will spend his life binding the prisoner he will never know he guards.*

*When the moon completes its thirteenth turn, the seal will need renewal. By then he will be strong enough. By then, perhaps, he will understand that I gave him a purpose greater than any life he might have chosen.*

*Forgive me, magpie. You were always going to collect this.*

The page ended there. Below it, blankness.

Marguerite did not move for a long time. The fire ticked. Ilex did not touch him, which was a mercy; Marguerite was not sure he wouldn't have come apart like wet paper.

Every finished piece in his workshop, whispering at once. The dead husband begging him to leave the valley. The voices bleeding louder past the border. Thirteen years of widows and mourners bringing him their bones, thirteen years of knots tied tighter around a prisoner he'd never seen, and he had thought it was a *gift.* He had built his whole gentle life on it — the comfort he gave, the last words he delivered, the small warm fame of the valley's bone-carver who hears the dead — and it had all been mortar. All of it. Every kindness he'd ever done had been a brick in his mother's wall.

"She used me," he said at last. It came out almost conversational, which frightened him more than screaming would have. "Before I was born. Before I could—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "There's a word for what she did. I keep reaching for it and finding only words like *sacrifice* and *purpose,* because she wrote them there for me, right there in her own hand, so I'd reach for those first."

Ilex crouched beside him. Close now, Marguerite could see the faint fading shimmer beneath the man's skin, the last residue of what he'd been. "May I?" Ilex asked, and took the ledger gently from his hands. He read the final page in silence. Then, softly: "This isn't the whole truth."

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Because I knew her." Ilex's voice was very quiet. "Wren Nakamura. Fourth of that name. She came to the choir once, years before you were born, asking after a voice she'd heard in the seams. We told her it was nothing. She knew we were lying — she could hear the lie the way you hear lies in relics — but she thanked us anyway, and she left, and she did not break the covenant for another six years." He closed the ledger. "A person who acts rashly does not wait six years. A person who acts cruelly does not write *forgive me* and mean it. Whatever your mother built, Marguerite, she built it weeping."

"Weeping doesn't unmake the wall."

"No," Ilex agreed. "But it suggests the architect had reasons the ledger doesn't record. Reasons she may have carried somewhere else." His eyes went, deliberately, to the cord around Marguerite's neck.

Marguerite's hand rose before he decided to raise it.

The keepsake hung there — it had hung there his whole life, a small carved disc of antler, worn smooth by his mother's thumb before it was ever worn smooth by his. She'd made it for him before she left. *So you remember me,* she'd said, though even as a child he'd wondered how he could forget a mother who was already gone. He'd slept with it clutched in his fist through every fever, every nightmare, every night the workshop whispers grew too loud. He had never once heard a voice in it. Bone-carvers know their own craft; he'd assumed it was simply bone, silent bone, the one piece of carving in his life that held nothing.

But he had never crossed the border wearing it before.

And since the night at the valley's edge — since his hearing had sharpened into agony and he'd heard his mother's voice among the dead, a voice he'd told himself was memory, was wishfulness, was fatigue — the disc had grown warm against his sternum. Warm the way a held breath is warm.

"I've been afraid to listen to it," he admitted. "Since the edge. Since I heard her."

"I'd be afraid too," Ilex said. "But Marguerite — the ledger tells you what she did. If there's anywhere she might have hidden *why*—" He spread his hands. "You're the bone-carver. Not me."

Marguerite lifted the cord over his head.

The disc sat in his palm, small as a coin, light as a lie. Thirteen years of warmth. He turned it over, and for the first time in his life he looked at it not as a son but as a craftsman — and saw what a craftsman would see. The grain of the antler didn't run the way antler grain runs. It spiraled. Inward. Toward the center. Toward a point no wider than a needle's eye, where the surface was polished down to translucence, and behind the translucence, if the firelight cooperated, if you squinted, if you already believed —

— something moved.

"Oh, Mother," he breathed. "You hid yourself in the one relic you knew I'd never dare read."

He set the disc on the ledger's open page, between the lines of her confession, and laid his fingertips against it the way he'd laid them against ten thousand bones belonging to ten thousand strangers. Gently. Like entering a sickroom.

The valley's workshop had taught him to expect echoes, fragments, last words trailing off mid-sentence. What came through the keepsake was none of those things. It was clear. It was present. It was *her* — tired, wry, and terribly awake, as though she had been sitting in the antler all along with her chin in her hands, waiting for him to finally pick it up.

*Hello, magpie,* said the voice of Wren Nakamura. *Took you long enough. Now — before you decide anything, before you hate me properly, you need to hear the rest. The ledger shows you the lock. Only I can show you the door. And you must understand both, because the thing I imprisoned —*

The voice faltered. For the first time, it sounded afraid.

*— the thing I imprisoned has begun to speak to you in your sleep. And it knows your name.*
