## 7. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

Ilex had been a god for four hundred years, and in all that time he had never once learned to sleep well. Mortal bodies, he was discovering, had opinions about this. They wanted straw mattresses and warm milk and to be told, apparently, that everything was going to be fine.

"You're doing the thing again," Marguerite said without opening his eyes. They were camped in the lee of a fallen wall, three days' walk from where the zealots had lost them, and Marguerite could sense restlessness the way he sensed everything else — through the bones of the world, faintly, like music through a floor.

"I'm not doing a thing."

"You're hovering. Gods hover. It's very annoying when you're trying to sleep."

"I am not—" Ilex stopped. Looked down at himself. He was, in fairness, floating approximately four inches off the ground, the way he did when his thoughts outran his feet. He settled back onto the dirt with as much dignity as he could gather. "The moon turned tonight," he said instead. "Did you feel it?"

Marguerite sat up. In the dark his face was all shadow and cheekbone, but Ilex had learned to read him anyway — the small stillness that meant *yes, and it frightened me.*

"The thirteenth turn," Marguerite said. "Final one." He touched the carved keepsake at his throat — his mother's voice sleeping in bone — the way other men touched a wound they'd decided not to look at. "How long?"

"Nine days. Perhaps ten. After that..." Ilex spread his hands. He had once held thunderstorms in those hands. Now they just looked like hands. "After that, whatever the unbound gods have become will be finished becoming it."

They walked at dawn. And by midday, Ilex smelled the forgetting before they saw it.

It smelled like a house after the family has moved out — dust and cold hearths and something sweetly rotten underneath, the smell of rooms no one remembers living in. The village appeared over a hill: Whitlow, according to the crooked sign at its edge, though the sign meant nothing now. The people of Whitlow were still there. That was the horror of it. They were mending fences and drawing water and sweeping steps, and every single one of them moved like a man walking through fog with a lantern that kept going out.

A woman stood at her doorway holding a child who did not know her. Not feared her. Did not know her — looked up with the polite blankness children give to strangers on roads.

"Maren," the woman whispered to the child. "Your name is Maren. Please. Please remember, sweetheart, it's only two syllables—"

"She's said that eleven times since we arrived," an old man told them, appearing at Ilex's elbow. His eyes were wet and steady, which made him remarkable; everyone else in Whitlow seemed to be leaking memory from somewhere. "Eleven times. And each time she says it, she believes it's the first. That's what they take first, you understand? The *telling*. You can hold a name if you keep saying it. Stop saying it and it goes soft, like bread left out." He squinted at Ilex. "You're not from here. You don't smell like us. You smell like—" He frowned, searching. "Like church. Like the inside of a bell."

*Oh no*, Ilex thought, with four hundred years of divine composure, *oh no, oh no, this is precisely the wrong moment for a mortal to perceive me accurately.*

"Travelers," he said smoothly. "We've come about the... fog."

"There's no fog."

"There is a spiritual fog."

The old man looked at him for a long moment, then at Marguerite, whose expression clearly communicated *you are making this worse.* Then he shrugged. "Come inside, spiritual-fog men. My wife made soup before she forgot she'd made it, so we've got rather a lot."

* * *

Her name was Doss, and she had forgotten her husband twice already that week, and both times he had introduced himself with such gentle patience that Ilex wanted to weep into the soup, which was excellent. This was the thing about mortals that gods were never taught in whatever passed for their upbringing: they were so much better at loss than divinity ever managed to be. They practiced it daily. Small losses, large ones, and still they set another place at the table.

By evening, Ilex understood what was feeding on Whitlow.

He went out behind the barn where the shadows pooled thickest and let himself unfurl — just slightly, just enough for his true shape to press against the skin of the world. And the world answered. Something vast and hungry turned its attention toward him the way a dog turns toward a whistle, and Ilex felt it *look* at him, and beneath its hunger he recognized, with sickening clarity, a cousin. A lesser god. A small power that had spent thirteen years starving in a world where prayers went unanswered, and had finally been given leave to eat.

It wasn't evil. That was the worst discovery. It ate memories the way he had once eaten worship — because existence demanded fuel, and no one had ever taught the small gods how to refuse.

"They don't know any better," he told Marguerite later, by firelight. The keepsake clicked against Marguerite's collarbone as he leaned forward. "The minor powers. They were bound into the covenant like everyone else, and when your mother broke it, they didn't get freedom. They got *hunger* with the leash off. A god that hasn't eaten in thirteen years doesn't negotiate, Marguerite. It devours."

"And you?" Marguerite asked quietly. "What do you eat?"

And there it was. The question Ilex had been circling for six chapters of his own long life, the truth he had carried folded up small since the day a wounded stranger collapsed at a valley's border and let a bone-carver save him.

He had not been wounded by zealots. Zealots couldn't touch him. He had been wounded by *falling* — cast down, demoted, stripped of name and domain by the convulsion that followed the covenant's breaking. Once he had been Vessel-of-the-Threshold, keeper of doorways and crossings and all the small mercies of arriving safely. When the covenant shattered, every threshold in the world shuddered, and the god of thresholds had shattered along with them.

"I eat nothing," Ilex said. "That's rather the point of me. I'm what's left when a god stops being fed. Scraps. An echo with opinions." He smiled, because smiling was cheaper than the alternative. "You saved a ghost, bone-carver. Congratulations."

Marguerite was silent for a long time. Then, in the tone of a man discussing the weather: "Does it hurt? Being scraps?"

"Constantly."

"Then stop," Marguerite said simply, as if Ilex had complained of a pebble in his boot. "If it hurts and it feeds nothing — stop carrying it. Whatever's left of you. Spend it. On something that matters."

* * *

The hunger came for Whitlow on the ninth night, under a moon so close to full it seemed to lean against the sky like a drunk against a doorframe.

Ilex felt it coming from miles away — that sweet rot-smell swelling into a tide. The small god had finished with the neighboring villages. Hallowford had forgotten it was called anything. The folk of Mere's Crossing had woken unable to recall why they lived where they lived, and had simply begun walking, patient and vacant, toward nowhere. Now the hunger rolled toward Whitlow like weather, and Ilex stood in the square with the whole village asleep around him and did the arithmetic of his own remaining self.

There wasn't much. A demoted god carries perhaps a candleflame of what he was. But a candleflame, held close enough, can keep a single house warm. Or a single village. For a little while. Long enough.

He thought about refusing. That was the strange part — how easy the refusal would have been. Walk away. Let Whitlow dissolve. Find Marguerite, find the covenant fragments, fix the world properly, at scale, the way gods think. He had spent four hundred years believing that power existed to be hoarded against need, that a god who gave himself away was a god who had failed at being a god.

But he had also spent those years hurting, constantly, quietly, from carrying a self that fed nothing. And Marguerite had looked at him across a fire and said *stop* the way you'd say it to a friend, not a supplicant. As if spending yourself were allowed. As if it were, in fact, the only sane arithmetic left.

"Well," Ilex said aloud, to the moon, to the hunger, to nobody. "Here goes four hundred years of dignity."

He knelt in the square of Whitlow and opened himself.

Divinity does not leave gently. It left him the way a river leaves a dry bed — tearing channels as it went. He poured the candleflame of himself outward and shaped it into the only thing he had ever truly governed: a threshold. A boundary drawn in mercy instead of stone, ringing the village like cupped hands. *Nothing crosses here that means harm,* he wrote into the fabric of the night. *Nothing feeds here that isn't invited.* The hunger struck the boundary and recoiled, baffled, offended, *hungry* — and circled, and circled, and found the village suddenly opaque, uninteresting, closed, the way a book snaps shut.

Inside the ring, dreams gentled. Somewhere a child — Maren, two syllables — slept without losing anything. Somewhere Doss murmured her husband's name in her sleep, keeping it soft and safe, saying it even now, especially now.

And Ilex grew heavier.

It happened slowly enough to notice, which was the cruelest gift. He felt the last of the Vessel-of-the-Threshold drain out of him like warmth from a cooling stone — the centuries, the crossings he had blessed, the names he had carried for travelers who never knew his. What remained was a body. Just a body. Knees that ached against cobblestones. Breath that came ragged. A heart hammering with the animal terror of a creature that had never before been in danger of *ending*.

He laughed. It came out cracked and human and astonished.

"Oh," he said, gripping the cobbles. "Oh, this is what it costs. This is what they pay, every day, for everything." Tears — actual tears, salt and hot and *mortal* — ran down his face, and he laughed harder, because it hurt, and the hurt proved it was real, and he had never once been real before. "Marguerite was right. It's allowed. It's *allowed*—"

The moon slid a hair closer to fullness. Nine days. Perhaps fewer.

* * *

Marguerite found him at dawn, sitting in the square with frost on his shoulders, looking like a man who had slept badly and been reborn.

"You did something," Marguerite said. Not a question. Bone-carvers heard changes in the world's music, and Ilex's note had changed — dropped from something silver and distant into something low, warm, finite.

"I spent myself," Ilex said. His voice was different too. Rougher. Better. "On Whitlow. They'll keep their names until this is over — or until I do. Whichever comes first." He flexed his fingers, watching them tremble, delighted by the trembling. "I'm mortal now, Marguerite. Completely. No thresholds, no divinity, no falling further than anyone else can fall." He grinned up at him, frost-lit and foolish and free. "Do you know I've never once been cold before today? It's *terrible*. I love it."

Marguerite stared at him for a long moment — and then did something Ilex had never seen him do: he laughed, really laughed, short and startled, the sound of a man setting down a weight he hadn't known he was holding. "You absolute fool. You beautiful idiot. You gave away being a god to protect a village of strangers."

"They're not strangers. Doss makes extraordinary soup. Besides—" and here Ilex's grin faded into something quieter, truer, "—someone had to prove it could be refused. Before you decide what to do with the covenant. Someone had to show you that power isn't a debt you owe. Your mother never learned that. Maybe you can."

The words landed between them and neither picked them up.

They broke fast in Doss's kitchen, where Doss served soup and studied Ilex with narrowed eyes. "You smell different," she announced. "Yesterday you smelled like the inside of a bell. Today you smell like *bread*. Like someone who should be given more soup." She ladled it decisively.

"That," Ilex said, "is the nicest thing anyone has said to me in four hundred years."

But when the bowls were empty, Marguerite went quiet in the way Ilex had learned to dread — hand pressed flat against the table, listening to something beneath it. Listening to the dead in the bones of the earth.

"What is it?" Ilex asked.

Marguerite lifted his head, and his eyes were terrible and bright. "The dead are all facing the same direction," he said. "Every soul between here and the horizon, standing like compass needles. They're pointing east." He swallowed. "Toward the ruin. Toward where the covenant broke." A pause. "And Ilex — my mother's voice woke up last night. She's singing. All nine days' walk away, and I can hear her singing."

Outside, the moon hung pale and swollen in the morning sky, counting down.
