## 8. Where the Covenant Was Broken

The ruin did not look like the place where the world had been broken.

That was Marguerite's first thought, standing at the treeline with Ilex beside him and the stolen ledger heavy against his ribs. He had imagined — he didn't know what. A crater. A black scar in the earth. Something that announced itself. Instead there was only a ring of standing stones on a bald hilltop, most of them tumbled, one wearing a bird's nest like a crooked hat. Grass grew between the cracks. Wildflowers had colonized the altar stone without any sense of occasion.

"It's smaller than I expected," Ilex said.

"Most terrible things are," said Marguerite. "It's how they get in through doors."

He had been hearing it since dawn — not a voice this time but a pressure, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. The dead were thick here. Thicker than anywhere he'd ever stood, and he'd stood in plague pits and old battlefields where his gift rang like a struck bell. Here they pressed against him from all sides, patient as sediment. Waiting.

And beneath them, deeper than the dead, something else. A hunger so old and vast it had stopped feeling like hunger and started feeling like geography.

His mother was waiting by the altar.

He had known she would be. He had told himself he hadn't, all the way up the hill, rehearsing surprise the way an actor rehearses grief — because some part of him understood that if he arrived unprepared, he would simply stop walking.

She looked like his mother. That was the cruelest part. Thirteen years should have changed her more. Her hair was still dark, still escaping its braid the way it always had, and she still had the little crease between her brows that appeared whenever she concentrated on carving. But her skin had a faint luminosity to it, like moonstone held up to a lamp, and when she turned to face them her eyes caught the light wrong — too deep, too slow, like looking down a well.

"Hello, my love," she said.

"You don't get to call me that," Marguerite said, "until you explain."

"I imagine I owe you rather a lot of explanations." She folded her hands. Even now, even half-divine, she had the manner of a woman about to discuss a slightly embarrassing household expense. "Thirteen years' worth. Perhaps we could sit—"

"No."

She sighed. "No. You never would sit, even as a boy. All right. Ask."

Marguerite's hands wanted to shake. He put them in his pockets, which was a coward's solution, and hated himself for it, and kept them there anyway. "The ledger says you broke the covenant deliberately. Ossian thinks you did it to free mortals. The gods think you did it out of malice. Neither of them is right, are they?"

"No."

"So tell me what's right."

She looked past him then — not evasively, he realized, but the way you look at someone across a sickbed, deciding how much truth they can carry. "There is a god under this hill," she said. "Older than the covenant. Older than the idea of covenants. It came before the others, back when worship was wild and shapeless, and it starved when the covenant taught mortals to pray *properly* — with names, with forms, with tidy little offerings. It cannot die. Gods can't. It can only grow hungrier. And thirteen years ago it woke, and if it had woken fully, it would have eaten every prayer in the world and then every pray-*er*, and kept eating until nothing remembered anything."

"And you stopped it."

"I broke the covenant," she said, "and I built the Hollow Valley on the wound, and I used the wound itself to bind it. A starving god cannot pass through a place where magic has already been hollowed out. There is nothing there for it to grip. Your valley isn't a sanctuary, Marguerite. It's a knot."

"The border was your doing."

"My doing. My dying, too, or close enough to it. What remains of me is mostly *it* now — I am the cork in the bottle." She smiled, and it was his mother's smile exactly, warm and tired and apologizing in advance. "But a seal made of one person wears thin. Mine is nearly worn through. Which is why the voices bleed into your carvings now, why the gods answer prayers again, why the moon is making its thirteenth turn. The knot is loosening."

Ilex shifted at Marguerite's shoulder. "You're going to ask him," the ex-god said quietly. Not a question.

"I'm going to offer," she corrected gently. "There's a difference. Offers can be refused." She turned back to Marguerite, and her luminous eyes were suddenly, unbearably ordinary — a mother's eyes, asking her son for help carrying something heavy. "You have the gift. Stronger than mine ever was. You've spent your whole life listening to the dead; you understand better than anyone alive that memory is the truest substance in creation. You could hold the knot. Take my place. And everything mends — the covenant restored whole, the gods bound back into their proper shapes, the small gods deflated back to harmless, the valley safe forever. Everyone lives. Everyone forgets. That's the price. Silence, forever, for everyone you've ever heard. But they'd be *safe*—"

"You used my unborn gift as the seal."

Her mouth closed.

"The ledger," Marguerite said, pulling it out at last, holding it up like evidence at a trial, though his hand was shaking now and he couldn't stop it. "Your handwriting. *The child will hear. The child will listen. The listening will be the lock.* You knew what I would be before I was born. You designed me. I'm not your son, Mother, I'm your — your hinge. Your mechanism."

"That is not true."

"Then say what is!"

For a moment she was silent, and in that silence the hilltop filled.

They came up through the grass like mist rising off a morning pond — the dead, hundreds of them, thousands, the ones whose last words Marguerite had carried in his hands over twenty years of carving. He recognized them one by one and it broke his heart in installments: the ferryman who'd drowned singing. The twins who'd died within a day of each other and had argued, in his workshop, over who got to haunt whom. Old Bramwen, the first soul he'd ever heard, back when he was seven years old and thought everyone heard grandmothers in their soup bones. They arranged themselves in a great quiet circle around the ruin, facing inward, witnesses at some trial no court had convened.

"They came when I called," his mother said softly. "They are here for you."

"No," said a voice, and it was Bramwen's, crackling and amused and fond. "We're here about *her*."

The old woman stepped forward out of the crowd, translucent as breath on glass, and looked at Marguerite's mother with an expression Marguerite had seen a hundred times on grieving faces: love and anger braided together so tightly you couldn't tell which strand was which.

"Tell him," Bramwen said.

"He knows what I—"

"*Tell him.*"

His mother closed her eyes. When she opened them, the well-light in them flickered, and she looked, for one moment, simply mortal and simply exhausted.

"Very well." She drew a breath she didn't need anymore. "When the starving god woke, I could not kill it and I could not outrun it. Binding it required a vessel of pure listening — something that could hold its hunger inside a single continuous act of attention, the way a song holds a note. My own gift was nearly spent; I'd already spent most of it raising the valley. So I waited. I waited for you to be born with a stronger gift than mine. And then—" Her voice caught. Actually caught, actually broke, and Marguerite felt the ground tilt under the certainty he'd been standing on. "And then I discovered I couldn't do it. You were three days old. I held you and I could not do it. So I did the next cruellest thing. I left. I let the binding rest on the *promise* of you — on what you would become — stretched thin across thirteen years, buying time, praying you'd never need to know. I stole your future to buy the world time, and I told myself it was mercy."

The wind moved through the standing stones, and the stones hummed faintly, like glasses rimmed by wet fingers.

"You made me an instrument," Marguerite said.

"Yes."

"And you never asked."

"No." She lifted her chin, and there was something in her face he'd never seen there before — not divinity, not guilt. Fear. His mother, who had faced down a starving god alone, was afraid of what her son would say next. "Ask me if I'm sorry. Go on. Because here is my confession, the whole of it: I would do it again. Every piece. I would lie to you again, leave you again, use you again — because the alternative was everything, everywhere, forgetting itself into nothing. I am not sorry. I am only sorry that it was *you*."

The dead murmured like a congregation. Bramwen looked at Marguerite, and her ghostly eyebrows rose in gentle inquiry: *Well?*

Marguerite took his hands out of his pockets.

They were steady now. That surprised him. Somewhere on the long walk up the hill, apparently, the shaking had finished its work.

"I believe you," he said. "That's the worst part. I believe every word. You'd do it again, and you'd be right to, and I'd forgive you again, because that's what sons do." He stepped forward, onto the altar stone, into the pressure of the buried god's hunger, which curled around his ankles like cold water. "But you're wrong about one thing. You said the choice is restore-or-rewrite, seal-or-unleash, silence-or-starvation. You and Ossian and the whole chorus of gods and ghosts have been arguing two verses of the same song." He drew his carving knife. "Nobody's written a third verse yet."

"Marguerite." His mother's voice went sharp. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"Mother," he said, and smiled her own smile back at her, warm and tired and apologizing in advance, "hush. I'm listening."

He set the blade against the back of his own hand — and above them, through the thinning clouds, the moon began its final turn.
