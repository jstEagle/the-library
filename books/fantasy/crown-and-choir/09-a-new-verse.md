## 9. A New Verse

The ruin had a name once. Marguerite could feel it trying to remember itself under his feet — the ghost of flagstones, the ache of walls that had been proud before they were rubble. His mother stood in the center of it, where an altar might have been, and she was not the woman from his memories. She was taller than memory. Her hair moved like smoke deciding which direction to be smoke in.

"Marguerite," she said, and his name in her mouth sounded like a bell struck underwater.

"You've been practicing," he said. His voice shook more than he wanted it to. "The dramatic entrance. The hair."

Something crossed her face that might have been amusement, if amusement hadn't been worn down to a nub by thirteen years of terrible decisions. Behind her, half-visible, something enormous shifted in the space between the broken pillars — a presence like famine given posture, like a stomach the size of a cathedral. The starving god. It watched him with attention so vast and so patient that his knees wanted to apologize for existing.

"I can restore everything," his mother said. "The covenant whole, the valley safe, your gift intact. All of it. You only need to stand here." She extended her hand. "Take my place. Be the seal. I am so tired, my love. I have held this door shut for thirteen years and I would like very much to sit down."

* * *

He almost did it. That was the part he would never tell anyone, not even himself in the honest hours: how easy it would have been to take her hand, to become a door instead of a man, to have a purpose so simple you could sleep inside it. He had spent his whole life listening to other people's dead. Wasn't there a kind of rest in becoming silence yourself?

But he thought about the widow in the Hollow Valley, waiting on a bench outside his workshop with her husband's knucklebone in her lap. He thought about the villages losing their own names, mothers calling children whose names had gone soft as fog. And he thought — with a clarity that felt like the first cold morning after a long fever — that his mother had never once asked him what he wanted.

She had only ever told him what he was *for*.

"No," Marguerite said.

His mother's hand stayed out. The starving god leaned closer, and the air went thin and sweet, the way air goes before snow.

"No?" she said quietly.

"No restoration. No bargain. You broke the covenant because you loved something and couldn't bear for it to starve, and then you solved starvation by building a prison around it and calling it mercy." He took the ledger of original terms from his coat — Prelate Ossian's neat, furious handwriting — and let it fall into the dust between them. "I read the terms, Mother. I know what forgetting cost everyone. Gods forgot why they'd made promises. Mortals forgot the promises were made. Everybody just kept paying into an arrangement nobody remembered signing."

"And you think you can do better?"

"I think," Marguerite said, drawing his carving knife, "that I can do differently."

* * *

Here is what no one had understood, not Ossian with his ledgers, not Serah with her fragments, not his mother with her thirteen years of vigil: a covenant did not need to be carved into stone. It needed to be carved into something that lived, that remembered, that could grow new understanding around old wounds.

And Marguerite Nakamura had spent thirty years learning to carve bone.

He rolled up his sleeve. The moon above the ruin hung swollen, one sliver shy of its thirteenth turn, and the light coming off it was the color of held breath. When the blade touched the skin of his forearm, it didn't hurt — or rather, it hurt exactly as much as any true thing hurts, which is to say completely, and entirely worth it.

"What are you doing?" His mother's voice cracked down the middle.

"My work," Marguerite said, and began to carve.

He carved the covenant's oldest terms first, the ones from Ossian's ledger — the promises gods had sworn and mortals had answered — cutting them fine and true into the bone of his forearm, each stroke singing through him the way every carving had ever sung. Then he kept going, past the old terms, into new ones, and the new clause came out of him the way his best work always had: not invented but found, already there, waiting under the surface like a shape in uncut ivory.

*Let nothing be forgotten by either side. Let the gods remember being mortal, and the mortals remember touching the divine. Let memory pass freely between realms, so that neither gods nor mortals may again rule through forgetting.*

"That will kill you," his mother said.

"Everything does, eventually. This way it buys something."

The choir arrived then. He heard them before he saw them — all the dead he had ever listened to, pouring over the broken walls of the ruin like a tide of candle flames. The widow's husband. Children he had carved memorials for. Strangers whose voices had bled into his work across the borderlands. They arranged themselves in rings around the altar, and their sound was not grief anymore; it was testimony.

They testified to her mercy — and it was real, he could hear it, the way she had fed the starving god her own divinity year after year so it wouldn't devour the world instead. They testified to her theft — and that was real too, the unborn gift she had taken from him, the seal she had set in his blood before he drew his first breath. Both things at once. That was what the dead did better than anyone. They refused to simplify.

"Listen to them," Marguerite said. "Really listen. You built a valley where none of them could reach you. Thirteen years of perfect quiet, and you call that peace? It's just a bigger cell, Mother. You gave the prison better lighting."

Her face broke. Not dramatically — worse than dramatically, in the small way faces break when someone has finally said the true thing. And behind her, the starving god made a sound that Marguerite felt in his carved bones: a low, endless hunger-noise that, now that he listened properly, had grief folded all through it. It wasn't hungry for worship. It was hungry to be *known*. Thirteen years sealed away from every mind that might hold a memory of it, and it had begun eating villages' names simply to feel them pass through it — the closest thing to company it could find.

"Oh," Marguerite said softly. "You poor enormous thing."

* * *

He finished the last line of the clause as the moon touched its turning point, and the covenant woke in his bones like sunrise happening inside his marrow. It didn't hurt anymore. It *sang* — every cut a note, every term a harmony, his skeleton become a bell tower staffed by a single exhausted carver who had, in fairness, always talked to things other people couldn't hear.

"Take it," he told the starving god. "Not worship. Not imprisonment. Remembrance. Everyone remembers you now — both sides, forever. You'll never be unknown again."

The god came forward the way a tide comes forward, inevitable and strangely gentle, and where it passed through the ruin, everything it touched lit briefly with recognition. It bent over Marguerite, vast as weather, and it *read* him — the clause written in his living bone — and something in its ancient starving posture eased, like a creature setting down a weight it had carried so long it had forgotten the weight had a name.

Then it turned to his mother.

"No," she whispered.

"It isn't punishment," Marguerite said. "It's just memory. All of it. Even yours."

The choir opened its ranks, and his mother looked at the dead she had tried to silence, and they looked back without accusation, which is the most unbearable thing the dead can do. She reached for Marguerite one last time — he let her touch his cheek, her fingers cool as river-stone — and then remembrance took her the way dawn takes a lamp: not violently, not cruelly, just thoroughly. She dissolved upward into the choir, into the voices she had feared and loved, and the last thing she left in the world was the shape of her hand on his face fading slower than the rest of her, because even dissolving gods get to keep their favorite part a moment longer.

The starving god settled into the world like rain settling into a drought-cracked field — everywhere, welcome, ordinary. Somewhere beyond the ruin, Marguerite sensed rather than heard it begin introducing itself to a village, carefully, politely, like a neighbor who had been away far too long.

Above, the moon completed its thirteenth turn.

The world held its breath. The clause in Marguerite's bones held its note.

And then, quietly, everywhere at once, both realms exhaled.

* * *

Marguerite sat down hard in the rubble and laughed until his ribs ached, which took very little encouragement given the state of them. Ilex — mortal now, gloriously, stubbornly mortal — limped over and sat beside him and offered the last heel of bread from their pack.

"So," Ilex said. "You rewrote the fundamental architecture of reality using a whittling knife."

"It's a very good knife."

"You're bleeding from places I don't think bones are supposed to bleed from."

"Also true," Marguerite conceded, taking the bread.

But when he lifted the bread to his mouth, he noticed something. The choir was still there, ringing the ruin, patient and bright. And for the first time in his life, he could see them clearly — and beneath their voices, beneath the great shared hum of two realms now remembering each other, there was a small, growing silence where his gift used to live. The clause had taken it. Every word he'd carved into his own bones had been paid for with the one sense he'd never chosen to spend.

The dead began to fade, returning gently to wherever the newly remembering world kept its dead. And Marguerite realized, with a feeling too large for one chest, that soon he would not hear them at all. Not the widow's husband. Not the strangers. Not, perhaps, his mother.

Ilex watched his face. "What is it?"

"The bill," Marguerite said, and found he was smiling. "It's come due." He looked east, toward the Hollow Valley, toward home, toward a widow on a bench and a carving he owed her — and wondered, for the first time in thirty years, what silence would sound like.
