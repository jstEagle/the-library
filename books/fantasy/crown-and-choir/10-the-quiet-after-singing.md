## 10. The Quiet After Singing

The valley had changed its mind about magic.

Marguerite felt it before he saw it — or rather, he felt nothing, which was the change. For twenty-nine years the Hollow Valley had pressed against his gift like wool packed into his ears, and now, walking up the cart road with his pack on his shoulders and Ilex limping beside him, the silence was simply silence. Clean. Ordinary. The kind of quiet a room makes when everyone in it has stepped out to fetch water.

"It's strange," he said. "I keep waiting for it to hurt."

"Give it time," Ilex said. "Grief always finds a new angle eventually."

"You're very comforting for someone who used to be a god."

"I was a *minor* god. We were mostly weather." Ilex smiled with half his mouth. He walked differently now — no glow beneath the skin, no faint smell of rain-before-rain. Just a lean man with a healing limp and a farmer's tan he'd acquired in the space of one mortal summer. "You'll notice I don't complain."

"I've noticed you complain constantly."

"That's different. That's character."

The valley gates — two leaning stones that had never been gates at all, only the habit of calling them so — stood open. Beyond them, the orchards hung heavy with late apples, and smoke rose from chimneys in the unhurried way of a place that had not yet learned what the world outside had become.

A child sat on the wall near the stones, legs swinging, and as they passed she looked up at Ilex and said, quite conversationally, "There's a lady made of candlelight sitting on your roof. She says her well went dry and she'd like to discuss terms."

Ilex stopped. Marguerite stopped.

"Terms," Ilex repeated.

"That's what she said. Mama told me not to talk to gods without an adult present, but you're nearly an adult, so."

Marguerite laughed until his ribs ached.

* * *

His workshop smelled of dust and bone shavings and the lamp oil Serah had left burning in the window every night for eight months, because she was Serah and would deny doing it until her dying day. She hugged him hard enough to crack something, then held him at arm's length, looked him over, and said, "You're thinner. And taller. And you have a scar."

"The scar is from a bell."

"Of course it is." She kissed his forehead. "Your mother would have—"

"Don't," Marguerite said gently. "Let's let her be finished."

Serah's eyes went wet and fierce at once, the way they did. "She'd have hated that clause of yours, you know. Memory shared between realms. She spent her whole life building a door and you turned it into a window."

"She built the door," Marguerite said. "I just put glass in it."

He slept for two days. On the third day, he sharpened his knives.

* * *

The widow's name was Anselma Voss, and she came to the workshop on a morning so bright it seemed apologetic, carrying the same bundle she had carried the first time: her husband Tam's shin bone, wrapped in linen gone soft from handling.

"You said—" She stopped in the doorway, and Marguerite watched her gather courage the way some people gathered eggs, one careful handful at a time. "You said last time that you'd carve it and I could hear him. One last word. But then everything went mad, and the moon, and the bells singing, and—"

"And I left," Marguerite said. "Sit down, Mistress Voss. Please."

She sat. She unwrapped the bone with hands that trembled, and set it on his bench between them like something holy, which it was, though not in the way she thought.

Marguerite picked up his finest knife.

Here was the thing he had not told anyone, because telling would make it real: when he carved now, he heard nothing. Not muffled whispers, not the old cotton-wool hush of the valley — nothing. The dead did not murmur through the marrow. The clause he had written into his own bones had cost him exactly what he had known it would cost, and he had paid it gladly, and still, every morning since, his hands remembered a listening they could no longer do, the way a sailor's legs remember the sea.

But he could still carve. He had learned, over years, to read grief in the grain of bone, and Anselma Voss's grief was simple and enormous, the grief of forty years of marriage ending mid-sentence. He shaped the memorial slowly, letting the knife find the lines Tam Voss's life had left there: a man who had mended fences badly and loved his wife completely, whose last words — the ones the bone had whispered to Marguerite all those months ago, begging him to leave the valley, because even a dead man can see a storm coming — had been a warning wrapped in tenderness.

The carving took three days. When it was done it was a small thing, a shin-bone whittled into a figure of a woman standing at a gate, holding a lamp, the gate open behind her.

Anselma turned it in her hands. Her lip trembled. "It's beautiful. But I don't understand — how do I hear him? You used to tell people what their dead said. Everyone knows that. Everyone in the valley knew."

Marguerite took a breath.

"Mistress Voss," he said, "something happened to me. Out there. I gave up my gift — traded it, spent it, whatever word you like — so that the covenant could be rewritten. So that the dead and the gods and we could remember each other instead of forgetting. It was worth it. I want you to know it was worth it. But I can't hear him anymore. I'm so sorry. I should have sent word—"

"Stop," Anselma said.

He stopped.

She was looking at the little carved woman at the gate. And her expression was doing something Marguerite had never seen a client's face do in all his years of listening: it was tilting, softening, focusing, the way a face turns toward a voice.

"He's talking," she whispered. "Oh — oh, he's *talking*. He sounds cross. He sounds like when I burn the bread." A tear slid down her cheek and she laughed at the same time, a helpless, delighted sound. "He says the gate's open and I've been standing in the yard for eight months like a fool. He says — wait. Wait, he's saying something else."

Her eyes widened.

"He says thank you. To you. He says—" She frowned, listening to something Marguerite could not hear, would never hear again, and translated with the care of someone reading aloud by candlelight. "He says, *Tell the carver he was right to go. Tell him the quiet isn't empty. It's just resting.*"

Marguerite found he could not speak for a moment.

"Is that all?" he managed finally.

"No." Anselma smiled through her tears, and there was mischief in it, Tam's mischief, forty years of it. "He also says you owe me a discount, since you didn't do the hearing part."

* * *

They began coming within the week.

Not pilgrims — the valley had had enough of pilgrims to last several generations — but neighbors. A farmer from two valleys over whose grandmother's ghost kept rearranging his tools and wanted, politely, to be asked before she continued. A young priestess of Vessia who had lost her congregation to the moon-turning and wanted to know if the goddess had meant anything she'd ever said (Vessia, when Marguerite wrote to ask, replied via a candle-flame that spelled out *MOST OF IT*, which he chose not to pass along). A small god of hedgerows who arrived at the workshop door in the body of a fox and sat there for two days until Marguerite carved him a proper shrine and negotiated a tithe of one egg per week.

The valley let them come. That was the strangest part — the old border, his mother's border, still stood, but it had become what a window is instead of what a door is: something you could see through, speak through, pass things across, without either side flooding the other. Magic moved through the Hollow Valley now the way light moves through water. Gently. With refractions.

Ilex stayed. He claimed it was for his knee. He planted a garden, argued with Serah about theology, taught the village children a game involving stones that was clearly an ancient rite of divination repurposed for gambling, and every evening sat on Marguerite's step while the lamps were lit and reported on the day's negotiations between realms.

"The candlelight goddess settled on three candles a month," he said one such evening. "The hedgerow god wants the egg increased to two."

"Absolutely not. One egg. He signed."

"He's a god. He'll claim divine inflation."

"Then he can walk back to the hedge."

Ilex grinned, and for a moment the lamplight caught him the old way, almost glowing, and Marguerite thought: this too. This is part of the price's payment. Not only what was lost. What stayed.

"Do you regret it?" Ilex asked quietly. "The gift?"

Marguerite considered. His hands lay open in his lap, knife-callused, silent. Somewhere beyond the window, the whole breathing world went on — the living and the dead and the small drunk newly-humble gods of it — and none of it spoke to him directly ever again.

"When I was a boy," he said, "I thought my gift was hearing the dead. It wasn't. It was being needed by the grieving. The hearing was just how it arrived." He turned his palms up. "Turns out the grieving still need me. Turns out the dead can reach them on their own now, if someone builds the gate wide enough. So no." He smiled. "I don't regret it. I just miss it. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," Ilex agreed. "They're not." And then, with the tactlessness of the recently mortal: "Also Anselma Voss says Tam wants to know when you're carving her a matching piece, because she's tired of standing at the gate alone."

Marguerite threw an apple core at him.

* * *

On the first night of autumn, he sat at his bench long after dark, and before him lay a bone.

Not a client's. His own commission, the one he'd been circling for weeks the way a tongue circles a missing tooth. It was a knuckle bone, small, yellowed, worn smooth — his mother's, carried out of the ruin where she had dissolved into the choir, recovered from the rubble by Ilex without being asked, pressed silently into his hand on the road home.

He had not known what to carve. Every design he tried felt like either a monument or an accusation, and she deserved neither; she deserved, at last, simply to be remembered accurately, which is harder than either.

Tonight he understood.

He carved no figure. No gate, no lamp, no sainted founder. He carved a window. Small, plain, four panes, the kind of window in the kind of house a bone-carver's son might have grown up in — and through the panes, in negative space, the suggestion of a woman looking out. Not imprisoned. Not sealing. Watching. Waiting to be seen back.

When the last shaving fell, Marguerite held the little window up to the lamp, and the light came through it warm, and nothing spoke to him, and nothing needed to.

Outside, far off toward the valley's edge, a bell rang once — the church bell from the ruined village, rehung last month, carrying in its bronze a fragment of the old covenant still singing its counter-melody. Except now the melody had changed. Now, when it rang, every god within earshot paused, however briefly, to listen — and every mortal paused too — and in that pause, brief as a held breath, both sides remembered the other existed.

Neighbors. That was all the clause had bought, in the end. Not peace, not harmony, not an end to argument. Neighbors. Gods who could be spoken to, and refused, and bargained down to one egg a week. Dead who could visit. Living who could listen without intermediaries, most days, if they were patient.

And him — deaf at last, and free, and needed anyway.

Marguerite set the little window on the sill where the lamplight would find it each evening, and blew out the flame, and in the dark of the workshop, for the first time in his life, the silence around him belonged entirely to the living.

Somewhere in it, faint and ordinary and utterly mortal, he heard Ilex swearing at the garden fence, and smiled, and went to help.
