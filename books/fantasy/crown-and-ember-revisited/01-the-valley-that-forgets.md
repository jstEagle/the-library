## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with a valley that forgets, Xanthe Ravensworth had learned over six years of careful observation, was not that it forgot. It was that it remembered *wrong*.

Take the compass on his workbench, for instance. He had brought it into the Vale of Sundering the day he arrived, a fine brass instrument with a needle so true it had once guided him across the Saltreach deserts without a single degree of error. Now the needle spun in slow, dreamy circles whenever he looked at it directly, and pointed stubbornly at his left boot when he didn't. He had named the phenomenon "the shy needle" and recorded it in his ledger under *Local Curiosities, Vol. III*, between the moss that grew only in the shapes of words nobody could read and the stream that ran uphill every third Tuesday.

He dipped his pen and wrote:

*Day 2,191. The needle continues its courtship of my footwear. Attempted to reason with it. Reasoning failed.*

It was good work, if he was honest with himself, which he tried very hard not to be before breakfast. Cataloguing a magic-dead valley required patience, rigor, and a certain talent for talking to objects that would never talk back — all skills he had honed in a former life he did not discuss, in cities whose names he wrote down nowhere, in a career that had ended the way careers end for men who make one map too many for the wrong people.

Here, in the valley, none of that mattered. Here, no spell held. No scrying eye could find him. No enchanted blade could taste his name. The valley forgot him the moment he turned his back, and he had come to think of that forgetting as the only kindness anyone had ever done him.

"You're a coward," he told the compass, because it was easier than telling himself.

The needle pointed at his boot.

"Fair."

Outside the workshop window, morning fog lay along the valley floor like spilled milk. Somewhere up the slope, old Marta's goats were screaming about something, as goats do, with the full theatrical despair of their kind. Xanthe allowed himself a small smile. Six years, and the goats still screamed the same three complaints: hunger, rain, and the existential horror of fences.

He was reaching for his ink pot when the first scream came from the road.

Not a goat. Goats, whatever their ambitions, could not manage terror. This was a human voice, cracked and ragged, and it was followed by the sound of a body meeting his gate at speed.

Xanthe was out the door before he had decided to move, which was itself remarkable; six years in the valley had sanded most of his reflexes down to the smooth wood of routine. A man lay tangled in his gate, or rather what remained of a man, because the stranger had been dressed in courier's grey once and now wore mostly mud and blood. His left leg bent where legs were not meant to bend. His right hand clutched a leather scroll case against his chest the way a drowning man clutches driftwood — not holding it, exactly. *Shielding* it.

"Don't—" the man rasped, as Xanthe knelt. "Don't take it. They'll know. It knows—"

"It?" said Xanthe, who was already tearing strips from his own shirt for bandages, because some habits of decency survive even cowardice. "You're bleeding out on my gate, friend. Let's worry about pronouns later."

The courier laughed, which was a mistake; the laugh became a cough, and the cough came away red. His eyes were gray and fever-bright and terribly, urgently awake. "You're the cartographer," he said. "The one who lives in the dead valley. I looked for you. Three realms. I *looked*."

Xanthe's hands went still on the bandage.

Nobody looked for him. That was the entire architecture of his life. He had built it the way he built maps — carefully, with nothing left open to interpretation. An exiled chart-maker in a forgotten fold of the world, cataloguing shy needles and illiterate moss. No past. No name worth carrying. Certainly nothing worth three realms of searching.

"You've mistaken me," he said, and heard how thin it sounded even as he said it.

"No," the courier said simply. "I haven't."

They got him inside somehow — Xanthe mostly, hauling the man's weight across his shoulders while the scroll case dug into both of them like a shared secret. He laid the courier on the cot by the fire, splinted what could be splinted, and boiled water for the wounds, and all the while the man watched him with that feverish certainty, and all the while the scroll case sat on the table between them like a guest who intended to stay.

"What's your name?" Xanthe asked, mostly to have something ordinary to say.

"Davin," said the courier. Then, with a ghost of professional pride: "Royal Courier, Third Circle. Or I was. Before there wasn't a royal anything anymore." He tried to sit up, failed, and settled for gripping the table's edge. "Listen to me. There isn't time for the polite version. Veylan Ash has taken four of the five realms. His inquisitors are behind me — maybe a day, maybe less. I carried this through the Glasswind canyons with two arrows in me, and I carried it here because *here* is the only place they can't follow. No spell holds in your valley. Their blood-compasses spin. Their truth-songs come out as nonsense." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile. "You live in the one place in the world that refuses to remember. Do you really think that's an accident?"

Xanthe set down the kettle more carefully than he needed to.

"I think," he said slowly, "that valleys are often just valleys."

"And I think," Davin said, "that you're the son of Aldous Ravensworth."

The fire popped. Outside, distantly, a goat made its case against existence.

"My father," Xanthe said, after a long moment, "was a surveyor. He died when I was young. I don't—"

"He didn't die," Davin said gently. "That's rather the point. But we'll get there. If we get there." His eyes drifted to the scroll case. "Help me open it. My hands won't."

Xanthe should have refused. He understood that even then, in the part of himself that still kept honest ledgers. Every instinct he had spent six years sharpening told him to put the stranger back outside, bar the door, and spend the rest of the afternoon arguing with the compass. The valley forgot; that was its gift. All he had to do was let it.

Instead he reached for the case.

The leather was warm. Not sun-warm — *blood*-warm, though Davin had not touched it since they'd come inside. And beneath the warmth, faint as a pulse felt through a wrist, something moved. Xanthe unclasped the case with fingers that suddenly did not feel entirely his own, and drew out the map.

It was beautiful. That was his first thought, the cartographer's thought, arriving ahead of fear as craft always does. It was drawn on some skin-fine vellum he did not recognize, and it depicted — he tilted it toward the light — nothing. Blank parchment, save for an elegant border of interlocking lines, like a fence drawn by someone who loved fences.

Then the light moved, and the map was no longer blank.

Roads bloomed across it like veins surfacing under skin. Rivers threaded themselves in silver. Mountains rose in delicate hatched relief, and forests spread in stippled green, and cities kindled one by one like lamps being lit down a dark street — and all of it *moving*, subtly, the way a living thing moves even in sleep. The whole map breathed. He could see it breathing.

"That's the Five Realms," Davin whispered from the cot. "All of them. As they were. As they—" He stopped, swallowed. "It changes. It remembers everything. It's called the Covenant Map, and it is the single most dangerous object in the world, and I have been running with it pressed against my heart for forty days."

"Why bring it here?" Xanthe asked, and his voice came out strange. "Why bring it to *me*?"

"Because it asked to come," Davin said, as if this were a reasonable sentence. "And because your father paid me, twenty-two years ago, to keep it until such time as a Ravensworth came asking. You came asking, Xanthe. You just didn't know it yet."

"I have never asked for anything in my life but quiet."

"Yes," said Davin. "Well. Fathers are rarely considerate about timing."

The map shifted in Xanthe's hands. He felt it — actually *felt* it, a tremor like a bird deciding whether to fly. And then, in the lower corner, in the empty margin where a cartographer signs his work, ink began to rise.

It came up through the vellum the way a bruise comes up through skin, darkening, gathering, forming strokes. Strokes forming letters. Letters forming a line of writing in a hand Xanthe knew better than his own — a hand he had watched write survey notes by lamplight, a hand that had once closed around his small one and promised, *I'll explain everything when you're older.*

He had waited a long time to be older.

The ink finished, and the margin read:

*Xanthe. At last. — Your father*

The workshop went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Xanthe did not remember sitting down. He was on the stool, though, and the map was in his lap, and his thumb was resting beside those five words as though he could keep them safe by touch. Something was happening to his face that he was glad no one could see. Six years of practiced forgetting, undone by five words in a dead man's handwriting.

"He knew," Xanthe said finally. His voice sounded scraped. "He knew I'd end up here. In this valley. He—"

"Look at the border," Davin said softly.

Xanthe looked. The elegant interlocking lines of the frame — he saw now they were not decoration. They were a seal. And the seal was drawn around the valley itself, a small fold of blankness at the map's edge, a place the roads refused to enter, a white space where the living geography simply stopped. His valley. His sanctuary. His cage, drawn with loving precision by a hand he'd spent half his life missing.

Every road out of the valley on the map ended the same way: in a small, deliberate mark. And every mark, he realized with a cold spreading through his chest, was shaped like a signature. His family's signature. Ravensworth work. Ravensworth ink.

"The valley doesn't forget," he heard himself say. "It was *made* to forget. Made to forget everything. Including me."

"That's why the inquisitors can't follow," Davin said. "And why you could never leave. And why—" He broke off, because his body chose that moment to remind him what forty days and two arrows cost. He slid sideways on the cot, caught himself, and gripped the table hard enough to whiten his knuckles. "There's more. There's so much more, and I need you to listen, because I don't think I'm going to be able to say it twice—"

A knock at the gate.

Three knocks, evenly spaced, unhurried. The knock of someone who has never in their life been kept waiting.

Davin's eyes went wide and fixed on Xanthe's, and in them was the answer to a question Xanthe hadn't asked yet.

"They found the gap," Davin breathed. "They always find the gap. Xanthe — the map. Take the map. Whatever happens, whatever they promise you, whatever they *show* you — the covenant is real, the throne is real, and Ash cannot be allowed to—"

The door of the workshop opened without further knocking, because apparently the lock, too, had opinions about visitors, and Xanthe had time to think the distinctly unhelpful thought that he had never gotten around to fixing it — and then the doorway filled with grey robes and a face like a closed ledger, and a voice like dry paper spoke.

"Good morning," said the inquisitor pleasantly. "We're looking for a stolen map. And it seems the valley has finally remembered something worth taking."

In Xanthe's lap, unseen beneath the table, the Covenant Map pulsed once against his palms — warm, alive, and patient as ink — and in the margin, beneath his father's words, a new line began, slowly, silently, to write itself.
