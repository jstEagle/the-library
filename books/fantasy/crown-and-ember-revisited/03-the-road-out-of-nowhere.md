## 3. The Road Out of Nowhere

I had been waiting on the moor road for three days when the cartographer finally came stumbling out of the valley, and my first thought was: *this is what the covenant sent me to die for?*

He was a thin man, Xanthe Ravensworth, all elbows and ink stains, walking like someone who had never once been chased in his life and was finding the experience deeply unfair. He kept stopping to look at his own palm — I could see him doing it even at half a mile's distance — and then looking up sharply as if expecting the hills themselves to have moved while he wasn't watching. Which, to be fair to him, they may have. The valley's seal was fraying, and fraying seals make the world around them nervous.

I stepped out from behind the standing stone with my hood up and my sword drawn, because there are ways to introduce yourself gently, and then there are ways that tell you everything you need to know about a man before he's said a word.

"Ravensworth," I said.

He screamed. Not a war cry, not a gasp — a full-throated, knees-bending, arms-flailing scream, and he threw the satchel he'd been clutching directly at my head. It bounced off my breastplate. Inside it, something clinked and squelched, which told me he'd packed his inks before he'd packed food, and I confess my heart softened toward him a little right then. A man who flees for his life with his pens is a man after my own ruined heart.

"You're dead," he said. "You're — the courier said — you're supposed to be—"

"Dead? Dying?" I pushed back my hood. "Which one disappoints you more?"

He blinked at me. He had the kind of face that did its thinking in public — worry moving across it like weather. "Serah Vane," he said slowly. "The Oathbreaker of Redford Bridge."

"They give you the full title in the valley, do they?"

"The courier gave me your name." He was backing up as he spoke, one careful step at a time, the way you'd retreat from a dog of uncertain temper. "He said if the map chose me, I should find you. He said you'd know what to do."

"And if the map hadn't chosen you?"

"Then I suppose you'd kill me?"

"I'd have let the inquisitors do it," I said. "Why waste good steel."

That was cruel, and I knew it was cruel, and I watched it land anyway. That was the test, or the first part of it. The courier — dear, doomed Aldric, who'd bled out on my floor two nights before Ravensworth ever left the valley — had made me swear to vet the man before I trusted him with anything. *"He's a good soul,"* Aldric had wheezed, *"but good souls fold under pressure like wet paper. Test him. Please. I'm dying, the least you can do is argue with me about it."*

So I argued. And now here was the wet paper himself, standing on the moor with the wind pulling at his hair, and I needed to know what he did under pressure.

"Show me your hand," I said.

"My hand?"

"The left one."

He hesitated — a long, telling hesitation — and then held it out. Across the palm, in lines that weren't quite scars and weren't quite writing, lay the living map. Even from three paces I could see it breathing. Faint gold light moved beneath his skin like fish under ice, tracing roads that no surveyor had ever walked, coastlines that shifted when he turned his wrist. My stomach tightened. I'd seen the map once, whole, in Aldric's hands, and it had looked like a promise. On this man it looked like a debt being collected.

"It hurts," he said, and then seemed embarrassed to have admitted it. "Not much. Like a word you can't remember."

"Does it talk to you?"

"Is it supposed to?"

"Aldric said it sang to him. Showed him things." I sheathed my sword, slowly, letting him watch me do it. "Roads. Names. Where the next anchor-stone lies."

"No," Xanthe said. "It just... bleeds light when I look at it too long. And last night it showed me—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Nothing. It showed me nothing."

A lie. A bad one, told by a man who had clearly never told a consequential lie in his life. I filed it away. Everyone carried their locked room; mine had bodies in it, so I wasn't going to pry open his on day one. But I noted it, the way you note a door left ajar in a house you're guarding.

"We walk," I said, and turned east.

"That's it? You test me by making me walk?"

"I test you by making you walk fast. The talking comes later."

* * *

We made poor time the first day, mostly because he asked questions.

Not the questions I expected — not *what is the covenant*, not *who is Veylan Ash*, not any of the sensible questions a man carrying the fate of five realms might ask. Instead: how far to the next water. Whether moor ponies bite. Why my sword was wrapped in black cord instead of a scabbard. Whether the inquisitors could track the map's light, and if so whether dousing it in his cloak would help, and if so whether that was the same cloak I'd suggested he use as a pillow, and if so, sorry about the pillow.

"They can't track the light," I told him. "They track the bearer. The light's just how they confirm the kill."

"Oh," he said. Then, brightly: "Well. Good thing we're walking fast."

He said things like that all afternoon. Little jokes dropped into the gaps where terror should have been, like stones laid across a bog. I began to understand it was how he kept from sinking. I've known brave men who never smiled and cowards who never stopped, and neither type survives long on a road like ours. But a man who makes jokes while checking over his shoulder every forty paces — that's a rarer animal. That's a man who's frightened and walking anyway.

Near dusk we crested the ridge above Harrow Fen, and I called the halt, and he sat down so abruptly you'd have thought someone had cut his strings.

"How long have you been following me?" he asked, after he'd got his breath back. "Before today, I mean."

"Since the valley. I was on the road behind you within an hour of your leaving. You leave a distinctive trail, cartographer. You stop to sketch things."

"They were important things! There was a boundary marker with pre-covenant script, do you know how rare that is—"

"You sketched a cow."

"It was an unusual cow."

I laughed before I could stop myself. It came out rusty, like a gate hinge in winter, and he looked so pleased at the sound that I regretted it immediately. Trust is a currency and I'd just handed him a coin. Still. Aldric had trusted him, and Aldric had been many things — fool, martyr, my friend — but wrong about people was not among them.

"Eat," I said, tossing him dried meat and the heel of a loaf. "Then sleep. We cross the fen at first light, and after the fen there's no more hiding. From tomorrow, everything hunts us."

"What hunts us exactly?" He said it lightly, around a mouthful of bread, and I watched him make himself ask it. That, too, went into the ledger. "Inquisitors, you said. How many?"

"On this road? A hunting pair, perhaps. Maybe three." I fed a stick into our small fire. "But pairs report to chapters, and chapters report to the Hand of Ash himself. Kill a pair badly and you don't lose them — you gain a hundred."

"So the trick is killing them well."

"The trick is not killing them at all. We're two people with no army, no city, and between us one working sword arm." I nodded at his ink-stained fingers. "Yours doesn't count."

"Rude," he said. "Accurate, but rude."

* * *

They found us on the third morning, in the gorse below the old drover's road, and I will say this for the inquisitors of Veylan Ash: they were punctual, professional, and entirely without imagination, which is why I loved killing them and hated respecting them.

There were four, not three, and they came in the classic crescent — two visible on the road ahead, walking slow and casual as pilgrims, and two swinging wide through the gorse to take us from the flank. I'd marked the flankers twenty minutes before Ravensworth noticed anything, mostly because the gorse had gone quiet. Moorland goes quiet around armed men the way a tavern goes quiet around a debt collector.

"Cartographer," I said quietly. "Don't turn your head. There are four of them. Two ahead on the road, two coming through the brush on your left."

I felt rather than saw him go rigid. "What do we do?"

"You're going to keep walking. Straight ahead, past the two on the road, like we haven't seen them. They want to talk first — inquisitors always want to talk, it's the priest in them. While they're talking, I'll handle the flankers."

"And then?"

"Then we run."

"That's the plan? Talk and run?"

"Do you have notes?"

"A few," he muttered, but he kept walking, and I slipped off the road into the gorse.

Here is what I remember of the next ninety seconds, and I remember it better than I remember most things, because blood has a way of sharpening the pen:

The flankers were young. That was the pity of it — Ash recruited his inquisitors young, filled their heads with covenant-heresy and the certainty of their own righteousness, and sent them out to murder cartographers. The near one heard me at the last moment and turned fast, blade already moving, and I'll grant him he was quick. Quick isn't the same as experienced. He led with a thrust I'd seen ten thousand times in training yards, and I caught his wrist, broke his grip, and put him down into the gorse with the pommel, hard enough to keep him down but soft enough to keep him breathing. The second one had more sense and less speed; he shouted, which ruined the ambush for everyone, and came at me wild. I took his legs out and knelt on his chest with my knife at his throat and hissed at him to be still, and — this is the part I'm not proud of — he was maybe nineteen, and his eyes were wide and white-rimmed, and I thought, *not another one, please not another boy on my conscience,* and I knocked him cold instead of cutting deep.

Meanwhile, on the road, everything went wrong in the specific way that plans involving civilians go wrong.

The two on the road hadn't waited to talk. One of them had simply drawn steel and walked straight at Ravensworth, and Ravensworth — to his eternal credit and my eternal astonishment — hadn't run. He'd thrown his satchel at the man. The inquisitor swatted it aside, ink bottles shattering, and grabbed the front of the cartographer's coat, and I came out of the gorse at a dead sprint because I had promised a dying man I'd keep this idiot alive and I am, whatever they call me, a woman who keeps her promises.

I reached them as the inquisitor raised his blade. I didn't bother with technique. I hit him like a battering ram, shoulder to ribs, and we went down together, and he was strong — covenant-fed strength, that greasy unnatural vigor they all carried — and for a bad moment we wrestled in the mud while his partner circled for a clean line at me.

"RUN!" I shouted at Ravensworth.

He didn't run.

Instead — and I saw this in flashes, between grappling with a man who wanted very much to kill me — Xanthe Ravensworth snatched up the broken neck of an ink bottle, dove onto the second inquisitor's back, and smashed it against the side of the man's head. Black ink sprayed everywhere. The inquisitor staggered, blinded, shrieking — covenant-touched skin burns where ordinary skin wouldn't, and there is a particular poetry in Ash's own holy writ searing his servants' faces — and Xanthe stood there holding the broken glass, panting, spattered head to foot in black like a chimney sweep, looking genuinely shocked at himself.

The one under me used the distraction. He twisted, got a hand free, and drove his dagger toward my throat. I caught it — barely — on the black-wrapped guard of my blade, felt the edge score the cord, and head-butted him into unconsciousness, which is undignified, effective, and the only move I've ever learned that I'd defend with my life.

Silence. Gorse ticking in the wind. Four inquisitors down, two breathing, none dead.

I rolled off the nearest one and lay in the mud a moment, getting my breath back, and looked up at the cartographer. He was still holding the broken bottle. His hands were shaking so hard the remaining glass chattered against his rings.

"You," I said, "are supposed to run when I say run."

"He was going to kill you," Xanthe said. His voice cracked in the middle. "You said they'd want to talk. You said talk and run. Nobody said anything about anyone almost dying."

"Welcome to the road."

He sat down heavily beside me in the mud and started laughing — high, slightly unhinged, helpless laughter, the kind that lives right next door to sobbing. After a moment, to my own disgust, I joined him. Two mud-covered fools laughing over unconscious fanatics on a moorland road, with the fate of the realms bleeding gold light out of one of our palms. Somewhere, Aldric was laughing too. I was sure of it. He'd always loved an absurd victory.

* * *

We walked until dark, putting miles between ourselves and the ambush site, and made a cold camp in the lee of a fallen wall because I wouldn't risk fire. Ravensworth tried to scrub the ink out of his hair with stream water and mostly succeeded in redistributing it. He looked, I decided, like a crow that had lost an argument with a chimney.

"Those men," he said eventually. He'd been quiet a long time, and I'd learned by now that his silences were heavier than his chatter. "The ones alive. Will they report us?"

"When they wake. If their chapter believes them." I wrapped my hands in the thin blanket and stared at the stars through the broken wall. "Two inquisitors bested by an oathbreaker and a scribe with an ink bottle. They may prefer to invent a different story."

"Will it matter? Us running, I mean." He turned toward me. In the starlight his face was pale and serious, all the humor gone out of it. "Serah. Tell me honestly. How long do we have?"

I could have lied to him. I considered it, briefly. He was barely keeping pace with the truth as it was.

"The free cities think they have until autumn," I said. "Bellharbor's council is debating surrender terms as if they're haggling over wool prices. Thornwake thinks its walls will hold. The northern leagues are arguing about whose banner leads the defense." I pulled the blanket tighter. "Ash doesn't need autumn. He needs the covenant anchors, and three of the five are already broken or dying. Once he holds the anchors, he holds the old magic, and walls don't mean anything to the old magic. Weeks, Ravensworth. We have weeks. Maybe six. Perhaps fewer."

The silence stretched. Somewhere out on the moor, a curlew cried, long and lonely.

"Weeks," he repeated. "To cross five realms and reforge a covenant I learned existed four days ago."

"Yes."

"With inquisitors hunting us."

"Yes."

"And the map wants me to pay for it somehow. It hasn't told me what yet, but it wants something. I can feel it. Like a creditor sitting politely in the corner of the room." He laughed, short and hollow, and rubbed his marked palm against his knee. "You know what the courier told me, before he died? He said the valley was built to hide me. My whole life — every compass spinning, every spell failing, that entire peaceful, forgettable hole in the world — it was a cage with my name on the door. Someone built it to keep me safe. Or to keep me *kept*. I don't know which is worse."

"Both," I said. "It's usually both."

He looked at me sideways. "What did they build to keep you kept?"

I almost laughed. Almost told him. *Redford Bridge, thirty-one souls, my order's justice executed by my own hand because the law said so and I believed in the law.* But the wall was cold against my back and the night was long and some confessions need a fire, and a drink, and a listener who's earned them.

"Ask me again when we've survived Bellharbor," I said.

"Is Bellharbor likely to kill us?"

"Everything is likely to kill us. That's the job now." I closed my eyes. "Sleep, cartographer. At dawn we cut south for the coast road, and there's something you need to see before we reach the city — something the map showed Aldric and refused to show me. He said only a Ravensworth could read it."

Beside me, I heard him go very still.

"Read what?" he said.

I opened my eyes. Far off to the east, faint as breath on glass, the horizon glowed the color of banked coals — though there was no town that way, no hearth, nothing human for sixty miles. Just the direction of the Ember Throne, and the old magic waking along the road to it, and whatever it was my dead friend had seen fit to leave locked inside a living map for a man who couldn't yet read his own inheritance.

"That," I said, "is what we're going to find out."
