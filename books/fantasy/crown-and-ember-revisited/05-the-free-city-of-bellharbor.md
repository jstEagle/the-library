## 5. The Free City of Bellharbor

Xanthe had seen Bellharbor exactly once before, from the deck of a grain barge he'd stowed away on at nineteen, and his chief memory of the city was that it smelled like money left out in the sun too long. It still did. Salt, tar, fish, and beneath it all the particular sweetness of ten thousand ledgers being balanced at once.

"Charming," Serah said, wrinkling her nose as they came through the Marsh Gate.

"It grows on you."

"So does mold."

The city rose in tiers above its harbor like a wedding cake built by a committee — merchant towers of white coral-stone, slate roofs green with sea-moss, and everywhere the flags: the nine bells of the Free Cities, rung in cloth, hanging limp in a windless morning. Xanthe counted them as they climbed. Not one flew at full mast. He didn't need to ask why. Fear kept its banners furled.

They had walked eleven days from the moor road, and Serah had not once let him forget what waited at the end of it. *Weeks, not months,* she'd said. The free cities were already negotiating. By the time Xanthe reached the council hall, the argument would be over — or worse, ongoing, which was how cities talked themselves into surrendering without ever taking a vote.

He felt the map against his chest where it lay wrapped beneath his coat, warm as a sleeping animal. Lately it had taken to warming whenever he thought about giving up, which he suspected was its way of commenting.

The council hall had once been a temple to some drowned god and still bore the traces: water stains climbing the walls like old ivy, an altar repurposed as the clerks' desk, and a ceiling painted with waves that some pious or pragmatic soul had covered over with the portraits of dead merchants. Nine chairs stood on the dais for nine councilors. Only seven were occupied. Two seats sat empty, and Xanthe learned from the whispering crowd that those two had sailed east three weeks ago and not been heard from since.

"Ambassadors," Serah muttered. "Or hostages. With Ash there's rarely a difference."

A clerk with ink-stained cuffs called their names — or rather, called *a name*, because Serah had registered them under *Master Quill of Fenwick* and *his bodyguard*, which Xanthe felt undersold them both.

"The council will hear petitions until the noon bell," the clerk droned. "Petitioners are reminded that the matter of the eastern treaty is under deliberation and unsolicited testimony regarding Lord Ash is—" he checked a paper, sighed, "—apparently permitted now, following this morning's ruling."

"Following whose ruling?" someone in the crowd asked.

"Councilor Vairre's. She threatened to read the whole chamber her correspondence until they agreed."

Xanthe looked at Serah. "Do you know a Councilor Vairre?"

"No," Serah said, in the tone of a woman who absolutely did.

Serah's POV

Dame Serah Vale had sworn, upon breaking her oath, never again to set foot in a seat of power, because seats of power were where oaths went to be manufactured. Yet here she stood in the gallery of Bellharbor's council hall, watching Dame Ilsabet Vairre — Commander of the Third Vigil, her old commander, the woman who had once made her eat rations standing in the rain for a month and had been right to do it — preside over a room full of merchants deciding whether to hand four hundred thousand people to a tyrant.

Life had a sense of humor. Serah had stopped finding it funny around the time she broke her blade.

Vairre hadn't changed. Silver at the temples now, more of it, worn like rank. Same straight back. Same habit of tapping one finger against the council table when she thought someone was lying, which in this chamber meant she tapped constantly, a soft arrhythmic rain of judgment.

"—and I put it to the council plainly," Vairre was saying, her voice carrying without effort, the voice of a woman who had shouted across battlefields. "Lord Ash's envoys offer us 'autonomy within his protectorate.' I have seen what autonomy within a protectorate means. I rode through Hollowmere after the third realm fell. Does anyone here wish to see the streets of Bellharbor vote as Hollowmere's streets vote? Shall we convene a parliament of ghosts?"

Murmurs. A fat guildmaster near the front said, loudly, "Ghosts don't pay taxes, at least," and got his laugh, and Serah hated him with a clean, uncomplicated heat.

Then Councilor Brandt of the Weavers' Guild rose, silk robes whispering, all reasonableness. "The commander speaks of war. War has costs. Ash's terms have none — no occupation, he says. Our ships keep sailing. Our coin keeps counting. The alternative is that he comes with two hundred thousand veterans and takes everything, including our sons." He spread his hands. "I have children, Dame Vairre. Do you?"

The chamber went very quiet. It was a cruel question, precisely calibrated, and Serah watched Vairre take it like a body blow she'd been expecting.

"I have the Third Vigil," Vairre said evenly. "Four thousand children, and every one of them knows exactly what they'll die for. Can your guild say the same?"

Tap. Tap. Went her finger against the table.

Serah should have stayed in the gallery. That was the plan; the plan was always to stay in galleries, unremarked, unnamed. But then the noon bell rang, and the clerk announced the closing petition — *Master Quill of Fenwick, regarding an antiquarian map* — and Xanthe walked down onto the marble floor of the council hall looking, Serah thought with despair, like a schoolteacher who had wandered into the wrong building. Shoulders apologetic. Ink on his fingers. She followed him down because someone had to, and because her legs decided before she did.

"State your business," said the moderator.

Xanthe opened his mouth, and Serah saw the exact moment he abandoned whatever careful speech they'd drafted on the road. She'd liked that speech. It had been short.

"My name," Xanthe said, "is Xanthe Ravensworth."

The name moved through the chamber like wind through wheat. Even Serah flinched. *Ravensworth* was a story grandmothers told — the family that had sealed the valley, the cartographers who had bound the realms, half history and half fairy tale, and mostly forgotten except by people who read too much, which in Bellharbor was everyone.

"That name means nothing without proof," Brandt said, but he'd gone pale under his silk, which Serah found encouraging.

"Probably not," Xanthe agreed, pleasantly. "But what I carry might. My lords and ladies — councilors — I'm not here to talk you into war. I've read enough history to know nobody's ever been talked into a war, only out of one afterward, when it's too late. I'm here to tell you what Ash actually wants, because his envoys are lying to you, and I think most of you already suspect it."

He unwrapped the map.

Later, Serah could never describe what it did in that room. It wasn't light, exactly. It was more that the hall suddenly remembered it had once been a temple, and something old leaned in to look. The living map spread across Xanthe's hands and *breathed* — coastlines shifting like tide, the five broken realms rendered not in ink but in something closer to memory, each anchor-point glowing faintly, and one — the valley seal — burning steady as a hearth.

"This is the covenant," Xanthe said. "Not a copy of it. Not a description. The thing itself, or as near as makes no difference. It binds the five realms, and it has kept Ash from taking them for thirty years, because as long as it holds, his claim is theft, and theft can be contested. He cannot be crowned while it burns." He paused. "It's failing. Three anchors cracked, one destroyed. And he knows, which is why his envoys are suddenly so generous."

Silence. Then the guildmaster who joked about ghosts stood. "And what would you have us do, cartographer? March? With what?"

"With time," Xanthe said. "The covenant can be reforged at the Ember Throne. Weeks, not months — that's what we have, and it's the same time Ash needs to march. Every week you hold your walls, refuse his terms, keep your fleets at sea, is a week bought for the reforging. You asked what your sons would die for, Councilor. Here." He touched the map, gently, the way Serah had seen him touch nothing else. "This. This is a thing worth the asking, anyway."

Serah had heard better speeches. She had certainly delivered more polished ones, back when she wore white and believed in things with sigils on them. But she watched the chamber — watched the weaver's man chew his lip, watched a shipwright's widow lean forward, watched old Councilor Vairre go utterly still, finger hovering over the table, not tapping.

Hope, Serah thought, is a rumor that spreads faster than plague and can't be quarantined.

Then Brandt stood again, smooth as poured cream, and ruined everything.

"A stirring tale," he said. "And proof? The council requires proof, Master Ravensworth. Anyone can bring us a glowing parchment and a famous surname. Ash's own spies could have sent you." He smiled without warmth. "Show us the covenant works. Show us one miracle, one sign, and Bellharbor stands with you."

Xanthe's face did something terrible and small. Because Serah knew — had known since the marshes, since the glasswind crossing — that the covenant didn't work like that. It wasn't a lamp to be lit on demand. It was a debt coming due, and its only miracles were paid for in Ravensworth blood and Ravensworth memory, and Xanthe would sooner let the city burn than demonstrate.

"Well?" said Brandt.

The silence stretched. Xanthe looked at the map. The map, traitorous honest thing, dimmed slightly, as if embarrassed on his behalf.

Xanthe's POV

Here is what nobody tells you about being a liar by trade: cartography is lying that got tenure. Every map flattens, omits, chooses. Xanthe had spent his life deciding what mattered enough to draw, and now, on the marble floor of a drowned god's temple, with four hundred merchants waiting to be convinced, he discovered the one thing he could not draw.

Proof. They wanted proof, and proof was the one commodity the covenant had never sold. It dealt in cost. Always cost.

"I can't," he said, and the words came out steadier than he deserved. "Not the way you mean. The covenant isn't a trick I can perform for you. What I can show you—"

"Is nothing," Brandt said kindly. "Then we thank you for your testimony, and the council will deliberate."

The chamber began to dissolve into argument, and Xanthe stood there holding a miracle nobody wanted, feeling the precise flavor of failure he knew best: the failure of the honest map in a room that preferred the flattering one.

Then a woman came down from the gallery.

She moved like weather. Grey travel cloak, sword worn at the hip with the ease of furniture rather than display, and a face Xanthe recognized from Serah's rare unguarded moments the way you recognize a coastline from a chart you studied years ago.

"Commander Vairre," the woman said, and the chamber hushed, because apparently rank still counted for something here. "I won't speak for the council. I'll speak for myself." She turned to Xanthe. "You said three anchors cracked. Name them."

"Glasswind — repaired, at cost. Hollowmere — destroyed, its people... remain. Thornwake — guarded, we believe, by the Order of the Vigil." Xanthe hesitated. "By her order," he added, nodding toward Serah.

Every eye in the chamber swung to the gallery behind him. Serah stood very straight, jaw set, wearing the expression of someone about to be audited.

"And how," Vairre said quietly, "would a stranger to these realms know the state of Thornwake's anchor unless she had stood before it?"

"Because I carried her reports," Xanthe said. "In the map. It remembers what its bearers witness. I've seen Thornwake's anchor through her eyes. I've seen—" he stopped himself from saying *the purge*, from saying *what she did*, because some proofs belonged to Serah alone to give. "I've seen enough to know that everything I've told you is true, and that Dame Vairre's own knights have known the anchors were failing for years and reported it, and were ignored, because ignoring it made the treaty easier."

Uproar. Vairre raised one hand and the chamber obeyed her like a crew.

"That report," she said, "was mine. Filed four years ago. Marked 'received' and shelved by this council." Tap. Tap. Her finger on the table, and this time it sounded like a countdown. "So. We have testimony, we have corroboration, we have my own shelved report, and we have a councilor demanding street magic as though the fate of the realms should turn on a conjuror's trick." She looked at Brandt. "I move that Bellharbor withhold all negotiation with Ash's envoys, raise the levy, and put its fleet to sea — pending the reforging this man describes. If he fails, you may all surrender to me first, and save Ash the trouble."

Laughter — startled, real laughter — broke the spell somewhere in the back benches. The vote, when the moderator finally forced it, went six to one, Brandt alone dissenting, and the nine bells of the free cities ran up the flagpoles at last, snapping full-mast in an afternoon wind that seemed to arrive specifically for the purpose.

Fragile hope, Xanthe thought. But hope nonetheless. He'd take fragile. He'd spent his whole life with nothing sturdier.

Afterward, in the colonnade outside, he watched Serah and her old commander circle each other like two cats who had once shared a basket and now disputed the territory.

"You look terrible," Vairre said.

"You look promoted," Serah said.

"They gave me a chair. It doesn't make the sitting easier." Vairre studied her a long moment. "Broken oath, they tell me. Broken blade, too, if the road gossip runs true."

"All true."

"And yet here you are, doing the job anyway." Something moved across Vairre's face — pride, maybe, filed under disapproval for tax purposes. "The Vigil could restore you, Serah. After Thornwake, after whatever penance—"

"No," Serah said, and it wasn't harsh; it was final, like a door with good hinges. "Thank you. But I'm done being restored by other people's terms."

Vairre nodded slowly, and Xanthe had the sense of a message passing between them that had nothing to do with words. "Then I'll say only this," the commander said. "The fifth realm road closes with the first snows. Ash's vanguard crossed the Ashen Ford yesterday — my scouts confirmed it this morning." She was already turning away. "You have less time than you told the council. You always have less time. Ride tonight."

She left. Serah stared after her for a long moment, then said, to the air, to Xanthe, to no one:

"She threatened to read them her correspondence. Four years I served under her and she never once bluffed." A pause. "That's how I knew we were truly lost, back then. And how I know we're not, now."

They made ready to ride that evening, horses procured through the complicated currency of gratitude and Serah's intimidating competence with stablemasters. Xanthe was tightening his last girth buckle when the map woke against his ribs.

Not warmth this time. Cold. A spreading frost-line of dread he'd learned to read the way sailors read weather.

He pulled the map out just enough to see, there in the lamplit stable shadows, and watched fresh ink crawl across the parchment in no hand he knew — black, deliberate, unhurried.

Words, forming along the eastern edge, where the fifth realm began:

*A Ravensworth rides to the Ember Throne.*

*Good. I have waited thirty years for one to arrive.*

Below it, almost as an afterthought, drawn with terrible delicacy: a single compass rose, its needle pointing not north, but directly at him.
