## 6. Ash Descends

Veylan Ash counted his armies the way other men counted their coins: often, and with pleasure that never quite curdled into satisfaction.

From the ridge above the Ashen Plain — named for him now, though he had not asked for the honor and would not have wanted it; a plain named for a man was a plain already half-forgotten — he watched ninety thousand soldiers strike camp in the grey hour before dawn. They moved well. Three years of discipline had burned the looting instinct out of them, or at least taught them to loot *after* the fighting, which was all any general could honestly promise. Banners snapped. Mules complained. Somewhere down in the third cohort a cook was shouting about a missing ladle with the passion of a man who believed the fate of nations hung on kitchen inventory.

Ash found the ladle more interesting than the banners. Armies were honest about two things only: how they fought, and how they ate. A cook who cared that much about a ladle fed men who trusted him, and men who trusted their cook did not desert.

"Your Grace." General Marrow-Vane rode up, dismounted with the creak of a body that had been armored too long, and stood at his shoulder. She was a tall woman built like siege equipment, and she reported as she fought: directly, and slightly too hard. "The vanguard will reach the Sable Ford by midday. The free cities' scouts are watching us from the western hills. They count us."

"Let them count," Ash said. "Counting is what frightened people do instead of acting. It keeps the hands busy."

"They've doubled the pickets at Bellharbor's land gate."

"Doubled?" He turned his head a fraction. "Doubled from how many?"

Marrow-Vane hesitated, which for her was a full confession. "Four hundred."

Ash looked back out over the plain so that she would not see his mouth. Ninety thousand against four hundred, doubled. The free cities had always governed themselves by committee, and this was what committees produced: arithmetic performed in public as courage. "Send word to our friends inside the walls. Not orders — *reassurance*. A frightened merchant does nothing; a reassured one opens gates."

"And if there are no friends left inside? Your Grace, the last courier we sent to Bellharbor—"

"I know what happened to the courier." Everyone assumed he had hanged the man. In truth Ash had paid him, thanked him, and sent him home through a different door. Fear made collaborators of good people, but gratitude made better ones, and it lasted longer. "There are always friends left inside, General. Cities are mostly doors, and every door has two sides."

He dismissed her with a nod and remained on the ridge alone, because there was one more thing he counted each morning, and he did it best without witnesses.

* * *

The tent at the heart of the camp was unremarkable by design — grey canvas, no standard, no guard worth noticing. Inside, it held a chair, a table, a brazier, and a cage that was not a cage.

It looked like a cage. Iron bands, silver filigree, the whole theatrical apparatus that people expected when they expected cruelty done properly. But iron holds no ghost, and silver only slows one. What kept the ghost contained was written beneath the floorboards in a hand older than any kingdom Ash had broken: nine words of covenant-script, inked in blood that had been dead three hundred years and still refused to finish dying.

The ghost sat inside its circle the way an old scholar sits in a library that has disappointed her — patiently, contemptuously, waiting out the century.

"You're brooding again," she said, before he had finished latching the flap. Her voice was dry leaves and riverbed stones. "I can hear it from here. Brooding has a sound. Like a mill grinding nothing."

"Good morning to you, Maerlin."

"It isn't morning. It's the ugly hour your kind uses for atrocities and weddings." She drifted to the edge of her binding and stopped where the script always stopped her, as she did every morning, as if today might be different. It never was. "You've moved camp. West. You're marching on the cities."

"I'm marching toward the cities. Marching *on* them is a decision I haven't finished making."

"You'll make it. You always do. That's the tragedy of you, Veylan — no, don't preen, I said *tragedy*. You decide everything, and understand nothing." The ghost folded her arms, an affectation she'd worn into death and refused to abandon, though her arms passed through each other when she did it. "Three hundred years I knelt at that altar while Ravensworths renewed their pretty covenant with my life, and in all that time, never once did one of them come talk to me first. You're the first jailer with conversation. I haven't decided if that makes you better or merely lonelier."

Ash poured tea — two cups, because ritual mattered even when the second cup was theater — and sat. "I have news."

"You have news," she repeated. "When you have news, you get that particular stillness. Like a heron over a pond. Out with it."

"A courier died in the valley. The dead valley, the sealed one, the place your murderers built to hide their shame." He drank. "Before he died, he passed something on. And the something chose a name."

The ghost went very still. Ghosts were mostly motion — drift, flicker, the restless eddy of unfinished feeling — so her stillness was louder than a scream. "A name," she said. "Whose name."

"Ravensworth."

For a long moment the only sound was the brazier and, distantly, the cook still mourning his ladle. Then Maerlin laughed, and it was the worst sound in the tent, because there was nothing in it but old, old anger. "Of course. Of course! The valley forgets, the seal sleeps, and the moment the world needs it least, a Ravensworth crawls out of the dark like a grub after rain. Which one? There were dozens by the end. Little pale creatures with clever hands and family hymns about duty."

"The heir. Xanthe."

She stopped laughing. "Xanthe." She said it slowly, tasting it. "That was the father's name for the son he hid. The one who wasn't told. The one who wasn't *trained*." Her eyes — hollow things that nonetheless managed to narrow — fixed on him. "And here you sit, drinking tea, smiling your small smile. You want him alive."

"I want him alive," Ash agreed.

"You want to *use* him."

"I want to use him," Ash agreed again, and set down his cup. This was the part he permitted almost no one to hear, and the ghost was not quite anyone, which made her safe. "General Marrow-Vane thinks I march to burn the cities. My captains think I march to burn the cities. Half the continent thinks Veylan Ash is a fire looking for a city." He leaned forward. "But fire is stupid, Maerlin. Fire doesn't distinguish between a granary and a temple. Fire wins battles and loses centuries. I have spent eleven years being called a usurper, and I have decided to earn the title properly — not by taking a throne, but by taking the thing underneath every throne."

"The covenant."

"The covenant." He rose and walked the edge of her circle, close enough that the script warmed the air between them. "Do you know why the Ravensworths bound you? Truly? Not duty. Not the realms. They needed power that could be *inherited*, and inherited power requires a mechanism that survives the inheritor. Blood. Sacrifice. Renewal. They built a machine that eats one innocent per generation and calls it law, and then they wrote laws praising themselves for operating it. And everyone — kings, councils, priests — everyone looked at that machine and said *yes, obviously, this is how the world must work.*" His voice stayed level, which took work. "The free cities will fight me because I am one man with an army. They will never fight the principle. Principle is the strongest fortification ever built, because no one thinks to besiege it."

"So you need a Ravensworth," the ghost said slowly, working it through, "because the covenant answers to Ravensworth blood. You can't seize the machine. You need the family key."

"I need the key, yes. But that's the smaller prize." He crouched to her level, which ghosts resented — being looked at eye to eye reminded them they used to have height. "Think, Maerlin. You of all souls should appreciate this. If I kill the boy and force the renewal, I hold the realms, and I am exactly what they say I am, and in forty years some other clever monster does to me what I did to the old order. But if the *last Ravensworth himself* stands at the Ember Throne and renews the covenant — willingly, publicly, with his own blood, in his own name — then it isn't conquest. It's succession. Legitimate, ancient, sanctified. The cities won't surrender to me. They'll surrender to *him*. To their own sacred law wearing a young man's face."

The ghost stared at him. "You want him to do it *voluntarily*."

"I want him to believe it's his idea, or his duty, or his penance — I'm not particular. Belief is cheap to manufacture if you control what a man learns about his own family." He straightened. "Which brings me to my problem. The boy knows nothing. Nothing of the covenant, nothing of the renewals, nothing of you. Right now he is a blank page, and blank pages are dangerous — anyone can write on them. Including whoever killed my courier and put the map in his hands."

"The oathbreaker," Maerlin said. "Serah Vale. We heard about her fall in the third realm, before you burned it." She said it lightly, and meant it as a knife, and he let it pass, because she was right and knives from the bound were the only honesty he got. "She'll tell him things. Wrong things, probably, but things."

"Then I'll tell him truer ones." Ash returned to the table and drew from his coat a thin packet — letters, old ones, sealed with wax gone amber with age. "His father wrote these. Never sent. A father hiding his son from a family duty, explaining everything, apologizing for everything. Do you know what a boy does with letters like that, Maerlin, once his father is dead and strangers are hunting him?"

"He believes them."

"He *needs* to believe them. Grief will do my editing for me." He weighed the packet in his hand. "The truth is a weapon like any other. Most people only ever sharpen one edge. I prefer both."

* * *

He walked the camp at dawn proper, as he did most days, hooded and unremarkable, because a ruler who is only ever seen from a dais becomes a painting rather than a person, and paintings cannot learn anything.

He passed the farriers, the fletchers, the trench latrines (a ruler who avoids the latrines learns only what advisors choose to smell). He passed the chaplains of the Unchained Flame, his own invention, a faith with no god and one commandment — *no crown above consent* — which his soldiers had embraced with the fervent relief of men who wanted permission to feel noble about winning. He passed the cook, finally reunited with his ladle, and paused long enough to say, "Smells like victory," which was a lie, since it smelled like barley, and enjoyed the man's flustered delight.

Small coin, these moments. But armies ran on small coin.

At the map tent, Marrow-Vane and the staff had pinned the western reaches with red thread: Bellharbor, Glasswind's approaches, the marsh roads, the long climb to the Ember Throne itself. Ash studied the threads for a while.

"One correction," he said finally, and moved a pin — not toward Bellharbor, but toward the empty country south of the moor road, where a sealed valley sat forgetting itself. "We don't converge on the boy. We herd him. Close every road except the one that leads where we want. Give him enemies behind and sanctuary ahead, and he'll run himself to us, thanking his luck."

Marrow-Vane frowned at the board. "And if he doesn't run true? If this Serah Vale takes him off the roads entirely?"

"Then we take the cities while he wanders, and he arrives at the Throne to find the world already arranged around him." Ash tapped the Ember Throne's mark once, gently, the way one taps a sleeping animal one intends to keep. "Either way, he reaches me. Men like him always reach the thing they were made for. It's practically a family tradition."

He was turning to leave when the courier arrived — mud-spattered, half-blown, a scout from the southern watch with the particular wildness of a man carrying news he didn't yet believe.

"Your Grace. The valley." The scout swallowed. "The seal. Our watchers report light under the ground last night, moving — moving *outward*, following the moor road west. Something's leaving the valley, Your Grace. Something that was in it for three hundred years just... started walking."

Ash looked at the map a long moment. At the little pin. At the red threads waiting like snares.

"Well," he said softly, and smiled the smile that had taught a continent to fear him, though the truth of it, just then, was closer to appetite than malice. "Run, then, little cartographer. Draw your road. Every line ends somewhere, and I already know where yours goes."

He rolled up the father's unsent letters and tucked them away against his heart, patient as ink drying.
