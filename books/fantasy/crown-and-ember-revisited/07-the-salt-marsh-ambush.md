## 7. The Salt Marsh Ambush

The marshes of the Sallow Reach smelled like a promise someone had broken.

Serah Vane had crossed them twice in her old life — once on campaign, once fleeing it — and both times she had come out the other side smelling like regret and rotting reeds. The third crossing was no different, except that this time she had Xanthe Ravensworth behind her, complaining about his boots.

"They're ruined," he said for the fourth time that morning. "Do you understand what these were? Cordovan leather. My father bought them in—"

"Your father bought them in a city you won't name, from a cobbler you won't name, on a day you won't name," Serah said, not turning around. "I know. You have exactly one story about your father and you ration it like hardtack."

"It's a good story."

"It's a boot story." She stepped onto a hummock of firmer ground and tested it with her weight before waving him forward. "And right now I'd trade your boots, my boots, and both our dignity for dry socks. Watch the third step. It lies."

"The *step* lies?"

"Marshes lie, cartographer. That's their whole profession. They show you firm ground where there's nothing but cold soup underneath, and they show you water where there's a road. Sound familiar?"

He was quiet for a moment. When she glanced back, he wore the expression he got when she poked at the thing he carried in his palm — the living map, seared into his skin, which he still touched the way a man touches a bruise to check whether it's real.

"I hadn't thought of it that way," he admitted. "Most people think maps are honest."

"Most people think knights are honorable, too." She smiled so he'd know it was a joke, mostly. "Keep moving. We want the causeway by dusk."

They didn't make the causeway by dusk.

* * *

It started with the birds.

Serah had learned to read marsh-birds the way other soldiers read banners. Egrets feeding meant safe water. A heron standing too still meant something big moved beneath. But when the egrets went up all at once — not flushed, just *gone*, lifting off the reed beds in one silent gray sheet — that meant men. Men moving carefully, in a line, closing a circle.

She caught Xanthe's sleeve and pulled him down into the reeds without a word.

"What—"

"Birds." She pointed with her chin. He squinted, saw nothing, and looked back at her with the open, maddening trust of a man who had spent three weeks learning to trust her and hadn't yet learned when to be afraid.

"Inquisitors?" he whispered.

"Could be smugglers. Could be eel-fishers." She listened. Under the wind and the water-slap there was something else: a faint, rhythmic chime, like small bells struck with fingernails. Her stomach dropped through the peat. "Inquisitors. That's a censering bell. They're sweeping for oath-magic."

"Why would they sweep a marsh?"

"Because we're in it." She was already unbuckling her pack, working fast and quiet. "Listen. They'll have the causeway blocked — that bell pattern is a net, not a search. They knew our route."

Xanthe went pale in the way he did when the arithmetic finished. "The envoy. Bellharbor's envoy. He knows the route."

"Merric knows the route," she said, "and Merric has been very friendly and very frightened since we left the city, and frightened men sell things. But that's a problem for an hour from now. Right now the problem is that there are eight of them, they're between us and the only dry road south, and you can't fight."

"I could learn to fight."

"You could learn to *lose convincingly*, which is more useful. Give me your cloak."

He gave her his cloak. She waded ten yards into the shallows, soaked it, wrung it half-dry, and came back shivering and grimly satisfied. "Put that on. Cold hides scent, and wet wool doesn't rustle. We go east, into the deep fen, away from the causeway. There's an island — a real one, stone under the peat — half a mile in. Old hermit's cell. We hold there till dark."

"And then?"

"Then we do something stupid and brave. Standard plan."

"That's not a plan, that's a mood."

"You're learning," she said, and pushed off into the black water.

* * *

The island was exactly as she remembered it, which is to say: smaller, colder, and occupied by the ghost of a man who had clearly died annoyed.

The hermit's cell was a low dome of fieldstone furred with moss, its door long gone, its single window staring east like a squinting eye. Inside there was a hearth, a shelf, and forty years of bird droppings. Serah got Xanthe inside, got him low, and got a tiny fire going — peat, nearly smokeless, barely enough to warm hands.

"We shouldn't have light," Xanthe whispered.

"We shouldn't have been followed either. The world is full of disappointments. Hands out."

He held his hands to the fire. In the ember-light, the map on his palm glowed faintly, lines shifting like veins under skin. Serah had stopped flinching at it around day nine. Now she found herself watching it the way sailors watch weather.

"It's restless tonight," he murmured. "It's been pulling east all day. Toward Glasswind, maybe. Or away from them." He flexed his fingers. "Sometimes I think it's afraid."

"Maps don't get afraid."

"This one does. This one *remembers*." He looked at her across the fire, and for once the humor was gone out of him entirely, and what was left underneath was younger than his face. "Serah. If they take me — if Ash gets the covenant — what happens to you? To your order? Would they take you back?"

The question landed in a soft place and sat there. Serah fed another clod of peat to the fire rather than answer immediately.

"No," she said finally. "The Order of the Sundered Oath doesn't take back what it breaks. That's rather the point of us. I broke mine in Hollowmere, and everything since has just been... interest accruing."

"There are worse debts than service."

"There are worse debts than *death*," she corrected gently. "Service to the wrong master is the worst kind. Ask me how I know."

"I'm asking."

"Not tonight." She heard it then — or felt it first, a tremor through the stone floor, the island itself listening — and put her hand over his mouth. Above them, faint as a memory, the censering bells chimed again. Closer. And beneath the bells, a voice pitched to carry across water:

*"Ravensworth! The Ember Throne offers amnesty to any who surrender the vessel of the covenant. The knight may walk free. The knight's oath-debt will be forgiven, in full, by decree of Veylan Ash himself."*

Silence. Water dripping somewhere. Xanthe's eyes enormous above her fingers.

"He's lying," Serah said quietly.

"Obviously."

"He's not, though. That's the ugly part. Ash keeps his word. It's the only currency he has. If you gave him the map and walked away, he'd let me walk." She took her hand from Xanthe's mouth. "Which means the choice is yours, and I'm going to sit here and watch you make it, and whatever you choose, I'll—" She stopped. Started again. "I'd understand."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Then he did the most infuriating, most endearing thing she had yet seen him do: he snorted.

"Walk *free*," he repeated. "To where? Every road out of here goes through territory he owns. Free is just a bigger cage with better furniture." He closed his fist over the map. "Besides. Your oath-debt isn't his to forgive. It never was. Debts belong to the people you owe them *to*."

"And who do I owe?"

"Hollowmere. The drowned. Everyone your order stood in front of while it happened." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse and better at once. "So we don't settle it with Ash. We settle it going east."

Outside, the bells stopped.

That was worse.

* * *

They came at moonrise, and they came wrong.

Inquisitors of the Ash covenant fought like liturgy — in threes, in silence, with censers swinging censer-smoke that ate courage and dulled the arm. Serah had drilled against them for six years. She knew their forms the way she knew her own prayers, which is to say: intimately, bitterly, and with a growing suspicion about who had written them.

There were five on the island's shore, wading the shallows in a crescent, and one already behind them — the reader, the one with the bell, standing atop the hermit's cell as if he'd grown there. Young. Calm. His censer swung in slow figure-eights, and the smoke came down the broken doorway like something poured.

"Knight Vane," the reader said. "Or whatever we call you now."

"Vane works," Serah said. She put herself between the smoke and Xanthe, sword drawn, and felt the old arithmetic begin — five front, one high, smoke cutting the middle, Xanthe unarmed, Xanthe *unarmed*, Xanthe who had spent the last mile quietly trying to memorize her footwork because he refused to be a burden—

"Give me the map," the reader said, "and the knight walks. That is the offer. It does not repeat."

"It doesn't need to," Xanthe said behind her. "Because the answer's no, and 'no' travels well."

The crescent closed.

What followed was four minutes that Serah would spend the rest of her life taking apart, and it began badly. The smoke reached her on the second exchange and turned her arms heavy, turned the world syrup-slow; she killed the first inquisitor anyway, a clean thrust she hated herself for needing, and took a slash along the forearm from the second that she felt only later. The third and fourth came together, patient, spending her strength against their shields the way the tide spends a seawall.

She was losing. She knew it the way you know weather. Behind her she heard Xanthe shouting — not screaming, *shouting*, words she couldn't parse, and the map-light flared red through the smoke like a wound opened in the air — and the reader on the roof laughed, delighted, hungry.

*"There it is,"* the reader called down. *"The vessel wakes. Hold him whole, brothers — the Throne wants it breathing—"*

Hold him. Not kill him. Take him alive, take the map, take the last free piece of the covenant and feed it to the Ember Throne, and let Serah Vane walk away with her forgiven debt and her empty hands and the rest of her life to wonder what it had been for.

The tide comes in. The seawall spends itself.

Serah Vane broke her oath-blade.

Not in battle — *on purpose*. She caught the second shieldman's counterstroke on her flat, twisted inside his guard, and drove her own sword down against the stone lip of the hermit's well until the steel sang one long, clear, breaking note. The blade snapped six inches above the guard, and the snap went through her like the snap of a bone, like a door closing, like the last thread between her and the white cloaks and the liturgy and the word *oath* parting clean.

Every inquisitor on the shore froze. Even the smoke seemed to pause.

Because an oath-blade was not a sword. It was a vow given temper and edge, quenched in the order's rites, and it could not be broken by any enemy — only surrendered, or shattered by the hand that bore it. Shattered, it released everything it held. Six years of discipline, of doctrine, of the order's borrowed strength in her arm — all of it went out of the broken steel in a single pulse of white light that rolled across the marsh like a small, furious dawn.

The shockwave took the two nearest inquisitors off their feet. The smoke shredded. The censering bell on the roof cracked down the middle and rang its last, a sound like a word cut short.

And Serah stood in the ringing aftermath holding half a sword, lighter than she had been in eleven years, and wept without quite knowing why.

* * *

Afterward there was blood to wash off, hers and others', in cold black water, and a boat to steal — a flat-bottomed eeler's punt, abandoned, which Xanthe insisted on calling "acquired" in a tone that suggested he was practicing for a life of crime.

They poled out into the channels while the inquisitors regrouped, such as remained of them. The reader had fled or been recalled; the marsh kept its own counsel. Dawn came up the color of pewter, then the color of nothing much, and the Sallow Reach unrolled ahead of them, endless and lying and, for the moment, empty.

Serah sat in the stern with the broken hilt across her knees. Half a blade. Half a life. She kept waiting for the grief to arrive properly, the way it always had — the heavy, orderly grief of the order, grief with ranks and rituals — and instead what came was something loose and strange and almost light.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm looking at your hand. You're bleeding through the bandage."

"I'm aware."

"You're also smiling. Those two facts don't fit together."

Serah turned the hilt over. The order's sigil — the sundered circle — stared up at her, and for the first time since Hollowmere it looked less like a brand and more like a door.

"When I broke my oath at Hollowmere," she said, "it was an accident. A failure. I ran, and the running broke it, and I've spent eleven years telling myself I'd go back and mend it. Serve out the sentence. Earn the white cloak back." She laughed — actually laughed, a rusty, disbelieving sound. "This is different. I broke it *choosing*. On purpose. For a man who complains about his boots."

"My boots are a tragedy."

"They truly are." She looked up at him — at the map-glow fading in his palm, at the soot on his face, at the stubborn, terrified, decent set of his jaw — and made the decision official, out loud, where the sky could witness it. "I'm not going back to them, Xanthe. Not after. Whatever's left of my redemption, it isn't theirs to give anymore. It's—" She gestured vaguely east, at the drowned realm, at the broken realms, at all of it. "It's out there. With your cause. Which is apparently my cause now. Gods help us both."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. Then, softly: "Serah. The map."

She followed his eyes. In his open palm, the living map had changed. The fine gold lines of the realms still crawled and shifted — but now, eastward past Glasswind, past the drowned coasts of Hollowmere, new marks were surfacing like bodies rising in a lake. Small ones. Dozens. Moving.

"Not armies," Xanthe breathed. "Look at the spacing. Look at the *lanterns*." He looked up at her, and the wonder in his face was terrible and bright. "Refugees. Whole columns of them, on every road to the Ember Approach. They're not fleeing toward the free cities, Serah. They're fleeing *with* us. Toward the throne."

The punt drifted. Somewhere behind them, the marsh lay silent over its dead, and somewhere ahead, the fifth realm waited with its fires.

"Well," Serah said at last, and picked up the pole. "Then we'd better not be late."
