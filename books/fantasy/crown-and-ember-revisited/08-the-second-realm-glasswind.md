## 8. The Second Realm: Glasswind

The door between realms was not a door.

That was the first thing the map taught me about Glasswind, and it was a lesson in humility, because I had spent three days drawing what I was certain was a door — a tall arch of standing stones on the moor's edge, sketched in careful ink with annotations about threshold theory that Serah had read over my shoulder and pronounced "very pretty and probably wrong."

It was not an arch. It was a wound in the air about the height of a sheep, and you had to crawl through it sideways, like a man apologizing his way into a tavern.

"Graceful," Serah said behind me as I emerged on hands and knees into grass that chimed.

"I'd like to see you do better."

"I did. Quietly. While you were narrating."

I got up, brushing dirt from my knees, and stopped. The chime wasn't the grass. It was everything. The wind moved through Glasswind the way a bow moves across strings, and the whole realm answered — low notes rolling off the distant canyon walls, high silver harmonics from blades of glass-reed swaying at our feet, and somewhere far off, a deep bell-tone that might have been a mountain breathing. The light came down through air so clear it felt rude to stand in it, and every surface held a faint inner glow, as if the world were lit from slightly inside itself.

I am a cartographer. I have stood on the spine of the Grayfell range at dawn and I have watched the tide come in at Bellharbor under three moons, and I thought I had used up my capacity for awe somewhere around age nineteen.

Glasswind made me fourteen again.

"It sings," I said, uselessly.

"Everything here sings." Serah walked past me, boots crunching on soil flecked with mica. "The canyons sing when the wind finds them. The reeds sing when anything touches them. The anchor-stone—" she paused, and her jaw tightened in a way I had learned to read as *the news is bad and I'm deciding how much of it to give you* — "the anchor-stone used to sing too. A note so low most people never heard it consciously. You just felt... held. Safe. Like the realm was humming to its children."

"And now?"

"Now listen."

I listened past the wind-harmonies, past the reeds, down beneath the music, and found it: a silence. Not absence — a hole. A place where a note should have been and wasn't, the way a missing tooth changes the shape of a smile. Once you heard it, you couldn't stop hearing it. The whole realm's song bent around that gap, strained toward it, mourned it.

"The crack," I said.

"The crack." She started walking. "Two days east, where the canyons narrow. Try not to touch anything that hums at you personally. Some of the glass takes compliments as invitations."

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a canyon wall that glowed faint amber and played, when the wind was right, something almost like a lullaby. Serah took first watch. I sat up late with the map open across my knees, watching it be alive.

I should explain the map, or try to, though explaining it is like explaining a friend. It was a courier's burden once, and before that — I suspected — something much older. On its face it showed the five broken realms in ink that shifted like weather. But it also showed things maps shouldn't know. When we crossed the moor road it had darkened along our route like a bruise healing. When inquisitors came within half a day of us it grew cold to the touch. And tonight, spread over my knees in a singing canyon, it was doing something new: little threads of gold light were crawling out from the mark of Glasswind's anchor-stone, feeling along the realm's rivers and roads like roots seeking water, and wherever they touched, tiny script bloomed and faded — names, I realized. Names of places, being remembered by the map even as the realm forgot them.

Because that was the other thing about Glasswind. The realm was forgetting itself.

A village we passed at midday had no name. Not a lost name — the villagers still spoke, traded, argued cheerfully about the price of lamp-oil — but when I asked a woman selling glass fruit what the village was called, she smiled warmly and said, "Here, mostly," and could not be persuaded there had ever been another answer. Her children had names. Her grandmother's name was gone. There was a grave behind her stall with no marker, and when I asked who lay there she looked at it for a long moment with an expression like reaching for a word on the tip of the tongue, and then said, "Someone loved, I think," and went back to her scales.

The cracked anchor was leaking memory. The realm was bleeding its past into the wind, and the wind carried it away in song.

I told myself, lying there with the map's gold threads creeping across its face, that this was why we had come. That fixing the stone would fix all of it. That the cost Serah kept not-quite-mentioning would turn out to be coin, or blood, or a favor owed to some glassy spirit — the ordinary currencies of questing.

I want it noted, for the record of whoever reads this, that I genuinely believed that.

* * *

The canyons narrowed for two days until we walked in a slot of blue glass barely wider than my outstretched arms, walls rising a hundred feet on either side, singing above us in chords that changed with the sun. My footsteps rang. Serah's rang lower. At one point a reed brushed my sleeve and produced a perfect minor third, and Serah said, without turning around, "You've been invited to something," and I said, "I decline," and the reed sulked back into silence.

On the third morning the canyon opened like a struck bell into a vast amphitheater of glass, and there was the anchor-stone.

Books describe anchor-stones as pillars. This had been a pillar once — obsidian-dark shot through with veins of light, tall as a watchtower, standing at the center of the amphitheater where every canyon's song converged. Now a crack ran through it from crown to root, wide enough to put my fist inside, and through the crack I could see — this is the part I still can't render properly in ink — *through*. Into a darkness that wasn't empty but expectant. The stone's light pulsed weakly, arrhythmically, a heart trying to remember its rhythm.

And around its base, kneeling in a rough circle, were people.

Not ghosts. Not quite statues. Figures of translucent glass, dozens of them, frozen mid-gesture — a woman with her hands raised as if catching something, a child reaching up toward a parent's hand, an old man leaning on a staff that had melted into the floor. They didn't move. But they weren't empty. When I stepped closer, each one hummed, very softly, a single sustained note, and the notes together formed a chord that my bones recognized before my ears did: they were holding the crack closed. Holding it with themselves. Had been, I understood suddenly, for years beyond counting.

"They were the keepers," Serah said quietly. "Once. The realm gave them a choice, long ago — become the patch, or let the stone fall. They chose." Her voice did something complicated on the last word. "This is what the covenant costs, Xanthe. Not coin. Not blood spilled *on* the stone. It takes payment in selfhood. Always has. The old rites dressed it up in ceremony, but underneath, every renewal was someone giving up a piece of what they were so the world could keep its shape."

I looked at the glass woman with her hands raised, catching nothing, forever. "Can they be freed?"

"When the stone is whole again. That's the mercy in it." She turned to face me, and I braced, because I knew the look. It was the look she'd worn on the moor road before testing me, before the ambush, before every hard truth she'd ever handed me like a bill presented without apology. "The repair needs material. Living memory. The stone will draw it from whoever works the mending — draw it *out*, permanently, and pour it into the crack the way you'd pour molten lead into a seam. You won't get to choose what it takes. The stone chooses what fits."

"How much?"

"No one knows until it's done. Could be a day. Could be a year of your life. Could be—" she hesitated, and I hated her a little for hesitating, because Serah did not hesitate, "—could be something you can't spare."

I looked at the stone a long time. At the keepers. At the crack, and the patient dark behind it, and I thought about the woman with her unnamed grandmother's grave, and the child reaching for a hand that was already glass.

"Well," I said, because humor is the only armor I've ever owned that actually fits, "at least it's not a riddle. I'm terrible at riddles."

Serah didn't laugh. She put her hand on my shoulder — she had done that exactly twice before, and both times someone had subsequently tried to kill us, which I chose not to mention — and said, "There may be another way. Slower. Riskier. We could go to the third realm, find—"

"No." I surprised myself with how quickly it came. "The village back there. The woman at the grave. If we leave, that keeps happening. Whole realms forgetting their own dead." I knelt at the base of the stone and set my palms against the warm glass. "My mother used to say I never could walk past a torn page. Said I'd stop mid-journey to mend books in roadside shrines and make us late for everything." I swallowed. "She'd understand this. Whoever else I lose, she'd understand this."

I pressed my hands flat against the stone, and began the mending.

* * *

I cannot tell you the technique, because there wasn't one. The map unrolled itself beside me — I hadn't opened it; it opened *itself*, pages lifting in a wind that wasn't blowing — and gold light ran from its ink into my arms and out through my palms, and the stone drank. That was all. The stone drank, and I paid.

The first thing it took was small, almost polite. The taste of my father's stew — some specific evening, some specific bowl, gone, and I only knew it was gone because I reached for it and found the shelf empty. I grieved it briefly and kept working. *Small,* I told myself. *Manageable.*

Then it went deeper, and it stopped asking.

I lost the smell of rain on the valley road outside my workshop. I lost the name of the boy who taught me to skip stones — his face stayed, his name became a smooth blank, a tooth-shaped hole. I lost the sound of my own voice at age ten, laughing at something, and I felt that loss like a small death, because I hadn't known I still carried it, and you cannot mourn properly what you didn't know you had until it's taken.

And then the stone found my mother.

I felt it coming the way you feel a wave gather — a great slow pressure behind my ribs, the mending widening, the crack drinking deeper, needing more, needing something *load-bearing*. And I understood, with terrible clarity, what fit. What the stone wanted. Not a smell, not a sound. A whole room of me.

Her hands, kneading bread, flour to the wrists. The exact pitch of her voice saying my name — not *Xanthe* the way strangers said it, but the way she said it, two syllables that meant *you are mine and I am glad*. The story she told on storm nights about the cartographer's daughter who married a mapmaker and swore her son would never inherit the family curse, and how she laughed when she said *curse*, and how I never asked what she meant, and oh — oh, that was the worst of it — the *not asking*. The question I had carried my whole life without knowing it was a question. *What did she mean by curse? Why did she laugh?*

The stone reached for all of it, gently, the way you lift a sleeping child.

And I let it.

I want to be honest in this account, more honest than pride usually permits. In the moment, kneeling in the singing amphitheater with the crack closing under my hands, I did not weigh the choice. I did not perform heroism. I simply saw the shape of the payment and paid it, because the alternative was the woman at the unnamed grave multiplied by a realm, and because some part of me — the part my mother built, whoever she turns out to have been — has never once in my life walked past a torn page.

The memories poured out of me like ink from a tipped bottle. I felt them go. I felt the crack drink them, felt the stone's pulse steady, felt the chord of the kneeling keepers rise by a full step as the gap beneath them filled. Light climbed the pillar vein by vein. Somewhere above, the canyons changed key — the whole realm modulating, grief resolving into something I didn't have a word for yet.

The last thing to leave was her voice saying my name. It went slowly, syllable by syllable, and I held onto the second syllable as long as I could, the way you hold a door against wind, and then the stone was whole, and the door was shut, and I knelt in the ringing silence with a mother-shaped absence in my chest and no memory of what had been removed from it.

That is the cruelest arithmetic of it. I did not remember losing her. I remembered *that* I had a mother — the fact survived, written into the covenant's ledger of my life — but the woman herself was gone. A heading with no chapter beneath it. I knelt there searching myself the way you search a house after a thief has been, cataloguing what remained, finding rooms intact and one room gone so cleanly that only the outline in the dust showed anything had ever been there.

Around the stone's base, the glass keepers stirred.

It was slow, like ice remembering it was water. The woman with raised hands lowered them, flexed fingers that caught the light. The child's reaching hand found — after years, after decades — the parent's hand, and closed around it, and the two figures leaned together, humming, whole. One by one they rose, translucent and trembling, and looked at the world with expressions I will spend the rest of my career failing to draw: the faces of people returning from a sacrifice they volunteered for, discovering that time had passed and been kind anyway.

The old man with the staff looked at me. He crossed the amphitheater — his steps rang like a struck goblet — and stood over me, and inclined his head.

"You paid," he said. His voice was wind through a bottle. "We heard it go. All of it. The stone sings what it's given, now. Your mother's voice is in the west canyon wall. If you ever want to hear it—" he stopped, hearing himself, and his glass face creased with something like apology. "Forgive me. That was cruel. Or kind. Up here it's hard to tell the difference."

"Is she—" My voice cracked. "Is it her? In the stone?"

"It's your love of her. Which was always the truest part." He rested a hand on my head, light as frost. "The rest of her belongs to you alone, cartographer. Even forgotten. Especially forgotten. Grief doesn't need the details. It needs only the fact, and the fact is yours forever."

He walked away toward the others, staff ringing, and I stayed kneeling a while longer, pressing my palm flat against the healed stone, listening to it hum with a note that was, I realized, partly mine. Partly hers. The realm sang it back to me from every direction, and I could not tell which parts of the harmony had been bought with what, and I understood that this was the covenant's true nature, the thing the old rites had buried under ceremony:

It did not take blood. It took *you*. Piece by piece, realm by realm, and called the transaction holy.

* * *

Serah found me at dusk sitting against the stone, the map open in my lap, drawing.

"What are you doing?" she asked, and there was something careful in her voice, the tone of someone approaching a wounded animal.

"Mapping." I didn't look up. "The keepers are going home tomorrow. Their villages don't remember them. Someone should write the names back before the wind takes the rest." My pen moved. "Except I've hit a problem."

"What problem?"

I turned the map toward her. Where Glasswind's villages should have been named, the ink was writing itself in a hand I recognized — my own, but younger, hurried, the hand of a boy copying letters he'd found somewhere he shouldn't have been. And beneath the village names, in that same boyish scrawl, a single line was surfacing through the paper like a body through lake-water, letter by letter:

*Ask your mother what she meant by the curse.*

I stared at it. Serah read it. The canyon-wind sang overhead, carrying, somewhere in its vast chord, a woman's voice saying a name in two syllables that I no longer recognized as mine.

"Xanthe," Serah said slowly, "when did you write that?"

"That's just it." My mouth had gone dry. "I don't remember writing it. I don't remember *ever* seeing it. But it's my handwriting, Serah. From before the valley. From before—" I stopped, because the sentence finished itself in a place I wasn't ready to go. *From before whatever my parents were hiding.*

The map lay open between us, its gold threads creeping steadily eastward now, past Glasswind's borders, toward the drowned dark of the third realm — Hollowmere, where the anchor hadn't cracked but shattered, and where, the old texts whispered, the first Ravensworth had knelt at an altar.

And in the margin beside that whisper, in my own childhood hand, a second line was beginning to surface.
