## 10. The Oathbreaker's Confession

The fire was Xanthe's idea.

"We could just sleep cold," he'd said, in the tone of a man who had never once slept cold and thought of it as a novelty, like bathing in milk or owning a second pair of boots. "Save the wood."

"It's the third realm's border we're crossing tomorrow," I told him. "Hollowmere. Cold there isn't weather, it's a resident." I stacked the branches myself, because if I let him do it he would arrange them by some cartographer's logic — north sticks to the south, apparently — and we'd spend an hour watching smoke rise out of a pile of unburned lumber. "And you'll want your hands warm. The map gets chatty near the drowned places."

He didn't ask how I knew that. He was getting better at not asking, my cartographer. Or worse. Hard to say which way that particular skill cuts.

We made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old border stones from before the realms were realms, back when they were just quarrels with geography. Good stone. Bad masonry. Someone four hundred years dead had believed very sincerely in walls.

Xanthe cooked. That was another thing about him — the man who had mapped three slaughterhouses for conquerors could make a stew that tasted like someone loved you once. He did it without ceremony, too, humming under his breath, tasting the spoon, adding a pinch of something dried from a pouch he never explained. I had decided not to think too hard about what a man exiled for cartography kept in his spice collection. Some mysteries are load-bearing.

"Serah," he said, halfway through his bowl. "You don't have to answer this. But you've been polishing that sword hilt for two hours, and you only do that when you're—"

"When I'm what?" I said, sharper than I meant.

"When you're about to lie to me," he said mildly, and went back to his stew. "Or yourself. I can't always tell the difference, and honestly neither can you, I suspect."

I looked at the fire. The fire looked back, the way fires do when you've got something rotting in you that needs air.

* * *

Here is what I had told him: I was an oathbreaker. Cast out of the Order of the Thornwake for cause unspecified, disgrace unspecified, wandering because wandering was cheaper than explaining. Every word true. Every word a wall around the truth, mortared tight, and he — being Xanthe Ravensworth, a man who had built himself a valley to hide in — had respected the wall, because that's what people do when they've built their own.

But walls are for sieges, and we weren't besieged anymore. We were two people sharing a fire on the road to a drowned kingdom, and tomorrow the map might show him things about his family, and tonight—

Tonight the wall could come down. Not all of it. One stone.

"You know what the third realm was called," I said. "Before."

"Hollowmere," he said. Then, carefully: "It wasn't always drowned."

"No." I turned my sword across my knees. Oath-blade, they called it, though mine had been renamed twice since — first *oathbroken*, when the Order struck my name from the rolls, and then, after the marshes, simply *broken*, since I'd snapped it over my knee to kill an inquisitor with half of it. The Order would tell you a blade like that carries the oath in its steel, that breaking it breaks the bond. What they don't tell you is that the bond was never in the steel. It was in the arm. And arms remember everything. "It used to be called Vell. A river country. Terraced fields, eel-fishers, forty towns along the water, each one arguing with the next about whose bells rang truer. Beautiful, stupid place. You'd have liked it."

"You've been there."

"I ended it there."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, something small decided against approaching our camp, which showed better sense than most creatures I've known.

"Veylan Ash wasn't Ash yet, then," I said. "He was Commander Veylan of the Ninth Vigil, and I was twenty-two years old and the finest blade the Thorns had produced in a generation, and I believed — this is the part you have to understand, Xanthe, I *believed* — that the covenant was dying. That the anchors were failing because the old blood-pacts were thinning, generation by generation, and that a strong hand at the center could hold them together until a proper renewal could be forged. Everyone said so. My commanders said so. The archivists said so, with charts. There were *charts*, Xanthe. I'm telling you this so you understand I wasn't a fool following a monster into the dark. I was a clever woman following a reasonable-sounding argument into a country full of people."

His spoon had stopped moving. He didn't look away from me, though. Credit where due; most men find somewhere else to put their eyes when a soldier starts talking like this. He watched me the way he watches maps — like the terrain mattered, even the ugly parts.

"The order came down in autumn. The Vellish parliament had voted to withdraw from the covenant negotiations. They wanted independence from the anchor system entirely — said the renewals cost them more than they got, said their own water-magic could stand alone. Maybe they were right. Maybe they were fools. Doesn't matter now, does it." I turned the broken blade so the firelight ran down the crack. "Ash — Veylan, then — declared the withdrawal a breach. Said the covenant couldn't survive one realm leaving, that the others would follow like rotten fence-posts. So we went in to secure the anchor-stone at Hollowmere Deep. Secure it. That was the word. Secure."

"And the towns?"

"Were in the way of securing it." I heard my own voice go flat and military, the parade-ground voice, and hated it, and kept going, because stopping meant never starting again. "There was a night. You'll hear it called the Night of Bells, if you hear it called anything, because the Vellish rang their church bells as an alarm, all forty towns at once, forty different bells in forty different keys, and it should have been chaos and instead it sounded — I'll swear this on anything you like — it sounded like music. Terrible music. The whole country singing out of tune together." My hands were steady. I made sure of that. A soldier learns to make her hands steady; it's the first thing they teach and the last thing that leaves. "By morning, the anchor was secured. By winter, the realm was sinking. The anchor-stone had been bound to the blood of the parliament — that was the old way, blood for stone — and we killed the parliament, so the binding failed, so the waters came up through every well and fen and cellar in Vell and did not stop coming up. Forty towns. The bells still ring down there sometimes, fishermen say. Out of tune."

Silence. Long enough that I counted the fire's breathing.

"They say the third realm broke itself," Xanthe said quietly. "That's the story. Anchor failure. Natural catastrophe."

"Ash tells it that way," I said. "Very moving, when he tells it. He tells it best to new recruits. I know, because I stood beside him while he told it, holding the standard, wearing my sister-knight's face, agreeing with my head that tragedies happen and no single hand is to blame." I finally looked at him. "It was my hand that cut the Speaker of the Parliament's oath-cord. Mine. She was seventy years old and she asked me — she asked *me*, because I was young and she thought young meant merciful — she asked me if there wasn't another way. And I gave her the speech. The charts speech. The covenant speech. And then I cut the cord, and eleven years later her country is a lake and her people vote in a parliament of ghosts, and the knight who did it walks around free with a spice pouch and a bad attitude, calling herself an oathbreaker like it's a wound she caught rather than a thing she chose."

"Serah—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Let me finish, because there's a part I haven't said and it's the part that matters. After Vell, I stayed. Eight more years. I told myself I stayed to fix it from inside, which is what cowards call ambition when they want to keep their rank. I rose. I became Ash's — Veylan's — right blade. And then one day I read the renewal schedules. The real ones, not the ones for the chart-room. I saw how often a renewal needed a death, and whose deaths they were, and how neatly the arithmetic always found someone poor or foreign or inconvenient, and I understood that I hadn't defended a covenant. I'd been a scythe for a machine that harvested people and called the harvest law." I set the broken blade down in the dirt between us, where it belonged. Dirt was honest. "So I broke my oath publicly, at the high altar, in front of the assembled Order, and told them everything, and do you know what they did?"

"Punished you?"

"Buried it." Her voice — my voice — came out with a laugh in it, the worst kind. "They stripped my rank and struck my name and sealed the records, because the truth about Vell would have cracked the Order's alliance with three realms' worth of councils. My confession exists nowhere but in my mouth. Which means, as far as any court in the five realms is concerned, I'm just a disgraced knight with a grudge. No testimony. No standing. Nothing." I shrugged, and it cost more than shrugging usually does. "You asked me once why I'm helping you. That's why. Not redemption — I don't believe the ledger balances that way. Justice. The kind nobody will legislate. If the law won't carry the truth of Vell, then I'll carry it myself, on my back, across every broken realm, and I'll set it down at the Ember Throne if I live that long, and if I don't, I'll have carried it further than it ever got carried inside the law."

* * *

Xanthe was quiet a long time. I braced for it — the recoil, the recalculation, the little shift in the eyes where a person decides what you're worth. I'd seen it before. From better people than him, too.

Instead he picked up the stew pot and refilled my bowl.

"Eat," he said. "Confessions burn calories. I read that somewhere. Probably in a book I shouldn't have been reading."

"That's not—" I stared at the bowl. "I just told you I drowned a country."

"You told me you helped drown a country, following orders, believing a lie, and that you've spent eleven years carrying the weight of it without letting anyone help." He sat back down across the fire, cross-legged, awkward, a man shaped like a question mark. "Yes. It's monstrous. You're not asking me to say otherwise, so I won't insult either of us. But Serah — I need to tell you something too, and if you're going to be sick of me afterward I'd rather we be sick of each other over a full meal."

My stomach dropped. Of course. Of course he had his own stone to pull out of his own wall. Everyone on this road did; it was becoming less a quest than a procession of penitents. "Xanthe—"

"Why do you think I was sealed in that valley?" He said it lightly, which was how I knew it wasn't light at all. "Officially? Cartographic misconduct. Unofficially—" He pulled the living map from his coat, and it lay on his palm glowing faintly, patient as a cat, and I remembered what it had shown us two nights ago before I pretended to fall asleep: a kneeling figure at an altar, dark-haired, with the Ravensworth jaw. "I've been trying not to look at that page. But I've seen enough maps of Vell to know a survey-grade rendering of conquest when I see one, and the map has shown me pieces, and I think — Serah, I think I know what my family was. I think I know what the covenant *ate*. And I think I've known since Bellharbor, and I've been hiding it from you, because if you knew that the man you're escorting to the Ember Throne comes from the family that built the machine you spent eight years swinging a sword for—" His breath went out of him. "Well. I wouldn't blame you for walking."

The fire settled. Somewhere far off, thunder that wasn't weather rolled over the hills toward Hollowmere — the drowned realm talking in its sleep.

I ate the stew. It was, infuriatingly, excellent.

"Here's what I know about machines," I said finally. "I served one for eight years and it never once asked me what I thought. It fed me reasons instead — charts, covenants, necessities — and I mistook the reasons for my own. That's what the covenant is, at root. Not the anchors, not the blood. The *reasoning*. A story big enough that good people stop noticing they're holding the knife." I pointed my spoon at him, which felt appropriately undignified. "You come from the family that wrote that story. Fine. Then you're the one person alive positioned to write a different one, and you'll do it knowing exactly how the old lies were built, because you were raised behind the wall they were hiding. That's not disqualification, cartographer. That's the entire job description."

He blinked at me. "That's the most generous thing anyone's said to me since exile."

"Don't get used to it. Tomorrow I go back to calling you 'map-boy.'"

"You've never once called me map-boy."

"There's a first time for everything."

He laughed — actually laughed, tired and cracked and real — and for a moment the fire was just a fire and the road was just a road, and two liars sat warm in the space where their truths overlapped.

"So that's the arrangement, then," he said. "No more walls?"

"Some walls," I said. "Load-bearing ones. But between us—" I nudged the broken oath-blade with my boot, there in the dirt where I'd left it. "Between us, nothing. Agreed?"

"Agreed." He hesitated, then: "Serah. The Speaker. Did she have a name?"

"Maren Vosk." It came out of me easier than I expected, the way names do once you stop using them as weights and start using them as torches. "Maren Vosk, Speaker of the Forty Bells. If we reach Hollowmere tomorrow, and if the dead will speak to us at all—"

"—then maybe someone can finally witness it properly," he finished. "Not a trial. A hearing. Your words deserve ears, not a verdict."

I looked into the fire and let that sit in me, warm as the stew, strange as hope.

Which was when the map flared in his palm — sudden, urgent, bleeding gold light up into both our faces — and Xanthe went pale in a way I recognized from battlefields, and said, in a voice gone very quiet:

"Serah. The water's showing roads. Under the lake. And I think one of them is expecting us."
