## 11. The Third Realm: Hollowmere

The map had been weeping all morning.

Xanthe noticed it first as a dampness against his palm where the living ink lay seared into his skin — a cool, salt-stinging wetness that had nothing to do with the drizzle. When he unrolled the parchment across a flat stone at the edge of the drowned causeway, he found the third realm rendered in blues so deep they seemed to have been mixed with grief itself, and along the coastline of it, thin silver lines ran down from the mountains to the sea like tear-tracks on a face.

"It's showing us the way in," Serah said, crouching beside him. Her broken oath-blade hung at her hip in two pieces now, wrapped in oilcloth — she hadn't let him mend it, and he'd stopped offering. "Or the way down. With Hollowmere it's usually both."

They stood at the top of a stair that descended into green water. Not a flooded street, exactly — a *stair*, wide and ceremonial, its steps worn smooth by centuries of feet, vanishing below the surface of a sea that should not have been there. Behind them lay moorland and grey sky. Ahead, water stretched to a horizon that shimmered faintly, like breath on glass.

"The realm drowned," Xanthe said. "You told me. But drowned *how*? Floods recede. This looks permanent."

"Because it isn't water." Serah drew her dagger — the plain steel one she'd carried since the marshes, since the oath-blade broke — and touched its tip to the surface. Ripples spread outward in perfect rings, and each ring, Xanthe saw with a lurch, was made of light. "It's memory. The whole realm is sunk in its own remembering. The covenant's anchor here wasn't just cracked like Glasswind's. It was shattered. Deliberately. Everything Hollowmere was got pulled down with it, and what's left refuses to forget."

"And the people?"

Serah was quiet for a moment. Wind came off the water and moved through her cropped hair.

"You'll see," she said. "Try not to stare. They hate that."

He meant to be respectful. He truly did. But when they waded down the seventh step and the water closed over their heads without wetting them — passing through it like a curtain of cold silk — and when the drowned city opened beneath them, lit from within by a thousand drifting lanterns of pale blue fire, Xanthe forgot every resolution he'd ever made and stared.

Hollowmere had been beautiful. That was the first thing. Spires of white stone rose from streets that spiraled inward like the whorl of a shell, and canals ran between them where once boats had glided, and gardens clung to balconies in frozen bloom. The second thing was the people: they walked those streets still, translucent as smoked glass, going about the small business of eternity. A woman swept a doorstep no broom could touch. Two children played a game with knucklebones that clicked soundlessly. A fisherman cast a net into a canal, over and over, catching nothing, forever.

And above it all, crowning the central spire, sat a vast domed hall whose doors stood open, spilling ghost-light onto the plaza below. From within came a sound Xanthe felt in his teeth rather than heard: a low murmur, rising and falling. Voices. Hundreds of them. Arguing.

"They're still voting," he breathed.

"Ninety years of continuous parliamentary session." Serah started down the last stretch of street toward the hall. "When the anchor broke, the council was mid-debate on a grain tariff. They never adjourned. Couldn't, I suppose. Now watch your feet — some of the paving remembers being slippery."

The hall swallowed them. Inside, tiers of benches curved up into darkness, and on them sat ghosts beyond counting, and at the center of the floor a Speaker's podium stood empty, because — Xanthe understood this immediately, the way one understands things in dreams — whoever spoke next would decide whatever was being decided, and nobody wanted to be the one who decided.

A ghost near the door turned. She wore the robes of a clerk, and her eyes were twin points of patient starlight. She looked at Serah for a long moment.

"An oathbreaker," the clerk said. Her voice arrived inside Xanthe's skull, dry as old paper. "And a *living* man. The last delegation sent to us was forty years ago. They came to ask whether Hollowmere consented to be written out of the maps."

Xanthe's stomach dropped. "I'm a cartographer," he said, before he could stop himself.

"I know," said the clerk. "We know what you carry. The whole realm felt it cross our threshold. The map that abandoned us has come home wearing a man's hand." She rose, robes rippling in a current that did not exist. "The Assembly will hear you, cartographer. Not because you deserve it. Because we have been debating procedure for ninety years, and frankly, an interruption will be a relief."

*

They gave him the Speaker's podium.

This, Xanthe reflected as he climbed the steps to face several thousand translucent citizens of a murdered nation, was possibly the worst thing that had ever happened to him, including the ambush in the salt marshes, including the memory the Glasswind anchor had taken from him — though that theft was strange now, a hole shaped like his mother that he kept reaching toward and finding only smooth walls.

Below him, the Assembly waited. He could feel their attention like cold water on his skin.

"Speak," said the clerk, from somewhere near the front. "You have until the tide turns, which, as the tide no longer turns here, means you may speak as long as you can remain interesting."

Gentle laughter rippled through the benches — the first warm thing he'd felt in this place. He clung to it.

"My name is Xanthe Ravensworth," he began.

The warmth died instantly. The temperature of the hall — if a hall of memory could be said to have a temperature — plunged. Ghosts rose from their seats, hundreds of them, a rustling field of indignation.

"Ravensworth," repeated a broad-shouldered ghost in a harbormaster's coat. "The forgers' name. The name on the seal that was supposed to protect us."

"Your family built the covenant," another called. "Your family *promised*."

"We paid our vows!" A woman near the front stood, and her voice cracked across the hall like ice splitting. "Every tithe, every oath, every year of fealty — and when the usurper came for our anchor, where were the realms? Where was the covenant? We called for aid for nine days. Nine days. I remember every one of them." Her hands, transparent, clenched. "Then the seal broke, and the water of remembering rose, and the living fled, and the realms *rewrote their maps*. Do you know what it is to be erased? To look for yourself in the world's memory and find a blank space?"

Silence. Even the knucklebone children outside, Xanthe sensed, had stopped playing.

"I don't know what it is to be erased," Xanthe said quietly. "But I know what it is to erase. I've done it."

He told them. Not the speech he'd rehearsed on the road — something had dissolved the rehearsal somewhere between the doorstep-sweeping woman and the nine-days mother. He told them about the valley that forgot, about discovering that his whole safe exile had been a cage built around him by people who loved him badly. He told them about Glasswind, and what repairing the anchor had cost — "a memory of my mother's voice. I can't tell you which one anymore. That's how thoroughly it's gone." And then, because the Assembly deserved it, because the blank spaces on maps deserved it, he told them about the boy he had been, seventeen years old, drawing a hidden realm's passes and river-fords for men with kind voices and coin-heavy purses, and what those men had done with his beautiful lines.

"I was complicit," he said. "I didn't know. Knowing wouldn't have fed me any differently, but that doesn't wash my hands. So when I tell you I've come to reforge the covenant, understand that I am not asking you to trust Ravensworths. I am asking you to help me make certain no one ever has to trust anyone's name again."

"Pretty words," rumbled the harbormaster. "The covenant took blood before. What does it take now?"

"That's what I came to learn," Xanthe said. "That's why I need you. Your anchor isn't cracked — it's destroyed. It can't be repaired. But the new covenant I mean to write needs anchors that are *chosen*, not inherited. Freely given. And I—" he spread his hands— "I don't actually know yet what a freely given anchor looks like. Nobody does. It's never been tried. Which means I need the one assembly in five realms that's been arguing about procedure for ninety years, because if anyone alive or dead understands the difference between consent and command, it's you."

A long pause. Then, from the back of the hall, a small clear voice — one of the ghost children, slipped in past the clerks:

"He talks funny. I vote we keep him."

Laughter again, wider this time, breaking tension like sunlight on water. The clerk raised a hand for order, and order, astonishingly, obeyed.

"The Assembly will deliberate," she announced. Then, drier than ever: "Briefly. For us."

*

Serah left him at the podium and walked the galleries alone, because there are things a knight knows how to do with crowds that a cartographer doesn't, and one of them is listening.

She moved along the upper tiers, and the dead let her pass — not warmly; she felt their regard slide over her like frost — but they let her pass, and she looked, and she listened, and what she heard rearranged something in her chest.

They weren't haunting Hollowmere. That was the thing no chronicle got right. They were *governing* it. Committees of ghosts argued drainage that would never drain and harvests that would never come in, not from madness, but from refusal — refusal to let the shape of their home dissolve even if everything in it had already ended. She passed a chamber where three elders adjudicated a property dispute between families who owned nothing but memories of houses, and ruled with such gravity and care that Serah had to stand very still for a moment, her hand pressed flat against the wall, breathing.

*I broke my oath,* she thought. *I told myself the law was rotten and walked away. But these people lost everything — realm, bodies, future — and they kept the law anyway. Built a country out of the keeping.*

Near midnight — or the memory of midnight; time here moved like honey — the clerk found her on the western gallery.

"You're the one who guarded him through the marshes," the ghost said. "Broke your blade doing it."

"Broke more than that."

"Mm." The clerk settled beside her on the bench, weightless. "I was the Keeper of Records, when we had records. I read the old covenant texts before the drowning. Every renewal, every clause." Her starlight eyes fixed on Serah. "Do you know what the boy is carrying? Truly?"

"A map."

"A ledger," said the clerk. "Of debts. And the final entry is written in his own blood, and he doesn't fully grasp that yet, and you — knight — I think you do." She paused. "When the time comes, and he stands before the Ember Throne believing his death is the price of everyone else's lives — will you let him pay it?"

Serah looked out over the drowned city, the little lanterns of the dead, the fisherman casting his endless net.

"No," she said.

"Good," said the clerk, and faded like smoke.

*

At dawn-that-was-not-dawn, the Assembly delivered its verdict.

They reconvened him at the podium, and the nine-days mother — her name was Aldessa Vane, he'd learned, and she had been a delegate when the realm died, and had not stopped being one since — stood to speak for them all.

"Here is what we decided, cartographer, after deliberation." A ripple of something almost like humor crossed the hall. "First: we will not rejoin your covenant. Not the old one. Never the old one. Our anchor is ash, our names were struck from the maps, and no vow sworn by the living will bring back one single street of this city. Understand that."

"I understand," Xanthe said.

"Second." Aldessa's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "We watched you today. We are very good at watching; it is most of what we do. You did not promise to save us. You promised to *ask*. In ninety years, you are the first messenger from the living lands who came to ask." She lifted her hand, and in it — impossibly, wonderfully — a ghost of an object: an ancient voting-stone, the kind delegates had once raised to carry a motion. "So we give you not our allegiance. We give you our *vote*. Carry it to the Ember Throne. When you write your new covenant — if you write it — the dead of Hollowmere will cast the deciding ballot. Consent given freely, witnessed by the forgotten." Her mouth curved. "And cartographer — if your new covenant demands blood, we will vote it down ourselves, and haunt you personally, all four thousand of us, forever. Consider that a binding clause."

"I accept the terms," Xanthe said, and found his eyes stinging, and did not hide it.

The voting-stone passed into his hands cool as riverbed pebbles, and heavier.

*

They emerged from the water-memory onto the moor under a real sky, real wind, real grey light, and Xanthe stood a long moment just feeling rain hit his face — ordinary rain, indifferent rain, glorious rain.

"Well?" Serah said.

He opened his hand. The voting-stone sat in his palm, and beside it, the living map had changed: the third realm's tear-track coastlines had brightened from grief-blue to a color he had no name for, something between dawn and forgiveness, and across the blank space where Hollowmere had been erased, faint letters were surfacing in his father's handwriting — the same hand that had once written *Xanthe* and broken his heart.

Not the realm's name. Something else. Three words, still forming, half-legible:

*They remember you—*

"Serah," Xanthe said slowly. "The map's writing something. About me. Or—" he squinted— "*to* me."

Serah leaned over his shoulder, and went very still.

"That," she said quietly, "isn't my father's hand. And I'd know it anywhere, because I burned the last letter he ever wrote me."

Far off, across the moor, a column of smoke rose against the clouds — thin, black, and moving fast. Ash's vanguard had entered the lowlands.

Whatever the map was trying to say, they had perhaps a day to read it.
