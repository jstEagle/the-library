## 12. Betrayal of the Waystation

The waystation at Greyfen Cross had once been a shrine, if the weathered statue by the door was anything to judge by — some forgotten saint with her hands outstretched, palms worn smooth by centuries of travelers who no longer remembered what they'd been asking for. Now it was a rest house for drovers and pilgrims, run by a round, cheerful woman named Bess who charged two coppers for soup and three for the truth about road conditions ahead.

"The Thornwake pass is open," she told them, ladling barley broth into chipped bowls. "Barely. Rockslide last spring took out half the switchbacks, and the order that keeps the shrine up top doesn't like strangers. But you lot don't look like you've got much choice, do you?"

"No," said Serah.

"None whatsoever," said Corvin Hale, the Bellharbor envoy, with the practiced brightness of a man who had spent thirty years making bad news sound like an opportunity. He was soft in the way merchants get soft — comfortable, well-fed, quick to laugh — and he had been traveling with them since Bellharbor as the council's eyes and ears. Officially he was there to report back on whether the covenant could truly be reforged. Unofficially, Xanthe suspected, he was there to make sure the council's investment didn't wander off into the mountains and die.

Xanthe rather liked him. This would later feel like an important detail.

They ate in the common room among drovers and a family of tinsmiths, under a low ceiling blackened by decades of hearth smoke. The map rode against Xanthe's chest beneath his coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and all through supper he felt it doing what it always did now — murmuring. Not words, exactly. More like a cat kneading a blanket: small adjustments, a sense of roads rearranging themselves in the dark, of distances being reconsidered.

"It's restless tonight," he said quietly to Serah.

She glanced at him over her bowl. She ate the way she did everything — efficiently, without waste, as though meals were a tactical exercise. "Restless how?"

"Like it's counting something." He turned his bowl a quarter-turn on the table. "Roads. Exits. It's been doing it since Bellharbor, but it's worse here."

"Here's a crossroads," Serah said. "Crossroads are where ambushes live. I've set enough of them."

Corvin laughed. "You two are a delight at dinner, I hope you know. 'Pass me the salt, also we may be murdered.'" He tore his bread in half and offered Xanthe the bigger piece, which was such a small kindness that Xanthe took it without thinking, and only later understood what it had cost the man to hand over.

* * *

They took rooms. Two coppers each, straw mattresses, doors that locked with a latch designed more for privacy than defense. Serah insisted on the corner room with sightlines to both the stairs and the back window, and put Xanthe next door, and Corvin across the hall, and then she walked the building's perimeter twice in the dark before she let herself sleep — lightly, on the floor beside the door, sword across her knees.

Xanthe lay awake listening to the map murmur.

He had learned, in the weeks since Bellharbor, to read its moods the way sailors read weather. There was the deep slow hum of it remembering — the forging, the altar, the kneeling figure whose face he still refused to look at directly even inside his own head. There was the sharp bright flicker when they crossed old anchor-roads. And there was this: a low, urgent thrumming, like a finger tapped against the inside of his ribs, that meant *something is wrong and you are not looking at it yet.*

He got up. He told himself it was to check on Serah, which was true, but mostly he wanted to stop feeling counted.

Her door was unlatched. Inside, she slept sitting against the wall, blade bare across her thighs, and even asleep she looked like a woman who had been arguing with someone and expected to resume shortly. He stood in the doorway long enough to confirm she breathed, then turned —

— and saw light under Corvin's door.

Not candlelight. Candlelight flickers. This was steady, cold, blue-white, the color of the inquisitors' seal-lamps, the color that had followed them out of the valley and across two realms and haunted the edges of every campfire since.

Xanthe's mouth went dry. He looked down the hall. Looked back at the door. And did the stupidest, most human thing available to him: he knocked.

The light died instantly.

For a long moment, nothing. Then Corvin's voice, warm and rumpled with feigned sleep: "Who's there?"

"It's Xanthe."

A pause — one beat too long. "Ravensworth! Ha. Couldn't sleep either? The beds here are criminal. Give me a moment."

Xanthe heard movement inside. A drawer sliding shut. Something soft — cloth? paper? — pressed flat somewhere low. When the door opened, Corvin stood in his nightshirt looking exactly like what he appeared to be: a fat, tired merchant far from home, blinking apologetically.

"Terrible sorry to wake you," Corvin said. "Did I leave my lamp burning? Bess'll have my hide, she counts the oil."

"You don't have a lamp," Xanthe said. His voice came out very quiet. "You have a seal-light. Inquisitor issue. I've seen them burn villages from the ridge above."

And there it was — the moment. Xanthe watched it happen on Corvin's face, watched the warmth drain out and something older and sadder surface underneath, and understood that the man had rehearsed a dozen versions of this conversation and none of them had included being caught by lamplight.

Corvin sighed. It was the sigh of a man setting down something heavy he'd carried a long way.

"Aye," he said. "All right. Come in, then. Mind the step, it's loose."

* * *

The room was neat in the way of a man who had already packed. His satchel sat by the window, buckled. On the table lay a folded paper, and Corvin made no move to hide it now, which was somehow worse than hiding it.

"Bellharbor has forty thousand souls," Corvin said, before Xanthe could speak. "Forty thousand, and walls older than the council, and maybe six hundred men who've ever held a spear. Ash's vanguard is nine days out when we left. Nine days, Ravensworth. I did the arithmetic on the first night out of the city and I haven't stopped doing it since."

"So you sold us."

"I sold your *route*." Corvin's voice cracked on the word, and he hated himself for it, Xanthe could see that clearly. "Your route. To a man who promised — who gave me his oath, written, sealed — that Bellharbor would be spared. Passed over. Left standing. Do you know what an inquisitor's written oath is worth?"

"Nothing," said Serah from the doorway.

She had her sword. Of course she had her sword. She was leaning against the frame with her arms crossed, and her face was terrible and calm, and Xanthe realized she must have woken the moment he'd gotten up — had probably followed him down the hall without a sound, because that was what she did, because trust for Serah was a muscle she exercised constantly so it wouldn't atrophy entirely.

"They're coming tonight," she said. "That's why the map's been counting. Isn't it?" She looked at Xanthe, and he nodded, and she closed her eyes briefly. "How many?"

"I don't know. Four? Five? The map feels... corners. Like a room with too many corners."

"Five, then. They travel in fives." Serah pushed off the doorframe. "Corvin. Where's the signal point?"

The envoy's shoulders sagged. "The shrine statue. A white ribbon on the left wrist means the quarry sleeps here. They were to come at the change of the watch, when the drovers' fires burn lowest." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I was going to warn you. That was the whole — I swear it on my mother's grave, I wrote the route wrong on purpose, I sent them to the mill ford instead of the bridge crossing, I bought you half a day, maybe a day—"

"A day," Serah repeated. "Generous. You sold our lives and threw in a discount."

"I sold a *chance*, knight, and I bought forty thousand people theirs!" For the first time Corvin raised his voice, and then immediately lowered it, glancing at the wall, at the sleeping house. His eyes were wet. "You think I don't know what I am? I've known since the night I sealed the letter. I'm a good man who did a coward's arithmetic. Forty thousand against three. Any council in the world makes my trade. Most just don't have the stomach to do it themselves — they send a Corvin Hale."

The room was silent except for the wind worrying the shutter.

"We go out the back," Serah said finally. "Now. Before the watch changes."

"And him?" Xanthe asked.

Serah looked at Corvin for a long moment. Whatever passed between them, it wasn't forgiveness — Xanthe doubted Serah forgave anything anymore; he suspected she considered forgiveness a currency she'd spent all of years ago. But there was recognition in it. Two people who knew precisely what fear cost.

"He comes with us," she said. "If the inquisitors find their contact missing, they'll assume he ran with us anyway. And if he stays—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to.

Corvin nodded, already reaching for his satchel, and his hands were shaking, and Xanthe thought: *he knew this was the price when he signed. He just hoped never to pay it.*

Mercy, he was learning, was not free. Someone always paid the toll. The only question was whether you chose who.

* * *

The back window opened onto the stable yard, and the stable yard onto a sheep track that climbed away from the waystation toward the dark shoulder of the Thornwake hills. They were halfway across the yard — Serah first, blades out, moving low; Xanthe behind with the map clutched to his chest; Corvin last, wheezing, thirty years of council chambers catching up with him all at once —

— when the world went white.

Seal-light. Not from the shrine statue. From the *road* — five mounted figures cresting the rise, lamps blazing, and the lead rider's voice carrying flat and clear across the yard:

"There. The fat one and the cartographer. The knight is secondary."

Later, Xanthe would piece together what happened in the next ninety seconds, though pieces were all he ever managed. Serah shouting *run, the treeline, go.* Himself running, stumbling, the map suddenly screaming against his ribs — not murmuring now but *howling*, every road in the world rearranging itself at once. Corvin, instead of running, turning — turning and walking toward the riders with his hands up, saying something Xanthe couldn't hear, something that had the shape of *the deal, remember the deal, the city—*

The inquisitor didn't even slow his horse.

The lance took Corvin Hale through the chest and lifted him off his feet, and the rider wheeled past the body without looking down, because to them Corvin had never been a partner. He'd been a key. Keys get used and discarded, and Ash's people had never pretended otherwise — that was the whole horror of them, that they were honest about what everyone else merely whispered.

Xanthe screamed. He would deny it later, and Serah would never mention it, but he screamed, and kept running, because running was the only prayer he knew.

An inquisitor cut across the yard to intercept him — tall, gray-cloaked, seal-lamp swinging — and then Serah hit the man sideways like a falling tower.

What followed was not fencing. Fencing is a conversation. This was an argument conducted in steel and desperation, two against one after the other four wheeled toward the treeline, and Serah fought the way she did everything — efficiently, without waste — but there were two of them and she was tired, weeks of tired, miles of tired, and Xanthe saw the moment it turned: the second inquisitor's blade slipping past her guard, opening her side from hip to ribs in a line of red that seemed, absurdly, to glow in the seal-light.

She killed them both anyway. He never learned how. When he reached her — having done nothing, having *done nothing* — the two inquisitors were down and she was on one knee in the mud with her hand pressed to her side and her teeth bared in something that was either a grin or a grimace.

"Well," she said. "That's going to scar."

"Serah—"

"Don't." She waved him off, tried to stand, made it halfway. "Horsemen. Treeline. *Move*, Ravensworth, grieve later — grief keeps, it's the one thing that keeps."

He got her arm over his shoulders. Behind them, in the yard, Corvin lay very still with his satchel spilled beside him, papers scattering in the wind — and among them, Xanthe saw as they stumbled past, a letter bearing the inquisitor's seal, and beneath it another letter, unsent, addressed in Corvin's careful merchant's hand: *To the Council of Bellharbor, concerning the true disposition of the covenant—*

He couldn't take it. He had Serah's weight on him and horsemen wheeling and no time. He looked at those scattered pages for half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat in which the map went suddenly, terribly quiet, the way it went quiet around graves — and then he ran.

* * *

They walked through the dark until the dark ran out, Serah's blood leaving a trail any child could follow until Xanthe bound it with strips of his own shirt and a poultice from her kit that smelled like pine tar and spite. By full light they were deep in the foothills, hidden in a hollow beneath a granite shelf, and Serah had gone gray-lipped and fever-bright, insisting between clenched teeth that she was fine, absolutely fine, she'd had worse shaving.

"You have not," Xanthe said. "I've seen you shave."

That earned him a weak laugh, which he chose to count as a victory.

He built a fire small enough to hide, boiled water, stitched what he could with shaking hands while she talked him through it in a voice gone thin at the edges. The map lay open between them — he'd unwrapped it to check the roads, and now it showed him something new: not the terrain ahead, but a single line of text surfacing slowly in the margin, in ink that gleamed wet and fresh, as though the map were writing it even as he read.

*The envoy's last letter lies in the wind at Greyfen Cross,* it said. *It names the fifth realm's gate. Ash's readers will reach it by tomorrow's dusk.*

Xanthe stared at it. "No," he said aloud. "No, no — Corvin's letter. The one he was writing to the council. If the inquisitors collect the scattered pages—"

"Then they learn whatever he'd figured out," Serah said. Her eyes were half-closed, feverish, but her mind was still working, grinding forward like a millstone. "Which was... what? He was cleverer than he let on. He asked questions on the road. About the Ember Throne. About what renews the covenant." She winced as Xanthe tied off a stitch. "Sorry. Sorry. Go on."

"He knew," Xanthe whispered. The realization arrived cold, whole, undeniable. "About the sacrifice. About the Ravensworth price. He worked it out before I did — that's what the letter was. He was warning the council what I'd have to pay." He looked at his hands, stained with her blood and his own guilt. "He betrayed us to save his city, and died for it inside an hour, and the worst part — the part I can't get past — is that I liked him. I *liked* him, Serah. Fear didn't turn him into a monster. It just made him do monstrous sums."

"Fear does that," Serah said quietly. "To everyone. The measure of a person isn't whether they're afraid. It's what they do in the ten seconds after." She shifted against the stone, hissing. "He gave us ten seconds. He turned around and bought us ten seconds with the only coin he had left."

Xanthe said nothing. He looked at the map, at the fresh ink drying in its margin, and thought about mercy and tolls and the price of passages, and about a soft, laughing man who had made a coward's arithmetic and then, at the end, in the yard, in the seal-light, turned around to face the horsemen with his hands raised — not begging, Xanthe realized now. *Reminding them of the deal.* Dying on the terms of a contract the other side had never intended to keep.

Fear made collaborators of good people. And the collaborators paid first.

"Thornwake," Serah said. Her voice was slurring now, fever pulling her under, but she caught his sleeve with surprising strength. "Two days. The pass. My — my former sisters hold the anchor there, and they will want my head on a pole, and I need you to promise me something before I sleep this off."

"Anything."

"When we get there — when I'm standing in front of them — don't you dare let them see you flinch on my behalf." Her grip tightened. "Whatever they say about me. Whatever's true. You look at me like I'm worth following, or this whole march dies at the border. Promise me, cartographer."

"I promise," Xanthe said.

She slept. He sat by the small fire with the map open on his knees, watching the margin ink shift and settle, and far to the south, beyond the hills, beyond sight, he imagined riders collecting scattered pages from the mud of a stable yard — piecing together a dead man's final honesty, and learning, page by page, exactly what a Ravensworth's blood was worth.

By dusk, the map had drawn him a new road. It climbed north, into Thornwake, straight through the heart of the order that had cast Serah out — and at its end, in small, urgent letters, it had added a warning in a hand he was beginning to recognize as his father's:

*They are expecting you now. All of them.*
