## 13. The Fourth Realm: Thornwake

The thorns announced the realm long before we could see it.

They grew along the border like a hedge that had ambitions — black-barked, taller than a man, each spine gleaming with a faint amber light that pulsed in slow rhythm, as if the whole wall of them were one enormous sleeping heart. Xanthe stopped at the edge of the bramble and unrolled the map across his forearms, and I watched his face do the thing it did when the map showed him something he didn't want to know.

"It's a fortress," he said. "Or it was. The map calls it the Briarhold." He traced a line with one finger. "The anchor-stone is at the center. And around it—" His finger stopped moving.

"Around it what?"

"Soldiers." He looked up at me, and there was something careful in his expression, the way there always was when he was trying to spare me and doing it badly. "Not Ash's soldiers, Serah. White cloaks."

I had been dreading this realm since Bellharbor. Some part of me had hoped Thornwake would be empty, or drowned like Hollowmere, or guarded by anything other than *them*. The Order of the Unbroken Vow kept its anchors the way a miser keeps coins: jealously, and with no thought for anyone who might need one.

"They'll know you're coming," Xanthe said gently.

"They knew I was coming from the moment I broke my oath-blade in the salt marsh," I said. "Word travels faster than we do when the word is *traitor*."

He winced. He'd been wincing at my word choices since the marsh, ever since I'd knelt in the shallows among the reeds and picked up the two halves of my sword and understood, finally and completely, what I had done and what I was now. An oathbreaker with no blade to break further. A knight with no order. It should have felt like falling. Instead, holding those broken halves, I'd felt something closer to setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying.

"Maybe we go around," Xanthe offered, without conviction. He was a terrible liar even to himself; the map had already shown him there was no going around. The thorn hedge ran the width of the realm, and the only gate was the Briarhold's gate, and the only way through the gate was whatever the sisters decided it would be.

"No," I said. "We don't go around. We never go around. That's how they trained me, remember? Face the thing. Name the thing. Let the thing break against you if it must." I smiled, and I knew it wasn't my good smile. "I'm going home, Xanthe. Let's be polite about it."

* * *

The gate of the Briarhold was grown, not built — an archway of woven living thorn, and beneath it, waiting in ranks of six, twelve women in white cloaks with silver pins at their throats. The pin was shaped like a stylus crossed with a sword, the old sigil of the vow: the pen that binds, the blade that keeps.

At their center stood First Sister Maren Cael, who had been my commander before she was anyone else's, and who had personally read the words that struck my name from the rolls. She hadn't changed. That was the cruelest part. Eleven years, and her hair had gone grey at the temples and nothing else about her had moved at all. She stood the way she'd taught me to stand, weight forward, hands loose, eyes already finished with you.

"Serah Vale," she said, and my name in her mouth sounded like a verdict being read out.

"Maren." I stopped ten paces off. Close enough for courtesy. Far enough that nobody had to pretend this was friendly. "You look well."

"You look like an oathbreaker walking a thief through holy ground." Her eyes went to Xanthe, cataloguing him — the map-case on his back, the burn-scar on his palm where the covenant lived now, the road dust on everything. "Is this the Ravensworth?"

"He has a name."

"I'm sure he does. Names are cheap." She stepped forward, and the rank behind her shifted with her, one motion, like a single animal. "Do you know what you've done, Serah? Do you know what walks behind you? The inquisitors burned two villages in Glasswind looking for him. Two villages. Because a broken knight couldn't do the decent thing and die quietly."

"The villages are Ash's work, not mine."

"The villages are *yours*, because you were there and they died anyway." No heat in it. That was Maren's gift — she could say monstrous things in the tone of a woman discussing grain prices. "You want passage. You want the anchor. Tell me why I shouldn't give you to the inquisitors instead and save everyone the trouble."

"Because the anchor is cracking," Xanthe said, stepping up beside me, which was either brave or foolish and was very likely both. "Because Thornwake will follow Hollowmere into the dark within the year, and your white cloaks can't hold back the unraveling any better than prayers can. Because the covenant is dying, Sister, and I'm the last chance it has of being reborn."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then, to my horror and my grudging admiration, she laughed — a short, hard sound with no warmth in it anywhere.

"The last chance. They always are." She turned back to me. "Here is how it will be, Serah. The law of the Briarhold hasn't changed while you were away sulking in exile. Any sister cast out may reclaim her place by combat — one bout, ritual forms, first yield or first blood. Win, and you walk back through these gates a sister again, and your Ravensworth passes under our protection, because we protect our own."

"And if I lose?"

"Then the Ravensworth leaves the way he came, and we watch from the walls while the inquisitors do what inquisitors do." She said it kindly, which was somehow worse. "That's the offer, Serah. Your redemption, purchased honestly, in front of witnesses. Everything you lost, given back. All you have to do is win."

And there it was. Laid out on a plate, gleaming. Eleven years I had dreamed some version of this — standing in the training yard at dawn, running forms alone, telling myself that someday, somehow, I'd get the chance to stand in front of all of them and prove the casting-out had been a mistake. That I'd earn my way back into the warm circle of the fire, the shared bread, the pin at my throat.

Xanthe touched my elbow, barely. "Serah. We can find another way."

"There isn't another way and you know it."

"Then let me say—" He hesitated, and lowered his voice. "Whatever happens in there. Whatever you choose. I wanted to have said it before, not after."

I looked at him — this soft-footed mapmaker who flinched at raised voices and had walked into a burning lowland carrying nothing but paper and stubbornness — and something in my chest did a complicated maneuver that I decided not to examine until later.

"Noted," I said. "After."

* * *

The combat yard sat at the heart of the Briarhold, ringed by thorn hedges so old they'd gone silver-grey, and the anchor-stone rose above it all like a held breath — a monolith of veined amber, cracked now, a single fissure running crown to base, weeping light. Even cracked, it filled the yard with a hum I felt in my teeth. Eleven years since I'd stood in this ground, and my body remembered every inch of it. Where the footing gave slightly near the eastern hedge. How the evening light came through the western gap and blinded a left-handed fighter. Which stones were honest and which would roll.

Maren faced me across the yard, and the sisters formed the ring, white cloaks hanging still in the windless air.

"Ritual forms," Maren said. "First yield or first blood. No oath-blades — you'll use practice steel, both of us. The vow doesn't recognize broken metal." She drew her own practice sword and rolled her wrist once, settling it. "Unless you'd like to surrender now and save us both the theater."

"You always did talk too much before a fight."

"And you always did listen too little." She saluted, crisp, correct. "Begin."

She came in fast, because Maren always came in fast — she believed in ending fights before doubt could grow roots. I caught the first cut high, gave ground, gave more, let the old patterns take my feet because my head was full of noise. This was the yard. This was the smell of crushed thorn-leaf and oil. These were the faces watching, and eleven years ago they had watched the same faces watch me leave, and I had spent every day since wanting exactly this and calling it something nobler than *wanting to be forgiven*.

Her second cut opened my sleeve. Third blooded my forearm — a thin line, first blood technically, but the ritual required the fight continue unless I yielded, and yielding meant leaving, and leaving meant Xanthe walking out alone into the thorns with the inquisitors a day behind us.

So I stopped fighting the way they taught me.

It's a strange thing, to unlearn yourself mid-combat. Every form I knew was an Order form, every counter an Order counter, and Maren knew all of them because she'd written half of them. So I did the thing Serah Vale the oathbreaker could do that Serah Vale the sister never could: I fought dirty. Not cruelly — I wouldn't give them that — but crooked. A shoulder feint with no sword behind it. A foot sweep from an angle no form sanctioned. I used the rolling stone near the eastern hedge that I remembered and she'd forgotten, because she'd been inside the walls for eleven years and I'd been fighting for my life on roads that didn't forgive.

The stone rolled. Maren recovered beautifully — she always recovered beautifully — but recovery costs a half-second, and a half-second is a country wide, and I was through it, practice blade resting at the join of her neck and shoulder, my weight settled, the point not pressing but present.

First yield or first blood. She had neither. But we both knew.

The yard was silent. Somewhere above us, the cracked anchor hummed its wounded note.

"Yield," I said quietly. "You know the forms. Say it, and I walk back through the gate a sister. Say it, Maren. It's yours to give and mine to take."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind her eyes. Something tired. I wondered, absurdly, if she wanted to lose. If some part of her had spent eleven years wishing someone would come back and beat her in this yard and make it all simple again.

She started to open her mouth.

And I stepped back. Lowered the blade. Took another step back, out of reach, out of claim, out of the circle entirely.

"Serah—" Xanthe said, from the hedge line.

"No." My voice cracked on it and I let it crack. I was done pretending my voice didn't crack. "No. I won't take it."

Maren stared at me. "You've won. The right is yours. Your place, your protection, your *name* restored—"

"My name was never yours to take and it isn't yours to give back." I heard the words coming out of me and they sounded like they'd been waiting eleven years in some locked room, and the door had finally come off its hinges. "You cast me out for asking questions. For refusing to burn a granary full of people whose crime was sheltering deserters. You called it discipline. You called it *order*. And I have spent eleven years letting you define what happened to me — calling myself broken, calling myself fallen, wearing your verdict like it was the truth about me." I pulled the two halves of my oath-blade from my pack and held them up, and the whole ring of white cloaks went very still. "I carried these like a wound. Like proof I'd failed. But you know what I figured out in the salt marsh? A broken blade is still steel. It just isn't a leash anymore."

"Serah," Maren said, and for the first time her voice was not the voice of the First Sister but of the woman who had once trained a fierce, stubborn girl in this same yard. "Come back. Whatever you think we did wrong—"

"What you did wrong was make obedience the whole of the vow." I dropped the blade-halves at her feet. Not thrown — placed, almost gently. "I'm not taking back my oath. I'm choosing exile. Do you understand the difference? Exile is mine. I pick where it goes. I pick what it builds." I looked around the ring, at the young faces, the ones who'd been children when I left, watching me with something that wasn't contempt — I'd expected contempt and found something hungrier and sadder instead. "Keep your gate. Keep your stone. But hear me, all of you: the covenant is dying because it was built on blood taken, freely given by no one. And everything built that way falls. Everything. Ask Hollowmere's ghosts, if you doubt me."

I turned my back on the yard — on the fire-circle I had mourned for eleven years — and walked toward the gate, and Xanthe fell in beside me without a word, which was exactly right, because there were no words yet, and he knew it.

Behind us, Maren's voice rang out across the yard, formal and carrying:

"Passage is granted. The Ravensworth goes to the stone." A pause, and then, quieter, only for my back: "You always listened too little, Serah Vale. But by the vow — you were never wrong about the granary."

I didn't turn around. My eyes were doing something I refused to let twelve witnesses see.

But I did stop, one last time, at the gate arch, and put my hand flat against the living thorn, and feel the amber pulse of the anchor beneath it, faint and arrhythmic, a heart working too hard.

"Hold on," I told it. "Help is actually coming. Real help. Whether you like the shape of it or not."

* * *

We made camp that night beyond the hedge, in sight of the Briarhold's lights, and Xanthe built a small fire and said nothing for a long time, which was his particular genius. He could keep silence like a room you could rest in.

Finally he passed me the tea tin and said, "You could have had it back. Everything."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you take it?"

I watched the steam rise off the cup. "Because I finally understood the difference between going home and going back," I said. "Going back means the place decides who you are. Going home means—" I stopped, because the sentence had arrived somewhere I hadn't planned, and Xanthe was looking at me over the fire with an expression I recognized, because I'd been wearing versions of it since Bellharbor.

"Means what?" he asked softly.

"Means the place comes with you." I drank the tea too fast and scalded my tongue and pretended I hadn't. "Your turn. The map's been twitching since sundown. What does it want tomorrow?"

He unrolled it reluctantly, and the firelight caught the ink, and I saw his face change — that dread-wonder again, deeper now, older. The lines of the map had rearranged themselves overnight. The Ember Throne sat at the top of the page like a sun, and the route to it had shortened impossibly, days folding into hours, and beneath the throne, in fresh ink that glistened like it had just been spilled, new words had appeared in a hand I'd seen before on the courier's dying lips:

*One renewal remains. One payment remains. The ink must come willingly.*

Xanthe closed the map slowly. When he spoke, his voice was carefully level, the voice of a man reading a bill he'd known was coming.

"We're a day from the Ember Approach," he said. "And according to this, my family's debt is due at the end of the road."

He looked up at me across the fire, and the amber light of the distant anchor glinted in his eyes, and I saw that he'd already begun deciding something I was going to spend the next three chapters of his life arguing against.

"Get some sleep, Serah," he said gently. "Tomorrow we climb."
