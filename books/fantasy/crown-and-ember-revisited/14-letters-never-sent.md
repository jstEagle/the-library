## 14. Letters Never Sent

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time the map decided to speak.

I want to be clear about that: *decided*. I had long since stopped pretending the map was a passive thing, a document, an object. It lived in my palm the way a scar lives in skin — quiet, patient, and capable of reminding you at the worst possible moments that it was there at all. For six days since Thornwake it had done nothing but ache faintly, the way it always did when Serah and I argued about whether we should sleep in shifts (we should) or whether she was fit to stand watch with a wound in her side (she was not, and did anyway).

But tonight, as I sat sharpening a pencil I no longer strictly needed — old habits being the last things to die in a cartographer, after dignity and sleep — the map woke.

Light bled up through my skin. Not the searing white of the transfer in the valley, when the dying courier's blood had burned it into me. This was softer. Amber. The color of lamplight in a room you remember being happy in.

"Xanthe." Serah's voice from across the camp, low and immediate. She was on her feet with her hand on her broken blade before I could say a word, which was very Serah: ready to fight a map.

"It's all right," I said, and my voice came out strange, far away. "It's — showing me something."

"Showing you what?"

I didn't know yet. That was the thing about the map. It didn't show geography, not really, not anymore. It showed *memory*. I had learned that in Glasswind, when it had walked me through the first forging of the covenant — and the kneeling figure at the altar, the one I had hidden from Serah, the one whose face I still couldn't look at directly even in my own thoughts.

The amber light gathered, and then, hovering above my open palm in letters of fire and ink, words began to appear.

Not words meant for me.

Words meant for no one.

*My dearest Wren,* said the first line, in a hand I knew better than my own.

I stopped breathing.

*My dearest Wren. The valley holds. The seal holds. I have checked the anchor-stones twice and the third time for fear, which your mother always said was my defining feature. You are four months old and you have your grandfather's scowl, which I pray you outgrow, because it has brought nothing but trouble to this family.*

"Xanthe." Serah had crossed the camp and crouched beside me, and I felt her take my shoulder, and I could not turn my head. "Whose writing is that?"

"My father's," I said.

The letters kept coming. Letter after letter after letter, stacked in the air like pages shaken loose from a drawer, each one dissolving as the next took its place. My father's hand — Aldous Ravensworth, the man who had left the valley when I was six and never come back, the man whose abandonment I had carried like a stone in my chest for thirty years — writing to a wife and son he had, apparently, written to *constantly*.

Letters never sent. Letters that could never be sent, because the valley was sealed and he was outside it, and the seal that kept the world's magic out kept everything else out too. Even words. Even love.

*Wren has learned to crawl,* went a letter dated perhaps two years after the first. *I have this on the word of the courier, who describes it at length and with more enthusiasm than I strictly require, but I confess I read that passage eleven times. Eleven. A man who once redrew the borders of three realms in a single sitting, and I am reading about crawling eleven times.*

I laughed. It came out of me like something breaking.

Serah's grip tightened on my shoulder. "Xanthe—"

"He wrote to us." My voice cracked on the second word. "Every week. Every *week*, Serah. For — how long —"

The map answered, because the map had apparently decided tonight was the night for answering everything. The letters sped up, flickering faster, and I saw the dates. I saw the years stack up. Twenty years of letters. Twenty years of a man sitting down at the end of every week to tell a sealed valley about the weather in the lowlands, the price of wool, the creeping gray stain of Veylan Ash's armies on the horizon — and about a boy growing up that he could only love at a distance, through a courier, through glass.

*He maps things,* said a letter from my tenth year, and my laughter died in my throat. *The courier says he draws the valley. The whole valley, every stream and stone. Wren, I don't know whether to be proud or afraid. The gift is in the blood. The gift was always in the blood. And you know what the blood is for.*

I went cold.

*You know what the blood is for.* Five words, in my father's handwriting, and they hit me like the inquisitor's blade had nearly hit Serah in the marshes. Because I *didn't* know. That was the whole shape of my life: I knew I was a Ravensworth, I knew the valley was a prison I'd been born into, I knew my father had left and never returned, and I had resented all of it — the exile, the absence, the silence — as a boy resents a debt he never agreed to.

The letters kept coming, and they began, gently, terribly, to explain.

*Serah — no. Not her. Wren. My dearest Wren.*

*The covenant renews every generation. I have known this since I was your age, since my father sat me down at this same desk and told me in the same flat voice I am struggling not to use now. The Ember Throne does not run on prayer or on virtue. It runs on Ravensworth blood. One of us, at every renewal, given to the anchor-stones. That is the price of the seal. That is the price of every seal. The first bargain was struck with a life and every bargain since has been struck the same way, and the realms stay whole, and the free cities stay free, and no one outside this family ever has to know what the ink is made of.*

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightbird called, ordinary and indifferent.

"My father," I said, and had to start again. "My father was the sacrifice. Wasn't he. The last renewal."

Serah said nothing. She didn't have to. She had read enough of the letters over my shoulder, and she was a knight, and knights know what vows cost even when they've broken their own.

The map flickered, and the letters jumped forward — my father's hand shakier now, the dates closer together — and I understood that I was right, and that he had known it too, for years, the way a man with a wasting sickness knows it, quietly, while he goes on writing letters about the weather.

*The renewal comes in the spring. I have made my peace with it, or I tell myself I have, which may be the same thing. But I find I cannot stop doing the arithmetic. If the covenant takes me, it will not come due again until Wren is an old man. Perhaps an old man with grandchildren of his own. Perhaps the world will have found another way by then. Perhaps Ash will be dust and the Throne will crumble and some clever soul will redraw the whole covenant in a kinder hand.*

*Perhaps. But perhaps is not a plan, and I was never a man who could leave a map unfinished.*

*So here is the plan, Wren, and forgive me, forgive me, forgive me: I am going to lie to you.*

The camp seemed to tilt. Serah's hand was the only fixed point, and I gripped it without meaning to, hard enough that I probably hurt her, and she let me.

*When you are old enough to ask why I left, the courier will tell you I abandoned you. He has instructions to say it plainly and without excuse, because I know my son, and I know that if you believe I had a *reason* — a duty, a sacrifice, anything noble — you will go looking for that nobility. You will go looking for the Throne. And the Throne is a door that opens from your side, Wren, and once you walk through it, the covenant will know your name, and it will hold you to it, and I will have done nothing in my life but hand my son to the altar with a ribbon on him.*

*So hate me. It's the kindest thing I can give you. Hate me, and stay in the valley, and grow old mapping streams that never change, and let the Ravensworth line end in peace. The valley will keep you. It was sealed for you before you were born. Your mother and I built your cage with our own hands and called it a cradle, and I have never once stopped being ashamed of how grateful I am that we did.*

I couldn't read anymore. That is not a figure of speech; the letters blurred, and my eyes burned, and thirty years of resentment came apart in my chest like wet paper.

He hadn't left. He had *died* — given to the anchor-stones in the spring after I turned six, which was when the couriers stopped coming, which was when the valley went truly silent, which was the year I had decided, at six years old, that my father had chosen the world over me. I had built my whole self on that decision. The exile. The bitterness. The careful, armored way I loved no one and needed no one. All of it founded on a lie my father had *commissioned*, paid for with his own death, because he thought being hated would keep me safe.

"He let me hate him," I whispered. "On purpose. He *arranged* it."

Serah was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough in a way I'd only heard once before, the night she told me about the third realm. "That's not a lie, Xanthe. That's a wall. Some of us build them out of broken oaths. He built yours out of himself."

"And it worked," I said. "Gods help me, it worked. I never once looked for him. I never once asked why. I sat in that valley for thirty years being *angry*, and the anger was a lock, and I kept it locked myself." I looked down at my palm, at the map glowing there, patient as ever. "He built the cage and handed me the key and told me it was a stone."

*There is one more thing,* said the final letter, and the map saved it for last, because the map, I was beginning to understand, had a sense of drama that could only be hereditary. *If you are reading this, then the courier found you, and the map chose you, and my wall has failed — which means Ash is moving, and the covenant is failing, and the renewal has come due whether the world is ready or not.*

*I am sorry. I am so sorry. I wanted you to have thirty more years. I wanted you to die an old mapmaker in a valley with no magic and no wars and no throne, and I wanted the Ravensworth debt to die with me. But the blood remembers, Wren. The covenant keeps its ledger. And if the map has found you, then the ledger has found you too, and the final payment —*

The letter stopped. Not faded. *Stopped*, mid-line, the ink breaking off the way a sentence breaks when the hand writing it is no longer attached to a living man. The last renewal. The spring I turned six. My father, at an altar I had now seen in the map's memory, kneeling where his own father had knelt, giving the anchor-stones exactly what they asked for.

The light sank back into my palm and went out.

For a while, neither of us said anything. The fire ticked. Serah's breathing was slow and controlled, the breathing of a woman deciding what to say, which I had learned to recognize and dread.

"The final payment," she said finally.

"Don't."

"Xanthe."

"*Don't.*" I stood up, and my legs were unsteady, and I was furious, and the fury was the only thing holding me upright. "You want to say it? Fine. The final payment falls to me. The last Ravensworth. That's the arithmetic, isn't it? That's always been the arithmetic. One of us per renewal, and the renewal is *now*, and Ash is marching, and I am the ink." I laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "Do you know the worst part? I spent thirty years hating him for leaving. I built my entire *life* out of that hatred. And it turns out the hatred was a *gift*. It was the last thing he ever gave me, and it was a lie, and it was *kind*, and I don't know what to do with that. I don't know where to put it. You can't map a feeling, Serah. Believe me. I've tried."

"I know," she said quietly.

"And now the kindness is spent. The wall's down. The debt's come due. He bought me thirty years with his life and I've — what, wasted them? Hiding in the valley he died to keep me in?" My voice was rising and I couldn't stop it. "He'd have been better off telling me the truth. At least then I'd have had thirty years to *decide* something instead of—"

"Stop." Serah stood too, slowly, favoring her wounded side, and took me by both shoulders, and her grip was iron and her eyes were wet and furious. "You listen to me. Your father spent his death buying you time, and you did not *waste* it. You learned every stream in that valley. You learned to draw a world so true that a dying courier crossed a continent to put a living map in *your* hands. Whatever happens at that Throne — whatever the old bargain demands — you are not the ink, Xanthe. You are the *hand*. There is a difference, and I have broken one oath in my life, and it was because I finally learned that difference, and I will not watch you unlearn it."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to fall apart. I wanted, more than anything, to sit down and write a letter to a dead man, *my dearest father*, and tell him that the hatred was returned to sender, and that I understood, and that I forgave him, and that I was so angry about the forgiveness that I could hardly breathe.

What I said was: "The Ember Approach. How far?"

Serah held my eyes a moment longer, making sure I was steady. Then she released my shoulders and nodded west, toward the black line of the mountains, where the sky had begun — very faintly, very far away — to glow the color of a fire that never went out.

"Three days," she said. "Two, if we push. And Xanthe — look."

I looked. On the horizon, above the pass we would have to climb, the glow was spreading. And beneath my feet, faint as a heartbeat, the map pulsed once, twice, three times — like something counting down.
