## 15. The Fifth Realm: The Ember Approach

### Xanthe

The first refugees caught up with us at dawn on the second day.

I heard them before I saw them — the particular sound a large group of tired people makes when it is trying very hard to be quiet. Creaking cart axles. A baby that had learned, somewhere in its short and terrible education, that crying was expensive. The soft percussion of hundreds of feet on frozen mud.

Serah heard them too. She had my shoulder pinned to a fence post and her blade half-drawn before I'd finished saying "Do you hear—"

"Refugees," she said, after a long moment, and let me go. She sheathed the sword with the expression of someone disappointed not to be murdered. "From the lowlands. They're ahead of the fire."

"How far behind the fire?"

"Behind it." She said it flatly, but I'd learned by then to hear what she wasn't saying. *Not far enough.*

They came up the road out of the south in a straggling gray river: farmers with their livestock on ropes, a miller pushing a wheelbarrow of what I sincerely hoped were tools, an old woman carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, which she refused to explain to anyone. A boy of perhaps ten walked beside an ox and read aloud from a battered primer to keep himself awake. Behind them all, where the road bent down toward the plains, the sky was the color of a bruise going septic — orange at the seam, black above, and under it all a dull red pulse like something breathing.

Ash's vanguard. Burning the lowlands as it came.

"They'll slow us down," Serah said. It wasn't cruelty; it was arithmetic. She did arithmetic the way other people breathed.

"I know," I said.

"We have two days. Maybe less now, if we carry their children for them."

"I know," I said again, and started walking toward the column.

She caught my sleeve. "Xanthe. What are you doing?"

"What does it look like? I'm going to introduce myself to the man with the wheelbarrow. He looks like he has opinions about organization, and frankly we could use some."

For a moment she just stared at me, and I braced myself for the lecture — about exposure, about inquisitors, about how a man with a living map seared into his palm should move through a crowd like smoke and not like a town crier. But then something happened to her face that I had almost never seen happen to it. The corner of her mouth moved. Upward.

"You're going to get us all killed," she said, sounding, for the first time since I'd known her, like she might enjoy watching it.

* * *

His name was Doss Halloway, and he did indeed have opinions about organization, most of them delivered at volume. Within an hour of meeting him I had been conscripted into his informal government of the road — a cabinet consisting of himself (provisions), a midwife named Tansy (health), the old woman with the birdcage, whose name was Mother Quill and whose portfolio appeared to be *contradicting Doss*, and me, who had been assigned the title of "map-man" with no discussion whatsoever.

"There," Doss said, jabbing a thick finger at the fork ahead, where the road split around a burned-out tollhouse. "Left goes through the Fenlow cut. Right goes over the ridge. Which one?"

Everyone looked at me. I looked at the map.

The map, I should explain, had changed. Since Thornwake — since Serah had stood bleeding and victorious in front of her former sisters and refused, magnificently, to take any of them back — the thing in my palm had stopped being merely a record of realms and anchors. It had begun answering questions. Not in words. In pressure, in warmth, in the faint sensation of ink rearranging itself beneath skin, like a cat deciding whether to sit on your lap.

I held my hand flat over the fork and waited.

Warmth, leftward. A pulse of it, steady and sure, and underneath it something I can only describe as *satisfaction* — the feeling of a path being pleased to be chosen.

"Left," I said. "Through the cut."

"Left," Doss repeated, and turned and bellowed it down the column in a voice that had clearly spent decades directing harvest crews. "LEFT THROUGH THE CUT! MAP-MAN SAYS LEFT!"

"Does it always do that?" asked the boy with the primer, materializing at my elbow the way children do, without any regard for the fact that adults find this unnerving. His name was Pip. He had attached himself to me somewhere outside Fenlow Cross and showed no signs of detachment.

"Does what?"

"The hand thing." He pointed at my palm. "Going all glowy."

"It doesn't go glowy."

"It went glowy just now."

"That was reflected light off the snow."

"It's not snowing, and your hand is under your cloak."

I looked down. My hand was, in fact, glowing faintly under my cloak, in open defiance of everything I had said. I pulled the cloak tighter.

"It's a map," I told him, with the dignity available to a grown man losing an argument to a ten-year-old. "Maps know things."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Our priest said maps are pride. That wanting to know where you are is how the Adversary gets you."

"Where is your priest now?"

"Went back for his books." Pip said it simply, without drama, and somehow that made it worse than if he'd wept. "He said knowledge was worth the fire. I think maybe he was wrong, though. Because now nobody knows where anything is except you."

I didn't have an answer for that. So I put my glowing, prideful, possibly-damned hand on the boy's shoulder and steered him toward the front of the column, where Tansy the midwife was already scolding someone about wet boots.

* * *

We made the Fenlow cut by midafternoon, and that was where the fire caught sight of us.

I don't know how else to describe it. We came around the shoulder of the gorge and there it was below us — the plain we'd crossed two days ago, all twelve miles of it, burning in a line so straight and so patient it could only be deliberate. Ash's vanguard didn't rampage. It *mowed*. The flames advanced in ranks, orderly as soldiers on parade, and where they passed they left not ash but a kind of glassy smoothness, as if the land itself had been erased rather than destroyed.

At the head of the column, people began to stop. To turn. To look back at what they were walking away from, and to understand, all at once and together, exactly what it was.

Nobody screamed. That was the terrible part. There was just this long, collective silence, four hundred people breathing at once, and then Doss Halloway said, very quietly, "My mill's in that," and Mother Quill said, "Your mill was a debt trap and you know it," and the small human machinery of the column ground forward again, because what else was there to do.

Except that it wasn't only grief I felt, standing there on the gorge wall with the heat on my face. Underneath the grief, shameful and bright, was the map — and the map was *singing*.

It took me a moment to understand why, and when I did, I had to hold onto the rocks.

It wasn't singing about the fire. It was singing about the people. Four hundred heartbeats strung along the road behind me, and every single one of them, without knowing it, was pulling at the covenant's threads. Every vow made on this road — Doss organizing strangers, Tansy binding wounds, Mother Quill feeding people from a larder she'd carried twenty miles for no reason she could name — every small freely given thing hummed along the old lines like a plucked string. The covenant had been starved for centuries, fed only by blood and fear, and here was this ragged column of the displaced, accidentally playing it like a harp.

*Xanthe.* Serah's voice, close. She'd come up beside me without a sound, because of course she had. "You've gone strange. Stranger."

"The map likes them," I said. "The refugees. Listen—" I caught myself. She couldn't listen; only I could feel it. I tried again. "The covenant runs on vows. Freely given ones, now, since Hollowmere. And these people — they haven't stopped giving them. They share food with people they met three days ago. They carry each other's children. Nobody ordered it. Nobody's paying them." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Serah, they're doing it better than the realms ever did. The realms needed anchor-stones and altars. These people just... do it. On a road. In the cold."

Serah watched the column pass below us — the oxen, the carts, the boy reading aloud, the woman with the empty birdcage — and when she spoke, her voice had that rough quality it got when she was keeping something level by force.

"At the order," she said, "we had a saying. *Oaths are the bones of the world.* I believed it. I built my whole life on it, and when my oath broke, I thought I'd broken something in the world." She nodded down at the column. "But that's not bone, down there. Bones don't walk away from a fire and still feed each other. That's something else. Something that doesn't need an oath-blade to hold it up."

"Sinew," I offered.

"Don't ruin it."

"I'm a cartographer. Ruining things with terminology is my entire profession."

And there it was again — that almost-smile, gone as fast as it came, but real. She turned back toward the mountain. "Two days' climb from here. One, if the weather holds and the column can keep pace."

"The column will keep pace," I said. "Doss will see to it. Doss would see a glacier keep pace."

* * *

### Serah

I had forgotten what mountains did to hope.

On the flats, hope was a thing you could lose gradually, the way you lose a coin through a worn pocket — a little at a time, barely noticing. Mountains took it all at once. You crested a ridge expecting the summit and found another ridge, and another beyond it, each one white and silent and utterly indifferent to the fact that behind you the world was being erased by a patient man with an army.

By the second morning of the climb, the column had thinned in spirit even though it hadn't thinned in number. People still walked. But they walked like the map was a story someone had told them, and Xanthe — gods help me, the fool — kept stopping to tell it to them anyway.

I found him at noon on the second day, sitting on a rock above the trail with Pip beside him, holding his glowing hand out flat while a circle of maybe thirty refugees watched it the way country folk watch a diviner's rod.

"—and here," he was saying, tracing air above his palm, "this warm spot, that's the Ember Approach itself. The last realm. The throne's at the top of it, under the glacier, and the whole mountain is—" he paused, hunting for a word, "—*listening*. It's been listening for six hundred years. For anyone willing to come and speak to it."

A stocky woman near the front — Tansy, the midwife — folded her arms. "And when you speak to it, what then? Things change? Just like that?"

"Not just like that. It has to agree. And so do the realms. And so do—" he hesitated, and I knew what he wasn't saying: *the dead of Hollowmere, whose votes may decide everything.* "So does everyone it binds. That's the whole point. That's what's different about what we're trying to do. No more altars. No more payments. Just agreement, renewed every year, by everyone."

"And if it says no?" someone called from the back.

"Then we ask again next year," Xanthe said. "That's the deal. Forever, if it takes."

There was a laugh at that — tired, disbelieving, but real — and then Mother Quill's voice cut through it like a knife through broth: "Six hundred years of kings feeding people to a rock, and this one wants to *negotiate* with it." She shook her head at the sky. "Well. About time somebody tried manners."

I hung back at the edge of the little gathering, arms crossed, doing my job, which was watching the treeline and the ridgelines and the road behind, and pretending I wasn't listening to every word.

Here is what I would not have said to any of them, and what I will say now, to no one, in the privacy of my own skull: I had spent eleven years believing the world ran on broken things. Broken oaths, broken bodies, broken bargains — that underneath all the ceremony, power was just violence wearing nice clothes, and the only choice anyone got was which side of the violence to stand on. I had served Veylan Ash because he admitted it openly. I had hated him honestly, which is more than most people manage.

And now here was a soft-handed mapmaker telling a crowd of fleeing farmers that the world could run on *consent*, and worse — here were the farmers, nodding. Not the way crowds nod at prophets, hungry and hollow. The way they nodded at a plan. At work. At something with steps.

Tansy raised her hand like a schoolgirl, then seemed embarrassed and lowered it halfway. "My daughter's back in Bellharbor. If the council surrenders—"

"The council won't surrender," Xanthe said.

"You don't know that."

"No," he agreed. "I don't. But I know that if we reach the throne, surrender stops mattering. Ash's whole claim sits on the old covenant — the right of conquest, sealed in blood. Break the seal, break the claim. His armies aren't fighting for wages, Tansy. They're fighting because the covenant's stolen power makes them believe they can't lose. Take that away and they're just cold, tired men a long way from home."

"And you can do this?" the stocky woman pressed. "One man and a burnt knight and a magic hand?"

"One man, a burnt knight, a magic hand, four hundred refugees, a talking miller, a woman with an empty birdcage, and a boy who's read more of a primer than I ever finished," Xanthe said gravely. "Don't sell us short."

The laugh came again, bigger this time, and rolled down the column like water finding a channel, and I watched it go — watched tired faces turn forward instead of back — and understood suddenly what the courier had seen in this man, dying in a valley that forgot, and why he'd bled a living map into his palm rather than let it die with him.

Not power. Not destiny. The man made people feel *included* in their own rescue. It was the most dangerous talent I had ever seen, and I had seen men throw lightning.

* * *

We camped that night in the shadow of the last ridge, in a bowl of stone the locals had once called the Shepherd's Cradle, and for a few hours the column almost managed to be something other than afraid. Fires were lit — small ones, screened — and Doss produced, from the depths of his provisions cart, a barrel of salted plums that he distributed with the gravity of a man distributing crown lands. Someone had a fiddle. Someone else knew the song that goes with it, mostly.

I sat apart, as I sat apart every night, sharpening a blade I no longer technically owned, and Xanthe — as he did every night, because he had no sense of self-preservation or boundaries — came and sat down next to me anyway.

"You could join the song," he said.

"I could also set myself on fire. Both are equally appealing."

"The plum was good, though. Admit the plum was good."

"The plum was adequate."

He ate his in silence for a while. Below us the fires dotted the Cradle like fallen stars, and above us the glacier wall of the Ember Approach caught their light and gave it back redder, and somewhere past the ridge, faint but ceaseless, the lowlands burned.

"They're coming with us tomorrow," he said finally. "To the throne. Doss asked. Formally. Said the column votes to see it done."

"Xanthe—"

"I told them it might be nothing. I told them the mountain might reject the new terms, or the ghosts might vote against, or Ash might reach us first. I told them every way it could go wrong." He turned the plum stone over in his fingers. "And Doss said — and I quote — 'Course it might. Everything might. But we'd rather lose it standing at the top than hiding in a fen.' And then four hundred people cheered, and I didn't know what to say, so I said 'left through the cut,' which made no sense, and they cheered again."

Despite myself — despite eleven years of discipline and one broken oath and a scarred throat that had once sung orders I'll answer for until I die — I laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate in winter.

"They're not following you because you're the heir of the Ravensworths," I said. "You know that, don't you? Half of them don't even know what a Ravensworth is."

"I know."

"They're following you because you told them the truth and then asked instead of commanded. Do you understand how rare that is? How rare it's *always* been?" I looked at him — really looked, the way I hadn't let myself since the marshes, since the broken oath-blade, since the night I chose his cause over my redemption. "Kings don't ask. Prophets don't ask. Ash certainly doesn't ask. Six hundred years of covenant and not one soul on a throne ever thought to simply *ask*."

"Maybe that's why it needs remaking," he said quietly. "Maybe asking is the whole repair."

Below us, the fiddle stumbled, recovered, and swung into a faster tune, and a dozen exhausted voices picked it up badly and joyfully. Above us the glacier glowed its deep banked red, and against that glow, high on the ridge, I saw something that took the laugh out of my chest entirely.

Figures. Standing along the crest of the pass. Still as sentries, backlit by the fire-glow of the burning lowlands, and there were too many of them to be wind-carved snow, and they were too evenly spaced to be lost travelers.

I was on my feet with my hand on my hilt before my mind caught up. "Xanthe. The ridge. Now."

He followed my eyes. The music below faltered as others saw what we saw — the dark row of figures on the skyline, motionless, watching.

Then the foremost figure stepped forward to the edge of the cradle, and the firelight caught a banner unrolling from a spear: not Ash's black sun. A truce banner. White, edged in gold, ancient as the covenant itself.

And a voice came down the mountain, clear and cold and carrying, pitched — I realized with a chill — not for the four hundred, but for one man:

"Xanthe Ravensworth. The Usurper requests the courtesy of a parley. He offers safe conduct." A pause, precise as a knife laid on silk. "He asks that you bring the map. And he asks that you come alone — though he suspects," the voice added, almost gently, "that you won't."
