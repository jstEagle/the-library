## 18. Reforging

The Ember Throne did not look like a throne. That was the first thing that struck me, standing in the vast hall at the summit of the world with ash drifting down through a hole in the ceiling like gray snow. It looked, instead, like an altar that had been dressed up for a wedding by someone who hated weddings — all black stone and gold inlay and a basin at its heart that had once held blood and now held only dust.

I had imagined it a hundred times on the road. I had imagined it burning, because it was called the Ember Throne and maps teach you to trust names. But the fire had gone out of it centuries ago, and what remained was something worse than fire: furniture. The furniture of an argument that had been going on so long everyone had forgotten it was an argument at all.

"It's smaller than I expected," Serah said behind me. Her arm was bound where the inquisitor's blade had found her at the waystation, and she stood the way she always stood when something mattered to her — very still, as if stillness were a form of armor.

"Most terrible things are," I said. "That's how they get built."

The map was awake against my chest. I could feel it through my coat, a warmth like a sleeping animal, and when I drew it out it unrolled itself across my hands without being asked. The ink moved. It always moved now, but tonight it moved with purpose, rivers of light crawling across the parchment toward the center, toward the small black mark that represented this room, this moment, me.

Somewhere below us, down the mountain and across the lowlands, Veylan Ash's vanguard was burning the barley fields of the Ember Approach. We had climbed past the refugees for two days — old men carrying grandmothers, children carrying nothing because there was nothing left to carry — and every one of them had looked at me as though I were a ladder thrown down a well. I had smiled at them. I am not proud of how much effort those smiles cost.

"You're doing the face," Serah said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've already decided something terrible and you're rehearsing how to tell me."

I folded the map. "I'm going to accept his offer."

She didn't move. That frightened me more than shouting would have. Serah shouted the way other people breathed; her silence was a drawn blade.

"Xanthe—"

"He offered it under truce," I said quickly. "Renew the covenant with a Ravensworth as the sacrifice — me — and he spares the free cities. Every city. Bellharbor first." I thought of the council chamber, the merchants' faces, the way hope had flickered in them like a lamp in wind before they demanded proof I couldn't give. "It's a good bargain. It might be the only just one. My family built this cage. It seems fitting that a Ravensworth pays the last toll on it."

"Fitting," Serah repeated. She crossed the hall toward me, boots ringing on stone, and stopped close enough that I could see the exhaustion under the iron of her. "Do you know what I learned serving Ash, before I learned better? That tyrants never ask you to do evil. They ask you to do the *fitting* thing. The noble arithmetic. One life weighed against ten thousand. And the arithmetic is always correct, Xanthe, and it is always a lie."

"The covenant eats one innocent per generation," I said. "That's the system we'd be preserving. I know. I've known since the letters."

"And yet here you stand, volunteering to feed it one more time, which is exactly what it wants. Exactly what *he* wants. Ash doesn't fear your death, cartographer. Your death is his coronation. He gets his covenant renewed *and* the man who refused him destroyed by his own conscience. He couldn't have designed a better trap if he'd had a thousand years." A ghost of her old dry humor touched her mouth. "He may have had a thousand years. He doesn't strike me as someone who sleeps."

* * *

I walked to the throne alone. Serah let me, which I understood later was its own kind of trust — or its own kind of gamble — but in the moment it felt like abandonment, and I suppose both things were true.

The dais steps were worn hollow in two places, side by side, as though generations of Ravensworths had knelt in exactly the same spots. My father's letters had described this. *We kneel where they knelt,* he had written, *because the stone remembers the shape of us and will accept no other posture.* I had resented those letters for fifteen chapters of my life — for the exile, the valley, the sealed roads, the father who fled rather than tell me any of it — and only on the road, reading them by campfire while Serah pretended to sleep, had I understood that fleeing *had been* the telling. He had run from the valley to spare me the altar, and the valley's seal had kept me safe the way a lock keeps a prisoner safe, and I had spent thirty years calling it abandonment.

I knelt. Not in the worn hollows. Beside them.

The map unrolled across the altar stone and began to bleed.

Not blood — light, the same bleeding light the dying courier had clutched in the workshop a lifetime ago, back when I thought magic was something that happened to other people in other places. The light spread along the old lines of the covenant: five realms, five anchors, five threads converging on the throne. Glasswind's cracked singing canyons. Hollowmere's drowned parliament. Thornwake's thorned heart. Two more I had repaired or witnessed or mourned. And at every anchor, I could feel the covenant the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck — patient, ancient, hungry.

*I need ink,* it seemed to say. *You are the ink.*

"I know," I said aloud. My voice sounded very small in that enormous room. "That's the offer, isn't it? Blood for binding. It's always been blood. My family's whole legacy is a subscription paid in Ravensworths."

The light pulsed. Somewhere far beneath the mountain, I felt the anchors lean toward me like flowers toward a window.

"And here's my counteroffer."

I took out my pen.

It is a modest thing — crow-quill, walnut shaft, the nib worn crooked from thirty years of drawing coastlines nobody paid me for. In the valley I had used it to catalog a place that forgot itself, and it had felt like writing letters to no one. Now I dipped it in the map's own light, because I had no inkwell and the map had decided, apparently, to be helpful, and I began to draw.

I drew a new line beside the old one. Then another. I did not erase anything — you cannot erase a covenant written in the blood of generations, and I suspect trying would have taken my hand off at the wrist. Instead I did the one thing I knew how to do better than anyone alive.

I redrew the map.

Here is what I wrote, in the careful hand my father taught me, the hand that had once mapped a hidden realm for murderers and had spent three decades trying to earn forgiveness from no one in particular:

*Let the anchors be fed not by blood given unwillingly nor blood given nobly, for both are theft — the first steals the life, the second steals the choice. Let each anchor be fed by vows freely given by the living, renewed each year in assembly, in every realm, by every soul who wishes to give and none who are compelled. What is given freely cannot be stolen. What is renewed yearly cannot rot. What belongs to everyone requires no sacrifice, because everyone is already paying.*

My hand shook on the last line. Ink made of living light is heavier than it looks.

The realms resisted. I want to be honest about that, because the songs will probably skip over it. The covenant was old — older than my family, older than Ash, old enough that it had grown roots through the bones of the world — and roots do not surrender their soil politely. The line I drew bucked under my pen like a living thing. The light turned the color of an old bruise. The anchors slammed shut, all five at once, and the cold that came off them frosted my breath and cracked the flagstones around the dais.

*No,* said the covenant, in the wordless way stone says things. *This is not the terms. This has never been the terms.*

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Serah found me on my knees an hour later — or what felt like an hour; time moves strangely around altars — with my pen worn down to a stub and the new lines glowing fitful and weak, like a fire that cannot decide whether to live.

"They're refusing," I told her. "All five anchors. The vows won't take. I don't—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I rewrote the law of five realms and the realms have voted no. There's a lesson in humility somewhere in that."

"There's a lesson in everything if you stare at it long enough," Serah said, and then she went quiet, and I watched her do the arithmetic she hated. "The ghosts."

I looked up. "What?"

"Hollowmere. You promised them consent, not command, and they gave their blessing grudgingly — you told me that yourself, on the marsh road, when you were delirious with fever and I was pretending not to listen. Grudging blessings are worth more than joyful ones. Joy agrees with anything." She crouched beside me, wincing at her bandaged arm. "But the drowned realm has no anchor anymore. It was destroyed. Which means the new covenant needs a sixth thread, and a sixth thread means the old four-to-one balance breaks, and a broken balance means the vote is open."

"The vote isn't a vote," I said. "It's stone and blood and—"

"Xanthe." She put her good hand flat on the map, over Hollowmere's drowned mark, and the ink shivered. "You wrote *every soul who wishes to give.* Did you mean it, or did you mean every soul with a pulse?"

I stared at the map, at the small blue-gray smudge that was Hollowmere, and remembered the parliament of the dead — rows of translucent figures still arguing procedure after two hundred years, because the dead, I had learned, are the only citizens who never stop believing in process. They had refused the old covenant. *It abandoned us while we lived and wants our loyalty now that we're ghosts,* their speaker had said, in a voice like water over gravel. *Why should the drowned pay for the dry?*

And I had promised them. Standing knee-deep in ghost-light in a flooded assembly hall, I had promised that whatever came next would belong to everyone, and I had meant it, and I had not understood until this exact moment that meaning it was the whole mechanism.

"They cast the deciding votes," I whispered. "Because they're outside the old terms entirely. Because they were never fed on and never fed it. Because—" I started laughing again, properly this time, giddy and appalled. "Because the only voters with clean hands are the dead ones. That's either the most hopeful thing I've ever learned or the most damning, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Both," Serah said. "It's usually both."

So I called them. I pressed my palm to Hollowmere's mark and spoke their names — I had memorized the rolls in the flooded hall, all four hundred and twelve of them, because a cartographer's curse is that names stick — and the water of the drowned realm rose up through the map itself, through stone, through miles of mountain, and pooled on the Ember Throne's floor like moonlight spilled from a jug.

They came as reflections first. Faces in the puddle-light, hundreds of them, tilted upward. Then they gathered themselves into shapes, and the shapes arranged themselves into rows, and the rows became, unmistakably, a quorum.

The speaker stood foremost — a woman with kelp in what remained of her hair and the expression of someone who had chaired a difficult meeting and intended to chair a more difficult one.

"Cartographer," she said. "You rewrite the covenant. The realms refuse. And now you summon the dead to outvote the living. Explain why this is not simply the old theft wearing new clothes."

"Because," I said, kneeling among them, my ruined pen still in my hand, "the old covenant took. It took blood and called it duty. My new terms ask. And asking means accepting that the answer might be no — even from the dead. Even from you. Especially from you." I swallowed. "I'm asking. Will you join the covenant? Freely, once, and freely again each year, forever, with the right to walk away every single time?"

Silence. The kind of silence a parliament makes when it is actually considering something, which is the rarest and most beautiful silence in any world.

And then, one by one, four hundred and twelve drowned voices said *yes* — some warmly, some grudgingly, one with what I swear was a parliamentary objection attached — and the sound of it went through me like a bell through a chest of drawers.

The new lines caught.

Light roared up out of the map, out of the throne, out of the five stubborn anchors that had refused me all night, because the deciding votes had tipped the balance and stone, whatever else it is, respects a lawful majority. The covenant remade itself around me — I felt it pass through my body like a river changing course — and everywhere it went it left the new terms behind it, bright and strange and young: vows, freely given, renewable, revocable. No blood. No Ravensworths. No more worn hollows in the dais steps.

The basin at the heart of the throne filled — not with blood, but with light, thousands of tiny motes of it, and I understood with a jolt that each mote was a vow, arriving in real time. A fisherwoman in Bellharbor swearing by her nets. A glass-singer in Glasswind humming her consent. A Thornwake sister laying a sprig of rowan on her order's cracked anchor. Ordinary words, ordinary people, feeding the wards of the world one honest sentence at a time.

It was, without competition, the finest map I have ever drawn, and I hadn't drawn it alone, and that was precisely what made it work.

"Well," I said, sitting back on my heels, ink-stained and shaking and lighter than I had felt since the valley. "I believe that's the covenant reforged."

"Modest as ever," Serah said. But she was smiling — really smiling, the rare one, the one she rationed like a miser — and she reached down and pulled me to my feet.

Which is when the far wall of the hall exploded inward, and the temperature dropped as though winter had walked into the room wearing a crown, and I heard, cutting through the settling dust, a voice I knew from a parley tent and a nightmare:

"How touching. I do hate to interrupt a signing ceremony."

Veylan Ash stood in the breach, and behind him, bound in chains of shadow, floated the ghost of the covenant's first victim — the one whose freedom my new terms had just guaranteed.

Her eyes, as they turned toward her captor, had already begun to burn.
