## 20. What Justice Costs

The Ember Throne had stopped being an altar and become a courtroom, which was to say: it had become a place where people argued.

Xanthe noticed this on the third morning after the breaking of Ash, when he came down from the guest quarters he'd been given — quarters with a bed in them, an actual bed, the first in months — and found the great hall full of folding chairs. Someone had carried up hundreds of them from the refugee column. They were arranged in rows facing a plain wooden table where three clerks sat with ink pots and long rolls of paper, and above them, where Veylan Ash's banners had hung, someone had strung laundry. It was drying there. Nobody seemed to think this was strange except him.

"It's practical," Serah said, when he mentioned it. She was propped against a pillar with her arm bound to her chest, looking thinner than she had any right to look after surviving what she'd survived, and eating an apple with the concentration of a woman who intended to finish it. "You can't hold a reckoning in a room that still smells like the old one."

"The old one smelled like ash."

"Exactly." She took another bite. "Laundry smells like Tuesday. Like people who expect to have Tuesdays again."

He'd learned, over the miles between Bellharbor and here, not to argue with Serah when she was being practical. It was a losing battle and she enjoyed winning it too much.

* * *

They had called them truth-hearings, and the name had been Xanthe's, though he'd offered it half-apologizing, the way you offer something you're afraid is too small for the occasion. The councils of the free cities had wanted trials. Of course they had. Trials had verdicts, verdicts had sentences, sentences had endings you could point to afterward and say *there, that's done.* A trial was a map with clean borders. He understood the hunger for it better than most; he'd spent his whole life drawing clean borders over places that refused to stay clean.

But under the new covenant, justice wasn't something a magistrate could hand down from a throne. It had to be assembled, the way his new maps were assembled — from testimony, freely given; from consent, freely withheld. So instead of trials there were hearings, held in the open air of the throne hall, where anyone who had been wronged could come and speak, and anyone accused could come and answer, and no one left with a sentence but everyone left with witnesses.

It was slower than a trial. Gods, it was slower. Some hearings ran three days. Some ran three weeks. There was a woman from the lowlands who had lost two sons to Ash's levies and who came back every single day to hear the testimony of the men who had conscripted them, and she never spoke, never wept, just sat in the third row with her hands folded, listening, as though grief were a subject she intended to master completely before she allowed herself to graduate from it.

"You could hurry them along," a Bellharbor merchant suggested to Xanthe, gently, in the way merchants suggest things. "Set limits. Appoint judges. People are tired."

"They're tired," Xanthe agreed. "And if we appoint judges, in ten years those judges will be the ones deciding who counts as a person, and we'll have built Ash's machine again with nicer paint." He'd said it more sharply than he meant to, and then apologized, and the merchant had accepted the apology with the grace of a man who had made his fortune knowing which apologies to accept.

That was the thing nobody warned you about remaking the world: afterward, everyone wants you to be finished. The war ends and they want the peace to arrive the way bread arrives at a bakery — warm, sliced, on schedule. But justice outside the law didn't come out of an oven. It came out of rooms full of tired people telling each other the truth, badly, at length, with interruptions. It was the slowest magic he knew.

Some wrongs, he learned, could not be undone at all. This was the lesson the hearings taught most stubbornly. You could return a farm. You could not return a father. You could burn the ledgers of the inquisition, and they did, in a ceremony that Serah attended and described afterward as "satisfying in the way that hitting your thumb is satisfying, briefly, and then your thumb still hurts." What remained, when restoration ran out, was witnessing. A clerk wrote it all down. The woman in the third row listened. And somehow — Xanthe watched it happen enough times to believe it — being witnessed was not nothing. It was not a sentence. It was not even comfort, exactly. But a man who had tortured prisoners in Glasswind stood in front of the survivors of that torture and said their names aloud, each one, from memory, because the covenant required him to say them and because saying them cost him something visible, and one of the survivors, an old glassblower with burned hands, stood up at the end and said, "I don't forgive you. I want that written down too. But now you know my mother's name, and before today you didn't, and that's worth more than your hanging would have been."

The clerk wrote both statements down. Both went into the record. Neither erased the other.

Xanthe thought about that old glassblower for days.

* * *

Serah was healing, though she pretended not to be. The wound at the gate had been bad — bad enough that for two days he had sat beside her cot doing sums in his head to keep from thinking, and the sums hadn't helped, and she'd woken on the third day and immediately complained about the porridge, which was how he'd known she'd live. Now she walked the halls of the throne complex every morning, grimly, like a soldier serving a sentence of convalescence, and she interrogated him at dinner with the same rigor she'd once used testing him on the moor road.

"Have you eaten anything today that wasn't ink?"

"I had bread."

"When?"

"...This morning."

"That was yesterday's morning. Sit down. There's stew."

She cooked, now that her arm worked well enough to hold a knife. It turned out the oathbreaker knight of the third realm's purge could make a passable venison stew, and she guarded the recipe like a state secret, and Xanthe suspected — though he'd never say it aloud, because she'd deny it with violence — that feeding him was how she took care of things she couldn't bring herself to talk about. She talked plenty about everything else. About the order, about Thornwake, about the sister she'd beaten in ritual combat and then embraced anyway, which had scandalized everyone including, visibly, the sister. But about the gate — about the hours she'd held it while the new covenant ignited and her blood soaked into the stones — she said only, "I held it," and then asked if he wanted more salt.

One evening, watching her ladle stew with her off-hand because the other was still splinted, he almost told her where he was going the next day. Almost. Then he looked at her face, at the careful blankness she wore around anything that smelled like penance, and decided some pilgrimages you had to take alone.

* * *

The first victim's name was Ysolde Marran.

He had learned it the way he'd learned most things lately: slowly, and against his will. For the whole journey she had been *the bound ghost*, *the first victim*, *the leverage* — a shape in Ash's keeping, a horror shown to him across a parley table like a coin held out to a child. Ash had kept her name deliberately, Xanthe realized later. A prisoner without a name is a thing. Ash had needed her to remain a thing, because the moment she became a person, the moment anyone asked *who was she, what did she love, why her* — the whole architecture of the covenant's founding began to creak.

The new covenant had freed her. That was in the terms Xanthe had written, or rather insisted on, over the objections of realms that feared what a freed ghost might do: *no soul shall be bound in service of the anchors, and all souls so bound are released, with witness and honor.* The ghosts of Hollowmere had voted for it. Their parliament of the dead had debated for a night and a day, and then the Speaker — a drowned harbor-master named Pell, who had a voice like water going over stones — had risen and said, "We know what it is to be used by the living. Free her. And write down her name."

Ysolde Marran. A shepherd's daughter from the first realm, taken eight hundred years ago to be the covenant's first payment. His family's first payment. The first Ravensworth sacrifice had not been a Ravensworth at all — that was the lie his father's letters had softened, the comfortable fiction that the price had always fallen on the willing line. No. At the first forging, when the realms were burning and the founders needed blood to seal the bargain, they had not yet learned to spend their own. They had spent hers.

He found her on the mountain below the throne, in a meadow of late-blooming crocus, because of course that was where a freed ghost would go. Not the hall. Not the record rooms. Up. Into the wind and the open light, where nothing could be built on top of her.

She was easier to see than he expected. The Hollowmere ghosts had been pale, translucent, sorrowful as fog. Ysolde was solid-seeming, dark-haired, dressed in wool that had gone out of fashion before any kingdom he'd ever mapped existed. She was sitting on a rock, and she was — he stopped at the edge of the meadow, uncertain — she appeared to be braiding grass.

"You're the mapmaker," she said, without turning. Her voice was ordinary. That undid him more than a haunting voice would have. Eight hundred years of bondage and her voice sounded like a woman asking about weather.

"Xanthe Ravensworth."

"Yes." She finished the braid, set it on the rock beside her — there were others there, a dozen little braids, laid in a row like offerings or like counting — and turned to look at him. Her eyes were brown and level and entirely unimpressed. "I've been waiting to see whether you'd come. Some of you don't. The founders never did."

"No," he said. "I don't imagine they did."

"They sent clerks. Afterward, in the early years, clerks would come up the mountain with documents for me to witness. As if witnessing were a favor they were granting *me*." She patted the rock beside her, an invitation so casual it made his chest ache. He sat. The stone was cold through his coat, or perhaps it wasn't, or perhaps ghosts made their own weather. "So. You rewrote it. The covenant."

"I did."

"Without blood."

"Freely given vows. Renewed yearly. By assembly." He recited it like a man reciting his own confession, which in a way he was. "Consent instead of—"

"I know what you wrote. I read it." She raised an eyebrow. "Yes, ghosts can read. You'd be astonished what we can do with the time." And there it was — gentle humor, dry as the moor road, and Xanthe felt something in his shoulders loosen for the first time since the throne. "I'm not here to thank you, cartographer. Don't make that face. I'm grateful. Gratitude and thanks are different things. Thanks is a debt paid. Gratitude is a debt remembered." She picked up another blade of grass. "I'm here because you're going to carry this the rest of your life, whatever it is you're carrying, and I know what that weight does to people, and I'd rather you heard it from me than from the quiet voice in your own head at three in the morning."

"What does it do?"

"It makes you think the fixing was yours. That the cost was yours. That the story is yours." She snapped the grass cleanly. "Eight hundred years, boy, and do you know what I remember most? Not the binding. Not the altar. I remember my sister coming up the hill to tell me our dog had puppies. That's what they took from me — not my death, my *Tuesday*. Everyone thinks the crime is the big dramatic thing. The crime is that I never got to see the puppies."

Xanthe laughed, and then the laugh caught in his throat and became something else, and Ysolde Marran watched him come apart on her mountainside with the patience of someone who had waited eight hundred years and could wait out one cartographer's tears without difficulty.

When he could speak again, he said, "Your name is in the record. First entry. Before the founders' names."

"Good," she said simply. "Keep it there. And here's the rest of what I came to say, so listen, because I won't say it twice." She turned to face him fully, and for a moment the shepherd's daughter was gone and something older looked out — not a weapon, not anymore, but a woman who had seen the whole architecture of power from underneath. "Justice outside the law is slower and harder and it will never once feel finished. You'll wake up some mornings and think, a trial would have ended this. A rope would have ended this. And you'll be right — it would have *ended* it, the way a landslide ends a conversation. Don't you dare envy the landslide." She tapped his knee with one insubstantial finger. "You gave the realms a harder thing than victory. You gave them homework. Every year, the assembly. Every year, the renewing. Every year, some tired clerk writing down somebody's grief. They'll curse you for it within a decade. Let them. Cursing the mapmaker means they're using the map."

"And you?" he managed. "What happens to you now?"

"Now?" She looked out over the valley below the throne, where the refugee camps were becoming towns, where the laundry lines of the free cities' levies fluttered among the blackened banners. "Now I'm going to stay a while. Watch the assemblies. Make sure they don't get lazy." A pause, and then, drier: "And eventually I'm going to find out what ghosts do when they're finally nobody's business. I'm told it involves travel. I've never traveled. I was taken at nineteen and I've been on this mountain for eight hundred years, and I find I have opinions about that."

"I could draw you a map," Xanthe said, and then caught himself, because the original map was dying with the old covenant, and the new cartography was barely begun, and he had promised himself — had he not? — that he would stop offering people things he hadn't built yet.

But Ysolde smiled like a woman accepting a promise she intended to collect on. "Yes," she said. "Draw me one. Take your time. Do it properly. That's rather the whole point of you, isn't it?"

* * *

He walked down the mountain in the gold end of the afternoon, past the crocuses, past the first hearing-tents of the autumn assembly already rising in the fields, and at the bottom of the path he found Serah waiting, arms crossed, splinted arm and all, wearing the expression of a woman who had known exactly where he'd gone and had decided, magnanimously, not to follow.

"Ysolde Marran," she said.

"You knew."

"I keep records. Old habit." She fell into step beside him, and for a while they walked without speaking, which was its own kind of conversation between them by now. Then: "Was it what you needed?"

"It was what I didn't know I needed. Which I believe is your definition of a good teacher."

"Hm." She glanced sideways at him. "There's a letter came for you. From Bellharbor. The council — the new council, the one that wouldn't take your word without proof — they're convening the first annual assembly in spring. They want the man who wrote the covenant to attend. Formally. With honors." Her mouth twitched. "There's also a second letter. Thinner. Different hand."

He took it. The thin one. He broke the seal standing there in the road, and read it twice, and felt the ground tilt gently beneath him in the way it always did when the past reached forward and tapped him on the shoulder.

It was from the steward of his valley. Or what had been his valley. The seal, the steward wrote, was thinning ahead of schedule — faster than the new covenant should allow, faster than any model predicted. Roads were appearing on the far side that weren't on any map. Travelers were already drifting in, confused, calling it by an old name Xanthe hadn't heard since childhood. The valley, the steward wrote, was remembering itself. And it was asking, in the way valleys ask, for its cartographer to come home.

Serah watched him fold the letter. "Well?" she said.

"Well," Xanthe said, and found he was smiling, "it appears I'm going to need a map of a place that doesn't exist yet."
