## 1. The Keeper of Small Charms

The trouble with selling charms in a city where magic is a hanging offense is that you have to be very good at pretending your wares are worthless.

August Okafor had gotten good at it. Seven years good. She could hold a luck-knot up to the light, let a customer squint at the cheap brass wire and dyed thread, and say, "Honestly, it's just to make you feel better," in a voice so flat and bored that the customer always bought two. The trick wasn't hiding the magic. The trick was hiding that there was anything worth hiding.

"Three coppers," she said to the baker's wife, who came every second Tuesday for a headache charm and every fourth Tuesday for something she never named out loud, just pressed her hand flat against her chest and looked at August with wet, hopeful eyes.

"Three coppers," the baker's wife agreed, and counted them out one at a time, as if the ceremony of it mattered, and maybe it did. "You're a saint, Okafor. A little saint."

"A little fraud, more like." August tied the charm in brown paper, the way you'd wrap a fish. "It's thread and wire and a prayer I half remember. If it works, it works because you believe it will."

"And if it works because I believe it will," the baker's wife said, taking the parcel, "then it works."

August had no argument for that. She never did. That was the whole architecture of the thing, the whole beautiful rotten scaffolding of it: in the city of Corvale—named, naturally, for the family that had ruled it for a hundred years and outlawed the thing she sold in paper parcels—belief was the only magic a person was allowed. So she sold belief, licensed and taxed and stamped with the inquisitors' lead seal, and underneath the belief, folded so small you'd miss it, she sold the other thing.

Not much of it. A thread of warmth in a fever charm. A whisper of steadiness in a sailor's knot. Enough to keep her hand practiced, no more. A musician who never plays forgets her scales, and August Okafor had no intention of forgetting hers.

The bell over the door chimed and the baker's wife went out into the grey morning, and August stood alone in her shop with its shelves of harmless nonsense and its single, carefully unremarkable floorboard, and she was content. She told herself she was content. She had gotten very good at that, too.

* * *

The shop was called Underhill's, which was a joke she'd never explained to anyone, because explaining it would require admitting she'd named it after herself, and admitting that would require admitting she had a sense of humor, and admitting *that* would require having someone to admit it to.

She opened the shutters. The street outside was doing its morning arithmetic: carts and costermongers and a pair of inquisitors walking their rounds in grey coats, their boots making that sound she'd learned to sleep through and never stopped hearing—*clack, clack, clack*, like a slow metronome counting down something. The inquisitors didn't look at her shop. They never did. That was the other half of the architecture. A charm-seller with a clean license and seven years of dull, punctual tax payments was furniture. Furniture didn't get hanged.

She put the kettle on. She watered the sad fern in the window that refused, out of what she suspected was pure spite, to die. She took down her ledger and made the day's first entry in a hand so deliberately plain it looked like a child's: *Tues. 3 charms sold. 1 headache, 1 luck, 1 unlisted.*

The unlisted one was the baker's wife. It was always the baker's wife.

At noon a boy came from the lamplighters' guild with a note she'd paid him a copper a week to deliver, rain or shine, whether it held a message or not—a note with nothing on it but a smudge of ash, which meant *nothing to report, all quiet, all sealed.* She'd been sending that same nothing to a man named Doss at the inquisitors' liaison office for seven years, and Doss had never once opened it, because a charm-seller who reports nothing is a charm-seller who is watched by no one, and August had spent a great deal of money and more patience making sure that was true.

She burned the note in the stove and felt the small, familiar satisfaction of a system working. Then she locked the shop, turned the sign, and went down into the dark.

* * *

The floorboard lifted on iron hinges she'd oiled that morning, because she oiled them every morning, because a squeaking hinge is the kind of small failure that kills careful people. Below it, a ladder went down into a brick throat, and the throat went down farther than any cellar in Corvale had a right to go, down past the water lines and the old plague crypts and the foundations of the founding dynasty's first palace, which everyone knew had burned in the war, which everyone knew had been a terrible war, which everyone knew had been worth it, because that was what everyone knew, and August had spent seven years not thinking too hard about what everyone knew.

At the bottom, the Ember Gate.

She'd never found words for it that didn't sound like either a madwoman's diary or an inquisitor's confession, so mostly she didn't try. It was a door. That was the honest word. A door of black metal shot through with veins of dull red, standing free in a vaulted chamber the size of a chapel, no wall around it, no frame, just a door standing upright in the middle of the air's dark, and it was warm. It had always been warm. She pressed her palm flat against it every night the way you'd check a child's forehead, and every night it answered with that low, banked heat, like a hearth in the next room, like something alive and patient on the far side of a wall.

The ward-lines she'd laid in chalk and salt and her own blood, renewed weekly, were the only reason the warmth stayed banked. She walked them now, as she did every night, checking each anchor-point with her fingertips. North anchor, sound. East anchor, sound. The chalk had gone grey at the western anchor—she knelt and redrew it, murmuring the sealing words her mentor had taught her, words in no language she could name but which her mouth had somehow known the first time she spoke them, the way a mouth knows a hymn.

*"Ember, keep. Ember, sleep. What was given, what was taken, let it lie."*

She said it the way she said it every night, and she felt, as she felt every night, the Gate's heat dip by a degree, obedient, drowsy, held.

Seven years of this. Seven years of chalk and salt and blood, of the ladder and the ledger and the lie about the war, of pressing her palm to a door that no one else in the world knew she kept. She was thirty-one years old and she had spent a third of her life being the only person in Corvale who knew the Ember Gate was real.

Her mentor, Maren Cole, had kept it before her. Maren had kept it for thirty years before that, alone, and had died on the inquisitors' scaffold rather than give up its location, and her last words to August—shouted from the cart, actually, which was not the dignified transmission of sacred office one might hope for—had been: *"Keep it shut, girl. Whatever knocks, whatever calls, whatever it promises—keep it shut."*

So August kept it shut. She was good at it. She was, in fact, the best in the world at it, because she was the only one doing it, and some nights that thought arrived in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, and she had to sit on the cold floor with her back against the warm door and wait for the well to finish swallowing.

Tonight was one of those nights.

She sat. The vault was silent in the particular way that deep places are silent—not quiet, but *listening*. The Gate breathed its heat against her spine. Somewhere above her, muffled through forty feet of brick and earth, the city went about its evening: bells, cartwheels, a drunk singing something about a girl from the salt towns. August pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them and let the loneliness have her for exactly as long as she'd trained herself to allow—ten breaths, no more—and then she stood, and brushed off her skirts, and told herself, in the flat, bored voice she used on customers, that she was content.

She was a woman of routine. She liked routine. Routine was a wall you built out of days, and if you built it thick enough, nothing got through.

She walked the ward-lines one last time. She pressed her palm to the Gate.

And the Gate pressed back.

* * *

It had never done that.

Seven years. Two thousand five hundred and some nights of palm to metal, of banked and drowsy heat, of a door that was a door and nothing more. And now, under her hand, the Ember Gate pushed—gently, the way a sleeper pushes at a blanket—against her palm, and the veins in the black metal flushed from dull red to a bright, living ember-orange, and the warmth that had always been a hearth in the next room became a hearth in the room, and then a forge, and August snatched her hand back and stumbled and caught herself on the eastern anchor and felt the ward-line *sing* under her fingers like a plucked wire.

"Ember, keep," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "Ember, sleep. What was given—"

The Gate flared.

Not open. Not yet. But the light of it went through her like a shout goes through a quiet house, up the ladder, through the floorboard, out into the shop, out into the street, and August knew—she knew with the cold, total certainty of a woman whose whole life is built on nothing getting through—that something had gotten through. That something had seen. That somewhere above her, in the grey lamplit streets of Corvale, in an archive or a barracks or a grey-coated office, something had just turned its head.

The Gate settled. Slowly. Reluctantly, if a door could be reluctant. The veins dimmed from ember to dull red to that old banked glow, and the heat withdrew to a hearth-in-the-next-room, and the vault was silent again, listening.

August stood with her back against the cold wall, her heart going like a hammer, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for seven years.

Maren had kept the Gate thirty years without a single flare. Maren's mentor, the old records said, had kept it fifty. And on the seventh anniversary of the night Maren died—on this night, of all nights, when August had walked the lines and said the words and done everything right, everything *exactly* right—the Gate had reached for her like a hand reaching out of a dream.

Doors don't do that. Doors don't *want* things.

Unless something on the other side was asking. Or something on this side was answering. Or—her mouth went dry—unless the Gate had finally noticed that the woman keeping it shut was the last of a line of one, and had begun, in its patient, burning way, to wonder what would happen when there was no one left to say *keep*.

She climbed the ladder. She lowered the floorboard. She sat in her dark shop with the sad fern and the shelves of harmless nonsense, and she did not light a lamp, and she did not make an entry in the ledger, and she did not send Doss his smudge of ash, because for the first time in seven years she had something to report and would have hanged before reporting it.

Outside, the lamplighters were making their rounds. Somewhere a drunk was still singing about the girl from the salt towns. The city was settling into its evening the way it had settled into a hundred years of peace built on a war everyone knew had been worth it.

And in her chest, under the fear, under the weariness, under the contentment she wore like a coat, August felt something she hadn't felt since Maren's cart had rolled away from the scaffold: the thin, terrifying, unmistakable sensation of a story starting.

She told herself it was nothing. She was very good at telling herself things.

The Gate stayed warm all night, and for the first time, she left her palm on it until she fell asleep.
