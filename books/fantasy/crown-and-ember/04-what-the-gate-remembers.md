## 4. What the Gate Remembers

**August**

The vault swallowed them whole.

One moment they'd been standing in her shop—Wren Vale clutching a stolen page of prophecy like it might bite, August halfway through telling him to leave and never come back—and the next the floor had opened beneath them like a mouth. Not falling, exactly. Being *pulled*. The Gate's light had flared up through the floorboards, through the cellar hatch she kept locked with three charms and a lie, and then there was no shop at all, no city, no anything but ember-colored dark.

She landed hard on stone that was warm as skin.

"August." Wren's voice came out thin. He was on his knees beside her, one hand pressed flat to the floor as if checking whether the world still existed. "What is this place?"

"My workplace," she said, because her mouth had always defaulted to jokes when her hands were shaking. "The other one."

The vault was a sphere of black granite, smooth as an egg's interior, maybe forty feet across. No door. No stairs. The only light came from the far wall, where the Ember Gate stood in its frame of fused iron and glass—a doorway of slow-burning gold, sealed behind seven interlocking bands of warding script that pulsed like a heartbeat. Her heartbeat. She'd known that for seven years. She'd never told anyone.

Seven years, and tonight it had reached up through forty feet of rock and *grabbed* someone.

"It's never done that before," she said quietly. "In seven years, it has never once reached for me."

"Maybe it wasn't reaching for you."

She looked at him. He was still holding the codex page, crumpled now, and in the Gate's light his face had gone the color of old paper. A junior archivist with ink-stained fingers and a scholar's stubborn chin. Nothing about him said *chosen*. But nothing about her did either, and the Gate had just proved how much its opinion mattered.

"The date," he said. "The codex named tonight. It described—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It described your scar. Left temple, curving down like a struck match. I thought I was reading a coincidence. I thought—"

"Prophecies don't do coincidences." August got to her feet. Her legs felt borrowed. "Come here. Slowly. And whatever happens, don't touch the wards."

He came. Of course he came; he was the sort of man who walked toward the thing that frightened him, which August suspected was either very brave or very stupid, and she was beginning to suspect it was both.

They stood ten feet from the Gate. Up close, the gold light wasn't steady—it moved, layered, like looking into deep water where something enormous turned over far below. The warding bands hummed a note she felt in her teeth.

"I've stood here every night for seven years," she said. "I've recited the sealing liturgy four thousand times. It has never once looked back at me."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's been waiting for an audience."

The Gate flared.

Not the gentle pulse she knew. This was a roar of light, silent and total, and the vault walls went transparent—not breaking, just *becoming*—and August Okafor fell out of her own body into somewhere else entirely.

* * *

**Wren**

He was standing on a hillside under a sky the color of a bruise, and the grass beneath his boots was burning.

He knew this battle. Every child in the Nine Cities knew this battle—the Field of Ashen Kings, where the first King Aldric had broken the Mage-Lords' host and ended the War of Cinders. There was a painting of it in the Archive's great hall, forty feet wide: Aldric on his white horse, sword raised, fire rolling off the enemy lines like a tide going out. Wren had copied that painting's inscription onto an exam paper when he was nineteen. *Here magic was defeated, that men might be free.*

Except.

Except the army on the burning hillside was flying *Aldric's own banners*, and the men they were slaughtering wore no armor at all.

Wren tried to run toward them and couldn't—he had no body here, only attention, only witness. He was a point of sight hanging above a battlefield that history had gotten exactly backwards. Below, farmers and weavers and two children, gods help him, two children among them, stood in a ragged line against a wall of professional soldiers, and the soldiers advanced with the unhurried confidence of men doing a chore.

And the soldiers' captain—the man on the white horse, the face that would be painted and carved and sung for a century—turned and looked directly up at Wren.

Not at the sky. At *him*. As if he could feel the century between them like a pane of glass.

"This is how kingdoms are made," the captain said, and his voice carried impossibly, the way things spoke inside dreams. "Not won. *Made.* Write it down, archivist. Someone will need to unwrite it later."

Then the vision folded like paper, and Wren was falling again.

* * *

**August**

The second vision put her in a war room.

She recognized it from engravings—the Council Chamber at Crownspire, the long table, the map of the Nine Cities pinned under iron weights. Around the table sat the founders: Aldric, his brother Maren, the general Corvane-the-Elder whose name the Inquisition still carried like a relic. August hovered near the ceiling, bodiless, watching them plan the end of the world.

Only they weren't planning a war. They were planning a *story*.

"There aren't enough mage-lords left to be a threat," Maren was saying, tapping the map. "We killed most of them ourselves. The rest are farmers now. Weavers."

"Which is precisely the danger," Aldric said. He was younger than his statues, softer, with the restless eyes of a man who'd won something he didn't know how to hold. "Magic isn't in the blood, brother. We've always known that. It's in everyone—it wakes in the miller's daughter same as the king's son. Which means every household in the realm is an armory, and every armory is a threat to the crown."

"So we take the armories."

"We take the *magic*." Aldric leaned over the map, and August watched his finger trace the lines of the Gate-shrines, all nine of them, the sister-doors that had stood open since before anyone remembered. "The shrines are conduits. Always have been. We seal them, we build the siphons beneath, and everything the people's blood would have woken flows instead into the vaults under Crownspire. Into *us*. One generation, and no commoner will ever feel so much as a candle-flame in their palms. Two generations, and they'll believe they were born empty."

"And the ones who remember?" the old general asked. "The ones who felt it once?"

"They become the story." Aldric smiled, and it was the smile from the paintings, the one every schoolchild learned to revere, and August felt something in her chest turn over and die. "We give them a war. A terrible war, fought by heroes, ended by sacrifice. Magic caused it, we'll say. Magic must never rise again. Grief is the best mortar there is, gentlemen. Build a wall out of a graveyard and no one will ever ask what's buried underneath."

The vision held on his face a moment too long. Long enough for August to understand, with a vertigo that had no bottom, that she was not being shown a secret.

She was being shown the *foundation*.

Everything stood on it. The law that hanged magicians. The Inquisition. The curfews, the licenses, the little brass charm-seller's badge she'd worn for seven years like a leash she'd made peace with. Seven years she had guarded the Gate believing she was keeping a lid on a weapon. She had been a lock on a theft. She had been guarding the *loot*.

The room dissolved. She screamed without lungs, without sound, and fell.

* * *

**Wren**

There were more visions. He lost count after the ninth.

A staged massacre, the "Mage-Lord atrocity" that justified the first Purges—staged by the crown's own soldiers wearing stolen robes, the bodies arranged with a director's care while a court painter sketched from the ridge. A forged treaty, inked in three different hands and aged with smoke. A hundred years of chronicles being written to order, archivists like himself bent over desks, copying lies until the lies grew calluses and passed for bone.

But the vision that broke him was small.

A kitchen. A woman grinding flour, humming. Her daughter, six years old, making the hearth-fire dance with a laugh—little ribbons of flame rising off the coals like startled birds, because that was what children's magic looked like before it was trained or beaten out of them. The mother caught the girl's hands gently and said, *Not where anyone can see, my love. Not anymore.*

That was all. That was the whole vision. A mother teaching her child to hide, in the first year of the peace, in the first year of the freedom.

Wren had spent his life in the Archive. He believed in documents the way priests believed in gods—that truth lived in them, that enough careful reading would redeem any lie. And now he hung above a kitchen and understood that the Archive itself was the crime. Not a library. A *laundry*. A place where the dynasty washed its blood out of its clothes, year after year, until even the scholars forgot the stains had ever been there.

He had spent six years cataloguing the usurper's history. He had been proud of it. He had been, without knowing it, a janitor of the forgery.

When the kitchen faded, he found himself falling alongside someone else—August, her form flickering at the edges like a bad print, and their eyes met in the space between visions, and neither of them needed to speak. The Gate was showing them the same wound from two sides. She had guarded the door. He had dusted the shelves. And the door and the shelves had been built by the same thieves.

* * *

**August**

The last vision was the worst, because it was almost kind.

She stood in a field at dusk—no battle, no council chamber, just grass going gold at the edges—and people were coming out of their houses. Hundreds. Thousands. Ordinary faces, miller's and mason's and midwife's, walking toward nine distant points on the horizon where lights burned like low suns. The shrine-gates, open. Magic moving through the world like weather, waking in open palms, and nobody hiding, nobody hanging, nobody teaching their daughters to be small.

It lasted perhaps ten seconds. Then it guttered like a lamp out of oil, and August understood that she hadn't seen the past or the future.

She'd seen the *receipt*. What was taken. What the Gate, if it ever opened, meant to give back.

The vault reassembled around her with a sound like a book closing. Granite, warm underfoot. The Gate's gold light settling back to its patient pulse. Her knees hit the stone and she let them, because standing suddenly seemed like a skill she'd have to relearn.

Wren was on the ground too, ten feet away, curled around the codex page like it was the last true thing in the world. Maybe it was.

"How much did you see?" he rasped.

"All of it." Her voice came out flat and strange. "The war that never happened. The staged battles. Aldric planning the theft over a map like a man planning a harvest." She laughed—one dry, awful note. "I took an oath on the founding chronicles, Wren. When Halvard died, I swore on his memory and on the realm's true history to keep the Gate shut. I swore on a forgery. I've been guarding the evidence for seven years. I've been the usurper's *night watchman*."

"Don't." He crawled toward her, and the look on his face stopped her cold—not pity, something fiercer. "Don't do that. You kept a door shut for seven years because a dying woman asked you to, and you thought she was protecting the world. You weren't their watchman. You were the only honest guard they ever posted."

"Halvard knew." August stared at the Gate. "She must have known. She was Keeper before me, and her master before her, all the way back—someone in that chain read the truth and chose the lie anyway. Or chose the peace. Is there a difference? Tell me there's a difference."

"I don't know." He said it simply, no comfort in it, and she loved him a little for that. "Maybe there isn't. Maybe that's the next question."

The Gate pulsed. Once. Twice. And then, softly, in a voice that was not a voice but arrived anyway, the way warmth arrives through a window—

*You have seen. Now choose what you carry out of this room.*

August looked at Wren. Wren looked at August. Between them lay the codex page, and on it, in ink a century old, the date that was tonight.

"The inquisitors will have felt that flare," she said. "Corvane's hounds can smell gate-light through solid rock. We have minutes, maybe less."

"Then we go up, and we decide fast." Wren gathered the page, and his hands were shaking, and his eyes were not. "Because I've spent my whole life believing the Archive held the truth, and I just watched it hold the cover story instead. If the histories are forged, then the law is forged. And if the law is forged—" He stopped, and finished very quietly: "Then everything I've ever obeyed is just the crime, written down neatly."

Above them, faint through forty feet of granite, the bells of Crownspire began to ring the curfew.

It was hours early. Someone had already told them.
