## 5. Laws Written in Fear

The stairs came up out of the dark like a throat, and Wren climbed them with August's hand locked around their wrist, both of them breathing like people who had run a long way from something that could still be behind them.

Which it could. The Gate's flare had lit the vault like a struck match inside a paper house. Anyone looking at the right patch of sky would have seen the glow bleed up through the cobbles of Copperlane, and anyone looking was exactly what Wren feared most.

They emerged through a trapdoor into the back room of the charm shop, into the smell of dried lavender and beeswax and old paper—August's smell, Wren thought absurdly, as if they'd known her long enough to have a smell for her. They'd known her for one hour. One hour and a hundred years of prophecy.

August barred the door and stood with her ear against the wood, listening to the street.

Wren listened too, and what they heard was wrong.

Copperlane at this hour should have been loud—the evening carts, the argument outside the tannery that never ended, someone always singing badly two doors down. Instead there was a hush with weight to it, the specific silence of a city holding its breath. And under it, boots. Not many. Just enough. Moving in pairs, unhurried, the way men walk when they own the streets they're walking on.

"Curfew," August said quietly. "That bell wasn't the vespers bell."

Wren's stomach dropped. They'd heard the bell an hour ago and filed it away as noise, because they were an archivist and archivists filed things away; that was the whole job. But now, standing in lavender-scented lamplight with the Gate's warmth still humming in their bones, they heard it again in memory: three strokes, pause, three strokes. Not vespers. Summons.

"Who calls a curfew?" Wren asked, though they knew. Everyone in the capital knew. You just went your whole life without saying it aloud, the way you didn't say certain words over graves.

"Inquisitors," August said. "The Order of the Sealed Flame. They come when the Gate stirs." She turned, and her face in the lamplight was older than it had been an hour ago. "Seven years I've kept it quiet. Seven years, and tonight it decides to sing."

"It didn't decide anything." Wren heard their own voice go sharp and didn't rein it in. "It answered me. Or I answered it. Whatever happened down there—it knew my name before I ever touched it."

"I know."

"You *know*? You don't seem—" Wren stopped, pressed their palms against their eyes. "I'm sorry. I've never been in a vault before. I've never been hunted before. I'm an archivist. My greatest crime to date is misfiling a land grant from the reign of Aldous the Second, and even then I corrected it before anyone noticed."

Something moved across August's face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if she could afford one. "Aldous the Second."

"The land grant was very boring."

"Then you're overdue for some excitement." She said it lightly, but her eyes went past Wren to the shuttered window, and the lightness died there.

* * *

They watched the street through the gap in the shutters, taking turns, because you didn't put two faces at one window when the Order was in town. That was another thing everyone knew and no one said.

The inquisitors wore grey, not black—that was the first thing Wren noticed, and it unsettled them more than black would have. Black was theater. Grey was bureaucracy. These men moved through Copperlane with lanterns and ledgers, knocking on doors, speaking politely, writing things down. A woman came out of the dyer's house across the lane with her hands held out in front of her, palms up, and an inquisitor looked at them the way Wren looked at a manuscript: closely, professionally, checking for something.

"Gate-touched," August murmured. "When the Gate flares, it leaves traces. People who were near it, dreamed of it, born under the wrong star. The Order has lists."

"Lists of what?"

"Names. Marks. Families that have been watched for generations." August stepped back from the shutter. "My mentor used to say the Order doesn't fight magic. It *collects* it. Every person they take, every charm they confiscate, everything goes into their vaults. Nothing is destroyed. Why would they destroy it? It's theirs. It was always theirs. That's the whole point of the law."

Wren felt the codex's page burning behind their eyes—the page Supervisor Halloway had torn from their hands, the final entry in a century-old hand:

*On the eve of the third moon's waning, the keeper with the scarred palm shall stand with the scholar of dust, and the Ember Gate shall know its own again.*

Tonight was the eve of the third moon's waning. The scarred palm was downstairs, ten feet from where Wren stood, wrapped in linen because the Gate had seared it during the flare. The scholar of dust was Wren, who had spent six years cataloguing other people's truths and never once expected to be one.

"They'll come here," Wren said. "Eventually. Charm sellers are the first door they knock on. That's not deduction, that's procedure—I've read the seizure manifests. Charm shops are always item one."

"Then we have until they finish the dyer's house." August was already moving, pulling a floorboard near the hearth, lifting out an oilcloth bundle. Inside: a travel cloak, a knife, a small clay jar sealed with wax. "You need to leave the city tonight."

"*We* need to leave the city."

"No." She said it flatly, and Wren heard the guardian in it, the seven years of it. "The flare was mine. The scar is mine. If they're hunting gate-touched, they're hunting me. You're a junior archivist with clean hands. You walk out the front of this and you're nobody."

"I'm not nobody." Wren's voice cracked on it. "August, the codex names *both* of us. Halloway has the page. By morning he'll have reported it—he reports everything, it's how he got his office—and by tomorrow noon there will be a warrant with my name on it, and I won't be a junior archivist with clean hands, I'll be the scholar of dust, wanted for heresy, and I'll wish to God I'd left with you tonight."

August paused, the clay jar in her hands, and looked at Wren properly for the first time since the vault. Really looked. Wren held still under it and did not look away, which surprised them both.

"You read the manifest," she said finally. "The seizure records. Why?"

"Because someone has to know what they're taking." Wren swallowed. "Because history is just paperwork that won, and I wanted to see what the losing side's paperwork looked like."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Then August set the jar down and laughed—a short, exhausted, real laugh, the first Wren had heard from her. "Scholar of dust," she said. "The Gate called it true."

* * *

What Wren did next, they would think about for the rest of their life, in the way you thought about a scar: often at first, then less, then suddenly all at once on some ordinary afternoon years later.

Their credentials were in their satchel. Archive seal, reader's pass, the letter of appointment signed by the Master of Records—everything that made Wren Vale a person in the eyes of the law. Without them, Wren was a ghost. With them, Wren was traceable: every document they'd pulled in the last week was logged, timestamped, cross-referenced. Including, Wren realized with a cold slide of the stomach, the request slip for the prophetic codex. In their own handwriting. Dated four days ago.

Halloway wouldn't even need the confiscated page to find them. He'd need only the ledger.

So Wren took the credentials out, and the request slips, and the marginal notes they'd made in a cipher of their own devising—which would protect them from no one, because the Order employed better cryptographers than Wren would ever be—and they carried them to the hearth, where August had already laid a fire.

And hesitated.

This is the part Wren would remember. Not the burning. The hesitation. Six years of being *Archive*, of being a small reliable gear in the machine that kept the realm's memory—the machine that, Wren now understood, had been built to keep only one memory, the usurper's memory, and grind all others into dust. Six years, and their entire self was folded into a letter of appointment with a ribbon seal.

"It's just paper," August said softly, behind them.

"It's not just paper." Wren's hands shook. "It's me. It's every version of me that ever existed on record. I burn this and there's no going back to being—who I was this morning. A person with a desk and a salary and a supervisor who thinks I'm promising."

"Do you want to go back to that?"

Wren thought about the vault. About the visions the Gate had poured through them: the staged battle at Redford Bridge, soldiers paid to die theatrically while the founding king watched from a hill; the forged chronicle, inked in a single year and backdated a century; the line of kings, each one inheriting not a crown but a *story*, and enforcing the story with fire. About the last vision, the one that had made Wren's knees give—the household fires, a thousand of them, hearth after hearth after hearth, and masked riders collecting what the flames gave up, carrying the stolen embers to the mountain. Magic hadn't been outlawed because it was dangerous. It had been outlawed because it had been *harvested*. The law wasn't a wall around danger. It was a fence around a pasture.

"No," Wren said. "God help me, no."

They struck the flint themselves. Wouldn't let August do it. If this was treason, it would be treason in their own hand.

The letter of appointment caught slowly—ribbon curling first, then the vellum buckling, then flame running up through the Master of Records' signature, erasing it stroke by stroke. Wren fed it the request slips, the ciphered notes, a draft letter to Halloway they'd written in a moment of panic and never sent. The fire took it all with an almost cheerful greed, and the heat of it hit Wren's face, and they stood there watching six years of respectability become light and smoke, and felt—this was the terrible part, the part they'd never admit to any tribunal—*thrilled*.

Terrified and thrilled in equal measure, the way you feel on a high roof edge. Some deep animal part of Wren had been waiting their whole careful life for permission to jump.

"There," August said quietly. "Now you're nobody."

"Now I'm nobody," Wren agreed, and found that they were smiling, and found that the smiling frightened them more than the inquisitors did.

* * *

The knock came at the quarter-hour, as Wren had predicted it would. Two knocks, polite, bureaucratic. August opened the door with a shopkeeper's smile fixed in place, and Wren stood behind her in the shadow of the doorway, hood up, hands buried in their sleeves, heart going like a triphammer.

The inquisitor was young. That was the worst of it—young, earnest, a ledger under his arm and a lantern held high, his grey cloak still carrying the creases of its folding. He looked at August the way a student looks at a textbook answer he half-remembers.

"Madam Underhill. Licensed charm seller, Copperlane ward." He checked his list. "You are aware of the curfew."

"I am, sir."

"You are aware that the Order of the Sealed Flame has authority to inspect premises licensed under the Charm Statutes."

"I am."

He stepped inside without waiting to be asked, because he didn't need to ask, and that too was procedure. His lantern swept the shelves—luck-knots, fever-charms, the little braided cords August sold for a copper apiece—and his pen hovered over his ledger, recording, recording. Wren watched him work and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this polite young man with his neat entries was the same instrument as the forged chronicle, the harvested hearths, the century of manufactured war. Not cruel. *Procedural.* The usurper's line had learned that cruelty wasted energy; procedure cost nothing and never slept.

"Business has been good?" the inquisitor asked.

"Fair, sir. Charms are cheap comforts."

"Mm." He turned a luck-knot over with one finger. "There was a light seen in this district tonight. An unusual light. From below the ground." His pen paused. "You would report such a thing, if you witnessed it."

"Of course, sir."

"Of course." He wrote something. Wren's mouth went dry as ash. Then the young man's gaze lifted, snagged on the second figure in the shadows, and moved toward them—

—and August stepped sideways, casually, into his sightline, a shopkeeper adjusting her stance, and said, "Careful of the third shelf, sir, the hook comes loose," and the moment broke, and the inquisitor wrote his final entry, and tipped his hat, and said the curfew lifted at dawn, and left.

The door closed. The bar went across. Wren let out a breath they felt they'd been holding for six years.

"He saw me," Wren whispered.

"He saw a hood. There's a difference, and tonight it's the whole width of your life." August banked the hearth fire, scattering the last flakes of Wren's former self among the coals. "We move at the false dawn, before the patrols change. There's a way down to the river that doesn't touch a main street."

"*We*," Wren repeated, and this time August didn't correct it.

She sat down across from them instead, unwrapped the scorched bandage from her palm, and looked at the wound the Gate had left there. It wasn't a burn, Wren saw now. It was a mark. Fine lines, deliberate as script, radiating from her lifeline like the branches of some tree drawn by a careful hand.

"That wasn't there before tonight," August said. "Seven years of sealing rituals, and it never marked me. Tonight it wrote on me." She turned her hand in the light. "Whatever the Gate wants, it stopped wanting to be left alone."

Wren leaned close, archivist to the bone, and studied the lines, and felt their skin prickle, because the marks weren't random. They were letters. Old ones. Pre-dynasty script, the kind that appeared in exactly three surviving fragments, all of them disputed, all of them locked in the Order's vaults where no scholar could read them.

Except Wren had read one. Once. Before it was seized. Before Halloway. A fragment of a text the catalogue had listed, with the Order's particular genius for euphemism, as *Materials Pending Review*.

"I can't read it all," Wren said. "But I know the first word."

August looked up. "What word?"

Wren's voice came out very small, and very steady, the way voices do when they carry more weight than they can hold.

"*Return*."

Outside, the curfew patrol passed in the empty street, boots keeping time, and somewhere across the sleeping city the Ember Gate hummed beneath the cobbles like a struck bell that no one but the two of them could hear, waiting, patient as a century, for the rest of its sentence.
