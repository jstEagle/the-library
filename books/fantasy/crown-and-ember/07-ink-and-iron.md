## 7. Ink and Iron

The safehouse was a bookbinder's attic above a shuttered tannery, and it smelled of glue, leather, and the ghost of old smoke. Wren had chosen it from a list of dead archivists' haunts—places the Inquisition catalogued but never bothered to raid, because no one hid treason among the tools of a trade so boring.

August sat across the worktable from her, sleeves rolled, hands wrapped around a chipped mug of chicory she wasn't drinking. The lamplight caught the scar at her temple—that pale seam Wren had memorized from a page of century-old vellum before ever seeing it on a living face. It should have felt strange by now. It didn't. It felt like reading a favorite passage and finding it better than remembered.

"You're staring," August said, without looking up from the codex.

"I'm thinking."

"You're staring *and* thinking. I can feel it."

Wren pulled the codex toward herself and smoothed the cracked cover flat. "Then think with me. We've been at this for six hours."

"We've been at this for six hours because you keep starting over." August set down her mug. "You read the same line four times, frown like it insulted your mother, and turn the page back."

"Because it's wrong," Wren said. "That's what I keep telling you. The cipher doesn't fail on the difficult passages—it fails on the easy ones. Look." She turned to the leaf they'd been fighting since dusk: a dense block of glyph-pairs in two registers, one inked, one blind-stamped into the paper itself. "The inked register decodes beautifully. Clean substitution, standard monastic key, the kind they teach second-years. It gives us a history of the Gate. Warding rites. Keeper's oaths. All of it coherent, all of it dull."

"And the stamped one?"

"The stamped one gives us gibberish. Which is impossible, if it's the same cipher. Two registers of the same system can't produce one sense and one nonsense—unless—" Wren stopped herself, pressed her palms flat against the table. "Unless they're not the same system. Unless the stamped text is a *key* that only works when you stop trying to read it as text."

August leaned back. For a long moment the only sound was the tannery's settling timbers and, far below, the curfew bells marking the third hour past midnight.

"Say it plainer," August said.

"The blind stamping isn't writing. It's pressure-patterns. Where the binder's tool pressed hardest, where it barely touched. Someone encoded information in the *depth* of the impression, not the shape." Wren traced one finger over a row of glyphs without touching them. "You'd need to run your fingers over the original to feel it. Which means whoever made this never expected it to be read with eyes at all."

"Who reads a book with their hands?"

"A keeper," Wren said quietly. "A keeper who took an oath of silence and needed somewhere to put the truth anyway."

August went very still. Wren watched her arrive at it—the same cold, vertiginous arrival she'd had herself, hours ago, alone with the thought.

"My mentor couldn't write it down," August said. "She couldn't *speak* it. So she pressed it into the binding." Her voice had gone rough. "This is Sable's work."

"You're sure?"

"I bound books for her for three years before she—" August stopped. Swallowed. "I know her hand. I know her tools. She had a stamping wheel she never let me touch. I used to think she was just possessive." A short, unsteady laugh. "I used to think a lot of things about her."

Wren slid the codex across the table until it rested under August's fingers. "Then read it the way she meant it to be read."

For a moment August didn't move. Then she closed her eyes and drew her fingertips slowly along the first line of blind-stamped glyphs, and Wren watched her face do something complicated—grief and recognition braided together, a woman touching a dead friend's voice.

"Pressure here. Deep, then shallow, then deep-deep-shallow..." August murmured, eyes still shut. "It's a sequence. Not letters. It's... intervals. Like the bell-codes the harbor uses." Her fingers moved faster. "Deep is long, shallow is short. It's a *count*. Wren, it's counting something."

"Counting what?"

August opened her eyes. They were bright and wet and furious all at once. "Households. It's counting households. Look—" she pulled the codex back, flipped pages with sudden urgency, matching stamped sequences against inked ones, her scarred brow furrowed. "Here. The inked text says 'the Gate was sealed in the year of the Sundering to contain the war-magic of the fallen kings.' Standard doctrine. But the stamped layer underneath runs through district after district, street after street—names, Wren. *Names.* Ashrow Lane. The Mirefield holdings. The weavers' quarter in Vell. Hundreds of names, each one paired with a number, each number paired with a word."

"What kind of word?"

August's lips moved silently as her fingers walked the lines. Then she stopped, and the color left her face.

"'Withdrawn,'" she read aloud. "'Taken.' Every name has a number and every number says *taken.*" She looked up. "It's not a war record. It's a receipt. This is an inventory of everything the Gate holds."

Wren's heart was pounding. "An inventory of—"

"Magic. Household magic. Hearth-bonds, craft-gifts, the little workings every family used to have before the Edicts." August's voice was shaking now, and she didn't seem to care. "My grandmother used to tell me her mother could coax a bread oven hot with a song. Everyone said it was a story. A charming lie old women tell." She pressed both hands flat on the codex. "It's not a lie. That woman's gift is in here. Written down. *Confiscated.*"

The lamplight seemed to dim around the truth of it. Wren found she had to grip the edge of the table.

"The founding dynasty didn't imprison the war," she said slowly. "They collected."

"They *harvested.*" August laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "Seven years I've kept that Gate sealed believing I was holding back a plague. Believing the seal was mercy. And it's a vault. I'm not a warden, Wren. I'm a *storekeeper.* I've been guarding their treasure room."

"No." Wren reached across the table before she could think about it, covered August's hand with her own. "You were keeping faith with someone who loved you. She just couldn't tell you why. There's a difference."

August looked at their hands. Didn't pull away. "Is that scholarship talking?"

"That's me talking."

Something shifted in the air between them—subtle, dangerous, warm. August turned her hand over beneath Wren's, slow enough that Wren could have withdrawn, and didn't. Palm to palm now. August's skin was cool; the lamplight made the scar gleam faintly, like a seam of silver.

"Keep talking, then," August said, very low. "What else does my mentor's ghost want us to know?"

Wren should have pulled her hand back and returned to the cipher. Instead she let the silence hold a moment too long, cataloguing absurd things: the ink stain on August's knuckle, the way steam curled off the untouched chicory. Then she cleared her throat and reclaimed her hand—reluctantly, and they both knew it—and drew the codex close again.

"There's a final section. Stamped, not inked. I assumed it was corrupted. Now I don't think so." She turned to the last leaf. "Run your fingers over this one."

August bent over the page. Her fingers moved slowly, then slower, then stopped entirely.

"Wren."

"What?"

"It's not a count." August's voice came out thin. "It's instructions."

* * *

The instructions took another hour to decode, mostly because August refused to believe them and made Wren check every sequence twice, and Wren checked every sequence twice, because she didn't believe them either.

But the cipher held. Sable's hidden hand did not lie.

"The Gate doesn't open outward," Wren said, pacing now, unable to sit still, gesturing with the charcoal stub she'd been using for notes. "That's the whole false premise. Every account, every doctrine, every terrified sermon—the Gate as a door that war pours out of. But according to this, the Gate has no inside direction at all. It's a *return mechanism.* Sealed, it holds. Opened, it gives back. Everything it holds goes home. To the bloodlines. To the descendants of these names."

"Every gift," August said, "to every heir. All at once."

"Including to people who'd use them badly. Including to soldiers in Corvane's army who are themselves descendants of the robbed." Wren dropped into her chair. "Do you understand what that means? Half the reason the Edicts hold is that ordinary people are afraid of magic—afraid of *other people's* magic. But if opening the Gate returns everyone's own inheritance to their own hands simultaneously—"

"Then there's no army of sorcerers to fear," August finished. "There's just... everyone. Whole again." She rubbed her face. "And the usurper's entire claim collapses overnight. You can't justify hoarding power as protection when every farmhand in the realm suddenly has his grandmother's hearth-song back in his chest."

"So the prophecy isn't naming the person who unleashes the war," Wren said. "It's naming the person who ends the theft."

August was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was careful, stripped bare. "Sable died on the scaffold telling me the Gate must never be opened. Her last words to me were *keep it shut, keep it shut, keep it shut.* And the whole time she'd already written out how to open it. Pressed into a book she left where I'd find it." She shook her head slowly. "Why not tell me? Why make me guard a lie for seven years?"

"Because if you'd known, you'd have opened it," Wren said simply. "Years ago. Alone. Without proof, without allies, without the codex, without anyone who could explain to the cities what was happening. You'd have died on a scaffold next to her and the Gate would've been re-sealed by whoever came after, and the theft would have gone on for another century." She held August's gaze. "She wasn't lying to you. She was buying you time to become the person who could do it right."

"That's a generous reading."

"It's the accurate one. I'm an archivist. Generosity and accuracy happen to be the same discipline."

August laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, exhausted and bright—and Wren felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs and settle there, unwelcome and warm.

"We'll need the key-stone," August said at last, sobering. "The codex confirms it. The Gate answers to the keeper's stone or nothing. And Corvane will have figured that out too, if he's half as good as his reputation."

"Then we move tomorrow."

"Tonight," August corrected. "Curfew lifts at dawn. If we're seen traveling after sunrise we're dead, and worse, the codex is confiscated, and this dies with us." She stood, began rolling the codex into an oilcloth sleeve with the practiced economy of a woman packing to flee. "There's a sister-shrine. Sable mentioned it once, drunk on plum wine—said the founders built them in pairs so no single keeper could ever—" She stopped mid-motion. Looked up. "So no single keeper could ever *alter the record.* The shrine will corroborate the codex. Independent stone, independent witness."

"Proof that can't burn," Wren breathed. "If we can copy it, the free cities can't dismiss it as forgery."

"Now you're thinking like a smuggler instead of an archivist."

"Now I'm thinking like both. There's a difference?" August smiled at that—small, crooked, real—and Wren realized with a lurch that she would cross any border in the realm to keep that smile supplied with reasons.

Outside, the fourth bell tolled. Two hours of dark left.

August slung her pack over one shoulder and paused at the ladder, looking back. "Wren Vale. Six hours ago I didn't trust you with my shop keys."

"And now?"

"Now I'm about to commit treason with you, which is either a promotion or a diagnosis." She descended. Wren followed, taking the codex against her chest like a heartbeat, and thought: *both.*

Below, the tannery yard lay silvered and silent under the last of the moon. August checked the alley, then waved Wren forward—and that was when they both saw it.

Chalked on the tannery gate, fresh enough that the dust still clung to Wren's sleeve when she brushed past: a single mark. The Inquisition's survey sign.

Someone had found the safehouse.
