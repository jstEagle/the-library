## 9. Sanctuary of Thieves

The rain started as we crossed the Tanner's Canal, and by the time we reached the old tannery district it had settled in for the night, a fine grey drizzle that turned the cobbles to grease and made every lantern halo like something holy. Wren walked beside me with the codex wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, one hand pressed over it the way you'd press a wound.

"You're sure about this," they said. Not a question, really. A hope I'd say no.

"No," I said. "But it's the only door I have left."

The Undermarket didn't exist. That was its first defense. On any surveyor's map, the streets below the tannery district were marked as collapsed cellars and flooded cisterns, condemned after the fire of Corvane's grandfather's grandfather's time. The city had written them off, and the city's forgotten places had done what forgotten things do—they'd filled up with people.

We went down through the door behind the glue-boiler's shed, which was exactly as unremarkable as it needed to be: rotten wood, rusted hinges, a smell of tallow and wet stone. I knocked the pattern—two, pause, three, pause, one—and waited while something heavy shifted on the other side.

A slot scraped open. One eye, brown and bloodshot, took me in.

"Shop's closed," said the eye.

"It's August."

"I know who it is." The eye moved to Wren, lingered, came back to me. "Who's the scholar?"

"A friend."

"Friends." The eye narrowed. "You haven't brought a friend down here in seven years, August Okafor. Friends get people hanged."

"So does standing in a doorway arguing. Open up, Doss."

The slot closed. Bolts turned—not the quick rattle of someone eager, but the slow reluctant grind of someone doing arithmetic he didn't like. Then the door swung, and Doss Vane stood in the gap, a squat man gone soft around the middle, his once-famous hands now just hands, scarred from a charm-gone-wrong years before I'd ever met him. Behind him, the passage breathed out warmth and noise and the smell of frying onions, and my chest did something complicated, because for seven years I'd been the only person in Varrow who knew what I knew, and here was a whole city under the city, alive and lit.

"Doss," I said. "I need the council."

"The council," he repeated flatly. "At midnight. In the rain. With an archivist on your arm and—" he tilted his head, listening to something I couldn't hear, or maybe pretending to "—and whatever that is you're carrying under your coat. August. What did you do?"

"I didn't do anything. That's rather the point."

* * *

The Undermarket council met in what had been the tannery's rendering hall, a vaulted room where forty years ago vats of fat had simmered and now strings of paper lanterns hung between iron hooks, glowing amber over tables of mismatched chairs. Maybe sixty people were there when we arrived, and more kept coming, because word travels fast underground, and nothing travels faster than the news that the Keeper has come down off her hill.

I knew most of their faces. That was the trouble with being the licensed charm-seller who looked the other way—that was the whole architecture of my double life. Maren Quick, who sold luck-knots two stalls down from me above ground and taught unlicensed hedge-craft below it. Old Sef, who could still a fever with a touch and hadn't touched anyone in six years out of fear. The Ferrow twins, who ran messages and small charms and larger lies. And at the head table, presiding with the weary authority of a woman who'd never asked for any of it, Mother Hesk—the closest thing the Undermarket had to a government, which is to say, the person everyone shouted at until she gave them permission to do what they'd already decided.

She listened to me for eleven minutes without interrupting. I told her about the Gate. About the flare. About the codex, and the prophecy, and the date, and the inquisitors, and Corvane tracing the flare to my district. I told her about the visions the Gate had shown us—the staged war, the forged history, a hundred years of law built on a lie. I kept my voice level. I'd had practice.

When I finished, the rendering hall was quiet enough that I could hear the rain finding its way down through forty feet of stone.

Mother Hesk folded her hands. "You want sanctuary."

"I want shelter for the night. For both of us. And I want the Undermarket to know what's coming, because if Corvane finds the Gate, he'll find the ways down here too. He'll come for all of you. Not because you've done anything—but because you exist."

"And what would you have us do?"

"Nothing. Tonight. Just shelter."

"That's not what you want." She wasn't unkind about it. She was worse than unkind; she was accurate. "You want us ready. You want us willing. You want the Undermarket to stand behind you when you do whatever it is you're going to do with that Gate."

Wren stirred beside me. "The codex says—"

"I know what the codex says." Mother Hesk's eyes flicked to them, then back to me. "Word travels fast underground, Keeper. A prophecy names your date. It named you seven years ago, apparently, and nobody thought to mention it to the rest of us."

"I didn't know either," I said. "Until three days ago."

"Three days." She let that sit. "Seven years you've kept the Gate. Seven years you've known what's under this city, and you told no one. Not me. Not the council. You sold charms upstairs and played guardian downstairs and told yourself it was protection." She shook her head slowly. "It was the same thing the usurper does, August. Deciding for everyone else what they can't be trusted to know."

The words went in clean, the way true things do. I'd had seven years to dress up my silence as duty. She stripped the clothes off it in a sentence.

"I made a promise," I said.

"To a dead man. To a promise built on a lie—you said so yourself." She spread her hands. "So here is my question, and I'll ask it plainly because you deserve plainness: if we shelter you tonight, tomorrow, when the hounds come—and they will come—do we burn with you? Or do you have a plan that ends anywhere except a pyre?"

And there it was. The honest answer was *I don't know*. The plan was a codex half-decoded, a sister-shrine none of us had located yet, and a Gate that hummed beneath our feet like a held breath. Sixty faces watched me weigh it, and I watched them right back, and I understood suddenly why governments hate open questions.

"I don't have a plan that ends well," I said. "I have a truth that ends the lie. Sometimes that's all a beginning is."

Old Sef laughed—one dry bark, quickly smothered. Maren Quick didn't laugh at all. She stood up, arms crossed, and her voice came out low and hard.

"I've got a daughter, August. Nine years old. She does little charms—flowers opening, moths dancing. Nothing. Less than nothing, by the law. If they take her for it, they won't hang her, they'll do worse, they'll make an example, and I will trade every secret in this market to keep that from happening. Do you understand me? Every secret. Including yours." She sat back down. "I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you what fear does. Ask your dead mentor."

Silence again. Somewhere in the dark of the hall a baby started crying and was hushed.

Then a voice from the back, cracked and old: "Let her speak for herself, then. The Gate. What does it want?"

Everyone turned. It was Tam Broadstairs, ancient, half-blind, who'd been running messages since before the outlawry and claimed—he always claimed—to remember the last time the Gate had flared. He shuffled forward into the lantern light, squinting at me with milky eyes.

"They say it remembers," he said. "They say it remembers everything. So ask it what it wants, girl, and tell us true, because some of us are too old to run and would rather die facing the right way."

"I don't know what it wants," I said. "That's the truth. But I know what it showed us. A war that never happened. Heroes who never lived. A hundred years of theft dressed up as law." I looked around the hall, at the lanterns, at the faces, at the fear. "The magic you're all hiding—it was never dangerous. It was never theirs to take. It was *yours*, and they built a crown out of stealing it, and the Gate is the receipt."

That landed. I felt it land—not everywhere, not in everyone, but in enough. Sef's chin lifted. One of the Ferrow twins stopped chewing her thumbnail. Even Doss, by the door, went very still.

Mother Hesk watched it land too, and I saw her do the arithmetic of a leader: not *is she right*, but *how many believe her, and what will that cost*.

"Shelter," she said finally. "Tonight, and tomorrow night if you need it. Food, a safe room, and my silence. That's what your years of kindness buy, August, and I'll not pretend it buys more than it does." Her gaze hardened. "But hear me. The council doesn't follow you. Half these people think you're a saint and half think you're a spark near a powder store, and both halves are wrong. Whatever you do with that Gate, you do it carrying your own weight. The Undermarket survives by staying small and staying hidden, and I will not spend sixty lives on one woman's prophecy."

"That's fair," I said.

"It isn't," she said. "It's just what I can give."

* * *

They put us in the old foreman's office off the rendering hall—a stone room with a cot, a brazier, and a window that hadn't seen sky in a century. Wren sat on the cot with the codex across their knees, working the cipher by brazier-light, lips moving, fingers tracing. I stood at the door a long moment before I spoke.

"Maren's right," I said. "About fear. I spent seven years thinking silence was safety. It's just fear wearing armor."

Wren looked up. "Hesk's wrong, though. Partly. Staying small and hidden—that's how they win. That's how they've won for a century. Everyone hiding their little magics, their little selves, waiting for it to blow over. It never blows over. It just gets quieter."

"You sound like the codex."

"I sound like someone who burned their credentials four nights ago." They smiled, tired. "There's no going back up the ladder for me, August. There's only forward, and whatever's at the bottom of it."

I sat down next to them. Close enough that our shoulders touched. Neither of us moved away.

"The shrine," they said. "The codex keeps circling it. *Where the kings were crowned hollow, the truth stands in stone.* If we can find it—if we can copy what's carved there—we don't need the Undermarket's blessing. We need paper and ink and enough hands to spread it faster than Corvane can burn it."

"And the route there?"

Wren hesitated. "That's the part I haven't decoded yet."

Of course it was. Of course the one thing we needed was the one thing still locked. I leaned my head back against the cold stone and laughed, quietly, because the alternative was screaming.

Somewhere above us, muffled by forty feet of rock, a bell began to ring. Once. Twice. Then a pattern—three-and-one, three-and-one—the curfew bells shifting into something else, something the Undermarket had a signal for. Doss appeared in the doorway so fast he must have been running, and his face told me everything before his mouth caught up.

"Inquisitors," he said. "Two dozen, maybe more. They're sweeping the tannery district. And August—" he swallowed "—they're not searching houses. They're asking after a charm-seller. By name. With a drawing."

He held up a paper, rain-spotted, ink still sharp. My own face looked back at me, younger somehow, harder, the scar rendered in careful crosshatch.

Below it, in the Inquisition's neat bureaucratic hand: *WANTED FOR HERESY AND GATE-TREASON.*

Wren stood up slowly, the codex clutched to their chest. "How?" they whispered. "How do they already have a drawing? We only surfaced yesterday."

Doss wouldn't meet my eyes. None of them would. And in the doorway behind him, half-shadowed, Maren Quick stood with her arms wrapped tight around herself, looking at the floor, looking at anything but me—and I understood, with a cold drop through my stomach, that somewhere in this room of frightened people, someone had already talked.
