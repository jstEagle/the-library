## 10. The Free Cities' Silence

The envoy arrived at the third hour past midnight, and she came the way all important things came to the Undermarket: sideways, unannounced, and smelling faintly of river water.

Wren was awake because sleep had become a country they no longer issued visas to. Three days since the vault. Two days since the curfew bells began tolling at sundown instead of midnight. One day since Corvane's criers had started reading names in the squares above—names of gate-touched citizens, which now meant anyone whose hands glowed when they were frightened, which meant everyone, eventually.

So Wren sat on a crate of smuggled lantern-oil in Sable Maren's back room, copying the codex cipher by candlelight while August argued with a one-eyed fence named Doss about whether a boat or a bribe would get them out of the city faster. The envoy slipped through the curtain like smoke deciding to be a person.

She was small, gray-cloaked, with the sunburned look of someone who spent her life on decks. She took in the room—the cipher pages, August's scarred knuckles, Doss's knife resting not-accidentally near his hand—and said, "Which of you is the archivist?"

"I'm—" Wren started.

"She's asking me," August said quietly. "Answer carefully."

"Vessa Sol," the woman said. "Envoy plenipotentiary of the Concord of Free Cities, which is a grand title for the last person they could spare." She pulled back her hood. She looked exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "I've been looking for you for six days. The Gate flared, and every government from here to the Salt Coast felt it in their teeth. Then the codex theft was reported, then the shrine rubbings went missing, and my masters did something unprecedented."

"They sent help," Doss said.

"They sent *me*," Vessa said, "to ask you to stop."

---

They gave her tea because there was nothing else to give. She held the cup without drinking it, turning it slowly, and told them about the armies in the voice of a woman reading a shipping manifest.

"The vanguard crossed the Ashford ford nine days ago. Thirty thousand regulars. Behind them, two more columns—sixty thousand by midsummer. The Concord has counted them. We have their supply schedules, their marching orders, the name of every colonel." She set the cup down. "We also have eleven cities, four hundred miles of undefended river, and a combined militia of perhaps eight thousand people who own more fishing spears than swords."

"So you'll fight," August said.

"No."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Wren heard it echo for a long time.

"The Concord Council voted last night. Nine cities for surrender terms, three abstaining, one—mine—for resistance, and I will note for posterity that mine contains the largest number of drunk fishermen in the realm." Vessa's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "We send envoys offering submission. We open the gates. In exchange we petition for the preservation of our charters, our markets, and—this was debated at length—our lives."

"That's it?" August stood up. The lamplight caught her scar, the long pale line across her cheekbone that a century-old prophecy had described before Wren ever saw it. "That's the great defense of the free cities? You just—lie down?"

"We do the arithmetic," Vessa said, without heat. "Eight thousand against ninety thousand isn't a battle, Keeper. It's a longer list of names for the criers to read. My council chose to keep its people alive rather than make them heroic corpses. I've come to tell you this because you should know: whatever you're building, whatever the codex says, no army is coming to help you. There is no army."

Wren had been silent through all of it, the way he'd been trained to be silent in archives—present, observing, irrelevant. But something in him cracked open at that word, *irrelevant*, applied now to everything he'd ever believed in.

"You're wrong," he said.

Every head turned. He hadn't spoken loudly. It surprised him that it landed anyway.

"The Concord thinks this is a war," Wren said. "It isn't. It never was. The war you're surrendering to preemptively—that war was invented. Staged. There are records, Vessa. There's a shrine two weeks south with the founding dynasty's own confession carved in granite, and I made copies of every inch of it before Corvane's people burned what they could find." His hands were shaking; he pressed them flat on the cipher pages. "The Ember War didn't happen. The 'great rising' that justified outlawing magic—it was theater. Paid actors and staged massacres and a royal bloodline that built its throne on a forgery. The Gate doesn't start wars. It gives magic back. To everyone. Including your fishermen."

Vessa looked at him for a long moment. Then at August. Then back.

"You believe that," she said. Not cruelly. Almost sadly.

"I can prove it."

"Can you?" She finally drank her tea. "Archivist. I read dispatches for a living. Do you know what I read this morning? A report from the Council's own scholars—honest ones, some of them, who examined the rumors of your shrine evidence before it was seized. Their conclusion: the carvings could be later forgeries planted to destabilize the realm. Their recommendation: suppress discussion to avoid giving the usurper pretext for reprisals." She spread her hands. "You see the shape of it. Even the truth needs someone willing to hear it. And the people with the power to hear it have decided that hearing it costs more than ignoring it."

"Then they're cowards," August said.

"Yes," Vessa agreed simply. "Mostly. Some of them are tired. A few are bribed. And a handful genuinely believe surrender is mercy, and they may even be right. Cowardice, wisdom, corruption—they wear the same coat, Keeper, and I've stopped trying to tell them apart." She leaned forward. "I didn't come only to deliver the bad news. I came because the vote was nine to three to one, and I watched honest people choose surrender, and I have been unable to sleep since. So. Convince me. If you can convince me, I can get back inside the walls of Vhal Marren before the surrender envoys depart, and I can put your proof in front of the three cities that voted to fight. That's all I have. One city of drunk fishermen and a boat."

---

What followed was four hours that Wren would remember for the rest of his life, though at the time it felt like drowning.

He laid out everything. The codex, its cipher broken page by page under his fingers over the past three days. The Gate's visions—August filled those in, haltingly, describing staged battlefields and kings receiving laurels for slaughtering hired extras. The shrine rubbings—or what remained; Corvane's men had taken most of the physical evidence, but Wren had copied the inscriptions into his own hand before fleeing, because he was an archivist and copying was the one thing he had never once failed to do. The lineage of the founding house, traced through forged chronicles. The arithmetic of stolen magic: every household taxed once a generation, not in coin but in power, siphoned north to feed the dynasty and its chosen few.

Vessa listened the way a good auditor listens—not agreeing, not objecting, just taking inventory.

And Wren watched her take inventory and understood, with a sick slow certainty, that inventory wasn't enough. Because halfway through his third hour, he caught himself doing the thing he despised in other scholars: performing certainty. Speaking louder where the evidence was thinnest. And beneath the performance, the real thought surfaced like a drowned body:

*It doesn't matter how true it is if no one with power chooses to act on it.*

Truth, in the world Vessa described, was just another cargo—and the ports were all closed.

He stopped talking. The candle had burned down to a thumb-width stub. August touched his shoulder once, briefly, and it steadied him more than the whole archive ever had.

"You've convinced me of one thing," Vessa said at last.

"That it's true?"

"That you believe it's true. Which, historically, is a different thing entirely." She rose, joints cracking, suddenly old-looking. "But here is what I know that you don't, archivist. I carried the surrender terms myself. I sat in the room when the Council sealed them. And I will tell you what none of the dispatches say, because none of the dispatches dare." Her voice dropped. "The usurper's terms include a clause. Article Seven. All persons found to carry unlicensed magical capacity, upon annexation, to be subject to 'assessment and disposition.' Disposition." She let the word sit there. "Your Gate returns magic to everyone. Do you understand what happens to a population that suddenly glows, in a realm whose law says glowing is hanging? Article Seven isn't surrender terms. It's a harvest schedule."

The silence afterward was total. Doss stopped pretending to clean his knife.

"So the Concord surrenders," August said slowly, "and delivers its own citizens to the pyre."

"The Concord surrenders and calls it peace." Vessa picked up her cloak. "I've done my duty. I've delivered the Council's message. Now I'm going to do something the Council didn't order, which is why I waited until I was certain no one else in this room was a coward." She looked at Wren directly. "You said the prophecy names tonight's date. Names her." A nod toward August. "Names you. Tell me about the prophecy. All of it. Because if the Concord won't fight, and the law won't listen, then the only weapon left in this whole rotten realm is the thing the usurper's own grandmother wrote down a century ago—and I want to know exactly what it promises."

---

Wren recited it from memory, because he had never once managed to memorize anything less than perfectly, and because the words had been living behind his teeth for three days:

*"When the ember sleeps beneath the crown of lies, a keeper marked by flame shall stand beside the one who reads what ash survives. On the eve of the ninth day's dark, the Gate shall drink its fill of stolen years, and the crown shall learn what it means to owe."*

"Ninth day's dark," Vessa repeated. "That's—"

"Tomorrow night," August said. "Tomorrow is the new moon. The ninth day's dark."

"And the armies reach the outer wall when?"

Vessa was quiet. "The vanguard's scouts were seen at the river fork this morning. Main force invests the walls within four days. But the vanguard itself—" she exhaled— "tomorrow night is not impossible. They travel fast when they think no one will resist."

"So it's tomorrow," Wren said. His own voice sounded far away. "The prophecy is tomorrow."

"Prophecies," Doss grunted from the corner, "are what fools use to pick the date they die."

But nobody laughed, because the terrible thing, the thing Wren could feel settling over the room like frost, was that Doss might be right—and that it no longer mattered. Every road led to the same place. The Concord would surrender and feed its people into Article Seven. The law would hunt them until the law ran out of things to burn. Corvane's inquisitors were closing district by district. And somewhere south, ninety thousand soldiers marched toward a wall that no one intended to defend.

There was no institution left to appeal to. No court, no council, no archive, no throne. He had spent his whole life believing that truth, properly documented, properly cited, would eventually prevail—that scholarship was a kind of slow justice. And here was the lesson of his entire career arriving all at once, wearing a gray envoy's cloak: *the record does not defend itself.* Someone had to stand between the truth and the fire, and every institution designed for that purpose had already chosen the fire.

Which left them. A charm seller who'd promised a dead man she'd never open the Gate. An archivist with ink-stained fingers and a head full of forbidden history. One city of drunk fishermen. And a date written in ash a century ago, arriving on schedule.

"I'll need a boat," Vessa said into the quiet. "And I'll need your copies. All of them. If I'm going to stand in front of three councils and call their surrender a massacre, I want every line of your hand to wave at them." She paused at the curtain, then turned back, and for the first time her exhaustion cracked and something harder showed underneath. "One more thing, and then I go. The Council's decision wasn't unanimous. The three dissenting cities fought hard. And the reason they lost wasn't fear." Her jaw tightened. "It was a letter. Arrived two days ago, bearing the seal of the Inquisitor-General. Corvane offered each council member who voted for surrender a personal guarantee of amnesty—and each member who voted to resist, a personal promise that their names would top Article Seven's list. He broke the Concord from inside with a piece of paper."

Wren felt the cold go through him. "Corvane knew about the vote before you did."

"Corvane knows about everything," Vessa said. "Including, I would wager, that you are sitting in this room right now." She pulled up her hood. "Get your proof to me by dawn, or don't. Either way, Keeper—archivist—when the ninth day's dark comes, you'll be standing at that Gate alone, because there is no one else coming. I'm sorry. I truly am. I spent six days hoping I'd find an army down here instead of two people and a stack of paper."

She was gone the way she'd come, sideways, into the dark.

August stood very still by the lamp. When she spoke, her voice was the guardian's voice, the one Wren had first heard across her shop counter, calm as a locked door.

"Dawn, then. We copy everything. And then tomorrow night—" she touched the wall, and far below, Wren felt it too, a pulse like a second heartbeat under the floor— "we stop waiting for permission."

Above them, muffled through layers of stone and market noise, the curfew bell began to toll.
