## 11. Corvane's Offer

They took her at the tanner's row, of all places. August would remember that afterward—the indignity of it. Seven years of keeping the oldest secret in the realm, and she was folded up like laundry between two barrels of lime, by men whose boots smelled of cheap tallow.

She'd gone out for salt and lamp-oil. That was all. Wren had begged her to stay in the Undermarket until dark, and she'd smiled at him—actually smiled—and said something clever about how a woman who couldn't buy her own lamp-oil had already lost. She'd worn the gray hood, kept her scar angled away from the lamplight, moved like a charm-seller moving through a city that mostly ignored charm-sellers.

But Corvane's inquisitors didn't grab people on suspicion. They grabbed them on *information*, and someone—she'd have time to hate this question later, and she would hate it thoroughly—had told them where she walked.

The sack over her head smelled of onions. Her hands were bound with waxed cord, the kind that bit. They put her in a cart that rolled for a long time, uphill, then stopped, and she heard gulls.

*Harbor district.* The inquisitors' safehouses clustered there, above the fish-presses, where screaming could be explained by the machinery.

They walked her down a corridor and sat her in a chair. Someone removed the sack with surprising gentleness, and light came back into the world: a low room, whitewashed, one window shuttered against the sea-smell, a brazier burning though the evening was mild. And across from her, pouring tea from a plain clay pot as if they were two merchants settling a price on wool, sat Inquisitor-General Corvane.

She had expected the hound of the chapel stories—black robes, iron collar, eyes like nails. What she got was a tired man in his fifties with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone who read late into the night. He looked, absurdly, like an archivist. Like Wren, twenty-five years on, if Wren had chosen the other side of every door.

"You're not what I pictured either," he said, reading her face without embarrassment. "The Keeper of the Ember Gate. I imagined someone taller."

"I get that," August said. Her voice came out steady. Good. Whatever else happened, let him hear steady. "Though I'll admit you're disappointing too. I thought the General of the Inquisition would have better tea."

He glanced at the pot. "It's terrible. I drink it anyway. Habit." He set a cup before her, then—this she noted carefully—did not offer to untie her hands. "August Okafor. Licensed charm-wright, second class. Fines paid, taxes current. You've been a model citizen."

"Everyone says so."

"And yet." He sat back, lacing his fingers over his knee. "Seven years ago, the Ember Gate beneath this city flared so brightly it woke every ward-stone in the palace vaults. The records call it a tremor. I've read the actual readings. It was a scream. And seven years ago, a licensed charm-wright named Okafor inherited a shop two streets from where the flare originated." He let that sit. "You understand my difficulty."

"My difficulty," August said, "is that I'm tied to a chair."

"Yes. I apologize for the cord. My predecessor would have used iron." Something crossed his face—not quite apology. Fatigue wearing apology's coat. "I want to be honest with you, Keeper, because I believe honesty is the only currency left worth anything. So: we know the Gate flared again, four nights ago. We know a junior archivist named Wren Vale accessed a restricted codex the same day and has since vanished. We know you were seen entering the Undermarket with him. And we know—" here his voice went very quiet, very level, "—that the codex contains a prophecy naming the two of you, and tonight's season, and the opening of the Gate."

The room tilted slightly. August kept her face still through long practice, but inside, cold water poured down her spine. *The codex.* Wren had carried it. Had copied it. Had—

"The page was confiscated," she said. "Your own archivists took it."

"A page." Corvane smiled thinly. "Vale is a scholar, Keeper. Scholars copy things. It's a disease of the profession. We recovered fragments from his quarters. Enough."

Enough. The word hung there, and August made herself look at it straight. If they had enough, why was she drinking bad tea instead of hanging? Because Corvane wanted something. Because whatever the prophecy said, he believed it more than he believed his own law—and belief, she was beginning to learn, was the most dangerous thing a man could carry.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"Straight to business. Good. I respect that." He leaned forward, and now she saw it, the thing under the tiredness: certainty, banked and glowing like coals. "The Gate has a key-stone. Every warding does; it's the anchor point, the heart of the lock. Your mentor knew this. Your mentor died rather than yield hers. I'm offering you a different ending."

"Go on."

"Amnesty. Full pardon—for you, and for the Vale boy. New papers, new names if you want them, passage to any free city you name. The shop stays yours. You keep selling your little charms, and no one ever asks you another question as long as you live." He slid a folded paper across the table toward her bound hands. "All of it, for one stone."

August looked at the paper. She didn't touch it. "And if I refuse?"

"Then in three days the vanguard reaches the outer wall, and the city burns, because that is what war does, and I will not be able to stop it. And you will hang beside your archivist in the square while the fires start." He said it without relish. That was the worst part. He said it the way a man reports weather. "I don't want that. Despite everything you've seen—everything the codex has told you—I don't want that. You think I'm the villain of this story. I'm not. I'm the man holding the lid on a pot that killed a hundred thousand people once, and I am begging you not to take the lid off."

"The war never happened."

Something flickered behind his eyes. "The war—"

"The Gate showed us." She leaned forward as far as the cord allowed, matching him. "Staged battles. Empty villages burned by their own garrisons. A founding king crowning himself on a lie, and his scribes writing it into every chronicle in the realm. There was no ancient war, General. There was a theft. Your whole doctrine—the laws, the hangings, the hundred years of fear—it's a fence around stolen goods."

For a moment—one breath, maybe two—Corvane's certainty guttered. She watched it happen, watched something move behind his face like a fish under ice. Then it sealed over, smooth as riverstone, and when he spoke again his voice was almost kind.

"When you've served the Inquisition as long as I have," he said, "you learn that everyone with a heresy has a vision that proves it. The Gate lies, Keeper. It's the oldest thing in the world and the oldest things lie the way rivers flood—because it's their nature. The founders sealed it to protect us. That is the faith I keep, and it has held this peace together for a century, and I will hold it together with my last breath if I must." He stood. "Think on my offer. You have until morning."

"And after morning?"

"After morning, the cord comes off and the questions begin, and I promise you—" he gathered the teapot, paused at the door, "—I will regret them more than you will."

The door shut. The bolt fell. August sat alone in the whitewashed room with the sea breathing somewhere beyond the shutter, and did the only useful thing left: she thought.

Because here was the truth of it, the truth she turned over like a charm-stone in her palm: *he meant it.* Every word. Amnesty, safety, the shop, Wren's life signed and sealed. All she had to do was hand over a rock.

A rock that anchored a seal built on murder. A rock that kept magic locked away from every farmer and midwife and soldier who'd once held it, back before the theft, back when it belonged to households instead of kings. If she gave it up, she'd be safe. Wren would be safe. The city might even survive the war that was coming for it, marching under banners stitched from a hundred-year-old forgery.

Safe. She tasted the word and found it rotten.

Her mentor had died rather than yield. August had spent seven years telling herself that was duty, noble and clean. Now she saw it differently: Sera hadn't died for the seal. She'd died for a promise made to her by liars, and the cruelest thing the usurper's line had ever done wasn't stealing the magic. It was making good women guard the theft and call it honor.

The shutter rattled in the wind. Somewhere below, the fish-presses groaned.

August tested the cord first—waxed, tight, knotted by someone competent. Fine. She'd been tying knot-breaker charms for customers since her second year at the shop. The trick was that the charm needed a spoken word, and the room was surely warded against speech-magic; she could feel the faint pressure of it, like swimming against a current.

But wards against speech weren't wards against silence. And there was one working in her repertoire that needed nothing but breath and patience—a slow unbinding, the kind she sold to anxious mothers for stuck drawers and stubborn clasps. It would take an hour. Maybe two. She settled into the chair, closed her eyes, and began.

* * *

Footsteps came twice in the night. Both times she slumped, breathing slow, cords loose but arranged to look binding. The second set of footsteps paused longer at the door, and a slot scraped open, and she heard Corvane's voice murmur—low, tired, almost gentle:

"Still thinking, Keeper?"

August kept her breathing even and said nothing, and after a moment the slot closed, and the footsteps withdrew, and she hated how much the gentleness in his voice had cost her. Because some traitorous part of her had wanted to answer. Had wanted to say *yes, I'm still thinking, tell me about the peace you've kept, tell me who it was peace for.*

Morning wouldn't come fast enough. But dawn, when it finally grayed the shutter-slats, brought something better: voices in the corridor, arguing. Two guards, bored, hungry, complaining about rotation.

"—told you the harbor shift changes at bell, not before—"

"—then explain why the General wants the girl moved before bell—"

The bolt scraped. The door opened onto two inquisitors in gray, one young with a broken nose, one older and heavier, both squinting against the lamplight of the corridor. The older one stepped forward to haul her up—

—which is when every lamp in the corridor died at once, snuffed by a whisper of borrowed flame, and a voice she knew better than her own heartbeat said, from somewhere near the ceiling beams:

"Gentlemen. Look at me and I'll drop something worse than darkness."

Wren. Here. *Here*, in an inquisitor safehouse, having followed her captors across half the city with nothing but scholarship and suicidal nerve, crouched in the rafters with what sounded like one of August's own flash-charms palmed and primed.

The younger guard lunged blind. August moved on instinct—shoulder into his knees, the way Sera had drilled into her a lifetime ago—and put him into the wall. The older one swung at the sound; Wren dropped from the beams onto his back, all elbows and terror, and they went down in a tangle that would have been funny in any other life.

"Keys," Wren gasped. "Left hip, I watched them—"

She got them. She got the door open, got Wren's wrist, got them both into the corridor and running, past the dead lamps, past the smell of fish rising through the floorboards, down a stair and through a door that opened onto gray morning and the harbor's rot-and-gull chaos.

They ran. Four streets, five, until Wren dragged her into the mouth of an alley and they stood bent double, laughing and gasping in the same ragged breath, alive, impossibly alive.

"You absolute fool," she managed. "How—"

"The rotation schedule. Inquisitors file them with the harbor registry—public record, if you know which drawer to bribe." His grin faded. He caught her face in both hands, checked her eyes, her jaw, her wrists, cataloguing damage like a scholar checking a manuscript for water marks. "Did they hurt you?"

"No. He poured me terrible tea and offered me the world." She pulled back before her body could decide anything foolish about his hands on her face. "Wren. Listen to me. He knows about the codex."

His grin died completely. "What?"

"Fragments. From your quarters. He quoted the prophecy back to me—our names aren't in it, but the date is, and the Gate, and enough shape that they know what's coming. And he's not guessing, Wren. He *believes*. He thinks the Gate is a monster and the founders were heroes and he's the last man standing between the world and the flood." She shook her head slowly. "You can't argue a man out of a faith. Believe me. I tried."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Gulls wheeled overhead. Down at the quay, fishermen were hauling nets, oblivious, and the vanguard was a day closer, and somewhere in the palace vaults a man with ink-stained fingers was learning right now that his prize had slipped out a dead-lamp corridor.

"So we run," Wren said at last. "The sister-shrine. Tonight, before they tighten the net."

"We run," she agreed. Then, because the weight in her chest wouldn't stay silent: "He offered me amnesty. For both of us. Papers, pardons, the shop. Everything. For the key-stone." She met Wren's eyes. "I need you to know I thought about it. For one whole minute, in that chair, I thought about it."

"I know," Wren said simply. "I'd have thought about it too." A ghost of a smile. "What changed your mind?"

August looked back the way they'd come, toward the safehouse, toward the tired man who honestly believed he was saving the world by keeping its chains oiled.

"He apologized for the cord," she said. "And meant it. That's how I knew." She turned for the alley's far end, where the Undermarket waited and the road beyond it. "Come on. We have a shrine to rob and a history to steal back."

Behind them, faint across the rooftops, the harbor bells began to ring—not the hour, not the tide, but the third pattern, the alarm pattern, rolling quarter by quarter across the waking city.

Corvane knew they were gone.

And now, August thought grimly, taking Wren's hand and running, he knew exactly where the last true history in the realm was written—in stone, at a shrine, a full day's ride north.

The race was already lost. Unless they won it.
