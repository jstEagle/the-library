## 12. Night on the Salt Road

**August**

The salt road ran white under a half-moon, and every step of it hurt.

Not the walking—though seven years of vault duty had not prepared my knees for the escarpment, and Wren had been limping since the ford—but something older than that. The Gate's key-stone hung against my sternum on its cord, and it was warm. It hadn't been warm when I took it from Halcyon's body. It hadn't been warm through seven years of sealing rituals and small charms and lying to customers about the weather-bones. Now it beat against my chest like a second heart that didn't quite match my rhythm, and I knew what that meant.

It knew where we were going. It wanted to go faster.

"Tell me again why we couldn't take the river barge," Wren said behind me. Her voice had that archivist's flatness she used when she was in pain and refused to admit it. "I've seen your forged papers. They're very good."

"Barges have manifests. Manifests get read." I held a branch aside for her and immediately regretted it, because it put us close enough that I could see the bruise Corvane's people had left along her jaw, yellowing now, ten days old. "Roads are stupid and slow, which means nobody watches them."

"You sound like you learned that from someone who loved you."

"I learned it from someone who got hanged for being watched."

She went quiet at that. She'd been quiet most of the day, and I knew why. We both did. There is a particular silence that falls between two people who have agreed not to say a thing, and we had been carrying it up the mountain pass like a third pack.

The Undermarket was two days behind us and burning, or so the glow over the hills suggested. Dessa was dead or gone; the twins had scattered east with their ink-fish and their grudges. Everything August Okafor had built in seven years of careful, unlicensed kindness—gone in one night of fire because I had let a stranger with beautiful handwriting follow me home.

And still the stone warmed against my chest, patient as a debt.

"There," I said. "The waystation. Half a roof, if the map's honest."

"And if the map isn't?"

"Then we sleep in the salt scrub like pilgrims and complain about it in the morning."

* * *

**Wren**

The waystation had been a shrine once, before the Reforms stripped the icons out and left the walls standing like pulled teeth. Someone had built a stove into the old altar niche, which struck me as either blasphemy or the most honest form of worship remaining in the realm. August lit a fire while I checked the shutters, and neither of us spoke, because speaking would lead to the other thing, and we had both agreed—the way you agree to a wound being cauterized—that the other thing could wait until after.

After. It was remarkable how much weight a single word could hold when you loaded it with everything unsaid.

My satchel sat against the wall with the rubbings I'd already made at the first shrine, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, the codex. I thought about the cipher, because thinking about the cipher was safe. The Gate doesn't start wars—it returns stolen magic to those it was taken from. Eleven words that had undone a century of doctrine in the time it took to read them. I had spent my whole life learning to read carefully. I had never been taught what to do when reading changed everything.

August sat back on her heels by the fire and looked, for a moment, unbearably tired. Not the dramatic exhaustion of storybooks but the grinding kind, the kind that lives in the wrists and the ankles. She caught me looking and raised an eyebrow.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is. You've been composing speeches since noon."

"I don't compose speeches."

"You alphabetize your fears. I've watched you do it. You'll talk about the terrain, then the provisions, then Corvane's patrol routes, all very sensible, and the whole time there's a fourth thing sitting behind your teeth."

"The terrain is genuinely concerning," I said, and she laughed—a short, real laugh, the first in days—and the sound of it did something unfair to my chest.

"August." My mouth was dry. "The shrine is three days further. After the shrine, there will be evidence to distribute, cities to convince, an inquisitor-general hunting us, and a war arriving whether we speak or not. And we said—we agreed—"

"Duty first." She said it easily, the way you repeat a rule you're testing for cracks. "Yes. We were very clear. Very mature. I remember."

"We were."

"So this is you alphabetizing."

"This is me trying to be sensible about the fact that I can't stop looking at you," I said, and it came out more precisely than I intended, the way truth does when you've drafted it silently forty times. "At the worst possible moments. On the prison dock. In the middle of a riot. While you were bleeding on the floor of a smuggler's boat, I was cataloguing the exact color of the light on your face, and I want you to know that I am aware of how useless that is. How dangerous. I know what it costs."

* * *

**August**

There it was, laid out neat as a footnote: she loved me, and she hated herself for the timing.

Seven years I had kept a gate and called the keeping contentment. Seven years of telling customers *yes, the weather-bones are accurate, no refunds* and coming home to an empty room above the shop, and I had gotten so good at wanting nothing that I mistook the practice for peace. Then this scholar walked into my shop with ash on her cuffs and a dead man's date in her pocket, and within a week I was committing treason, and within a month I was sitting by a stolen fire realizing that the loneliest thing about my life had never been the Gate at all.

"Wren." I crossed the room and sat down beside her, close enough that our shoulders touched. She didn't move away. She didn't lean in, either. We had rules about leaning. "You know what happened to the last person I let past my guard?"

"Halcyon died for it."

"Halcyon died *of* it. And I promised her ghost I'd keep the door shut forever, and I've spent every day since learning to call that promise a life." I turned the key-stone over in my hands. Warm. Eager. "So believe me when I tell you I understand deferred things. I'm the reigning expert. I kept a miracle in a basement and called it dust."

"But?"

"But deferring isn't the same as refusing." I looked at her, and the firelight made her look younger and braver than she'd allow. "I'm not refusing. I want that on the record, in your archive voice, witnessed and dated. I'm not refusing. I'm asking it to wait. Because if we do this—if I let myself have you—I will start protecting that instead of the mission, and one of these mornings I'll make the safe choice instead of the right one, and people will burn for it. Cities, maybe. You saw what I nearly did at the convoy. I chose you over thirty prisoners and I'd do it again, and that's exactly why we wait."

Wren was silent a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the salt scrub with a sound like paper turning.

"That is the most romantic argument I've ever heard," she said finally, "and I've read the court poets. Badly."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly bad poetry."

"August." She reached over and took my hand—the hand holding the stone, so that we were both touching the warm thing, both bound to it. "I don't need a promise of later. I've spent my life among promises of later; archives are full of them. Letters never sent, dedications written to the dead. What I need is to know it's real *now*, even if it stays unwoken. Say it plainly. Once. So I can carry it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, too far to matter yet, thunder—or artillery—muttered along the horizon.

"It's real," I said. "I love you. Plainly, once, for the record: I love you, Wren Vale, and when this is done—when the Gate is open and the lies are burned and whatever world comes next exists—if you'll have me, I intend to be unbearable about it for the rest of my life."

She laughed and cried at the same time, quietly, the way scholars do everything, and leaned her forehead against mine, and we stayed like that without leaning any further, which felt like the bravest thing either of us had ever done.

"To be continued," she whispered. "In the archive sense. Unresolved volume."

"Cliffhanger," I agreed. "Very marketable."

* * *

**Wren**

We slept in shifts, as always, and it was my watch when the sky to the south went wrong.

Not firelight this time—not the orange of a burning district. Something paler, colder, spreading along the horizon like frost across a window. I stood at the broken window of the waystation and watched it and felt the codex's final entry rearranging itself in my memory, the line I had read a hundred times since the archive: *when the pale fires march upon the free shore.*

"August." My voice came out wrong, and she was awake instantly, hand on her knife, because that is who she is. "Look south."

She looked. I watched her face do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic I was doing, arriving at the same terrible sum.

"That's not the cities burning," she said slowly. "That's the army. That's the vanguard moving at night with magefire. They're not waiting for spring. They're not even waiting for dawn."

"The shrine—"

"Is three days away, and the cities are two." She was already pulling her boots on, movements quick and certain, and I understood suddenly that this was what she had been all along, under the charm-seller: a woman built for emergencies. "We can't outrun that. Nobody can."

I picked up the satchel. The rubbings, the codex, eleven words that could unmake a century. Three days of evidence, two nights before the war arrived, and one confession, newly made and carefully shelved, burning steady as a banked coal between us.

"Then we split the difference," I heard myself say. "We go tonight. We stop sleeping in shifts."

August looked at me—at the satchel, at the southern sky, at whatever she saw in my face—and nodded once.

Outside, the wind changed direction, and carried with it, faint and unmistakable, the smell of smoke.

And beneath my coat, in the satchel against my heart, the codex pages shifted as if stirred by that same wind—as if the century-old ink itself had begun, very softly, to glow.
