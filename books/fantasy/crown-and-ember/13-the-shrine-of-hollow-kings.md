## 13. The Shrine of Hollow Kings

The sister-shrine did not want to be found.

We had walked the salt road for two days, following instructions August's mentor had left in a cipher only she could read—a riddle of standing stones and drowned waymarkers, of "where the river forgets its name." The land grew stranger as we went. The heather turned grey, then white, then to something like ash that crunched underfoot without ever seeming burned. By the second evening I stopped asking what it meant and started writing down where it began, because that was the kind of scholar I was: even with inquisitors at our backs and a bounty on both our heads, some part of me kept taking notes.

August noticed. Of course she noticed.

"You're cataloguing," she said, not unkindly. We were sharing the last of the bread, sitting back to back against a leaning menhir so old its carvings had worn to suggestion. "The end of the world, and you're cataloguing."

"Evidence doesn't survive enthusiasm," I said. "It survives method."

She laughed—short, surprised, the laugh of someone who hadn't expected to find anything funny this far from the city. Then she leaned her head back against my shoulder, just briefly, just long enough to mean something, and stood up and said we should make the last mile before dark.

I have thought about that moment more times than any other on this whole terrible road. Not the gate. Not the trial. That small weight against my shoulder, two seconds of borrowed peace. If you want to know why I did everything that came after—the rubbings, the copies, the courtroom—you could do worse than start there.

* * *

We found the shrine at moonrise, exactly where the cipher promised: beneath the hill that looked like a closed eye.

It wasn't hidden so much as *unremembered*. The entrance was a fissure in the hillside, half-choked with root and stone, and when August pressed her palm to it the way she pressed her palm to the Ember Gate, the rock didn't open—it *recognized*. A seam of ember-light ran up through the stone like a vein remembering how to beat, and the hill exhaled, and the roots drew back from the doorway as if they'd been holding it shut out of respect rather than accident.

"Seven years," August murmured. "Seven years I've been keeper, and I never knew this place existed."

"Your mentor did."

"My mentor knew a great many things she died before telling me." There was no bitterness in it anymore. Just the flat grief of someone who has finished being angry at the dead and moved on to being angry at everyone else.

Inside, the air was dry and warm and smelled faintly of hot iron, the same smell the vault beneath the city had. The passage descended in a slow spiral, and the walls changed as we went—from rough-cut granite to fitted blocks to something smoother, paler, veined with that same ember-light. I dragged my fingers along it as we walked and felt a hum, very faint, like a struck bell heard through a wall.

Then the passage opened, and I forgot how to breathe.

The chamber was vast—a hollowed hill, ribbed with pillars, and every surface, floor to unreachable ceiling, was carved.

Not decorated. *Carved.* Words, tens of thousands of them, in the old high script that only three people in the capital could read and I was, until recently, one of them. Between the columns of text ran images in deep relief: processions, harvests, households, children. And above them all, repeated across every wall like a refrain, a single image: a crowned figure reaching into a small glowing house and drawing out a handful of light.

The same image. Over and over. On every wall. For as far as the ember-light reached.

"Wren," August said quietly. She had gone still in the way she went still before a sealing ritual, all her restlessness gathered into one point. "Tell me what it says."

So I did what scholars do. I found the beginning.

* * *

The beginning was over the entrance, where beginnings usually are, and it was a dedication—which is to say, a confession dressed in ceremony. My hands were shaking by the third line. August watched me translate aloud, and I watched the color leave her face word by word.

*"In the first year of the Binding, Aldric the First, Crowned by Necessity, caused this record to be cut, that those who come after might know the price their comfort was purchased with, and judge us as they find true."*

"Aldric," August said. "The founder. The *Liberator*. The man they name every bridge and hospital after."

"The same." I moved along the wall, translating as fast as my eyes could pull the meaning from the stone. It was a census. That was the horror of it—that it was *bureaucratic*. Parish by parish, household by household, the founding dynasty had recorded what it took. *"From the house of Merrin at Coldford, the gift of mending, taken. From the widow Anthe at Greywater, the gift of finding, taken. From the miller's son Osric, the gift of stilling water, taken."* Thousands of entries. Tens of thousands. Every gift of magic any common family had ever shown, catalogued, confiscated, carried away to be hoarded behind the Gate.

"They didn't outlaw magic," I said. My voice sounded wrong in the chamber, too small against all that stone. "August. They didn't outlaw magic. They *nationalized* it. Every hanging, every purge, every law for a hundred years—the whole point was to make sure no one outside the crown ever discovered they had anything worth taking. The war, the ancient enemy, the reason for the Gate—all of it manufactured so that stealing from every household in the realm would look like salvation."

"I know," August said. "I'm reading the pictures."

She was standing before one of the great reliefs, the recurring one: the crowned figure and the little glowing house. But now, with the text to anchor it, I saw details I'd missed. The figure's hand didn't reach gently. The door of the house was broken. Small figures inside—children, by their size—were carved with their arms raised, and the sculptor had given them faces, and the faces were not grateful.

"There's more," I said. "There's always more. Look—" I had found a section set apart, framed like scripture, and it was. It was the origin of the lie itself. *"In the year of the False War, the King's own companies burned three villages of his loyal subjects and named the ashes an enemy's work, and the realm wept and gave him its gifts willingly thereafter. Let it be cut here that the enemy never was. Let it be cut here that we made him."*

Silence. The ember-light pulsed once, twice, like a slow heartbeat.

August sat down. Not gracefully—she simply folded, right there on the stone floor, in the dust of a century's undisturbed dark, and put her head in her hands. I had seen her afraid. I had seen her grieve. This was neither. This was the particular devastation of a woman discovering that the promise she had built her entire life upon—the sacred duty, the lonely vigil, the seven years of small charms and smaller comforts—had been the lock on someone else's theft.

"My mentor died for this," she said, into her hands. "They hanged her for *heresy*, Wren. For whispering that the Gate should open. And the whole time, the proof was sitting under a hill two days' walk from the city, waiting for someone to come read it."

"Then we read all of it," I said. "Every word. And then we take it home."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about truth: it weighs.

By the third hour in the shrine my shoulders ached as though I'd been carrying sacks of grain. Nothing physical had changed—I was kneeling on stone with charcoal and paper, doing work I loved—but the knowledge had mass, and it accumulated. Every entry I copied added to it. *Taken. Taken. Taken.* The census of the stolen went on for walls and walls, parishes I knew by name, families whose descendants probably sold fish in the Undermarket or swept floors in the archive where I'd spent six years dutifully shelving the sanitized version of history.

I worked until my fingers were black to the wrist. Rubbings first—paper stretched over the carved letters, charcoal worked across, so that even if my translation were questioned the stones themselves would testify in exact silhouette. Then fair copies of my translation, written small and tight in a cipher of my own devising, distributed across four separate notebooks so that no single seizure could destroy the whole. August watched me build the redundancy without comment, but I caught her expression: something between admiration and sorrow, as if she were watching me armor myself for a battle she wished I didn't have to fight.

"You should sleep," she said, somewhere past midnight by the feel of it.

"So should you."

"One of us has to keep watch." She settled against a pillar near the entrance, knife loose in her hand, and looked at me across the ember-lit dark. "Wren. What you're making in there. Do you understand what it will do?"

"It will turn the free cities," I said. "If the envoy is honest—and I think she was, God help us—they've already decided to surrender. You don't surrender to a war you believe is righteous. You surrender to one you believe is *lost*. Show them this—" I gestured at the walls, at ten thousand carved confessions, "—and the war stops being lost. It becomes *ours*. Every farmer whose great-grandmother's gift is listed on that wall becomes a combatant. Corvane's army is made of those farmers' grandchildren."

"And when they realize it," August said quietly, "they will burn everything the dynasty built. Maybe everything, full stop. Truth isn't gentle, Wren. I've guarded a sealed door for seven years because people agreed it was kinder shut."

"That door was guarding a lie."

"I know." She turned the knife over in her fingers. "I'm not arguing. I'm asking if you understand the cost. Because I've paid costs before, and I'll tell you the trick of them: you never pay them all at once. You pay in installments. And the interest is always worse than you budgeted for."

I put down the charcoal. My hands were steady; that surprised me. "My whole life," I said, "I believed the archive was the most important room in the world. That if you kept the records safe, you kept the truth safe, and if you kept the truth safe, everything else would follow. Six years. And then I opened one codex and found out the records themselves were the crime scene. Everything I trusted about my work was built on top of this." I touched the wall beside me, the cool stone, the faint pulse beneath. "So yes. I understand the cost. I'm paying it already. But I would rather die carrying the real thing than live comfortably curating the forgery."

August looked at me for a long moment. The ember-light moved on her face, and her scar caught it, the pale line the prophecy had named me to find.

"There it is," she said softly. "That's the thing I couldn't name, the night you walked into my shop. It wasn't the scar. It was that look. Like someone who'd already decided what they'd burn down and was just waiting for the match."

"You gave me the match."

"No," she said. "The Gate did. I just refused to stand in front of it." She smiled then, tired and real, and I understood that whatever we were deferring on the salt road, it was not going to stay deferred forever. Some debts collect themselves.

* * *

I slept two hours, dreamed of burning villages carved in stone, and woke to August shaking my shoulder—not gently.

"Light," she whispered. "Outside. Moving. More than one torch."

I was on my feet with the notebooks bundled against my chest before my mind fully woke, which told me something about what the last week had made of me. We killed the ember-light—August pressed her palm to the wall and the veins dimmed to nothing, and the shrine became a black throat around us—and we listened.

Voices. At least four. And beneath the voices, a sound I knew from the archive district, from the night the inquisitors first came: the wet crunch of a crowbar persuading old stone.

"They followed us," I breathed.

"No." August's voice was flat and certain. "They were *sent*. Corvane traced the vault flare to my district weeks ago. He's known about the sister-shrine longer than we have—he just needed us to lead him to the codex connection." She laughed, low and bitter. "God. We led them straight here. Two days of careful tradecraft, straight to the one place in the world where the evidence lives."

"We can defend the entrance—"

"With what? I have a knife and a bad attitude. You have charcoal." She was quiet a moment, and I could hear her thinking, that rapid practical arithmetic she did. Then: "The rubbings stay with me. The ciphers go in your coat. If they take one of us, the other walks out with half the truth, and half is enough to seed it."

"Don't talk like that."

"Scholar," she said, and her hand found mine in the dark and squeezed once, hard, "we are past talking like anything else. Listen—they're widening the fissure. We have maybe a hundred breaths. Is there another way out?"

There was. I had mapped it in my first hour, the way I map everything: a crawl-space behind the eastern pillar where the census wall ended, breathing cold air, angling upward toward the hill's far side. I told her, and she nodded, and we were moving—

Which is when the first torchlight spilled down the spiral passage, and a voice I had hoped never to hear again called out, echoing off ten thousand carved confessions:

"Archivist Vale. Inquisitor-General Corvane thanks you for the translation. He's been trying to read the old script himself for *months*."

They had known the language barrier was the only lock on this place. And they had used us as the key.

August's hand closed around my wrist, and we ran for the crawl-space as boots rang against the stones of the Hollow Kings.
