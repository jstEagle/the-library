## 14. Betrayal in the Undermarket

The message came on a strip of birchbark, folded into a paper bird that fluttered through the Undermarket's smoke-hole and landed in my tea.

*Route burned,* it said, in a hand I'd known for twenty years. *Come to the Drowned Lamp. Alone. —Dess.*

I read it three times while the market went about its business around me—a fence haggling over a crate of unlicensed luck-knots, a child conjuring light in her cupped hands the way children here did when they thought no one with a badge could see, which lately was never. The bird sat on the rim of my cup, wings folding down into ordinary bark.

Dessin Marlowe had been the first friend I made below ground. Before Wren, before the codex, before any of it—back when I was a new Keeper sneaking down from my shop at midnight to pour a little of the Gate's warmth into charms I couldn't sell above. Dess ran the Drowned Lamp, a tavern built into an old cistern where the water still sang against the stones. He'd hidden me during the Purge scare of my third year. He'd named his daughters after two of my mother's lullabies.

He also had the worst instincts of any man I loved, and he always wrote *route burned* when he meant *you're being hunted*, and he always asked me to come alone, and I always came.

I should have learned by now.

* * *

The Drowned Lamp smelled of tallow and river-moss, as it always had. Dess stood behind the bar polishing a mug that was already clean, which was how I knew everything had already gone wrong. His wife wasn't there. His daughters' chalk drawings were gone from the wall behind the taps—the little suns and cats and crooked houses they redrew every week.

"Dess." I kept my voice easy, the way you do when you've spotted the tripwire and don't want the man who set it to know. "Where are the girls?"

"With their aunt. In Harrowfen." He didn't look up from the mug. "They left yesterday."

"Yesterday." Yesterday we'd come back from the shrine with rubbings of stone older than the crown, with proof pressed into wax paper that the founding dynasty had reached into every hearth in the realm and stolen what was burning there. Yesterday Wren had spread those rubbings across a table in the Lamp's back room and said, voice shaking, *this ends the lie*. Yesterday I'd told Dess everything, because he was Dess, because he'd earned it, because twenty years of friendship felt like a kind of armor.

"How much did they pay you?" I asked.

That got his eyes up. They were red-rimmed and steady, and I hated him a little for the steadiness, and hated myself more for the hate.

"Nothing," he said. "That's the thing, Aug. That's the whole rotten thing. Nobody paid me anything."

"Corvane's people found you anyway."

"A woman came in two nights ago. Grey cloak. She ordered water—just water—and she drank it slow, and then she put three things on my bar." He held up fingers. "A warrant for my arrest. A writ saying the girls go to the Foundling Houses if I'm taken. And a list of names of everyone in this market who's helped you, with little ticks beside the ones they'd already collected." His hand shook, finally, on the last finger. "They had your name ticked, Aug. And mine. Unticked. Like a sum somebody hadn't gotten around to doing yet."

"So you did it before they had to ask twice."

"I did it so they'd never have to ask at all." He came around the bar, and I stepped back without meaning to, and something in his face cracked at that—something old and tired and permanent. "You think I wanted this? You think I sat up all night feeling noble? I sat up all night feeling like exactly what I am, which is a father before I'm anything else. They gave me until dawn. I gave them your route to the shrine and the hour you'd pass the Salt Gate coming back, and then I went upstairs and watched my girls sleep and thought: *this is what survival costs now. This is the going rate.*"

"The rubbings," I said. My voice sounded far away. "Wren's copies of the codex pages. Everything was in the back room."

"I know." He closed his eyes. "Inquisitors took it an hour ago. All of it."

"And Wren?"

"Wren—" He stopped. Started again. "Wren stayed behind to burn what wouldn't fit in the bags. The inquisitors doubled back. Aug, they took her off this street in a black wagon not two hours after you left for the shrine."

* * *

The back room door hung open on its one good hinge. Ash in a basin, half-burned scraps of cipher notes curling at the edges like dead leaves. A chair knocked over. One boot-print in candle wax, small—Wren's boots were small—and beside it the deep tread of garrison-issue leather.

On the table, squared neatly in the center, someone had left the wax paper sleeve from one rubbing. Empty. A taunt or a bookkeeping note; with Corvane's people it was always both.

I picked it up. My hands were doing fine work without me, calm and precise, the way they did sealing rituals, the way they'd done everything for seven years. Somewhere under that calm a part of me was screaming in a register only dogs and Gates could hear.

Seven years. Seven years of keeping a secret so heavy I'd forgotten it had a weight, and in three weeks Wren Vale had walked into my shop with a page of ash-written prophecy and made me feel less alone than I had since Halvard died, and now she was in a black wagon headed for the Inquisitorial Court, and the evidence that could have saved the free cities was in a grey-cloaked woman's satchel, and the man who'd sold us was standing in the doorway behind me smelling of tallow and shame.

"You should run," Dess said. "They'll come back for the tavern's regulars. They'll come for you tonight."

"I know."

"Aug—"

"Don't." I turned. He flinched like I'd swung at him. "Don't apologize. Don't explain again. If you apologize, I'll have to decide whether to forgive you, and I can't do that right now, because if I forgive you then I'm agreeing that what you did was forgivable, and if I don't then I'm saying a man should let his children go to the Foundling Houses for a cause, and I—" My voice went out from under me. I stood in the wrecked back room of my friend's tavern and heard myself say, quietly: "There isn't a clean answer, Dess. That's the whole cruelty of it. There is no version of last night where everybody wakes up whole."

He nodded. Tears went down his face and he didn't wipe them, and I understood suddenly that he'd wanted me to hit him, to curse him, to give him some wound he could carry as payment. I wouldn't give it to him. Kindness was crueler, sometimes. I'd spent seven years learning that.

"Get your wife out of Harrowfen," I said. "Whatever aunt has the girls—if Corvane's people unticked your name once, they'll retick it when this is done. Go north. Don't tell me where."

"Where will you go?"

"To get her back." I tucked the empty wax-paper sleeve inside my coat, next to my heart, where I kept things that hurt. "And then to finish what she started, even if I have to do it with nothing but my own voice and a room full of people who'd rather not listen."

* * *

The Undermarket had gone quiet in the wrong way by nightfall. Not empty—never empty—but quiet the way a body goes quiet listening for footsteps. Stalls shuttered early. The light-conjurers worked with cupped hands and no laughter. At the mouth of the Weftway, two of Corvane's wardens stood checking papers, and I watched a knot-seller I'd known for a decade turn his own customers in with a jerk of his chin, and couldn't even blame him, because Dess's arithmetic was running in every chest down here: *father first, citizen second, friend somewhere far below that, if there's room.*

Sela found me in the passage behind the tannery, where I was trying to think and mostly failing.

"They're holding her in the Court cells," she said. Sela ran the mender-stall, sharp-eyed and quicker-tongued, one of the few who'd stood with me openly. "Not the prison. The Court. Trial in three days—they want it public. They want the city to watch them break an archivist on the steps of law."

"Public is good," I said. "Public means witnesses."

"It means gallows with an audience." She gripped my arm. "August. The rubbings are gone. The copies are gone. Whatever Wren memorized, they'll wring out of her before three days are up. What do you actually have?"

I looked at my hands. Charm-seller's hands. Keeper's hands. Hands that had held a promise shut for seven years and a truth open for three weeks, and were now empty of everything except one blank sheet of wax paper and a friend's betrayal folded flat inside me like a letter I'd keep rereading.

"I have the Gate," I said. "And I have a courtroom full of people who've all had magic stolen from their grandmothers' hearths and been told it was for their protection. Corvane wants an audience." I felt something settle in my chest—not hope, nothing so gentle, but its stubborn cousin. "Let's give him one worth remembering. Get word to everyone who owes me, everyone who doesn't, everyone who's afraid. Three days."

"And you?"

"I'm going to need a disguise, a writ, and a very stupid amount of nerve."

Sela almost smiled. "Same as always, then."

* * *

I went back past the Drowned Lamp once, on my way to the Weftway, though I had no reason to and every reason not to. The lamps were lit. Through the window I could see Dess at his bar, alone, wiping down wood that was already clean, and beside his elbow a fresh sheet of chalk paper where one of his girls must have drawn before she left: a crooked house, a crooked sun, and two stick figures holding hands under it, one tall and one taller, drawn from memory of a story her father must have told a hundred times.

*The charm lady who keeps the dark warm,* Dess used to say, when they asked about me.

I stood in the cold with my breath smoking and found, to my genuine surprise, that I could not hate him. I tried. I turned it over looking for the hot coal of it and found only grief, and under the grief a terrible understanding, and under that the question I knew would follow me up out of the Undermarket and all the way to whatever came next:

If they'd taken Halvard's memory and put it on a table and said *open the Gate or he burns*—what would seven-years-ago August have done?

The same thing. To the letter. That was the sickness of it. Not weakness, not cowardice—just love, weaponized by men who'd never needed to love anything to sleep soundly.

I pulled my coat tight and climbed toward the surface, toward curfew and lamplight and the long road to a courtroom where an archivist with ink-stained fingers would stand alone in front of the whole machinery of a lie.

Three days.

Hold on, Wren. I'm coming, and I'm bringing the truth with me—even if it's only the piece of it that fits in my mouth.

Above me, far beneath the city, so deep that most people had forgotten it was there at all, the Ember Gate pulsed once against my senses—slow, patient, and bright as a struck match.
