## 15. The Trial of Wren Vale

They dressed me for the occasion.

Two warders came at dawn with a bundle under their arms: a plain grey smock, unbleached wool, the uniform of the accused. No buttons. No laces. Nothing a heretic could hang herself with in the night. They took my boots too, and left me shoes of soft leather that made me walk like a ghost in my own cell.

"You'll want to eat," the older one said. He was maybe fifty, with the flat, tired face of a man who had done this work so long it had stopped being anything at all. "The tribunal runs long."

I ate. Bread, thin soup, water that tasted of the copper pail. I had expected to be sick with fear. Instead I found myself cataloguing—old habit, archivist's habit—the texture of the bread, the angle of light through the grate, the number of steps from my cot to the door (nine). If these were to be my last hours, some part of me insisted on filing them properly.

Another part of me was doing arithmetic.

Three days since they'd taken me on the salt road. Three days since August's face had disappeared behind the closing door of the wagon, since the shrine rubbings had been torn out of my hands by men who smelled of lamp oil and iron. Corvane had the evidence now. He would burn it, or lock it in the deepest vault of the Bastion, and either way the stone beneath the Hollow Kings' shrine would keep its secret another century.

Unless someone spoke it.

That was the thought I kept returning to, turning it over like a coin whose edge I hadn't yet read. A public trial. A gallery. Inquisitors, clerks, witnesses—citizens dragged in off the street because the law required an audience for heresy. The law wanted people to *watch*. The law believed that watching taught obedience.

The law, I thought, had not read enough history to know what crowds do when you hand them a story better than your own.

* * *

The courtroom had been a granary once. I knew this because everything in Ashfall's old quarter had been something else first—a temple become a courthouse, a courthouse become a prison, a prison become whatever the usurper's ministries needed it to be this decade. The city wore its past like paint layers, and every regime scraped down to bare wood before applying its own coat.

They brought me up through a side passage into the dock, and for one disorienting moment the room was nothing but noise—hundreds of bodies packed onto benches, the murmur swelling as I appeared, then falling away in a wave, bench by bench, until the silence itself felt like a held breath.

Inquisitor-General Corvane presided from a raised chair beneath the scales-and-flame of the Ministry of Purity. He did not look at me when I entered. That was deliberate; I understood the choreography. The accused is meant to search the judge's face for mercy. Withholding it is the first sentence.

Instead he watched the gallery. Counting them, perhaps. Or reading them. I had seen him do this once before, through a doorway, in the Bastion—his eyes moving across a room the way a man checks a ledger for errors.

To his left sat the tribunal: three assessors in dove-grey robes, a recorder with fresh ink, and behind them, ranged along the wall, six scribes whose job, I would learn, was to write down every word I spoke so that none of it could be claimed to have been misunderstood.

Good, I thought. Write it down. Every word.

"Prisoner," the middle assessor said, "state your name for the record."

"Wren Vale." My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Junior archivist, Third Repository, eleven years' service."

"Your credentials were revoked yesterday."

"I know. I burned them myself."

A ripple through the gallery. Corvane's gaze finally came to me, and there it was—not hatred, which I could have fought, but attention. Total, patient attention. The look of a man listening for the exact note where the music goes wrong.

"The charge," the assessor continued, "is heresy against the Doctrine of Sealed Flame. Namely: the propagation of false histories concerning the Ember War, the founding of the realm, and the lawful suppression of wild magic. Do you confess?"

Here it was. The hinge of the whole machine. Confess, and be fined or flogged, returned to some quiet corner of the archives with my tongue still mine. Refuse, and the tribunal would proceed to evidence, to testimony, to the slow ceremonial machinery of condemnation—and somewhere in that machinery, a rope.

I thought about August. About her hands on the gate-wardings, seven years of small kindnesses sold over a counter while she carried the heaviest secret in the world alone. About her mentor, hanged for the same silence I was being offered the chance to buy back.

"No," I said. "I recite."

Corvane's head tilted, very slightly. The word wasn't in their script.

"Inquisitor-General," I said, louder, addressing him directly because the gallery needed to see me address him directly, "you have seized documents from me. Rubbings taken from the sister-shrine at Hollow Kings. You believe them forged, or corrupted, or the work of seditionists. I am a trained archivist. I certify provenance for the Third Repository—I have done it eleven years. So let me state plainly, for your record and your scribes: those rubbings are genuine. The stone is genuine. And the stone says—"

"That will do," Corvane said.

"—that the founding dynasty did not *defeat* wild magic. They *stole* it."

"Gag him," Corvane said, almost gently, and the room moved.

* * *

They didn't gag me. That was the first surprise.

Two warders crossed the floor toward the dock, and I opened my mouth to keep speaking anyway, to spend breath faster than they could close the distance—but Corvane raised one finger and they stopped. Mid-stride. Like hounds.

He studied me for a long moment. Then he turned to the assessors and said something too low for me to hear. The middle assessor frowned. Corvane said it again, and whatever he said made the man sit back as though the chair had been pulled out from under his assumptions.

When Corvane turned back to me, he said: "Let him speak, then. Let us hear what a junior archivist believes entitles him to hang with a full throat."

And I understood, in a cold flash, what he was doing. He was giving me rope of a different kind. He had read the gallery the way he read all rooms—he knew that silencing a small grey man mid-sentence would make a martyr of whispers, but letting him talk would make an example of him. Every heresy, given enough rope, strangled itself in front of a proper audience. That was doctrine. That was *faith*. Corvane believed, truly and completely, that truth was on his side of the scales.

He was going to let me speak because he was certain I would lose.

Later—in the years after, when people asked me how I found the courage—I would never quite know how to explain that it wasn't courage at all. It was bookkeeping. Eleven years of certification, of provenance chains, of knowing exactly which sources carried weight. I looked out at that gallery—fishwives and clerks and two off-duty soldiers near the back, a woman holding a sleeping child, an old man with the calloused hands of a smith—and I thought: *they've never been permitted to hear it. Not once in a hundred years. Nobody has ever stood up in a room full of them and simply read the ledger aloud.*

Somebody should. It might as well be the person who found the page.

"Hear then," I said, "the account of the Ember War. As recorded—not by me, not by any seditionist, but by the dynasty's own quartermasters. Their own paymasters' rolls. Their own private correspondence, sealed in the Third Repository under writs your ministry has never bothered to open, because no one imagined anyone would look."

The recorder's pen scratched. The gallery leaned forward without seeming to know it was leaning.

"There was no invasion from the eastern wilds," I said. "There were no ember-cults burning villages. There was no war at all—at least, not the war in your schoolbooks. What the rolls show is this: in the spring of the year the codices call 312, the household registries of every province in the realm were recalled to the capital 'for correction.' Correction took fourteen months. When the registries returned, every entry recording a household gift—the old covenant, the one where magic lived in kitchens and forges and birthing-rooms and belonged to everyone who tended it—had been struck. Rewritten. Reassigned to the crown."

A voice from the gallery—someone couldn't help it—"Reassigned? Magic can't be—"

"It can," I said. "It was. The Gate doesn't make power, citizens. It *holds* it. It has been holding yours for a hundred years. Your grandmother's hearth-fire. Your grandfather's green thumb. The song your mother sang that could calm a colicking infant—that was never just a good song. It was taken. Filed. Locked behind the Ember Gate under a seal, and called a great victory."

"Prisoner," the assessor said weakly, "these claims—"

"These claims are itemized." I turned to the scribes along the wall. "Rolls 44 through 61, Repository vault C, accession numbers I will dictate if your recorder can keep pace. Cross-reference the grain levies of the same seasons—because here is the part the shrine stone makes plain, and the part your General already knows, since he has had my rubbings for three days and an archivist of his quality will have verified them within one. There were staged battles. Villages evacuated in advance and burned empty. Soldiers paid double rations to weep convincingly for artists' sketches. The Ember War was *manufactured*, gentlemen, in its entirety, to justify one thing: a law making it treason for ordinary people to hold what had always been theirs. The Doctrine of Sealed Flame is not a shield over the realm. It is a padlock on a stolen house."

The gallery had gone very quiet. The kind of quiet that has weight and temperature.

I looked at Corvane. He had not moved. But his hands—resting on the armrests of his chair—had gone absolutely still, and I remembered what August had told me about him once, in the Undermarket, in the blue hour before the raid: *he's not a liar. That's what makes him dangerous. He believes the cage is mercy.*

"Say it's false," I said to him, and the room heard me say it. "You've had three days. Say the rubbings are forged. Produce the real stone. Open vault C. You are a believer, Inquisitor-General—I have never doubted that. So *verify*."

For one heartbeat—one long, suspended heartbeat in which the whole granary hung on him—I thought he might actually do it. I have thought about that heartbeat many times since. I believe there was a version of Corvane, standing in that room, who wanted nothing more than to open the vault himself, certain to the marrow that the rolls would prove me wrong, that the forgery would unravel, that the world would snap back into the shape his doctrine promised him it held.

Then, behind him, one of the dove-grey assessors leaned over to whisper in another's ear, and I watched Corvane follow the whisper with his eyes and arrive, a half-second later, at whatever the whisper contained—and I watched him understand that he was not permitted to verify. That verification had been forbidden to him, too, all along. By whom, and why, and what else the rolls in vault C might say about the men who gave him his writs—I don't know what he saw in that half-second. I only know that when his gaze came back to me, something behind it had shifted a single degree, like a door left unlatched.

"The prisoner's testimony is noted," he said, and his voice was perfectly level, and only I, at that distance, heard the effort it cost. "It is also heresy. The tribunal will—"

He didn't finish.

Because the old man with the smith's hands stood up.

* * *

I want to get this right, because everything afterward happened fast, and memory compresses fast things into a single roar. But the beginning deserves its own slow beat, because the beginning was one man and one sentence.

The smith stood up—he had to brace himself on the bench-back, an old man's rise—and he said, in a voice cracked with age and carrying anyway:

"My mother sang to the goats."

That was all. Four words. Nonsense, out of context. Except it wasn't out of context, because I had just spent ten minutes telling five hundred strangers where their songs had gone, and I watched the sentence land in the gallery the way a spark lands in dry flax.

A woman near him laughed—nervous, reflexive—and then stopped laughing, because the smith wasn't joking. His face was wet.

"She sang and the goats came home in any storm," he said. "Every farm on our valley said so. We thought she was blessed. Then the Registries came and corrected us, and after that year the goats ran wild every winter, and my mother said her songs had gone flat, like a cracked bell, and she died thinking she'd lost the blessing through some sin of hers." He turned, slowly, and looked up at Corvane, and his voice shook. "You put it in a vault. You put my mother's blessing in a *vault* and told her she'd lost it."

"Sit down," a warder said.

"Sit down yourself," said the woman with the sleeping child—who was no longer sleeping. "My son was born blue and cold and my aunt breathed on him three times and he pinked up like a plum, and the midwife swore afterward she'd never seen it, except she *had*, everyone's aunt did it, before the corrections—"

"ORDER," the assessor shouted, hammering, and the hammering was the worst thing he could have done, because order was exactly what the room had decided, collectively, in the space of ninety seconds, that it was finished with.

I did not incite the riot. I want that in whatever record survives me. I stood in the dock with a sore wrist and a heretic's smock and I said nothing at all, because there was nothing left to add. The crowd did it themselves. That was the whole point. That was always the whole point: you cannot give people back a hundred years of stolen fire, but you can give them back one true sentence, and watch them pass it hand to hand like a bucket brigade, each one adding their own—*my uncle dowsed water with a hazel twig, my grandmother's bread never failed, the lamplighters used to sing the fog off the harbor*—until the gallery was not a gallery anymore but a chorus, and the chorus was standing, and the benches were scraping, and somewhere at the back the two off-duty soldiers were not helping the warders, which was the detail Corvane would later say he should have seen coming, because armies are drawn from households too, and every soldier's grandmother had sung to somebody's goats.

The warders waded in. The assessors fled. The recorder, to his eternal credit, kept writing until a thrown inkwell ended his professional detachment.

Through all of it Corvane stayed in his chair.

I will carry the image of that to my grave: a man alone on a raised dais in a storming room, hands still resting on the armrests, face composed, eyes fixed on the middle distance—not on me, not on the mob—on some interior horizon where a doctrine he had served for thirty years was visibly cracking along lines he had never authorized anyone to draw. He looked, in that moment, less like a judge than a man reading a ledger he had audited a thousand times and finding, at last, the error. Finding that it went all the way down.

Then the doors burst open at the back, and it got worse, and it got different.

* * *

They came in wearing the colors of the Second Vanguard—not inquisitors' dove-grey but army red-and-black, mud on their boots, march dust on their cloaks, and leading them a courier with a sealed dispatch case and the specific, terrible urgency of a man whose news outranks everyone in the building.

The riot didn't stop so much as pause, the way weather pauses around a lightning strike. Even the warders froze.

"General Corvane?" the courier called across the chaos. "Dispatch from the Wall. Priority seal. The vanguard requests—" he glanced at the heaving gallery, the overturned benches, the heretic standing untouched in the dock, and visibly decided none of it mattered— "requests immediate instruction. The free cities have opened their gates, sir. All of them. Unopposed. And the army column has halted at the Salt Ford and refuses—"

He stopped. Swallowed.

"Refuses to advance, sir. The men are saying—" and here his voice dropped, though in the ringing silence of that paused room every soul in the granary heard every word— "the men are saying their hands have started glowing."

Beside me, at the dock rail, a warder was staring at his own fingers with an expression I recognized from a vision the Gate had shown me in the dark beneath the city: wonder arriving where obedience used to live.

Corvane rose from his chair at last. He looked at the courier. At the gallery. At me.

"Untie the prisoner," he said.

And then, quieter, in a voice I was perhaps the only living person positioned to hear entire, the Inquisitor-General of Ashfall said the sentence that sent me running for the door before the ropes were even off my wrists:

"And someone find me the charm seller. Before the army does."
