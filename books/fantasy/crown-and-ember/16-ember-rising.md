## 16. Ember Rising

The Undermarket taught me that a riot is just a rumor with teeth.

I stood in the smuggler's tunnel beneath Tanner's Row, watching forty people I'd sold love-charms and fever-knots to over seven years argue about whether to die for me. Torchlight made their faces into something older than the city above us—faces from the century before magic went underground, maybe. Faces that remembered being whole.

"We don't storm anything," said Marisol Vane, who ran the counterfeit-document stall and had never once in her life done anything she couldn't deny later. "We don't have numbers. We don't have weapons worth the name. We have—" She gestured at the lot of us: a charm-seller, an archivist's ghost of a reputation, two fence-brothers, a hedge-witch whose license had been revoked for healing someone, and a boy of perhaps sixteen who kept touching the knife at his belt like he was checking it was still real. "This."

"We have them not expecting it," said Doss, one of the fence-brothers, "which is worth more than numbers."

"They'll expect something," Marisol said. "The trial's been all anyone talks about for three days. Riots in the gallery. Half the garrison pulled to the courthouse district." She wasn't arguing anymore; she was counting, out loud, the way she counted coins. "Which means the prison convoy moving Wren Vale to the High Keep won't be fat with guards. It'll be lean and fast and mean."

"Lean is what we want," Doss said.

"Mean is what we get," his brother Ferren muttered.

They looked at me then, all of them, because apparently that was the arrangement now: I had fed them and shielded them and lied on their licenses for years, and so when I asked for something impossible, they owed me the courtesy of considering it. Debts called in. I'd known this would happen someday. I hadn't known how much it would cost me to spend them.

"The convoy leaves the Old Prison at dawn," I said. "Takes the Salt Road spur up to the Keep. Two miles of open ground with a cutting at the halfway mark—the old quarry road crosses there. If we're in the cutting before the wagon comes through, we can drop the rockslide Doss has been promising me he could manage since the year I met him."

"I can manage it," Doss said. "With help."

"You have help."

"I have a hedge-witch and a boy with a knife." He scratched his jaw. "It'll do."

Marisol threw up her hands. "And after? Say you stop the wagon. Say you cut her loose. Then the whole city's martial law by noon and every unlicensed practitioner from here to the river gets burned out of their holes, starting with everyone standing in this tunnel."

"That's already coming," I said. Quietly, because it didn't need volume. It had been true since the vault flared, true since Corvane traced the light to my district, true since Wren stood up in that courtroom and recited a hundred years of stolen history until the gallery came apart like wet paper. "It was always coming. The only question left is whose side we're standing on when it lands."

Nobody spoke. The torch hissed. Somewhere far off, water dripped with the patience of stone.

It was the hedge-witch, Olla, who broke it. She'd been silent the whole council, working a knot of red thread between her fingers the way she did when she was deciding something her hands already knew.

"She pulled me out of an inquisitor's cell six years ago," Olla said. "August did. Before I knew her name. A stranger walked into the holding house and bought my freedom with a forged writ and a bribe she couldn't afford." She looked at me. "You told me once you didn't remember why you did it."

"I remember," I said. "You were twenty-two and terrified and you hadn't done anything wrong except exist."

"Well." Olla tied off the thread and tucked it into her sleeve. "Seems like a reason to stand in a quarry cutting at dawn."

One by one—not all of them, not even most of them, but eleven, twelve, thirteen—they stepped toward the torch. Marisol held out longest, arms crossed, jaw set, and then she exhaled the breath of a woman conceding a bet she'd already lost in her heart.

"I'm not fighting," she said. "I'm forging. You'll need writs, seals, a prisoner-transfer order if this goes sideways. Give me until midnight." She jabbed a finger at me. "And August—if we're hanged, I want it noted in somebody's history book that I argued against this."

"Somebody's history book," I said. "That's the idea."

* * *

We were in position three hours before dawn, which is two hours too long to lie on cold stone in October.

The quarry cutting was a throat of granite where the old mine road crossed under the new one—a place the city had built over and forgotten, all rubble and dead bracken and the smell of rusted iron. Above us, the stars were the hard, washed-out kind that come just before frost. Below, the Salt Road spur curved pale as a scar through the dark.

Wren was somewhere inside that dark, in a wagon cage, and I let myself think about her exactly once and then put it away, because thinking about her was a fire that wanted feeding and I needed my wits cold.

Doss had chosen his rock well: a shelf of spoil-heap perched above the crossing, wedged with timber props he'd been loosening since midnight. Olla crouched beside him with both palms flat against the stone, murmuring to it—not a spell anyone licensed would recognize, just the old listening, the asking-stone work my mentor had taught me before the teaching became a crime. The boy with the knife—I'd finally gotten his name, Tam—was stationed at the cutting's mouth with Marisol's forged transfer writ and instructions to look official, which mostly meant looking like he belonged anywhere more than he currently did.

Ferren and I took the low ground behind the fallen scree, crossbows we'd bought from a man who pretended not to know what they were for.

Then we waited, and the waiting did what waiting does.

"Can I ask you something?" Ferren said, barely a whisper.

"If it's quick."

"Why you? Seven years you've kept your head down. Sold your little charms, paid your taxes, kept the Gate shut. Everyone in the Undermarket thought you were the careful one." He turned his head, cheek against the stone, eyes on the road. "What changed?"

I watched the empty road and thought about the honest answer and decided, at last, to give one.

"A scholar walked into my shop and read me a page out of a book," I said. "And everything I'd been telling myself about keeping my head down turned out to be the same lie the usurper tells. That silence is safety. That the Gate stays shut because shutting it keeps peace." I flexed my hand; the scar across my palm ached in the cold, the way it always did before weather and, lately, before decisions. "Silence isn't peace, Ferren. Silence is just the sound the theft makes while it's happening."

He was quiet a moment. "My mother used to say the war was a hundred years ago and best left buried."

"Your mother was lied to. So was mine. So was everyone who ever lit a candle in a licensed chapel and thanked the Crown for protecting them from their own hands." Down the road, far off, a lantern swung—just a flicker, gone. My chest tightened. "Here they come."

* * *

They came lean, like Marisol promised. One lead rider, four outriders, the wagon with its barred rear door and its driver, six soldiers in all plus the driver. Lean and fast—and, I saw as they drew level with the cutting, mean: the wagon wasn't the standard prison cart but a black-lacquered thing with iron filigree over the windows. An inquisitor's carriage. Someone important had decided Wren Vale would not arrive at the High Keep alive unless alive suited them.

Or—colder thought—that she wouldn't arrive at all, and the carriage was bound somewhere quieter than the Keep.

No time to find out. The lead rider entered the cutting. The wagon followed, wheels grinding on frost-stiff ruts.

"Doss," I breathed.

The timber cracked. For one horrible heartbeat nothing else happened—then the spoil-heap sighed, shrugged, and came down the slope in a slow-motion roar, stone and frozen earth pouring across the road ahead of the wagon and behind it, sealing the cutting at both ends. Not enough to bury anyone. Enough to make the world a dead end.

The lead rider wheeled. The outriders scattered, shouting. And Tam stepped out of the dark at the wagon's forward wheel with Marisol's forged writ held up like a lantern and his voice pitched into a register I'd have sworn he didn't own:

"Inquisitor's office! Stand down! Quarantine order—the prisoner carries ash-fever!"

Ash-fever. The one word no soldier wants to hear at close quarters. Two of the outriders actually backed their horses up. The lead rider didn't; he kicked his mount forward, sword half-drawn, and got three feet before Olla's asking-stone work finished what Doss started—a crack shot up from beneath the road itself, not large, just enough, and the rider's horse stumbled screaming and went down and the rider with it.

Then it was noise. Crossbows from the scree—I looted the first soldier's attention rather than his flesh, bolt slamming into his shield rim, and he dove for cover exactly where I wanted him, behind the wagon's blind side, away from the door. Ferren dropped the second with a bolt through the thigh, clean and nonlethal, because we were thieves and healers and forgers, not butchers, and I meant to keep it that way as long as the night allowed.

The driver fled. The remaining soldiers clustered around their downed sergeant, and that was the moment the arithmetic of morale did our work for us: five confused men, ash-fever shouted in their ears, a road buried at both ends, and attackers they couldn't count in the dark. They broke. Ran back along the spur the way they'd come, scrambling over the rockfall, leaving their sergeant groaning and their prisoner behind.

I was at the wagon door before the echo faded.

The lock was inquisitor-grade, wax-sealed and warded, the kind that bites. I pressed my scarred palm flat against it anyway, because I'd spent seven years learning the Gate's locks and this was a child's toy beside them, and I whispered the unwinding my mentor had taught me for doors that believed themselves holy. The ward shivered. Bitter smoke curled from the keyhole. The seal split like ripe fruit.

The door swung open onto darkness and a voice, hoarse but unmistakably amused:

"Took you long enough. I had a whole speech prepared about dramatic timing."

"Wren." Her name came out of me like something escaping. I climbed into the cage and found her by touch—wrists chained, cheek bruised, ribs probably cracked from the arrest, and grinning at me in the dark like a scholar who'd just proven a theorem nobody wanted true. "Are you—"

"Later. Cut first, talk after. That's the traditional order."

I sawed through the chains with Ferren's bolt-cutters, and the moment her hands were free she grabbed my collar and kissed me, brief and fierce and tasting of iron, and then pushed me away and said, "The evidence. Corvane seized the shrine rubbings, but I memorized the cipher key. All of it. Every line of the codex is in my head now, August, and they can't burn what's in my head."

"No," I said. "They really can't."

She swayed. I caught her. Behind us, Olla was already binding the sergeant's leg wounds—because of course she was, because that was the whole point of everything, that we were the ones who bound wounds—and Tam stood watch with his forged writ still clutched in his fist like it might protect him from what he'd just become.

"We can't go back through the Undermarket," Marisol had said, and she was right; the tunnel exits would be watched within the hour. So we went the other way, down into the quarry itself, through the drowned lower galleries where the old mine flooded every winter—cold, knee-deep, slow going, but unwatched, because the Inquisition had never bothered to learn the city's forgotten veins.

Halfway through the flooded gallery, Wren stopped, leaned against the stone, and laughed—quietly, helplessly, the laugh of someone whose body had finally caught up to her courage.

"What?" I said.

"The trial," she said. "When they dragged me out, the whole gallery was on its feet. Clerks. Students. A judge's wife. They were saying the words with me, August. The founding dynasty stole the magic. Stole it from every household. I heard it in a hundred voices." She looked at me, water dripping off her chin, eyes shining. "One voice makes truth contagious. I learned that in a courtroom. What do you suppose it does out here?"

I didn't answer. Because down the flooded gallery, faint but carrying, came the sound of bells—the courthouse quarter, ringing not the alarm but the fire-bell, over and over, and underneath the bells, thin and distant and impossible, a crowd roaring.

* * *

Corvane found the wreck of his convoy at midmorning.

I know because he told me. Not that morning—weeks later, or what felt like weeks, though it may only have been days—but I set it down here where it belongs, because the story isn't mine alone anymore.

He stood in the quarry cutting with his cloak beaded with drizzle and his men dragging the rockfall clear, and he read the scene the way he read everything: precisely, completely, without comfort. Forged writ. Ash-fever theater. A warded lock broken by a technique that appeared in no manual the Inquisition possessed. And in the mud beside the wagon tracks, half a boot-print and a smear of blood from a wound already bound—with skill, with care, by someone who had stopped to treat the man she'd helped shoot.

His sergeant, bandaged and gray-faced, watched him read it.

"They treated me," the sergeant said. "The witch. Set the bone, gave me a draught. Left me water and told me—" He hesitated.

"Told you what?"

"That I should ask myself who chained me to the wagon."

Corvane said nothing. He stood a long time in the drizzle, looking east toward the free cities, where the vanguard he'd marched ahead of was even now closing on walls he'd sworn to hold, and looking west toward the courthouse quarter, where the fire-bells had rung all night and the crowd had not dispersed, not once, in three days. Where clerks and students and judges' wives had learned a sentence and could not unlearn it.

Doctrine said the crowd was infection. Doctrine said the cure was fire and cordon and the burning out of every unlicensed vein in the city. Doctrine had carried him thirty years, and he had built himself out of it plank by plank, the way a man builds a house on a foundation he's never once thought to dig beneath.

But doctrine also said the law protected the innocent. And the law had put a wounded scholar in a black carriage bound for a hole no record named. And the people who freed her had bound their enemy's wounds and left him water.

Dig beneath, then.

He gave the order to return to the garrison, and his voice, when it came out, was level and quiet and wrong in a way his officers had never heard—too calm, the calm of a man re-counting a ledger he'd trusted for thirty years and finding, column by column, that the sums no longer balanced.

And in the flooded dark beneath the quarry, moving slowly toward the light, August Okafor carried a bruised scholar on her back and did not yet know that the Inquisitor-General's certainty had begun, that morning, to crack—or that the armies at the wall were closer than any of them dreamed, and the Gate was waiting, patient as stone, for its keeper to come home and choose.
