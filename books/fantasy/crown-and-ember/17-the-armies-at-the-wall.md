## 17. The Armies at the Wall

Corvane had always believed the wall would save them.

That was the doctrine, after all. The whole architecture of his life—forty years of it, from novice inquisitor to Inquisitor-General, from a boy memorizing the Edicts by candlelight to a man who signed execution orders with a steady hand—rested on a single load-bearing truth: *magic is fire, and fire must be walled.* The Ember Gate was the door in that wall. He was the hinge. He had never questioned it, because questioning it would have been like questioning gravity, and he had watched men who questioned gravity fall.

Now he stood on the ramparts of Vell's Crossing and watched the usurper's vanguard come down the salt road like a spill of black ink across white paper, and for the first time in his life, Corvane felt the ground shift under a truth he had built everything on.

They were not stopping at the wall.

* * *

He had received the order three days ago, sealed with the double-crown sigil, written in the hand of Lord Chancellor Merek himself. Corvane knew that hand. He had copied it as an exercise in his novitiate years; Merek had been young then, already chancellor, already famous for the elegance of his script and the coldness of his arithmetic. The order was brief. It was also, Corvane realized as he read it a fourth time, insane.

*The vanguard will arrive within the week. You will hold Vell's Crossing at all costs. No retreat is authorized. The Inquisition garrison will stand to the last man.*

To the last man. Corvane had read those words before, in histories, in dispatches from wars he'd studied the way a surgeon studies anatomy. They were words you wrote about a rearguard action. About a sacrifice. About a door you intended to close behind someone.

But there was no one behind him. There was nothing behind him but the free cities—the same cities the Crown had spent a century calling lawless, heretical, gate-touched—and beyond them, nothing at all but coastline.

He had sent a query. Requesting clarification. Requesting reinforcements. Requesting, if he was honest with himself, one sentence—one single sentence—that would let him keep believing.

The reply came this morning. One line, in Merek's elegant hand:

*Reinforcements are not available. Hold.*

Corvane stood on the wall with the order folded in his glove and did the arithmetic he had spent forty years refusing to do.

* * *

The garrison numbered six hundred. The vanguard, by his scouts' best count, numbered nine thousand, with siege engines behind them and a second column beyond the ridge that hadn't yet crested. Six hundred against nine thousand was not a battle. It was a message, written in the blood of six hundred men, and Corvane was beginning to understand what the message said and to whom.

It said: *the Inquisition died here, defending the realm against the gate-touched heretics.* It said: *look what they cost us.* It said: *when the new edicts come, when the purges widen, remember that the Inquisition gave its last breath holding the door.*

It was a story. A founding myth, manufactured in advance, with Corvane's name carved into its martyrdom before he was even dead.

And the bitterest part—the part that made his hands shake so badly he had to grip the parapet—was that it was a good story. He could see its shape with a craftsman's eye. He had spent his life enforcing stories; he knew a well-made one when he stood inside it. The heroic garrison. The treacherous cities. The noble sacrifice that justified whatever came next. Merek had not even needed to invent the war. He only needed to spend six hundred lives to make it true.

Corvane thought of Wren Vale, standing in the trial hall, reciting stolen history in a voice that shook and did not stop. *The ancient war never happened,* she had said. *You are guarding a door to a theft.* He had ordered her silenced. He had told himself it was mercy—a quick trial, a swift end, cleaner than what the Undermarket traitors got.

She had been right. That was the thing he could no longer un-know. She had stood in chains and spoken the truth while he sat above her in judgment, and every word she'd said had been confirmed this morning by a single elegant line of handwriting: *Hold.*

* * *

"General." Captain Ilsa Renn climbed the rampart stairs two at a time, helm under her arm, sweat cutting tracks through the dust on her face. She was thirty, competent, loyal—the kind of soldier Corvane had spent decades cultivating precisely because she did not ask questions. "Scouts report the second column has crossed the ridge. They'll be in range by dusk."

"And the third?"

Renn hesitated. "There is no third column, General. The scouts have been over the ground twice. The second column is all there is."

All there is. Nine thousand men to kill six hundred and burn a legend into the world. Corvane almost laughed. Almost.

"Have the men been fed?" he asked.

Renn blinked. "Yes, General."

"Then feed them again. Double rations." He turned from the wall. "And Captain—how many of your soldiers can you trust to follow an order they don't understand?"

Her jaw tightened. He watched her decide whether the question was a test. "All of them," she said finally. "That's what you trained them for."

"Yes," Corvane said quietly. "That's exactly what I trained them for." And he heard, in his own voice, the confession underneath: *I built the machine that is now being used to grind me up, and it works perfectly, and it does not care.*

* * *

He went down into the garrison chapel—not a chapel, really; the Inquisition did not worship, it *memorialized*. The Hall of Seals, they called it, where the names of every inquisitor who died in service were cut into slate, floor to ceiling, wall to wall. Forty years of names. Corvane had added more than half of them himself, chisel in hand, believing each one a brick in the wall that kept the fire caged.

He walked the rows now with a lantern, reading names he knew. Novices he'd sworn in. Captains he'd promoted. A boy named Pell who had drowned chasing a smuggler's skiff, seventeen years old, whose mother had sent Corvane a letter thanking him for giving her son a purpose. Purpose. Corvane had signed the order that sent Pell onto that water, and Pell's death had gone into the ledger as *service to the realm*, and the realm had used it, the way it used everything, the way it was using him now.

He stopped at the newest slab. Blank stone, waiting. His own name would go there, eventually. Merek had seen to that in advance—he understood now why the order specified *no retreat*, why the dispatches had stopped coming, why the supply trains had been rerouted south three weeks ago without explanation. The blank slab wasn't a possibility. It was a reservation.

And yet.

And yet the terrible thing, the thing he kept circling like a tongue finding a cracked tooth, was that some part of him still wanted to hold the wall. Not for Merek. Not for the story. But because the doctrine had been his spine for forty years, and a man does not easily stand up straight without one. If the Gate opened—if magic flooded back into the world, into the hands of farmers and fishwives and children—then everything he had done, everyone he had burned, every name on these walls, became not tragedy but *theft*. Not sacrifice but complicity. He had spent his life guarding a lie, and the lie had spent his life like currency, and now the ledger was coming due.

Wren Vale had begged him once, through the bars of her cell—he remembered it with humiliating clarity—"If you're wrong, General, wouldn't you want to know before you die? Wouldn't anyone?"

He had not answered her then. He answered her now, alone, to a hall of dead men's names.

"I wanted to be right," he said. "That was all I ever wanted. To be right."

The lantern guttered. Somewhere above, the first siege engine fired, and the wall shuddered, and dust sifted down between the slates like ash.

* * *

Dusk came, and with it the assault.

Corvane commanded from the central tower because that was where the general belonged, and because the tower had the best view of the road, and because some stubborn remnant of the believer still stood at his post. Below him, six hundred inquisitors held the wall against nine thousand with the desperate competence he had drilled into them, and they were *good*, gods help them, they were better than the vanguard expected, and for two hours the line held, and Corvane found himself weeping without noise, without moving his face, tears simply falling off his jaw onto the battlement stone while he called corrections to the gun crews.

They were dying for a forgery. Every one of them. And they were magnificent.

At full dark, the eastern gate buckled. Renn appeared at his elbow, blood on her cheek that was not hers, and reported the breach with the flat calm of a soldier reading a weather report, and asked for the order.

Every order Corvane had ever given had fit inside the doctrine. This one did not fit anywhere.

"Fall back to the inner keep," he said. "Seal the armory. And Captain—send riders south. Not to the capital." He watched her face in the torchlight. "To the free cities. All of them. Tell them what's coming. Tell them the Crown's own vanguard burned its way through an Inquisition garrison to reach them. Let them see the proof with their own eyes."

Renn stared at him. "General, that's—"

"Treason. Yes." He said it the way he'd once pronounced sentences, evenly, without flinching. "I've spent my life enforcing the law, Captain. I find, at the end of it, that I'd like to know what the truth weighs. Consider it an experiment."

For a long moment she looked at him—at the blood and the dust and the tears he hadn't bothered to hide—and then she saluted, which was the strangest mercy of the whole long day, and went to do it.

* * *

Alone on the tower, Corvane took out the Chancellor's order and read it one final time. *Hold. To the last man.* Elegant script. Cold arithmetic. Somewhere south, in a warm room, Merek was writing the next draft of history, and Corvane's death was already a paragraph in it.

He fed the order to the brazier and watched it curl.

Then he took out the other paper—the one he had written himself, an hour ago, in his own blunt hand. It named the Gate. It named the codex. It named August Okafor, charm-seller, keeper, the woman whose shop he had raided and whose escape still stung like a splinter worked deep into the palm. It named everything he knew, and it was addressed not to the capital but to the woman herself, care of the Undermarket, because there was one thing left he could do that Merek had not planned for.

A true believer, at the end, could still choose what to believe in.

Below, the vanguard poured through the broken gate, and the fires began, and Corvane turned his horse toward the ember light rising over the city where the Gate slept—the Gate he had guarded his whole life without ever once asking what was behind it.

He was going to open the door himself, or die trying to warn the woman who could.
