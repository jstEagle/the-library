## 1. The Date That Wasn't Tomorrow

The dead were complaining about the weather again.

They always did, in autumn, when the wind came down off the Vellamarch hills smelling of iron and old leaves. Theo Amberdale had learned to carve through their chatter the way a man learns to sleep through church bells — not by ignoring it, exactly, but by letting it become the shape of the silence.

*Cold,* said the grandmother who'd been dead forty years and still haunted the shop's back stair. *Colder than the winter we buried the twins. Mark me, boy, it'll snow before Feastday.*

*It's always cold in here,* muttered someone else — a soldier, maybe, or a sailor; his voice came and went like tide. *You'd think a man could get warm even after he's done with breathing.*

Theo didn't answer. Answering was the first mistake. He kept his eyes on the piece of riverbone clamped in his vice and let the knife do the talking: small sure strokes, cutting the first petals of a mourning lily into the charm's face.

The widow Maren Collick had come at opening bell, wringing her gloves, and asked for a charm for her husband's grave the way other people asked for bread — apologetically, as if grief were something she ought to have budgeted better. Forty years married, and the man had gone in his sleep with the harvest still stacked in the barn.

"Something plain," she'd said. "He was a plain man. But—" and here her voice had caught on the hook every grieving voice catches on, "—something that says he was *here*. That he mattered."

Plain and mattering. That was the whole trade, if Theo was honest about it. Bone held memory better than wood or stone — everyone knew that much, even now, even after the outlawing, when saying so aloud could cost you your hands. What nobody knew — what no living soul in Vellamarch could know — was that bone held more than memory for some makers. That when Theo carved a name into riverbone, he sometimes heard the named one answer.

Not today, though. Old Collick was quiet. Some of them were. They crossed over properly, or drifted somewhere the hearing couldn't reach, and left behind only the permanent residents of Vellamarch's dead — the murmurers, the grumblers, the ones with nowhere else to be.

*Boy.* The grandmother again, sharper now. *There's a man standing across the street.*

Theo's knife paused.

*Been there since you lit the lamp. Just standing. Looking at your shop like it owes him money.*

"He's probably waiting for the chandler," Theo said under his breath, and immediately hated himself for it. Talking to the air. That was the second mistake.

*The chandler closed an hour ago,* the grandmother said, with the satisfaction of the dead, who were never wrong about anything except when they were, which was often enough to ruin a man.

Theo didn't look up. Looking up was the third mistake. He finished the lily's third petal, blew the bone dust from the groove, and told himself the tightness in his chest was nothing — the weather, the cold coming early, the ordinary dread of any man in Vellamarch who made his living touching the things the Crown had forbidden.

Five years he'd been careful. Five years of plain charms, honest prices, no names spoken back to widows, no secrets offered to strangers, no sign that Theo Amberdale heard one single thing beyond what ears were built for. Five years was long enough, he told himself, that a man could almost stop flinching at footsteps.

Almost.

---

The bell over the door rang at dusk, and it wasn't the man from across the street — or if it had been him, he'd changed into a bookseller, which was its own kind of strange.

"I'm looking for Amberdale," said the newcomer, setting down a crate with a grunt. "The carver. I've a delivery."

Theo wiped his hands and stood. "You've the wrong idea of my stock, friend. I sell charms and keepsakes. Books aren't—"

"It's one book." The bookseller was young, rain-wet, nervous in the way of men doing something they'd been paid too little for. "Sold to me in a lot off a dead estate, up past Harrow Fen. There's a slip says it goes to the bone-carver on Tanner's Row, and the slip's older than I am, and frankly, sir, I'd just as soon not own it another night."

That was odd. That was several kinds of odd stacked on top of each other. A delivery slip decades old, naming a shop that hadn't existed five years ago, addressed to a man who hadn't lived in Vellamarch then.

"Who paid you?"

"A gentleman. Didn't give a name. Paid double and asked me to come tonight." The young man was already edging toward the door. "Look, it's paper and leather, it's not cursed as far as I can tell, and I've got a wife expecting me. No offense meant to your trade."

"No offense taken," Theo said slowly. "But I'm not paying for—"

"You're not paying. It's paid." The bell rang. The door shut. And through it, faintly, Theo heard the bookseller mutter to the empty street, *"Mad, the whole lot of them,"* and couldn't argue.

He stood a long moment looking at the crate.

*Open it,* said the grandmother, from her stair.

*Don't,* said the soldier-tide voice.

Between the two of them, they covered most of life's decisions, Theo had found.

He opened it.

Under straw and oilcloth lay a book bound in green leather gone dark with age, its corners worn round as river stones. No title on the spine. No title on the cover — only a stamped device he'd never seen and somehow recognized anyway: a crown woven through a garden gate.

His mouth went dry. Every child in Vellamarch learned the shape without learning its meaning, the way children learn to fear deep water without being taught. The Crown and Garden. The sigil that appeared in no official record, on no coin, over no courthouse door — and appeared everywhere if you started looking for it, scratched into cellar walls, knotted into old quilts, hidden in the margins of tax ledgers by clerks fifty years dead.

A book with that stamp was not merely contraband. Contraband got you fined. This was the kind of thing that got a district burned.

*I don't like it,* said the grandmother, uncharacteristically quiet. *I don't like it and I'm dead, which means I've seen worse than you ever will, and I still don't like it.*

"Noted," Theo whispered.

He should burn it. He should burn it right now, unopened, unread, and sweep the ash out with the morning refuse, and tell no one, and never think of it again. That was the smart play. That was five years of staying alive distilled into a single sensible act.

He carried it to the workbench instead, because curiosity was the flaw his mother had scolded him for and the gift the dead relied upon, and because some part of him — the part that had walked out of his home village at nineteen with one bag and no backward glance — already suspected why the book had found its way to *him*, tonight, by the hand of a stranger paid by a nameless gentleman.

The lock was a simple brass clasp, corroded but willing. Inside, the pages were heavy rag paper, yellowed soft as cloth, filled edge to edge with a spidery antique hand. Not scripture. Not law. Prophecy — or what passed for it, the fever-dreams of some long-dead seer, dates and fragments and riddling half-lines, each entry headed by a date in red ink.

Theo turned pages carefully, reading without meaning to, the way you read gravestones in passing.

*Third of Frostmonth, in the year the bridge fell. The garden will be dug up and planted again. The diggers do not know whose hands plant them.*

*Midwinter, the year of two coronations. A door will stand where no wall is. Woe to the mason; blessed the key.*

On and on, century after century, the same dry voice cataloguing a future that had mostly already happened, or hadn't, or happened sideways. Theo felt the familiar itch of the hearing — not voices this time, but the sense that the book itself remembered being written, that the words still held the warmth of the hand that set them down.

Then he reached the last dated page, and stopped breathing.

The ink was not old.

Every other entry in the book was faded to the color of weak tea, brown and ghostly. This final date was written in red so fresh it seemed still wet, a hundred years impossible, the paper beneath it unyellowed, unaged — as though the entry had been written yesterday, or this morning, or while Theo had been carving lily petals for Widow Collick and listening to the dead complain about the cold.

The date was today's.

Feastday-eve. The seventeenth of Harvestmonth. The very day he'd woken, eaten bread, opened the shop, complained inwardly about the price of tallow.

Below it, in the same impossible fresh red, a single line:

*The maker who carries the dead shall stand at the threshold, and the fire shall know his name.*

And beneath that line, smaller, in different ink — older ink, brown and faded like all the rest, as if it had been waiting a century for the red to catch up to it — a name.

**Theo Amberdale.**

The shop was silent. Even the dead had gone quiet, and in five years they had never once gone quiet. The grandmother on her stair, the tide-voiced soldier, the dozens of others who drifted through the walls of the bone-shop like smoke through cracks — all of them silent, all of them, Theo realized with a horror that arrived a half-second after the fact, *watching*.

*Say it isn't you,* the grandmother whispered finally. *Say it's another man with your name.*

"There's no other," Theo said. His voice sounded far away. "My father picked it out of a hymnal. There's no other."

*Then close the book, boy.*

He closed the book. It didn't help. The name stayed printed on the dark of his vision, red and brown, fresh and ancient, both of them his.

Someone had known. A hundred years ago, someone had sat at a desk with this book open and written his name beneath a prophecy dated to this night — knowing, or guessing, or *arranging*, that on the seventeenth of Harvestmonth a bone-carver would hold this exact volume in his shaking hands. The delivery slip older than the courier. The nameless gentleman paying double. Tonight of all nights.

Which meant it wasn't a mistake. The bookseller had said *sold to me by mistake*, but mistakes didn't carry a man's name a century ahead of his birth.

Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds down Tanner's Row, and his shadow passed the window once, twice — and then passed a third time without a lamp in his hand, and did not pass again.

Theo looked down at the book. Looked at the hearth, where a low fire kept the carving knives warm and dry. One act. One sensible act.

Instead he heard himself say, to the empty shop, to the listening dead: "Who sent this?"

Silence. Then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the stair and the rafters and the marrow-deep places between them, the dead answered as one — not in words, but in a single indrawn breath, the sound a crowd makes when the executioner lifts his hood.

And in that breath, clear as a bell, clear as his own heartbeat, Theo heard a voice he had never heard before and knew instantly: vast, slow, and old beyond any grave in Vellamarch, carrying the smell of smoke and the weight of folded wings.

*"Finally,"* it said. *"Now we begin."*

Across the street, unseen by anyone living, three figures in grey cloaks turned together toward the bone-shop's glowing window — and began, unhurried, to cross.
