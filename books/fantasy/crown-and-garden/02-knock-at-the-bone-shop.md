## 2. Knock at the Bone-Shop

The knock came at half past nine, which was wrong in three ways.

First, customers didn't come at half past nine. The bone-shop's lamp was lit for Theo's own work, not for trade; anyone wanting a mourning charm came at noon or after supper, when grief had finished its first meal and grown hungry again. Second, the knock itself — two raps, a pause, two more — had the rhythm of someone who'd been taught to knock by an institution. Grieving people knocked soft and apologetic. Angry people knocked once, hard. Nobody knocked like a metronome unless they'd been issued the rhythm along with their boots.

Third, and worst: the dead went quiet.

They never went quiet. Five years in Vellamarch, five years of sleeping with wax in his ears because the murmuring never stopped — old grievances, unfinished sentences, the wet complaint of the drowned, the dry patience of the buried. The dead were weather to him now. He had stopped hearing them as voices years ago and started hearing them the way you hear rain on a roof: constant, ignorable, and suddenly deafening when it stops.

It stopped the moment before the second set of knocks.

Theo set down his knife. On the bench before him lay the widow Marta's charm, half-finished — a knucklebone of ox, carved into the shape of a closed hand, meant to hold her husband's last words so she could press it to her ear on bad nights. It was licensed work. That was the joke of it, the bitter little joke he told himself at night: everything he sold was licensed, stamped, filed with the Crown's Office of Permitted Remembrance. He did real carving for the living and let the registry believe that was all he did. The listening he kept free of charge, off the books, invisible.

Except tonight the dead had gone quiet, and quiet, he understood with a cold spreading through his chest, was its own kind of testimony. They knew who was at the door.

He wiped his hands on his apron, thought about taking the apron off, decided that would look worse, and opened the door.

The woman on his step wore grey. Not the grey of poverty — the grey of uniform, dyed somewhere expensive, cut close to the body so there was nothing for a desperate hand to grab. Her hair was bound back tight enough to look painful. She held a ledger under one arm the way other people held scripture, and she smiled the way a key smiles going into a lock.

"Master Amberdale." Her voice was pleasant. Pleasant the way frost is pretty. "Inquisitor Hale. Crown Office of Inquiry. I wonder if I might trouble you for a few minutes."

"Inquisitor." He stepped back, which was either courtesy or surrender, and hated himself for not knowing which. "Of course."

She entered the way water enters a crack — no wasted motion, no interest in the room beyond what the room could tell her about him. And the shop had plenty to tell. Theo saw it through her eyes as she turned slowly: the hanging racks of finished charms, each tagged with its registry number; the jars of prepared bone sorted by animal and by use; the workbench with its knives and rasps and the half-finished closed hand lying in its bed of sawdust. A lawful shop. A boring shop. A shop that had spent five years arranging itself into a portrait of harmlessness.

Hale looked at all of it the way you look at a face you're memorizing.

"Charming place," she said. "Smaller than I expected."

"Bone doesn't take up much room."

"No," she agreed. "I suppose it doesn't. Though I'm told the best carvers need surprisingly little material. A single finger-bone, properly worked, can hold a whole life's worth of voice. Is that your experience?"

The question was a hook dressed as small talk, and they both knew it. Licensed charms held nothing but comfort — carved shapes, keepsakes, the registry's own euphemism: *objects of remembrance*. What Hale had just described, casually, precisely, was outlawed craft. She wanted to see if he flinched at the description.

"Registry limits are four ounces per charm," Theo said. "Most of my work is ox and sheep. Pig, if someone wants something cheap."

"Mm." She drifted toward the rack of finished pieces, trailing one gloved finger below the tags without touching them. "You've been in Vellamarch five years. Before that?"

"South. Small towns. You learn every trade twice in small towns."

"And before that?"

"I was born somewhere south of that."

Something flickered across her face — not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of a person who has heard a hundred liars and knows that the good ones and the bad ones both eventually give you the same gift: a detail too smooth, a past with no corners in it. His past had no corners. He could feel it, rounded and polished from five years of handling, and he could feel her feeling it.

"You'll forgive the questions," she said, turning back to him with the smile reassembled. "This is a general inquiry. Unlicensed charm-work has been found in the Drowned Quarter. Three pieces this month. All of them well made — very well made. Registry work is crude by comparison, mass-stamped, done in workshops by the dozen. These were made by someone with a *hand*." She looked at his hands, then, openly. At the fine scars across his knuckles where slips happened. At the callus on his right thumb, deep as a hoof. "Someone who learned properly. Someone who listens to the material."

"The material talks to anyone who works it long enough," Theo said. "That's just craft."

"Isn't it just." She let the silence sit between them like a stone on a table. Then, lightly, as though changing the subject: "May I ask what you're working on?"

"It's a commission. Widow Marta Ossen, Candlemaker's Row. Her husband died in the spring flood. It's in the book." He nodded at the ledger chained to the wall by the door — the registry copy, open to the current page, where Marta's name sat in his own careful handwriting beside a registry stamp. He'd been meticulous. He was always meticulous. Meticulousness was the whole architecture of his survival.

Hale crossed to the ledger without asking permission and read it. Her glove hovered over the entries. "Marta Ossen. Yes. Filed eleven days ago, charm commissioned, purpose: object of remembrance." She glanced at the half-finished piece on the bench. "A closed hand."

"She wants to hold it against her ear."

"That's an unusual shape for a registry charm."

"It's an unusual grief."

For a moment — one unguarded moment — something moved behind Hale's eyes that wasn't the Inquisitor at all. Something tired, and human, and briefly sorry. Then it was gone, filed away wherever she kept such things, and the smile returned to its post.

"Of course," she said softly. "Forgive me. We see so much fraud in this line of work that we forget most grief is honest." She straightened. "I'll not keep you, Master Amberdale. You've been cooperative, and your papers are in order. I'll say this only because it costs nothing: if a man with your skill were ever tempted toward unlicensed work — if the material ever talked to him a little too clearly — the Office would want to hear it from him first. Voluntarily. There's a difference in how those cases end."

"I understand."

"Do you?" She paused at the door, ledger under her arm, and studied him one last time with that lock-picking attention. "Five years in Vellamarch and no family listed, no guild, no temple attendance, no debts, no vices. A man with no corners, Master Amberdale. In my experience, men without corners have usually cut them off themselves." She inclined her head. "Good evening. Mind the draft — it's bad for the bone."

The door closed behind her. Her footsteps went down the street, unhurried, metronomic, and faded.

The dead came back all at once, a tide of murmur and grievance, and Theo discovered he had been holding his breath long enough to see spots. He put both hands flat on the workbench and breathed until the shop stopped swimming.

*She knows,* said the ghost of old Tam, the cooper, who had haunted the building since before Theo rented it and considered himself management. *That one knows.*

"She suspects," Theo said aloud. He'd stopped hiding the habit of talking to them years ago; the shop was warded with salt and privacy-charms of his own devising, and besides, the neighbors already thought him odd. Odd was safe. Odd was camouflage. "Suspicion isn't proof."

*Suspicion is how it starts,* said Tam. *Started with suspicion in my day, too. Suspicion, then a list, then a cart.*

"Thank you, Tam. Very comforting."

*I don't do comfort. I do accuracy. It's why you keep me.*

He should burn the page. The thought arrived fully formed, the way the truest thoughts do, wearing no clothes. The prophecy book — the mistake, the catastrophe, the thing that had been sitting in his back room for six days like a lit fuse — the seller had slipped it into a lot of ordinary grimoires by accident, or claimed it was an accident, and Theo had found the date inscribed inside it in ink gone brown as dried blood: today's date. Tomorrow's date, rather, when he'd first read it. And beneath the date, in a different hand, older, smaller:

*the maker Amberdale opens*

He should have burned it that night. He hadn't, because burning felt like agreeing to something, and because some traitor part of him needed to read it again, needed to run his thumb over his own name in a century-old book the way you'd probe a tooth that shouldn't hurt. Six days of probing. And now an Inquisitor had stood in his shop and described his hands to his face.

He went into the back room. The book was where it always was, wrapped in oilcloth behind the loose brick, and unwrapping it felt like unwrapping something with a pulse. The prophecy pages were thin as onion skin, dense with a script that predated the current alphabet — the seller had called it Old Court, had called the book worthless, had practically apologized for including it. Worthless except for one date and one name, written in a later hand, in the margin, waiting for him like a trap waits.

Theo carried it to the shop's iron brazier. He hesitated only once — the length of one breath, the length of a man checking whether he's the kind of man who burns books — and then fed the margin page to the coals.

It caught fast. Thin paper, old ink, a curl of orange, a breath of smoke that smelled inexplicably of roses, and then ash. He watched the ash settle in the brazier's bowl, grey flakes drifting down through the updraft, and felt the first loosening of relief —

— and then the ash moved.

Not scattered. Not blown. *Moved*, deliberately, the way ants move, the way iron filings move near a lodestone, sliding across the bowl's curve and gathering, gathering, arranging themselves into strokes, into lines, into letters, small and precise and unmistakable, spelled out in the corpse of the page he'd just murdered:

**AMBERDALE**

The brazier ticked as the coals shifted. Somewhere above, Tam's ghost muttered something that might have been a prayer, if coopers prayed.

"That's not possible," Theo said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him, and which felt like evidence of shock rather than composure. "Paper burns. Ash is ash. Ash doesn't—" He stopped. He was arguing with cinders. He was a man who heard the dead complain about their funerals, standing in a warded shop in a country that officially did not exist, telling a bowl of ash that it couldn't spell.

The ash held its formation. Patient. Certain. It looked less like writing now and more like a signature — the flourish of something that had signed its work before and expected to sign it again.

*Burn the rest,* said Tam quietly. No sarcasm in the old ghost now. *Burn the whole book, lad, and sweep the bowl into the river.*

Theo took the book — heavier now, or his arms were weaker — and fed it to the coals page by page. Cover, spine, binding-cord, everything. The smoke rose black and sweet and wrong, and the dead in the walls had gone quiet again, all of them, every voice in Vellamarch holding its breath, and when the last of it was cinders he stood over the brazier with a poker in his hand like a man defending a bridge.

The ash stirred.

It gathered more slowly this time — there was less of it, after all — but gather it did, flake by flake crawling into formation with the inevitability of water finding its level, and when it finished, the bowl held not one word but several, ranked and neat, a sentence, a verdict:

**AMBERDALE OPENS THE DOOR**

Below that, smaller, almost shy, as if added as an afterthought by whatever intelligence lived in burnt things:

**TONIGHT**

Theo looked at the word *tonight* for a long time. Outside, the streetlamp hissed. A cart went by, wheels rattling over cobblestones, some honest tradesman heading home to an honest house in a country that was on no map and never had been, and none of it — the cart, the lamp, the ordinary night — none of it knew what was written in fire-remains on his workbench.

*Tonight,* repeated Tam, in the tone of a man reading a death warrant over someone's shoulder.

"He can't come tonight," Theo said. "She was just here. She said my papers were in order. She *left*."

*She left,* Tam agreed, *to write her report.*

The poker was cold in his hand. Theo set it down, because holding it made him feel like a man preparing for a fight he'd already lost, and losing fights you've prepared for is worse than losing ones you haven't. He swept the ash into a jar — he couldn't bring himself to flush it into the river, couldn't bring himself to touch it either, so the jar got a lid and a corner of the shelf, evidence preserved against his own better judgment — and then he stood in the middle of his lawful, meticulous, corner-less shop and did the arithmetic of his life.

Option one: stay. Barricade, ward, trust the salt-lines and the registry stamps and five years of invisibility. But the dead had gone silent for Hale, and ash had spelled his name, and a book a century old had booked him an appointment for tonight. Staying wasn't caution anymore. Staying was standing in a field during the lightning.

Option two: run. South, the way he'd come, out of — 

Out of what? The thought snagged, the way it always snagged, the thought he'd never let himself finish in five years. Out of Vellamarch. Except where was the border? He'd lived here five years and he had never once seen a boundary stone, a customs gate, a marked road out. He'd arrived, he realized — he actually stopped, mid-thought, and made himself look at it — he had arrived five years ago with no memory of the journey's last day. A gap. A smooth place in his memory, worn round like the rest of his past, and he had never once asked why.

The ash sat in its jar on the shelf, spelling its patient sentence. *Amberdale opens the door.* Not *finds* the door. Not *flees through* the door. *Opens.*

"For whom?" he asked the jar.

The ash, being ash, declined to elaborate.

Three slow knocks landed on the front door of the shop.

Not Hale's rhythm. Not a customer's. Three knocks, spaced wide, deliberate, the knock of something that had all night and knew exactly where he was — and underneath the sound, rising through the floorboards, through the foundations, through the bones of the building itself, Theo heard the dead of Vellamarch begin, very softly, very far away, to sing.
