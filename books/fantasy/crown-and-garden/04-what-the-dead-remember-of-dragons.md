## 4. What the Dead Remember of Dragons

The stairs went down farther than any cellar had a right to go.

Wren Sallow took them two at a time, a candle stub in one hand, and Theo followed because the alternative was standing in the tenement stairwell until the Inquisitors found him. Behind them, at the top of the steps, the chalk sigil she'd drawn on the doorframe was already fading — she'd told him it would. *Chalk forgets on purpose,* she'd said. *That's how you know it's ours.*

*Careful,* said the dead woman who'd been following him since the bone-shop. She was old, and fussy, and had been dead for thirty years without once learning to mind her own business. *Stairs are treacherous when you're frightened.*

Everything was treacherous when you were frightened. He'd learned that five years ago, when the edicts came down and every hedge-witch and charm-seller in Vellamarch learned to smile with their mouths only. Five years of carving mourning bones and listening to the dead mutter through his hands, and never once letting his face change. Five years of being alone in a crowd of voices.

The stairs ended in earth. Real earth — he could smell it, loam and cold stone and something older underneath, something like struck flint and hot copper. The cellar opened out around them into a room that shouldn't have fit beneath the tenements above: low vaulted ceilings ribbed with root-grown timber, shelves of jars glowing faintly green, cots along one wall, a long table scarred by a hundred knives. A dozen people looked up from their work. A forger with ink-stained fingers. Two women sorting herbs. A boy no older than sixteen sharpening a knife with the lazy competence of someone who'd done it ten thousand times.

Nobody asked who he was. That was the first strange thing. In the city above, everyone asked; asking was how you survived.

"New blood," said the knife-boy, not unkindly.

"He's the bone-carver," Wren said. "He's mine tonight. Nobody touches him, nobody questions him, nobody sells him." She looked around the room as she said it, and Theo understood that this was a real rule here, enforced, and that her saying *mine* was not affection but jurisdiction. "Where's Marisol?"

"Root-cellar proper," said the forger, jerking her chin toward a low arch at the far end. "With the bone."

Something in the room changed when she said it. Not much. A stillness, like birds before weather. The knife-boy's whetstone paused mid-stroke.

"The bone," Wren repeated, flat.

"She wanted to show him. Said if he was what the chalk said he was—"

"I said nobody questions him. I didn't say anything about showing him things." Wren rubbed her face. "Fine. Come on, carver."

"What bone?" Theo asked.

Wren didn't answer. She lifted the candle and ducked through the arch, and after a moment's hesitation — *go on,* said the dead woman, *you've already sold your whole life for worse reasons* — Theo followed.

* * *

The inner cellar was smaller, rounder, older. The walls here were raw stone, uncut, sweating cold. And in the center of the floor, on a bed of black sand, lay a bone.

It took Theo's eyes a moment to understand what they were seeing, because his mind kept trying to make it smaller than it was. It was a rib. Curved like a cathedral arch, longer than he was tall, mottled grey-white with veins of amber running through it like frozen fire. It lay half-buried in the sand, and the sand around it was scorched black in a perfect circle, though nothing else in the damp cellar showed so much as a scorch mark.

A woman knelt beside it. Grey-streaked hair braided tight, sleeves rolled, hands rough as a farmer's. She looked up as they entered, and her eyes went straight past Wren to Theo and stayed there.

"So," she said. "You're the one who hears."

Theo's mouth went dry. "I don't—"

"Don't." She raised a hand, gentle but absolute. "I've been a Gardener for twenty years. I've lied to magistrates, forgers, and my own mother. You're bad at it, carver, and we haven't got time for you to practice." She turned back to the bone. "This is why you're here. Or part of why. Wren found you faster than I expected."

"You sent me?" Theo said, to Wren.

"I sent inquiries," Wren said. "Marisol sent me after you specifically. She wouldn't say why." There was an edge in her voice — the edge of someone who hated not knowing things.

"I'll say why now," Marisol said. "Because the bone has been quiet for eleven years, and three nights ago it started humming. And yesterday, when word came up the line about a bone-carver in the Drowned Quarter who'd caught Hale's attention—" she looked at Theo again, and her voice dropped, almost reverent, almost afraid, "—the bone started speaking."

"That's not possible," Theo said automatically.

"No," Marisol agreed. "It isn't. Come here anyway."

Every dead voice he'd ever heard had come through his hands — through bone he carved, bone he touched, the material holding what the living left behind. He knew, intellectually, that he could refuse. His feet carried him forward anyway. He knelt on the cold floor beside the black sand, and because there was no other way to do this, because it was the only prayer he'd ever known, he laid both palms flat against the great curved rib.

The world went silent.

Not quiet — silent. Every murmur, every grievance, every dead voice that had trailed him since childhood, all of it cut off at once, like a door closing on a noisy room. For one vertiginous second Theo thought his gift had burned out, that touching this thing had killed it, and he felt relief and terror in equal measure.

Then something spoke.

It did not come through his hands. It came through the floor, through the stone, through the roots of the country itself — a voice like a fire seen across a valley at night, vast and far and warm.

***Little maker.***

Theo snatched his hands back. He was on his feet without remembering standing. "What—"

"Easy," Marisol murmured. But she was leaning forward too, hungry-eyed, watching the bone as if she could see the voice.

***You flinch. They always flinch. Sit down, little maker. I have been dead a hundred years and I am very tired, and I did not wait through all that silence to watch you run.***

Theo sat. His legs made the decision for him.

"What are you?" he whispered.

***I was called Ember-of-Ashes. I am what remains. Your kind would say ghost. Your kind is wrong about most things.***

"A dragon," Marisol breathed. She'd gone pale. "Eleven years I've kept this bone. Eleven years I've prayed over it and gotten nothing, and it's a *dragon.*"

***A rib, technically. The rest of me is scattered from here to the salt marsh. What the Crown couldn't burn, it buried. What it buried, the earth remembers. I remember through the earth. Mostly.*** A sound moved through the cellar then, low and rolling, and it took Theo a moment to recognize it as laughter — dry, cracked, like a fire settling. ***Mostly is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, little maker. Ask your question. You're swelling with it.***

Theo's mouth worked. He had a hundred questions and they were all trying to get out the same door. Finally the one that came out was the smallest and the largest:

"Why can't I hear the other ghosts right now?"

Silence. Even Wren, behind him, had stopped breathing.

***Because I am loud,*** Ember-of-Ashes said. ***When I speak, the small voices step back. They know a greater dead when they feel one.*** Another crackle of that settling-fire laugh. ***Do not mistake courtesy for absence, little maker. Your dead are still there, pressed against the walls of you, waiting for me to finish. They are patient. The dead learn patience whether they want it or not.***

"How do you know what I am?" Theo asked. "What I can do?"

***I know what the prophecy says.***

The word landed in the cellar like a dropped glass. Wren stepped forward. "Prophecy. What prophecy?"

***The one your Crown burned every copy of a century ago,*** the dragon said, ***and failed to burn, because you cannot burn what lives in bone. I am the last copy, little gardener. I am the last copy of everything.***

Marisol's hands had closed into fists on her knees. "Read it," she said. Her voice shook. "Whatever it says — read it."

***Patience.*** The warmth in the voice banked lower, coals instead of flame. ***First, the little maker should understand what he is sitting inside of. Carver. Bone-worker. You have spent your life among the dead. Tell me — what do the dead remember?***

Theo blinked. "Remember?"

***They cling to their grievances, I know. Their unfinished business, their petty debts. But underneath all that noise — what do they hold onto? What does a soul keep, when everything else is taken?***

He thought of the widow's husband, whose ghost had spent its whole haunting complaining about a debt of four silver marks. He thought of the drowned men of the river district, who spoke endlessly of water, of cold, of the specific weight of wet coats. He thought of his mother, though he tried not to, though her grave was across the city and her voice had gone quiet years ago, or he had stopped listening, he'd never been sure which was worse.

"They remember places," he said slowly. "That's what stays longest. Names fade, faces fade, even their own names go eventually. But places — houses, roads, the smell of a kitchen. The dead remember where they were. Longer than they remember who."

***Yes,*** said Ember-of-Ashes, and the word filled the cellar like heat filling a room. ***Yes. You understand faster than the scholars did, and they had decades. Now hear me, all of you, because I will say this once and my strength is not endless.***

The amber veins in the rib brightened. Just slightly. Just enough to throw moving light across the raw stone walls, like sunlight through honey.

***One hundred years ago, this country stood on every map in the world. Vellamarch. Say it.***

"Vellamarch," Marisol said. Wren echoed her, uncertain, like a word in a language she'd almost forgotten.

***And then the treaties came, and the purges, and the burning of the hedge-craft and the hearth-craft and everything that could not be taxed or commanded. And the Crown of the south — your Crown, the one whose hounds sniffed at your door today, little maker — could not conquer Vellamarch outright. So it did something cleverer. It erased us. From the maps. From the treaties. From memory, where it could reach memory. A country that does not exist cannot be conquered. It can only be forgotten.***

"But we exist," Wren said. "We're *here.*"

***Are you?*** The question was soft, and terrible. ***Look at your borders, little gardener. Can you find them? Walk to them? No. Because the borders of Vellamarch were never lines on paper. They were fire. My fire. When the purge came, the last loyal hearth-keepers lit a ring of dragon-flame around the country — a ward so old and deep that the land itself forgot how to be anywhere else. Inside the fire, Vellamarch persists. Outside it, the world has agreed, politely and officially, that there is nothing here at all.***

Theo felt the floor tilt under him, though the floor hadn't moved. All his life he had lived in a city with a name nobody outside used. All his life he'd crossed bridges that appeared on no map, paid taxes to a Crown that officially owned nothing here, spoken a dialect the scholars called extinct. He'd thought it was poverty. Neglect. The ordinary indifference of empires to their margins.

It was a spell. His whole life was inside a spell.

"The wards," Marisol said. Her voice had gone thin. "The fire. Is it — how long does it burn?"

***Ah,*** said the dragon, and the gentleness in it was worse than anger. ***There is the wound. Little keeper of bones, tell her. You of all people know what fires do.***

"They gutter," Theo said. His mouth felt far away. "All fires gutter."

***A hundred years is a long time for a fire that has nothing left to feed on. The hearth-keepers are gone. The dragons are gone — I am what's left, and I am a rib in a cellar, a voice with no lungs. The ring burns still, but thinner every year. Thinner every season. There are nights — and you have felt them, all of you, you've blamed the weather — when the edges of this country flicker. When streets that have stood for centuries grow briefly uncertain of themselves.***

The knife-boy's face rose in Theo's memory — no, not the knife-boy, the stories the Gardeners told, the lost lanes, the door that opened onto a wall, the neighbor who swore her street had been longer when she was a girl. Everyone laughed at those stories. Everyone had one.

"It's dying," Marisol said. "The border is dying."

***Embers remember being flames,*** Ember-of-Ashes said. ***But remembering is not enough. Which brings us, little maker, to the prophecy.***

Theo's skin prickled. Somewhere above them, beyond layers of earth and tenement and lying chalk, Inquisitor Hale was asking polite questions with a ledger open on her knee. He thought of the book in his shop, the date inked a century ago that matched today, his own name beneath it in faded brown script. He'd burned that page. The ash had spelled his name again.

"Tell me," he said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. Maybe the dead lend you their steadiness when you need it. Maybe that was the one mercy in the gift.

***The prophecy was written by the last hearth-keeper, the night the wards were lit,*** the dragon said. ***She was dying, and she was a vain, brilliant, stubborn woman, and she refused to leave the future without instructions. It speaks of a fire that gutters, and a country forgotten, and a door. A threshold out of Vellamarch — or perhaps not out. The words are old and slippery. But the door has a keeper.*** The amber veins pulsed, slow as a heartbeat. ***A maker who carries the dead. One who hears what the living bury, and shapes what the dead leave behind. The prophecy gives his trade. It does not give his name.***

"But the book did," Theo whispered. "The book gave my name."

***Then the book was copied later, by someone who dreamed true,*** said the dragon, ***or someone who arranged matters and called it prophecy. Both kinds of people existed. Both kinds still do.*** A pause, and when the voice came again it was closer somehow, warmer, aimed at him alone. ***Little maker. Jonah— no. Theo Amberdale. Do you know what I hear, when I listen to you?***

"No."

***Grief. Old grief, worn smooth like a river stone. You have been carrying your dead so long you've forgotten they're not yours to carry alone.*** The fire-light dimmed, banked, almost tender. ***I know that grief. I have carried a whole country's death for a hundred years, alone in the dark, talking to sand. So hear me, bone-carver, and hear me kindly: I am not asking you to be brave. I am not asking you to be chosen. I am telling you that the door in the prophecy is real, and the fire is failing, and you are the first person in a century who might be able to find it.***

"And if I can't?" Theo asked. "If I'm just a man who carves charms for widows?"

***Then Vellamarch goes out,*** the dragon said simply. ***Like a candle. Like a name. And no one will mourn us, because no one will remember there was ever anything here to mourn.***

The silence afterward had weight. Marisol had her head bowed. Wren stood rigid by the arch, arms crossed, and Theo realized she was gripping her own elbows hard enough to bruise — a woman holding herself together by force.

*Well,* said the fussy dead woman, suddenly present again at the edge of his hearing, her voice small and familiar as a draft under a door. *That's put the cat among the pigeons, hasn't it.*

Theo almost laughed. Almost cried. Both felt equally likely.

"One more thing," he said aloud. "The date. In the book. Tomorrow's date — today's date — was written a hundred years ago, next to my name. If the prophecy doesn't give names, then who wrote mine? Who's been waiting for me?"

For the first time, the dragon's voice hesitated.

***Ah,*** said Ember-of-Ashes. ***Yes. That.***

The amber veins flickered — and guttered, all at once, like a candle pinched between wet fingers. The cellar went dark except for Wren's candle stub, and the great rib lay inert and silent on its black sand, just a bone again, just the oldest dead thing Theo had ever touched.

"He's tired," Marisol said quietly. "Eleven years of silence, and he spends it all in one hour. Give him time. He'll come back." She looked up at Theo, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "You heard him. You know what's coming now. The Crown, the wards, all of it. There's no walking away from this, carver. I'm sorry. I truly am."

Theo knelt there a moment longer, his hand hovering over the cooling bone, not quite touching it.

"Wren," he said. "The book I burned. The page with my name on it."

"What about it?"

"There was more under my name. A second line. I only remembered it just now." His heart was beating somewhere up near his teeth. "It said: *the door opens inward.*"

Wren stared at him. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know," Theo said. "But I think somebody in this city does — and I think I know where the dead go to talk about it."

Above them, faint through a hundred feet of earth and stone, a church bell began to toll midnight. And in the dark of the root-cellar, on its bed of scorched sand, the last dragon's rib gave one slow pulse of amber light — like something turning over in its sleep, beginning to dream of fire.
