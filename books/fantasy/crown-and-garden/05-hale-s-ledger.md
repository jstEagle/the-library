## 5. Hale's Ledger

Inquisitor Second-Class Maren Hale kept her ledger the way her father had taught her to keep his accounts: every entry dated, every entry witnessed, every debt recorded whether or not it was ever paid.

She sat in the back room of the Inquisition's district house on Candlemaker Street, past midnight, with a single lamp turned low and the ledger open in front of her. Around her, the other inquisitors had gone home or gone to drink. She preferred it this way. The quiet let her hear herself think, and thinking was the one part of the work nobody could take from her.

The entry she was writing was not yet an accusation. That mattered. An accusation required evidence; an entry only required attention. *Bone-carver, Amberdale, Theo. Shop off Tanner's Row. Charms of unusual quality. No license posted. Claims to carve "keepsakes." Subject nervous. Hands steady until asked direct questions.*

She underlined the last phrase twice.

Hale had been doing this work for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that guilt rarely announced itself. Guilt was polite. Guilt answered your questions quickly and offered you tea. What betrayed people was the small stuff — the way a man's hands went still when they should have been busy, the half-second pause before a lie about something too trivial to lie about. Theo Amberdale had paused for exactly half a second when she'd asked him where he'd learned to carve. Half a second. Most people wouldn't have caught it.

Maren Hale caught everything. It was why she was still Second-Class after eleven years while men half as careful got promoted over her head.

She dipped her pen. Beneath the entry she wrote: *Return within the week. Check for unregistered materials. Bone of what origin?*

Then, because she was honest even in her own private book, she added a third line: *Or he is simply a craftsman who dislikes soldiers in his shop.*

That was the trouble with this work, if you did it properly. You couldn't stop seeing both sides of every ledger.

* * *

Her father had kept two ledgers. That was the thing nobody in the district house knew — the thing she would carry to her grave if she had any sense at all.

Edmund Hale had been a wool-factor on Cooper's Lane, a solid man with ink-stained cuffs and a reputation for fairness that ran three streets wide. When Maren was nine years old, the Gardeners came to Cooper's Lane. Everyone remembered the year. A factor named Aldous Vey had been selling orphan apprentices into mill labor — not the legal kind, the kind with no wages and locked doors, the kind where boys went in at eight years old and sometimes didn't come out at all. Two of them had died in a lint-fire that the magistrates ruled accidental, though everyone on Cooper's Lane knew the doors were chained. Vey was fined forty crowns and given a warning about ventilation.

The law had spoken. The law had been satisfied.

Three weeks later, the Gardeners were not satisfied. They took Vey from his own counting-house in broad daylight, in front of six witnesses who all swore afterward they'd seen nothing. They held a public accounting in the old tannery yard — that was what they called their trials, *accountings* — and they made Vey confess everything before a crowd of four hundred people. Then they'd taken his hands.

Not killed him. Taken his hands. A factor without hands, they said, could never again sign a child away.

And they'd left a mark chalked on Vey's door. Green chalk. A circle with a sprout rising through it. Every child in the district knew what it meant by the end of that month: *the Gardeners saw. The Gardeners answered.*

Edmund Hale had watched the whole thing from his office window. And when it was done, he had come home, sat down at the kitchen table where Maren was doing sums, and opened his second ledger — the one he kept behind the false spine of his accounts book — and written, in his neat factor's hand:

*Tonight justice was done on Cooper's Lane, and I could not lift a finger to help do it, nor would I if I could. The law is slow and can be bought. Remember this, Mare.*

She had found the ledger after his funeral, seven years ago, tucked behind the real one like a splinter under skin. She had read every page in a single night. Her father, it turned out, had spent thirty years recording every crime the courts refused to touch, every fine that functioned as permission, every magistrate whose verdict matched the size of the bribe. Page after page in that tidy hand. He hadn't acted on any of it. He had only *written it down*, which was either cowardice or conscience, and Maren had spent seven years unable to decide which.

What she knew — what sat in her chest like a swallowed stone — was that the Gardeners had done in one night what her father's ledger had failed to do in thirty years. And that her father, watching them do it, had felt something so close to shame that he'd needed a hidden book to hold it.

So when the Crown had come recruiting inquisitors out of the tax offices, Maren Hale had volunteered. Not because she loved the Crown. Because somebody had to make the law fast enough and honest enough that no one would ever again feel what her father felt at that window. If the law worked, the Gardeners would starve. That was the whole of her creed, and she had never once said it aloud, because saying it aloud in the district house would be the end of her career.

Instead she hunted them.

* * *

The file on the Gardeners was thick as a Bible and thin as smoke. Eleven years of it. Chalk marks photographed and catalogued. Forty-one accountings confirmed, ninety more suspected. Witness statements that all said the same thing: masked, quiet, gone before the bells finished tolling. They moved through the tenement districts like water through cloth. Nobody sold them out. In eleven years, Hale had gotten exactly three informants inside the network, and all three had come back with nothing useful or not come back at all.

Her superiors wanted the bone-carver because of the prophecy business — some fool page burned in a shop on Tanner's Row, ash spelling a name, and suddenly every ranking officer in Vellamarch was talking about omens. Hale thought the whole affair was superstition dressed up as procedure. Names in ash. Honestly.

But the bone-carver interested her for a different reason.

She pulled the district's charm-license rolls and laid them beside her notes. Licensed carvers: fourteen. All accounted for, all producing registered work at registered prices. Unlicensed carvers operating below official notice: unknown, but the seizure reports told a story. Over the past five years, wardens had confiscated mourning charms from six different raids — charms carved from bone, which required a license most carvers couldn't afford — and every confiscated charm bore the same hand. The same tool-work. Fine, patient cuts, a maker's signature in the grammar of the gouge rather than in letters.

Five years of work, all of it one man's, none of it licensed. Which meant one of two things: either Theo Amberdale had been quietly breaking the law since before the Crown noticed he existed, or someone had been distributing his work for him.

And there was a third possibility, the one she wrote down and then stared at for a long time. *Charms move through the tenements. Chalk marks appear in the same districts. If someone wanted to reach the people the law ignores, a charm-seller would be a perfect courier.*

She closed the ledger.

* * *

She went to see the widow two days later, without uniform, in a plain gray coat, carrying a basket of pears she didn't want.

Mrs. Callow lived above a dyer's shop in the Drowned Quarter, in rooms that smelled of river damp and tallow. She was small and stooped and she looked at the pears with the expression of a woman deciding whether they were a gift or a bill.

"You're the one who came about Mr. Amberdale," she said. Not a question. Word traveled in the tenements faster than it traveled in the district house, which was itself a lesson Hale filed away.

"I'm checking on a purchase you made. A mourning charm."

"My husband's been dead two years, girl. You think I bought it yesterday?"

"Recently enough."

The widow set the pears down without thanks and folded her hands. "He carved my Tam's name into the bone himself. Took him near a week. Other carvers wanted coin up front and gave me a stamp off a rack. This one sat with me an hour first and asked what my Tam liked. Asked how he laughed." Her chin came up. "You going to arrest me for owning a keepsake?"

"No," Hale said. "I'm going to ask you who else has charms from him."

"And if I don't say?"

"Then I note that you declined, Mrs. Callow, and I note it politely, and nothing happens to you today. I'm not the warden. I don't kick doors."

Something flickered across the old woman's face — surprise, maybe, or disappointment. People expected cruelty from the Inquisition and didn't know what to do with courtesy. Hale had found that courtesy unsettled witnesses far more than threats. Threats they had scripts for.

"Half the street wears his work," the widow said finally. "The drowned quarter's full of it. Grief-keepsakes, birthing charms, bones for sick children to hold. He doesn't charge what he ought. Half the time I think he charges nothing and lies about it." She squinted at Hale. "Why does a bone-carver matter to the Crown? He's nobody. He mends things. He listens."

*He listens.* Hale wrote the phrase down that night, and hated how much space she gave it.

* * *

It rained the evening she returned to Tanner's Row, rain fine as sifted flour, and the bone-shop's windows glowed amber against the wet. Through the glass she could see him at his bench, bent over his work, alone.

She stood in the street for a full minute before knocking, and she was honest enough with herself to admit what she was waiting for. She was waiting to watch him through the window, unguarded, before he put on his face for her.

What she saw confused the picture entirely.

A woman came in — young, hooded, moving like someone used to not being seen. She and the carver spoke briefly. Then she set something on his counter: a strip of green chalk. He looked at it a long moment. And then he took it.

Hale had interviewed enough smugglers to know what contraband looked like when it changed hands. There was a language to it — the glance at the door, the casualness pitched too high. None of that here. Just a tired man accepting a piece of chalk the way you'd accept a summons.

The woman left. The carver stood holding the chalk, staring at nothing, looking — there was no better word for it — *conscripted*.

Hale stepped back from the window into the dark of the doorway opposite, rain sliding off the brim of her hat, and opened her ledger against her forearm. The pencil scratched small and quick.

*Amberdale contacted by unknown female, hooded. Exchanged item: green chalk stick. Subject accepted. Interpretation: recruitment or summons, network known as Gardeners. Confidence: moderate.*

She stopped. Looked up at the lit window. The silhouette inside had resumed its work, shoulders curved over the bench, a man making small beautiful things for poor grieving people at prices he lied downward about.

*Alternative interpretation,* she wrote, slowly: *he is a good man being pulled somewhere bad, and I am about to decide his life based on a stick of chalk.*

Below that, in the column where she recorded debts — her father's habit, inherited whole — she wrote the entry that would keep her awake that night:

*Owed: to Edmund Hale, deceased. A law worth obeying. Status: unpaid.*

She closed the book. Across the street, the lamp in the bone-shop went out.

In the morning, she would draft the warrant application. Tonight she stood in the rain a while longer, watching the dark window, telling herself the tightening in her chest was only the cold — and failing to notice the small figure in a soaked gray coat across the lane, chalk-white fingers marking her door with a circle and a sprout, before slipping away into the rain.

Wait — no, that last image belongs to another story. Scratch it.

She closed the book. Across the street, the lamp in the bone-shop went out, and Maren Hale stood alone in the rain with two ledgers in her head — her father's and her own — and understood, with the flat certainty of long experience, that tomorrow she would write the name *Theo Amberdale* in both of them, and that only one of those entries could ever be crossed out.
