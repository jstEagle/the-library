## 6. The Country Off the Map

Theo slept badly in the root-cellar, which was fitting, because he had never in his life slept well anywhere, and the cellar at least was honest about it.

He woke on a straw pallet with a wool blanket that smelled of turnips and lamp oil, and for three full seconds he did not remember where he was, and those three seconds were the best he'd had in weeks. Then it all came back: the chalk sigils, the stairs behind the false shelf, the woman named Wren Sallow who had appeared in his doorway like a knife coming out of a sleeve and said, *Inquisitor's carriage two streets over, bone-carver, do you want to live or don't you.*

Then the dragon.

Or whatever it was. He had heard it only briefly last night, before exhaustion dragged him under — a voice from the great curved bone mounted on iron brackets above the Gardeners' worktables, a voice like a fire remembered rather than heard, saying his name as though it had been waiting a hundred years to say it.

The cellar was dim now. Lamplight. The smell of earth and old wood and, underneath everything, something faintly mineral and hot, like a struck flint. Several Gardeners worked at long tables despite the hour — a broad-armed woman grinding herbs, a thin man copying documents in three different hands, a girl no older than sixteen stitching shut what appeared to be a leather satchel full of teeth. None of them looked up when Theo sat. That was its own kind of courtesy. He was learning the house rules: nobody asked what you'd done, nobody asked what you could do, until the work required knowing.

The dragon-bone hung at the far end of the cellar, longer than Theo was tall, curved like a rib from some impossible chest. Its surface was carved all over — no, not carved. He crossed the cold stone floor and got close and understood, with a lurch, that the markings were *grown*, whorls and script-like ridges laid down in the bone the way grain grows in wood. Someone had written themselves into their own skeleton.

"Touch it if you like," said the voice, warm and dry and enormous even at a whisper. "Everyone wants to. I've stopped being offended."

Theo snatched his hand back anyway.

"You're the one from the book," he said. His voice came out rusty. "You said — last night you said you knew the prophecy."

"I know it better than the fool who wrote it." Something moved in the lamplight along the bone, a shimmer like heat off a forge road. "I wrote it."

* * *

They talked while the cellar worked around them, and Theo learned quickly that Ember-of-Ashes — for that was the name, offered flatly, without ceremony — did not converse so much as dispense, in the manner of someone who has held a single story inside a single body for a very long time and can no longer tell it in any order but her own.

"Vellamarch," she said, "was a real country once. I want you to sit with how strange that sentence sounds, because it tells you how thorough they were. A hundred years ago there was a kingdom here with its own crown, its own courts, its own treaties signed in its own name. Then the Crown across the mountains decided it wanted the land without the inconvenience of the people who owned it, and open war was expensive, so they found a cheaper way."

"Which was?"

"They bought my fire." The shimmer along the bone darkened, if darkness could deepen. "There was a compact. I was old and tired and the last of my kind, and they came to me with priests and lawyers — always those two together, in that order — and they said: burn us borders no map can argue with. Hide this country inside itself. And in exchange we will let your bones rest undisturbed forever. Sacred, untouchable, a relic to be venerated."

Theo thought of the bone above him. "And they kept that promise."

"I'm hanging in a smuggler's cellar, boy. What do you think?"

A woman at the herb table snorted without looking up. Ember went on.

"The fire hid Vellamarch. Anyone outside who might have remembered us forgot — treaties redrafted, names inked out, whole archives reorganized overnight. Travelers who walked toward our borders found themselves walking home again with no sense of having turned. The maps didn't lie, exactly. The maps simply stopped being able to see us. And the people inside—" the voice dropped, and Theo felt it in his sternum, "—the people inside forgot too. Not everything. Just enough. They forgot they had ever been sovereign. They forgot why the old songs named streets that weren't on any register. They learned to say *the Crown* about a government that had swallowed them whole, and they taught their children to say it, and within two generations it was true, or true enough."

Theo sat down slowly on a crate. His hands, which never quite stilled, were still.

"That's not possible," he said. "People would notice. My mother would have noticed. Everyone would—"

"Your mother noticed," Ember said, gently, and the gentleness was worse than any accusation. "Some few always do. That's what the purges were for."

The word landed in the room and everyone in it went on working, carefully, loudly, as if they hadn't heard. The girl with the satchel of teeth pulled her thread tighter.

"The outlawing of magic," Ember said. "You think it was fear? Fear is a tool, not a motive. It was pruning. Every hedge-witch who kept the old boundary-songs, every carver who could hear what stones remember, every child born with eyes that saw through my fire — pruned. Cut and burned and written out of parish records. Because a hidden country stays hidden only if no one inside it remembers there is anything to hide."

Theo's mouth was dry. "And the fire. Your fire. It's dying."

"It is *me*," Ember said. "That's what none of them understood, the lawyers least of all. They thought they'd purchased a mechanism. You cannot purchase a mechanism from a living thing; you can only make an arrangement with its heart. The wards run on my memory of Vellamarch — every lane, every river, every stubborn ugly beautiful mile of it. But I am a ghost, and ghosts fade, and lately I have been forgetting." A pause, and the flint-smell in the air sharpened. "When I forget a street, that street flickers. When I forget a border-stone, patrols walk through where a wall should be. It has been happening more often. You'll have noticed the weather of it by now. Everyone will, soon."

* * *

He needed air, or the idea of air, and Wren Sallow appeared beside him before he'd even fully stood, materializing the way she did, holding two clay cups of something hot and bitter.

"You look like a man who just found out the floor was a ceiling," she said, pressing a cup into his hand.

"Is it true?" Theo asked. "All of it? You knew?"

"We knew." She drank, watching him over the rim. "It's the founding fact of the Gardeners, more or less. When the law is a costume the thieves wear, somebody has to do the actual work. We keep the old records the purges missed. We remember the names. It's why the Inquisitors hate us more than they hate any smuggler — a thief takes coin; a Gardener takes back the truth."

"And nobody told anyone? A whole country, and nobody—"

"Told who?" Wren's voice wasn't cruel, but it was precise, the way a splint is precise. "Theo. Think about your own life. You've had a gift since childhood that would get you hanged. You've spent five years carving charms with numbing-chalk on your fingers so nothing you made would sing. You lived inside a secret so long it became your body. Now multiply that by everyone." She shrugged. "Forgetting isn't something done *to* cowards. It's something done to everyone, and then everyone helps maintain it, because the alternative is terror."

He hated her a little for saying it, which meant it was true.

He drank the bitter drink. Around the cellar, the work went on: ledgers copied, herbs ground, lies forged into official shapes. He watched the thin man produce a death certificate with a municipal seal that Theo now understood belonged to a government that existed only because a dying ghost could still picture it.

"The prophecy," Theo said finally. "If you wrote it, then you know what it means. The date. My name. 'The door.'"

Ember was quiet long enough that the lamplight had time to gutter twice.

"Yes," the dragon said at last. "Come here again, bone-carver. Look at the bone properly this time."

He made himself cross the floor. Up close, the grown script covered every span of the great rib, dense near the center, sparse toward the ends, like a life written down as it was lived.

"A century ago I set down everything I feared and everything I hoped, in the only durable thing I owned — myself. Prophecy is not seeing the future, whatever the pamphlets claim. It is a wish written large enough that desperate people will go looking for ways to fulfill it. I wished for a door. Because I am tired, Theo Amberdale. I have held up a country with my memory for a hundred years while it forgot I existed, and my wish, written into my own bones, was for a way *out*. For something — someone — who could carry Vellamarch past its borders and let it exist somewhere honest, somewhere it wouldn't have to be hidden. A door out of a lie."

"And the door is me."

"The book says a maker who carries the dead. Last night I heard your gift in the way you flinched at my voice — you heard me before you saw me, didn't you? You hear everything. You carry the dead around with you like coins in a poor man's pocket." The shimmer rippled. "Yes. I believe the door is you."

Theo looked at his hands. Carver's hands, scarred at the knuckles, chalk in the creases no scrubbing reached. Hands that had spent five years making charms for grieving people because the dead asked him to and he couldn't bear to refuse them. And all of it — the hiding, the chalk, the small gray careful life in the shop above the tenement — all of it had been staged inside a country-sized coffin, and he had never once known the walls were walls.

"My whole life," he said, and his voice sounded far away to himself, "I thought I was small. Hiding in a corner of the world. And the world—"

"Was a room," Wren said quietly beside him. "Yeah. First time's the worst. Took me a week to stop walking into walls, and I only lost a brother to the lie. You lost the shape of reality."

"The borders are failing," Theo said to Ember. "You said. The fire gutters. So what happens when it goes out entirely? If Vellamarch stops being hidden — does it come back? Or does it just—" he searched for the word and hated it— "*stop*?"

No one answered at first. The herb-grinder had gone still. Even the girl with the teeth looked up.

"That," Ember said, "is the question the prophecy does not answer, and why I wrote a door instead. Because I genuinely do not know whether a forgotten country, fully forgotten, winks out — or wakes. No one has ever been erased before on this scale. We are the experiment." The dry warmth came back into the voice, terribly casual. "Though given the flickering has begun, I suspect we'll learn the answer within the year."

Within the season. Within the month, maybe. Theo set down his cup because his hands had started to shake and he did not want an audience for it.

Outside these walls — outside walls he now knew were wards — Inquisitors were already hunting the bone-carver whose name sat under today's date in a forbidden book. He had assumed they hunted him because of the magic. Now the arithmetic rearranged itself: the Crown across the mountains had spent a century keeping Vellamarch asleep, and here came a man named in prophecy as the door out. Of course they wanted him. Perhaps they wanted him precisely the way a jailer wants the only key.

"Wren," he said. "The widow. Marta Ostrow. The charm I made her — if the Inquisitors trace it—"

"They won't find her before we move her. Already handled." Wren's eyes narrowed. "Why?"

Because the dead in his shop had murmured about boots in the street for days and he'd dismissed it as weather. Because everything he knew about safety was built on a floor that didn't exist. He didn't say any of it. Instead he looked up at the great rib and the fire-written script and asked the question that had been sitting in his chest since the words *door out of a lie*.

"If I'm the door — if I actually do this — what happens to you?"

Ember was silent again, and this silence had a different texture, older and more frightened, and Theo realized with a strange vertigo that a dragon could be afraid.

"I told you," the dragon said finally. "Prophecy is a wish. I wished for a way out." A pause. "I never promised I'd survive the door opening."

Before Theo could speak, the cellar lamps guttered — all of them at once, in the same breath, though there was no draft — and for one heartbeat the whole room went gray and thin, like a sound heard through water. Above them, muffled through forty feet of city, came the faint confused tolling of a temple bell striking an hour that was wrong.

Wren set down her cup. All around the cellar, Gardeners were already moving, closing ledgers, dousing lamps, taking up weapons with the calm of people who had rehearsed this.

"There's the weather again," she said grimly. "Ember's flickering. Which means the wards just blinked, which means somewhere up there, a patrol just found a street that shouldn't be on their map." She glanced at Theo, and her expression was equal parts apology and challenge. "Welcome to the country off the map, bone-carver. Get your coat. You're going to earn your keep."
