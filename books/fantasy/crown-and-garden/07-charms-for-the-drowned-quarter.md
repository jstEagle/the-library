## 7. Charms for the Drowned Quarter

The boy had been dead nine days when they brought Theo to see him.

Not the body — the body was long gone, surrendered to the Drowned Quarter's indifferent tide. What Wren Sallow laid on the trestle table in the root-cellar's workroom was a shoe. One small leather shoe, water-swollen, the laces still knotted in the careful double bow a mother teaches and a child performs wrong forever.

"Tam Arlow," Wren said. "Seven years old. Pulled out of the Cistern Reach three weeks back, though he'd been in the water longer than that, if you follow me."

Theo didn't follow her, and said so.

"The coroner ruled it an accident." She turned the shoe over. Inside, inked faintly against the leather, someone had written *TAM* — the way poor mothers mark everything, so the pawnshops can't strip a family bare without leaving evidence. "Slipped off the walkway. Happens all the time down there, they say. The Reach takes two or three children every winter."

"And the Gardeners think otherwise."

"The Gardeners know otherwise." She set the shoe down with a gentleness that sat strangely on her sharp face. "His sister saw a man. A grown man, walking beside Tam on the walkway at dusk, holding his hand like a father would. An hour later Tam was in the water. The sister is eleven. She told the magistrate's clerk what she saw, and the clerk wrote it down as *child's confusion*, because the man she described wears a gray coat, and gray coats belong to men who pay taxes on their houses in the Hill Quarter, and eleven-year-old girls from the Drowned Quarter don't get to put those men in front of judges."

The cellar was quiet around them. Somewhere above, through the old root-cellar's earthen ceiling, Vellamarch went about its evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the bells of a church that officially did not exist ringing for a congregation that officially did not exist either. Theo had been living beneath that impossible country for four days now, sleeping in a hammock strung between shelving racks, eating soup with people who broke laws the way other people broke bread, and every morning he woke expecting it all to have dissolved into the ordinary world where he was only ever a bone-carver with nothing wrong in him at all.

It hadn't dissolved. That was the terrifying part. It kept being real.

"You want me to ask him," Theo said.

"I want you to do what you do." Wren's voice didn't soften, but something behind it did — she looked away toward the stairwell, jaw tight. "I'm not going to pretend I'm comfortable asking. You're not a tool, and I know it. But there's a woman named Sera Arlow who has spent nine days being told her son drowned himself by accident, and there's a man in a gray coat who will do this again, because no one has ever once made him stop. The courts won't touch him. So we will."

"The law—"

"The law," Wren said, "is a door that only opens outward. You've known that your whole life. You just never had anywhere else to stand before."

He wanted to argue. He found he couldn't. Five years of hiding his gift had taught him exactly how much justice the law had for people like him: none, held in reserve, available never. He had survived by being small and silent and lucky. Tam Arlow hadn't been lucky.

"All right," Theo said. "But I need to be alone with him. With the shoe. And I need you to understand that whatever he says, I don't decide what happens after. I'm not—" His hands flexed at his sides, bone dust in the creases of his knuckles. "I'm not the one who decides anything. I hear. That's all I am. A door that hears."

Wren studied him for a long moment. "That's more than most people are," she said finally, and left him alone with the dead.

* * *

The shoe sat in the center of the workroom table under a single oil lamp, and Theo sat across from it with his hands flat on the wood, feeling like a fraud, feeling like a priest, feeling like both at once and neither.

He had heard the dead since he was six years old. His mother had bound the gift tighter when the purges came — that much Ember had hinted, that much he suspected — but binding wasn't erasing, and the voices leaked through anyway, murmuring at the edges of every room he'd ever entered. Griefs. Grievances. The small unfinished business of the finished. For five years in Vellamarch he had pretended not to hear, and the pretending had nearly eaten him alive.

Now he opened the door on purpose, and it was worse, somehow. Better. Worse.

"Tam," he said quietly. "My name is Theo. I carve bones. I'm not here to hurt you."

The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

They always leaned. He'd stopped noticing years ago, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. But tonight he watched it happen and felt the old fear rise in his throat — the fear that had kept him alive and hollow — and he pushed through it, because a woman named Sera Arlow deserved better than his fear.

"I know what happened to you," he said. "I know it wasn't the walkway."

The cold arrived first. It always did. Not temperature exactly — presence, the way a room changes when someone enters it, translated into the language of skin. Then the voice, thin and wet and seven years old:

*I can't find my other one.*

Theo's chest seized. "Your other shoe?"

*Mama needs them both. She'll count them. She counts everything at night, when she thinks we're asleep. If one's missing she'll think I lost it and she'll be sad about the wrong thing.*

Oh, he thought. Oh, this is why I hid. This is why hiding was mercy.

"We'll find it," Theo said, and his voice cracked on the promise, and he let it crack, because lying smoothly to the dead seemed like a special category of sin. "I swear it. But Tam — I need to ask you about that night. About the man in the gray coat."

The cold pulled back. Recoiled, almost. The lamp flame shrank to a blue bead.

*He smelled like the church,* the boy said at last. *Like candles but wrong. Like candles burning in a closed room for a hundred years.*

"What did he want?"

*He wanted me to come see the fish.* A pause, and then, smaller: *There aren't any fish in the Reach. I knew that. I'm not stupid. But he held my hand and his hand was warm and Mama works until dark and nobody holds my hand anymore because I'm too big for it, except I'm not, I liked it, and then—*

The voice stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker hits the edge of what can be said.

"You don't have to tell me the rest," Theo whispered.

*The water was warm at the top,* Tam said, conversational now, terrible now, the flat recitation of a child describing weather. *Cold underneath. He put my hand on the rail so my fingers would be there. So everyone would say I gripped it myself and slipped. He knew how. He'd done it before.* A beat. *Two times before. He told me while he did it. He talks while he works, like Papa used to talk while he carved. He said names. He said, "That's Ollie Pike, that was. And little Nettie Corr before him."*

Theo's hands had gone white on the table's edge. He realized distantly that he was shaking.

"Where," he managed. "Tam, where does he live? Where do we find him?"

And the dead boy answered, simple as arithmetic, the way the dead always answered when you asked them true questions — location meant nothing to them, walls meant nothing, the whole city lay open to their sight like a map drawn on glass:

*Gray House. Nine Chimney Lane. Third floor, the rooms facing the water. He keeps a box under the floorboard by his bed. There are shoes in the box. Four of them. None of them mine.*

The lamp guttered and steadied. The cold withdrew, reluctantly, like a child called away from a window.

*Tell Mama I didn't slip,* Tam said, from very far away now. *Tell her I was brave. Tell her I counted to ten underwater, like she taught me, and I got to ten.*

Theo put his head down on the table among the lamplight and the salt-stained leather and wept, silently, the way he'd learned to weep in five years of hiding — noiselessly, thoroughly, alone.

Then he wiped his face, picked up the shoe, and went to find Wren.

* * *

Nine Chimney Lane climbed the hillside above the Drowned Quarter in a crooked line, tenements leaning together like conspirators, laundry strung between them like bunting for a country that didn't exist. Gray House stood third from the corner, taller than its neighbors, its stucco recently repaired, its windows glazed with actual glass instead of oiled cloth. Money lived here. Not Hill Quarter money, but money enough — money that had flowed downhill from somewhere and pooled in a man's pockets.

Wren cased it with him from the shadow of a cooper's yard across the lane. Beside her, a Gardener Theo knew only as Fen — a broad, quiet woman with a forger's hands — sketched the building's entrances on a scrap of slate.

"Third floor, water-facing rooms," Wren murmured. "Your ghost was precise?"

"Ghosts are always precise. It's the living who lie." Theo kept his eyes moving. "Under the floorboard by the bed. Shoes."

Fen's slate pencil paused. "Shoes."

"Four pairs. Children's." He heard his own voice go flat and strange. "None of them Tam's."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Down the lane, a woman beat a rug over a rail, and the dull thuds sounded like a slow heart.

"Here's what happens now," Wren said. "We don't kick the door. Kicking doors is for Inquisitors and amateurs. Fen lifts the floorboard tonight while he's at his club — he keeps membership at the Gilded Anchor, plays cards Thursdays, we've watched him three weeks waiting for a case worth the risk. We take the shoes. We take any papers. Tomorrow morning, copies of what we find go to the magistrate's clerk, the parish record office, and the families of Ollie Pike and Nettie Corr — sealed, witnessed, unforgeable, because Fen forged the seals herself. And the man comes before a real court or the whole city learns why he hasn't."

"And if the court protects him anyway?" Theo asked. "Gray coats, Wren. You said it yourself."

Wren's smile had no warmth in it at all. "Then the families learn anyway. And a man who drowns children finds that doors stop opening for him, and credit stops flowing, and his club asks him politely to resign, and one night soon he walks home past the Reach and hears the water saying his name. We don't kill, Amberdale. We make sure the world remembers. It's slower than a knife. It lasts longer."

Theo thought of the Crown's justice: swift, sealed, and sold to the highest bidder. He thought of Inquisitor Hale's polite voice and her ledger full of names. He thought of Tam counting to ten underwater.

"It's right," he said slowly, wondering at himself even as he said it. "It's terrifying, and it's outside everything I've ever been allowed to believe in, and it's right."

"That's the Gardeners," Wren said. "Get used to it."

* * *

They lifted the floorboard at half past nine, by lamplight shielded behind a shuttered window, while the man in the gray coat — Master Aldous Renk, wool factor, pillar of two parishes — drank port at the Gilded Anchor and laughed at jokes Theo would never hear.

The box was tin, rusted at the corners, and heavier than shoes should be.

Four pairs of children's shoes, arranged neatly, toes pointing toward the wall. And beneath them, folded papers: letters of condolence received, letters of sympathy sent, a coroner's fee receipt dated twice — once for Nettie Corr, once for Ollie Pike — and a page of figures in a careful hand. Payouts. Insurance sums collected on the lives of children whose deaths he had arranged, insured through brokers who never asked why a wool factor held policies on dockworkers' sons.

Fen copied everything by lantern light, her forger's fingers steady as a surgeon's. Wren stood watch at the window. And Theo knelt on the floorboards of a murderer's bedroom, holding a dead child's shoe, listening to the room itself.

Because the room remembered. Rooms always remembered; the dead soaked into plaster and timber like rain into chalk. This room was saturated. Two ghosts clung here, faint, older than Tam — a girl and a boy, Nettie and Ollie, hovering at the edges of his hearing like voices through a wall.

*He washed the coat,* the girl-ghost whispered, over and over, a loop of horror. *He washed the coat in our basin.*

And the boy-ghost said only one thing, quiet and endless: *Cold. Cold. Cold.*

Theo knelt there in the lamplight with the shoes in his lap and understood, finally and completely, what he was. Not a curse. Not a freak to be hidden. A witness. The only kind the drowned would ever get.

"Wren," he said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him. "We need to return these to the mothers. Tonight. Before he gets home. Sera Arlow needs to know her son counted to ten."

Wren looked at him across the murdered children's bedroom, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod without argument.

* * *

Sera Arlow took the shoe with both hands, the way you take communion, the way you take a verdict.

She lived in a single room above a rope-walk, and she listened to Theo's account — all of it, the hand held warm, the fish that weren't there, the counting — without weeping, without moving, her face gone smooth and terrible as carved bone. When he reached the end, *he got to ten*, her mouth trembled once and firmed again.

"You're certain," she said. "Certain it was him. Renk."

"I'm certain your son told the truth," Theo said. "He has no reason left to lie."

She nodded slowly. Then she kissed the small wet-swollen shoe, set it on her table facing the door — so Tam could find his pair, she said, so he wouldn't lose his way home — and looked up at Theo with eyes like the Reach in winter.

"What happens to him now?"

"Justice," Wren said from the doorway. "Real justice. Slow, public, and permanent."

Sera Arlow considered this. "Good," she said. "And if your justice fails — Gardener or no — you bring him to me, and I will show you what a mother's justice looks like. I've raised four children on this street. I know how to make things last."

Walking back down through the Drowned Quarter, past the black water slapping at the pilings, Theo felt something shift in his chest that frightened him more than any Inquisitor ever had.

For five years he had hidden, and hiding had kept him alive. But standing in that rope-walk room, watching a mother set a shoe facing the door, he had felt — for the first time since the outlawry, since the purges, since his mother's grave — *useful*. The gift he'd spent half a lifetime burying had walked into a murderer's house and come out carrying the truth in a tin box.

Justice outside the law felt right. That was the terrifying part. Because if this was right, then everything he'd believed about safety, about silence, about staying small — all of it was wrong, and had always been wrong, and the life he'd built on those beliefs was built on sand.

"You're thinking loudly," Wren said beside him. "Even for someone who talks to ghosts."

"I'm thinking that I burned a prophecy page to keep from being caught," Theo said. "And four days later I'm breaking into a wool factor's house with forgers and thieves, and it feels like the first honest thing I've done in five years."

Wren laughed — short, surprised, real. "Welcome to the Gardeners."

They came up out of the lanes onto the ridge road, and Theo stopped.

Below them, the Drowned Quarter spread out under the moon — and beyond it, at the far edge of the city where no lamps burned, the invisible border shimmered. He'd seen it flicker once before, the night of the First Guttering, a heartbeat when the streets at the edge went translucent as breath on glass. He'd told himself it was exhaustion.

It wasn't exhaustion. Far out along the border, in the dark, the shimmer stuttered again — longer this time, a full three seconds, the outline of a whole country blinking like a dying lamp — and in the silence after it, Theo heard, faint and vast and weary, a voice he now knew as well as his own.

Ember-of-Ashes, last dragon of Vellamarch, sighing across the miles:

*Sooner than I hoped, maker. Sooner than I feared. Come home — we have less time than the prophecy promised, and I have a confession that will change what you think you owe me.*

Theo looked at Wren. Wren, who had heard nothing, saw his face and read it anyway.

"The fire's failing faster," she said.

"The fire's failing faster," Theo agreed, "and Ember wants to tell me something I'm not going to like."

They ran for the root-cellar together, and behind them the border flickered once more, like an eye trying to stay open.
