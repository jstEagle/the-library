## 8. Wren's Price

Wren Sallow had three rules for the root-cellar, and the first was: never let anyone see you count the exits.

There were four. The stairs up to the tannery, which could be barred with an iron rod in under two seconds. The coal chute, too narrow for a grown man in armor but not for a grown man without it. The old well shaft that went down further than anyone had bothered to map, and the crawl-space behind the drying racks that smelled of tannin and, on bad nights, of the river. She counted them now, out of habit, while she watched Theo Amberdale stand in the middle of her sanctuary looking like a man who had been handed a live coal and told it was a gift.

Ember-of-Ashes had spoken to him. She'd seen it happen — seen the boy go still and strange, the way the old Gardener dead went still when the dragon-bone was brought into a room. And now he knew something she didn't, and he wasn't telling her all of it, and that was fine. That was the Gardener way. Everyone in the network had a locked drawer somewhere. Hers had a name in it.

"Sit down," she said. "You look like you're about to fall over, and if you fall over, you'll crack your head on the cider press, and then I'll have two ghosts in here instead of one, and one of them will be insufferable."

Theo sat. He sat the way tired people sit — all at once, like a puppet with cut strings — on the bench beside the cold hearth. Up close he looked worse than he had in the bone-shop. Five years of hiding will do that to a face. She'd seen it on forgers and fence-runners and once on a midwife who'd spent a decade healing people the hospitals wouldn't touch. The hiding carved lines of its own.

"You knew," he said. "About the bone. About Ember. Before you came for me."

"I knew there was a bone in my cellar that talked, if you had the ears for it." She poured him cider from the jug on the shelf — the good jug, the one she saved for recruits and funerals, which in the Gardeners' arithmetic were often the same event. "I don't. Never had the gift. That's why I need you."

"You need me." He took the cup but didn't drink. "Everyone keeps needing me. The dragon needs me. The prophecy needs me. Now the Gardeners."

"Yes," Wren said, because lying to recruits was a debt you paid for years, and she'd learned to stop taking the loan. "I do. And I'm going to tell you what that costs, and what I'm asking, and then you're going to hate me a little, and then you're going to decide anyway. That's how it went for me."

He looked at her then, properly, and she saw the carver in him — the way his eyes went to hands first, to the small truths people carried in their knuckles. She folded hers around the cider cup and told him.

* * *

"My brother's name was Tam," she said. "Tammas Sallow. He was a cooper by trade and a bad liar by nature, which in Vellamarch is a dangerous combination. When the Crown outlawed the working of charms — real outlawing, with the gallows in it, not the polite kind they'd had before — Tam kept doing it anyway. Not because he was brave. Because he was kind, and kindness is worse. Bravery you can argue with. Kindness just keeps getting up in the morning and going back to the sick houses."

Theo's cup had stopped halfway to his mouth. She kept her eyes on the hearth. It was easier to say things to a cold hearth.

"There was a fever in the Drowned Quarter. You'll know it — you work the tenements. The Crown physicians had written the Quarter off. Plague logic: cull the loss, save the ledger. Tam went in with a satchel of fever-charms his mother had taught him to cut. Bone charms, actually. Ash and elder, the cheap woods, because bone was for the ones who could pay." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He saved eleven people that I could ever count. Maybe more. The Crown's records say he caused the fever. They produced witnesses — three of them, respectable men, one of them a physician whose practice Tam's charms had ruined, because you can't charge a grieving family for a cure that already worked. False testimony. The whole machine of it — the trial, the verdict, the sentence — ran in nine days. Tam's trial took less time than the fever did."

The cellar was very quiet. Even the drying racks seemed to hold their creak.

"They hanged him on a Tuesday," Wren said. "Market day, so there'd be a crowd. My mother went and stood at the front so he'd see one friendly face. She stood there until they cut him down, and then she went home and made supper, because that's what you did, and she never spoke his name again as long as she lived. Said it out loud once at the end, though. Delirious. Said, *Tam, the door's open, come in out of the rain.*" Wren turned the cup in her hands. "I was seventeen. I spent two years being angry at the wrong things — at the witnesses, at the magistrate, at God in whatever shape He was taking that season. And then an old woman with a green chalk mark on her door found me breaking into a records office to steal my brother's trial transcript, and instead of turning me in, she asked if I wanted the *real* transcript. The one with the parts the court struck out. And that was how I found the Gardeners."

"The Gardeners," Theo said slowly, "keep court records?"

"The Gardeners keep everything the Crown throws away. Confessions bought with bread. Verdicts reversed in private. Names of the dead with the word *innocent* written under them in the clerk's own hand, because some clerks can't sleep either." She finally looked at him. "We don't just break people out of gaols, bone-carver. We keep a ledger of our own. Every name the law got wrong. There are four hundred and six of them. I've read every entry. I can recite the first twenty in my sleep, and often do."

Theo set his cup down. "And you need my gift to clear them."

"I need your gift to *hear* them." The words came out harder than she'd planned, so she let them stay hard. Honesty with an edge was still honesty. "The dead don't haunt courtrooms, Theo. They haunt the places they died, and the people who loved them, and sometimes the people who killed them. The Crown burned its witnesses' testimony into the record, and the record is a wall of stone, and stone doesn't talk. But the dead do. To you. A drowned boy told you where his killer lives — I heard about that, it travels — and that boy's mother will sleep tonight for the first time in three years because a bone-carver listened. Now multiply that. Four hundred and six times. Mothers and brothers and sons who were hanged on lies, and every one of them has a story that ends with *they got it wrong,* and you're the only person in Vellamarch who can write it down."

"That's not justice," Theo said quietly. "That's an archive."

"It's the only justice that lasts," Wren said. "Gallows justice rots with the rope. But a name cleared — a name cleared is a stone that stays turned over. And stones turned over, bone-carver, have a way of starting avalanches." She stood, because sitting still while saying this had become unbearable. "The Crown didn't outlaw magic because magic is dangerous. The Crown outlawed magic because magic is *evidence.* A charm-healer can prove the fever wasn't caused by the man they hanged. A bone-carver can put a murdered child's voice in a magistrate's ear. Your gift is a witness that can't be bought and can't be hanged, and that's why they'll burn you for it, and that's why I'm asking you to use it anyway."

* * *

Theo didn't answer right away. Wren gave him the silence, because she'd learned that from the old woman with the chalk — recruits need room to hate the truth before they can carry it.

He turned the cider cup in circles on the bench. "You said I'd hate you a little."

"Do you?"

"I'm deciding." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "You found me because of the prophecy. Don't pretend otherwise. You came to my shop because a hundred-year-old book has my name in it, and you thought I might be useful to the fire, and to the Gardeners, and to your four hundred and six names. All of it at once. I'm not a person to you, Wren. I'm a hinge."

The word landed harder than she expected. She thought of Tam's hands, the cooper's hands, the way they'd turned a stave and known it was true. *A hinge,* he'd said once, laughing, holding up a barrel lid. *The whole thing swings on me, Ren.*

"No," she said. "You're not a hinge. Hinges don't get asked." She crossed the cellar and sat down across from him, close enough that he'd have to look at her. "Here's the part you'll hate most, so I'll say it plain and then never say it again. Yes, I came to your shop because of the prophecy. Yes, I thought you might be useful. And yes, when I stood in that bone-shop watching you lie to an Inquisitor so badly that I nearly stepped in to save you from yourself — I thought, *there's a man who's been hiding so long he's forgotten what his hands are for.* And I wanted to give them back to you. That's the truth. Both truths. I want to use you and I want to free you, and I have never once in my life been able to tell those two things apart, because that's what was done to me, and I do it to everyone, and I'm sorry, and I'm not going to stop."

Theo stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a man finding a floor where he'd expected another fall.

"You rehearsed that," he said.

"Every word. For three days."

"It was terrible."

"It was honest."

"It was both." He picked up the cider and drank at last, a long swallow, like a man accepting terms. "All right. Wren. Here's mine, since we're trading. I heard the dead for the first time when I was six years old, and I thought I was cursed, and my mother told me I was cursed, in so many words — told me to hide it, always, that hearing was the same as being seen, and being seen was the same as dying. I have spent my whole life being a door with the lock thrown, and the dead knocking, and me pretending I can't hear. And last night a drowned boy put his hand in mine and said *please,* and I listened, and it was the first time in five years I've felt like a person instead of a hiding place." He set the cup down. "So yes. I'll hear your names. I'll clear what I can clear. Not because you used me well — you did, and I hate it — but because I'm done throwing the lock."

Wren felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the bone-shop. She didn't let it show. Rule two: never let a recruit see you're relieved. Relief reads as need, and need reads as weakness, and — no. She caught herself. That was Hale's arithmetic, the Inquisitor's arithmetic, the Crown's whole cold ledger. The Gardeners had a different one.

Rule two, the real one: when someone gives you a truth, hold it like a bone you've been trusted to carve.

"Then we start tonight," she said. "There's a name at the top of the list I've never been able to close. A ferryman, Aldous Merrin, hanged fourteen years ago for scuttling his own boat. Forty-one drowned. The Crown said he took bribes to overload the ferry and cut the lines. But Merrin's daughter joined us two years back, and she's spent every coin she has trying to prove the boat was rotten — Crown-owned, Crown-inspected, Crown-refused-repair, three times, in writing. If the dead man will speak to you —"

The bone on the shelf spoke first.

It was a small sound, a sound like a log settling in a banked fire, and Theo's head came up as if someone had called his name. Wren watched him listen to the silence she couldn't hear, and watched his face do something complicated — the look of a man hearing a confession he'd been promised.

"What," she said. "What is it."

Theo's voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a man carrying something fragile across ice. "Ember says the confession she owes me — the one she warned me about. She says I should hear it before I agree to anything. Before I agree to *you,* before I agree to the prophecy, before I agree to anything at all." He looked at the dragon-bone, and then at Wren, and his eyes were frightened in a new way. Not the fear of being caught. The fear of being *told.* "She says the prophecy isn't what the book says it is. She says she wrote it herself. And she says —"

He stopped. Swallowed.

"She says the door out of Vellamarch was never meant for me to walk through. It was meant for me to *be.*"

The coal chute rattled, once, in the wind. Wren counted the exits without meaning to, all four of them, and understood suddenly that none of them led anywhere she'd want to go.

"Then we'd better hear the rest of it," she said. "Tonight."
