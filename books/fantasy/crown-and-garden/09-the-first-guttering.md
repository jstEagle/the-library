## 9. The First Guttering

Theo learned to tell the time by the dead.

The drowned quarter's ghosts grew restless at dawn, when the tide turned and dragged their memories loose from the mud. The mill-children woke at noon, always asking for bread they'd never finished eating. And in the deep hours past midnight, when even Wren's root-cellar sanctuary went quiet, the old dead — the ones who had forgotten their own names but not their grievances — murmured like wind through a keyhole.

So when the voices stopped, Theo woke instantly, completely, the way a man wakes when the floor disappears beneath him.

He sat up on his pallet between shelves of drying herbs and jars of rendered tallow, and he listened to nothing.

Not silence. Silence was a thing he knew, the ordinary hush of living rooms where no one spoke to him unless they could see him. This was different. This was absence with weight behind it, like a held breath, like the pause between lightning and thunder stretched out until you forgot which one you were waiting for.

"Ember," he whispered.

The dragon-bone lay wrapped in wool on the shelf above him, warm as a sleeping bird. It didn't answer. It hadn't answered much since the confession — since Ember told him, in a voice like banked coals, that the prophecy was its own dying wish and not fate at all. Three days of sulking silence from a creature two hundred years dead. Theo had almost gotten used to it.

But this wasn't sulking. He put his hand against the wool and felt the bone shivering.

*Something's wrong,* the bone said, without words, the way heat says it through a door.

Then the lamp went out.

Not guttered — went out, all at once, as if the flame had been plucked from the wick by invisible fingers. Theo stood in perfect darkness and heard Wren bolt upright across the cellar, heard her knife leave its sheath before her feet touched the floor.

"Theo." Her voice came low and level, the voice she used for forgeries and emergencies, which were often the same thing. "Tell me what you feel."

"I don't feel anything."

"That's what frightens me."

* * *

They came up through the trapdoor into the chandler's back room, and then up again into the street, because Wren had a rule: when the wards did something new, you looked at them. The Gardeners' district — the Drowned Quarter proper, the tenement warrens south of the canal — existed inside a fold of Vellamarch that most maps declined to acknowledge. The Crown's surveyors had walked these streets a century ago and written down that they were marshland. The wards kept the lie alive, kept the Inquisitors' eyes sliding off the doors and their boots finding only mud where cobbles should be.

Theo had lived his whole life inside that lie without knowing it was a lie. Five nights in the Gardeners' company had taught him to see the seams: the way lamplight bent slightly wrong at the corner of Sallow Street, the way the moon over the canal seemed a finger's width too large, the faint sweet smell of dragon-fire under everything, like a hearth burning in a room you couldn't find.

Tonight the smell was gone.

Wren stood in the middle of the street in her grey coat, hood down, hair plastered to her forehead by river damp, staring upward. Theo followed her gaze and forgot how to breathe.

The sky was flickering.

Not clouds. Not lightning. The sky itself — the deep violet-dark of it, the scatter of stars — stuttered like a candle in draft. For a heartbeat he saw the stars of home, the constellations every child in the Drowned Quarter learned: the Fisherwoman, the Ox-Cart, the Weeping King. Then the sky blinked, and the constellations were strangers, arrangements he'd never seen, and the moon hung in the wrong quadrant entirely. Then blink, and home again.

"It's never done that," Wren said. "In eleven years. It has never done that."

Around them the street was waking. Doors opened along the tenements, cautiously, the way people open doors during earthquakes. Old Marta from the chandlery stood in the doorway in her nightgown, holding a rushlight that burned steady while everything else flickered. A fisherman Theo didn't know came up the alley at a run and stopped dead, staring at the same sky.

"The lamps," the fisherman said. "Look at the lamps."

Down the length of Sallow Street, the street-lanterns were going out one by one — not blown, not failing, but *unlit*, each flame vanishing with a small sound Theo felt more than heard, a sound like a syllable being swallowed. Then, three heartbeats later, they relit themselves, all together, and the light that came back was subtly wrong. Warmer. Older. The color of fire Theo had only seen once before, coiled around a bone in a wool wrapping.

Dragon-fire. The lanterns of the Drowned Quarter had been burning it all along. He'd walked beneath it his whole life and never known he was standing in a dragon's breath.

"Wren." His mouth was dry. "What happens if they go out and stay out?"

She didn't answer immediately. She was doing sums behind her eyes; he could see her doing them, the same look she wore pricing a forgery or counting exits.

"Then the streets stop pretending," she said finally. "And everyone who can see them is standing in the open when they do."

* * *

The minute lasted longer than a minute. Theo counted it afterward and made it ninety seconds, though time itself seemed to stutter along with the sky, stretching and folding, so who could say. Ninety seconds, near enough, in which the Drowned Quarter stopped existing.

It began with the smell of salt.

The canal — the real canal, the one that ran stinking and silver through the district — vanished. Not drained: *vanished*, replaced by an open wound of grey water that stretched to a horizon Theo had never seen, a sea-road with ships on it, masts like winter trees. The tenements around him thinned like breath on glass. Through the wall of the building beside him he could see, for one horrible instant, straight through brick and plaster and someone's hanging laundry into a market square he'd never visited, full of people in foreign clothes buying eels from a stall that flew a flag he didn't recognize.

The wards had failed. All of them, everywhere, all at once — and the country underneath had shown through, the way a painted floor shows the boards beneath when the paint wears thin.

Vellamarch was a coat of paint. That was the thought that arrived, cold and complete, in the middle of the flickering. A century ago someone had painted a country over this place — over the harbor, the sea-road, the foreign flags — and called the paint Vellamarch, and everyone born under it had believed the paint was the world.

Including me. *Especially* me.

Then the paint tore, and something else stepped through it.

Boots. That was the first thing: boots on cobbles, many boots, in step, coming from the north end of Sallow Street where the ward-stone stood disguised as a milestone. Theo knew that sound. Every child in Vellamarch knew that sound the way farm children know weather. Inquisitor patrols marched in cadence of eight, cloaks hemmed with lead-weighted chain so they wouldn't billow and betray them at night, and their boots hit the ground like a metronome set to dread.

"There shouldn't be patrols here," Wren breathed. "There have never been patrols here. The wards won't let them—"

The wards weren't letting anything anywhere tonight.

Theo grabbed her sleeve and pulled, and for once she let herself be pulled, and they went over a fence, through a dyer's yard rank with the smell of mordant, into the maze of alleys behind the tanneries that even the Gardeners' chalk-maps marked with a warning squiggle meaning *don't bother*. They crouched behind a stack of bark hides, and through the gap Theo watched the patrol pass.

Eight men. Lead-hemmed cloaks. Lanterns burning white and steady — real flame, Crown flame, none of your dragon nonsense — and the light from those lanterns fell on the cobbles of Sallow Street and did not bend. Did not slide away. Fell clean and hard and honest on stone that had spent a hundred years refusing to be seen.

One of them stopped. A young one, face soft above the black gorget, and he turned in a slow circle, staring at the tenements, the canal — the wrong canal, still showing through in patches, grey and vast — the impossible sky. He looked like a man waking inside someone else's dream.

"Sergeant," he said. "This street isn't on any chart I've ever seen."

"It's on tonight's," said the sergeant, and tapped his lantern. "Ward-failure dispatch. Everything the light touches is ours till it's mended. Move."

Everything the light touches. Theo pressed himself flatter against the hides and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what he was watching. The wards would mend themselves — Ember's fire was dying, not dead, and it had gutters left in it. But now the Crown had seen. Now there was a dispatch, a sergeant with orders, a young soldier memorizing streets that officially did not exist. You could repaint the wall, but the crack was mapped now. Written down. Filed.

Countries didn't die all at once. They died the way lamps went out: one flame at a time, with a small sound like a swallowed word, and someone taking notes the whole while.

* * *

The patrol passed. Another followed it, twelve minutes later, walking a different street — Theo tracked them by sound, by the dead.

Because the dead had come back.

Halfway through the flickering, mid-heartbeat, mid-blink, the voices had returned like blood rushing into a numb limb — painful, chaotic, all of them talking at once. But they were changed. The drowned quarter's ghosts had always been local, parochial, bound to their mud and their grievances. Tonight they sounded *afraid*.

*It went dark,* an old woman's ghost was saying somewhere overhead, in the empty air above the tannery yard, in the tone of someone reporting a disaster. *I couldn't see my house. Fifty years I've watched my house from the window and I couldn't see it. Where was my house?*

*I heard the sea,* said another voice, deeper, older, a man who had perhaps drowned on the grey water Theo had glimpsed. *I haven't heard the sea since I died here. I thought I'd dreamed it.*

*They're coming. They're coming with their white lights. Hide us, hide us, hide us—*

"They're frightened," Theo whispered.

Wren looked at him. In the wrong-colored lantern light her face was all angles and shadow, and for once she didn't ask him to explain what he meant, didn't perform her usual elaborate politeness about his gift. She just looked at him the way she'd looked at the sky.

"What do frightened ghosts do?" she asked.

"I don't know. Cling to things, maybe. To places. To—" He stopped. "To people."

"Then we need to get ahead of this." She was already moving, low and quick, back toward the fence. "Every safehouse, every cache, every family that shelters us — if the wards keep guttering, the Inquisitors will be able to see all of it. We've got hours, maybe. Ember said the fire comes back after each failure, yes? Stronger for a little while?"

"For a little while. Each time weaker overall. Like—" Theo searched for it. "Like a man coughing blood but insisting he's fine between coughs."

"Cheerful. Come on."

They moved. Over the fence, down the alley, Wren pausing at the mouth of each street to check the lanterns' color — dragon-warm, still, mostly — before waving him across. Theo followed, and the dead swirled around him like startled birds, and he did the only useful thing he knew how to do: he listened.

*The milestone,* the old woman ghost insisted, tugging at him the way cold tugs at a door left open. *The white-light men are gathered at the milestone. There's a man writing. Writing our street. Writing it down in a book. You mustn't let them write it, boy, once it's written it stays written, that's how they took the churchyard in my grandmother's day, they wrote it and wrote it until it was true—*

Theo caught Wren's arm at the corner of Tanner's Row.

"There's a clerk at the ward-stone. At the milestone. Taking notes." He swallowed. "The dead say once it's written it stays written. They remember it happening before. To other places."

Wren went very still. Somewhere behind them, down the length of Sallow Street, a lantern guttered — one long stutter, dark, dark, dark — and relit, and the light that came back was thinner than before. Paler. More nearly ordinary flame and less nearly dragon.

"How long," she said quietly, "has the dead woman's grandmother been dead?"

"A hundred years. Maybe more."

"So it's happened before. Slowly. Street by street." Her jaw tightened. "And we're watching it happen fast."

She turned toward the milestone. Toward the scratch of the clerk's pen, audible now that he listened for it, faint and industrious under the boot-cadence and the flickering sky. Theo looked at her and understood what she was deciding, and hated that he understood, and understood that too.

"We can't fight eight Inquisitors," he said.

"No."

"And a clerk."

"No," Wren agreed. "We can't fight them. But we might be able to *misplace* them." And there it was, the Gardener's grin, sharp and sudden, the expression of a woman who had spent eleven years answering crimes the law ignored and had stopped being impressed by the law's arithmetic. "A ledger burns, Theo. A ledger falls in a canal. A ledger is carried off by a very confused pair of ghosts who owe me a favor from 1841."

"You can't order the dead around."

"No," Wren said, starting down the street, drawing him with her into the flickering dark. "But apparently I keep company with someone the dead *ask*."

Above them the sky blinked — Fisherwoman, strangers, Fisherwoman — and the last thing Theo heard before they reached the milestone was the old woman ghost, urgent as smoke:

*Hurry, boy. Hurry. The country's going out.*
