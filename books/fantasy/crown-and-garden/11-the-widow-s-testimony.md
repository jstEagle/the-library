## 11. The Widow's Testimony

The news came at dawn, carried by a boy who smelled of river mud and fear.

Theo was in the root-cellar sanctuary's work corner, carving a practice piece he didn't need and couldn't stop making — a small bird with folded wings, the kind of charm his hands made when his mind was somewhere else. The cellar was quiet at that hour. Ember's bone rested on its shelf of velvet, humming its faint heat into the dark, and the dead who drifted through the Gardeners' refuge kept a polite silence around him, as if they understood that even a man who heard ghosts needed mornings.

The boy burst down the stairs two at a time.

"Master Amberdale — they took her. The widow. Marla Coss."

The knife stopped against the bone.

"Say that again," Theo said, though he'd heard it perfectly.

"Marla Coss. Inquisitors came to her rooms on Candlemaker's Row before first bell. Took her and everything she owned. They put her in a wagon and — " the boy swallowed, "— and they read out the charge in the street so everyone could hear. Possession of an unlicensed charm. Carved bone, maker unknown."

Maker known, Theo thought. Maker standing right here with shavings still on his sleeves.

Wren Sallow appeared in the doorway behind the boy, already dressed, already awake, because Wren was always both. She had a way of arriving exactly when bad news did, as though she'd been standing behind it all along, waiting for it to knock. Her eyes went from the boy to Theo to the half-carved bird in his hand.

"How long ago?" she asked.

"An hour. Maybe less."

"Then we have until the afternoon, maybe evening, before she's processed." Wren said *processed* the way other people said *buried*. She turned to Theo. "Sit down. You've gone the color of tallow."

He hadn't sat down. He was standing, and then he was moving, pulling on his coat, and Wren caught his arm with fingers like iron wire.

"Theo."

"It was my charm," he said. "I carved it for her husband's grave. I sat in her kitchen and drank her terrible tea and I listened to her dead man talk about how she over-salts the stew, and I made her something so she could sleep at night, and now they're going to — "

"I know what they're going to do. Sit. Down." She pushed him onto the stool with no ceremony at all. "You are the single most valuable person in this city right now, and you do not get to walk into an Inquisitor's holding house because your conscience itched. That is not how any of this works."

"Then how does it work?"

"That's what I'm about to go find out." She looked at the boy. "Get Tam and Odile. Wake them properly. Council in twenty minutes." Then back to Theo, softer, which for Wren meant only slightly less sharp: "You'll speak at council. You'll say your piece. And then you'll do what the Gardeners decide, because that's the whole point of us. We don't act alone. Alone is how people die."

*Alone is how people die.* He thought of five years of hiding, of biting his tongue while the dead begged him for help, of every small cowardice worn smooth as river stone. He thought of Marla Coss's kitchen, the smell of onions and beeswax, the way her hands shook when she'd asked, *Is he there? Is my Aldous there?*

He thought: *Alone is also where I've been living.*

"I'm coming to council," he said.

Wren studied him for a long moment, the way she studied locks and ledgers and anything else that might be lying to her.

"Yeah," she said finally. "I figured you would."

* * *

The Gardeners' council convened around a scarred table that had once, according to Tam, been a tavern door. There were nine of them at full muster, but this morning seven: Tam the forger with his ink-stained knuckles; Odile the healer, gray-braided and unhurried; the twins, Brack and Sef, who moved like one person split in two; old Maren, who ran the safehouses; the boy, Pip, too young for the table but present anyway because Wren believed in letting children watch how decisions got made; Wren; and Theo, sitting at the edge like a guest who suspected the invitation was a mistake.

Tam laid out what they knew, drawing on the tabletop in chalk because paper was for people with money.

"Marla Coss, fifty-one, laundress, widowed fourteen months. No family. No money. No friends worth torturing. The Inquisitors took her for the charm and nothing else — she has no connection to us that anyone can trace." He paused. "Except you."

"They don't know it's me," Theo said. "Hale suspects. Suspecting isn't knowing."

"Hale doesn't need to know," Maren said. Her voice was dry as old rope. "She needs a name for her ledger, and a bone-carver who won't confess is worth more than one who will. A confession ends the investigation. A mystery feeds it. Every day you stay free, she has reason to keep pulling threads. And every thread leads back here eventually."

"So we let an innocent woman hang for a mourning charm," Theo said.

"No one said that," Wren snapped.

"You were all thinking it."

Silence. It had the specific texture of a room full of people who had, in fact, been thinking it.

Odile spoke at last, gently, the way she probably spoke to the dying. "Theo. What you have to understand about rescue is arithmetic. Holding houses sit inside the ward-lines, inside the patrols, inside the districts where our chalk means nothing. To take her out, we send six or seven people through streets that will be watched twice as hard after an arrest like hers. If we lose one — just one — Gardener to the Inquisitors, they get a name. From a name they get a face. From a face they get a door. And behind every door we have is someone who can't run. The sick ones. The ones hiding from warrants older than you are. We are not seven people, Theo. We are forty-three, counting the ones in the safehouses, and every one of them is standing behind whoever walks into that holding house."

"I understand the arithmetic."

"Do you? Because I don't think you do yet. I think you understand grief, which is different. Grief says *one woman*. Arithmetic says *forty-three*."

"And what does justice say?" Theo asked. "Because I was told — Wren told me, the first night, when she pulled me out of my shop — that the Gardeners exist because the law stopped answering. That you answer crimes the courts ignore. That a woman whose husband died in the Drowned Quarter flood deserves better than a ledger that rounds her off." He stood up. His legs felt strange, too light, as though his body had already decided before the rest of him caught up. "This is the exact crime. Not some cousin of it. This is the law punishing the grieving for grieving wrong. If we walk past this one, then everything else is just — preference. Picking the cases that are convenient."

Pip, at the end of the table, was watching him with open-mouthed attention. Theo noticed and hated it, and kept talking anyway.

"She bought tea for a stranger because she was lonely. That's the whole crime. Somebody drank her tea and carved her a charm and now she's in a wagon headed to a cell where they'll ask her questions until she gives them a description, and she *will*, because she's brave but she's not stone, and then Hale will have my face and my hands and my shop, and after that none of us gets to choose whether this is convenient or not. The choice isn't rescue versus safety. The choice is rescue now, on our terms, or capture later, on theirs."

Nobody spoke. Ember's bone hummed faintly on its shelf across the cellar, and Theo realized he could hear it listening.

Maren broke the silence. "You've thought about this more than you let on."

"I haven't thought about anything else since the boy ran in."

"He's right about one thing," Tam said slowly, turning his chalk over. "If they break her, and they will — not because she's weak but because nobody's strong in front of the candle-irons — Hale gets the description within days. Better we move while the description is still a rumor."

"And worse," Sef added, "if the Gardeners are seen to abandon a widow we helped, every soul in the tenements hears about it by morning. Our protection is belief. Belief is fragile."

Brack nodded. "People forgive us failures. They don't forgive indifference."

Odile closed her eyes briefly, a healer conceding a wound. "Vote, then. All in favor of extraction?"

Five hands. Odile herself raised hers last, with the expression of a woman signing away something she'd fought to protect.

"All opposed?" No hands. Odile sighed. "Motion carries. Wren — plan it. And Theo—" she fixed him with a look that had set broken bones and knew how much it hurt, "—you will not be on the entry team. You're the asset. Assets stay behind."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

"What you're good at," Wren said, and there was something almost like a smile at the corner of her mouth, thin and sharp as a blade's edge. "You're going to talk to the dead. Marla's holding cell used to be a chapel crypt. Half the people who died in that building never left it. If anyone knows the layout, the guard rotations, the cracks in the walls — they do."

Theo stared at her. "You want me to interview ghosts for reconnaissance."

"I want you to be useful in the one direction the Inquisitors can't follow." She rolled up the chalk map with the flat of her hand. "Council adjourned. Pip, fetch me the cistern keys. Tam, I need three faces forged by noon. Theo — with me."

* * *

They planned in Wren's room, which was less a room than a fortified closet: maps pinned three deep, a cot she clearly never slept on, and a window that opened onto a chimney shaft rather than a street. She spread the holding-house plans Tam had lifted from a magistrate's office two years back and marked them up while Theo closed his eyes and listened.

It was harder here, underground, behind stone — the dead came through thinner, like voices shouted through walls. But the holding house was close, four streets east, and its dead were many and restless, and once he found the frequency they came in a trickle, then a stream.

*A woman who'd starved in 1841, muttering bread recipes.*

*A debtor who'd hanged himself, reciting the names of everyone who'd lent to him, in order of how much he hated them.*

*And then, faint but clear, a turnkey. Old, bitter, dead thirty years, pacing his old route out of habit.*

"—rounds at dusk and again at midnight, always midnight, lazy lot, they skip the west stair because it stinks of the drains, always skipped it, even when I was alive I told them —"

Theo's eyes flew open. "West stair. The guards skip it. It smells of the drains."

Wren didn't question it. That was the thing about her he was learning — she filed impossibilities without blinking, as though the world had shown her enough impossible things that wonder had become a kind of paperwork. "Show me."

He guided her through the next hour, ghost by ghost: the chapel crypt beneath the east wing where the bones of paupers were stacked like firewood; a grate in the crypt wall loose since before the dead turnkey could remember; the yard where the wagons came in; the count of lamps lit each night (eleven, always eleven, the miserly warden wouldn't pay for a twelfth). Wren drew it all, cross-checked it against Tam's stolen plans, and wherever the plans and the dead disagreed, she trusted the dead.

"Your gift," she said at one point, not looking up from the map, "is the most illegal thing in this city. You know that. Hearing the dead — that's not charm-craft. That's root magic. Old magic. The kind they burned people for even before the outlawry."

"I know."

"And you spent five years hiding it. Biting your tongue. Letting the dead scream at you and doing nothing."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Theo looked at his hands. Bone dust in the creases, always, no matter how he scrubbed. "Because the last time I answered, someone noticed. A girl in my village, drowned in the millpond. I told her mother where to find the body. Three days later an Inquisitor came asking why the carver's boy knew things carver's boys shouldn't. My mother lied for me. Paid for it, I think — she was never the same after. So I learned the lesson. Answer no one. Help no one. Stay small." He laughed, short and ugly. "Stayed small for five years and look what it got me. My name in a prophecy book and a widow in a wagon. Hiding didn't save anyone. It just delayed the bill."

Wren set down her pen. For a moment she was quiet, and in the lamplight Theo saw something in her face he hadn't seen before — not the sharp operative, not the recruiter, but the sister of a hanged man.

"My brother Tomas," she said, "was arrested for theft he didn't commit. The witness who lied about him was paid twelve crowns by the man Tomas had caught embezzling. Twelve crowns, Theo. My brother's life cost less than a good coat. And afterward, when I went looking for anyone — anyone at all — who would say *this was wrong*, loudly, where it mattered, there was no one. The courts had their story. The papers had their story. Everyone stayed small." She tapped the map, the holding house, the little marked grate. "That's why the Gardeners exist. Not because we're brave. Because staying small is a slow way of dying, and I refuse."

She rolled the map. "Extraction team meets at the cistern gate at moonset. Six of us. You'll be at the safehouse on Bell Lane with Odile — that's not negotiable, don't waste breath. When she's out, she goes to the farmstead north of the walls until it cools. And Theo —"

"Yes?"

"When she asks who pulled her out, what do you want us to tell her?"

He thought about it. About Marla Coss drinking her terrible tea, asking a stranger if the dead really lingered, and crying when he said yes — crying with relief, with the awful comfort of it.

"Tell her the truth," Theo said. "Tell her her husband wouldn't shut up about the stew."

* * *

Bell Lane safehouse was a dyer's attic that smelled of vinegar and mordant, and Odile spent the waiting hours teaching Theo to bind a wound, ostensibly as a useful skill and obviously as a way to keep his hands busy. The sun crawled. The dead of Bell Lane murmured through the floorboards — a dyer's apprentice who'd fallen into a vat, singing work songs — and Theo found himself answering under his breath, an old habit, giving them the courtesy of acknowledgment even when he could offer nothing else.

Moonset came and went.

The plan was simple, as Wren's plans went: the twins through the west stair with the grate irons; Tam at the wagon gate with forged transfer orders; Wren inside; Maren driving the second wagon. Ninety minutes, in and out, before the midnight rounds discovered the skipped stair was no longer being skipped.

At the ninety-minute mark, Theo was standing at the attic window with his heart hammering, watching the dark bulk of the holding house four streets east.

At one hundred minutes, a lamp flickered in the holding house yard. Then another. Then — wrong, all wrong — a dozen, torchlight spilling over the wall, and the sound came across the rooftops a heartbeat later: bells. Not the midnight rounds' single chime but the rapid triple-strike of alarm.

Odile was beside him instantly. "What do you see?"

"Lamps. Too many. Bells." His mouth had gone dry. "Something's happened."

They waited, and waiting was its own particular torture, every minute a year. Theo gripped the windowsill and did the only thing left to him: he reached out along the streets, past the wards, searching the dead near the holding house for anything — a glimpse, a whisper, a sign —

And found chaos. The dead of the holding house were pressed against its walls like moths against glass, agitated, echoing the panic of the living. Fragments reached him:

"—they came in through the drains, through the *drains*, who leaves the west stair unguarded—"

"—the woman's gone, the Coss woman's gone—"

"—found a chalk mark on the crypt grate, green chalk, the *mark*, it's the Gardeners, it's actually them—"

Theo sagged with relief so sudden his knees nearly buckled. "She's out. Odile — she's out. They got her through the crypt."

"Thank the roots," Odile breathed. Then, sharply: "Then what's the alarm for?"

He listened harder, dredging the panicked chorus, and the answer surfaced cold as pond water.

"They found a body. One of theirs — one of ours. A Gardener. Dead in the crypt." His voice sounded far away to himself. "The twins went in together. Only one came out."

* * *

The second wagon reached Bell Lane an hour before dawn, and it was Wren who climbed down first, and Wren who was carrying most of another person's weight, and Theo knew before he saw the face that it was Brack — or Sef; they'd never taught him to tell them apart, and now one of them would carry that knowledge alone forever.

Sef. It was Sef alive, white-faced, holding his brother's hand as they laid him on the dyer's table, and Odile was already moving, already cutting cloth, and Theo stood useless in the vinegar-smelling dark while the healer worked.

Marla Coss huddled in the corner wrapped in Maren's coat, alive, breathing, staring at nothing.

Wren crossed the room to Theo. There was blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers, and her voice, when it came, was scraped raw.

"We got her out clean. Clean exit, perfect timing, the whole thing went like the map said. And then a patrol lantern swept the yard early, and Brack doubled back to draw them off, and—" Her jaw tightened. "He drew them off. He just didn't come back from it. Sef got to him too late."

"Can Odile—"

"He's beyond healing. He's hanging on out of stubbornness, and he'll let go when he's ready, and until then we keep watch and we don't say goodbye where he can hear." She looked at the table, at the twins — at the twin, and the space where the other should have been — and when she turned back her eyes were wet and furious. "One rescue, Theo. One widow. And it cost us Brack, and it told the Inquisitors exactly who we are and where we strike from. Everything Odile warned about, come true in a single night."

Theo said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either an apology or a defense, and both would have been obscene.

But then Marla Coss rose from her corner, steadied herself against the wall, and crossed the room on unsteady legs until she stood in front of him. Up close she looked ten years older than she had in her kitchen, bruised at the wrists where the irons had been, but her eyes were clear.

"You're the carver," she whispered. "Aldous said — in the cell, I couldn't sleep, and he was *there*, I could feel him, the way I feel him when I hold the charm. He said the man who made it would come. He said, *tell him thank you, and tell him the stew was fine, he worried over nothing.*"

Somewhere behind Theo, Sef made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob.

Marla gripped Theo's hands with both of hers, hard, desperate. "They asked me questions for six hours. Candle-irons and cold water and a man reading names off a list, asking which ones I knew. I gave them nothing. Do you know why?" Her voice cracked. "Because a boy drank my tea and listened to me cry and carved my dead husband's love into a bit of bone, and no one had done a kind thing for me in fourteen months, and I decided that if kindness had that price, I'd pay it gladly."

She released his hands and stepped back, suddenly shy again, the widow returned.

"So whatever it costs you all — whoever that poor boy is on the table — don't you dare regret it. Regret is for people who did nothing."

Wren exhaled slowly, long and shaking. Across the room, Odile looked up from her work and gave the smallest nod: *she's right, and I hate it, but she's right.*

Theo looked at Brack's grey face, at Sef holding his hand, at Marla standing straight as a candle in the vinegar-dark, and felt something shift in his chest — some last bolt drawn back, some door he'd kept locked for five years swinging open onto a country he'd forgotten he lived in.

He was still standing there, changed, when the cellar door banged open and Pip came tumbling up the stairs two at a time, white as whey, a scrap of paper crushed in his fist.

"Wren — Wren, there's a notice nailed up outside, all over the district, they printed hundreds —"

Wren took it. Read it. And went very still.

Theo leaned in. The notice bore the Crown's seal and a woodcut likeness — crude, but unmistakable, the strong jaw, the carver's hands — above a single line of type:

**WANTED FOR UNLICENSED CHARM-CRAFT AND CONSPIRACY: THEO AMBERDALE OF VELLAMARCH. FIVE HUNDRED CROWNS, ALIVE OR DEAD.**

Below it, smaller, in the Inquisitor's formal hand, a line that made the blood drain from Theo's face:

*Any who shelter him shares his crime.*

Every safehouse. Every Gardener door. Every one of the forty-three.

Wren crumpled the notice slowly in her fist. "Well," she said quietly. "No more hiding, then."
