## 12. Raid on the Root-Cellar

Wren Sallow had three rules for the sanctuary, and she'd chalked them on the inside of the cellar door where every Gardener could see them: *One. Nothing true written down. Two. No one comes twice in one week. Three. If the bells ring wrong, you burn the chalk and go out through the water.*

The bells rang wrong at half past eleven on a night of hard frost.

She was upstairs in the tenement above the root-cellar, mending a coat that belonged to a dead man because his widow couldn't afford a new one, when the sound came — the Vellamarch bell pattern for *all's well*, played one beat too slow, the way Crown men rang it when they wanted the neighborhood to sleep and not look out its windows.

Her hands went still over the needle.

Below her feet, thirty feet down through floorboards and packed earth, the root-cellar held eleven people: Theo Amberdale bent over a case ledger, Ember's dragon-bone wrapped in oilcloth under the drying racks, and a scatter of Gardeners doing the ordinary work of an extraordinary crime — forging travel papers, boiling willow bark, arguing about whether the drowned-quarter case should be closed or pursued. Among them, seventeen-year-old Tam Ives, who joined the network eight months ago after his father's debts got him sold to a workhouse, and who had never once followed rule two.

Tam had come twice this week. Wren had told him off for it Tuesday. He'd grinned and said the cellar felt more like home than home did.

Now the bells rang wrong and Wren was already moving, coat still half-mended, needle still in her hand.

* * *

She took the stairs three at a time and came into the cellar's antechamber — the fake root store, all turnips and sawdust and a door that looked like it hadn't opened in years — and found Old Marta already there with her ear against the wood.

"Boots," Marta said. "A lot of them. Coming up Cooper's Lane, not hurrying."

Not hurrying meant they thought they had time. It meant they thought no one knew.

"Who else is down there?"

"Theo. Fen. the twins. Tam." Marta's jaw tightened around the boy's name. "And the bone."

The bone. Ember-of-Ashes, last dragon in Vellamarch, sleeping in oilcloth beneath a shelf of pickled beets. If Hale took that, she took everything — proof, weapon, heart of the whole enterprise.

Wren pulled the chalk from her sleeve. Rule three: burn the chalk, go out through the water. But there were four people below who didn't know yet, and one of them was a boy who'd come twice in one week because he loved the place too much to stay away.

"Get to the cistern gate," she told Marta. "Open it and stand in the dark. Anyone comes out, you pull them through and don't wait for stragglers."

"And you?"

"I'm going to buy us ninety seconds." She was already chalking the sigil on the antechamber wall — the false-door mark, the one that made a plastered-over archway read as solid stone to any eye that wasn't looking for the trick. "Ninety seconds is either plenty or nothing at all."

Marta went. Wren spat on her thumb, smudged the chalk just enough to make it look weathered, and dropped through the trapdoor into the cellar proper.

* * *

They heard her coming and turned — Theo with a carving knife still in his hand, shavings curling on the table beside a half-finished charm; Fen with a ledger he was already feeding to the stove; the twins frozen mid-argument; Tam by the far shelves, an arm's length from the oilcloth bundle, looking like a boy caught stealing jam.

"Bells," Wren said. One word did it. She'd drilled them until the word worked like a slap.

Fen shoved the ledger deep into the coals. The twins went for the escape tunnel — the long way, through the flooded section, the route that took ten minutes they didn't have. Theo stood where he was, knife in hand, and looked at the oilcloth bundle, then at Wren.

"We can't carry it through the water," he said. "It's too heavy wet, and if we drop it—"

"Then we don't carry it. We hide it." She was already moving, pulling the beet shelf away from the wall. Behind it, the old coal chute, bricked halfway up. "Theo — behind the bricks. Now. Chalk dust over the mortar so it reads as original."

He hesitated. She saw it — the bone-carver's instinct, the maker's horror at burying something living in a wall. Ember's voice lived in that bone. Wren had heard it herself once, low and vast, like wind deciding whether to be kind.

"Theo." She grabbed his shoulder. "If they take it, everything dies. Every ward, every hidden street, everyone in this room. Bury your ghost for one night."

Something crossed his face — she'd learn later it was the dead murmuring their opinion — and then he moved, bundling the oilcloth, wedging it into the gap behind the loose bricks, packing rubble in after. His hands shook. She pretended not to see.

"Tam!" she called. "Water route, now, with the twins."

But Tam had gone pale in a new way. He was staring at the table. At the case ledger's twin — the working copy, the one with names in it, the one Fen hadn't reached because it had been under Tam's elbow while he sat reading it like a novel, which was exactly what he'd been doing.

"It's fine," Tam said. "I'll put it back in the—"

The knock came at the top of the stairs. Three blows, measured, polite. Inquisitor-polite.

Everyone stopped breathing.

"Root cellar of the Halloway tenement," said a voice Wren knew from reports and nightmares — level, unhurried, a voice that asked questions the way surgeons asked permission. "Inquisitor Hale. We've had a report of unlicensed charms sold from these premises. Open the door, please."

*Please.* That was the worst part of Hale. The manners.

Wren moved without deciding to. She snatched the working ledger off the table, pressed it flat against her stomach under her coat, and pointed — tunnel, twins, now — with her chin. Then she looked at Tam, who hadn't moved, who was standing between her and the stairs with the particular paralysis of someone whose body has understood before his mind that there is nowhere left to go.

"Tunnel," she mouthed.

"I can't swim the flooded bit," he mouthed back, and she saw it was true, had always been true, had never come up in eight months because nobody asked a boy if he could swim, they just assumed—

The door at the top splintered.

* * *

Later, Wren would reconstruct those ninety seconds a hundred times and find a different failure each time. If she'd sent Tam first instead of the twins. If she'd burned the chalk on the inner door, filling the stairwell with the blinding stink-signal that would have brought every neighbor in the tenement down coughing and given them cover. If she'd simply thrown the ledger to Fen and taken Tam's arm herself.

In the moment, there was only light — lantern-light pouring down the stairs, boots, grey coats, and Inquisitor Hale coming down last with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman inspecting a pantry.

"Evening," Hale said. Her eyes did the room in one pass: the stove with its curling ash, the beet shelf slightly ajar, the fresh scratch-marks of brick-dust on Theo's sleeves. "You're keeping late hours for vegetables."

"This is a private residence," Fen began, in his best aggrieved-tenant voice, and Hale said, "It's a rented storage space leased to a cooperage that closed in spring," without looking at him, and Fen shut up, because she'd done the paperwork and they hadn't.

Two Inquisitors went for the twins, who had gotten exactly four steps toward the tunnel and now stood with their hands up, wearing the expressions of people about to become very boring very fast. A third kicked open the coal chute's brickwork, stared into the cavity, and called, "Clear. Old rubble."

Old rubble. Wren kept her face bored and her heartbeat somewhere behind her ribs, counting.

Hale walked the room slowly. She touched the drying racks. She looked at the stove, and Wren watched her notice the ash — paper ash, ink-flecked — and file it away in whatever ledger she kept behind her eyes. Then she came to Tam.

"And you are?"

"Nobody," Tam said. "I live upstairs. I was getting—" He gestured vaguely at the shelves. "Turnips."

"You were getting turnips," Hale repeated. She was standing close enough that Wren could see the boy's throat move. "At midnight. With your coat off."

Tam looked down at his coatless shoulders as if betrayed by them.

"He's simple," Wren said, stepping in, letting her voice go flat and tired, the voice of a landlady perpetually put upon. "He does errands for me. He forgets things. You want the turnips, take the turnips, and get out of my cellar, because I've got a delivery at dawn and you're standing on my stock."

Hale turned to her. Up close her eyes were pale and patient and absolutely awake, and Wren understood why the network feared her more than any magistrate: she wasn't cruel, and she wasn't stupid, and she enjoyed none of this, which meant nothing could be used against her.

"Sallow," Hale said. "Wren Sallow. Forger. We have a description of you from the Marsh Lane forgery, two years old. You've cut your hair since."

"Lots of people cut their hair."

"Yes." Hale almost smiled. "They do."

And then the third Inquisitor, the one by the shelves, said, "Sir. There's a bundle here," and the world tilted.

He'd gone past the chute, past the rubble, deeper along the wall — to the second hiding place, the older one, the cache where the Gardeners kept their emergency stores: coin, blank papers, medicine. And wrapped in sacking on top of it, because Tam had left it there Tuesday when he'd been carrying it up for Theo and gotten distracted, lay a single carved bone charm. A mourning piece. Theo's work, unmistakably — the grain-work fine as eyelashes, the little door motif at its center that Theo carved into everything without seeming to know he did it.

Hale took it in both hands. Turned it in the lantern light. And Wren watched her face do something complicated — recognition, yes, but also something almost like reverence, the look of a person finding a signature they'd been hunting and finding it beautiful.

"Amberdale," Hale said quietly.

"He's a carver," Wren said. "Sells at the Ghost Market. Half the quarter wears his bits of bone."

"Half the quarter does." Hale slid the charm into an evidence wallet with a gentleness that frightened Wren more than violence would have. Then she looked at Tam again, really looked, and Wren saw the arithmetic finish behind her eyes: boy, wrong place, wrong time, no papers on him, no story that survived contact with questions.

"Take him," Hale said.

"No—" Tam's voice cracked. "I didn't do anything, I was getting—"

"Tam." Wren stepped between them, and two Inquisitors stepped between her and him, and it happened fast and slow at once, the way disasters do. "Tam, look at me. Say nothing. Not one word. Do you hear me? Not one word, and they can't make anything stick. We'll have you out by—"

"Wren." Hale's voice, quiet, almost kind. "Every minute you argue is a minute I spend learning his name. Move."

They took him up the stairs. Tam didn't fight — he did what she'd told him, went silent and limp, eyes finding hers over a grey shoulder, and the last thing she saw was his mouth shaping the words *not one word* back at her, obedient even now, even terrified, and somehow that was the worst of it.

The door closed. Boots faded up Cooper's Lane. The bells resumed their honest pattern, all's well, all's well, lying.

* * *

They gave it a quarter hour, then Marta's whistle sounded soft from the cistern gate, and Wren led them out through the water — Fen and the twins wading the flooded stretch with lanterns hooded, Theo carrying nothing, arms empty, having surrendered his ghost to a wall for nothing.

They regrouped in the tannery loft: eleven Gardeners becoming nine, sitting among hides and lime vats, steaming faintly in the cold. Someone poured tea nobody drank. Fen laid out what they'd lost — the working ledger, thank the drowned gods, had stayed against Wren's ribs the whole time and now lay in her lap, damp with her own sweat — and what they'd kept: the bone, the sanctuary itself, most of the people.

"A clean escape," Fen said finally. "All things considered."

"All things considered," Marta echoed, without conviction.

Theo hadn't spoken since the wall. He sat apart, elbows on knees, staring at his own hands as though they belonged to the dead he heard. When Wren sat beside him he said, quietly, "She took the charm because of me. Tam was holding it because of me. Everything in that room tonight was there because a book missold itself to a bone-carver five weeks ago."

"That's one way to count it."

"It's the accurate way."

"The accurate way," Wren said, "is that a boy who couldn't follow rule two got careless, and an Inquisitor who's better at her job than we'd like got lucky, and neither of those things is your fault, they're just the weather. We work in weather, Theo. We don't get to choose it."

He looked at her then, and there was something in his face she recognized from mirrors — the specific guilt of people whose gifts cost other people things. She'd worn it for six years, ever since her brother's name had gone onto a Crown warrant partly because of testimony she'd failed to stop. She knew its weight to the ounce.

"They'll question him," Theo said. "Tonight. They'll sit him under a candle and ask him questions until—"

"He won't talk. I told him not to."

"You told a terrified child not to be terrified."

"Yes," Wren said. "That's the job."

But her hands weren't steady, and Theo saw, and she hated that he saw, and she got up to pour tea she wouldn't drink because standing still was unbearable.

* * *

It was near three in the morning when the argument started, because grief curdles into blame if you leave it sitting, and the Gardeners were human before they were anything else.

"We should have cleared the cache weeks ago," one of the twins said. "We keep saying we'll rotate the stores and we never do."

"We should have moved the boy out after the first warning," the other said. "Wren flagged him herself. Twice in one week."

"I flagged him," Wren said, "and I let him stay, because throwing him out meant throwing him to the streets alone, and that's not what we are."

"Isn't it?" Fen, quietly, not cruel. "What are we, Wren? Because tonight we learned we're a group that hides dragon bones in walls while our children answer for us. Hale didn't come for the bone. Look at what she took. Papers' worth of nothing, and a boy. She came to take a *person*, because a person talks, and a talking person gives her the district."

Silence. The lime vats breathed their sour breath. Somewhere below, the city slept its lied-to sleep.

"She'll work him gently," Marta said. "That's worse. That one doesn't break people, she *persuades* them. By morning he'll want to help her."

"Then we get him back before morning," Wren said.

Fen laughed, short and bitter. "From the Inquisition's holding cells? Under Candle House? With the whole order alerted? With what?"

"With what we have." She set the rescued ledger on the table between them, and the room went quiet, because everyone understood what it was: names, routes, safehouses, the whole architecture of nine years of careful illegality. "This is what she wants. This is what she'll trade for. And if we're wrong — if she won't trade, if she's already past wanting — then we're finished anyway, and at least we'll be finished trying."

"That's not a plan, that's a funeral with extra steps."

"It's a start." She looked around the loft, at the faces she'd sworn to keep whole, at the empty space where Tam should have been sitting, complaining about the tea. "First light, I go to the Ghost Market. There's a smuggler there who deals in dead men's memories, and if anyone knows how Candle House thinks from the inside, it's the dead who used to work there."

Theo raised his head. "The Ghost Market," he said slowly. "The dead there would know the cells. The guards' habits. Which locks stick." He was waking up, she could see it, the gift turning from wound to instrument. "Wren — the dead remember everything about that building. Everything."

"Then tomorrow we ask them," she said.

But sleep didn't come, and near dawn she climbed to the loft window and looked out across the rooftops toward Candle House, its tower black against the graying sky, one window lit on the fourth floor. Interrogation floor. She let herself imagine it — Tam in a chair, small in a big room, candlelight on his face, Hale across from him with that terrible patience, asking gentle questions. *Your name. Your age. Who taught you the chalk marks.*

*Not one word,* Wren whispered to a boy who couldn't hear her, in a city built on things no one was supposed to hear.

Behind her, Theo spoke from the dark of the loft, and his voice had gone strange and distant, the voice he used when the dead were close:

"Wren. The ghosts are saying something about Candle House. They keep saying the same thing." A pause. "They say: *the girl shouldn't have touched the bone.*"

Cold walked up her spine. "What girl?"

"The Inquisitor," Theo said. "Hale. She's holding Ember's charm right now — and the dead say she can *hear it.*"
