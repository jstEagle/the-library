## 13. Interrogation by Candlelight

The holding cells beneath the Inquisitors' Hall had no windows, which Hale had always considered a design flaw rather than a feature. Darkness made prisoners imaginative. They invented rescues, invented accomplices, invented gods who might take an interest. A candle, by contrast, told them the truth: that the room was small, the door was thick, and no one was coming.

She carried two candles herself tonight, and a ledger under her arm.

The boy — he was barely more than that, seventeen at most — sat on the cell's stone bench with his hands flat on his knees, watching her come down the corridor as if she were an appointment he'd been expecting. He'd been in the cistern raid three days ago. Three days of Hale's best people working on him, and according to the reports they'd gotten nothing. Not a name, not a district, not a syllable. One of her interrogators had requested reassignment afterward, citing fatigue. Hale had read between those lines.

She unlocked the cell herself and stepped inside, setting one candle on the floor between them and taking the stool opposite, close enough that their knees nearly touched. The boy watched all of this without moving.

"Tam Corren," she said. "Sixteen years old. Apprentice cooper until eight months ago, then no recorded trade. No family on the rolls except a grandmother in the Saltrow tenements, who was informed you'd been conscripted into harbor labor." She let that sit. "That was kind of them. The Gardeners, I mean. Kind lie."

Tam said nothing. His eyes were brown and steady and very young, and there was something in them Hale kept trying to dismiss and couldn't. It wasn't defiance. Defiance she knew how to break; it was almost a relief to break it. This was something else. He looked at her the way an apprentice looks at a master craftsman doing shoddy work — with a kind of patient, pitying attention.

"You're not afraid," she said. "Everyone's afraid. Even the ones who don't show it are afraid somewhere underneath. But you're sitting there like I'm the one in trouble." She smiled, thin. "Are you? Am I?"

Nothing. His breathing didn't even change. She'd seen trained agents crack faster than this, men twice his age with codes drilled into them. But Tam Corren wasn't performing silence. There was no performance in him at all. He simply sat inside it like a room.

Hale opened the ledger across her knee.

"Here's what I know about the Gardeners," she said, conversational now, the way she talked to witnesses she liked. "I know they fixed the water pumps in Dyer's Row for free, which is charity. I know they burned the warehouse ledgers of a grain factor who was adulterating flour during the lean winter — that's two counts of arson, but also, I'll note, the man was poisoning children, so I understand the appeal. I know they pushed a customs magistrate off the Tallow Bridge three years ago after he took bribes to look away from a slaver's manifest. And I know" — she turned a page — "that every time my patrols have closed within a street of your root-cellar sanctuary, someone has moved the operation forty-eight hours ahead of us. Every time. Which means either you have a leak in my Hall, or your chalk marks do something my cartographers haven't figured out yet."

Still nothing. But something flickered at the word *chalk*. Flickered and was gone, fast, and Hale filed it away with the care of a woman who had built a career on filing things away.

"I grew up in Vellamarch," she said, quieter. "The real one. Before the Crown decided it was a rounding error on a treaty map. My father held a minor judicial post here. You want to hear something funny? Nobody ever tells me what happened to Vellamarch. I ask senior officers and they look at me like I've asked about the weather in a country that never existed. Because officially—" she tapped the ledger, "—it doesn't. We're stationed in a province called Meridian Reach, Third District. That's what the papers say. And yet everyone here says Vellamarch. Everyone remembers a border crossing that isn't on any road. My mother used to sing a lullaby about dragon-fire and swore she learned it from her grandmother, and when I asked her where the dragons went, she looked frightened. Not sad. *Frightened.*"

Tam's gaze shifted, just slightly. Interest. Real interest.

"So here is my offer," Hale said. "Not amnesty. I can't give that, and we'd both know I was lying. But information. You tell me how the Gardeners' network runs — the sigils, the safehouses, the chain of command — and I will tell you what I find in the sealed archives about what was done to this country. All of it. Whatever it is. I've spent six years pulling threads, Corren, and every thread ends in a lock I don't have the rank to open. You people clearly know things. I want to trade."

For the first time, the boy moved. He leaned forward slightly, elbows on knees, and studied her face with open, unhurried curiosity, as though she were a puzzle box he was considering whether to bother opening. Then, softly, he spoke — the first words anyone had gotten out of him since the cistern.

"You'd give us up anyway," he said. His voice was hoarse from disuse but level. "After. You'd tell yourself the trade was worth it. You already have. I can see it behind your eyes. You're a good woman standing in a bad building, and the building always wins."

Then he sat back, folded his hands, and returned to being a room.

* * *

Hale walked the corridor twice before she trusted herself to go back in.

It wasn't the refusal that stung. Refusal was weather; she'd stood in worse. It was the accuracy. *A good woman standing in a bad building.* She had said versions of that sentence to herself in the dark, in exactly those words, more than once over the past year, and hearing them in a sixteen-year-old's mouth felt less like interrogation than like being read aloud from her own private papers.

Her zeal, her superiors called it. Deputy Director Ossen used the word approvingly, which should have been a warning in itself. *Hale gets results because Hale believes.* And she did believe — or she had believed in something adjacent to belief, something she'd built from her father's memory and her mother's frightened face and the humiliation of her family name, dragged through the mud fifteen years ago when the Gardeners had exposed her uncle — a magistrate, corrupt to the bone — in broad daylight, nailing his forged ledgers to the courthouse doors while a crowd laughed. Her father had never recovered the family's standing. Her father had never really recovered at all. She had joined the Inquisitors to prove that law mattered more than mockery, that justice belonged in institutions and not in chalk marks and midnight raids by people who answered to no one.

And now she was sitting on a stool by candlelight getting morally audited by a child.

She went back in.

Tam hadn't changed position. She retrieved her stool, sat, and did something she had planned not to do: she set the second candle down beside the first and simply stayed quiet. Let the silence work on both of them. Interrogation was a craft, and the amateurs always filled silence with threats. Threats cost nothing to make and everything to keep, and prisoners could smell the difference. Silence said: *I have time. I have patience. I am not going anywhere.*

They sat like that for a long while. The candles burned. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the Hall floor, muffled and distant.

It was Tam who broke first, and not with words. He lifted one hand — slow, deliberate, keeping it well away from anything that could be construed as reaching — and pointed at her belt.

Hale looked down. Hanging beside her seal-ring case was a small object she'd forgotten she wore: a bone charm, plain and unadorned, carved with a simple spiral. Her grandmother's. Or the woman her family had always called her grandmother, though the records of that generation were smoke. Hale had worn it since she was nine years old and had stopped thinking of it as anything but part of her uniform.

"What about it?" she said.

Tam touched his own throat. Beneath his collar — she saw it now, and cursed herself for missing it three days running — hung a cord. He drew it out carefully and let the pendant swing free into the candlelight.

It was a bone charm. Carved bone, pale as milk, worked into the shape of a moth with its wings half-folded. The craftsmanship stopped her breath for a moment. Whoever had cut it had understood that bone has a grain the way wood does, had followed it instead of fighting it, had turned the natural striations into the veins of the wings. It was beautiful. It was the kind of thing that got made by someone who had done it ten thousand times, whose hands knew the material the way a singer knows a song.

And it was not the first such charm she had seen.

Hale went very still. Six months ago, a raid on a fortune-teller in the Lantern Quarter had turned up a bin of contraband charms, most of them crude mass-work — but three of them had been different. Finer. A sparrow, a knotwork ring, a small sleeping fox. She had catalogued them personally because fine work meant a master craftsman, and master craftsmen left trails. The file still sat in her office, tagged and half-forgotten among forty others.

She thought of the mourning charm described in the widow Maren Ostrey's arrest report. Bone, finely worked, spiral motif. The widow had refused to say who sold it to her. The widow was currently sitting in a cell two floors above, awaiting transport.

Slowly, deliberately, Hale reached to her own belt and unhooked her grandmother's charm. She held it up beside Tam's moth, in the light, comparing.

Different subjects. Different scale. But the same hand. She was certain of it — certain the way you're certain about handwriting, that deep pattern-recognition that lives below argument. The same economy of line. The same way the carver let the material's flaws become features instead of hiding them. The same slight asymmetry in the finishing strokes, left-handed pressure, consistent across every piece.

Three confiscated charms. One widow's charm. And a moth hanging around the neck of a prisoner who wouldn't speak, given to him — she would bet her commission — by the same person, because that was what the Gardeners apparently did. They marked their own. Or they blessed them. Or they mourned them in advance.

"Well," Hale said quietly. "There you are."

Tam's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a tightening, a flicker of what might have been regret, or warning. He hadn't meant to reveal it. She understood that immediately. He'd shown her the moth for some other reason — kinship, maybe, or a message, *we are not so different* — and only realized too late what a craftsman's signature was worth to a hunter.

"It's all right," she heard herself say, and was surprised to mean it. "I won't take it from you."

His brow creased. Suspicion, of course. But underneath the suspicion, something else — the same patient, pitying attention as before, but warmer now, complicated now.

Hale stood, hooked her grandmother's charm back onto her belt, and collected her ledger. At the door she paused with her hand on the frame.

"The widow Ostrey," she said, not turning around. "Two floors up. Transport orders arrive tomorrow morning. You should know that I'm the one who signed them, and you should know that I'll be reviewing them again tonight." She looked back at him over her shoulder. "You said the building always wins. Maybe. But buildings are made of people, Corren, and people are slower than buildings to fall."

She blew out one of the candles and left him the other, which was against regulation, and which she found she did not care about.

* * *

Her office at midnight smelled of cold tallow and old paper. Hale lit her desk lamp, pulled the Lantern Quarter file from the stack, and laid it open beside a fresh sheet.

She wrote the date. Then, beneath it, working from memory first and then checking against the evidence tags: *sparrow, knotwork ring, sleeping fox — Lantern Quarter seizure, sixthmonth. Moth — prisoner Corren, current. Mourning charm, spiral — widow Ostrey, current. Unidentified carved token — recovered from cistern raid site, thirdmonth.*

Five pieces. Same hand. She spread her notes around them like a snare drawn shut.

The carver worked in bone exclusively. The carver was left-handed. The carver's motifs ran to animals and spirals — folk patterns, older than the Crown, the sort of thing grandmothers wore and official doctrine called superstition. The carver had supplied at least one charm to a Gardeners' operative, possibly several. And the carver was good enough that a widow in the Drowned Quarter had bought one and treasured it enough to be arrested for it rather than give up its maker.

Hale dipped her pen and wrote, in her precise, upright script:

*Bone-carver. Likely resident, Drowned Quarter or near. Likely known to Gardener network. Possibly the source of the charms seized in Lantern Quarter. FIND HIM.*

She sat back and looked at the words, and the lamplight made the ink shine wetly, and for a moment she felt the old clean pull of the hunt — the narrowing world, the converging lines, the satisfaction of a pattern resolving. Then Tam Corren's voice came back to her, hoarse and level: *you'd tell yourself the trade was worth it.*

She thought about the widow upstairs. Maren Ostrey, fifty-eight years old, whose crime was grieving with something beautiful in her hand. Whose transport orders Hale had signed with a clear conscience because the paperwork was correct, because the statute was clear, because law was the wall that stood between order and the mob — even when the mob had better manners than the magistrates.

Hale pulled the transport orders toward her and read them again. Correct paperwork. Clear statute. A grandmother, in a cell, for a carved bone.

*A good woman standing in a bad building.*

She did not tear the orders up. She was not ready to be the kind of person who tore up orders, and she suspected that if she ever became that person, everything she understood about herself would have to be rebuilt from the foundation stones up. Instead she took out her pen and amended them — delayed the escort by one day, citing staffing shortfalls, a delay so ordinary no one would question it. A day. It was nearly nothing. It was the largest thing she had ever stolen from the Crown.

Then she opened the sealed archives request form — the one requiring a Deputy Director's countersignature, the one covering the treaty-era records concerning a country that officially did not exist — and stared at Ossen's name printed neatly in the approval line.

Outside, far off toward the river, the city lamps flickered. Just once. Just for a breath, as if the whole of Vellamarch had blinked.

Hale looked up from her desk, frowning, and waited for the light to steady. It did. She told herself it was the wind.

It was not the wind.
