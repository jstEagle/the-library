## 14. The Ghost Market

The smuggler's name was Vetch, and Wren said it the way you'd say the word *knife* — carefully, with respect for what it could do to you.

"He trades in memories," she said as we crossed the flooded district after midnight, our boots finding the raised stones by feel. "Not stories about the dead. Memories *of* the dead. What a drowning man saw in his last minute. What a grandmother whispered on her deathbed. The dead leave residue, and Vetch bottles it."

"Is that possible?" I asked, though I already knew the answer was yes, because everything I'd spent five years hiding had just become the least strange thing in my life.

"You bottled yourself for thirty years," Wren said. "Why not everyone else?"

She had a point. We walked on through streets where the tide breathed in and out of cellar doors, and I thought about how many kinds of silence there were. The silence of people who couldn't hear me. The silence of ghosts with nothing left to say. And now this third kind — the silence of the dead being bought and sold, their last moments decanted into vials like lamp oil.

I should have felt horrified. I did feel horrified. But underneath the horror, shamefully, there was something else: the pull of a man who has been alone with his gift his whole life, hearing that somewhere, someone else could hear them too.

Even if he sold them.

* * *

The market didn't exist until you were inside it.

That was the first thing I understood. Wren led us down a stair into a cistern that stank of brine and old stone, past a door with no handle, and then she pressed her palm flat against the wet wall and spoke three words I didn't catch — and the wall wasn't a wall anymore.

Beyond it was a cavern lit entirely by candles set in niches carved from bone. Hundreds of them. Thousands. And between the candles, stalls.

Stalls made of driftwood and ship-ribbing, hung with jars, vials, bundles of hair tied with red thread, teeth strung on wire, folded papers stained brown with age. And vendors — some living, hunched and quick-eyed, and some not living at all. They stood at their stalls the way any merchant stands, except they were gray at the edges, translucent at the wrists, and when they moved you could see the candle flames right through their hands.

"The dead run half the stalls themselves," Wren murmured. "Vetch's idea. Who better to sell a memory than the one it belongs to? Consent keeps the Crown's Inquisitors out, technically. There are laws about desecration, and technically, no one here is desecrating anyone."

"Technically."

"Vellamarch runs on technically." She pulled her hood lower. "Stay close. Don't stare at anything. Especially don't stare at anyone who isn't breathing."

I did my best. It was like walking through a cathedral that had been converted into a fish market. A ghost woman sold locks of hair that still held her love for whoever had worn them. A living man with scales tattooed up both arms traded in "final sights" — the last thing the drowned saw, decanted into glass eyes you could hold to your own socket. A child's ghost — or the shape of a child; I refused to look closely — turned a crank on a little music box, over and over, and each turn played back ten seconds of someone's mother singing.

And everywhere, threaded under the haggling and the dripping water and the crackle of bone-candles, I heard them. Not speaking to me. Just — present. The way a city is present. A hum of the dead so dense it wasn't voices anymore, just weather.

Except that as we went deeper, the weather changed.

It started with one of them. A stall-keeper, gray to the elbows, arranging vials with hands that passed through the wood. Her head came up as we passed, the way a dog's does, and she looked at me — not at my face, at my chest, my throat, somewhere behind my eyes — and she said, quietly, in a voice like ash settling:

"*Ember.*"

Wren's hand found my arm. "What did she say?"

"I don't—" But I did. Because then another ghost turned. And another. Down the whole long row of stalls, the dead stopped working, stopped selling, stopped turning their cranks and threading their wires, and every gray face swung toward me like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"*Ember-of-Ashes,*" someone said further back, deeper, older. "*The fire is walking.*"

"I'm not—" I said aloud, stupidly, to a crowd of the dead. "My name is Theo Amberdale. I'm a bone-carver. From Copperlane."

A tall ghost detached himself from a stall selling funeral bread and drifted close enough that I could see the candlelight through his cheeks. He studied me with the particular patience of the dead, who have nothing but time.

"You carry her voice," he said. "In your bones. She rides in you like a passenger in a coach, and every soul in this room can hear the wheels."

Beside me, Wren had gone very still. "Theo," she breathed. "They can hear *her*? Through *you*?"

"I don't know what they hear."

"They hear Ember-of-Ashes," the tall ghost said, and around him, the murmur ran through the market like wind through wheat: *the last dragon, the last dragon walks in flesh, the door-walker, the door-walker comes.*

The word *door* landed in my stomach like a dropped stone.

"We're looking for Vetch," Wren said quickly, loudly, cutting across the murmur with the flat authority of a Gardener used to being obeyed. "We have coin and we have discretion. Which way?"

The tall ghost smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was the smile of someone who knows that discretion died the moment I walked in. "Third gallery. Follow the smell of ink. He'll want to see you, carver. He'll want very much to see you."

* * *

Vetch turned out to be alive, small, and neat as a ledger.

He sat behind a desk built from a ship's stern — the wood curved up around him like the arms of a throne — in a gallery hung with hundreds of labeled jars. He wore a green coat buttoned to the throat and spectacles with four lenses, two per eye, and he looked at us over them the way an apothecary looks at a specimen.

"Theo Amberdale," he said. "Bone-carver, Copperlane, unlicensed charms, currently wanted by the Inquisition. And the Gardeners' own Wren Sallow, come down from the light to trade in things she pretends not to condone." He folded his hands. "You've caused quite a stir tonight. Half my stockholders have abandoned their stalls to follow you around. Do you know how difficult it is to sell grief when all the grieving dead have wandered off to look at a boy?"

"We want information," Wren said. "About the prophecy of the door. About what 'the door' means. We heard you might have memories of the scholars who wrote it down."

"Memories of dead scholars." Vetch nodded slowly. "I do. I have the dying thoughts of Magister Corvel, who copied the prophecy in his final year, and the last hour of Abbess Rill, who taught it before the outlawing, and — if you have truly deep pockets — a fragment of the memory of the man who *heard* it first, a shepherd called Odo, who claimed a voice in the mountain told him the whole of it."

"How much?" I asked.

"For Odo?" Vetch named a figure that made Wren inhale. Then he looked at me again, over the four lenses, and his fingers drummed once on the ship-wood. "But perhaps we needn't speak of money at all. Perhaps we could trade."

"We don't trade people," Wren said sharply.

"No one said anything about trading people." Vetch rose, unhurried, and lifted a jar from the shelf behind him — small, plain, unlabeled. Inside, instead of liquid or smoke, there was simply a darkness, dense as packed soil. "I want a sample. One memory, freely given, of the dragon's voice as it sounds *through you*. You've been carrying the last dragon around Vellamarch in your bones, carver. Do you know what collectors would pay for even a second of her? Do you know what *I* would learn? The dead in this market have been whispering her name since you walked in. She was theirs too, once. Everyone's, once."

The horror I'd been holding off finally arrived, cold and complete. This was what my gift would be, if the world got hold of it. Not a curse to hide, but a vein to mine. Every voice I'd ever heard, sliced into samples and shelved.

"She's not merchandise," I said.

"Everything is merchandise," Vetch said, without malice, the way you'd say *water is wet*. "The only question is whether the owner consents. That's my entire philosophy, carver. That's why the dead work my stalls willingly. So — consent. Will you give me one memory of her voice? Freely? In exchange for Odo the shepherd?"

"And if he says no?" Wren asked.

"Then I sell you Corvel and Abbess Rill at full price, and you'll leave knowing less, and the whispering will continue, and within a month every smuggler, spy, and Inquisitor in Vellamarch will know exactly where the door-walker buys his secrets." Vetch smiled thinly. "I am not threatening you. I am describing commerce."

I looked at the jar of darkness. I thought of Ember-of-Ashes curled in her root-cellar dark, weary beyond centuries, guarding a country that had betrayed her, waiting for a boy with a book and a name. What would she say? Probably something dry and terrible. Probably she'd tell me to charge triple.

But it wouldn't be hers to give. That was the thing. Whatever the dead in this market sold, they sold their own deaths, their own last sights and songs. Ember's voice passing through me was hers, and she hadn't consented to anything but trusting me.

"No," I said.

Wren glanced at me. Vetch tilted his head, genuinely curious. "No?"

"Not her voice. But I'll give you something else freely, and you'll take it because it's worth more." My heart was hammering and I had no idea where the words came from, except that they came from the same place the carving came from, the place where my hands knew things my mind didn't. "A memory of my mother. Her voice, saying my name. Whole and uncut. Given by me, who owns it, about myself. Collectors pay more for the living's memories of the dead — you know that — because grief makes them vivid."

Silence. Somewhere in the galleries, the murmur of the dead had gone quiet, listening.

Vetch removed his spectacles and polished them on his green sleeve, which I understood, suddenly, was what he did when he was recalculating. "You'd give that away. Your mother's voice."

"To buy a shepherd's dying thought," I said. "Yes."

"That's either very foolish," Vetch said, replacing the spectacles, "or very much like her. The dragon, I mean. She also paid with what she loved." He gestured, and a gray-handed assistant — a ghost with a clerk's collar — drifted forward bearing a second jar, empty, its stopper of wax already scored for opening. "Sit down, carver. This will feel like a cold key turning behind your eyes. Don't fight it."

* * *

It felt exactly like that.

I sat in the curve of the ship-wood throne and Vetch held the empty jar to my temple and murmured a word, and something drew out of me — gently, horribly, like a splinter leaving flesh — and I heard, one last time, in the space where it had always lived:

*Theo. My Theo. Whatever they tell you, whatever you hear — you were never the wrong thing. You were only early.*

Then it was gone. Not faded. Gone. A hole shaped like my mother's voice, sitting where my mother's voice had been for twenty-five years.

I hadn't realized, giving it, that I'd be able to feel the absence. I sat there blinking while the ghost-clerk stoppered the jar and labeled it in a fine antique hand, and Wren gripped my shoulder and said my name twice before I answered her.

"It's done," I managed. "Odo. Now."

Vetch handed the second jar to Wren — the payment for Odo went to her purse, apparently; Gardeners kept accounts — and then, instead of the promised goods, he leaned back and laced his fingers over his waistcoat.

"One more piece of trade," he said. "Gratis. Because you interest me, and because I collect interesting things the way other men collect stamps." His four lenses caught the candlelight. "You asked what the door means. Here is what I know from the residue of dead men. The prophecy was spoken by a voice in the mountain, heard by Odo the shepherd. Odo believed it was the dragon. But Odo was wrong — or only half right. The residue doesn't lie, carver, and in Odo's dying memory, there is a detail he never understood, and neither did the scholars who wrote it down, because they only ever read the words forward."

"What detail?" Wren asked.

"The voice said: *when the last fire gutters, a door will open.* And every scholar assumed the door opened onto escape. Onto outside. Onto a country that exists." Vetch smiled, and this time it reached his eyes, and I wished it hadn't. "But Odo heard it in the original tongue, and in the original tongue, *door* and *hearth* are the same word. Ask yourselves, children, before you spend my shepherd's memory: what if the door doesn't lead out of the country? What if it leads the country back in?"

The candle-flames in their bone niches all bent sideways at once, though there was no draft.

And in the sudden hush, every ghost in the Ghost Market — hundreds of them, filling the galleries, hanging from the rigging of the ceiling, crowding the stairs — whispered the same word together, soft as snowfall:

"*Hearth.*"

Then, closer, at my ear, in a voice I knew better than my own heartbeat — a voice that should have been sealed in a jar on Vetch's shelf, and was, and yet was here, beside me, warm as bread:

"*Theo. My Theo. Some things you cannot sell, because they refuse to be owned.*"

The jar on the clerk's tray rattled once, like a struck bell, and went silent.

Wren was looking at me with wide eyes. "Theo. Your face. What—"

"Walk," I said, standing, my legs unsteady under me. "We need to walk. Now."

Because I understood two things at once, and both of them were shaking me. First: the door was the hearth. The First Hearth, where the fire had been lit. Everything pointed backward, inward — not out of Vellamarch, but *into* it.

And second: my mother's memory had just refused to stay sold. Which meant somewhere in the dark of this market, in defiance of commerce, in defiance of death itself, my mother was still choosing to stay near me.

And she had never once, in all her years of silence, sounded so afraid.

"*Run, my Theo,*" she whispered, fading. "*He collects more than memories. Run.*"
