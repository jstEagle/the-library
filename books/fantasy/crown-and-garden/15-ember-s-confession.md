## 15. Ember's Confession

The Ghost Market had left something on Theo that he couldn't scrub off.

Not dirt — the smugglers' tunnels had been filthy enough, but filth washed away with lye and cold water in Wren's safehouse basin while she stood guard outside the door. This was different. It was the feeling of having been *handled*. The dead in the market had turned toward him when Ember's name passed his lips — hundreds of them, faces slack with longing, pressing against the veil like children at a shop window — and one of them had reached through far enough to touch his wrist before the smuggler's iron bell scattered them.

He could still feel where it had touched him. A cold ring around the bone of his wrist, like a shackle fitted by a careful hand.

"You're quiet," Wren said, when he came down to the root-cellar. She was mending a lantern chain by candlelight, pliers working the small links with a surgeon's patience. "Quiet even for you."

"The dead liked my voice tonight." He sat on the root shelf across from her, elbows on knees. "That's not a sentence I ever thought I'd be proud of."

"Did you learn anything worth the risk?"

He turned his wrist over. The cold ring was still there. "I learned that the market-dead know Ember-of-Ashes. By voice, not by name. They heard her when she was alive." He looked up. "Wren, they *heard* her. Which means she spoke to people once. Which means she wasn't always just a ghost in a bone."

Wren set down the pliers. In the candlelight her face did the thing it always did when she was deciding how much truth to spend: a small tightening around the mouth, like a purse string drawn. "Then go ask her yourself. She's been waiting for you since the market, if the bone-heat is any sign."

It was. The dragon-bone lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floor stones where the Gardeners kept their most dangerous things — forged Crown seals, unregistered medicines, the ledger of every debt Vellamarch's magistrates had quietly refused to collect — and Theo could feel its warmth through three inches of earth and oak, like a hearth behind a wall.

* * *

He uncovered it alone. Some conversations you don't have with an audience.

The bone was longer than his forearm, curved like a rib, honey-dark and shot through with veins of gold that only showed when the candle moved. When his fingers closed around it, the cellar fell away.

Not literally. He'd learned the difference by now. The walls stayed; the candles stayed; but everything became *background*, the way a room becomes background when someone you love walks into it.

*You went among the memory-thieves,* said Ember-of-Ashes. Her voice was not a sound so much as a warmth behind his sternum, a banked-fire voice, patient and old and very tired. *You smell of them.*

"They knew your voice."

*I sang over this city when it was young and its borders were bright.* A pause, long enough that Theo thought the contact had slipped. *They were right to remember the singing. They are wrong about everything else.*

"Tell me what they're wrong about," Theo said, "and tell me why your name is in a prophecy book with today's date beside it, and tell me what a door is. In that order or any order. But tell me."

The warmth gathered. He had the sense of an enormous head turning, of an eye older than the country opening in the dark.

*Sit down, maker. This will take the shape of a confession, and confessions are best heard sitting.*

Theo sat on the cold cellar floor, the rib-bone across his knees like a sleeping animal.

*You believe the prophecy chose you,* Ember said. *You believe some great loom wove your name into its pattern a hundred years ago, and that you have been walking a road it laid. I have felt you thinking this. It is written all over you, the way guilt is written on a man who has stolen something he cannot return.*

"It isn't?"

*No.* And then, quieter, with something that might have been shame in a creature that had never needed the word before: *I wrote it.*

The cellar was very still. Somewhere above, water ran through old pipes, the city's thin blood.

"I don't understand."

*A century ago, this country made a bargain with me, and I with it. My fire held the borders — not stone walls, maker, fire. A border of flame invisible to every eye but mine, burning in the space between what is and what is permitted to be. While it burned, Vellamarch existed. Maps recorded it. Treaties named it. Ships paid harbor-tithe at Greywater Sound. And in exchange—*

She stopped. Theo waited. The bone pulsed faintly under his hands, like a heartbeat deciding whether to continue.

*In exchange, they gave me their dead.* The words came out flat now, scraped clean of music. *Every citizen of Vellamarch, at death, was carried to me. Not buried. Carried. Their bodies fed my fire, and their memories — oh, their memories were sweeter than any fuel. I kept them. All of them. Every harvest song and every quarrel and every last word spoken at every deathbed for two hundred years. That was the bargain: my fire for their remembrance. The country would live forever because its dead would live in me, and I would live forever because its dead would feed me.*

"That's—" Theo started, and didn't have the word.

*Yes,* Ember said. *Whatever you were about to call it, yes.*

"What happened?"

*What always happens. A king died who had never seen me except from a distance, through smoked glass, because his grandfather decided dragons frightened the trade envoys. His ministers decided the bargain was superstition. They built a new kind of border instead — paper borders, treaty lines, garrisons. They told me my services were no longer required. They stopped sending me their dead and began burying them in ground like ordinary animals, and they drew up documents saying Vellamarch would be dissolved into the neighboring crowns within a generation, its name struck from every map, its existence a clerical error to be corrected by time.*

The fire-voice trembled. Not with weakness. With something much more dangerous.

*And I let it happen. Do you understand me, Theo Amberdale? I could have burned their treaties in their archives. I could have lit my borders so bright that every cartographer in the world would have gone blind rather than erase us. Instead I sulked. Like a dog put out of doors, I took my fire and I let it gutter, year by year, decade by decade, and I watched the maps forget us and I thought: good. Let them forget. Let them learn what a country is worth when its heart stops keeping watch.*

Theo's hands had gone cold on the warm bone. "But the borders held. Something's been holding them. People live here. I grew up here."

*Embers hold embers. I am not dead, maker, only dying very slowly, and my dying has a shape. For a hundred years the fire has burned low but not out, because a fire remembers what it is even when its keeper has forgotten. Vellamarch exists in the way a coal exists: truly, but barely, and only until someone breathes wrong.*

"And the prophecy book."

*Ah.* The warmth withdrew a little, the way a person steps back before admitting the worst. *Forty years ago, a scholar came to me. A woman with ink-stained fingers and a heretic's curiosity, asking about the old bargains. No one had asked me about the bargains in sixty years. I talked to her. Too much. And in my weariness — in the particular weariness of guarding something that does not want to be guarded — I told her what I wished would happen. What I dreamed, at the bottom of my slow dying, might yet redeem all this.*

"She wrote it down as prophecy."

*She wrote down my wish and called it prophecy, and other scholars copied it, and booksellers sold it, and somewhere in the copying it hardened from a wish into a fate. And I let that happen too. Because it was easier to believe the future was already written than to admit I had failed to write it myself.*

Theo stood up. He hadn't decided to stand; his body simply refused to keep sitting under what was being said. The rib-bone slid to the floor, and even separated from his hands it glowed faintly in the dark, patient, unrepentant.

"So there's no door."

*There is a door.*

"There's no *prophecy*. There's no weaving, no pattern, no reason my name is in that book except that a tired ghost complained to a scholar forty years ago and got poetic about it." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it. "Do you know what I've been through in the past month? Inquisitors burned my shop. I've hidden in chalk-marked cellars. I dug a drowned boy's killer out of the dark because the dead asked and I can't refuse them. I've been hunted across a city that officially doesn't exist for a destiny that turns out to be — what? Your *dying wish*? Your complaint filed with the universe?"

*Yes,* Ember said simply.

"I heard the dead my whole life! Every night, every hour, voices in the walls, in the graveyards, in the river — I thought I was cursed, then I thought I was chosen, and both of those were at least *bearable*, because a curse has an author and a calling has a purpose. But this?" He was shouting now, at a bone, in a cellar, and part of him watched himself do it with distant disgust. "This means I'm conscripted. This means a dragon couldn't be bothered to save her own country so she handed the bill to a bone-carver a century later. You didn't write a prophecy. You wrote a *debt collection notice*."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on him like deep water.

*You are angry,* Ember said at last.

"You think?"

*I am glad of it. Anger is fuel, and you will need fuel. But before you burn me with it, maker, hear the rest, because the rest matters more than your grievance and mine.*

Theo folded his arms. He did not sit back down. "Make it fast."

*Everything I told the scholar was true. That is the terrible joke of it. The door is real. The maker who carries the dead is real — that was my phrase for what you are, though I confess I imagined a braver sort of person. The choice at the threshold is real. I wished for a redeemer because I was too weary to be one, and the universe, which has a cruel sense of humor, appears to have sent me a man who shouts at bones in cellars.*

"Flattering."

*Here is what I did not tell the scholar, because I did not understand it until these recent weeks, watching you through your gift like a candle seen through a wall.* The bone brightened, gold veins kindling one after another, and the warmth came back into the voice — but changed now, sharper, urgent. *A wish spoken in despair binds no one. But you, Theo. You have been choosing. Since the day you opened that book, you have chosen again and again to act — for the widow, for the drowned boy, for strangers who will never carve your name anywhere. I wrote a wish. You have been writing something else entirely, with worse materials and better hands.*

Theo wanted to throw the accusation back at her. He wanted to say *none of it counts if the foundation is rotten*. But the drowned boy's face rose in his memory — the specific relief in a child's ghost when justice finally arrived, late and illegal and real — and the accusation stuck in his throat.

"Why tell me now?" he said finally. "Why confess? You could have let me keep believing in the loom."

*Because the fire gutters, maker. You saw the First Guttering. You know what comes next. And a man who believes he is fated will walk to his doom obediently — but a man who knows he was merely asked will demand to know exactly what he is agreeing to. You deserve to be asked. Properly asked. Even if the asking is a century late and comes from a coward.*

"A coward," Theo repeated.

*I guarded this country for two hundred years and abandoned it out of spite in its worst hour. Yes. Coward is the gentlest word in the language for what I am. Now you know it, and you may carry it alongside your grievance, and we shall see which of us the weight crushes first.*

He laughed. It came out wrong — half sob, half bark — and he crouched and picked the bone up off the floor because despite everything, despite all of it, his hands didn't want to leave it lying in the dark.

"So the prophecy is a wish. Fine. Then wishes can be refused." He turned the rib over, gold veins swimming. "I could walk away. Find the border where it's thinnest, slip out while it still holds, and let Vellamarch finish dying on its own schedule. You said yourself the door leads out."

*That door exists.*

"Then give me one reason — one honest reason, dragon, no poetry — why I shouldn't take it."

The warmth considered him for a long moment. When Ember answered, the fire-voice was smaller than he had ever heard it, worn down to coals.

*Because you already know what happens to the people left inside a country when its fire goes out. You felt it at the First Guttering — the wards failing, the patrols pouring into streets that had forgotten themselves. Multiply that by every soul in Vellamarch. Multiply it by the dead in my keeping, whom I will fail one final time. Or do not multiply it. Simply ask yourself whether the man who dug a drowned child's justice out of a riverbed can walk out of a dying country and sleep soundly on the other side of its border.*

"That's not a reason. That's a trap."

*It is a mirror,* Ember said. *There is a difference. Though I grant it is difficult to see from inside.*

Theo sat down again, slowly, the bone across his knees. Above him the pipes murmured, the city breathed, the dying fire held a line it no longer believed in.

"Wren's brother was executed on false testimony," he said at last. "Hale's family was humiliated by the Gardeners before she was born. Everyone in this story is carrying a debt from before their time." He turned the bone in his hands, watching the gold move. "Now me."

*Now you,* Ember agreed. *Unless—*

Footsteps on the cellar stairs. Fast ones, two sets, and Wren's voice pitched high and tight in a register Theo had learned to dread:

"Theo — get up here. Now. The widow Marta's been taken to the Assize Hall. They found your charm on her."

The bone flared gold in his hands, and for one instant the dragon's warmth felt less like comfort than like a struck match.

*Well, maker,* Ember said softly. *Your wish has come looking for you.*
