## 16. Prison Break at Low Tide

Wren Sallow had stolen a lot of things in her life — bread, documents, a magistrate's seal once, which was a long story involving a goat — but she had never stolen a person out of the Crown's own gaol. She stood on the rim of the Drowned Quarter's oldest cistern and decided that tonight she would cross that off.

Below her feet, through three broken grates and thirty feet of black water, the Saltcellar Gaol breathed. It was built on pilings over what used to be a reservoir, back when Vellamarch was allowed to have reservoirs, back when it was allowed to be anything. The Crown had walled the old waterways shut and forgotten them, which meant the Gardeners hadn't. Wren had spent four nights learning the cisterns from a forger named Mott who drew maps like prayers: careful, obsessive, annotated with the places he'd nearly died.

"The tide turns at moonset," Mott said now, crouched beside her with a shuttered lantern. "Low water gives us maybe forty minutes before the channels start filling again. After that, anyone still down there is a drowned Gardener."

"Cheerful," said Wren.

"I'm an optimist. I said *forty minutes*."

She looked back at her people. There were seven of them crouched along the cistern rim, dressed in the gray oilskin the dockworkers wore, faces smeared with soot so they'd read as shadows if a patrol lantern swept wrong. Tam — the boy they'd lost in the raid on the root-cellar, sixteen years old, all elbows and stubbornness — was somewhere below them behind iron bars, waiting to be questioned by someone worse than Inquisitor Hale, because Hale had been pulled back to the capital and everyone knew what that meant. The Regent wanted names. The Regent would get them out of a frightened child one way or another.

Unless they got there first.

"One rule," Wren said quietly. "Nobody talks down there. Sound carries on the water like you wouldn't believe. Hand signals only. And if I give the signal to leave—" she caught each of their eyes in turn, "—you take it. Even if it's me we're leaving."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about the Gardeners: they'd all already been left by somebody. They knew how it worked.

Except Theo Amberdale, who was standing at the edge of the group with his hands jammed in his pockets, looking like a man who'd been dragged to a funeral he wasn't sure whose it was.

"You didn't have to come," Wren told him, low.

"Yes, I did," he said. "You know why."

She did know why. She just hated it. Five days ago he'd put his hand flat against the wall of the safehouse and gone white as tallow and said, *Tam's alive. He keeps humming. The dead next to him say he won't stop humming.* And then he'd said something worse, something that had rearranged the whole plan: *The prisoners who died in the Saltcellar — there are dozens of them. They remember every corridor. They can tell us where the patrols walk.*

His curse. His terrible, outlawed, five-years-hidden gift. A compass made of ghosts.

"Stay close to me," Wren said.

"That's my line," Theo said, and almost smiled, and then the moon went behind the cloud cover and it was time.

* * *

They went down through the grates on knotted ropes, into air that tasted like rust and rot and old rain. The water at low tide came up only to their thighs in the main channel, black and warm as bathwater, which was somehow worse than cold. Wren counted strokes with her fingers, kept the column moving along the left-hand wall where Mott's map showed a maintenance ledge wide enough to walk. Overhead, the underside of the city: drain mouths, cellar doors, the occasional grate leaking lamplight like a wound.

Theo moved beside her, and she watched him do the thing he did — head tilted, listening to nothing.

"How many?" she breathed.

He held up fingers. Ten. Then folded two. Then hesitated, and his jaw tightened, and he held up eight again but his face said the number was bigger than his hands.

"They're loud," he whispered when they reached the ledge. "Drowned men are always loud, but these ones— Wren, some of them were Gardener-adjacent. Smugglers who ran supplies. A healer they took twenty years ago. They've been waiting." He swallowed. "One of them says he's been waiting for someone who could hear him since before I was born."

"Tell him thank you," Wren said, "and that the wait's over."

Ahead, the channel forked. Mott raised a fist; the column stopped. Somewhere above and to the right, boots crossed a stone floor — the gaol's lower gallery, guards pacing their rounds. Wren pressed herself flat against the wet wall and felt her heart going like a bird in a sack.

Then Theo touched her arm. Pointed left. Made a shape with his hand — the signal they'd agreed on for *this way, trust me.*

Mott frowned. His map said right.

Theo leaned close to Wren's ear, so close his whisper was barely breath. "The drowned man on the ledge says the right channel floods first when the tide turns. Says the map is twenty years old and the sluice gate rotted open nine years ago. Right channel is a trap now."

Wren looked at Mott. Mott looked at his map. His map was beautiful. His map was wrong.

"Left," she signed.

They went left.

* * *

The cell block sat above the waterline like a barnacle on a ship's hull — a squat iron-studded annex built out over the reservoir on those same old pilings, reachable from the cisterns by a ladder bolted to stone that no inspector had climbed since the country stopped being a country. Wren went up first, slow, testing each rung, and came out through a hatch into a coal store that smelled of tar and mice.

Through the gap under the door: torchlight, and voices. Two guards, playing dice.

Wren counted her breaths. Behind her, one by one, the others rose out of the hatch like things surfacing from a lake. Theo last, dripping, shivering — he'd never learned to hide the shaking, and she found, absurdly, that she loved that about him. Everyone else could pretend to be stone. Theo couldn't. He'd spent five years pretending not to hear the dead and it had cost him more than any disguise ever cost her.

"Two guards," she mouthed. "We go quiet or we go fast."

Theo's hand closed on her sleeve. His eyes had gone distant, that terrible faraway look, and his lips moved without sound, and Wren waited, because she'd learned that waiting on Theo's ghosts was never wasted.

"There were prisoners here," he whispered. "Last winter. A man died in that coal store — fever. He says the guards drink. Both of them, every night this week, the tall one drinks more. He says the tall one sleeps like the dead between second bell and third." A pause. His expression flickered — something painful passing behind it. "He says he knows because he's watched them every night since he died, and nobody ever watches the watchers back until now."

Second bell came, muffled, from the gallery above.

They gave it ten minutes. Then Wren opened the door on a corridor lit by a single guttering lamp, both guards visible through the bars of their watch-nook — one upright and bored, one slumped against his partner's shoulder with a bottle hidden under his cloak, snoring like a sawmill.

Fast, then.

Mott took the awake one. Wren had argued about this — Mott was a forger, not a fighter — but Mott had a way of putting people to sleep with a sap and a sincere apology that was honestly its own kind of art. It went clean. The guard folded. Wren caught him before he hit the floor and they laid him down gently, because the Gardeners didn't kill unless there was no other road, and there was always another road if you were willing to bleed a little for it.

Keys. Torch. Down the corridor.

The cells were iron-barred cubbies stacked two high, and the smell hit Wren like a fist — unwashed bodies, fear-sweat, the sour breath of a place built to break people slowly. Most of the cells were empty. The Crown didn't waste food on prisoners it intended to question.

Tam was in the third cell down.

He was curled on the bare shelf of a bunk with his knees to his chest, and when the torchlight touched him he flinched so hard he nearly fell off, and then his eyes found Wren's face and something in him came apart — silently, because he'd already learned in four days of captivity that crying loudly got you visited by the wrong people. He came apart quietly, the way children learn to grieve in cities like theirs, and Wren got the keys in the lock with hands that were steadier than she deserved.

"Hey, sprout," she whispered. "Told you we'd come."

"You didn't," Tam croaked. "You said *maybe.*"

"I'm revising my policy on honesty." She got the door open. He fell against her and he weighed nothing, nothing, and she thought about her brother — about the night the Crown executed him on a lie, about how she'd arrived eleven minutes too late, about how she had measured every hour of her life since against those eleven minutes. Not this time. This time she was early. Let the ledger show one entry on the right side.

Behind her, Theo had gone rigid.

"Wren." His voice had changed — flattened, urgent. "The dead here are saying the tide turned early."

"What?"

"The sluice. The drowned man was wrong about one thing, or lying — some of the dead lie down here, they're bored, they're angry — the water's coming up the east channel already. We have maybe fifteen minutes, not forty, and the ladder route floods at the base." He pressed both hands over his ears and then forced them down. "There's another way. They're showing me. There's a drainage culvert behind the punishment cell, it runs up under the guard barracks, comes out in a cistern yard past the wall—"

"Is it guarded?"

"It was guarded. The man who knew about it was hanged for trying it." Theo met her eyes. "He wants to help us use it anyway. He says—" Theo's throat worked. "He says nobody's ever finished his escape for him."

Wren looked at Tam, swaying on his feet, at her six soaked Gardeners, at the dark corridor and the rising water she could suddenly hear — a soft, greedy sound under everything, like the whole reservoir turning over in its sleep.

"Fifteen minutes," she said. "Show me."

* * *

The culvert was exactly as narrow as a hanging man's last idea. They went through bent double, single file, in water that went from ankle-deep to knee-deep as the tide pushed in behind them. Wren kept Tam ahead of her and Theo behind Tam, and the whole way Theo murmured directions in the smallest voice she'd ever heard him use — left here, mind the drop, the pipe junction, duck — and every direction came true.

It was the strangest march of her life: being led through the dark bowels of a Crown prison by a man taking dictation from its corpses.

At the junction where the culvert split, Theo stopped so suddenly Tam walked into his back.

"What?" Wren hissed.

"There's someone following us." His voice cracked. "A prisoner. Not dead — living. He got out of his cell when the guards changed. He's been following our sound." Theo turned toward the black tunnel behind them and called, very softly: "This way! Up and left, follow my voice!"

A splash. A ragged gasp. Then footsteps, stumbling closer, and out of the dark came a man Wren recognized from wanted bills nailed to the fish-market posts — Aldous Ferry, the counterfeiter, missing three weeks, presumed transported. He was gaunt as a fence rail and crying without any shame about it.

"Bless you," he kept saying. "Bless you, bless you, I heard voices, I thought I'd gone mad, I thought the dead were finally calling me in—"

"In a way," Theo said.

They went up. The culvert's final climb was a rusted rung-ladder through a vertical shaft, and at the top a grate that Mott attacked with a pry bar while Wren held the lantern and listened to the water climbing beneath them. It was at their waists now. Tam went up first, hauled through by hands; then Ferry, who needed two pulls; then the others, counting off in whispers like a rosary.

Theo was last. Of course Theo was last.

The water hit his chest at the bottom of the shaft and Wren saw it happen — saw him stop, look down into the black flood filling the prison's throat, and listen to whatever was rising with it. His face did something complicated. Grief and gratitude fighting for the same ground.

"They're saying go," he called up to her, water dragging at his words. "All of them. Every one. They say—" he laughed, or sobbed, or both, "—they say tell the Gardeners they held the door."

And then he was coming up the rungs with the reservoir roaring at his heels, and Mott slammed the grate, and they rolled out into a weed-choked cistern yard under a sky going gray at the edges, eight of them plus one half-drowned counterfeiter, breathing like landed fish.

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Tam started laughing — a cracked, hiccuping, sixteen-year-old laugh — and Ferry joined in, and then Mott, and Wren sat down in the nettles and let it move through her too, the wild unhinged joy of people who had gone into the dark expecting to pay and walked out having stolen instead.

They'd done it. No blood, no bodies, no trail a magistrate could follow. The Gardeners had reached inside the Crown's own teeth and taken back one of their own, and the Crown would wake tomorrow and find its fist full of nothing.

Beside her, Theo wasn't laughing. He was sitting with his arms around his knees, watching the grate, listening to the water settle.

"Eighteen," he said quietly.

"What?"

"Drowned men in the Saltcellar. I counted them properly on the way out. Eighteen voices, and every one of them helped." He shook his head slowly. "Five years I hid this. Five years I treated it like a disease. And tonight it was—" he searched for the word and landed on it with visible surprise, "—a compass. The dead aren't my haunting, Wren. They're my map. Every ghost in this country is standing at some corner pointing the way home."

Wren looked at him — this bone-carver who'd spent half a decade shrinking, who'd burned prophecy pages and lied badly to Inquisitors and flinched from his own gift — and saw what the Gardeners had actually recruited, without meaning to. Not a liability. An instrument. Maybe the instrument.

"Don't let that go to your head," she said. "You still can't lie to save your life."

"Noted."

She helped him up. Around them the others were already moving, fading into the pre-dawn streets in twos and threes, Tam wrapped in Mott's spare oilskin, Ferry promising debts he couldn't count. The city smelled like rain coming. Somewhere across the rooftops, the dragon-fire wards flickered — Wren saw it plainly now, a stutter in the lamplight of the whole eastern district, a heartbeat missed — and she filed it away with the small cold dread that had lived under her ribs since the First Guttering.

They walked home through streets that officially did not exist, carrying a boy the Crown officially had no record of, guided home by a man who officially heard nothing.

And three districts away, in a candlelit office, Inquisitor Hale unrolled a chart covered in green chalk marks and began, patiently, to connect the lines.
