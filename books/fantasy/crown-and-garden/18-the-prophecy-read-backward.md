## 18. The Prophecy Read Backward

The scholar lived above a shuttered dye-house in the Tanners' Reach, where the river ran the color of old bruises and even the pigeons looked like they'd given up on being admired. Wren had told Theo only three things on the walk over: the scholar's name was Ilsa Vane, she had been expelled from the Royal Collegium for asking the wrong questions, and she had not left her rooms in four years.

"She's not dangerous," Wren added, taking the stairs two at a time. "She's worse. She's *thorough*."

The door was covered in chalk. Not Gardener sigils — Theo had learned enough of those by now to read the alphabet of them, the small hooked marks that meant *safe passage* and *one of ours* and *the water is watched*. This was different. This was mathematics, or something that wanted to be mathematics, columns of figures marching across the wood in a script that hurt to look at sideways, the way a bright day hurts after a long illness.

Wren knocked in a rhythm — three, pause, two, pause, one — and the door opened on a chain before she finished.

"You're late," said a voice like dry paper.

"We brought you the thing you asked for."

"I asked for a great many things. Most of them impossible."

"Then it's lucky we brought the one that wasn't." Wren lifted the oilcloth bundle she'd been carrying under her arm, and Ilsa Vane's door shut, unchained, and opened again in the space of a breath.

Theo had imagined an exile as someone diminished. Ilsa Vane was not diminished. She was a tall woman gone stooped from decades of bending over desks, with ink-stained fingers and eyes so pale they seemed to have been washed too many times, and her room was a country unto itself — books stacked to the ceiling in load-bearing towers, maps nailed over the windows to keep the light out or in, he couldn't tell which. A single candle burned on a table that had been cleared, with visible violence, of everything else.

"You're the bone-carver," she said, looking at Theo the way a jeweler looks at a stone she suspects is flawed in an interesting way.

"People keep saying that like it explains something."

"It explains everything. Sit down. Both of you. Touch nothing, breathe on nothing, and if you must sneeze, do it into the river."

* * *

The bundle contained the book.

Theo had burned the page with his name on it five years' worth of nights ago — no, he corrected himself, eleven days ago, though it felt like a life — and he had thought that was the end of it. But Wren had gone back to the shop of the bookseller who'd sold it to him by mistake, and the bookseller, once the Gardeners' particular brand of persuasion had been applied, had produced three more copies from a cellar full of things the Crown had ordered destroyed and he had quietly declined to destroy.

Ilsa Vane unwrapped the oilcloth with the tenderness of a midwife.

*The Verses of the Hearth and the Hollow*, the spine read, in gold that had gone brown. Beneath it, smaller: *Being a True Account of the Founding Fire, and of What Must Come After*.

"There are four known copies in the world," Ilsa said. "The Collegium holds one, locked. The Regent's private library holds one. One was burned publicly, with great ceremony, forty years ago — I attended, as a girl. It was theater. They burned a decoy." She laid her hand flat on the cover. "And then there is this one, which has been passing hand to hand among smugglers and fools for a century, and which no one read properly, because everyone reads prophecies the same way."

"Which is?" Theo asked.

"Front to back."

She opened the book. The pages were heavy, foxed at the edges, the ink gone the color of dried blood. Theo leaned in despite himself. He knew these verses. He had burned them. *When the fire fails and the name is struck from the map of nations, there shall come a door, and the door shall be a maker who carries the dead, and through the door the chosen shall pass out of the shadow of Vellamarch into—*

"Into what?" he'd asked Ember once, in the root-cellar, and the dragon had been silent for a long time.

*Into what it says,* Ember had finally answered, and would not elaborate.

"Here." Ilsa's finger stopped at the final verse, the one that had kept Theo awake for eleven nights. *And the door shall open once, and only outward, and what passes through shall not return, and the country that was shall be forgotten even by its ghosts, and this is mercy, and this is the end.*

"Everyone reads that as an escape," Ilsa said. "A way out. The door leads out of Vellamarch, the chosen leave, the country dies clean instead of ugly. Every scholar who ever touched this text read it that way, and the Collegium built forty years of policy on that reading — containment, suppression, let the borders gutter, the prophecy itself says it's *mercy*." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "But prophecies are not written front to back. They're written the way fires burn. From the end backward. The founding verses of Vellamarch — the ones carved into the First Hearth itself, the ones no one has been allowed to read in a hundred years — those are the *last* verses of this book. The whole text runs the wrong direction. It's a palimpsest of time. You read it the way you'd read a river, from the mouth back to the spring."

Wren frowned. "You're saying it should be read backward."

"I'm saying it was *written* backward, and reading it forward is like reading a letter addressed to someone else. Watch."

She turned to the final page, the verse of the door and the mercy and the end, and then she began, in a flat scholar's voice, to read it aloud — from the last line to the first.

Theo heard it happen the way you hear a chord resolve.

*And this is mercy, and this is the end* — read backward, the words turned inside out, became: *and this is the beginning, and this is the charge.*

*The country that was shall be forgotten even by its ghosts* became *the country that is shall be remembered even by its dead.*

*What passes through shall not return* became *what returns shall not be refused.*

*And the door shall open once, and only outward* became *and the door shall open always, and only inward.*

Theo sat back. The room seemed to tilt gently, like a boat.

"That's not — words don't do that," he said. "You can't just reverse a sentence and have it mean the opposite. That's not how language works."

"It is exactly how prophecy works." Ilsa's pale eyes were bright now, fever-bright, the brightness of a woman who had waited four years in a room full of maps for someone to bring her the proof. "The Verses were composed as a *spell*, carver, not a poem. A spell spoken forward does one thing; spoken backward, it undoes that thing and does its opposite. The founding mages of Vellamarch wrote the country's death and its rebirth into the same text, and they hid the rebirth inside the death, because they knew that anyone who found the book would be looking for a way *out*. Cowards read the front of the book. It takes a particular kind of desperation to read the back."

"Why?" Wren said quietly. "Why hide it?"

"Because a door that leads out saves one person. A door that leads in saves everyone. And the founding mages knew that if the choice ever came, the person standing at the threshold would have to *choose* — and they were not confident, apparently, in the character of their descendants." Ilsa turned the book around so the last page faced Theo. "The door doesn't lead out of Vellamarch, bone-carver. It never did. The door leads Vellamarch *back into existence*. The fire fails, the name is struck, the country dies — and the door is how it comes back. Not an escape. A resurrection."

* * *

Theo walked to the window — the one window not papered over — and looked out at the Tanners' Reach, at the tenements and the dye-house smoke and the children running chalk games in the mud, and he tried to feel what he was supposed to feel.

Hope, presumably. That was the obvious thing. Eleven days ago he had learned his name was written in a book of doom, and every hour since had been a countdown, a narrowing, a country literally going dark around him while the Regent waited at the edges with her pen and her treaties and her patient, legal appetite. *The door is a way out* had been the one mercy in all of it. Not for him — he'd already understood, in the wordless way you understand things at three in the morning, that a door out of Vellamarch would not be a door he could walk through and leave the rest of it behind — but at least the shape of the doom had been simple. Run. Flee. Save yourself. Everyone knows how to read that story.

Now the story had turned inside out, and the shape of it was worse and better in the same breath.

"The fire's dying," he said, without turning around. "Ember told us. It gutters a little more every week. Last month the wards failed for a whole minute and Inquisitors walked streets that officially don't exist. If the door is a resurrection, it's a resurrection on a deadline."

"Then you had better hurry," Ilsa said, "and you had better be right about yourself. Read me the verse again. The one that names the door."

Theo took the book. His hands knew the page by now; he'd burned this verse once and the ash had spelled his name in the cinders, and he had not slept properly since. He read it forward first, the coward's way: *there shall come a door, and the door shall be a maker who carries the dead.*

"Now backward," Ilsa said.

He read it backward, and his voice caught in the middle, because the reversed words were not a reversal at all. They were the same words, wearing different clothes. *A maker who carries the dead* — backward, inside out — became *a maker whom the dead carry.* The hinge of the sentence swung the other way. He had read it, all these days, as a man who *has* a gift, who *holds* the ghosts, who carries them like a pack on his back. Read the other direction, he was not the porter. He was the passenger. Every ghost he had ever heard — the murmuring dead of the bone-shop, the drowned boy of the Drowned Quarter, his mother, Ember-of-Ashes herself — they were not his burden.

They were his legs.

"The dead carry you to the door," Ilsa said softly, watching his face. "That's the true reading. The maker doesn't bring the dead to the threshold. The dead bring the maker. Which means—" she tapped the table, once, hard, "—every voice you've ever heard, every ghost that ever spoke to you, is part of the mechanism. They're not witnesses to the prophecy. They're *participants* in it. The country was founded on a fire lit with a dragon's dying, and it will be relit by a maker carried by the dead, and the dead of Vellamarch have been walking you toward this your entire life without your consent."

Theo set the book down very carefully, because his hands had started to shake and he did not want to drop a hundred-year-old book in front of a woman who would never forgive him.

"Without my consent," he repeated.

"Prophecies rarely consult," Ilsa said. "It's their great failing as a genre."

* * *

They argued for two hours. Wren wanted logistics — where was the First Hearth, how did they reach it, what did the Gardeners need to do to hold a path open. Ilsa wanted the book back and, Theo suspected, wanted to be left alone with it for a decade. Theo wanted something he couldn't name, and it took him until the candle had burned down to a thumb's width to say it out loud.

"If the door leads in," he said, "then the verse about mercy — *this is mercy, and this is the end* — backward, that's *the beginning, and the charge*. Whose charge? The founding mages are dust. Ember wrote the prophecy — Ember admitted it, it was her dying wish, her despair dressed up as fate. So who is the charge *for*?"

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment, and something in her washed-out eyes shifted, the way light shifts when a cloud moves off the sun.

"For whoever the dead carry," she said. "That's the cruelty of it, carver, and the mercy too. The charge passes down the chain of the dead, hand to ghostly hand, until it reaches living fingers. Whoever hears the dead. Whoever the dead have been walking." She closed the book. "You asked once, I imagine, why you? Why you were given the hearing, why your name is in the book, why the last dragon's ghost attached itself to you out of all the people in this forgotten country."

"I've stopped asking why," Theo said. "The answers are all worse than the question."

"Then ask a better question." She slid the book across the table to him. "Ask what the dead want. Because if Ilsa Vane's reading is correct — and I have staked my life, my exile, and four years of my sanity on its being correct — then the dead of Vellamarch have been carrying you toward a door for your entire life, and they have not been doing it *to* you. They've been doing it *with* you. Ask them."

"I hear the dead," Theo said. "I don't interview them."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "But there's one dead who's been talking to you longer than any of the others, isn't there? One voice you heard before you ever carved your first charm. Before the bone-shop. Before any of it." Her pale eyes held his, and Theo felt the floor of the conversation open under him like a trapdoor. "Every maker has a first ghost, bone-carver. The one who taught them to listen. Who was yours?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. Wren was watching him, and he knew his face had gone the color of the river outside, because there was only one answer, there had only ever been one answer, and he had spent eleven days running from a prophecy when he should have been running toward a grave.

His mother's grave. In the bone orchard on the hill above the old quarter, under a stone he had carved himself at fourteen, with a ghost he had never once thought to *question* — because she was his mother, because she was safe, because she was the one voice in all the world that had never asked anything of him.

And if Ilsa Vane was right — if the dead carried him, if the charge passed down the chain — then the first link in that chain, the hand at the very beginning of it, the hand that had set him walking—

"Her grave is on the far side of the city," Wren said quietly. She had read his face too. "And the wards fail again tonight. Ember says we have until the next guttering, maybe two."

Theo picked up the book. It was lighter than it should have been, the way a door is lighter once you know it opens inward.

"Then we'd better walk fast," he said. "There's someone I have to ask why she never told me."
