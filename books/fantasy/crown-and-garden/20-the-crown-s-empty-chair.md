## 20. The Crown's Empty Chair

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy on a government mule who would not meet Inquisitor Hale's eyes.

*Present yourself before the Regent's Council. Ninth hour. Come alone. Come unarmed.*

She read it twice by the grey light of her window and felt the particular cold that official paper carries — the kind that means someone above you has decided something about your life without asking. Inquisitors were not summoned to court. They were sent out like hounds and called back like hounds, but never invited inside the house.

She dressed in her formal blacks anyway. Brushed the dust of Vellamarch's invisible districts off her boots until they looked like a loyal woman's boots again.

Her superior, Lord-Commander Vasque, was waiting in the courtyard with his hands folded behind his back, watching the gate as if he expected an army.

"You've been busy," he said.

"I've been working."

"Working." He turned the word over like a coin he suspected was clipped. "Three weeks in the drowned quarter. A prison break through the cisterns that my own wardens swear could only have been done with help from *inside*. And a prisoner who sat across from you for six hours and gave you nothing, while you walked away with—" he paused, delicately— "a bone charm."

Hale kept her face still. "Evidence."

"Evidence." He almost smiled. "The Regent's council has questions, Inquisitor. Not about you." His voice dropped. "About what you've found."

"And if I've found nothing?"

"Then lie well," Vasque said. "You're better at it than you think. I've watched you do it to yourself for years."

He handed her up into the waiting carriage himself, which was its own kind of message. Commanders did not open doors for subordinates unless they wanted something, or feared something, or both.

* * *

The palace quarter of Vellamarch did not officially exist, which had never stopped it from being the most heavily guarded ground in the country. Hale had been inside twice before — once for her induction oath, once to receive commendation after the Marlowe affair — and both times she had noticed the same thing: the deeper you went toward the throne room, the fewer windows there were. By the inner corridors there were none at all. The Crown, whatever else it believed, understood that people who could see the sky started having ideas about it.

They brought her in through the servants' passage this time. That was new. That was worse.

The antechamber outside the council hall held a dozen petitioners, clerks, and minor lords, all pretending not to look at one another. Hale recognized a few faces from intelligence briefings. Master Corvel, the treasury man whose ledgers had absorbed three scandals without ever quite catching fire. Lady Ashvine, whose family name appeared on half the shipping charters in the capital. And near the far wall, apart from everyone, a thin man in grey with the ink-stained fingers of a treaty clerk, watching Hale with the flat professional interest of someone cataloguing her.

A herald opened the doors. Petitioners went first, one by one, into the hall and out again within minutes. Then a clerk touched Hale's shoulder.

"Inquisitor Hale."

The council chamber was smaller than she'd imagined, and colder. No banners. No portraits. At the far end stood the Regent's chair — high-backed, black oak, draped with the crowned-garden sigil — and it was empty.

Lord Chancellor Dree stood beside it instead, a small precise man with white hair cut close as a hedge, holding a single sheet of paper as though it might escape him.

"Inquisitor," he said pleasantly. "Thank you for coming alone. Sit, or stand, whichever keeps your spine straighter."

She stood.

"You've spent some weeks among the so-called Gardeners' territory," Dree said. "Chasing a bone-carver."

"Chasing an unlicensed practitioner, yes."

"Mm." He set the paper down on the arm of the empty chair, carefully, the way a man lays a card he intends to win with later. "And in the course of that work, you will have observed that the wards in those districts are... irregular. Failing. There was an incident last month — a minute, perhaps more, where several old streets became briefly visible to our surveyors. You reported it yourself."

"I did."

"Do you know what our cartographers found when they surveyed those streets?"

"No, lord chancellor."

"Nothing," Dree said. "That is the answer. Nothing. Streets that appear for sixty seconds and then are gone — no title deeds, no boundary markers, no record in any registry going back a century. Legally, Inquisitor, those streets do not exist. Neither does the district around them. Neither, if we follow the documents to their roots, does the province entire."

The cold in the room seemed to sharpen. Hale thought of the flooded cisterns, the chalk sigils glowing faintly under Wren Sallow's hand, the whole hidden architecture of a city that had been taught to forget itself.

"I'm aware the province's status is unusual," she said carefully.

"Unusual." Dree smiled thinly. "It is unprecedented, and it is deliberate, and it is nearly complete, and you are standing in the middle of it asking questions like a woman who hasn't read her history. So let me save you the trouble of digging further, because digging is now a liability — yours and mine."

He gestured, and the thin clerk stepped forward and unrolled a document across the table. Hale recognized the format immediately. Treaty vellum. Seals down the margin. She'd seen copies of the Accords of Severance during her training — the settlement that ended the border wars a hundred years ago.

"The original," Dree said. "Not the copy in the public archive. Read the fourth clause."

She read it. Read it again. Her mouth went dry.

*Should the territory known as Vellamarch cease, by any means, to maintain sovereign continuity of governance for a period of one year, all claims upon its lands and peoples shall lapse unto the Crown of the greater realm, without contest, in perpetuity.*

"Sovereign continuity," she said slowly.

"Governance. Administration. Recognition." Dree ticked them off on his fingers. "A country exists, Inquisitor, because other countries agree to see it. We have spent a hundred years making sure they agree to stop seeing this one. The maps were withdrawn. The embassies closed. The name struck from every register our neighbors keep. And here—" he tapped clause four— "is the door we built for ourselves. When Vellamarch's last institutions fail — when the wards go dark and the last offices of state dissolve — then legally, cleanly, irrevocably, this land becomes ours. No war. No conquest. Annexation by absence."

"That requires the wards to fail completely," Hale said. "The old dragon-wards. They've held for a century."

"They are failing *now*." For the first time, something moved behind Dree's pleasantness — something that might have been urgency, or hunger. "You saw the guttering yourself. Our engineers project total failure within the season. Perhaps weeks. The Regent's council has deliberated at length, and the decision is this: we do nothing. We let it die."

Hale heard her own heartbeat. "There are two hundred thousand people in those districts."

"There are subjects of the Crown," Dree corrected, "who will wake one morning as citizens of a realm they already pay tithes to. There will be a transition authority. Grain shipments. A census. It will be handled gently."

"And the ones who resist? The Gardeners? The practitioners?"

"The ones who resist," Dree said, "will be rebels against lawful annexation, and dealt with as rebels always are." He picked the treaty up and rolled it closed. "Which brings us to you."

* * *

They moved her to a side room — a map room, actually, though the maps on the walls were all wrong. Old ones. Hale found herself standing in front of a survey of Vellamarch drawn ninety years ago, every district labeled, every street named, borders drawn bold and certain in red ink. A country that used to believe in itself.

Dree came to stand beside her.

"Your investigation," he said. "Report it plainly. What do you have?"

"A bone-carver named Theo Amberdale. Practicing illegally. Connected to the Gardener network. Possibly significant to the ward-failure — the prophecy materials recovered suggest—" She stopped herself.

"*Suggest* what?" Dree's voice had gone very quiet, which she had learned, in interrogations, was more dangerous than loud.

Hale weighed it. Everything in her training said give the council what it wants; everything in the last month said the council was the thing she should be afraid of.

"There's a belief among the Gardeners," she said, "that the wards can be relit. That the country can be brought back onto the maps. If they succeed, the fourth clause dies, and the Regent loses the cleanest acquisition in a century."

Dree studied her profile for a long moment.

"You say that like it would be a loss."

"It's two hundred thousand people, lord chancellor. People who think they live in a free province. Waking up annexed because we let their lights go out on purpose—" She caught herself. Steadied. "I'm saying it like a fact. It would be resisted."

"By a network of healers and thieves and a carver who talks to bones." Dree almost laughed. "Inquisitor, let me tell you why you're really here. Your file is impressive. Your convictions are famous. Your family—" and here his voice softened in a way that made her skin crawl— "your family lost a great deal to the Gardeners, years ago. Your father's judgeship. Your brother's commission. All undone by their little extralegal theater. And yet in three weeks inside their territory, you have produced one prisoner, one broken interrogation, and one report that reads suspiciously like sympathy."

"I produced a raid that nearly broke their sanctuary."

"And lost the prisoner anyway." He let that sit. "Here is what happens next. The Regent — whom you will address as the chair, since the chair speaks through us — wants your final report within the fortnight. It will establish, beyond legal question, that the province's institutions have failed. That sovereignty is lapsed. That clause four may be invoked. You are the ideal author: an honest Inquisitor with a grudge, whose testimony no one can call political."

"And if my honest findings don't support invocation?"

Dree smiled at the map, at the bold red border of a dead country.

"Then you will be reassigned somewhere very quiet, and someone less scrupulous will write the report, and you will spend the rest of your career knowing exactly how it happened and exactly what your silence purchased." He straightened his cuffs. "The chair is patient, Inquisitor. It can wait a hundred years for a country. It cannot wait a fortnight for you. Think on which side of the ink you'd rather stand."

He left her alone with the maps.

* * *

She should have gone straight back. Written the report. Chosen the quiet posting, the long career, the slow forgetting.

Instead she took the long road out of the palace quarter, past the surveyors' offices, and stopped at the archive desk with her credentials showing, and asked for one thing: the original survey records for the province of Vellamarch, one hundred years present to one hundred years past.

The archivist, an old woman with spectacles on a chain, fetched a single slim folder and set it down almost apologetically.

"That's all there is?"

"All there is," the archivist said. "Every record older than that was removed in the year of the Severance. Official order. I've often wondered where they went."

"Who signed the removal order?"

The archivist slid a slip of paper across. One line, one seal, one signature.

Hale stared at it and felt the floor tilt beneath her, gently, the way the streets had tilted during the guttering.

The signature belonged to her grandfather.

* * *

She walked out into the evening with the slip of paper burning in her coat pocket like a coal. The city around her was doing its ordinary evening things — lamplighters, fish carts, children running chalk games in gutters — and she found herself looking at all of it differently. Counting it. Two hundred thousand people who didn't know they were already written out of the world, waiting on the goodwill of a chair that planned to let their fires die.

Wren Sallow would want to know. Theo Amberdale would want to know — though wanting to tell him meant walking into the Gardeners' arms with the one piece of truth that could either save the country or get her branded a traitor by morning.

Vasque was waiting at the garrison gates, and his face told her he already knew where she'd been.

"Well, Inquisitor? Did the chair make its wishes plain?"

Hale looked at her commander, at the man who'd opened her carriage door, and lied to him with the ease of long practice.

"It wants my report in a fortnight."

"Good." He nodded, satisfied, and turned away. "Make it the right one."

She stood in the gateway a moment longer, watching the lamps come on across districts that officially weren't there, and made herself one promise, quiet as chalk on stone:

She would write the report. Every word of it true. She just hadn't yet decided which truth it would tell — or who she'd have to become to carry it to the people who needed it most.

Behind her, in the darkening archive of the palace, a clerk in grey was already drafting the annexation proclamations.

He worked quickly. He had a fortnight.
