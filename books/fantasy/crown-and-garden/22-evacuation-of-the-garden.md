## 22. Evacuation of the Garden

The chalk came down first.

Theo stood in the doorway of the root-cellar and watched Wren scrub the green sigils off the cellar door with a wet rag, each stroke erasing a ward that had kept this place hidden for thirty years. The symbols didn't fade so much as surrender — the chalk lifting in pale streaks like something being bled out.

"Say it again," he said. "The part about tonight."

"You heard me." Wren didn't stop scrubbing. "Dawn tomorrow. Maybe sooner. Hale has half the district mapped; we found her survey marks on three corners this morning. When she moves, she moves at dawn." She wrung out the rag over a bucket of gray water. "Everyone scatters to the safehouse list by midnight. No exceptions."

"And you're telling them to leave the bone."

Wren's hands went still. In the lamplight behind her, the root-cellar was already half-emptied — shelves stripped, ledgers bundled into oilcloth, the long table where they'd planned cases for a generation standing bare as a coffin. But beneath their feet, under the floor, under the old stone that smelled of earth and candle smoke and years of other people's courage, Ember's bone lay wrapped in wool in its hiding place, exactly where it had lain since before Theo ever heard its ghost speak.

"The bone doesn't run," Wren said quietly. "That's the point of it. It's been here longer than any of us. It'll be here after."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one there is." She finally turned to face him, and he saw the night in her face — the informant's confession still raw, the network bleeding from its first real wound, a boy named Tam still missing somewhere in Crown custody. She looked like a woman holding a wall up with her shoulders. "Theo. We have forty-one people to move before sunrise. I don't have time for you to argue with a rock."

"It's not a rock."

"No," she agreed. "It's worse. It talks."

* * *

By eleven o'clock the Gardeners were ghosts leaving a house.

Theo worked the door with Old Maren, handing out bundles and pointing people into the dark. A healer named Cass with her satchels strapped double. The forgery twins, carrying nothing but their hands and a shared look of fury. A boy of fifteen who'd joined after his father was pressed into the Regent's labor gangs, weeping without sound as he took one last look at the room that had fed him for two winters.

Every one of them paused at the threshold. Every one of them touched the doorframe — a habit so old Theo hadn't noticed it until now, fingers brushing the wood the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder to say *still here, still yours.*

"They're saying goodbye," Maren said beside him. She was seventy if she was a day, spine curved like a drawn bow, and she'd been a Gardener longer than anyone living. "Thirty years I've walked through that door thinking it would always be there. You never think a place can be taken until it is."

"Is that what you tell yourself? That places get taken?"

Maren snorted. "No. What I tell myself is that a place is just people agreeing to meet somewhere. Move the people, keep the agreement, and the place travels with you." She hefted her bundle. "Your dragon understood that better than most. Why do you think it stayed with the bone instead of the ground?"

He had no answer for that. He watched the last of them go — Wren last but one, checking the street both ways with a smuggler's economy of motion — and then the cellar was empty, and it was midnight, and Theo Amberdale stood alone in a room that was about to belong to the Crown.

He did not leave.

* * *

He told himself he was searching the floorboards for anything missed. He was not. He knelt in the corner where the flagstones dipped, where a seam of darker stone ran like a vein, and put his palm flat against it, and listened.

The dead of the cellar were thin here — the Gardeners had buried no one below, that wasn't their way — but the walls remembered. Forty years of whispered plans lived in the mortar. A woman's voice, decades dead, saying *the north route is watched, take the cisterns.* A man laughing low over cards. Children, once — someone had hidden their family down here through a bad winter, and the ghosts of their games still clung to the rafters like cobwebs.

And beneath all of it, deeper than memory, warmer: a slow pulse of attention, patient as coal.

*You are still here,* Ember said. Not words, exactly. More like heat rising through stone. *I felt them go. One by one. Like birds leaving.*

"I'm not going either."

*Ah.* A long silence, in which Theo could feel the dragon considering him the way fire considers a log. *The stubborn one. Yes. I remember your kind. Makers are always stubborn. It is how they make things that outlast them.*

"I need to know where you are. Exactly. If I'm taking you out of here, I need—"

*I am not leaving.*

Theo sat back on his heels. "You'd rather be seized? Put in a Crown vault? Cut into trinkets for some Regent's trophy case?"

*Little maker.* The warmth sharpened, almost amused, almost sad. *Do you think I am in the bone? I am what the bone remembers. They could grind it to powder and scatter it in the sea and I would still be wherever the remembering is. But the bone matters to *you*. So tell me truly — why will you not leave it?*

Because, he almost said, because the prophecy names me, because you're my only guide out of Vellamarch, because losing you means losing the thread. All true. None of it the truth.

"Because everyone I've ever loved," he said instead, slowly, "is dead. My mother. The voices. You. The only things in my life that stay are things that can't walk away. And I am not—" His voice caught and he hated it. "I am not letting them take the last one."

The cellar was quiet for a long time. Somewhere above, wind worried the boarded windows.

*Then listen well,* Ember said at last, *because I will only say this once, and then we will do something foolish together, which is the way of dragons and makers both.*

* * *

The bone came up at two in the morning.

It was heavier than it should have been — Theo had always assumed that was imagination, but kneeling there with it unwrapped across his knees, wool falling away, he felt the weight settle into his arms like a sleeping animal. Dragon-bone didn't look like bone. It looked like wood that had dreamed of being metal: dark grain, faint whorls, a luster that moved when he moved. His carver's hands knew it instantly, the way they knew every material, and they told him what his mind refused to: this had been alive. This had *sung*.

Wrapped again, slung inside his coat against his chest, it beat against his heart with each step. Or perhaps that was just his heart. He genuinely couldn't tell anymore.

Getting out was the problem.

The Gardeners' routes were compromised — that was the whole reason for the scattering — and Wren had taken the last working map with her. Theo knew the tenements around the cellar the way a blind man knows his own hallway, by count and habit, but the patrols were new. Inquisitor lanterns swung at the ends of streets that had been safe at dusk. Green chalk marks he didn't recognize had bloomed on two corners overnight: Hale's survey marks, precise as surgery.

So he did the stupid thing. He asked the dead.

They crowded close the moment he opened himself — they always did, poor hungry things, five years of Vellamarch's murdered and mourned pressing at the edge of his hearing like moths at glass — and he stood in an alley mouth and let them pour over him.

*Soldier. Soldier on the corner, died at Marrow Ford, knows patrol signs.* An old woman, dead forty years: *the culvert behind the tannery, my son used it in the famine, the grate lifts if you lift from the left.* And everywhere, threaded through, the faint chorus that had grown louder all week as the wards weakened — citizens waking in their graves to half-memories of a country with another name, murmuring *Vellamarch, Vellamarch* like a word remembered mid-dream.

"Culvert," Theo whispered. "Left side. Thank you." The old woman's ghost made a sound he hadn't heard from the dead in years — not a grievance, not a plea. Something like satisfaction. A debt paid forward across death.

The culvert stank and ran shallow and took him under two streets full of boots. Twice he froze in the black while lantern light swept the grates above, the bone pressed to his chest, and twice the light moved on. When he climbed out at the tannery yard, dawn was still an hour off, and the city flickered.

He saw it happen for the first time with his own eyes: the far row of rooftops simply stopped — not destroyed, not darkened, *stopped*, a clean absence where houses should be, lasting three heartbeats — and then returned, slightly wrong, slightly faded, like a page printed twice. Across the district, dogs began to howl. Somewhere a bell rang with no hand on it.

The country was guttering. He was standing inside its last light, carrying its last dragon, and the men hunting him thought they were policing a slum.

*Faster, little maker,* Ember said, and for the first time Theo heard fear in the fire of it.

* * *

Wren was waiting at the safehouse — a dyer's loft near the old cloth market, reeking of mordant and indigo — and she was waiting with her arms crossed and murder in her posture.

"You absolute—"

"Don't." He set the bundle on the dyeing table and unwrapped it, and whatever she'd meant to say died in her throat. In the lamplight the bone seemed to gather the light rather than reflect it. Even Wren, who had never heard Ember speak, went still the way people go still in a cathedral.

"You went back for it," she said finally. "Alone. Through Hale's pickets. At night. During a *guttering*."

"Yes."

"You could have been taken. Then everything — the prophecy, the door, all of it — ends in an interrogation room."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stepped closer, and now the anger was shaking apart into something else, something that frightened him more. "Because I have lost everyone, Theo. Everyone. My brother first, and then piece by piece every person I let stand next to me, and I swore I would stop doing the arithmetic of love because it always comes out the same. And then you arrive, this stubborn, haunted, impossible man, and I catch myself doing the arithmetic again, and I *hate* it, and then you walk out at midnight into a city full of Inquisitors to fetch a *bone*—"

"Wren."

"—and come back," she finished, quieter. "You came back."

"I said I would."

She laughed, short and unsteady, and dragged a hand through her hair. "Nobody comes back. That's the whole joke of my life. Nobody comes back." She looked at the bone, then at him, and squared herself like a woman picking a burden back up. "Fine. It stays with you. It stays with *us*. But hear me, Theo Amberdale: if you ever do that again without telling me, I will haunt you myself, and unlike your ghosts I will be *extremely* annoying."

Despite everything — the raid coming, the country dissolving, the price waiting unnamed at the end of the prophecy — Theo smiled. It felt like the first true smile in weeks.

"Noted."

* * *

They were still awake at dawn, planning, when the knock came.

Not the heavy triple-fall of the Crown. A Gardener knock — the new one, improvised after the betrayal, known to only six people. Wren opened the door on Old Maren, gray-faced, breathing hard from a run no seventy-year-old should attempt.

"They didn't wait for dawn," Maren gasped. "Hale's columns moved an hour ago. They're not heading for the safehouses." She gripped the doorframe. "Theo — they're digging. Down at the root-cellar. They've got the floor up. They know something was under it."

Theo's hand went to his coat, to the shape against his ribs, already knowing — the way you know a dream was a warning only after the thing warned of has begun — that the bone was not what they wanted.

"They found the hiding place," Maren said. "Empty. And carved into the underside of the stone, where the bone lay all these years — there's writing. Old writing. Hale's got scholars reading it right now."

"What does it say?" Wren demanded.

Maren looked at Theo, and her old eyes were terrible with pity.

"It says *the door sleeps beneath the maker's hands*," she said. "Child — they're not looking for the bone anymore. They're looking for *you*."
