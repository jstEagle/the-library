## 25. The Purge Papers

The archive beneath the Hall of Inquiry had no windows, which Inquisitor Hale had always found fitting. Truth, in her experience, preferred the dark. It kept better there.

She had come down with a forged requisition slip — Wren Sallow's chalk-work was good enough to fool a clerk who'd already gone home for the night — and a lantern she kept turned low. Three levels of shelving rose around her, iron-bound ledgers stacked like the ribs of some enormous dead thing. The air tasted of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of old smoke that no amount of time had managed to wash out.

*Restricted: Sovereignty Acts, 1189–1194.* The shelf plaque was brass, unpolished, almost apologetic. Hale ran her gloved finger along the spines until she found the box she wanted. Sealed with black wax stamped by the Office of the Regent itself.

Her hands were steady. She noted that with professional detachment, the way she noted everything now. Steady hands, steady breath, steady pulse. As if the last three weeks hadn't happened. As if she hadn't stood in a flooded cistern in the dark across from a bone-carver who heard ghosts, and struck a bargain with him like any common conspirator.

*The truth about the purge,* he'd asked of her. *That's my price.*

And she had said yes, because she'd already begun to suspect, and suspicion was a splinter that worked deeper the more you left it alone.

She broke the seal with her thumbnail.

Inside were eleven documents. She took them to the reading table at the center of the aisle, laid them out in order, and began — as she had been trained to do since she was nineteen years old — with the oldest.

* * *

The first paper was dated the ninth of Frostmonth, 1189. It was a memorandum from the Crown's Office of Borders, addressed to the Privy Council, and it concerned a problem.

Vellamarch, the memorandum explained, was an embarrassment.

It was not a large country. It sat in a fold of the map between two greater powers, fertile and self-governing and stubbornly independent, its borders marked not by walls but by something the drafters called *sustained flame* — a phrase Hale had read before, in treaty abstracts, and always assumed was poetic language for watchfires or beacons. The memorandum disabused her of that notion in its second paragraph.

*The border-flame is maintained by a singular entity,* it read, *a creature of the elder kind, bound by covenant to the land's defense. Conventional incursion is therefore impossible. The Crown's surveyors have confirmed this three times at cost of four lives. Recommendation follows.*

Hale turned the page. The recommendation was signed by a man whose name she knew from portraits in her grandmother's hallway: Lord Aldous Vane, First Minister to the Regency Council. Her great-grandmother's cousin. Her family's name was woven through the next eleven pages like thread through cloth.

*Recommendation:* Vane had written, in a hand so elegant it looked rehearsed, *that the territory known as Vellamarch be removed from the legal record rather than taken from the ground. A country cannot be annexed if it does not exist. Let its name be struck from treaties, its coinage declared counterfeit, its charters voided by decree. Let its people be told their magic is criminal, and let the crime be discovered — staged, if necessary — so that outlawry provides the moral pretext for erasure. The border-flame cannot be fought. But fire requires belief, and belief requires memory. Erase the memory, and the flame will gutter on its own within a generation or two. We need only wait.*

We need only wait.

Hale read the sentence four times. Her hands were still steady. She was beginning to hate how steady they were.

* * *

The second document was the staging order.

It was brief, which somehow made it worse. Dated spring of 1190, authorized by the Regency Council, countersigned by six names — three of them Thornes, one a Marlowe, one a Vane, and the sixth, in smaller, more careful script: *E. Hale, Undersecretary.*

Edmund Hale. Her grandfather's older brother. The one the family never spoke of, the one her father claimed had died young and honorably in customs service. He had not died young. He had signed his name to the fiction that would erase a nation, and then, presumably, he had lived comfortably on the proceeds.

The staging order detailed the operation with bureaucratic precision. An incident would be manufactured in a Vellamarch border town — the papers named it Coldharbour, a place Hale had never heard of, because officially it had never existed. Witnesses would testify to sorcerous violence. The testimony would be false; the witnesses were listed by name, each with the sum they'd been paid appended beside it. The sums were modest. That was the detail that made her stomach turn — not grand corruption but petty bookkeeping, forty crowns here, sixty there, the price of a nation entered into a ledger like the price of grain.

Then the outlawry decrees. Then the purge.

The word appeared in the fourth document without ceremony. *Purge operations to commence upon ratification.* Attached was a schedule of targets: hedge-healers, charm-makers, bone-readers, weather-speakers, anyone whose gift might let them perceive what had been done. Anyone who might remember too well, or hear too much, or see the seams in the world where a country had been cut away.

They weren't hunting criminals, Hale thought. They were hunting witnesses.

And they had been hunting them for a hundred and thirty years. Her whole order, the Hall of Inquiry, the oaths she'd sworn at nineteen with her hand on the Crown's seal — all of it built on a lie with a filing number.

She thought of the young Gardener in the interrogation cell, silent as stone, watching Hale with eyes that held no hatred at all, only a kind of weary patience, as if waiting for her to catch up. She thought of the carved bone charm she'd found folded in the girl's sleeve, and the bone-carver whose handiwork it was, and the way Theo Amberdale had looked at her in the cistern dark — not afraid of her, exactly. Worse than that. He had looked at her like a man reading a difficult page and hoping it would say something different the second time.

She had hated him a little for that look. Now she understood it. He had already known what she was only now learning: that the law she served was not broken. It was working exactly as designed.

* * *

There was a letter near the bottom of the box that was not an official document. It was personal, written in Edmund Hale's hand, unsent — or perhaps sent and returned, its envelope marked *undeliverable, addressee withdrawn to Vellamarch district.*

Withdrawn to Vellamarch district. As if the man had been posted into a country that did not exist, which, Hale supposed, was precisely what had happened.

She unfolded it. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea.

*Miriam,* it began — her great-aunt, then. *You will have heard what I signed. Before you judge me, understand that I went to see it myself. I stood in Coldharbour after. There was a woman there, a charm-maker, and she was not burning anything or cursing anyone. She was sitting in the road holding a child's shoe, and she looked at me, and I have not slept properly since.*

*They tell us the flame will die on its own once the forgetting takes hold. They tell us no one will suffer who does not choose to suffer. But I have seen the wards, Miriam. I have seen the streets that are there and are not there. The people inside them go on living. They marry and bury and carve little charms against toothache, and every year the country around them grows thinner, like a coat worn through at the elbows. And we call this mercy. We call this order.*

*I am writing to tell you that if the flame dies while I am alive, I will go back and stand in those streets and let them blink out with me. Tell Father I said so. Tell him also that his name is on the third page and mine is on the second, and that when the reckoning comes, we will stand together in it.*

*Your brother, E.*

Hale set the letter down very carefully, as though it might bruise.

So one of them had known. One of them had seen, and grieved, and written it down, and then — what? Gone quiet. Grown old. Died in a house full of portraits and lies, and been remembered by the family as a minor functionary lost too soon, while the country he helped erase went on thinning year by year, wearing through at the elbows.

She thought of her father, who had raised her on stories of the Hall of Inquiry as a noble calling — *we keep the honest people safe from the unnatural, little star.* She thought of the oath. She thought of Inquisitor Brann, her superior, who last month had ordered a tenement searched on rumor alone and put three children in holding cells overnight because their mother had a healing-scar on her palms. Brann had called it diligence. Hale had called it diligence too, at the time. She had signed the report herself.

*We call this order.*

Her hands were shaking now. Good, she thought. Finally.

* * *

The last document was the one that changed everything, and it was the smallest — a single page, undated, unsigned except with the Regent's private cipher. A directive, not a record. Forward-looking.

*Re: Final Phase. The sustained flame approaches natural expiry. Estimated remaining span: less than one year, possibly less than one season. Upon confirmed failure of the border-wards, the territory reverts to the Crown under the Doctrine of Vacancy. No annexation proceedings required; the land will be legally vacant and may be claimed by administrative act. Ensure no organized resistance remains in the affected districts at the time of transition. The Gardeners' network remains the principal obstacle. Priority target.*

Priority target.

They knew. Of course they knew. The Regent's office had known for years exactly how long the fire had left, had been counting down with patient bureaucratic calm, waiting for a country to finish dying so it could pick up the bones without dirtying its hands. And when the wards failed — when the streets flickered and the citizens woke remembering — there would be soldiers ready, and the Gardeners would be the first names on a list, and the bone-carver who called himself a door would be somewhere below even that, filed under *obstacles, residual.*

Hale sat in the windowless dark for a long time, listening to the sound of her own breathing and the tick of the lantern cooling.

She had come looking for doubt, and instead she had found certainty, and certainty was heavier. Doubt you could carry. Certainty rearranged the furniture of your whole life and dared you to sit back down in it.

She thought about walking away. She could reseal the box, forge the requisition log, climb back up into the lamplit world where she was Inquisitor Hale, competent and loyal and safe, and spend the rest of her career enforcing a lie she could now recite from memory, page and number. Plenty of people did exactly that. Edmund Hale had done it for fifty years.

But she had stood in Coldharbour too, in a sense. She had stood in the root-cellar among the Gardeners, and in the cistern dark beside a man who carried the dead in his bones, and she had watched a captured girl refuse to betray her people under questioning that would have broken most soldiers. She had seen what belonging looked like when it was chosen instead of assigned.

*We keep the honest people safe.* Her father's voice, warm and certain, in the garden of her childhood.

The honest people, it turned out, were underground. They were carving charms and forging papers and answering crimes the courts wouldn't touch, and they were the only ones in this entire rotten arrangement acting as if the people of Vellamarch were real.

Hale gathered the documents, all eleven, and wrapped them in the oilcloth she'd brought for the purpose. Then, after a moment, she added Edmund's letter separately, tucked against her breastbone where it lay like a coal.

She could not undo a hundred and thirty years. She could not raise Coldharbour from the erased places or give back a single one of the lives the purge had taken. But she could do what her great-uncle had not dared to do.

She could deliver the truth to the people it had been stolen from. All of it. With her family's signatures attached.

She climbed the archive stairs two at a time, past the sleeping clerk, out into the night air of the capital, where the lamps burned steady and the streets held their shapes and everyone she passed believed, absolutely, in the country they were standing in.

By dawn she would be across the lines. By noon, if Wren Sallow's chalk-marks still ran true, she would be in the root-cellar, laying eleven stolen pages on a scarred wooden table and saying words she had never imagined crossing her lips:

*I'm Inquisitor Hale. My family helped erase your country. Here is the proof. What do you want me to do?*

She paused at the top of the stairs, one hand pressed flat over the letter above her heart, and allowed herself — just once, briefly, in the dark where truth kept best — to feel the full weight of what she was about to lose.

Then she opened the door, and walked out of the Hall of Inquiry for the last time, and did not look back.

Behind her, far below, the empty box sat on its shelf with its broken seal, and it would not stay undiscovered for long.
