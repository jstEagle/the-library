## 26. Regent's Hunt

The proclamation went up at dawn, pasted to every wall in the Drowned Quarter by soldiers who moved in pairs and never looked up.

Wren read it three times before the meaning settled into her bones like cold water.

*By order of the Lord Regent, martial law is declared over all districts within the old boundaries of the territory formerly designated Vellamarch. Curfew begins at dusk. Assembly is forbidden. All persons harboring practitioners of outlawed arts, or aiding networks of sedition known as "the Gardeners," will be tried as traitors. Rewards offered for information leading to arrest.*

*Traitors.* She'd been called a thief, a forger, a nuisance, a meddler — Hale had once called her *a persistent rash on the body politic*, which Wren had taken almost as a compliment. But *traitor* was different. Traitor implied there was a country to betray.

And that was exactly what the Regent wanted them to believe: that Vellamarch didn't exist, so anyone who loved it was a criminal against nothing at all.

She tore the proclamation down, folded it small, and put it in her pocket. Evidence. Or kindling. She hadn't decided which yet.

Behind her, the street was already filling with people reading the notices, and the silence coming off them was worse than shouting would have been. An old fishwife crossed herself with a hand missing two fingers. A boy who couldn't have been ten asked his mother what *martial law* meant, and the mother said *it means come home early,* and pulled him inside, and shut the shutters like she was closing eyes.

Wren walked back to the safehouse fast, taking the long way through the chalk-lines only Gardeners knew — a sigil here on a drainpipe, there under a windowsill, each one a small promise: *safe passage, safe door, safe hands.* She counted them as she went. By the third bridge she'd stopped counting, because she kept thinking about how easy it would be for one soldier with one bucket of water to erase them all.

The network had spent five years being invisible. Now invisibility was illegal.

* * *

The safehouse was a dyer's loft above the tannery row, full of hanging cloth the color of bruises. Twenty-two Gardeners were packed into it when she arrived, and every face turned toward her with the same question written on it.

She climbed onto a dyeing bench so they could see her.

"You've read the notices," she said. No preamble; they deserved better than speeches. "Martial law. Curfew. Us named by title, not just rumor — the Crown finally admits we exist. That's either good news or very bad news, and I'll tell you honestly I don't know which."

"Say the bad first," said Maren Oakes, the herb-healer, arms folded. Maren always wanted the bad first. It was why Wren trusted her arithmetic.

"The bad is this." Wren held up the folded proclamation. "Until now we worked in the gaps. The law ignored us because the law pretended our streets weren't real. That pretending is over. The Regent has decided to *see* us, and seeing is the first half of destroying. Every sigil, every safehouse, every name in our ledger is now worth silver to somebody hungry enough."

"And the good?" someone called from the back.

"The good is the same fact wearing its other coat." She let that sit. "For five years we've been outlaws against a country that claimed we didn't belong to it. Now the country has admitted, in ink, on every wall, that these districts are worth governing — worth fearing. You don't declare martial law on empty land. They've told every soul in the Drowned Quarter that their home matters enough to occupy."

A murmur ran through the loft. Not agreement, exactly. Something more dangerous than agreement — recognition.

"So we have a choice," Wren said. "We scatter. We go quiet, bury the chalk, stop answering crimes until the heat passes. Some of you want that, and I won't shame anyone who takes that road. Or—" and here her own voice surprised her, going rougher than she intended— "we stop hiding, and we become what they've already named us. If they're going to hang rebels either way, better rebels who chose it."

Silence. The wet cloth overhead dripped slow metronome drops onto the floorboards.

It was Tam Broadfoot — the young lockman they'd pulled out of the cisterns after the raid, still thin from interrogation, a fading bruise on his throat where Hale's manacles had sat — who broke it.

"They took my confession under candlelight and I gave them nothing," he said. His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "You know what kept me quiet? Not courage. Spite. I thought: whatever they do to me, tomorrow I'm still a Gardener and they're still afraid of us. If we scatter now, then it was all for nothing — my week in that cell, the Widow Ashen's arrest, everything. I didn't survive Hale's questions to die of caution."

Wren felt the room tilt toward him. Young Tam, who six months ago had been afraid of his own shadow in a doorway, now standing on a crate calling caution *death.*

"We vote," Maren said flatly. "Not cheer. Vote. Hands for scattering?"

Four hands. Wren noted each face; no judgment, and she'd see them safely out of the city herself if it came to it.

"Hands for staying open? For working in daylight, if we must, and answering the Crown's law with ours?"

Eighteen hands went up, some slow, some fast, and Wren looked across them and understood that the moment had happened already — that somewhere between the notice on the wall and this dripping loft, the Gardeners had stopped being a secret and started being something else.

Something with a name the enemy had chosen, but a shape they would choose themselves.

"Then we stay," she said. "And we change how we work. From tonight, no one moves alone. No one keeps more than three names in memory — write nothing, trust the chalk. And we send word to every cell in the city: the hunt has begun, but so has the answer to it."

She stepped down, and the meeting dissolved into knots of murmuring purpose, and Maren appeared at her elbow with the particular expression of a woman about to say an unwelcome true thing.

"That speech was good," Maren said. "Now tell me the part you didn't say. The part where the Regent doesn't need to find us at all. He just needs to let the wards fail, and everything we're standing on stops existing — and no trial required."

Wren had indeed not said that part. She'd been not-saying it since dawn.

"I know," she said. "That's why staying isn't enough anymore. We're not protecting the network from the Crown. We're protecting the country from disappearing. Different war, Maren. Bigger one."

"And Theo?"

"Wren!" Tam's voice, sharp with alarm, from the window. "Soldiers on tannery row. Not patrolling — *counting doors.*"

* * *

They watched from behind the dyed cloth, twenty-two breaths held among twenty-two bolts of fabric, as six soldiers moved along tannery row with a ledger between two of them. Counting doors. Marking chimneys that smoked past curfew hours. One of them paused beneath the drainpipe where Wren's chalk sigil sat faded under grime, and scraped at it idly with his boot heel, and Wren's heart went into her mouth —

— but he was only scratching an itch, and moved on, and the column turned the corner toward the river.

"They're mapping," Maren breathed. "Not hunting. Mapping."

"Hunting starts with mapping." Wren watched the corner long after the soldiers were gone. "Hale taught me that, years ago, when she nearly caught me in the silk district. She never chased. She drew. Chased thieves escape; drawn thieves wake up surrounded."

"You sound almost fond of her."

"I sound *afraid* of her, which is the same thing wearing work clothes." Wren turned from the window. "Get everyone out through the cellar route. This house is mapped now, or will be by nightfall. And send runners — I want eyes on every Inquisitor post between here and the palace quarter. If the Regent means to burn the wards on purpose, I want to know before the fire does, not after."

Maren hesitated. "And you?"

"I'm going to see a woman who used to be our enemy," Wren said, "and ask her whose side she's actually on. Because if she hasn't decided by now, the Regent is going to decide for her, and I'd rather know which way she breaks before she does it."

* * *

Finding Hale was easier than it should have been, which itself was information.

Wren left a chalk mark on the third lamp-post of Candlemaker's Row — the old signal, *parley, alone, neutral ground* — a signal they'd exchanged exactly twice in five years, both times during disasters too large for either side to enjoy. Then she waited in the shrine alcove of Saint Ferrow-the-Forgotten, a niche so old even the Church had lost track of which saint it held, which made it the most neutral ground in the city.

Hale came at dusk, out of uniform, in a gray traveling coat that fooled no one. She stood at the mouth of the alcove a long moment, looking at the chalk mark on the lamp-post outside, then at Wren.

"You signaled me," she said. "In wartime."

"It's not war yet."

"No." Hale stepped into the alcove. Up close she looked like a woman who had been sleeping in her office, which Wren suspected was literal. "It's a hunt. Wars are between things that both exist. He's declared that your whole country doesn't, so legally speaking he's just tidying."

"You've read the purge papers." Not a question. Wren had heard it from Tam, secondhand through the prison grapevine: *the Inquisitor woman asked strange questions, quiet ones, like a person checking her own math.*

"I've read them." Hale's jaw tightened. "My family signed them. My grandfather's seal is on page four." She said it the way a person reports a death. "So yes. Here I am. Ask your questions, Sallow, before a patrol walks this row and we both discover what this truce costs."

"One question." Wren leaned against the saint's cold stone pedestal. "The wards failed for a minute last week. Whole streets flickered. People remembered things they weren't supposed to remember. The Regent's response wasn't repair — it was martial law, curfew, occupation. Why does a government answer a dying border with soldiers instead of stonemasons?"

Hale was silent long enough that a moth found its way into the alcove and circled the dead saint's head.

"Because the flickering is useful to him," she said at last. "I've seen the internal memoranda. Every district that blinks out of certainty becomes legally ambiguous. Ambiguous land reverts to the Crown under the Annexation Compact — the same papers my family signed. He doesn't want to save the borders, Sallow. He wants them to fail *on schedule*, neighborhood by neighborhood, so that when Vellamarch vanishes, it vanishes with paperwork in perfect order."

"And the people inside when it goes?"

Hale's face did something complicated and quickly put away. "The palace quarter blinked during the last guttering. Eleven people inside. The memorandum classified them as *territorial losses*, not deaths. There's no category for killing someone with geography, so they invented one."

Wren had imagined many versions of this conversation. She had not imagined Hale handing her the worst of it freely, like a coin tossed across a table.

"Why tell me?" Wren asked. "You're still wearing their coat, even if it's gray."

"Because I followed the chalk." Hale said it simply. "Your little green signs, all over my city. I decoded the grammar months ago. I could have given the Regent your sanctuary district in a sealed report, and I sat on it, because I needed to understand what I'd be destroying. And what I found was courts for crimes my own order refused to prosecute. A drowned child's killer brought to account. A widow defended. And underneath it all—" her voice dropped— "a bone-carver who hears the dead, and a dragon's ghost, and a prophecy that says your friend is a door. I have spent my life believing the law was the shape of justice. Your shadows taught me the law is sometimes just the shape of power wearing justice's clothes."

"So defect. Openly. Stand in the square and read the purge papers aloud."

"Not yet." Hale's hand went to her coat, and Wren tensed — but she drew out not a weapon but a slim packet wrapped in oilcloth. "When I break, I break *usefully*. There are eleven regional commanders who'll follow me if I can show them the annexation schedule — proof the Regent intends to let populated districts blink out. That proof sits in the palace strongroom, and I can't reach it without becoming visible too soon. So: not yet. Soon. Days."

Wren studied her. Everything in her training said *trap.* Five years of instinct said *the Inquisitor is baiting you.* But she had watched this woman hold a captured boy gently. Had heard, through Theo, the testimony of the dead about Hale's father — a magistrate ruined by the Gardeners' justice, yes, but also a man who had once hidden a witch from his own court.

"Days," Wren repeated. "Theo doesn't have days. Ember says the fire is down to embers. When it gutters fully, there's no city left to defect *in.*"

"Then you'd best make sure your bone-carver finds his hearth quickly." Hale turned to leave, then stopped at the alcove's edge, her back to Wren. "Sallow. One thing more. The Regent has posted a new bounty tonight. Not for you. Not for the network."

"For Theo."

"For Theo Amberdale, alive. Alive specifically." Hale glanced back, and in the lamplight her expression was bleak. "The Regent doesn't want him dead, Wren. He wants him *brought in.* Which means somewhere in that palace, someone has read the prophecy too — and believes the door works in both directions. Whatever the Regent intends to carry through that door, or keep out of it, he needs your bone-carver breathing."

She stepped out into the dark and was gone, gray coat swallowed by gray street, leaving Wren alone with a dead saint, a moth, and the cold spreading knowledge that the hunt was never for the Gardeners at all.

The Gardeners were the terrain. Theo was the prize.

Wren pressed her palm flat against the stone pedestal, thinking of Theo in the root-cellar below the old sanctuary, sitting with a dragon's bone in his hands like a man holding a lit fuse.

*Hold on,* she thought at him, uselessly, across the distance. *They're coming, and this time they know exactly what they're hunting.*

Then she went to raise the rebellion she'd never chosen, against a hunt that had already begun.
