## 27. Ember's Last Bright Hour

The fire went out at the corner of Tanner's Row and no one noticed for eleven seconds.

Theo counted them because he had nothing else to do with his hands. He was standing under the awning of a shuttered chandler's shop, watching the street through the gap in his scarf, when the world hiccuped. The cobblestones — real cobblestones, worn smooth by two hundred years of cart wheels that officially never rolled here — flickered like a candle behind cupped fingers. For eleven seconds the corner of Tanner's Row was not there. It was not rubble, either. It was nothing. A blankness with edges, the color of paper before ink.

Then the fire caught again, weak as a dying man's cough, and the cobbles swam back into being, and a milk cart rattled across them as if it hadn't just driven over the edge of existence.

The dead saw it first. They always did.

*It's going,* they murmured from the walls, from the drains, from the packed earth beneath his boots. *It's going, it's going, it's going.* A hundred voices braided into one low drone, like bees deciding on a direction. Theo had lived inside that drone for five years without truly hearing it, the way you stop hearing a river. Tonight he heard it. Tonight it sounded less like murmur and more like warning.

He pulled his collar up and walked toward the root-cellar.

Wren met him halfway, coming out of an alley with chalk dust still on her fingers. She'd been renewing the sigils — the little green marks that told Gardeners which doors were safe, which stairs were watched, which walls remembered kindness. He could tell by her face that she'd seen the flicker too.

"You felt it," she said. Not a question.

"The whole street blinked." His voice came out flat. "Eleven seconds. I counted."

"Twelve on Sallow Street." She said it like a confession. "My street. My name on it, if you can believe the joke the universe is making. Twelve seconds of nothing, Theo. A woman carrying bread stood in the middle of the blankness and didn't fall, didn't scream, didn't even look down. She just kept walking, like her feet knew something her eyes refused to."

"They don't see it," Theo said. "Most of them can't. The wards don't just hide Vellamarch from the outside. They hide its absence from the people inside."

"I know." Wren rubbed her palms against her coat, chalk ghosting off in pale streaks. "That's what frightens me. If the fire dies while everyone's still asleep to it—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. The arithmetic was simple and terrible: a country that stopped existing would take its people with it, quietly, completely, and no one outside would ever file a complaint, because no one outside would ever know there had been anyone to complain about.

They walked fast after that. The dead walked with them — not beside them, but through them, a pressure like walking upstream in a crowd. *Faster,* said a voice Theo recognized as the ferryman who'd drowned in the Cistern War, back when the Cistern War had a name. *Faster, bone-man. We are thin tonight. We are thin as smoke.*

By the time they reached the tenement that hid the root-cellar, the sky over Vellamarch had gone a color Theo had no word for. Not dusk. Dusk was honest. This was the sky of a place deciding whether to keep having a sky at all — bruised violet at the edges, and at the center, directly above the First Hearth district, a faint ruddy glow, like coals seen through a grate.

Ember-of-Ashes was awake. Theo could feel it before he reached the cellar door, a warmth rising through the floorboards that had nothing to do with any stove.

* * *

The dragon's bone lay on the trestle table where it always lay, a curve of rib long as a scythe, honey-colored and veined with light. But tonight the veins were wrong. Theo had spent weeks learning the bone's moods the way you learn a friend's breathing — steady gold when Ember spoke, deep amber when it listened, a slow pulse like a heartbeat when it dreamed. Tonight the light ran thin and fast, guttering, and between one pulse and the next it simply stopped, and the cellar went dark, and then it started again, grudgingly, like a man agreeing to stay alive for one more errand.

*You feel it,* Ember said. The voice filled the cellar the way cold fills a cellar — everywhere at once, patient, old. *Do not pretend otherwise, maker. You have been feeling it for three days.*

"The streets are blinking," Theo said. "Tanner's Row. Sallow Street. Half the Drowned Quarter went out during the low tide and came back smelling of nothing."

*I know. I am the smell of nothing. Or rather, I am what keeps the nothing away.* A pause, and in the pause Theo heard something he had never heard from the dragon before: effort. The sound of a will hauling against its own weight. *The fire was lit from my bones a hundred years ago. Every ward, every hidden border, every street that exists only because we all agree not to say otherwise — it burns on what is left of me. And what is left of me is very small now, Theo Amberdale. I have been spending myself for a century. There is almost nothing left to spend.*

Wren stood near the ladder, arms crossed tight, listening the way she listened to everything — like a woman taking inventory of what could be stolen or saved. "How long?" she asked. No softening, no ceremony. Just the question.

*A season,* Ember said. *Perhaps less. Perhaps much less. The fire does not die the way a lamp dies, Gardener. It dies the way a country dies — unevenly, in patches, some places holding stubbornly while others go dark without ceremony. You have already seen the first dark patches. There will be more. And then—*

"And then the Regent gets what she wants," Theo said.

The words came out of him before he'd decided to say them, and once they were out he couldn't take them back. Because that was the other thing, wasn't it. That was the thing sitting under everything like a stone under snow. The Regent didn't need to conquer Vellamarch. She only needed to wait. Let the fire gut itself out. Let the borders fail. Let the country slide off the maps entirely — and then annex the empty space, legally, cleanly, with signatures and seals, land belonging to no one becoming land belonging to her. The purge papers Hale had shown them proved the design was a century old. The outlawing of magic, the erasure from the treaties, the slow strangulation of the wards — none of it was accident. All of it was patience.

*Yes,* Ember said simply. *The Regent has waited a hundred years. Her grandmother waited. Her great-grandmother drew up the first papers. They have treated my fire as a bank account they intend to see emptied. And I—*

The dragon stopped. The light in the bone dimmed so far that Theo could barely make out Wren's face across the cellar. When Ember spoke again, the voice had changed — smaller, stripped of its vastness, almost human in its weariness.

*I am tired. I want you both to understand that. Not noble-tired. Not martyr-tired. Tired the way a dog is tired after guarding a house whose family moved away years ago. I have kept a border around people who forgot me. Who were made to forget me. Who sing songs about harvests in fields that exist because of me and do not know my name.* Another pause. *I let it gutter. Do you understand? Some nights, when the watching grew too heavy, I did not hold the flame as carefully as I might have. I let it slip. A flicker here. A dark minute there. I told myself it was rest. It may have been surrender.*

Theo looked at the bone. At the thin, fast, failing light.

"You're telling us this now?"

*I am telling you everything now, because soon there will be no later in which to tell it.* The light steadied, barely. *And because what happens next will require you to forgive me, and I would rather ask while I can still be heard.*

Before Theo could answer, the cellar shook.

Not an earthquake. Nothing so clean. It was more like the whole city took a breath and held it — a pressure change, a wrongness in the ears, the sensation of standing inside a bell the instant before it's struck. Dust sifted from the ceiling beams. The jar of pickled eggs on the shelf rang faintly against its neighbors. And through the stone, through the earth, through every wall of every house above them, Theo heard the dead cry out — hundreds of voices at once, not words, just alarm, the sound of ghosts whose ground had briefly ceased to be ground.

Then it passed. The cellar settled. The egg-jars went quiet.

"What was that?" Wren whispered.

"That," Theo said, "was a ward failing somewhere big."

*The palace quarter,* Ember said grimly. *The oldest wards. The ones laid down the night the country was first hidden. They have never flickered before. Tonight they flickered.* A beat. *Go up, children. Go up and walk the streets. What is about to happen in Vellamarch tonight is not mine to describe. It must be witnessed.*

* * *

They came up into a city remembering itself.

It began, as far as Theo could ever reconstruct afterward, at the fountain in Cooper's Square — a squat stone thing, dry for decades, that every map outside Vellamarch insisted did not exist. An old man was sitting on its rim eating chestnuts out of a paper cone, and he stopped mid-chew, and he looked at the fountain the way you look at a face in a crowd that you haven't seen since childhood. Then he said, clearly, to no one and everyone:

"This used to run with wine. Coronation night. My mother carried me on her shoulders and the wine ran red down the spouts and everyone sang the old song, the one about the garden under the hill."

A woman selling roasted corn turned to him. "There's no coronation song," she said automatically. And then she stopped, because something was surfacing in her too — Theo could see it happen, watch the memory rise through her like a fish rising through dark water. "Wait," she said. "Wait. *Under the hill, under the hill, the garden grows where the fire won't kill—*" She faltered on the third line. She could feel the shape of it, the melody, the way you can feel a word you've forgotten on the tip of your tongue. "I know that song. Why do I know that song? Nobody taught me that song."

But somebody had. A hundred years ago. Before the forgetting. And the memory had been waiting in her the whole time, folded up small, sealed under the wards — and now the wards were thinning, and the seal was cracking, and everything buried was coming up at once.

Theo walked through it and felt it in the dead first, because he always felt things in the dead first. The ghosts of Vellamarch had spent a century pressed flat, muted, half-themselves — the wards that hid the living had also thinned the dead, starved them, made them whispers instead of voices. Now, as the fire guttered, the whisper-ward failed in patches, and the dead came up roaring. He felt them pour back into their fullness like water returning to a dried riverbed. The drowned of the Cistern War. The plague-dead of the Fennel Street houses. The executed, dozens upon dozens of the executed, men and women hanged in the square for magic or for nothing at all, their names struck from every record, their bodies dumped in lime pits outside the walls — they rose up through the cobbles of Cooper's Square in a tide Theo could feel through the soles of his boots, and they were *loud*, they were furious, they were singing.

Because that was the thing. They weren't screaming. They were singing. The coronation song. The garden-under-the-hill song. The dead of Vellamarch, freed for one ragged hour from a century of enforced silence, opened their throats with the old verses, and the living heard it — not the words, not yet, but the hum of it, the way you hear a choir through a cathedral wall — and it woke them further.

By full dark, Cooper's Square was full.

Not a riot. Theo had seen riots; the Gardeners had started a few. Riots have a texture of panic, of breaking glass, of running feet. This was something else. This was thousands of people standing very still in the dark, looking at each other, saying things like *"I dreamed about a dragon"* and *"my grandmother used to hum something"* and *"why can't I remember my grandfather's funeral, why can't anyone remember it, was there a funeral?"* A boy of maybe nine was drawing on the cobbles with a stick — drawing, over and over, the same shape: a long neck, a curled tail, wings folded like a closed hand. His mother stood over him with her hand over her mouth. Neither of them knew why he was drawing it. Both of them knew it was important.

An old woman climbed the dry fountain and stood on its rim, and when she spoke her voice cracked but carried:

"They took it from us. They took it and told us it was never ours." She pointed at the crowd, at the tenements, at the dark bulk of the hills beyond the rooftops. "This is Vellamarch. Say it. Say the name."

Silence. Long enough that Theo's heart stumbled. The forgetting ran deep; the name itself had been sanded out of tongues, a word that stuck in the throat like a fishbone.

Then a dockworker near the front — a huge man, gray-stubbled, with rope scars on his palms — said it. Quietly. Testing it. "*Vellamarch.*"

And the square broke open.

Not into violence. Into weeping, mostly, and shouting, and strangers embracing strangers, and the old woman on the fountain laughing and crying at once, and somewhere in the middle of it all someone started the coronation song and got four lines in before losing the fifth, and the crowd picked it up and lost it at the same line, together, a thousand voices stumbling over the same missing verse — and Theo stood at the edge of it all with tears freezing on his cheeks, hearing underneath the living voices the dead ones, the *thousands* of dead ones, singing the verse perfectly, the whole song, beginning to end, the way it had been sung a hundred years ago under a sky that belonged to them.

*They remember us,* the dead said, and for once they weren't murmuring to Theo alone. They were saying it to the crowd, into the crowd, through the crowd. *They remember us. Light a candle. Light a candle for the garden under the hill.*

And they did. Someone produced candles — the chandler's shop, the shuttered one, unshuttered itself, its owner throwing open the doors and handing out tapers by the fistful, shouting prices that were really just numbers he'd invented so no one would guess it was free. Within the hour Cooper's Square burned with a hundred small flames, and the flames reflected off wet faces, and the glow reached up toward that bruised, undecided sky, and Theo thought: *this is what the fire is. This is what it was always made of. Not the dragon's bone alone. Us. Our agreement. Our memory. Our refusal to forget.*

And then the Inquisitors came.

* * *

They came quietly, which was worse than loudly. Six of them, then twelve, then more, moving along the edges of the square in their gray coats, faces impassive, batons holstered — for now. They didn't disperse the crowd. They *counted* it. Theo watched an officer with a ledger move along the rim of the gathering, marking faces, and he understood with a cold drop in his stomach that the Crown had been ready for this. Had planned for this. The wards were failing on schedule, or close enough to schedule, and the Regent's people had known that a night would come when the sleepers woke — and they had a procedure for it. Count them. Name them. And tomorrow, when the fear set in, when people wondered at themselves for weeping in the streets over a country that officially did not exist — tomorrow the lists would become summons, and the summons would become examples.

A woman next to Theo — young, a laundress by her hands — saw the ledger too. She saw it and something went through her that Theo felt like a physical wind, because the dead pressed close to her, closer than to anyone else in the square, and where the dead press close, Theo hears.

Her name was Maren. He learned it from her mother's ghost, who stood at her shoulder wearing the smock of a woman who'd died in the Fennel Street purge forty years ago, and who was saying, urgently, *Maren, my love, my girl, listen to me, your grandmother marched in the last night of the old country, she carried a candle exactly like yours, and they wrote her name in a book exactly like that one, and we never found her body—*

Maren couldn't hear her mother. But she heard something, because she straightened up, and she turned to face the ledger-officer, and she lifted her candle high, higher, until the flame stood against the dark like a small defiant star.

"My grandmother's name was Odile Vay," she said, loud enough for the front rows to hear. "Say it back to me. Somebody say it back."

And the square said it. *Odile Vay.* And another voice called another name, a grandfather, a hanged uncle, a midwife erased from the guild rolls, and the names began to roll through the crowd like a tide, dozens of them, hundreds, people shouting the names of the forgotten into the faces of the men with the ledgers, daring them to write *those* down, daring them to arrest a whole city for remembering.

The Inquisitors withdrew to the square's edges and stood there, gray and silent, holding their books, holding their patience.

Theo slipped away before the counting reached his corner. Wren found him two streets over, breathless, chalk-white, alive with a ferocity he'd never quite seen in her before.

"It's happening," she said. "All of it, all at once. The Gardeners' safehouses are lighting candles in every window — every window, Theo, we'll be visible from the hills. Half the network says douse them. Half says light more." She grabbed his sleeve. "Ember. How much longer?"

"I don't know. Weeks. Days. The palace wards already flickered once."

"Then whatever we're going to be, we have to be it fast." She looked back toward the square, toward the forest of candle-flames, and her voice dropped. "Because the Regent won't wait for the fire to die on its own anymore. Not now. Not with ten thousand people singing in the streets. She'll *help* it die. Tonight, if she has to."

Theo felt it before she finished saying it — felt it in the dead, in the sudden sharp spike of their alarm, in the way the ferryman's voice cut through the chorus like a knife: *The palace. Something at the palace. They are feeding the wards to the fire, bone-man, they are burning the wards, burning them backward—*

He turned toward the palace district, toward the distant glow above the First Hearth, and saw it: the ruddy light in the sky was changing. Growing. Flaring wrong — flaring like something being consumed rather than something burning steadily, a great orange pulse with black gaps in it, the light of a fire eating its own fuel.

Above the palace, the oldest wards in Vellamarch were being fed into the flame — burned deliberately, catastrophically, to force the end the Regent had waited a century for. And the districts those wards protected, the densest quarters, the quarters full of families who had lit candles tonight and sung the old song —

Those districts were starting to blink.

Theo grabbed Wren's arm. "The Hearth," he said. "We have to get to the Hearth. Now. Tonight. While there's still a fire left to save."

The dead, all of them, every voice he had ever carried, said it with him, one word, one roar:

*Run.*
