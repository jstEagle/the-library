## 30. Wren's Argument

Wren Sallow had learned to count losses the way other people counted coins — quickly, privately, and always with the fear that she'd miscounted somewhere along the line.

Her brother, first. Eighteen years old, hanged from the gallows on Fetterman Bridge for a theft he hadn't committed, while a Crown witness lied with his hand on a holy book and the magistrate nodded along like a man agreeing with the weather. Wren had been fifteen. She had stood in the crowd with her mother's hand crushing hers and watched the rope do its work, and something in her had gone out through the soles of her boots and never come back.

Her mother, second. Not to the Crown — to silence. She stopped speaking in the year after the hanging, then stopped eating in the year after that, and by the third winter there was only a grave in the paupers' row behind Saint Aldhelm's and a name Wren couldn't say aloud without tasting iron.

The Gardeners, after that. One by one, over nine years: Ferro taken in a raid on the dye-works safehouse. Maren, drowned running the cisterns with forged papers sewn into her coat. Old Tamms, who died of nothing more dramatic than age but died in hiding because he couldn't risk a doctor who might ask questions. Wren had carved each of their names into the beam above the root-cellar door, and the beam was getting crowded, and every time she picked up the knife she told herself it would be the last name, and every time she was wrong.

And now Theo Amberdale stood at the mouth of the First Hearth's approach tunnel with dragon-fire dying in his eyes, talking about burning himself alive like a man discussing the price of bread.

No, Wren thought. Absolutely not. Not him too.

* * *

She found him an hour before dawn in the lamplit antechamber beneath the old granary, where the Gardeners had moved their last supplies and their last hopes. He was sitting on a crate of tallow candles, carving. Of course he was carving. The man carved when he was frightened, when he was grieving, when he was happy, when he couldn't sleep — bone shavings gathered around his boots like snow that refused to melt. In his lap lay a small figure, half-finished: a bird, maybe, or a hand holding something. Wren couldn't tell yet. With Theo you often couldn't tell until it was done, and sometimes not even then.

"You should be sleeping," she said.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep before executions." She said it lightly. It didn't land lightly. His knife paused against the bone.

"It's not an execution," Theo said. "It's a choice."

"That's what my brother said." The words came out sharper than she intended, and she watched them cut him, and some small ugly part of her was glad they did. "Right up on the scaffold. He said, *I choose to face this like a man.* And the rope took him anyway, and the choice didn't matter at all."

Theo set down the knife. He looked at her the way he always looked at her — steady, patient, infuriatingly kind, as if her anger were weather he'd decided to stand in rather than run from. "Wren—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't do the voice. Don't be reasonable at me. I've spent all night being reasonable at myself and it hasn't worked, so I'm done with it."

He folded his hands and waited. That was worse, somehow. That was much worse.

"I've been talking to Ember," she said. "While you were with your mother's ghost, while you were — whatever you were doing in the orchard. I asked the dragon if there was another way. Any way. A substitute. A trick. A loophole in the prophecy, a back door in the fire, anything." She paced the length of the antechamber, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the way she did when the walls felt like they were closing. "Ember says no. Fine. Dragons don't lie, apparently, they just let countries die slowly for a century out of spite, which is its own kind of lie if you ask me. So there's no way through the fire except you. I accept that. I hate it, but I accept it."

"Then why—"

"Because there's another way out!" She spun on him. "Through the border! The wards are failing anyway — you saw the streets flicker, you *felt* it, half the palace quarter blinked out with people still inside. The border is full of holes now. Holes big enough to walk through. Hale has maps — real ones, from the purge papers, showing where the old boundary lines ran before Vellamarch was erased. We could be through it in two days. Out. Past the fire, past the Regent, past all of it. Somewhere Vellamarch doesn't exist, which means somewhere nobody can ask you to die for it."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, gently, "You want me to run."

"I want you to *live.*" Her voice cracked on the word and she hated herself for it. "Is that so monstrous? Is wanting one person alive such a crime?"

"No," he said. "It's not monstrous. It's the most human thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

"Then come with me. Both of us. Hale too, if she'll come, though I wouldn't bet on it. We walk out through the failing wards and we keep walking until Vellamarch is a story we tell badly, and we let the country solve its own problems for once." She heard how it sounded even as she said it — bitter, small, the voice of a woman who had buried too many people to believe in causes anymore. She kept going anyway. "I joined the Gardeners because the law abandoned us. I stayed because somebody had to answer for the ones the courts wouldn't touch. But I never signed up for this. I never signed up to watch the one person I—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "I can't carve your name on that beam, Theo. I'm telling you now, plainly, so you understand: if you go into that fire, I will not carve your name. I'll burn the whole beam first. I'll burn the cellar. I'll burn every record of every Gardener who ever lived, and I'll start with you."

It was a threat made entirely of grief, and they both knew it, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

Theo stood up. Bone shavings slid from his knees. He crossed the space between them and didn't touch her — he never touched her without asking, which was one of about four hundred reasons she loved him — but he stood close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him like a banked stove.

"Tell me about your brother," he said.

"Don't you dare use him against me."

"I'm not using him. I'm asking." His voice was soft. "You never talk about him. Nine years, Wren. You talk about everyone else's dead like you're reading a ledger — names, dates, injustices, all in order. But not him. Not once."

"Because it's none of the prophecy's business," she snapped. "Because he isn't a lesson."

"He's a person," Theo agreed. "That's exactly why I'm asking."

And that was the thing about Theo Amberdale, the thing that had undone her from nearly the first week: he treated the dead as people, which meant he treated the living as people too, which meant he stood there asking questions no one had ever bothered to ask her, and waited, and waited, and eventually she answered, because refusing him felt like refusing her brother all over again.

So she sat down on the candle crate beside his work, and she talked.

* * *

His name had been Aldous. Everyone called him Doss. He was eighteen and funny and careless with money and careful with people, the exact reverse of Wren, and he used to carry her on his shoulders through the Drowned Quarter when the floods came, shouting that they were pirates, that the water was the sea, that they'd sail to real countries where nobody hung boys for stealing bread they hadn't stolen.

He'd stolen nothing. The witness — a man named Corvel, a debt-collector with a grudge and a grudge's memory — swore he'd seen Doss lift a purse from a merchant's coat. The purse was never found. The merchant later recanted, quietly, to a clerk who filed the statement somewhere it would never be read. The magistrate, whose brother-in-law owed Corvel money, declined to hear new evidence. The hanging went ahead on schedule, because schedules mattered, because order mattered, because a country that had been erased from every map needed desperately to believe it still had laws.

"Afterward," Wren said, staring at her hands, "Corvel got promoted. Did you know that? The witness who lied got promoted. He runs collections for the Regent's household now. He has a house on Lantern Row and a wife and two daughters, and every year on the anniversary I stand outside that house and I think about what the Gardeners would do to him, and every year I decide not to, because Doss wouldn't have wanted it, because Doss was the gentle one, because—" Her breath hitched. "Because if I kill him, I become the reason someone else joins the Gardeners. And the beam gets another name. And it never stops. That's the trick of it, Theo. That's the whole trick. Grief makes executioners of everyone eventually, unless somebody breaks the chain."

"And you think going into the fire is breaking the chain."

"I think going into the fire is *you*, chained to the front of it," she said. "A sacrifice isn't a break. A sacrifice is just grief with better publicity."

Theo laughed — a short, startled sound, surprised out of him. "Grief with better publicity. Ember would steal that, if dragons stole things."

"Let the dragon steal it. Let the dragon carry the whole prophecy off into the dark and lose it." She turned to face him fully, and made herself say the true thing, the naked thing, the thing she'd carried since the orchard, since the moment she understood what the backward reading meant. "I love you. I don't know when it happened. Somewhere between the root-cellar and the cisterns, somewhere between you holding that drowned boy's ghost like he was made of glass and you standing in the market square shaking with fury on behalf of people you'd never met. I love you, and I have lost everyone I have ever loved to this country — to its lies, its ropes, its erasures, its fires — and I am *begging* you, Theo Amberdale, not to make me lose you to it too. Not to make this country the thing that took the last of them. Come away. Let it gutter. Let it blink out. Countries die. People shouldn't have to."

The lamplight moved between them. Somewhere above, muffled through stone and earth, the city murmured — the marchers gathering, the candles being lit, ten thousand small flames preparing to walk on the Regent's hall. The Remembering, people were calling it. Wren could hear singing, faint and ragged, an old anthem that hadn't been legal to sing in a century.

Theo listened to it with his head tilted, the way he listened to ghosts.

"Do you hear that?" he asked.

"I hear it."

"That's what I'd be leaving." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and his voice was steady, and both of those things together broke her heart in a way that pure resolve never could. "Not the country on the maps. Not the Regent or the wards or the borders. *That.* Ten thousand people remembering they exist. Wren, I spent five years hiding in this city, deaf to everything but the dead, and I thought belonging was something that happened to other people. Then the Gardeners took me in. Then you took me in. And now the whole country is waking up and reaching for itself, and you're asking me to walk out through a hole in the border while it does."

"Yes," she whispered. "That's exactly what I'm asking. Because you reaching for it doesn't mean it reaches back. Because causes take and take and call it meaning. Because I have watched beautiful people feed themselves to beautiful ideas my entire life, and the ideas are still here, Theo, and the people aren't."

"The idea won't be here," he said quietly. "That's the point. If the fire dies, none of it is here. Not the anthem, not the marchers, not the Gardeners, not the wrongs we righted, not Doss's name spoken anywhere by anyone. It all goes out with the flame. There's no outside to flee to, love. There's just a hole in the world shaped like home, and whether we stand inside it or not."

"Don't call me that," she said, "if you're going to refuse me."

"I'm not refusing you." He took her hands. His palms were rough with carving scars and warm as living wood. "I'm asking you to come with me — not out through the border. To the Hearth. Tomorrow. Walk in with me. Stand beside me while I do it. Be the last voice I hear that isn't a ghost."

"That's not mercy, Theo. That's making me a witness."

"It's making you a promise." His grip tightened. "That I'm not choosing the cause over you. I'm choosing a world where you don't have to stand outside houses on anniversaries deciding not to become an executioner. I'm choosing the version of Vellamarch where a boy like Doss gets a court that listens. I can't build it. I can only light it. You and Hale and the Gardeners — you're the ones who build. That's why it has to be me at the fire and you at my side, and not the other way around, and not neither of us. Somebody lights it. Somebody builds. That's the whole shape of the thing."

Wren pulled her hands free and stood, and walked away from him to the far wall, and pressed her forehead against the cold stone, and let the silence stretch until it groaned.

She wanted to scream at him. She wanted to shake him. She wanted, most of all, to be the kind of person who could accept this gracefully, and she discovered she was not that kind of person, and probably never had been.

"If I stand beside you," she said finally, without turning around, "and you die — because it might not work, Theo, you know it might not work, the prophecy might be wrong, Ember might be wrong, the whole backward reading might be a scholar's pretty fantasy — if you die for nothing, I will spend the rest of my life knowing I stood there and let it happen. Do you understand what you're asking? You're asking me to hold the door open for the thing that kills you."

"I'm asking you to trust me," he said, "one more time, one further than feels survivable. The way you trusted the Gardeners. The way you trusted me with the drowned quarter. The way Doss trusted the world, right up until it failed him." A pause. "The world failed him. I know. And I can't fix that, and I won't pretend the fire fixes it either. But tomorrow, ten thousand people are marching on the Regent's hall carrying candles and carved bones, and Hale is going to read the purge papers aloud in the open square, and whether the fire catches or not — *whether I live or not* — that country is already coming back. The remembering has started. It doesn't stop when the person who started it stops. That's the difference between a sacrifice and a hanging, Wren. A hanging ends a life. This begins a country."

She turned around. He was still sitting on the candle crate, small and ordinary and stubborn, bone shavings at his feet, the half-carved bird in his lap. Not a hero. Not a prophet. Just a maker who carried the dead, about to set down his burden forever.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When it's done — if it works — you don't get to disappear into the hollow. I've seen what you look like when the voices leave; I watched you after the first guttering. If you survive and you wake up deaf to the dead and empty as a scraped bowl, you don't get to drift off alone somewhere to spare us the trouble. You come to me. You let me sit with you in the silence. However long it takes. However ugly it gets. Those are my terms."

Theo looked at her for a long moment, and then he smiled — tired, crooked, real.

"Those are acceptable terms," he said.

"And one more." Her voice steadied. "If it doesn't work — if the fire takes you and stays dark — I'm finishing it. My way. Whatever that costs. Promise me you won't haunt me about it."

"Wren—"

"*Promise me.*"

"I promise," he said, "that whatever happens, you won't be alone in it. That's the only promise I can actually keep. The other one belongs to tomorrow."

Above them, the singing grew louder. Dawn was coming, and with it the march, and with it the Hearth, and with it the hour when every argument would end and only the act would remain. Wren crossed the room and sat down beside him again, shoulder to shoulder, and picked up his knife, and finished the carving in his lap — a wren, it turned out, wings lifted, mid-flight.

"For luck," she said, and pressed it into his hand, and did not let herself think about the fact that it was also, possibly, the last thing his hands would ever make.

Far off toward the palace quarter, a bell began to ring — and then another, and another, waking street by street, until the whole hidden country was ringing like a struck flame.
