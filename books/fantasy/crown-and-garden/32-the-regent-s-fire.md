## 32. The Regent's Fire

The first thing to go was the smell of bread.

Theo stood at the window of the safehouse on Tanner's Row, watching the palace quarter across the river — the gold dome of the Regent's hall, the bell towers, the crooked rooftops where the wealthy had built their houses too close together for centuries and liked it that way. It was evening. Somewhere in those streets, ovens were cooling after the day's baking, and the wind should have carried yeast and woodsmoke over the water the way it always did.

The wind came, but empty.

"Wren," he said.

She was at the table, sharpening a knife she hadn't stopped sharpening since the evacuation three days ago. The Gardeners had scattered to eleven safehouses across the invisible districts, and Wren had spent every hour since mapping routes, counting heads, arguing with Theo about whether Ember's bone could even be moved without cracking it. She looked up at his voice and knew immediately. She always did now. "What?"

"The palace quarter just got quieter."

"Quieter how?"

He pressed a hand flat against the glass. Behind him, in the corners of the room, the dead stirred like dogs lifting their heads before a storm. He'd been living inside their murmur for so long he'd stopped hearing it as sound — it was more like weather, a pressure against the inside of his skull. Now the pressure was changing. Draining.

"It's not quiet," he said slowly, trying to find the words. "It's *thin*. Like someone opened a door at the far end of a house I'm standing in."

Wren put down the knife.

* * *

Ember felt it before either of them could say more. The dragon-bone lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards of the pantry — they'd moved her twice since the root-cellar fell, and each move felt like carrying a sleeping elder who might wake furious or might not wake at all. Now the boards themselves seemed to warm under Theo's bare feet as he crossed the kitchen, and when he knelt and pulled them up, the bone was humming.

Not singing. Humming the way a wire hums before it snaps.

*"He's burning them."* Ember's voice filled the cellar-dark of the pantry, vast and cracked and afraid in a way Theo had never heard from her. A dragon, afraid. *"Do you understand what I'm telling you, maker? The wards on the palace quarter are the oldest in Vellamarch. They were lit from the First Hearth itself, the day the country was hidden. And he is feeding them to the annexation ledgers one by one."*

"Why would he burn his own quarter?" Wren crouched at the edge of the floorboard hole. "Half the court lives there. His own people—"

*"Because dead men cannot contest a signature."* Ember's hum deepened. *"If the palace quarter blinks out tonight — if four thousand souls simply cease to be anywhere the world can find them — then by morning there will be no witnesses left inside the old borders who matter. The Regent will sign the annexation over an absence and call it mercy. He'll say the country died of its own sickness. That he buried it gently."*

Theo's hands had gone cold on the bone. "Can he do that? Burn wards that big?"

*"A ward is only fire and agreement,"* Ember said. *"He has the fire. And the agreement — well. He has spent thirty years making certain no one at court remembers enough to disagree."*

Outside, the bells began to ring. Not the hour — it was half past none. Alarm bells, ragged and overlapping, from the towers along the river. Then, fainter, from towers further away, towers that shouldn't have been audible from Tanner's Row at all except that the city's hidden geometry was flexing like something in pain.

Wren was already moving. "Get your coat. We need eyes on the river before—"

The lights went out.

Not all of them. Not like a storm. It was worse than that, because it happened street by street, block by block, in a pattern that meant nothing and therefore meant everything — lamplight guttering along the Embankment, then the whole eastern face of the palace quarter going dark as if a hand had passed over it, then dark again, then *there*, flickering back like a candle flame deciding whether to exist.

And with the light went everything else. For one full second, looking across the river, Theo saw not the palace quarter but a smear of nothing — not darkness, because darkness has shape, but an absence with the exact outline of forty streets. His eyes slid off it. His mind refused to hold it. It was like trying to look at the space behind his own head.

Then the light stuttered back and the streets were there again, and people were screaming, and Theo understood that some of them had spent that second nowhere at all.

"They're blinking," he said. His voice sounded wrong to him, flattened, like a man reporting casualties. "Whole blocks, Wren. In and out. Like—" He couldn't finish the sentence, because the comparison in his chest was too obscene: like a lamp running out of oil. Like a life running out.

* * *

They ran for the river.

Tanner's Row emptied around them as they went — neighbors in doorways, faces upturned, an old woman holding a candle in her shaking hand as though light were a thing you could defend with. Someone was shouting that the stars had moved. Someone else was shouting that the stars had *stopped*. Theo didn't stop to learn which was true. The dead were streaming past him now, not murmuring but crying out, ghosts from streets already gutted pouring toward the river like water finding a crack, and where they passed him their voices broke over him in fragments—

*—the oven was still warm, I was reaching for the loaves, why is my hand going through the—*

*—my daughter, my daughter is at her aunt's on Candle Street, is Candle Street still—*

*—I can't remember my own name, I can't, it went when the street went, tell me my name, somebody tell me my—*

Theo grabbed at that last one as it went by, seized the ghost the way you'd grab a drowning stranger, and gave her the only thing he had. "Marta," he gasped, because her memory spilled into him as he touched her — Marta, Marta Hoskins, seamstress, sixty-one years, a red door she'd painted herself. "Your name is Marta Hoskins. You painted your door red. Hold onto it."

She held. Then the pull of whatever was dragging them took her too, and she streamed away toward the river, and Theo stood in the street with his heart slamming and understood what he'd just done. He'd given a ghost her name back while the country ate her street. It was like handing water to a man falling off a cliff.

"Theo!" Wren, twenty yards ahead, silhouetted against the stuttering glow of the burning quarter. "Move!"

They came out on the Embankment steps, and the river showed them everything.

The palace quarter was dying in patches. Whole avenues lit and unlit like a signal fire being smothered and relit by argument. On the far shore, tiny figures ran through streets that vanished beneath them mid-stride and reappeared behind them, so that runners seemed to flicker, to stutter, to be many ghosts of themselves at once. The great dome of the Regent's hall glowed with a light that had nothing to do with lamps — a sickly amber radiance bleeding up from its foundations, the last wards there being fed to the flames.

And above it all, where the smoke should have been, the sky was doing something Theo had never seen sky do. It was thinning. Stars showing through in daylight-impossible places, constellations sliding, the whole vault over the palace quarter rippling like the surface of a pond disturbed by a dropped stone.

"That's the border," Wren breathed beside him. "That's the actual border. I've lived my whole life inside this thing and I've never—" Her voice caught. "It looks so *thin*."

*"Because it is."* Ember's voice came not from the bone — Theo had left the bone hidden — but from inside his own skull, riding the same channel the dead used, and somehow that was worse, hearing a living-ancient thing speak through death's doorway. *"Maker. Listen to me. What you see is the fire failing everywhere at once. When the palace quarter goes, the collapse will cascade. Every ward draws on every other. You have until dawn. Perhaps less."*

"Then we run now," Theo said. "Tonight. To the Hearth."

*"You cannot reach the First Hearth through those streets — not while they're blinking. The route runs under the palace quarter itself. Under the Regent's hall. He chose his ground well, whether he knows it or not."*

Theo stared across the water. Four thousand people. Bakers and clerks and children and the old woman with the red door whose name was Marta Hoskins, all of them flickering at the edge of a nothing that had their exact shape.

And then the Regent's hall flared white.

* * *

Later, Theo would try to describe it and fail, and try again, and fail differently every time. The nearest he ever came was this:

Imagine a chord played on an instrument with a thousand strings. Now imagine every string cut at once.

The white flare rose from the hall's foundations and spread outward through the quarter's remaining wards — not gently, not as light, but as *unmaking*, a wavefront of pure erasure rolling down avenue after avenue, and everything it touched simply stopped agreeing to exist. Streets. Lamps. The bridge at Ferrier's Gate, mid-span, so that its far half hung in air attached to a place that wasn't there anymore. People.

Theo felt them die.

Not saw. Not sensed. *Felt* — four thousand voices cut off inside his skull in a single instant, and not silenced the way the dead go silent, which is a change of key, a settling, a shift from speaking to remembering. This was different. This was deletion. One moment the dead of the palace quarter were a roar in him, terror and prayer and a mother screaming her child's name and an old man calmly setting down his cup as the wall of nothing came up his street — and the next moment there was no next moment for any of them, no ghost, no echo, no residue, nothing for even a bone-carver who heard the dead to catch and carry.

They didn't become ghosts. Ghosts were what Vellamarch's dying let you keep. The Regent's fire didn't kill these people.

It unpersoned them.

Theo went to his knees on the Embankment stones and screamed, and the scream came out of him torn and animal, because the silence where four thousand souls had been was the loudest thing he had ever heard. It rang in him. It had edges. He could feel the shape of the absence the way you feel the shape of a tooth that's been pulled — every missing voice a socket of nothing, and his mind crawling over each one, unable to stop, *there should be a baker here, there should be a girl here, there should be, there should be—*

Strong arms around him. Wren, on her knees too, holding him, her face wet, staring across the river at the smooth and terrible blankness where a quarter of the capital had stood since before anyone's grandmother was born. No ruins. No smoke. Ruins would have been evidence. There was only clean absence, and the river lapping at embankments that now bordered nothing, and above it the sky healing over like flesh closing a wound, seamless, starless, obscene.

"He erased them," Wren said. Her voice was dead calm, which Theo knew was the most dangerous voice she owned. "Four thousand people and he *erased* them, and tomorrow there won't be a single document saying they ever—"

She stopped. Because Theo had gone rigid in her arms.

"What?" she said. "Theo, what?"

He was listening. With everything he had, with his gift stretched out over the water like a net over an empty sea, he was listening for anything — a scrap, a splinter, one soul's worth of residue.

"There's one," he whispered.

"What?"

"One voice. Out of four thousand." His whole body shook. "One person in that quarter must have been standing somewhere the wards couldn't quite reach — a cellar, a boundary gap, I don't know — because everyone else is *gone*, Wren, gone gone, not even ghosts, but there's one. One thread left. And she's—" His face changed. "She's calling for help. She's calling for *anyone*. She says the streets around her are gone and the sky is wrong and she's standing in a little island of light and it's shrinking."

Wren gripped his shoulders. "Who? Who is she?"

Theo listened harder, and the answer arrived with such simple horror that for a moment he couldn't speak it.

"A clerk," he said. "Her name is Odile Ferran. She works in the annexation office." He looked up at Wren, and his eyes were streaming and burning at once. "Wren — she was carrying the purge papers' registry. She says she took them. She stole them tonight when the burning started, because her brother was one of the ones Hale's order disappeared two years ago, and she wanted proof before she ran." His voice cracked. "She's trapped in the middle of the erasure with the only surviving copy of the Crown's own confession, and the island she's standing on has maybe an hour of existence left in it."

Across the river, in the perfect blankness, a single point of candlelight burned — visible only because Theo's gift told him where to look, a pinprick of defiance in a wound the size of a neighborhood.

"And here's the worst part," Theo said, rising, wiping his face, his voice hardening into something he barely recognized as his own. "The only way to reach her runs through what's left of the palace quarter. Through streets that are coming apart by the minute. And underneath it — *underneath* the Regent's hall — is the road to the First Hearth."

Wren stood beside him. Down the Embankment, Gardeners were emerging from the dark — a dozen of them, then dozens, drawn by the bells and the silence and the impossible empty skyline, faces lit by the flicker of a country ending. Somewhere behind them, Hale would be seeing the same sky. Somewhere in the wreck of her old loyalty, she would be understanding, right now, exactly what she had served.

"We can't save the quarter," Wren said quietly. "It's gone. But we can save her, and we can save the papers, and we can reach the Hearth in the same night, through the same collapsing streets, because they're all in the same direction." She turned to him. In the stuttering light her face was iron and grief and resolve fused together. "How fast can you move?"

Theo looked at the single candle across the water. Around his feet, the dead of every district in Vellamarch were gathering — called by the great silence, crowding close, thousands upon thousands of voices pressing in against the erasure, and for the first time in five years of hiding, he raised his gift like a banner and spoke aloud to all of them at once.

"Show me the way through," he said. "Every ghost in Vellamarch — show me the way through."

And the dead, all of them, answered as one voice.
