## 33. Into the Hearth of the Country

The street ended.

Not blocked, not barricaded — *ended*, the way a sentence ends when the writer's hand slips off the page. Cobbles gave way to raw grey nothing, smooth as river stone and just as featureless, and beyond it, where the tannery district should have been, there was only a pale haze that hurt to look at directly, like snow-blindness in summer.

Theo skidded to a halt at the edge of it, Wren barreling into his back, Hale a half-step behind them both with her sword drawn for no enemy she could name.

"That was the Tanners' Row," Wren said. Her voice came out flat, which was how Theo knew she was frightened. Wren Sallow frightened sounded like arithmetic. "That was a whole street with four hundred people in it."

"It's not gone," Theo said. He could feel it — or he could have felt it, once, the dead who'd lived there, but his gift was guttering along with everything else, voices arriving in stutters now, syllables torn out of sentences. "It's *unremembered*. There's a difference. The difference is we can get it back."

"Then run faster," Hale said.

They ran.

* * *

Ember had told him where the First Hearth lay, three nights ago, in the last long conversation they would ever have whole: beneath the old Assize Hall, before the Crown built its courthouse on top of it, because the Crown had always been good at building on top of things. A century ago a dragon had breathed its fire into a stone basin in a cellar, and a country had lit itself from that flame like a candle from a hearth, and a century later the same country was going out room by room while its conquerors waited with fresh ink and blank maps.

"The Hearth will take you," Ember had said. Its voice had been thin by then, smoke more than fire. "But the way is through the old streets, and the old streets are the first to go. When a place forgets itself, carver, it forgets from the edges inward. The center holds longest. Run for the center."

So they ran for the center, and the city dissolved around them as they went.

It wasn't chaos. That was the worst part. It was orderly, the way rot is orderly, the way a book falls apart page by page rather than all at once. A lane would simply stop being mid-stride, and they'd find themselves running across open grey, and then the lane would remember itself again fifty yards on and they'd stumble back onto cobbles as if waking from a dream of falling. Shopfronts flickered between painted wood and bare suggestion. A fountain appeared and vanished and appeared, water arcing in impossible stillness. Somewhere a church bell rang, except the bell tower it rang from was three streets over and also, briefly, inside Theo's own chest.

And the people — the people were awake now, all of Vellamarch awake since the Regent burned the palace quarter, and they poured into the streets with candles and carved bones and the wild-eyed look of a country remembering its own name. They didn't flee the dissolving districts. They ran *toward* them, holding their little flames up like lanterns, shouting street names and family names and the names of shops and saints and songs, as if enough voices could hold a neighborhood together by sheer insistence.

Maybe they could. Maybe that was what memory was. Theo didn't have time to wonder.

"The cisterns are flooded," Wren gasped as they cut down Cooper's Lane — or what was currently Cooper's Lane; it kept changing its mind about being an alley. "Gardeners used them for the prison break. We can go under. The tunnels are older than the wards — older than the erasure. The Crown never mapped them because the Crown never knew they existed."

"Underground," Hale said. She said it the way other people said *ambush*.

"You have a better route, Inquisitor?"

Hale looked back the way they'd come, at the haze where the tanneries had been, at the crowd behind them pressing forward with their candles, at the sky, which had begun doing something wrong — a bruised, uncertain color, like the light couldn't decide what time of day it was because the day itself wasn't sure it existed.

"No," she said. "Lead on."

* * *

The cistern mouth gaped behind a collapsed washhouse, black water lipping at the stone steps. The smell hit them first — standing water, mold, and underneath it something older, mineral and cold, the smell of the city's bones.

Wren went down first, chalk stub already in her hand, drawing sigils on the wet walls as they descended so the tunnel would know they belonged to it. The Gardeners' marks glowed faint green and faded, green and faded, a heartbeat made visible.

Theo felt the dead before he heard them. Down here the drowned prisoners lingered, the ones who'd guided the break-out weeks ago, and their voices came to him in fragments, in pieces, a choir with half its singers missing:

*—left at the third junction—*

*—mind the low arch, mind the—*

*—is someone singing? I haven't heard—*

"Third junction," Theo said aloud. "Left. Mind the low arch."

Hale stared at him. Even now, even after the purge papers, even after the cistern truce, hearing him do it stopped her cold for half a second. Then she nodded and filed it away, that Inquisitor discipline, that terrible useful competence, and took point with her sword because the dark ahead might hold Regent's men or might hold nothing at all and she'd decided she'd rather be ready for both.

The water rose past their knees, then their thighs. The tunnel groaned overhead — not settling, Theo realized. *Unsettling.* The stone above them was part of Vellamarch too, and Vellamarch was forgetting downward now, the dissolution eating into the earth itself.

"How far?" Wren asked.

"Half a mile," Theo said. "Ember said the Hearth sits under the Assize Hall foundations. There's a stair from the old cistern gallery."

"And if the gallery's gone?"

"Then we're standing in the one place in Vellamarch that can't afford to be gone," Hale said, "and we make it un-gone." She said it dryly, but her hand shook on the hilt, and Theo saw that she'd noticed it shaking and hated it.

At the third junction the left passage was simply not there. Not flooded, not collapsed — absent, a wall of smooth grey where the tunnel should have continued, and beyond it, faintly, impossibly, they could see the continuation of the passage on the other side, ten feet of nothing between here and there.

The dead pressed close. *—through, you have to go through, the middle remembers longest, walk the middle—*

"Wren," Theo said. "Your chalk."

She understood before he finished. She waded forward and drew on the grey nothing itself — the Gardener's mark, the open gate, the sigil that meant *this door belongs to everyone* — and the chalk caught, actually caught, green light sinking into the blankness like water into parched ground.

"Memory needs a keeper," Wren whispered. "That's the whole doctrine. That's why we exist. Somebody has to keep writing it down."

She stepped through. The nothing held her the way a held breath holds, and then she was on the far side, soaked and grinning the fierce grin she got when the world did something impossible and she refused to be impressed.

"Come on, then," she said. "Both of you. Country's waiting."

* * *

They climbed the old stair with water streaming off them, and came up through a broken grate into the undercroft of the Assize Hall, and Theo stopped breathing.

He'd expected ruin. What he found was a cathedral.

The cellar vault soared higher than any cellar had a right to, ribbed in pale stone that predated every building above it, and every inch of it was carved. Not ornament — *record*. Names, thousands upon thousands of names, spiraling up the pillars and across the vaults in letters that shifted between scripts, between languages, between centuries. The founders of Vellamarch had carved their dead into the walls of the Hearth, and the dead had been keeping the ceiling up ever since.

And at the center, sunk into the floor, ringed by a low balustrade of black iron: the basin.

It was the size of a millwheel, a shallow bowl of veined grey stone, and it was nearly dark. What fire remained in it was a skin of orange light across the bottom, trembling, thinner than paper, and with each tremor the whole vault dimmed and the names on the walls flickered like reflections on disturbed water.

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, the city screamed and sang and remembered.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, it's beautiful. It's dying and it's *beautiful*."

"It's not dying yet." Theo crossed to the basin. His legs felt borrowed. This was it — the place from the prophecy, the place from the ash, the figure with the carved bones standing at the rim of a fire. He'd seen this room in burning pages for weeks. Standing in it was like walking into a mirror. "It's waiting."

"For you," Hale said. Not a question. She'd read the papers too. She knew what the backward prophecy required, or she thought she did, and Theo watched her arrive at the edge of the knowledge and flinch away from it. "Theo. The records said the relighting needed a maker's bone-memory. They didn't say—"

"They didn't say whose. I know." He put his hands on the iron rail. It was warm. Everything else in the world was going cold and blank and the rail was warm, and he understood suddenly that the warmth was Ember, what remained of Ember, pooled in the bottom of the bowl like the last coal in a banked stove. "I know exactly whose."

"Don't." Wren's voice cracked across the vault. She'd been circling the basin, reading the walls, cataloguing, doing what she always did with unbearable things — turning them into information — and now she stopped and turned and all the arithmetic was gone out of her face. "Don't say it. Don't stand there looking like you've already decided. We find another way. There's always another way, that's the whole point of the Gardeners, that's the whole point of *me*—"

"Wren."

"There's your mother's grave, there's the ghost market, there's Ember himself, there has to be—"

"*Wren.*"

She stopped. In the failing light her eyes were bright and furious and terrified, and Theo loved her so much it felt like another kind of burning.

"Not yet," he said gently. "We're not there yet. Right now we just have to keep this room alive until the march arrives. Can you do that? Keep the room alive?"

She wiped her face with a soaked sleeve, hard, once. "Yes," she said. "Yes. Fine. Give me chalk."

* * *

They held the undercroft for two hours, and it was the strangest work any of them had ever done.

Wren chalked the walls in great looping lines, reinforcing the oldest names, tracing the faded ones until they brightened. Hale stood guard at the stairhead, and twice turned away Crown soldiers who came down following the same maps she'd once carried — talked them out with pure Inquisitor authority, told them the cellar was structurally unsound, told them the truth in a tone that made it sound like a lie, and they saluted and withdrew, and Hale stood there afterward shaking.

And Theo knelt at the basin and fed it.

He didn't know how he knew to do it. He just knew, the way he'd known the dead's grievances all those years in the bone-shop, the way the gift had always been less a power than a listening. He laid his hands on the warm stone and he *gave* it memories — small ones, safe ones, the smell of his mother's workshop, the weight of his first finished charm, the sound of rain on the tenement roof the night Wren first pulled him through the alleys. Each memory sank into the fire and the fire brightened a fraction, and the names on the walls steadied, and somewhere overhead a street that had gone grey remembered itself again.

Small payments. He could feel, with perfect clarity, the shape of the large payment waiting. But the fire accepted small ones, for now, and he paid them gladly, and the room held.

The city held.

Through the vault ceiling they heard it happening — the march arriving, thousands strong, candles and carved bones and Hale's own voice ringing off the stones above as she'd read the purge papers in the square, though that had been hours ago and her voice had surely given out by now. The Regent's men had stopped coming down the stairs. Something larger was happening up there. Something was ending or beginning, and the fire in the basin trembled between heartbeats like a creature listening for its name.

Then the ceiling cracked.

Not stone — *existence*. A seam of grey opened across the vault directly above the basin, wide as a road, and through it they could see not sky but absence, and the temperature dropped, and every name on every wall began to gutter at once.

"No," Wren breathed. "No, no, we're at the *center*, the center holds—"

"The palace quarter," Hale said, white-faced. "The Regent said he'd burn the last wards. He's doing it now. He's pulling the whole country down around us to force the annexation before the march reaches him."

The grey seam widened. Chunks of nothing fell into it and were gone. And through the crack, faint and fraying, Theo heard Ember's voice for the last time whole — not in his ears, in the fire, in the basin under his palms, the dragon spending its final strength on three words:

*"Now, carver. NOW."*

Theo looked at the fire. He looked at Wren, who was already reaching for him, already saying his name in the voice she'd used for her brother. He looked at his own hands, maker's hands, hands full of every voice he'd ever loved.

He put his palms flat on the stone and began, at last, to listen to what the fire was asking for.

It asked for everything.
