## 34. Ember's Choice

The First Hearth was not what Theo had imagined.

He had pictured something grand — a temple, maybe, or a vaulted chamber of black stone where the country's first fire had been kindled with ceremony and kings watching. What he found instead was a room the size of a modest kitchen, sunk beneath the roots of an oak so old its trunk had grown through three floors of forgotten masonry above. The walls were rough-hewn. The floor was packed earth. And in the center, set into a basin of dragon-bone worn smooth by a century of burning, was the Hearth itself.

It held a flame no larger than a cupped hand.

That was all that remained of Vellamarch's borders. That little gold tongue of light, trembling in air that had gone cold around it, guttering like a candle in a dying man's grip.

"We ran through half a collapsing city for *that*," Wren said. She stood in the doorway behind him, chalk dust still on her fingers from the sigils she'd thrown at their backs the whole way down. Somewhere above them, streets were blinking out of existence — Theo could feel it now without even trying, the dead of the palace quarter still screaming in the space where the Regent's wards had burned them through. He tried not to feel it. He failed.

"That's how fires go," Ember said. "Not with a roar. With a whisper."

The dragon's bone lay across Theo's shoulders — he had carried it out of the root-cellar himself, refused to let anyone else touch it, and it was warm against his neck the way a sleeping animal is warm. But Ember did not speak from the bone anymore. The ghost stood in the middle of the room, or occupied the middle of the room the way heat occupies a hearth: a shape of deep red and old gold, vast even folded down small, wings drawn tight like a mourner's hands. Hale stood pressed against the far wall with her Inquisitor's coat scorched and her face pale, and she was looking directly at the dragon, which meant she could see it, which meant — Theo noted distantly, filing the thought away for a self that had time to be astonished — the dead spoke to her too after all.

"The Hearth takes a living flame," Ember said. "You know this. I told you the price on the road, and I will not soften it now. But before you step forward, Theo Amberdale, there is one thing left I must offer."

Theo's mouth had gone dry somewhere around the third collapsed bridge. "If it's another riddle—"

"It is myself."

The word filled the small room the way smoke fills a chimney. Wren went still beside the door. Even the little flame seemed to lean toward the dragon's voice.

"I am the last of my kind," Ember said. "I have been dead for a hundred years, and I have spent those hundred years keeping a country alive out of spite and grief and — if I am honest, and I promised myself honesty at the end — habit. My memory is vast. It holds every border I ever burned, every treaty I ever witnessed, every name of every soul who swore to me in the First Oath. A ghost's memory is not nothing. It is nearly everything." The great head lowered, eyes like banked coals level with Theo's own. "So I offer it. Take my memory to the Hearth instead of yours. Let the fire consume what remains of me entirely, and let me be the fuel. You keep your bones. You keep your gift. You walk out of here still hearing your mother's voice."

For one moment — one whole, shining, treacherous moment — Theo wanted it so badly his knees nearly buckled.

He heard his mother's voice already, faint, from the direction of the stairwell, from everywhere and nowhere, the way the dead sounded when they gathered close to someone about to die. *My love. My love.* Five years of voices, ten thousand grievances and lullabies and confessions, and the dragon was offering to let him keep every one of them. All he had to do was say yes, and Ember-of-Ashes, last dragon of Vellamarch, would burn away into nothing in his place.

"Yes," Wren said immediately. "Say yes."

"Wren—"

"Say *yes*, Theo." She crossed the room in three strides and grabbed his sleeve, and her hands were shaking. He had seen her hold steady through a raid, through a drowning, through the moment the palace quarter blinked out and she'd had to choose which screaming street to run toward. Her hands were shaking now. "You don't owe this country your *self*. You don't owe a prophecy anything. If there's any way — any way at all —"

"There isn't," Theo said quietly.

And he knew it. He knew it the way he knew the weight of a carving knife in his hand, the way he knew grain and whorl and the difference between bone that would hold an edge and bone that would crumble. He looked at the little flame, and the flame looked back — or something behind it did, something older than the dragon, older than the oath, the thing the First Hearth actually was. Not magic. Not really. A promise, kept alight. And promises made by the living could only be kept by the living.

"Tell them," he said to Ember. "Tell them why it won't work. Because I can feel it in the room. Everyone here already knows but me."

The dragon was silent for a long moment. When the shape moved, it was slower now, heavier, as though offering the bargain had cost it something it could not get back.

"A ghost," Ember said, "is an echo. I have told you this. What stands before you is not a dragon; it is the shape a dragon's death left in the world, the way a bell's shape remains after the sound fades. My memory is real. My love for this country is real. But neither of them is *alive*." The coal-bright eyes closed. "The Hearth was lit by living makers who gave freely of their own bone-memory — the first Vellamarchers, who chose to bind their children's children to a hidden country rather than watch it be devoured by treaty and annexation. Living flame for living flame. Memory given while the giver can still feel the loss of it. That is the covenant. That has always been the covenant."

"And you gave up feeling loss," Theo said slowly, "a hundred years ago."

"I gave up everything a hundred years ago." The dragon's laugh was a dry rustle, like ash shifting in wind. "I cannot pay a price, Theo Amberdale. That is the one mercy of being dead and the one cruelty. Nothing more can be taken from me. And so nothing I give is worth anything to the fire. If you carry my memory to that basin, it will gutter, and flare, and gutter again, and then it will go out, and we will both have lost, and Vellamarch with us." The eyes opened. "I knew this when I offered. I offered anyway. Forgive an old coward her last wish to be useful."

Wren's grip on his sleeve loosened. She didn't let go.

Against the wall, Hale spoke for the first time since they'd descended. Her voice came out scraped raw. "There has to be another way. There's always — the purge papers, the treaties, if the Regent's claim collapses publicly, if the March of the Remembering holds the square long enough—"

"The march buys hours," Theo said. "Maybe days. The fire doesn't have days. You saw the streets upstairs, Inquisitor. Half of them aren't there anymore." He turned back to the Hearth. "How long?"

Ember considered. "Until dawn. Perhaps less."

Dawn. Outside, thousands of people were marching with candles and carved bones, waking up to a country they hadn't known they'd lost, and they had until dawn.

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. Theo let them, because arguing was easier than standing still, and standing still was easier than looking at the Hearth.

Hale laid it out like a ledger, because that was how she fought: the purge papers proved the outlawry had been staged, which meant the law itself was void, which meant there might be some thread of legitimacy — the old courts, the sealed charters, something — that could restore recognition without the fire. "Countries come back from erasure," she said. "Poland. Nameless places in a dozen histories. Recognition is paper, and paper can be re-signed—"

"Paper signed by whom?" Wren shot back. "The Crown? The same Crown that burned the palace quarter an hour ago with people inside it? You think they'll re-sign anything? They'll wait until the borders fail, annex the land, and call it reunification, and everyone who marched tonight gets a tax stamp instead of a trial."

"Then we take it to the other powers. The coastal leagues. The—"

"With what army? With what map?" Wren's voice cracked. "You're describing a decade of diplomacy, Hale. We have until *dawn*."

"And you're describing—" Hale stopped herself. Theo watched her do it: watched the Inquisitor catch her own fury by the collar and hold it. When she spoke again her voice was quieter. "I'm describing a world where he doesn't die."

"He doesn't die," Wren said. "That's the worst part. That's the part I can't—" She turned away, pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "He loses everything else."

Theo sat down on the packed earth with his back against the root of the old oak and let them fight over him for a while, because it was oddly restful, being fought over. Around him, faint as frost forming, the dead had gathered. His mother was closest. He could feel her the way you feel weather coming — a pressure, a tenderness, an ache in the scar tissue of the soul.

"You could tell me not to," he said aloud, to no one, to her. "You bound my gift once to protect me. You could bind it again. You could make me deaf right now, and then I couldn't hear the Hearth calling, and maybe I'd walk away."

The silence she answered with was the loudest thing in the room.

"Yeah," Theo said. "That's what I thought."

Because that was the truth underneath everything, the truth he'd been circling since the night he found his name inked under today's date in a book that should never have reached his shop: his mother had bound him once, and it had saved his life, and it had also cost him twenty years of knowing what he was. She had chosen his safety over his wholeness, and she had carried that choice like a stone, and in the end — in the orchard, over her grave — she had asked his forgiveness for it, and he had given it, and the giving had been the truest thing he'd ever done.

He would not ask her to carry that stone twice.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said. She had crouched in front of him at some point; he didn't know how long he'd been staring at the middle distance. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious. "The quiet thing. The deciding-quietly thing. Theo, no. Whatever you've decided, un-decide it. We find another way. We *make* another way. I didn't dig you out of a bone-shop and drag you through half of Vellamarch so you could—"

"So I could what? Wren. Say it. So I could what?"

She stared at him. Above them, muffled by earth and root and a century of forgetting, the city groaned — some district flickering, some street remembering and unremembering itself.

"So you could leave me," she said finally, very quietly. "Like Tam. Like everyone. That's what you're going to say, isn't it. That you have to. That it's the door." Her jaw tightened. "I've heard that speech before. I've buried that speech before."

"This isn't leaving." Theo reached up and took her hand. Her knuckles were split from the cistern grate; he held them as gently as he held fresh-carved bone. "Leaving is walking out through a failing border and starting over somewhere maps still reach, and forgetting all of this, and letting the country die behind me. This is the opposite of leaving. This is staying so hard that I stop existing as the person who stayed." He smiled, and it hurt, and he let her see that it hurt. "You told me once the Gardeners answer crimes the law ignores. Nobody wrote that law either, Wren. Somebody just decided somebody had to."

"That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same."

She pulled her hand free and stood, and turned her back on him, shoulders rigid — and then turned back around and crouched again and gripped the front of his shirt with both hands, hard, like a woman holding onto a ledge. "Then hear me. Hear me once. If you do this — when you do this — I am not going to let you become a ghost too. Do you understand me? You'll wake up hollow and deaf and you'll want to drift, and I will *not allow it*. You'll carve plain charms and plant stupid gardens and drink bad tea with me every single morning until the hollowness fills in or it doesn't, and either way you'll do it *here*, with me, in the country you bought. Swear it. Swear it on your mother."

Theo swallowed past the knot in his throat. "On my mother," he said. "I swear."

Behind them, Hale had stopped pretending to study the walls.

* * *

When the arguing finally burned down to embers of its own, Ember called Theo to the Hearth.

The dragon had shrunk as the night wore on — Theo could see it happening, the great shape dimming, drawing inward, spending itself the way the city above was spending itself. What remained was barely larger than a horse, and translucent at the edges, and beautiful the way the last light through a window is beautiful.

"One last thing," Ember said. "Kneel."

Theo knelt on the packed earth beside the bone basin. Up close, the little flame made no heat at all. It made something worse: recognition. It looked at him the way his mother's ghost looked at him. It knew him. It had been waiting for him for a hundred years, the way all the dead waited, patiently and without blame.

"When I offered myself," Ember said, "it was not entirely a coward's wish. Part of it was true. So hear the true part now, and weigh it against the rest." The dragon lowered its head until its brow nearly touched Theo's, and its breath — cool, smelling of woodsmoke and snow — washed over him. "A hundred years ago I watched the living makers feed this Hearth, and I watched what it cost them, and I swore then that no one should ever have to pay it again. Then the betrayals came, and the treaties, and the slow erasure, and I grew tired, Theo. Tired is the honest word. I tended the fire out of duty, and duty thinned, and I let it gutter. Year by year. Decade by decade. I told myself the country had abandoned me, and perhaps it had. But I was its guardian, and I let it fade, and then I wrote a prophecy naming a savior so that the fixing of my failure would be someone else's hands." The coal eyes held his. "I conscripted you with my despair. I know that. Wren told me off for it in words I will treasure into oblivion."

Despite everything — despite the little flame and the gathering dead and the hour left before dawn — Theo laughed. It came out broken, but it was a laugh.

"She does have a way with words."

"She threatened to exorcise me. I believed her." The dragon's shape rippled — the ghost's equivalent of a bow, or a flinch, or both. "So this is what I have left to give, and it is not memory and it is not fire. It is this: I ask your forgiveness, Theo Amberdale. Freely given or freely withheld, I ask it. Not because forgiveness will lighten your burden — it won't, nothing will — but because you should not climb into that fire carrying my guilt along with your own. Your bones are heavy enough. Set mine down."

Theo knelt there in the cold, in the small room under the old oak, with the last dragon of Vellamarch bowed before him asking pardon, and he thought about his mother in the bone orchard, and about Wren's brother on the scaffold, and about Hale's family name inked on the purge papers, and about the drowned boy in the Drowned Quarter who had shown him where justice lived when the courts wouldn't go. Everyone in this story had failed someone. Everyone had been failed. The country itself was built out of a promise that people had kept badly for a hundred years and were, tonight, in the streets above, trying with candles and bare hands to keep well at last.

"If I forgive you," Theo said, "will you do one thing for me?"

"Anything within a ghost's power."

"When I wake up on the other side of this — deaf, hollow, whatever I am — I won't remember your voice. I won't remember any of this conversation. So tell Wren. Tell her everything you just told me, and tell her the part about the cowardice twice, so she can remind me when I'm being noble about it." He managed another broken laugh. "And tell my mother I said the binding wasn't her fault either. She'll argue. Let her argue. She's good at it."

The dragon made a sound Theo had never heard from it — low, and cracking, and unmistakably grief, the grief of a creature a hundred years past being allowed to shed it.

"Forgiven," Theo said. "All of it. The tiredness most of all. Get up, Ember-of-Ashes. Guardians kneel for no one, not even at the end."

The great shape rose. Dimmer now. Nearly done.

* * *

There was nothing left after that except the doing of it, and the doing of it required only that Theo stand, and cross six feet of packed earth, and place his hands on the rim of the bone basin where a century of makers had placed theirs.

He took off his coat. Wren folded it over her arm without being asked, and did not cry, and was visibly spending her entire fortune of strength not crying, and he loved her so much for it that it almost changed his mind — and then he understood, with a calm that felt borrowed from somewhere outside himself, that it was supposed to almost change his mind. That was the nature of the door. It wasn't a threshold in a wall. It was the moment a person weighed everything they were against everything they loved and stepped toward the scale anyway.

His mother's voice gathered close, all the dead gathering close, a crowd of whispers pressing in like mourners at a bedside, and he let them, and he listened one last time — to grievances and lullabies, to the widow's husband thanking him for the charm, to the drowned boy humming his little tune, to ten thousand voices he had hoarded like a miser for five years of hiding — and then he opened his eyes and looked at the flame.

Small. Stubborn. Waiting.

"Theo." Hale's voice, from the doorway, stripped of everything official. "For what it's worth. Vellamarch remembers you. Whatever happens after — I'll make sure of it. It's the one thing I'm still qualified to do."

He nodded, not trusting his voice.

He reached down into himself, to the place where the gift lived — the place his mother had bound and unbound, the place where every dead voice had made its home, the marrow-deep archive of everything he had ever heard and carved and mourned — and he lifted it up, the way he'd lift a finished charm from the bench, and he held it out over the fire in his two steady carver's hands.

The flame leaned toward him like a hungry child.

Below the ground, beneath the roots, beneath the country, something ancient drew one long breath —

— and above them, through the earth, through the oak, through a hundred feet of flickering stone, the whole city of Vellamarch went dark.
