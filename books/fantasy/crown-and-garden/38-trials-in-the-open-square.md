## 38. Trials in the Open Square

The tribunal met in what had been a grain exchange, because Vellamarch's actual courthouses had been Regent's property, and the Regent's property was being picked apart like a carcass. Someone had hung a scale of justice above the door — iron, hastily welded, visibly crooked. Inquisitor Sera Hale looked at it every morning on her way in and thought: *crooked is honest, at least.* A straight scale would have been a lie.

She was not testifying today. Today she sat in the gallery with her hands folded, wearing civilian gray that felt like a costume, and waited for her name to come up anyway. It always came up. That was the arrangement.

On the floor below, the tribunal's three judges took their seats: a magistrate with ink-stained cuffs, a weaver who had spent thirty years keeping the tenements fed during the forgetting, and — this had caused riots of argument in the papers — a Gardener. Old Tammas Vey, root-cellar veteran, hands like knotted rope, who had once been sentenced to death in absentia by the very order Hale had served. Now he sat on a bench with a cushion and judged cases. He looked uncomfortable. He looked, Hale thought, exactly as uncomfortable as he should.

The morning's case was a drowning. Not the drowned quarter case — Theo Amberdale's old work, already settled, already legend — but a newer one, from the flood year, when the cisterns backed up and three children went into the water and only the water knew why. The mother stood at the rail and said her piece. The accused, a lock-keeper named Brann, said his. The evidence was thin. The evidence would always be thin, now; half the Crown's archives had burned or been sealed or simply never existed, because a country erased from maps keeps no records it wants found.

"Call the witness," the magistrate said, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms went quiet when everyone knew whose voice came next.

They brought the boy in through the side door. Dead eleven years, drowned at nine, and he walked to the witness rail like any child dragged unwilling to school — shoulders up, chin down, resentful of the attention. He could not be seen by most. But since the Relighting, since the borders blazed back into memory, the dead of Vellamarch had grown *legible*. A bone-charmer's craft, once outlawed, was now licensed, regulated, taxed. There were six certified speakers in the city. Hale had signed off on two of their certifications herself, in her new capacity, which still made her laugh at night in a hollow way.

The speaker — a nervous young woman named Odell who kept glancing at her license as if checking it hadn't expired mid-ceremony — closed her eyes, touched the rail, and gave them the boy.

"He says the lock-gate was chained," she reported. Her voice wavered. "He says he watched the chain go on. He says—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He says the man who chained it whistled while he worked."

Brann the lock-keeper did not whistle, as it happened, ever again. He confessed before the judges finished conferring. And that was justice, clean enough, and the mother wept, and the crowd outside cheered when the sentence came down, and Hale sat in the gallery feeling the shape of the thing they'd built — and finding, as she always found, the flaw in it.

Because the boy had been dead eleven years. Eleven years his mother had begged the courts, and the courts had said the water takes what the water takes. Eleven years, and the only reason anyone had asked the child was that the Gardeners had broken into a records house, stolen a ledger, threatened a clerk, and forced the question — extralegally, violently, without one shred of lawful process. The tribunal was judging Brann today on evidence obtained by crimes the tribunal itself had declined to pardon.

Old Tammas Vey caught her eye across the floor. He'd been watching her watch. He inclined his head slightly, the way men do when they share a weight.

*Afternoon,* his look said. *It's your turn in the afternoon.*

* * *

They broke for bread at midday, and Wren Sallow found her on the steps.

Wren looked better than she had a year ago. The siege months had carved her down to wire and anger; the trials were putting something back. She still wore her chalk-stained apron, though chalk sigils were legal now, taught openly in the rebuilt districts. Some habits outlive their necessity. Hale suspected the apron was armor.

"You're brooding," Wren said, sitting down uninvited. "I can tell. Your shoulders do a thing."

"My shoulders are fine."

"They're practically around your ears." She handed over half a loaf, tore off her own portion. Below them, the square seethed — market stalls edging back toward the tribunal steps, vendors sensing crowds, the eternal commerce that no government collapse had ever managed to interrupt. "You're thinking about Brann."

"I'm thinking about how we got him."

"We got him right."

"We got him right *by burglary and threat.* If a Inquisitor had done what your people did to get that ledger, you'd have marched on my office with torches."

Wren chewed. Considered. "Yes," she said finally. "Probably. And I'd have been right to, wouldn't I? That's the problem, isn't it. We were both right. That's why nobody sleeps."

Hale almost smiled. "You've been talking to Theo."

"Every day." Something softened in her face when she said it, quick and guarded, like a door opened a crack on warmth and shut again. "He says the same thing, only slower, and with more metaphors about bones. He says a country relit has to decide what it forgives, and if it decides too fast it'll forgive itself everything." She brushed crumbs from her apron. "He also says you should eat more. He can't hear the dead anymore but apparently he can still hear me worrying about you, which I do not appreciate him noticing."

"Tell him—"

"I'm not your messenger."

"—that his testimony tomorrow matters more than mine," Hale finished. "Mine is confession. His is witness. Confession is cheap. Anyone can hand over their sins. It takes nothing but a bad night's sleep."

"And his?"

Hale looked out at the square. At the crooked scale above the door. "His is testimony that the Gardeners' justice worked. Every crime they answered, every case the law refused — if he stands up there and condemns it, then everything we build condemns itself. If he defends it, we've learned nothing. Either way it costs him something real. That's not cheap."

Wren was quiet a long moment. "He knows. He stayed up all night carving about it. Little practice charms, no magic, just — working his hands through it. There's a pile of them on the table like wood shavings." She stood, dusted off her skirt. "Gallery seat next to me tomorrow?"

"Front row," Hale said. "I want to see his face when I say the parts about me."

* * *

The afternoon session was hers.

She had expected dread. What she felt instead, walking down to the floor under a hundred eyes, was a strange administrative calm — the same calm she'd felt signing warrant after warrant back when warrants meant erasure. Bodies learn rituals quickly. Hers had learned this one: approach the rail, state your name, begin.

"Sera Hale," she said. "Formerly First Inquisitor of the Vellamarch District, formerly of the Order of the Question, formerly—" her voice caught, barely, on the old title she'd rehearsed away and failed to, "—formerly the daughter of Aldous Hale, who signed the Purge Papers."

The magistrate let the murmur run its course. "Inquisitor Hale, you've submitted a written account. Why speak besides?"

"Because paper can be sealed again." She kept her eyes forward, on the judges, on the crooked scale beyond them visible through the high windows. "Paper got us into this. My father's signature was on parchment in a locked drawer for a hundred years, and while it slept there, a whole country forgot itself. I don't trust drawers anymore."

"Then speak."

So she spoke. For an hour, then another. She told them about the Order of the Question — not the sanitized version, the real one. How interrogations were staged to produce confessions the Crown needed rather than truths it feared. How the outlawing of magic had never been about magic at all, but about sovereignty — a country couldn't be annexed while its wards held, so the wards had to be made criminal, and their keepers with them. She named names. Superiors. Colleagues. The man who had ordered her family's humiliation by the Gardeners and then quietly promoted her past it, buying her loyalty with her own grievance. She watched that name land in the room like a stone in still water.

And then, because she had promised Wren honesty and promised herself more than that, she told them about the Gardeners too.

"I hunted them for five years," she said. "I nearly destroyed their sanctuary. I took a child prisoner — a girl of sixteen, who said nothing under questioning, not one word, while I did everything short of the worst things my order trained me to do. And I will tell you why she said nothing: because she was protecting people who had done, in my professional assessment, genuine wrongs." She turned then, deliberately, to face the gallery where Wren sat. "The Gardeners healed people the law abandoned. They also broke knees. They forged documents. They extorted merchants. They decided, street by street, who deserved punishment — and they were usually right, which is the most dangerous thing an unlawful power can be. Rightness without process is just tyranny with good aim."

Silence. Somewhere in the gallery, someone began to applaud and was shushed by three neighbors at once, because this was a tribunal and not a rally, and Vellamarch was learning the difference, badly, one day at a time.

"Do you repent?" the weaver-judge asked.

"I repent my part in the purge. I repent the girl I questioned." Hale's jaw tightened. "I do not repent hunting the Gardeners. I repent *how* I did it — the methods, yes. But someone should have been asking their questions. They answered crimes no one else would touch, and they answered them alone, and a country that lets that happen doesn't get to act surprised when vigilantes bloom in the dark it left. My repentance is that I was the dark."

Tammas Vey spoke for the first time all day, his gravel voice carrying from the judge's bench. "And now you sit in our gallery and judge us."

"No," Hale said. "Now I testify against my order and let you judge yourselves. Those are different weights. Mine I know how to carry."

* * *

Theo Amberdale testified the next morning.

Hale had seen him across squares and cellars and one terrible flooded cistern, and he had always seemed to her like a man made mostly of listening — quiet, angled toward sounds other people couldn't catch. He wasn't listening anymore. She noticed it immediately, the way you notice a missing limb in someone you knew whole. When the room rustled, he flinched at the sound like it was new to him. Everything was loud now, she supposed. Everything was the first time.

He stood at the rail in his plainest coat, and beside him on the rail he laid out — she realized with a jolt — carved bones. Small ones. Practice charms, dozens of them, the pile Wren had described. He arranged them in a row like evidence, which, she understood a moment later, was exactly what they were.

"I was the Gardeners' speaker," he began. No preamble. "For two years I heard the dead tell me where bodies were buried, literally and otherwise, and I passed what I heard to Wren Sallow's people, and they acted on it. I'm here to tell you what that justice looked like from the inside, and I want to say first that I loved it." A ripple through the room. He let it settle. "I loved it because for the first time in my life, my curse was useful, and usefulness felt like belonging. When you've been hunted for your gift, someone handing you a purpose feels like salvation. That's not an excuse. It's a warning. Recruitment always feels like rescue."

He picked up one of the bones. Turned it in his fingers.

"This is a drowning charm. I made it for a widow whose husband died in the cistern collapse — the courts ruled it accident, the Gardeners ruled otherwise, and the ruling was correct. The man was murdered. We proved it. The killer answered for it." He set it down. Picked up another. "This is a protection sigil I carved for a smuggler, because the Gardeners needed the smuggler's routes, and the price of his help was my craft. Was that justice? He used those routes to move stolen grain to starving districts during the flood year. Yes. Also he moved weapons, once, that ended up hurting people the Gardeners claimed to protect. Also yes. Both true. Both mine."

He looked up at the judges, and his voice didn't rise but somehow filled the whole exchange hall.

"You want to know whether to pardon the Gardeners or punish them, and I'm telling you the question is broken, because the answer isn't one thing. They saved lives the law wouldn't save. They took liberties no law should allow. They were right so often that they stopped checking whether they were right, and that — that moment when you stop checking — is the exact moment my old order was born. The Inquisition started as people answering cries no court would answer. I've read the founding charters. They read like your manifesto reads. Like love letters to justice." His hand rested flat on the row of bones. "The difference between the Inquisition and the Gardeners is twenty years and a habit of certainty. That's the whole distance. You're standing on one side of it now, deciding whether to walk."

Old Tammas Vey leaned forward on his cushion. "And what would you have us do, bone-carver? You who carried both sides' sins?"

Theo was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice had changed — smaller, and truer.

"I'd have you make them answer for each case, one at a time, forever. Never a blanket pardon. Never a blanket condemnation. Justice that comes in bulk isn't justice, it's accounting." He gestured at the bones. "I made these with my hands and no magic at all, and it took longer, and they're worse, and they're honest. Do it that way. Slowly. Badly. Honestly. One case at a time until the end of the country's life, which I hope is a very long time from now."

Then, quieter: "Also, forgive Tammas Vey. He stole bread for forty years and gave most of it away. I heard his victims say so. All two of them. They're very fond of him."

Someone laughed — a wet, surprised sound — and the tension in the hall broke along a seam, and Hale found her eyes burning and could not have said for whom: the dead who'd spoken kindly, the living man who'd bought a nation's conscience at the price of his own hearing, or herself, still standing in the wreckage of everything she'd believed, holding a confession like a tool she no longer knew how to use.

* * *

The verdicts came down over the following week, and they were, as Theo had prayed, neither blanket nor clean.

The Gardeners' cases were reviewed individually — forty-one of them, then sixty, then a hundred and twelve as the ledgers surfaced. Some acts were pardoned outright: the healings, the flood-year feedings, the sheltering of the purged. Some drew sentences: fines, restitution, a few hard years for the knee-breakings, served, by their own request, rebuilding the districts they'd policed. Tammas Vey himself was sentenced to preside over the review board for the rest of his life, which everyone understood was both punishment and absolution, and which he accepted with the face of a man who had expected the axe and gotten paperwork instead.

The Inquisition's officers fared worse, as they should have. Hale watched former colleagues march to sentencing and felt nothing she could name — not satisfaction, not grief, something structural, like watching a wall come down that had been holding up your house. Her own sentence was read last: her confession counted as service rendered, her cooperation earned clemency, and in place of prison she was assigned — she actually laughed aloud when she heard it — to the Office of Records Recovery. Sealed drawers. Buried paper. A lifetime of opening the things her order had spent a century locking.

"It's a joke," Wren said, when Hale told her. "They've made you the anti-Inquisitor. You unask questions."

"I unseal them. The questions are all still there." Hale turned the appointment letter in her hands. Cream stock, official seal, the new seal — a hearth flame over an open door, because the design committee had insisted, and Theo had carved the original die himself, plain-handed, no magic, and it showed in the slightly uneven flame. "There's a list already. Two hundred sealed files recovered from the palace vaults. One of them is marked with the old Question insignia and a date I recognize."

Wren went still. "Which date?"

"The night my father signed." Hale met her eyes. "It's not a copy of the Purge Papers, Wren. It's flagged as correspondence. Personal correspondence. Someone wrote him a letter the night he signed, and someone decided a hundred years ago that no one should ever read it."

Outside the tribunal windows, the square noise rose and fell, the ordinary thunder of a country remembering itself. Hale folded the letter into her coat, over her heart, where confessions lived.

"I open it tomorrow," she said.
