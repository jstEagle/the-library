## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The storm arrived at the Guild of Impossible Things at half past nine, in a jar.

It had been carried up from the docks by a man named Oskel Vane, who sweated through his coat despite the autumn cold and would not set the thing down until Silas told him—twice—that the table was reinforced and she herself had signed the reinforcement order. The jar was no bigger than a wine bottle. Inside it, gray clouds turned on themselves with the slow patience of something that had all the time in the world, and lightning flickered behind them like a thought someone kept almost having.

"Found it off a wreck off the Sere coast," Vane said, wiping his palms on his trousers one at a time, as though each hand required individual reassurance. "Wreck was dry, mind you. Bone-dry. Three hundred years under salt water and not a drop got in." He nodded at the jar as if this explained everything. It did, more or less. That was the trouble with impossible things: they came with their own terrible logic attached, like receipts.

Silas put on her spectacles. She put them on slowly, because speed suggested excitement, and excitement suggested that an object was worth more than it was, and the Guild's whole reputation rested on the opposite proposition. She lifted the jar and turned it in the lamplight. The clouds followed the light. One of them, small and dense and darker than the rest, pressed itself against the glass where her thumb held it—a thumbprint of storm, clinging—and she felt, distinctly, the sensation of being *looked at* by weather.

"You're going to want to write down that it's worth nothing," Vane said. "Because if it's worth nothing, I can keep it."

"That is," Silas said, "the oldest trick in this building, and this building contains a door that opens onto last Tuesday."

Vane had the grace to look embarrassed. Silas set the jar down, opened her ledger to a fresh page, and began to write in the small, upright, unremarkable hand that had made her name quietly useful in a city full of loud people: *One (1) bottled tempest, provenance Sere coast wreck, claimed age 300 yrs.*

She worked the way she did everything: thoroughly, invisibly, and slightly faster than anyone expected. She checked the seal for maker's marks and found none, which meant either great age or very good forgery. She weighed it—four pounds, two ounces, absurd for its size, since storms are mostly air and air is mostly nothing—and wrote the weight down without comment. She held a tuning fork against the glass and listened. The fork sang a low, unhappy note, the note of a cellar before a summer squall, and she noted that too.

Then she asked the questions that mattered, in the flat tone that made clients forget she'd asked them at all.

"Who else has handled it?"

"A dockmaster. A priest who wouldn't touch it after looking. My wife."

"What did the priest say?"

"Said it was praying back." Vane shrugged, but the shrug cost him something; she could see that. "I don't hold with priests. But I haven't slept right since I picked the thing up. Feels like it's waiting for me to do something. Something specific. And I don't know what, and I'm starting to think it does."

Silas wrote: *Client exhibits sympathetic disturbance.* Underneath, after a moment's consideration, she added: *Justified.*

Here is what she knew about bottled storms, from three prior appraisals and one disaster the Guild did not discuss in front of paying customers: they were not captured weather. They were griefs, or vows, or arguments—some human feeling too large for a body, poured out into glass by someone who could not carry it anymore. The storm inside was only what the feeling looked like once it stopped pretending to be a person. They were not dangerous because of lightning. They were dangerous because they wanted, very badly, to be finished.

"This one's holding a grudge," she said. "Or a promise. Hard to tell which, from outside."

"Can you tell how much it's worth?"

"No," said Silas, and watched his face fall, and then gave him the rest of it, because cruelty by omission was still cruelty and her ledgers balanced both ways. "But I can tell you what it will do. In about six months, if nobody resolves whatever's in it, the glass starts to thin. In a year it breaks, and everything it's been saving comes out at once, all at the nearest person, and that person will be you, Mr. Vane, because it knows your hands."

He sat down without being invited. "How do I resolve it?"

"That depends on what it is. A grudge wants an apology delivered. A promise wants keeping. An argument—" she hesitated over this one, remembering the disaster— "an argument wants somebody to finally lose."

"And you can find out which?"

"I can try. The Guild takes forty percent. Standard rates. You'll sign here, initial there, and this clause means you won't sue us when you find out what your grandmother did."

That last part made him flinch, which told her it was a grandmother, which told her it was probably a promise, which told her the fee was fair. She wrote the estimate in pencil first—pencil, always pencil, until certainty hardened into ink—and slid the contract across the table.

While he read it, badly, the way they all did, Silas let herself look at the storm again. Up close, in the lamplight, she could see rain falling inside the jar: fine, silver, patient, falling from cloud to nowhere and rising again to fall forever. A storm that had been falling for three hundred years without ever landing on anything.

She knew the feeling. She kept it off the ledger.

* * *

By midnight Vane was gone, poorer by a deposit and lighter by some fraction of his dread, and the Guild had gone quiet in the particular way it went quiet—not silent, never silent, but settled. The Impossible Things had voices at night. The mirror on the second floor hummed old lullabies in no known language. The left-handed staircase creaked under the weight of footsteps that belonged to no one currently employed. In the vault, something knocked at intervals of exactly ninety-one seconds, had knocked for as long as anyone remembered, and was understood to be best ignored.

Silas worked through all of it the way a sailor works through weather.

Her office was the smallest room in the building, which suited her, because small rooms held fewer surprises. One lamp. Two shelves of ledgers going back nine years—the older books lived downstairs in the archive, under lock, in Corvane Hale's care and Corvane Hale's alone. A desk whose surface showed wood grain in the shape of a river delta, worn smooth by other people's elbows across a century of appraisals. On the wall, a calendar page for the current month, every day struck through in her neat diagonal strokes except the ones remaining, which waited in their little squares like unopened letters.

Tonight was the twelfth. She struck the eleventh through and paused with the pen above tomorrow's square.

Twelve days to the turn of the thirteenth moon.

There was nothing special about a thirteenth turn. Most years didn't have one at all—it took a lunar arithmetic she'd never bothered to learn for the extra moon to land inside a single year, and when it happened, the almanacs printed warnings that everyone laughed at and no one quite threw away. Bad luck for sailors. Bad luck for births. Bad luck for promises, said the almanac in the corner shop, and Silas, keeper of promises professional and otherwise, had read that line four times this week without knowing why.

She struck the square through anyway and went back to the accounts.

This was the work no one saw and everyone depended on: balancing the Guild's books, which were unlike any other books in the city, because the Guild traded in debts that could not be measured in coin. A debt of one favor, owed by a duchess, valued at—what? What was a favor from a duchess worth on the open market? Silas had spent two years building a conversion system, columns and sub-columns, exchange rates between gratitude and gold, between vengeance and time. The Crown's tax assessors had given up trying to audit her twice. The second assessor had left a note that said only, in a shaking hand, *your numbers add up, which frightens me more than if they didn't.*

She entered the evening's transactions. Forty percent of Vane's deposit to the house. Ten percent to Maren for the referral—he'd come in asking for her, and Maren had steamed him straight to Silas with the words *"you want the quiet one"* that Silas pretended not to have overheard and privately treasured. The remainder to the contingency fund, because bottled storms were the single worst risk class in the entire portfolio and she said so in the margin, annually, in ink, and was annually ignored.

The pen scratched. The lamp burned. Somewhere below, the knocking came and went, ninety-one seconds at a time, faithful as a heart.

When the accounts were done—and they balanced, they always balanced, that was the one law of the universe Silas had never seen violated—she sat for a moment with her hands flat on the desk and did the thing she did every night at this hour, the thing she would have denied doing if asked, the thing that was not on any ledger.

She prayed.

Not the way priests prayed. There was no kneeling, no incense, none of the machinery of worship that the temples sold at such markup. She simply spoke, quietly, to the dark above the lamp, the way you'd speak to someone working late in another room. The way you'd speak to someone you weren't sure was listening, but whom you couldn't quite believe wasn't.

"Evening," she said. "Slow one. Storm in a bottle, grief or a vow, we'll know by spring. Maren's well. She ate the last of the honey bread and left me the crust, which is either affection or arithmetic. Accounts balance. Nothing in the vault has escalated."

A pause. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself, which was only the draft from the window, which she knew, which changed nothing about how it felt.

"Fifteen years tonight," she said, and heard how it sounded, and made herself finish. "You know that. You were there. Or—you were somewhere. I've never actually been sure you were *here*, exactly, so maybe tonight doesn't count as an anniversary for you the way it does for me. If you're counting at all. If you can count."

The knocking below marked its ninety-one seconds and fell silent again.

"I'm not asking for anything," she said, which was true, and also not, and both halves of it sat in her voice where anyone could hear them. "I know the terms. I swore and you answered, once, and the answer bought me fifteen years, and a vow is a vow, and I keep my books. I just—"

She stopped. Rubbed the bridge of her nose under the spectacles. Started again, smaller.

"It would be nice to be certain. That's all. Not a miracle. Just certainty. One word. You managed a whole sentence once, apparently, so the budget exists."

Nothing. Of course nothing. Fifteen years of nothing, faithfully delivered, and she had long ago stopped expecting the silence to break and had instead built her whole life inside it, the way a snail builds inside its shell, the way a prisoner builds inside a cell until the cell is called home.

She blew out the lamp.

In the dark, her office held the shapes of ordinary things: desk, shelf, chair, the coat on its hook, the calendar with its struck-through days. Ordinary things were honest. Ordinary things never promised you an answer and then withheld it for a decade and a half while you kept your side of the bargain down to the letter. She loved ordinary things the way one loves a friend who has never once surprised you, and she was wise enough, most nights, to know that this was not love at all but something wearier and more durable, and she was lonely enough, every night, to accept it.

* * *

The stairs complained under her boots on the way down—only hers, only the real weight; the Guild's ghosts used the other staircase—and she let herself into the archive to log the evening's acquisitions, because a thing wasn't truly acquired until it existed in triplicate.

Down here the air smelled of paper and cold stone and, faintly, of the sea, though the sea was a hundred miles away and the smell had never been explained and had been formally classified, in Ledger Nine, Volume Four, as *ambient impossibility, harmless, do not investigate further (see incident, year 3).*

Corvane's section was locked. Hers ran along the near wall: nine years of her own neat hand, spines labeled in the same upright lettering she used everywhere, a wall of evidence that a person existed here, worked here, noticed things. She pulled the current volume, entered the storm by accession number, cross-referenced Vane's contract, and was turning to reshelve it when her eye caught the slip of paper tucked into the delivery tray.

A job ticket, in Maren's looping scrawl. Estate sale, the old Aldery house out past the tanneries—deceased client, heirs liquidating, standard appraisal run, nothing exotic expected. Scheduled for tomorrow. And beneath the particulars, in Maren's hand, the note that made Silas stand still in the cold dark with the ticket in her fingers:

*They're selling the library too. Whole thing. Books older than the Guild. You'll want to see this one yourself—I found something with your name in it.*

*Your actual name,* Maren had written. *Come early.*

Below the archive, faintly, the knocking started again. Ninety-one seconds. Ninety-two. Ninety-three—

—and stopped mid-count, which had never once happened in recorded memory, and did not start again.

Silas stood in the dark among nine years of perfectly balanced truth and felt, without any evidence whatsoever, the floor of her orderly life tilt one degree, the way a ship tilts before you consciously notice the sea has changed.

She put the volume back. She locked the cabinet. She folded Maren's ticket into her breast pocket, over her heart, where important documents went, and climbed the stairs toward her narrow bed, telling herself—as she struck tomorrow's square mentally, preemptively, already—that it was nothing. An heir's joke. A coincidence of names.

The Guild of Impossible Things dealt in many commodities. Coincidence was not one of them. Every member learned that in their first month, and Silas Joubert had been a member for fifteen years, and she lay awake in the dark for a long time anyway, listening to the silence where the knocking should have been, waiting for morning.
