## 2. Thirteenth Turn

The Vellamont estate smelled of camphor and old money, which is to say it smelled of a family trying to preserve something that had already rotted.

Silas Joubert stood in the library on the third morning of the inventory, sleeves rolled to the elbow, a jeweler's loupe hanging from a cord around her neck, and made her eleventh note of the hour in a ledger bound in grey calfskin. *Lot 214. One orrery, brass, seven planets, eighth arm broken. Venus tarnished. Mercury missing entirely.* She wrote it in her small, even hand, each numeral the same height as its neighbors, because a ledger that could not be read at a glance was a ledger that could be read however a magistrate pleased.

"You're very quiet," said the estate's executor, a narrow man named Pethis who had been wringing his hands since dawn.

"Counting," Silas said.

"There's rather a lot to count."

"That's why they hired me." She did not look up. Looking up invited conversation, and conversation invited questions, and questions were the one inventory item she could not appraise. The Guild of Impossible Things had sent her because the Vellamont sale had a reputation: a family that had owned, among other oddments, a bottled storm, a mirror that showed Tuesday, and — if the rumors held — a library with books that had no business existing. The Crown's censors had already been through the house once. They had taken four crates and left a chalk mark on the door that meant *inspected, insufficient.* Silas had memorized the mark's angle and height out of habit. Everything worth memorizing was worth memorizing precisely.

Pethis hovered. She let him. In fifteen years of keeping accounts for the Guild she had learned that silence was a tool with a hundred uses, and most of them were kinder than speech.

At last he retreated to the hallway, where he could be heard arguing with an appraiser of silver about whether gravy boats constituted a *set*. Silas turned back to the shelves.

The Vellamont library ran to some four thousand volumes, most of them the usual sediment of a wealthy house: sermons, genealogies, novels with uncut pages, atlases of countries that no longer existed. She worked shelf by shelf, left to right, because disorder was how mistakes were born. She catalogued a first edition with a wine ring on the cover. She catalogued a prayer book with a pressed flower that had turned the color of dust. She catalogued, with a small private satisfaction, an atlas in which the country of Aurelle had been inked out — not printed over, but *inked*, by hand, a whole nation blotted into blackness by someone with a steady brush and strong opinions. The Guild paid well for those. Erasure was a kind of information.

It was on the second-to-last shelf, wedged behind a false row of sermons, that her fingers found the book that did not want to be found.

It was bound in something that was not leather. Dark, matte, faintly warm, as though it had been sitting near a fire — though the shelf was against the north wall, and the house's fires had gone cold years ago. There was no title on the spine. There was no title anywhere. When she drew it out, dust fell away from it in a single clean sheet, as if the book had been waiting to be clean.

*Careful,* she told herself, and did not listen, and opened it.

The first page was a date. Not a publication date — a *date*, written in a hand so like her own that for one absurd moment she thought she had written it: the same small numerals, the same even height. The date was a hundred years old.

The pages after that were not a book in any sense she knew. They were entries. Ledger entries. *Received:* and *Paid:* and *Owed:* — but the currencies were wrong. *Received: one winter, unseasonably mild. Paid: a name, forgotten by its owner. Owed: the sound of a door, to be delivered in the hundredth year.* She turned page after page, faster now, her meticulousness fraying at the edges like a rope under load. It was a ledger. It was a ledger of the kind the Guild kept — of the kind *she* kept — and it was a hundred years old, and every entry in it was an impossibility.

She was a professional. She had appraised a bottled storm; she knew what it sounded like when thunder was unhappy in its glass. She had priced a saint's fingerbone and determined it to be a pig's, and had said so, and had been thanked. She did not believe in omens. She believed in weights, measures, margins of error, and the honest arithmetic of what a thing was worth.

But when she reached the final third of the book, her hands stopped being steady, and she noticed, distantly, the way one notices weather from indoors.

The entries had changed. They had stopped being about winters and names and doors. They had become a story told in the grammar of accounting — and the story was about a girl.

*Owed: to the orphan, bread, in the winter of her ninth year. Paid: by the god who does not answer.*

Silas's breath went thin. She was nine once. Everyone had been nine. But not everyone had been an orphan begging at the shrine of Veth, the silent god, the god of debts and silence, pressing her cracked hands together in the snow and offering the only thing she owned — *anything, take anything, I'll pay it back, I swear it, I'll pay it back* — and not everyone had been answered, once, in a way she had never told a living soul.

She turned the page.

*Owed: to the girl, fifteen years of service, to be rendered in ledgers and small invisibilities. Paid: her life, extended past its arithmetic.*

She was standing in a dead woman's library holding a hundred-year-old book that knew the shape of her life, and the executor was arguing about gravy boats in the next room, and the light through the tall windows had gone the color of pewter, and Silas Joubert — who did not startle, who did not tremble, who had once appraised a haunted violin without flinching — sat down on the floor without deciding to.

The book lay open across her knees.

*Owed: to the keeper of ledgers, one truth, to be delivered on the thirteenth turn of the moon.*

The thirteenth turn of the moon. She knew the phrase the way you know a word you've never said aloud. The almanac in her satchel — she checked it with hands that felt borrowed — gave the moon's age for tonight. Twelve turns complete. Tonight the moon would make its thirteenth, an intercalary turn, a turn the calendar pretended did not happen, a thirteenth moon in a season that should hold only twelve.

Tonight.

She found the entry. It was near the end, on a page whose edges had been reinforced with strips of paper pasted down by a careful hand, as though someone had known this page would be read often. The date at its head was tonight's. Not the night a hundred years ago — *tonight's* date, the date sitting in her almanac, the date she had written that morning on the Guild's day-sheet in her own small hand.

And beneath the date, in that hand so like hers:

*Owed: to Silas Joubert, keeper of ledgers for the Guild of Impossible Things, the whole of the truth, beginning with the door—*

The sentence stopped. Mid-word. Mid-*word* — the ink simply ended, the final letter half-formed, as if the pen had been pulled away, or as if the writer had reached the edge of what he was permitted to know.

*Beginning with the door.*

Silas read it eleven times. She was aware, in the way of a person watching her own house flood, of the ordinary sounds of the estate continuing around her: Pethis's voice, a cart in the lane, the tick of the broken orrery on its table as though its missing planet still pulled at it. She was aware that her life, up to this moment, had been a ledger she believed she understood — modest income, modest debts, a god who never spoke, a guild that valued her precisely because she asked for nothing. She had built that life the way she built every ledger: entry by entry, balanced, invisible. She had thought invisibility was safety.

The book knew her name. Her full name. Silas *Joubert* — and no one used the Joubert, because she had invented it herself at fourteen, plucked from a gravestone in a pauper's yard, because a girl with no family needed at least the *shape* of one. She had told exactly one person. Maren. And Maren would no sooner write it in a century-old book than fly.

Unless the book was not written by a person.

Unless the book had been written the way her god worked: silently, in debts, in the grammar of what was owed.

She looked at the entry again. *Beginning with the door.* Aurelle. The inked-out country in the atlas three shelves away. The word rose in her with a certainty that had no provenance, the way you sometimes wake knowing something you were never told. She did not know why. That was the worst of it. She did not know *why* she knew.

Somewhere below, a bell rang — the estate's supper bell, or Pethis's patience ending. Footsteps on the stairs. Silas did the thing that fifteen years of ledgers had trained into her spine: she became invisible. She closed the book. She stood. She straightened her sleeves.

And then, because the footsteps were coming up the library stairs and the book was warm against her palms and the half-sentence was still burning behind her eyes, she did the second thing. The thing she had never done. The thing that, in the ledger of Silas Joubert, would have to be entered as a debt of her own.

She stole it.

Not the whole book — the whole book would be missed, would be inventoried, would be *asked about*. She turned to the final entries, the ones with her name, the ones with tonight's date, and she cut. Her knife was small and very sharp and had opened a hundred sealed lots; it opened the binding's old thread in four strokes, quiet as a breath. The pages came free in a sheaf no thicker than a letter. She slid them into the false back of her ledger, behind the grey calfskin, where she kept the things that were not quite accounts — a dried sprig from a shrine, a coin from a country that no longer existed.

She put the mutilated book back behind the false row of sermons, spine out, dust sheet drawn over it, and she had just smoothed her sleeves when Pethis appeared in the doorway with a candle and an apology.

"Miss Joubert. Supper's laid, if you — good heavens, you look pale."

"Bad light in the north shelves," she said. Her voice was level. Her voice was always level. It was, she realized with a sensation like the floor tilting a degree past true, the first lie she had ever told in the course of her work, and it had come out of her smooth as a coin from a purse. "I'll finish the inventory tomorrow. The last two shelves."

"Of course, of course. The censors come back Friday, so —"

"Friday," she said. "Everything will be in order."

* * *

She walked back across the city with the ledger against her ribs and did not run, because running was a thing visible people did.

Lantern-lighters were working the avenues. The moon was not up yet, but the sky had that thinned, expectant quality it gets before an intercalary turn — or perhaps that was only her. She catalogued her own symptoms the way she catalogued lots, because it was the only discipline she had left: pulse elevated. Hands steady by force. A recurring image, unbidden, of a door. Not a door she knew. A door in a hollow, in a country that had been inked out, and the certainty — again, unprovenanced, unbearable — that she had seen it before.

*Beginning with the door.*

The Guildhouse stood where it always stood, which is to say in a street that most people walked past without seeing, behind a door that most people's eyes declined to report. The sign above it read *APPRAISALS. FAIR RATES.* It was the most honest lie in the city. Inside, the hall smelled of tallow and paper and the particular cold of objects that had outlived their owners. Someone was laughing in the common room — Maren's laugh, low and carrying, the laugh that had been the soundtrack of Silas's adult life.

Silas stood in the entryway with her hand on the door and understood that she was standing on the far side of a line. On one side: the guild that had raised her, the ledgers she kept, the friend laughing over wine, the small ordered life balanced to the last copper. On the other: a sheaf of stolen pages and a half-sentence with her name in it.

She thought about burning the pages. She was good at burning things cleanly; it was part of the trade. She thought about it for the length of one breath, honestly, the way she appraised anything.

Then she thought: *a debt is a debt,* and heard, in the silence where a god's voice should have been, nothing at all — and went inside.

She climbed to her room. She locked the door, which she had never done. She took out the pages and laid them on her desk in order, weighted at the corners with the tools of her trade — the loupe, the balance weights, the small knife — and read them again from the beginning, slowly, as work. As inventory.

The entries told a story in the grammar of accounting, and now that she read it as a whole, the story had a shape she recognized the way you recognize a face across a street: a god, going silent. A bargain, made in desperation. A girl, placed in the path of the story like a coin placed on a scale. And a door, owed, to be opened on the thirteenth turn of the moon — by her.

The last page was the page with tonight's date. The half-sentence. *Beginning with the door—*

Below it, in a different hand — older, cramped, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — someone had written a single line, so faint she had to hold the page to the candle to catch it:

*If she reads this before the turn, tell her: the ledger can be balanced another way.*

There was no signature. But Silas knew handwriting the way other people knew faces, and the hand was Corvane Hale's — her guildmaster's, the man who had pulled her off the street at fourteen and taught her that every debt could be entered, and every entry could be survived.

The candle guttered. Outside, past the shutters, the moon was rising on its thirteenth turn, and it looked, through the slats, like the bright edge of a coin about to be spent.
