## 3. What the Guild Owes

She did not sleep.

That was the first entry in the night's ledger, and Silas Joubert recorded it honestly, because she recorded everything honestly — it was the one virtue she had never had to fake. She sat at her desk in the small room above the counting floor with the stolen page flattened beneath a brass paperweight shaped like a scale, and she read it until the words stopped being words and became simply marks, and then she read it again until they were words once more, and worse.

*Silas Joubert.* Her full name, in a book a century old. *The thirteenth turn of the moon.* Tonight's date, entered before she was born, before her mother was born, before the ink in the bottle on her desk had been pressed from oak galls still hanging on trees that were now tables.

And beneath it, in the same careful hand, the same hand that had written *rent paid in full* and *two candles, tallow* and *the girl eats too little, see to it* in the Guild's ledgers for twenty years — the sentence that ended mid-word, like a man interrupted at his desk.

*If she reads this before the turn, tell her: the ledger can be balanced another—*

Another way. Another way *what*. She had turned the page. The next page was a receipt for six silver spoons, dated eighty-one years ago, and the spoons were still in the Guild's inventory, and that was somehow the most frightening thing of all: that the world had simply continued, ordinary and accounted for, on the other side of a sentence about her.

The moon went down. The bells of the counting houses rang the small hours. And when the grey light came up over the rooftops of Vell's Crossing and the shutters of the Guild of Impossible Things opened one by one below her window, Silas folded the page into quarters, tucked it inside her coat against her ribs, and went down to do the thing she had never once done in twenty years of meticulous, invisible service.

She was going to ask the Guild a question.

* * *

The counting floor smelled the way it always smelled at dawn: tallow, ink, cold tea, and the faint mineral tang of the bottled things kept in the vault below. Maren was already at her desk, sleeves rolled, hair pinned up with what appeared to be a fork, working through a crate of appraisals from the Tallowman estate with the grim efficiency of a woman who had decided the morning would not defeat her.

"You look," Maren said without looking up, "like a ledger that's been left out in the rain."

"I didn't sleep."

"I can see that. I have eyes, and also you have ink on your face." Maren finally looked up. Her eyes did the thing they always did — the quick, warm, cataloguing sweep that missed nothing, because Maren had been a pickpocket before she was a keeper, and old habits were the only debts that never expired. Her gaze lingered a half-second on the left side of Silas's coat. On the place where a folded page might sit against a woman's ribs.

"You went back to the estate sale," Maren said. Not a question.

"I finished the inventory."

"At three in the morning."

"The light was better."

Maren set down her pen. Around them the counting floor was waking — old Ferro winding the great clock that kept Guild time, the apprentices hauling the day's first crates, someone arguing with the bottled storm in the vault again, a low mutter of thunder through the floorboards like a dog complaining in its sleep. All of it ordinary. All of it the world Silas had spent twenty years keeping balanced, entry by entry, so that nothing in it could ever surprise her again.

"Corvane wants you," Maren said quietly. "First bell. He's in the map room."

Silas's heart did something she refused to enter into any record. "Did he say why?"

"He said—" Maren hesitated, and Maren never hesitated; hesitation was a cost and she did not pay costs she could avoid. "He said, *tell her the estate sale is concluded.*"

* * *

The map room was at the top of the Guild house, a round chamber with a domed ceiling painted with a sky that no longer matched the real one — the Guild had bought the painting secondhand from an observatory that had gone out of business, and no one had ever paid to update it, and so above the maps of every country that was and some that weren't hung the stars of two hundred years ago, slightly wrong, slightly out of date, beautiful in the way that only obsolete things are beautiful.

Corvane Hale stood at the long table with his back to the door, and he did not turn when she entered, and that was the second wrong thing, because Corvane always turned; Corvane had a host's manners bolted onto a merchant's soul, and he turned for everyone, apprentices and inquisitors and the occasional ghost the Guild was holding in escrow.

On the table before him lay the estate sale inventory. Her inventory. Her handwriting, her columns, her neat marginal notes. He had gone through it page by page. She could see where his thumb had pressed hardest — the pages covering the library, the books, the lot numbers.

"Lot forty-four," Corvane said, still not turning. His voice was level. That was the third wrong thing. Corvane's voice was never level; it was warm, or it was dry, or it was the particular gentle irony he used when a client tried to sell the Guild a forgery, but it was never level. Level was what a voice became when a man was holding himself together with both hands.

"Lot forty-four," Silas said. "The library of the Aurelle estate. One hundred and six volumes, condition variable, appraised at—"

"Silas."

Just her name. He turned then, and she saw his face, and she understood in one cold drop why he had not turned around.

Corvane Hale was pale. Not tired-pale, not old-man-pale, but the pale of a man who has seen something at the bottom of a strongbox that he had paid good money never to see. His hands, resting on her inventory, were perfectly still, and she knew how much that stillness cost, because she had learned stillness from him.

"You found it," he said.

She could have lied. She had watched him lie beautifully for twenty years; she knew the whole grammar of it. But the page was against her ribs, warm from her body, and her name was written on it in his hand, and some entries, once made, cannot be quietly reversed.

"Yes," she said, and took it out, and unfolded it, and laid it on the table between her inventory and his hands.

For a long moment he did not touch it. He looked at it the way a man looks at a snake he is certain is dead. Then he picked it up, and read it, and she watched him read his own handwriting, and watched something move behind his face that she had no column for, no category, no neat marginal note. It was not guilt. She knew guilt; the Guild traded in it. It was older than guilt. It was the look of a man reading a bill that has finally come due, one he has known about for a very long time, and has paid a great deal to defer.

"Where," he said, "is the rest of it."

"The book? Lot forty-four, volume—"

"I know which volume." The level voice cracked, just slightly, like ice under a boot. "Where is it *now*, Silas."

"In the vault. Unappraised. I brought back only—" she touched the page "—this."

"Why."

It was not the question she had expected. She had expected *how dare you*, expected the cold arithmetic of Guild discipline, expected at worst the door and at best a fine entered against her name. She had prepared for all of it on the walk up the stairs. She had not prepared for him to ask her *why* in the voice of a man who genuinely needed to know, as if the answer might save him or damn him and he could not yet tell which.

"Because it has my name in it," she said. "Because it has tonight's date in it. Because it's a hundred years old, guildmaster, and I don't believe in impossibilities, I *appraise* them, and I am telling you as a professional that this page is impossible, and I want to know what the Guild knows that I don't."

Something flickered across his face at that — *what the Guild knows* — something almost like a laugh, if a laugh could be a wound.

"The Guild," Corvane said, "knows what it is paid to know. That is the whole of our covenant. That is what keeps us alive in a country where the Crown burns people for knowing too much." He held the page up to the grey light from the dome, and for a moment the painted stars of a dead sky shone through the paper, through her name, through the sentence that ended mid-word. "Do you know what the Inquisition pays for a prophetic text? Not money. They pay in *not burning the house it was found in*. They pay in neighbors. They pay in the continued existence of everyone whose name appears anywhere near it."

"Then we should give it to them."

"We should *burn* it." He said it fast, too fast, and then he made himself slow down, and she watched him do it, watched him put the merchant's patience back on like a coat. "We should burn it," he said, gently now, terribly gently. "That is the appraisal, Silas. That is my professional judgment, and I have been doing this longer than you have been alive. A page that names a living Guild member in a prophetic text is not an asset. It is a fire that hasn't started yet. We are keepers of impossible things, not makers of them."

"And the book?"

"The book goes in the furnace. Tonight. Both of them, together, and the ash goes in the river, and the lot number is struck from the inventory and the sale is recorded as void, and no one — *no one* — speaks of it again." He set the page down. He smoothed it flat with both hands, an oddly tender gesture, the way he smoothed the Guild's ledgers when the day's accounts balanced. "This is what the Guild owes you, Silas. Twenty years of your excellent, invisible work, and what it owes you in return is a long life in which nothing interesting ever happens. Let me pay that debt. Burn it, and go back to your columns, and in a month this will be a story you tell badly at midwinter."

He was already reaching for the candle.

And Silas — who had taken the page, who had come here to give it to him, who had told herself on the stairs that this was what a good keeper did, that you surfaced the anomaly to the senior authority and let the senior authority resolve it — heard herself say:

"No."

The candle stopped.

"It's evidence," she said, and was surprised at how steady her own voice was. Level. She'd learned that from him too. "It's evidence in a document the Guild has held for — how long, guildmaster? How long have we had this book? Because the estate sale was last week, but this page was *cut out* before I ever saw the volume. The stub is still there. Someone cut it out a long time ago, and folded it, and kept it, and it was in the lining of the estate's writing desk, which means someone in that house was keeping it the way we keep things. And I want to know who, and I want to know when, and I want to know why your handwriting is on it."

The silence in the map room was total. Below them, faintly, the Guild went on — the clock, the crates, the storm muttering in its bottle. Above them the painted stars hung wrong and old.

"Silas," Corvane said at last, and there was something in his voice she had never heard in twenty years, and it took her a moment to name it because it had no place in him, and the name, when she found it, was *grief*. "There are entries in this world that are not yours to audit."

"Everything is mine to audit. That's the job."

"Not this one." He folded the page — her page, her name — along her own creases, precisely, and held it out to her, and that was the fourth wrong thing, because Corvane Hale never gave anything back. "Keep it. Burn it yourself, tonight, and bring me the ash, and I will enter the whole matter as resolved. That is the last time I will ask."

She took the page. Their fingers did not touch. He turned back to the window before she reached the door, and the last thing she saw of him was his hands, flat on the table, perfectly still, pressing down on her inventory as if to hold the whole ordinary world in place by force.

* * *

Maren was waiting at the bottom of the stairs, which was the fifth wrong thing, because Maren's desk was forty feet away and Maren did not abandon her work for anyone short of a fire.

"Well?" she said.

"He wants it burned."

"Did you give it to him?"

"I gave it to him. He gave it back." Silas heard how it sounded and didn't care. "Maren. He knew. Before I said a word, he knew what lot forty-four was, he'd been through my inventory page by page, and when he looked at the page he looked at it like a *bill*. Like something he'd been dreading for years."

Maren said nothing. Maren, who always said something, who had an opinion the way other people had blood — Maren looked at the stairs, and then at the window, and then at Silas, and her face did a thing Silas had never seen it do, which was close, like a ledger being shut.

"You should burn it," Maren said.

"You too?"

"I'm not *him*, I'm not telling you what to do, I'm telling you what I'd do." She lowered her voice. The counting floor was filling; apprentices, clients, old Ferro's clock striking the hour with its one cracked note. "Silas. The Crown pays inquisitors to find pages like that. If Rook's people ever—"

"You know Inquisitor Rook's name?"

Everyone knew Dessa Rook's name the way sailors know a storm's name; it was not knowledge, it was weather. But Maren had said it *fast*, and *specifically*, and Silas had spent twenty years learning to hear fast and specific.

"Maren. Why do you know her first name?"

"Because everyone does." Maren picked up her pen. Her hand was steady. Maren's hands were always steady; it was one of the thousand things Silas had never thought to audit until this morning. "Go home. Sleep. Burn the page or don't, but do it *outside* this building, and don't tell me which, and if anyone asks me, I'll say you spent last night here working and I covered your desk, because I did, because that's what I'm doing right now, actually, and you're welcome."

"Maren—"

"*Go.*" And then, softer, with the warmth coming back into her face like sun through a shutter, the Maren Silas had known for fifteen years, the Maren who had taught her to pick locks and keep columns and laugh at funerals: "Whatever this is — whatever it turns out to be — you don't carry it alone. That's not what I'm for. Now get out of my light, I have a crate of Tallowman spoons that won't appraise themselves."

Silas went. Out through the counting floor, past the great clock, down the steps into the grey morning street, with the page folded against her ribs and her friend's lie settling over her like a coat that almost fit.

It was only three streets away, standing in the market square watching fishwives argue over eels, that she made herself say it plainly, in her own head, in her own neat hand, the way she entered everything:

*Maren lied to me just now. Not for me. For the Guild. And I couldn't tell what else she wasn't saying.*

The first crack. Small, hairline, the kind of flaw you only find because you have spent your whole life learning to look for flaws in everything.

She bought an eel she didn't want, because the fishwife expected it, and because a woman standing in a market doing nothing at all is a woman someone notices. And as she turned for home, she saw, across the square, by the fountain, a grey-cloaked figure who was not buying anything, and not selling anything, and not doing anything at all except — briefly, professionally, the way a keeper checks a lot number — looking at her.

The figure turned and walked away before she could cross the square.

Silas stood in the cold with the eel going heavy in her hand, and understood that the appraisal had already begun, and that she was the lot.

*If she reads this before the turn, tell her: the ledger can be balanced another way.*

She needed to know who had written that sentence.

And she was suddenly, terribly certain that the one person who could tell her had just ordered it burned.
