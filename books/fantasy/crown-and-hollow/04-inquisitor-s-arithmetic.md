## 4. Inquisitor's Arithmetic

The tip arrived the way all the best ones did: folded inside a complaint about a barking dog.

Dessa Rook read it twice by lamplight in her office above the Chancery of Public Truth, while rain worked at the window like a patient locksmith. The dog complaint was genuine—signed, dated, addressed to the wrong office entirely, which was how clever people liked to smuggle things past her clerks. Tucked into its crease, on paper of no particular quality and no particular watermark, four lines in a careful, unremarkable hand:

*The Guild of Impossible Things keeps more than ledgers.*
*Ask what they appraise that has no price.*
*A book sold at the Vessing estate sale should not exist.*
*The thirteenth turn comes.*

No signature. No scent of perfume or pipe smoke. The ink was cheap and the hand was disciplined—the writing of someone who had practiced being forgettable, which was itself a kind of confession.

Rook set the note down and looked at it for a long moment, the way she might look at a lock before deciding whether to pick it or break it.

"The thirteenth turn," she said aloud, to the empty room. Her voice had gone hoarse from three days of interrogations; it always did. "Someone's been reading the old heresies."

She should have burned it. Anonymous tips about prophecy were usually bait—rival guilds settling debts, spurned lovers with grudges, occasionally one of her own underlings testing whether she was still sharp enough to catch them feeding her nonsense to see what she'd do. She had a drawer full of such notes, catalogued and cross-referenced, each one a small monument to human pettiness.

But this one used the phrase *thirteenth turn*, and nobody used that phrase anymore unless they'd read something they shouldn't. It appeared in exactly eleven documents in the Crown's restricted archive, all of them fragments, all of them seized in raids so old the raiders' grandchildren were dead. Eleven mentions across a century, and every single one had been followed by blood.

Rook knew because she had read them all. Twice. Once for the work, once for Wren.

She rose, stiff from the chair, and went to the cabinet against the far wall—the one with three locks, the one her superiors pretended not to see her open. Inside, behind the confiscated grimoires and the sealed testimony of executed prophets, was a thin folder with a worn edge, and inside *that* was everything the Crown knew—or claimed to know—about the country of Aurelle.

Which was nothing. Which was the point.

Aurelle did not appear on maps. It did not appear in treaties, census rolls, tax ledgers, parish records, or the memories of anyone officially consulted. A hundred years ago there had been a war, and at the end of the war there had been a peace, and the peace had included a clause so thorough that it had not merely ended Aurelle but *unwritten* it—its name struck from every record, its roads rerouted, its survivors scattered and sworn to silence. The Edict of Forgetting, the archivists called it, though none of them would say it loudly. You could find traces if you dug: a coin with the wrong king's face, a lullaby in a dialect that matched no living tongue, a boundary stone in the northern hills carved with words that made literate men go quiet.

And you could find prophecies. That was the trouble. Every fragment the Crown had ever seized that mentioned Aurelle also mentioned a door, and a moon, and a turn that came after the twelfth. And every person who had ever been caught possessing such a fragment had died for it—not because the Crown feared the future, Rook's instructors had insisted, but because prophecy was sedition wearing a holy face. To say the world *must* unfold a certain way was to tell every subject that their obedience was decoration. That their choices were already written. That the Crown itself was just a scene in someone else's play.

Rook had believed it once the way you believe a wall is solid: without thinking, because you lean on it.

Then Wren had found a prophecy.

Her sister had been nineteen, a seamstress's apprentice with quick fingers and a laugh like a dropped bell, and she had found—in the lining of a secondhand coat, of all ridiculous places—a scrap of vellum with nine lines of verse predicting a fire. Not a great fire. A small one. A chandler's shop on Tanner's Row, a specific night, a specific hour. Wren had shown it to Rook the way you show a curiosity, half laughing—"your whole office hunting traitors and here's real magic folded in an old coat"—and Rook, twenty-three and earnest and already too good at her job, had done the correct thing. She had reported it. The scrap was confiscated. The shop was watched.

The fire happened anyway. On the night. At the hour. And Wren—who had gone back to the shop that evening, who had stood across the street watching the windows as though the prophecy were a play she'd bought a ticket to—had been close enough when the oil stores caught to breathe the smoke that killed her.

The inquiry afterward had concluded the fire was accidental. A lamp, a draft, bad luck. But Rook had sat with the ashes of that conclusion for fifteen years now, turning them over, and she had never once managed to make them mean anything except this: *the prophecy was true, and knowing it changed nothing, and my sister died of a piece of paper.*

That was the arithmetic she carried. Prophecy promised knowledge and delivered helplessness. It turned people into spectators of their own lives. Wren hadn't run into the shop to warn anyone—she'd stood and *watched*, because some part of her had believed that if it was written, it was already over. The paper hadn't killed her. The believing had.

So yes. Dessa Rook hunted prophetic sedition, and she was good at it, and she slept poorly, and she told herself every one of those facts was separate from the others.

* * *

In the morning she took the tip to her superior, Underchancellor Bravv, a man built like a filing cabinet and about as easy to move.

"The Guild of Impossible Things." He said the name with distaste, as though it were a stain on his desk. "Appraisers. Curiosity brokers. They're licensed."

"Licensed to handle objects of unusual provenance," Rook said. "Which makes them the perfect fence for prophetic material. Who else can sell a cursed mirror at auction without anyone asking where it came from?"

"Who's your source?"

"Anonymous."

Bravv's eyebrows performed a small, skeptical pilgrimage. "You've built a career on anonymous tips, Rook. Half your commendations."

"And the other half on verifying them before I act." She laid the note on his desk, aligned precisely with the edge. "This one cites the Vessing estate sale. I've already pulled the inventory. Item forty-one: 'a bound volume, leather, contents illegible.' Sold to a private buyer through the Guild's brokerage. The Guild's own appraisal sheet describes it as—and I quote—'*of no particular interest.*'"

"So?"

"So the Guild's appraisers are the best in the city. If a book was genuinely of no interest, they wouldn't have touched it. And if it *was* of interest, then describing it as worthless means either incompetence—which doesn't happen there—or concealment." She tapped the last line of the note. "And whoever wrote this knew enough to be afraid. *The thirteenth turn comes.* That's not street talk. That's archive language."

Bravv studied her the way he always did, with the weary patience of a man who had learned that Rook could not be dissuaded, only delayed. "If you raid a licensed guild on the strength of an unsigned note and find nothing, the Guildmasters' Council will have your commission for a rug."

"If I find nothing, I'll apologize in writing and pay for the door I break." She straightened. "I'm not asking for a raid. I'm asking for a visit. One ledger-keeper, invited politely, answering questions about a single estate transaction. If there's smoke, I'll come back for the fire."

He waved her off with two fingers, which in Bravv's language was a signature.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a narrow building on Candlemaker's Court that looked, from the street, like the home of a moderately successful dentist. Brass plate. Clean windows. A door painted the green of old money. Rook had walked past it perhaps forty times in her career and never once gone in, partly because the Guild kept its licenses immaculate, and partly because she distrusted any institution whose name was a joke. Jokes were load-bearing walls. You knocked on them and something structural fell on you.

She went in anyway, alone, in plain clothes, carrying the inventory sheet and a warrant she hoped not to use.

Inside smelled of paper, lamp oil, and something faintly metallic, like the air after lightning. A young woman sat at the front desk—dark-haired, ink-stained to the second knuckle, with the particular posture of someone who had spent ten thousand hours sitting correctly and resented every one of them. Her nameplate said M. QUILL. Her eyes said *I have already decided whether to lie to you, and you will enjoy finding out.*

"Inquisitor Rook," Dessa said, showing the badge rather than the warrant. "Chancery of Public Truth. I have some routine questions about an estate sale."

"Of course." M. Quill produced a visitor's book without being asked. "Sign in, please. Third column. We're very fond of records here."

"I'd heard."

"What would you like to know?"

"A volume from the Vessing sale. Lot forty-one. I want the buyer's name, the sale price, and the appraiser who handled it."

Something moved behind the clerk's eyes—quick, controlled, gone. Rook had spent fifteen years learning to read that movement. It was the look of a person hearing a number they already knew, confirmed.

"Lot forty-one." Quill opened a ledger without hurry. Her finger traveled down the page. "Bound volume, leather, contents illegible. Appraised at... two crowns. Purchased by a private party." She looked up. "I'm afraid our client records are sealed under brokerage privilege. You'd need a warrant for names."

"I have one."

"Then I'll fetch our guildmaster." Quill closed the ledger with a soft, final sound, like a coffin lid practicing. "Corvane Hale handles all inquiries touching privileged material personally. He's very thorough. He'll be delighted."

She left through a door behind the desk, and Rook stood alone in the entry hall, doing what she did best, which was noticing.

Noticing the dust-free shelf where a book-sized object had recently sat. Noticing that the visitor's book's previous page bore yesterday's date and a single signature, and that the ink was the same cheap iron-gall as the anonymous note in her pocket—no, she corrected herself, *similar*. Cheap ink was cheap everywhere. Noticing, most of all, the smell again, stronger here: ozone and old storm, the smell of a thing kept in a bottle.

And beneath it, almost lost, another smell. Beeswax and ash. Like a candle burned down to nothing in a closed room.

Like a page that had been fed to a flame very recently, and carefully, and not thoroughly enough.

* * *

Corvane Hale was not what she expected. She had prepared herself for a fat spider of a man, all rings and reassurance; instead she got someone tall and gray and precise, with the courteous exhaustion of a physician who has delivered too much bad news to bother pretending otherwise. He received her in a study lined with glass cases, each holding something impossible—a feather that fell upward, a key that fit no lock and opened every one, a bottled storm that churned slow and silver behind its glass like a thought trying to become weather.

"You're investigating lot forty-one," Hale said, before she'd finished sitting. "May I ask why? The item was worthless. I said so myself."

"Worthless things don't get brokered through your house. Your fees would eat the sale."

"Sometimes we take worthless things because the world is full of grief, Inquisitor, and grieving families need somewhere to put the objects they can't keep and can't burn." He folded his hands. His knuckles were wrong somehow—too smooth, like wax. "What is it you think we've done?"

It was a trap question, and she declined it. "I think you handled a book whose contents were described as illegible by an appraiser who can read eleven dead scripts. I think your clerk flinched at the lot number. And I think someone in this city recently wrote me an anonymous letter using phrases that appear only in restricted Crown archives." She let that sit. "I'm not accusing you of anything yet, Master Hale. I'm telling you what I see. You may tell me what you see."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—something crossed Hale's face that she had seen only twice before in her career, both times on men who knew exactly when they were going to die. Not fear. *Scheduling.* The look of a man consulting an internal calendar.

Then it was gone, and he smiled, and the smile was warm and terrible and perfectly calibrated.

"I see a diligent officer," he said, "and a wasted morning. The book was junk. The buyer was a collector of junk. And whoever wrote to you knows less than they think." He rose, ending the audience the way a judge ends one. "But do come back anytime, Inquisitor. We deal in impossible things. I imagine you and I will have a great deal to discuss eventually."

Walking back out into the gray afternoon, Rook found her hands were cold, and it took her a block to understand why.

Because Hale hadn't denied anything. He hadn't blustered or lawyered or threatened. He had *confirmed* her—every suspicion, every inference—with the serene confidence of a man reading from notes. As though her arrival were not a surprise but an appointment. As though somewhere in that paper-smelling building there was a page with her name on it, and today's date beside it, and a line waiting to be written about what she would do next.

She stopped at the corner of Candlemaker's Court and looked back at the green door.

Prophecy was sedition. Prophecy was helplessness dressed as knowledge. Prophecy had killed her sister by teaching her to stand still and watch. Fifteen years of arithmetic said so, and the sum had never once come out different.

But standing there in the rain with the smell of beeswax and ash still in her nose, Dessa Rook felt, for the first time, the other side of the equation—the thing Wren must have felt, holding that scrap of vellum in a secondhand coat. The vertigo of suspecting that the world had a text, and that someone else was holding it.

She wrote in her case journal that night, by lamplight, in her own disciplined hand:

*Guild of Impossible Things. Concealment confirmed. Subject: Corvane Hale. Note: he expected me.*

And below it, because she was honest even with herself, especially with herself:

*Request archive access, Edict-era fragments, full set. There is a door in these prophecies. I intend to find it before they do.*

Three streets away and one floor up, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and loneliness, a ledger-keeper named Silas Joubert was copying accounts by candlelight, unaware that her name had just entered a second ledger—that of the Crown—and that the arithmetic of her life had begun, column by column, to close.

The thirteenth turn was coming. Now it had two hunters.
