## 5. A Country That Isn't There

The Guild's archive lived in the basement, which was appropriate, because everything the Guild preferred not to think about lived in the basement. Silas had spent four years cataloguing it and still hadn't reached the far wall. There were ledgers bound in eel skin, ledgers bound in bark, one ledger that appeared to be a brick until you looked at it sideways. There were deeds to houses that had burned down before their owners were born. There was a drawer labeled *Wishes, Unfulfilled* that nobody opened twice.

She went down at dawn with a lamp and a cup of cold tea, because the archive was honest in a way people weren't, and she needed honesty this morning more than she'd ever needed it before.

The stolen page lay against her ribs, folded into quarters inside her shirt, where it had spent the night growing heavier. She had weighed it once, out of habit—paper, ink, a scrap of calfskin binding. Four ounces, maybe less. It should not have been able to press on her lungs like a paving stone. But there it was: her name, in a hand a century dead, and tonight's date beneath it, and then the sentence that stopped mid-word as though the writer had been interrupted by something worth interrupting for.

*On the thirteenth turn of the moon, the door of Aurelle opens to her who—*

To her who what.

She set the lamp on a reading table and pulled down atlases.

Here was the thing about the country of Aurelle: it did not exist. This was not a matter of opinion or of borders shifting, which happened often enough that cartographers kept erasers in business. Aurelle had been *unmade*. In the oldest atlas in the collection—a survey commissioned three kings ago, its pages soft as cloth—the space where Aurelle should have been was not blank and not water. It was a wash of gray, deliberate and even, like a censor's brush, with a single line of annotation in faded sepia:

*Aurelle. Removed by decree. Do not survey.*

Silas ran her thumb along the gray. Someone had painted over a country the way you'd paint over a stain, and every map since had inherited the silence. The newer atlases showed forest there, or hills, or nothing much at all—terrain so unremarkable no one had ever bothered to walk it. One mapmaker had simply written *poor ground* across two hundred square miles and moved on.

Poor ground. She almost laughed. Somewhere under that apology for geography was a country with a name, a language, a door—and a prophecy that knew her handwriting before she'd learned to hold a pen.

*Opens on remembering.* She'd read those words forty times in the dark of her room, holding the page close to the candle. The rest of the book—the parts she'd skimmed before stealing the crucial leaf—had been the usual prophetic porridge. Kingdoms rising, crowns falling, a comet shaped like an eye. But the Aurelle passages were different. They were specific. They were *bookkept*, if she could call it that: dates given as balances, names entered as debts. A door that opens on remembering. A price entered in advance. And a woman whose name matched hers to the last letter.

Which meant someone, a hundred years ago, had known her.

She worked through the morning the way she did everything—methodically, cross-referencing, letting the archive talk. War records were kept upstairs in the Crown's section, but the Guild kept its own versions, because the Guild kept accounts of everything, including the things kingdoms pretended hadn't happened. She found the war in a ledger from ninety-eight years back, entered between a shipment of reliquaries and a dispute over a haunted mill.

*Paid: to the Crown's quartermasters, in settlement of the Aurelle action, 40,000 crowns.*

*Received: from parties unknown, one (1) sealed directive regarding the disposition of survivors. Contents not inspected per terms.*

*Note: The Guild is asked to forget this entry. Fee waived.*

Someone had paid the Guild to forget. And the Guild—which forgot nothing, which was the entire point of the Guild—had accepted the fee and filed the memory where no one would look, in a basement, under dust, in a hand so cramped it might as well have been cipher.

Silas sat very still.

There were survivors. The directive concerned the *disposition* of survivors. You disposed of cargo, of bodies, of inconvenient papers. You did not dispose of people—except that apparently you did, a hundred years ago, and the people who arranged it had wanted it forgotten badly enough to pay professionals.

She copied the entries into her notebook in her own tight script, and then, after a moment, copied them again onto a separate slip of paper, because she had already learned this week that some knowledge was safer in duplicate—one copy for keeping and one for losing on purpose, whichever necessity required.

Her hands were steady. She noted that too, clinically, the way she noted everything: *Subject exhibits no tremor. Subject may be in shock.*

Ivet the cartographer kept rooms above a chandler's shop in Tanners' Row, which meant his maps smelled faintly of tallow and his landlady smelled faintly of him complaining about it. He was seventy if he was a day, thin as a compass needle, with ink stains up both forearms like old bruises and eyes the pale gray of unfinished vellum. He'd been a client of the Guild for thirty years—not for anything impossible, just appraisals. He sold a map now and then, and he liked to know what it was worth, and he liked more to be told the truth about it, which made him rare among clients and precious to appraisers.

He also owed the Guild eleven crowns, carried on the books for six years, and had never once mentioned it, which Silas found more honorable than most men's honesty.

"You look terrible," he said, opening the door. "Sit down. Don't touch the wet ones."

Two maps hung drying from a line strung across the ceiling, weighted at the corners with chandler's offcuts. Silas sat carefully among the drift of paper—maps everywhere, rolled, flat, stacked in strata like sediment—and put her notebook on her knee.

"I need to ask you about a place," she said.

"Places are my trade."

"Aurelle."

Ivet went still in a way she'd never seen him go still. Not startled. Something older than startled. He stood with his teapot half-poured for the count of three breaths, then finished pouring anyway, as if the ritual of it would carry him past the word.

"No one says that name," he said. "You understand that. Not in a hundred years has anyone said it to me aloud."

"The maps say it doesn't exist."

"The maps say what they're told." He handed her the cup and lowered himself into his chair, and for a while he only looked at his own hands, which were trembling very slightly, which she understood completely, because hers had been all morning. "Why do you ask?"

She had prepared a lie on the stairs. She discarded it now, partly because Ivet deserved better, and mostly because she was so tired of lying to herself that extending the practice to him felt like a waste of good craft.

"Because my name is in a book a century old," she said. "With tonight's date next to it. And the book talks about a door in Aurelle."

The teacup rattled against its saucer. He set it down with exaggerated care, the way she handled the bottled storms, the way everyone handled things that might go off.

"Show me."

She unfolded the page on the table between the drying maps. Ivet bent over it, and she watched him read, and she watched something move behind his face—recognition, yes, but underneath the recognition something worse, something like homesickness for a place he'd never admitted missing.

"This hand," he murmured. "I know this hand."

"From where?"

"From nowhere. From—" He straightened, pressed his knuckles against his mouth. "Miss Joubert. I'm going to tell you something I've never told anyone, and I'd take it kindly if you didn't have me committed afterward."

"I keep secrets professionally."

"Yes. Yes, I know. That's why I'm telling you." He crossed to a chest in the corner—oak, iron-bound, locked with a key he wore on a cord around his neck—and lifted out a folder of loose sheets. He laid them on the table one by one, like a man laying out evidence at his own trial.

They were roads.

Beautiful roads—Ivet's linework was famous for its clarity—running through countryside rendered in loving detail: hedgerows, milestones, bridges, a chapel with a squat bell tower, fields divided by low stone walls in a pattern Silas had never seen in any province of the Crown. Village names in a script she couldn't read. A city on a river bend, drawn with such precision she could have counted its towers.

"Where is this?" she asked.

"That's rather the question, isn't it." Ivet sat down heavily. "I drew them. Every one. Over forty years. I've never seen any of these places. I've never met anyone who has. And I want to be clear with you, Miss Joubert, because I am a professional and I do not use the word lightly: these are not inventions. When I draw from imagination, I know it. There's a looseness to invention, a freedom. These aren't loose. These are *remembered*. I remember drawing them the way you remember copying something already in front of your eyes. Only nothing was in front of my eyes except—" he tapped his temple— "in here. Roads I've never walked. A language I've never spoken, and yet I can write it, see? Fluently. Read it aloud, even, though I don't know what most of it means."

He picked up the sheet with the city on the river and turned it toward her. At the bottom edge, in that same fluent unknown script, was a dedication of sorts—a cartographer's colophon, the little formal note mapmakers signed their work with.

"What does it say?" Silas whispered.

"I've never been sure. Best I can make out, it says: *Drawn from the memory of the city itself.*" He laughed, short and awful. "A city with a memory. A city asking to be remembered. What kind of nonsense is that?"

Silas looked down at the page against her ribs, at the words she knew by heart now. *The door of Aurelle opens to her who—*

Opens on remembering, the earlier passages said.

And here was an old man who had spent forty years involuntarily remembering a country that officially did not exist, drawing its roads in perfect detail, hearing its cities ask to be remembered.

"It's not nonsense," she said. "I think you're one of the door hinges."

Ivet stared at her. Then he began, very quietly, to laugh—the real kind this time, shaky and disbelieving, the laugh of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton.

"Hinges," he said. "Forty years I thought I was going mad. Mad men don't get dedications from cities." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, leaving a gray smear of ink. "There's more. You'll want the whole account if we're doing accounts. Three times in my life I've dreamed the same road—start to finish, every milestone, every stone wall. I woke the third time and drew it without thinking, the way you'd write down a phone number. When it was done I sat looking at it, and I swear to you on my mother's grave that I knew, absolutely, where it *ended*. Not on the paper. On the earth. I could feel the direction of it like a toothache. Northeast. Past the gray on the maps. Into poor ground."

Silas's skin prickled. "How far northeast?"

"Four days' ride, if the road exists. Five if it only exists when you're remembering it properly." He looked at her, and his pale eyes had gone sharp and young. "That's the part that frightens me, Miss Joubert. Sometimes, walking home at dusk, I pass streets I've drawn. Streets that shouldn't be there. And I think—maybe the road doesn't exist *until* someone remembers it. Maybe that whole country has been sitting behind a curtain for a hundred years, held shut by everyone agreeing not to look. And maybe—" his voice dropped, "—maybe it's getting tired of waiting."

The lamp guttered, though the windows were shut. Outside, a church bell rang the hour, and got it wrong—rang twelve when Silas's watch said half past eleven—and neither of them remarked on it, because remarking felt dangerous, like stepping on ice to test it.

"I need to go there," Silas said. "Before tonight. Before the thirteenth turn—I don't even know how many turns are left, but the page gives tonight's date, so whatever it wants from me starts now."

"Tonight," Ivet repeated. His eyes went to the window, to the thin daylight. "Then we'd best leave within the hour. And Miss Joubert—" he gathered his maps back into the chest, moving fast now, decisive, sixty years of professional habit converting fear into logistics, "—before we go, there's a small piece of business. Eleven crowns. Six years on your guild's books. I'd like it settled."

Silas blinked. "Now?"

"Debts should be cleared before long journeys. My mother's rule. Also—" and here he smiled, thin and wry, "—if your book is right and this road ends somewhere the maps gave up on, I'd rather walk into it owing nothing. Clean pages. It matters, at the end of things, what you still owe and what's been paid."

She thought of the ledger in the basement. *The Guild is asked to forget this entry. Fee waived.* Thought of her own name in a dead man's book. Thought of a god who had gone silent, and whatever balance stood between them, unpaid, unspoken, accruing interest for fifteen years.

"All right," she said. "Eleven crowns, struck from the debt-book. You'll have my mark on it before we ride."

Ivet nodded once, satisfied, and began rolling maps into a leather case—roads to a country that wasn't there, drawn by a man who'd never been, dedicated by the city itself.

Silas folded her stolen page along its old creases and slid it back against her heart, and noticed, for the first time, that the final interrupted word had begun to trouble her less like a question and more like an instruction.

*To her who remembers.*

Outside, the bell rang again. Twelve, again. Insistent, this time.

Neither of them said what they were both thinking: that bells ring twelve twice only for one reason.

That somewhere, the twelfth hour had already started, and did not intend to finish.
