## 7. Ledgers Do Not Lie

The oldest ledger in the Guild of Impossible Things was kept in a room that did not officially exist.

Maren had worked for the Guild eleven years, and it had taken her nine to earn the key. It was a small iron thing, cold as a coin pulled from a well, and it opened a door on the third landing that most members walked past without seeing. Corvane called the room the Deepstack. Everyone else called it nothing at all, because acknowledging it felt like acknowledging a debt you hadn't yet been billed for.

She went down on the last night of the eleventh moon, alone, with a lamp, a pot of ink-thinner, and Silas's absence pressing against her like weather.

Silas had gone out. That was the whole of it—gone out, at an hour when honest ledger-keepers were asleep or pretending to be—and she'd taken something with her. Maren had seen the shape of it under her coat: paper, folded small, held the way you hold a wound closed. She'd said nothing. She'd watched her friend walk out into the fog toward the river district and told herself it was guild business, appraisal business, some bottled storm or haunted mirror that needed collecting at midnight because those things always needed collecting at midnight.

Then she'd come down here instead of going home, because lying to yourself is easier with work in your hands.

* * *

The Deepstack smelled of cedar shavings and old vellum and, beneath both, something faintly mineral—the smell of stone that has never seen sun. Shelves climbed into darkness overhead. The ledgers here were not like the working books upstairs, all smudged columns and coffee rings and Maren's own careful marginalia. These were the Guild's memory. Every debt ever contracted, every impossible thing ever brokered, every price ever paid, going back past the founding, back past—

Back further than it should go, actually. That was the thought that had brought her down tonight, the splinter under her skin. The Guild's charter said one hundred and forty years. The oldest ledger's spine said otherwise. She'd noticed it a month ago and told herself the charter was wrong, charters often were, and then she'd spent a month not thinking about it, which is its own kind of labor.

Tonight she took the first volume off the shelf and set it on the reading table and opened it, and the lamplight fell across pages that made her breath go strange.

They were accounting entries. Of course they were. Everything in this building was accounting entries; Corvane said once, laughing, that God kept double-entry books and the Guild merely kept up the practice. But these entries were wrong in a way Maren's trained eye caught before her mind could name it.

*Received: one country, condition used. Paid: silence, in perpetuity.*

She read it three times. Her hand went to the date column and stopped there, hovering, because the date was impossible. Not illegible, not smudged—impossible. The entry was dated ninety-eight years ago, and it was written in an ink that had not faded, in a hand she knew better than her own mother's.

Corvane Hale's hand. The particular way he crossed his sevens. The little hook he put on his terminal r's, like a man pulling a door shut behind him.

Maren sat down slowly on the reading stool, because her knees had opinions about standing.

He was sixty years old. She had drunk wine with him at midwinter. She had watched him lose at cards and grieve genuinely over a dead client's dog. His hands were a living man's hands, liver-spotted and steady, and they had not been writing ledgers ninety-eight years ago, because he had not existed.

Except the hand said otherwise. And ledgers do not lie—that was the first law of the house, older than the charter, older than the shelves. A ledger might be misread, misfiled, misunderstood, but it did not lie. If the book said Corvane Hale had written an entry ninety-eight years ago, then Corvane Hale had written an entry ninety-eight years ago, and the world simply had to make room for that fact, the way it made room for bottled storms and doors that only opened from one side.

She turned the page.

*Paid to V. Joubert: one life, held in trust. Terms: service until called.*

Her mouth went dry. Veth Joubert—not Silas, but close enough that the letters seemed to reach off the page and take her by the collar. She knew the surname was common in the river wards. She knew that. She read the entry again anyway, and again, hunting for some other explanation, and found none, and found also that her heart was doing something ugly and hopeful and afraid all at once.

* * *

She worked through the entries in order after that, because order was how she coped, and there were so many of them. Page after page in Corvane's hand, decades apart by their dates but seamless as a single sitting. And as she read, a shape emerged from the accounting the way a figure emerges from fog—not suddenly, but inevitably, so that by the time she could see it whole she couldn't remember the world without it.

It was a prophecy. Transcribed as bookkeeping.

Not in the language of prophets—no burning wheels, no thrones of cloud—but in the language of debts and deliveries, which was somehow worse, because debts could be *managed*. She read:

*Liability recorded: Aurelle, one nation, outstanding balance of grief. Due date: thirteenth turn of the moon, year of the hollow tide.*

*Asset acquired: one keeper, female child, born within the borders of above. To be raised in ignorance of origin. Depreciation schedule: none. This asset must not learn what it is.*

And below that, in smaller script, cramped against the margin as if the writer had been ashamed of it or hurried or both:

*Payment withheld from Veth: voice. Duration: until fulfillment. Note—do not let it speak to her. It will warn her. Silence is load-bearing.*

Maren put the lamp down because her hand had begun to shake and she didn't want to crack the glass.

*Do not let it speak to her.*

Silas prayed. Everyone knew Silas prayed—to Veth, the silent god, the god who never answered, the joke of the upper floors, *old Silas and her quiet god*. Eleven years Maren had watched her friend fold her hands over the day-book and murmur into that silence, patient as rain, faithful as arithmetic. And now a ledger entry, written before either of them was born, said the silence was not the god's nature.

It was a term of contract. Someone had bought it.

Someone had bought it, and the someone wrote in Corvane's hand.

* * *

She didn't know how long she sat there. Long enough that the lamp burned low and she had to feed it oil with hands she ordered to be steady, one motion at a time, like a woman learning her trade over again. Long enough that the cold of the Deepstack got into her joints and made a home there.

She thought about going to Corvane. That was the correct procedure—anything touching the Deepstack went to the guildmaster, immediately, no exceptions, and Maren was a great believer in procedure. Procedure was what kept impossible things from eating people. You followed the forms, you logged the anomalies, you trusted the structure, and the structure held.

But the structure was the thing that was wrong. The structure had Corvane's handwriting all through it, dated across a century, and if she carried this to him he would look at her with those kind, tired eyes and say something reasonable, and she would believe him, because she always believed him, and then she would be—what? Complicit? She tested the word and flinched from it.

Or he would do something else. She found she could no longer guess what.

She thought about going to Silas, and that was worse, because Silas had gone out tonight carrying a folded page held like a wound, and Maren suddenly understood—with the terrible clarity that arrives too late to be useful—that whatever Silas had found, it had found her first. The entries on these ancient pages weren't predictions scattered at random. They were instructions. *To be raised in ignorance of origin.* *This asset must not learn what it is.* Silas wasn't a keeper who had stumbled into a prophecy.

She was the prophecy's principal asset. Its collateral. Maybe its payment.

And Maren had watched her walk into the fog and said nothing, and was sitting here saying nothing still, and every minute of silence was a line item accruing somewhere in a book she couldn't close.

* * *

Footsteps on the stair.

Maren killed the lamp so fast she singed her fingers on the wick, and stood in the dark with her heart slamming, listening. The steps were unhurried, familiar, weighted toward the left heel. Corvane's tread. Of course. He came down here sometimes; everyone knew he came down here sometimes; that was normal; that was fine.

The door opened. Lamplight from the landing spilled across the floor and stopped just short of the reading table where the old ledger lay open, and Maren stood very still beside the shelf with her back against the cedar and her hand flat against a volume titled, in gilt worn to ghosts, *Deliveries Outstanding.*

Corvane crossed the room. She heard him stop at the table. Heard the particular silence of a man reading something he already knows by heart.

"You've been at the first volume," he said, to the dark, in the mild voice he used for appraisals. "I can tell. You shelved it half a finger out of true."

Maren stepped into the light. There was no point hiding; he'd known she was there before he opened the door, probably before he started down the stairs. "Guildmaster."

"Late night." He looked at her over the lamp he'd relit, and his eyes were kind and tired and bottomless, and for the first time in eleven years Maren understood that the kindness and the tiredness might both be performances, or might both be real, and that she could no longer tell the difference, and that this was perhaps the most frightening thing the Deepstack contained. "Trouble sleeping?"

"I was checking a provenance," she said. It was even true. "For the Delverne estate."

"Mm." He closed the old ledger. Closed it gently, the way you close a coffin. "Find anything?"

Every answer she owned lined up behind her teeth. *Yes. Your handwriting from before your birth. A country sold for silence. A child raised as collateral. My best friend purchased before she was born.* She looked at his face and thought about Silas walking home through the fog right now, trusting the Guild, trusting her, trusting the world Maren was at this moment deciding whether to keep propped up like a stage set.

"No," Maren said. "Nothing unusual."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—relief, or satisfaction, or grief; she couldn't sort them and didn't try. "Good," he said. "The old volumes are mostly administrative. Charters, fees, quarrels about storage rates. Nothing worth losing sleep over." He tucked the ledger under his arm, and paused at the door, and gave her the smile he gave clients when a deal closed clean. "Go home, Maren. Get some rest. Big week coming. Thirteenth turn of the moon is nearly on us, and I want the books balanced."

He left. She listened to his footsteps climb away, unhurried, weighted toward the left heel, and stood in the cold until the lamp burned itself down to a blue nub.

*Thirteenth turn of the moon is nearly on us, and I want the books balanced.*

He knew she'd read it. He had to know. And he'd let her lie to his face and walked away satisfied, which meant her lie had served him—which meant, in some accounting she couldn't yet read, she had just made her first entry in a book she wanted no part of.

Maren gathered her things with hands that no longer shook, because they had passed through shaking into something colder and more useful. Upstairs, in the working light of the scriptorium, she would pull the current debt-book and find Ivet the cartographer's page, and Corvane's, and anyone else's whose names appeared in those impossible entries, and she would know exactly what each of them owed and to whom.

Because ledgers do not lie. That was the first law of the house.

And somewhere in the fog between here and the river wards, Silas Joubert was carrying a stolen page toward answers that a hundred-year-old account book had already priced—and Maren had just promised the author of that account book that she'd seen nothing at all.

She blew out the lamp, locked the door that did not exist, and went up to find out what her silence was going to cost.
