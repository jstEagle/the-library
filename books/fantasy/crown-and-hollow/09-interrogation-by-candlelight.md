## 9. Interrogation by Candlelight

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its records in a cellar that smelled of tallow and old paper, and Inquisitor Dessa Rook had been sitting in it for three hours, waiting for the candle to matter.

That was a trick her first mentor had taught her, back when she was still young enough to believe the Crown's work was simple. *Never interrogate by daylight,* he'd said. *Daylight makes men brave. A single candle makes them honest, because they can watch it burn down and know exactly how much time they have left to lie in.* She had watched many candles since. She had learned that most people broke not from fear but from arithmetic—the quiet, grinding calculation of what a lie would cost them versus what the truth would. Rook was good at arithmetic. It was, if she was honest with herself, the only sacrament she had left.

The woman across the table from her had been doing sums too. Rook could see it in her eyes, which moved—methodically—to the candle every few minutes, gauging the wax, then back to Rook's face with nothing readable in them at all.

Maren Voss. Second ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. Thirty-one years old, unmarried, no debts of consequence, no vices the Watch could find except an expensive taste in ink. Her file was thin because her life was thin, and Rook had learned long ago that thin files were either honest lives or very good liars, and there was no way to know which until the candle burned lower.

"You're comfortable?" Rook asked. It was a foolish question, asked softly, the kind one asks a guest.

"I'm chained to a chair," Maren said. Her voice was even, pleasant, the voice of a woman reading figures aloud. "But the chair is well-made. Whoever your carpenter is, he does good work."

"He's retired now. He confessed to everything within the hour, both times we brought him down here." Rook turned a page of the folder before her, though she did not need to look at it. She knew its contents the way she knew her own hands. "Maren Voss. You've kept the Guild's accounts for nine years."

"Eleven. The first two were apprenticeship. I wouldn't count them as mine."

"Eleven years of accounts." Rook set down the folder. "And in eleven years, how many impossible things has your guild actually sold?"

The question was a knife slipped in sideways, and Rook watched Maren feel the edge of it—not flinching, but noting the cut. "That depends on what you'd call impossible, Inquisitor."

"Let's be precise. That's the whole point of ledgers, isn't it? Precision." Rook leaned back. "I've read your guild's seized manifests. Bottled storms. Love drawn off like water. A door that opens on yesterday. Those are the items listed for sale. My question is whether anyone ever bought them."

The candle hissed. Somewhere above them, the safehouse creaked—it was one of the Crown's own, a debt-collector's front on Candlemaker's Row, chosen because no one ever looked twice at a building where people went in frightened and came out poorer.

"Why does it matter," Maren said, "whether the goods were real?"

"Because," Rook said, "there are two kinds of crime in this room, and I'd like to know which one I'm charging you with. The first is fraud. Selling lies to grieving people at grieving prices. It's ugly, but it's small, and frankly the Crown doesn't much care—if fools want to buy bottled weather, that's their tithe to their own hopelessness. The second crime is sedition." She let the word sit. "Prophecy. The selling of futures. And that, Maren Voss, the Crown cares about very much."

Something changed in Maren's face. It was small—a stillness arriving where motion had been—but Rook had spent eleven years learning to read stillness the way sailors read water, and she saw the current beneath it.

"You've been accused of worse," Maren said.

"Yes. I have." Rook did not look away. People expected her to deny it, or to perform hurt, and the denial itself became a lever. Instead she gave it plainly: "When I was twenty-three, I wrote a report suggesting that the famine forecasts issued by the Temple of the Turning Year were prophetic in nature, and therefore treasonous, and should be suppressed. My superior filed it. The following spring, a woman named Liss Rook—a forecast-reader, third rank, my older sister—was arrested for the same crime in a different city, tried in secret, and burned. She was guilty of nothing except writing down what she believed would happen, which is what forecast-readers do, which is what I do." Rook folded her hands on the folder. "So yes. I've been accused of worse. And I am the reason my sister is ash. That's why they gave me this commission. I'm thorough about prophecy because I cannot afford not to be."

She had told this story perhaps six times in her career, always as a blade—here is my wound, and here is why my wound gives me authority over yours. But tonight, saying it in a cellar that smelled of tallow, watching the candle flame bend, she felt the other thing she never said: that she did not hunt prophecy because she hated it. She hunted it because she needed it to be a lie. Because if the future could truly be known—written a hundred years early, sealed into books and ledgers—then Liss had not died for anything she chose. She had died as a character dies, on a page someone else had written, and all of Rook's careful arithmetic was just ink following ink.

She needed prophecy to be fraud. Fraud could be punished. Fate could only be obeyed.

"That's a sad story," Maren said quietly. "It's also true, which is rarer. You didn't have to tell me the true version."

"No. I didn't."

"So you're offering me something. Truth for truth."

"I'm offering you this." Rook slid a sheet across the table, under the candle's light so Maren could read it without straining. "An anonymous tip, delivered to my office four days ago. It names your guild. It says—and I quote—*'They are not selling what is written. They are making what is written come true. Ask the second ledger-keeper about the old book.'*"

Maren read it twice. Her expression did not change, but her breathing did—one shallow catch, quickly mastered. Rook marked it. *Ask the second ledger-keeper.* Someone who knew the Guild's hierarchy intimately had written this tip. Someone with elegant handwriting, if the quality of the penmanship meant anything, and it usually did.

"Do you recognize the hand?"

"No."

"Would you recognize it if you saw it again?"

"Everyone in the Guild writes beautifully, Inquisitor. It's a professional requirement. You'd be recognizing all of us or none of us."

Rook smiled, thinly. "You see, that answer is a lie, but it's a *useful* lie, and I appreciate useful things. Here's what I know, and you can spend your remaining time deciding how much of it matters to you." She stood and began to walk the perimeter of the cellar, slowly, boots soft on stone. "Three nights ago, an estate sale was held at the Harrowgate house, on Weeping Lane. Among the lots catalogued was a book bound in grey calfskin, roughly a century old, containing prophecies. The catalogue entry for that lot was altered after printing—the page number scratched out and rewritten in a hand I'm told resembles your guild's house style. The book itself vanished from the sale before the auctioneer's men reached it. Two days later, my agents raided the Harrowgate house and found, tucked into a chimney, this."

She returned to the table and laid down a scorched fragment of paper, no larger than a playing card. On it, in faded brown ink, a few words survived:

*...the thirteenth turn shall find her at the hollow, and the door...*

"The rest burned," Rook said. "Or was made to look burned. Now. Here is what interests me, Maren Voss. If your guild stole a century-old prophecy book to *sell* it, you'd have sold it. That's what sellers do; inventory is a cost. Instead, someone risked exposure to remove a single page—or to make me think a single page was removed—and the remainder went into a chimney. Which means the book wasn't merchandise." She stopped walking. "Which means I have to ask myself a question I have never had cause to ask in eleven years of this work. Not *who is trading in prophecy.* Something else entirely."

The candle guttered, steadied.

"And what question is that?" Maren asked. Her voice was still level. But her hands—chained, resting on her knees—had gone white at the knuckles, and Rook thought: *there. There is the floor dropping out from under you. I know that feeling. I lived inside it for eleven years.*

"I ask myself," Rook said, "whether the Guild of Impossible Things has ever sold anything at all."

Silence. Long enough that the flame bent and righted twice.

"Look at your own manifests," Rook pressed, softer now, almost gentle, because gentleness was a crowbar when applied correctly. "Bottled storm, catalogue number 411. Sold seven times over eleven years—always bought back by the Guild itself within a season, through intermediaries, at a loss. A love-charm, number 88, same pattern. A coin that lands on its edge, number 12, purchased and repurchased four times. Your guild buys its own miracles back, Maren. No merchant does that. Merchants let go of inventory. Curators don't. *Keepers* don't." She sat down again, leaning forward into the candlelight so her face filled Maren's vision. "You're not a shop. You're a reliquary. And the thing you're keeping—"

"Don't," Maren said.

It was barely a word. It came out cracked, and Maren closed her eyes immediately afterward as if she could take it back down off the shelf, and Rook felt the whole cellar shift around them, the way a room shifts when a load-bearing wall cracks.

"—is coming true," Rook finished quietly. "You're not fulfilling orders. You're fulfilling a prophecy. The sales, the buy-backs, the theatrical appraisals—it's cover for something else. Custody. You're keeping something on schedule. And whatever schedule it is—" she tapped the burnt fragment, "—it's running out, isn't it? Thirteenth turn of the moon. That's soon. That's weeks."

Maren opened her eyes. And for the first time in three hours, Dessa Rook saw the whole woman behind the ledger-keeper: exhausted, terrified, and—this was the part that unsettled her—*resolute*. Not the resolution of a criminal protecting stolen goods. The resolution of someone standing guard.

"If I tell you nothing," Maren said carefully, "what happens to me?"

"You go to the Crown's cells pending trial. Sedition charges. You'll likely hang; prophecy cases don't pardon, they make examples." Rook held her gaze. "If you tell me the truth, I have discretion to classify this as fraud instead. Fines. Closure of the guild. Unpleasant, survivable."

"And Silas?"

Rook kept her face still, but inside, something rang like a struck bell. *Silas Joubert. First ledger-keeper. The name had appeared nowhere in the tip, the manifests, or the raid evidence—and yet here it was, offered freely, the one name Maren had guarded through three hours of candlelight and finally lost custody of in a moment of fear.*

"Silas Joubert," Rook repeated. "Your first ledger-keeper. Where is she?"

Maren's jaw tightened. The crack had already done its damage; now she simply stopped talking altogether, folding her silence around the name like a cloth around broken glass.

Rook sat back. She should have pressed—every instinct, every technique, eleven years of craft said press now, while the wound was open. Instead she found herself looking at the candle, nearly half-gone now, and thinking about her sister, and about the phrase from the anonymous tip that had been circling her mind all evening like a bird that would not land.

*They are not selling what is written. They are making what is written come true.*

If it was true—if some zealot or schemer had spent a century arranging a prophecy's fulfillment—then everyone caught in the mechanism was doubly damned. Guilty in law, innocent in fact. Actors punished for a play they hadn't auditioned for. Liss had been such an actor. So, God help her, might the quiet woman chained across the table, guarding a name that wasn't hers to give.

"Inquisitor," Maren said suddenly. Her voice had changed—lower, urgent, and Rook realized with a jolt that this was the first fully honest thing the woman had said all night. "Whatever you think you've understood—you understand less than nothing. You're holding one candle in a country that's been dark for a hundred years. If the thirteenth turn comes and the door isn't opened, it won't be one guildmaster who hangs. It will be—" She stopped herself, hard, teeth closing on the sentence.

"A door," Rook said. "The tip mentioned a door. The fragment mentions a door. *The thirteenth turn shall find her at the hollow, and the door.* What door, Maren?"

But Maren had shut. Completely. She looked at the candle and began, with dreadful calm, to count the remaining wax aloud, as though timing herself for something.

Rook rose, gathered her papers, and signaled the guard at the stair. As she reached the bottom step, Maren spoke once more, not counting anymore, not looking at her.

"She'll balance it," Maren said. "Whatever it costs. She always balances it. That's all you need to know about Silas Joubert, and I'd thank you not to make me sorry I told you."

* * *

Upstairs, the night was cold enough to bite, and Rook stood on the safehouse steps letting it do so, turning the burnt fragment over in her gloved hand.

*...the thirteenth turn shall find her at the hollow, and the door...*

A door that a guild of accountants guarded like a relic. A first ledger-keeper whose name surfaced only under pressure. An anonymous tip written in beautiful handwriting by someone who wanted the Crown hunting the very conspiracy he described—which meant either a traitor settling scores, or an architect tidying loose threads, feeding the hounds to move the quarry.

Rook looked at the fragment a long moment. Then she took out her notebook and, by the light spilling from the doorway, wrote a single line beneath her notes:

*Reframe: not criminals fulfilling greed. Custodians fulfilling text. Find the author of the text.*

She underlined *author* twice, put the notebook away, and called for her horse. The moon was up—thin, a day past new, climbing toward its turns. Eleven left, by her count.

She intended to reach the Guild's first ledger-keeper before the twelfth.

Somewhere across the sleeping city, on roads she could not yet see, Silas Joubert was already traveling toward the same hollow, and the door in it, and the hundred-year lie waiting on the far side of both.
