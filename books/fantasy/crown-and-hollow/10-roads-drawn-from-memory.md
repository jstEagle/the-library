## 10. Roads Drawn From Memory

They left at the hour the city called the dead watch, because Ivet insisted that roads drawn from memory were easiest to see before dawn, when the world's own lines went thin.

Silas had argued for daylight. Daylight was when she did her best work—when ink dried properly, when appraisals could be made by honest light, when a thing was what it was and nothing else. But Ivet had only looked at her with those pale cartographer's eyes and said, "You want to find a country that isn't there. You don't get to also want it to be convenient."

So they went in the dark.

He had a mule named Biscuit who smelled of wet straw and grievance, and he had his satchel of instruments: compasses that pointed at things other than north, a brass alidade worn smooth by forty years of thumbs, and a roll of paper so old it had gone the color of tea. And he had the map. Not on paper—the map was in him, Silas understood now, folded into whatever part of a man holds his dreams. He walked with his eyes half-closed, one hand out like a man feeling along a wall only he could touch, and where he walked, Silas followed.

The road out of Vell's Crossing was ordinary for the first three hours. Ruts, mud, a shrine to some minor harvest saint with coins gone green in its cupped stone hands. Silas counted their pace the way she counted everything, marking distances in her ledger-mind, and found the numbers comforting. Numbers did not lie. Numbers did not have dates in them that matched your name.

"You keep tallying," Ivet said without turning around. "I can hear you doing sums back there."

"Someone has to know how far we've come."

"And what will you do with knowing? Frame it?"

She didn't answer. The truth was that knowing was all she had left. Three days ago her life had been a balanced column; every entry reconciled, every debt accounted, every prayer spoken into silence and filed away neatly under *unanswered*. Now there was a page torn from a century-old book hidden against her ribs, and her name written on it in an ink that should have been dry before her grandmother was born, and the sentence it ended on—

*On the thirteenth turn of the moon, Silas Joubert shall—*

Shall what. She had read the fragment four hundred times. Shall open. Shall die. Shall remember. Shall be unmade. The sentence simply stopped, as if the man writing it had been interrupted, or had grown afraid of his own pen, or—as she was beginning to suspect—had wanted it to stop exactly there, so that everyone who read it would lean forward into the dark after it.

"Tell me about the dreams again," she said. "From the beginning. Leave nothing out because you think it sounds mad. Mad is the least of my problems."

Ivet shifted the alidade on his shoulder. For a while there was only Biscuit's hooves and the wind moving through hedges.

"I was thirty-one," he said. "A journeyman mapmaker, good but not brilliant, working the coast roads for the Admiralty. And I dreamed a road. Not near a road—a *particular* road. White stones set on edge, both ditches full of blue flowers I'd never seen, running up a hill to a gate with two towers. I woke up and drew it, because that's what I do, the way you'd wake up humming. Then I dreamed it again. And again. Eleven nights running, same road, same gate, same flowers. On the twelfth night I dreamed what was past the gate." His voice did something complicated. "And then I stopped sleeping, for a while."

"What was past the gate?"

"A city," Ivet said. "Clean streets. People walking. A fountain shaped like a woman holding up a broken crown. Everyone in it was—" He hunted for the word. "Patient. That's the only way I can say it. They weren't waiting, exactly. They were *patient*, the way stone is patient. And every dream, the city was a little further away. Like someone carrying it off down a long hall, and me standing still, watching them go."

"Aurelle."

"No one says that word," Ivet said mildly. "You said it like it costs nothing."

"It doesn't cost me anything. It's not mine." Yet. The thought arrived uninvited and she put it down firmly, the way you'd pin a moth to a board.

They came off the King's Road before sunrise, at a place where a drover's track bent north through beech woods, and here Ivet stopped, closed his eyes fully, and stood swaying slightly like a man listening to music through a wall.

"It's here," he said. "The white-stone road. It runs through these woods. It has always run through these woods. Only—" He opened his eyes and looked genuinely apologetic. "Only it isn't there."

Ahead of them the beeches stood grey and leafless in the pre-dawn, and between them there was no road at all, just leaf-litter and root and the general indifference of forest floor.

"But you can walk it anyway."

"I can walk where I remember it being. Whether the ground agrees to hold me is another question." He smiled, and for the first time Silas saw that beneath the wry cartographer there was a man profoundly frightened, who had been frightened for years, and had gotten very good at folding the fear small. "Keep your hand on my pack strap. If the ground disagrees, don't let go of me. Things that fall off remembered roads don't always fall onto ordinary ones."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate. Reassurance is for people selling you something."

Silas took the strap. Her other hand rested on the ledger in her coat—the real one, paper and leather, her whole professional self—and on the stolen page beneath it. Then Ivet stepped forward off the track and into the trees, and the ground held.

It held, and it was wrong.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. One moment her boots pressed leaf-litter that crackled honestly; the next, the same stride landed on something smoother, harder, faintly warm, and when she looked down she saw white stones set on edge, marching ahead of them through the beeches, each ditch brimming with blue flowers that had no business blooming in the cold. The flowers were the exact blue of the ink on the prophecy page. She decided not to think about that, and failed immediately.

The road ran uphill through trees that grew sparser as they climbed, and the light came up strange. Not wrong-colored—wrong-*timed*. The sun cleared the hills behind them and threw shadows forward, long and sharp, and Silas noticed that her shadow lay pointing the way they were going, which was correct, and then noticed that Ivet's lay beside hers but *longer*, much longer, stretched thin as poured honey across the white stones, though they walked side by side.

"Your shadow," she said.

"Don't look at it," Ivet said. "Looking encourages it."

"Encourages it to do what?"

"To keep going when we stop."

She looked away from it. She was a ledger-keeper; she knew about entries that got worse the more attention you paid them.

* * *

By midmorning they had passed out of the beech woods into country that Silas could not name, and this bothered her professionally before it frightened her personally. She had spent fifteen years appraising estates across four provinces. She knew the crops, the hedgerows, the dialects of field walls. This land fit none of her categories. The fields were divided by low walls of pale stone laid without mortar, fitted so precisely a knife blade wouldn't slip between them. The wheat—if it was wheat—stood in rows too straight, heads too heavy, gold too deep, as if the color had been applied rather than grown. There were no crows. There were no fences mended with different wood than the original. Everything was either perfect or abandoned; there was no middle state, no comfortable decay, no human mess.

"It's been kept," she said slowly. "All of it. Like a house shut up with sheets over the furniture."

"Now you understand why I stopped taking commissions after the first year," Ivet said. "I tried to chart this place from memory. Do you know what happens when you draw a map of somewhere that doesn't exist and then check it against the world?" He shook his head. "The world starts deferring to the map. Little things. A stream moves six feet to match my line. A hill softens its slope. I burned every sheet and swore off dreaming, and then the Crown's survey office offered me a fortune to locate certain roads near the Aurelle border, and I remembered what was past the gate, and I told them no, and they told me yes was not optional." His jaw tightened. "That was nine years ago. I've been drawing false maps ever since. Beautiful ones. Full of deliberate errors. It's the only sabotage a mapmaker gets."

"The inquisitors will have your true charts."

"They'll have my errors. Rook's people seized my office in Vell's Crossing yesterday, I'd wager. Let them follow my beautiful lies into marshland." He glanced sideways at her. "You flinched at her name."

"She questioned my friend Maren two nights ago. Maren gave her nothing, but Rook doesn't need much. She does arithmetic. That's what makes her dangerous—she's not a zealot, she's an accountant with a warrant." Silas heard herself and almost laughed. "God help me, I respect her."

"Respect her from far away, then."

At noon they stopped at a well beside the road—a round stone well with a roof of slate and a bucket on a chain, perfectly preserved, impossibly clean. Ivet drank. Silas looked into it instead, and the water looked back with the flat patience of a mirror, and far down at the bottom, where a coin should have been, there was a key.

"Leave it," Ivet said quietly.

"It's just a key."

"Nothing here is *just* anything. That well has been waiting for someone to want that key since before either of us was born. Wanting is how it gets you." He wiped his mouth. "In Aurelle, they say, desire was a door. That's why the door only opens on remembering, and never on wanting. Remembering is looking back. Wanting is reaching through."

"Who told you that? Who's *they*?"

"My dreams," Ivet said, "have a narrator. Don't ask me to describe the voice. Every time I try, I lose a week."

They walked on. In the early afternoon Silas saw the birds.

There had been no birds all day, which she had noted in her ledger-mind under *anomalies* with a small, steady dread. Then, over a ridge of pale hills ahead, a flock rose—starlings, or something shaped like starlings, thousands of them, boiling up into the sky in that fluid way flocks have. Except the flock was flowing backward. Wings beat and the birds lost ground. They wheeled in patterns that unwound as she watched, spirals tightening in reverse, until the whole murmuration sank tail-first into the grass of the hillside and vanished, and the grass did not so much as bend.

Silas stopped walking. Her hand found Ivet's pack strap again without her deciding to reach for it.

"They flew backward," she said.

"Yes."

"Why backward?"

"Because we're walking into yesterday," Ivet said. "Or yesterday is walking toward us. Or—" He stopped himself, and she watched him do the thing he must have done a thousand times alone in his study: take the impossible fact, fold it small, and put it in the pocket where he kept his fear. "The twelfth hour," he said instead. "That's what my narrator calls this country. The twelfth hour of a clock that struck thirteen once. Everything here is the last moment before midnight, held. Nothing decays because decay would mean time had moved on. Nothing changes because change was what the war brought, and they refused it."

"Who refused?"

"The survivors. The ones behind the door." He looked at her then, really looked, with an expression she couldn't file anywhere. "You asked me once why I agreed to guide you, when I've spent nine years refusing kings. I'll tell you now, since we're past the point where lying helps. Because when you walked into my shop and said the word *Aurelle*, the narrator in my dreams went quiet for the first time in a decade. Silent. Listening. Whatever's been talking to me in my sleep for ten years, Silas—it knows your name. And it's afraid of you, or hopeful of you, and I need to know which before the moon turns."

Silas said nothing. Against her ribs, the stolen page felt warm as a coal.

* * *

Toward evening the road began to descend, and the wrongness deepened from uncanny to unbearable in small increments, the way water heats.

First the shadows. As the sun lowered behind them, every hedge and wall and standing stone cast shadows eastward, and the shadows began arriving *early*. Silas watched a milestone throw its shadow a full minute before the sun's angle allowed it—an impossible black finger pointing down the road, twitching slightly, like a dog dreaming. Then her own shadow peeled away from her boots and lay pointing down the road too, and it did not twitch, and somehow that was worse.

Then the light itself. It thickened. Not dimmed—thickened, going amber and slow, so that Biscuit's trotting legs seemed to move through honey, and when Silas raised her hand she saw five fingers and the ghost of five more, trailing, as if her gesture were arriving late to itself.

"We're close to the border of it," Ivet whispered. "The edge of the keeping. Past here, the twelfth hour leaks."

"Leaks into what?"

"Into us. Look at your hands. Count your fingers."

She counted. Five. She counted again. Five, and the ghost-trails fading, and a cold sliding feeling under her sternum, the sensation of a number refusing to reconcile.

And then, last, the sound. All day the country had been quiet in the specific way of held breath. Now, as the amber light thickened toward a sunset that seemed in no hurry to finish, Silas heard bells. Far off, down in the hollow where the land gathered itself into hills and shadow, bells ringing—not a peal, not a warning. A single bell, striking once, then a pause too long, then once again. Patient. Counting.

"Twelve strikes," Ivet said. His voice had gone thin. "It always stops at twelve. Ten years of dreams and it has never struck thirteen. Tonight's the eleventh turn of the moon. Two nights left, if your book is right."

"It's right," Silas said. "It's been right for a hundred years."

They crested the last hill in the thick amber light, and below them lay the hollow.

There was no city. There was no gate with two towers, no white road continuing down, no fountain shaped like a woman holding a broken crown. There was only a bowl of green land, unnaturally smooth, empty as a swept floor, with the single bell-tower standing alone at its center—just the tower, roofless, its bell hanging silent between strokes—and around it, in a wide ring, the grass grew in patterns. Rows. Foundations. Streets' worth of foundations, traced in paler green, the ghost-print of a city pressed into the earth like a flower in a book.

Every foundation was exactly the shape of a home.

Silas stood at the hilltop with the wind moving through her coat, touching the page against her ribs, and felt awe arrive in her chest like a swallowed sun—and then curdle, instantly, completely, into fear. Because the bell below struck again, and this time she counted the interval, because counting was the only prayer she had, and the interval was shortening. The bell was speeding up. Twelve had never sounded so much like a countdown.

And in the doorway of the roofless tower, small with distance, a figure stood watching them. It did not wave. It did not approach. It simply stood with the patience of stone, and raised one hand—not in greeting.

In invitation.

"Ivet," Silas said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; she would examine that later, the way her body stayed calm while the rest of her fell. "How many people did you say lived in your dream-city?"

"Thousands," Ivet whispered. "Why?"

"Because there's someone in the tower," Silas said, "and I don't think it's been waiting for me."

Behind them, back along the white road they had walked since dawn, hoofbeats sounded—many horses, moving fast, and above the tree line, thin and unmistakable in the thickening amber air, a whistle. Three notes. The Crown's riders, calling to one another in the old patrol code.

Rook had found the road after all.
