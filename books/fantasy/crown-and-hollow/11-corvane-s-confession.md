## 11. Corvane's Confession

There is a room beneath the Guild of Impossible Things that does not appear on any floor plan, because a man who plans to live a hundred years learns early not to write down the one thing that matters.

Corvane Hale went down to it the night after Silas brought him the page.

He had burned it, of course. Or he had held it over the candle long enough for her to believe he'd burned it, and then closed his fist around the flame and let the paper die in his palm instead of the fire, because some things you do not feed to fire twice. Fire has witnesses. A fist has only knuckles and whatever secrets they can hold.

Now he sat at the desk in the dark room with a fresh ledger open before him, and he did not write in it. He simply needed it there, the way an old soldier needs a sword across his knees when he tells the story of the war. The leather was cracked along the spine. He had rebound it himself four times in a century. The pages inside were older than the binding, older than the Guild, older in places than the Crown whose inquisitors were even now sharpening their arithmetic against him.

"Speak," said the god.

Veth's voice came from everywhere in the small room and nowhere in particular — from the stone, more than anything, the way cold comes from stone. It was thinner than it had been. A hundred years ago it had filled this chamber like water filling a cup. Now it was the ring left by the water, the ghost of a tide line, and Corvane had learned to lean toward it the way deaf men lean toward conversation.

"I don't know what to say," he admitted. It was not a thing he said often. In a century, he could count the occasions on one hand and still have fingers left for prayer.

*You have known what to say since the night I first spoke through you,* Veth said. *Say that.*

He laughed. It came out wrong — too dry, too short, the laugh of a man who has forgotten where laughter lives in the body. "That isn't a confession. That's a crime."

*All confessions are crimes,* said the god. *Otherwise they would be called conversations.*

* * *

The night it began, Corvane Hale was twenty-six years old and dying of a fever in a rented room above a dyer's shop in a city called Aurelle.

He remembered the city better than he remembered his own mother's face, which he supposed was the point of what happened next. Aurelle had been beautiful in the way of things built by people who expected to be remembered: pale towers, bridges like drawn bows, a river so clear the fish seemed to swim through air. He had come as a young accountant with a talent for numbers and no talent whatsoever for staying alive in plague season, and the fever had taken him on a Tuesday, and on Wednesday something sat down beside his bed.

It did not look like anything. That was the first thing he understood about Veth. Every other god wore shapes — bulls, ravens, mothers, kings. Veth wore silence the way other gods wore crowns, and when it chose to be perceived at all, it was perceived the way you perceive a person standing just behind your shoulder: not seen, but absolutely, terribly *known*.

*I can save you,* it said, into the fever and the dark. *But I am dying faster than you are, and I cannot do both alone.*

"What do you want?" Corvane asked. His voice was a ruin. He remembered being proud of himself for haggling even at death's door; it seemed to him then, and still seemed to him now, the most honest moment of his life.

*A bargain. I will give you years — more than any man is given. And in exchange, you will hold my words when I can no longer hold them myself. You will be my hand. My ledger-keeper.* A pause, and the pause itself had weight, like a coin set down on a table. *And one day, when I ask, you will give me back.*

"Give you back what?"

*Everything,* said Veth. *I do not know yet what shape that takes. Neither do you. Sign anyway.*

So Corvane Hale, twenty-six years old, sweating out his life on a stained mattress in a city that had eleven months left to exist, signed anyway.

The fever broke by morning. By the following spring, Aurelle did not exist.

* * *

Here is what the histories refuse to record, because the histories were written by the winners and the winners had written themselves out of the story first:

Aurelle was not destroyed in the war. Aurelle was *withdrawn*. Its king had made a bargain of his own — with what power, Corvane never learned, though he had spent decades suspecting it was with prophecy itself, with the idea of a foretold victory — and when the war turned against them, the whole country had folded itself away behind a door that opens only on remembering, and its people had stepped inside, and the maps had gone blank, and the world had agreed, with the terrible fluidity of the world, to forget.

Except that a country is not a purse you can hide under a floorboard. A country is a debt, and debts accrue interest. Behind the door, Aurelle did not age and did not hunger and did not die — and did not live, either. Eleven moons passed outside for every year inside, or perhaps the reverse; Corvane had stopped trying to make the arithmetic behave. The survivors stood in their pale towers like figures in a music box, waiting for someone to wind them, and the winding key was memory, and memory rots.

Veth was the god of that country. Not its patron — its *substance*, in a way Corvane still did not fully understand. When Aurelle withdrew behind the door, Veth went with it, and a god shut out of the world's remembering is a god bleeding out slowly through a wound the size of a nation.

*I wrote the book,* Corvane said to the dark room. "You know I wrote the book."

*I dictated it,* Veth corrected, gently, in the way that a surgeon is gentle. *Through your hand. You were awake for all of it. Do not let yourself pretend otherwise. Pretense is how men like you rot.*

Men like you. As if they were two kinds of creature, the god and the man, instead of two halves of one bargain grown old together.

"Yes," Corvane said. "I dictated it. We dictated it." He opened the old ledger at last, to the page near the front where everything began, and read aloud, because reading it aloud was the only penance that had ever felt like penance. "'In the hundredth year of the hollow moon, a keeper of accounts shall find her name already written, and the date beside it, and the sentence unfinished, because the ending belongs to her.'"

"You gave me the frame," he said. "Every word of the frame. But you left the middle blank, Veth. You left a hundred years of blank middle, and told me to fill it, and I want you to hear what I filled it with, because you were quiet tonight and I need — "

His voice failed. He was one hundred and twenty-six years old, and his voice failed like a boy's.

* * *

The blank middle looked like this:

It looked like a foundling house fire in the borderlands, arranged with a moneylender's precision, so that a cartographer named Ivet — a man who owed the Guild a debt and dreamed of roads — would happen to be passing and would carry out an infant girl with pale eyes.

It looked like a series of small, patient nudges across fifteen years: a recommendation here, a coin placed there, a rumor seeded in the right ear at the right market, until the orphan girl Silas Joubert walked up the steps of the Guild of Impossible Things and asked, in a voice that tried very hard not to hope, whether they took apprentices.

It looked like Corvane Hale standing in his own doorway, looking down at a scrawny, furious, meticulous nine-year-old who reminded him of no one, because she reminded him of *himself* — the same hunger to be useful, the same armor made of competence — and saying, "We take anyone who can keep a ledger straight," while inside his chest something that had once been a heart turned over like a page.

It looked like love. That was the monstrous part. He wanted to say that to the god plainly, and he found he could not, so he said the accounting version instead.

"She trusts me," he said. "Fifteen years, and she trusts me the way she trusts nothing else in this world. She brings me her doubts wrapped in reports. She stands a little straighter when I praise her work. Last winter she was sick with lungfever and I sat outside her door three nights because if she died the prophecy died, and I told myself that was the reason, and it was a lie, Veth. It was a lie and we both know it. I sat there because she is *mine*. I chose her name out of a list of forty-one foundlings because her grip was strong, and somewhere between choosing her and raising her, the choosing became something else, and I do not know when, and I do not know how to put it in a book."

The silence stretched. Stone-silence, cold-silence. Then:

*You know exactly when,* Veth said. *The night she prayed to me for the first time.*

Corvane closed his eyes.

She had been fourteen. She had knelt on the cold flagstones of the guild chapel — a bare room, a bare altar, because Veth required no ornament, only attention — and she had said, in the flat careful voice she used for inventory: *"They say you're the god who listens without answering. Good. I don't need answers. I just need somewhere to put things. So: today Maren got the plum cake at dinner and I didn't, and I'm not angry about the cake, I'm angry that everyone always assumes I don't want the cake, and I wanted you to know that somewhere, so it doesn't sit in me."*

And she had crossed herself in the manner of Veth's silent faithful — one finger pressed to the lips, then laid flat on the breastbone, *word kept, word kept* — and gotten up and gone back to work.

*And you stood in the doorway,* Veth said, *and you thought: she is speaking to the part of me that is dying, and she doesn't know it, and if the bargain holds she will be the last voice I ever hear, and I could not bear it, and I loved her in that instant the way a drowning man loves the shore — which is to say desperately, and selfishly, and with every intention of reaching it regardless of what it costs the shore.*

"Yes," Corvane said. "That."

*Then say the rest of it,* said the god, and its thinness now was almost tenderness. *Say the part you came down here to say.*

* * *

"The thirteenth turn is in nineteen days," Corvane said.

He laid his hands flat on the old ledger, on a century of his own handwriting, entries in three different inks because ink goes out of production and a man must adapt, entries that recorded purchases and bribes and deaths and one birth, all of it arranged, all of it steered, all of it *composed*, so that a woman who believed her life was her own would walk, step by accurate step, into a sentence written before her grandmother was born.

"In nineteen days she stands at the hollow. In nineteen days she opens the door with her blood — the blood of Aurelle, which is why I stole her out of it, why she had to be *born* there, why I — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Why I took an infant from the only home she had and delivered her into a life of ledgers and loneliness, because the prophecy requires a child of the hollow, and there were only three children of the hollow left outside the door, and the other two died of ordinary causes, and God forgive me, I remember feeling *relieved*."

The stone drank the words.

"And when she goes in, she does not come out. That is the price. The seal requires a keeper on the far side — a living mind, a remembering mind, holding the door shut from within forever. She stays. Aurelle sleeps. You — " and here his voice did fail again, fully, and he let it. "You end, Veth. Inside, with them, painlessly, at last, the way you've begged me for sixty years. The bargain completes. The debt closes. Everyone is saved except the one person I — "

He pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth.

Except the one person he loved more than the god, more than the Guild, more than the hundred borrowed years that had let him build all of this. Except Silas, who trusted him, who brought him her doubts wrapped in reports, who had stood in his office two nights ago holding a stolen page and watching his face go white, and who had seen enough — because she was not fooled, she had never once been fooled, that was the curse of raising a child in your own image — to understand that his fear was not for her safety but for his *plan*.

"She'll fight it," he whispered. "When she learns the whole of it, she'll fight it. She'll hate me. She should hate me."

*Yes,* said Veth.

"You could tell her yourself. Answer her. One word, one dream, one sign — she has prayed to you for fifteen years, you owe her — "

*I owe her everything,* Veth said, *which is precisely why I will not spend it. If I speak to her, she comes to the hollow as a servant of a god. If she comes as a servant, she stays as a prisoner. But if she comes of her own will — knowing the cost, choosing the cost — then the door is not a cell. It is a vow. There is a difference, Corvane. You of all people know there is a difference. It is the difference between us and everything else that calls itself divine.*

"It's a difference that gets her killed."

*It's a difference that makes her free,* said the god. *Even inside the door. Especially inside the door.*

Corvane sat for a long time. Somewhere above him, muffled by stone and floors, the Guild went about its evening: footsteps, the clink of the appraisal room, Maren's laugh — bright, loyal, doomed-to-be-heartbroken Maren, whom he would also spend like currency if the arithmetic demanded it, and knew it, and despised himself for knowing it, and would do it anyway, because that was what a hundred years of bargains made of a man: a ledger that walked and lied to itself about having a heart.

At last he rose, took the candle, and did the thing he had done every night for fifteen years and would do for nineteen more.

He climbed the stairs to the corridor outside the apprentices' wing, and he paused outside her door, where a thread-thin line of lamplight showed beneath it, and he listened to the small sounds of Silas Joubert working late — the scratch of her pen, the turn of a page, once, very softly, the sound of her speaking to a god who never answered.

"Word kept," he murmured, and pressed one finger to his lips, and went to his study to write tomorrow into motion.

Because the reports had come in that evening, and two of them were bad. Rook had raided the estate house hours behind Silas. And Ivet the cartographer had erased his own name from the debt-book and bought passage east — east, toward the hollow — and Corvane had read the departure manifests three times, hoping he was wrong, knowing he was not.

She was going early. Without the moon. Without the plan.

And far to the east, in a hollow where a country slept behind a door of memory, something that had been waiting a hundred years felt her coming, and began — faintly, faintly, for the first time in fifteen years — to answer.
