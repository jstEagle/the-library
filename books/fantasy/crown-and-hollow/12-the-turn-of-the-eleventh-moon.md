## 12. The Turn of the Eleventh Moon

The road to Aurelle did not exist, and so they traveled it anyway.

Silas had learned in the first two days that Ivet's dream-roads obeyed their own arithmetic. They bent where no river ran. They climbed hills that the maps insisted were valleys. Twice they had walked through a stand of pines that shed needles upward, drifting back to their branches like rain reversed, and Silas had stopped cataloguing the wrongness because cataloguing was a habit for a world that still made sense.

Now it was the eleventh night, and the moon came up thin as a paring knife.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower—Crown-built, centuries old, its stones furred with moss that grew in the shape of script. Ivet said it wasn't script. Ivet had been saying very little since the birds began flying backward at noon, all of them at once, a whole sky of small bodies rowing against the wind with the grim efficiency of things following an order.

"Eleventh turn," Ivet said now, feeding the fire stick by stick, as if fuel were a language he was trying to speak softly enough not to be overheard. "One more after this."

"I can count," Silas said.

"You count everything. It's what I like about you." He didn't look at her. "But counting won't help tomorrow night. Tomorrow the road stops being a road. It becomes a door, and doors want to be opened by someone specific."

She knew. She had known since the hollow first appeared on the horizon that morning—a dip in the land like a cupped palm, holding shadow the way other places held water. Something in her chest had leaned toward it, the way a plant leans toward light, and she had hated the leaning, because it hadn't felt like hers.

It had felt like being remembered by something.

* * *

She took first watch because she always took first watch, and because sleep had become a country she could visit but not stay in. The fire burned low. Ivet slept sitting up, his satchel of impossible maps clutched to his chest like a child's doll, and above them the moon thinned another degree, and Silas opened her ledger out of pure habit and found she had nothing to enter.

That frightened her more than the backward birds. Fifteen years of entries. Fifteen years of writing down every small impossibility the Guild bought and sold—the bottled storm, the widow's borrowed hour, the debt paid in names—and every entry had been a way of saying *the world is legible, the world keeps its promises, I am a woman who can read it.* Now the ledger lay open on her knees and her pen wouldn't move, because there was no column for *I was born in a country that doesn't exist and my whole life has been arranged around it like flowers around a coffin.*

So instead she prayed.

She hadn't stopped praying in fifteen years, even though Veth never answered. That was the joke Maren used to make—that Silas kept books for a god of silence and prayed to him nightly, the most one-sided correspondence in the Guild's history. But Silas had reasoned it out long ago: a god who went quiet was not necessarily a god who left. A debtor who stops writing is not necessarily freed of the debt. And she had sworn herself to Veth in exchange for survival, and survival was a payment plan, and you did not stop honoring a payment plan just because the other party had gone dark. That was bad bookkeeping. She would rather be damned than be a bad bookkeeper.

"Veth," she said, to the fire, to the thinning moon, to the hollow waiting on the horizon like a held breath. "I don't know if you're there. I don't know if you were ever there. But if you are—if any part of you is still listening—I need to know one thing."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind them, in the dark, a bird called once and then thought better of it.

"Was it real?" Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "The vow. The answer. You answered me once. Fifteen years ago, when I was starving in the temple ruins and I swore myself to you, you answered—one word, one warmth, one *yes*, whatever it was. I've built fifteen years on that single coin. And now Corvane tells me you dictated a prophecy, that my life is a sentence you started before I could walk, and I don't know anymore whether I chose you or whether you were chosen for me. So I'm asking. Not for rescue. Not for power. Just—" her hands tightened on the ledger, "—just tell me if we were real. You and me. The one true entry in a falsified book."

Nothing.

Wind moved through the tower stones, making the moss-script shiver. The moon pared itself thinner. Silas closed the ledger, and was reaching to bank the fire when the night broke open behind her.

Not sound first—light. A white bloom of lantern-signal from the ridge east of the camp, then its answering twin from the west, and she understood with a cold, professional clarity that they had not stumbled on the camp. They had surrounded it hours ago and waited for the moon to rise high enough to see by.

"Crown," Ivet breathed, awake instantly, and there was no time for more because the soldiers came down the slope in a wedge, six of them, seven, eight, chainmail whispering, crossbows leveled, and a sergeant's voice ringing flat across the hollow: *"In the name of the Crown, stand and be counted!"*

Stand and be counted. Even now, some cold clerk-corner of Silas's mind appreciated the phrasing. Everything was accounting, in the end. The Crown counted heads; the Guild counted debts; prophecies counted down.

*Ivet,* she wanted to say, *run,* but running was already happening to both of them, chaos exploding outward—fire scattered, satchel torn, Ivet's voice cracking on her name—and then a crossbow bolt took him through the shoulder and he went down into the ferns and she was alone in the open with eight soldiers closing like a book.

She ran. Of course she ran. She was a ledger-keeper, not a hero; her body knew only the oldest arithmetic, self minus danger. She got perhaps thirty yards, far enough to see the trees ahead resolve into three more soldiers she hadn't counted, and then the world simplified to a single line of force: a crossbow bolt meant for her spine.

She heard it leave the string. She had time to think, absurdly, of the bottled storm on the Guild's shelf, of how she had once appraised it and wondered what a trapped thing dreamed about—

And she prayed. Not the nightly prayer, not the careful, liturgical, one-sided correspondence. She prayed the way a drowning woman grabs at anything, words tearing loose without grammar: *Veth, please, please, I know you're quiet, I know maybe you were never there, but if you were, if any of it was real, if I was ever anything to you but a line in your book—*

The bolt stopped.

It did not miss. It did not strike wood or deflect off armor. It stopped, mid-air, a hand's breadth from her shoulder blade, hanging in the moonlight like a fish suspended in ice, and the whole night stopped with it. The soldiers froze mid-stride. The fire's smoke hung in a gray ribbon. Even the moonlight seemed to have thickened, gone syrupy, and in that terrible beautiful pause Silas felt it again for the first time in fifteen years:

Warmth. Behind her sternum. Faint as a coal under ash, flickering, guttering—but there. And with it, pressed against the inside of her skull like a mouth against a window, a presence she knew the way you know a voice you heard once, long ago, in the worst night of your life.

*Little keeper.*

The words were not words. They arrived the way meaning arrives in dreams, whole and aching, and they were so faint that she felt how much it cost to send them, felt the presence straining toward her like a man shouting through a wall with his last breath.

*You asked if we were real.*

"Yes," Silas whispered. The frozen bolt trembled in front of her eyes. "Yes. Tell me."

*Real. Ours. The one entry I did not write.*

Something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing. Fifteen years of prayers into silence, fifteen years of wondering if she'd built her soul on a misreading—and here, at the end, the god she served had spent what little remained of itself to say: *you were mine, freely, and I never arranged that.*

*Listen.* The warmth guttered, steadied. *I am dying. Corvane's bargain is eating me from the edges. I have strength for this one act and almost nothing after. So hear me, keeper, because I will not be able to say it twice.*

The soldiers hung frozen around her, faces slack, a drop of sweat suspended on a sergeant's jaw. The bolt hung. The moon hung.

*The prophecy is a cage with two doors. Corvane knows one door: blood and obedience, the thirteenth turn, the keeper enters and never returns. That is the letter he wrote with my failing voice. But I dictated a century ago through a dying man's mouth, and dying mouths slur. There is room in the letter he does not know is there. Memory opens the door—not blood. Remembering, not sacrifice. He built your life to fit his reading. Build yours to fit mine.*

"What's the other cost?" Silas asked. She was a ledger-keeper; she knew every gift carried a figure in the opposite column. "What do you pay? What do I?"

The presence hesitated, and in the hesitation she felt its fear—vast, ancient, undignified fear, a god afraid the way animals are afraid, with its whole body.

*Tomorrow, when you open the door, you will meet me face to face. And I will ask you to stay. The prophecy requires a keeper sealed inside with me forever. If you refuse—* the warmth flickered like a candle in wind— *if you refuse, I do not know what happens. To me. To Aurelle. To you. Refusal was never written. You would be improvising, little keeper, with a country and a god as your materials.*

"And if I say yes?"

*Then you keep me company while I end. Alone, in the dark, for however long a dead god takes to finish dying. That is the door Corvane built for you. That is the salvation he purchased with your whole life.*

The warmth began to fade. She felt it going the way you feel sleep leaving, limb by limb, and she reached for it with everything in her.

"Wait—Maren. Rook. The soldiers—"

*Your friend burns for you even now. Your enemy carries a name she does not know is written. Go to the hollow. Trust the cartographer's roads and nothing else. And Silas—*

The last of it gathered itself, spent the last coin in its purse:

*When you stand in front of me tomorrow and I ask you to stay—remember that I released you from the vow tonight. Before you decide. So that whatever you choose, you choose free. That is my last miracle. That I let you go before you ever had to run.*

And the night resumed.

Sound crashed back in—boots, shouts, the bolt finally completing its arc and burying itself in a tree trunk with a thunk that sounded obscenely ordinary. The soldiers blinked, confused, their moment of frozen purpose smeared into hesitation, and Silas did not waste the gift. She turned and ran for the ferns where Ivet lay bleeding, hauled him up with strength she didn't know she had, and ran.

Behind her, someone shouted the sergeant's question again—*stand and be counted*—and no one stood, and no one was counted, because the counting had failed, because something had reached across fifteen silent years and subtracted her from their arithmetic.

* * *

They walked most of what remained of the night, Ivet leaning on her shoulder, the bolt wound bound with strips of her shirt. When the sky went gray they sheltered in a culvert beneath the dream-road, and Ivet, feverish, watched her with narrowed eyes.

"The bolt stopped," he said. "I saw it. I'm a mapmaker, Silas. I record things. The bolt stopped."

"Yes."

"That was your god."

She looked at her hands. They were steady. She was almost proud of them. "He answered," she said. "For the first time in fifteen years. One conversation, and it nearly killed him." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Do you understand what that means? It means he was always there. Every night I prayed into the dark, he was there, listening, unable to speak, letting me believe I was talking to nothing. Fifteen years of one-sided letters—and he read every one."

Ivet was quiet for a while. The culvert dripped. Somewhere above, the false dawn brightened toward a day that would end at the hollow.

"There's a word for that," Ivet said finally. "When someone watches you suffer and says nothing, and calls it love."

"Don't." Her voice came out flat and hard. "Don't you dare. You weren't there. You didn't starve. You didn't swear yourself to the only thing that ever chose you and then spend fifteen years faithful to a silence." She stopped, breathing hard, and made herself say the rest, because a ledger-keeper records even the entries that shame her. "And now he's dying, and his last act was to free me from my vow so that I could choose him honestly. And I don't know yet whether that's the most beautiful thing anyone's ever done for me or the cruelest trap ever baited. Both readings balance. That's what frightens me. The books balance either way."

Ivet touched her wrist, gently, his hand shaking with fever. "Then tomorrow," he said, "when you stand in front of him—check the figures yourself. Don't take anyone's word. Not his. Not Corvane's. Not even mine."

She nodded. And above the culvert, through the gap where the dream-road crossed the gray sky, the moon set thin as a fingernail clipping, one turn from gone.

One more night. One more turn. And somewhere ahead, in a hollow shaped like a cupped palm, a door waited that opened on remembering—and inside it, faint as a coal under ash, something that loved her was waiting to ask her to stay forever.

Silas closed her eyes and, for the first time in fifteen years, did not pray.

She was afraid that, this time, something might answer.
