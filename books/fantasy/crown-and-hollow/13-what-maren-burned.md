## 13. What Maren Burned

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its oldest ledger in a room with no windows, which was appropriate, because the things written in it could not bear to be looked at by daylight.

Maren had known about the ledger for eleven days. She had spent those eleven days doing what she did best, which was nothing at all — carrying trays, balancing columns, laughing at Ivet's jokes, asking Silas about her travels with a face so carefully bored that Silas never once suspected she was being studied. That was the trick of ledger work. You did not hide the truth by locking it away. You hid it by burying it under ten thousand ordinary entries until no one thought to dig.

But Maren had dug. And what she had found in Corvane's hand, dated decades before the ink that carried it, was not an entry. It was a sentence being served.

Tonight, while Corvane dined with the Crown's tax assessor upstairs — an appointment Maren herself had arranged, three weeks ago, for exactly this reason — she unlocked the windowless room and took the ledger down from its shelf.

It weighed more than it should have. She had told herself that was sentiment, but her arms knew better. Some books are heavy because they know what they carry.

She laid it open on the table beneath a single candle and turned to the entries in Corvane's hand. *Received: one god, dying. Paid: one hundred years of arrangement.* And below it, transcribed in the tidy accounting script they were all taught as children, line after line of prophecy dressed up as inventory. *Item: a keeper born behind a door. Item: a debt collected on the thirteenth turn.* Item, item, item. A woman's whole life broken into lots and valued.

Maren read it through once, slowly, the way you'd read anything you intended to burn. She wanted to be able to say later that she had known all of it, every word, before she fed it to the flame. Loyalty deserved honesty even when honesty was unbearable.

Then she took out her knife and began cutting pages free.

The binding resisted. Old glue, old pride. She worked the blade along the spine with the patience of someone who had repaired ledgers for twenty years, and the pages came away in sheaves, whispering against each other like leaves remembering autumn. She stacked them neatly — of course she stacked them neatly; she was who she was — and then she paused, knife resting on the table, and asked herself the question she had been refusing to ask for eleven days.

*Is this treason?*

Rook would say so. Rook, the Crown's inquisitor, the woman who had already come once to the Guildhouse with her arithmetic and her tired eyes and her list of questions that were really knives. Prophecy was sedition; the law said so; the war that erased Aurelle had been fought over less than this. If Maren burned these pages and was caught, she would hang for it, or worse, because there was always worse when the Crown felt frightened.

And if she didn't burn them, Silas would hang instead.

That was the whole arithmetic. It fit on one line. Ledger work, in the end, was simple.

Maren struck a match against the table's edge and touched it to the first page.

Corvane's handwriting curled brown, then black, then lifted off the paper entirely in a breath of smoke that smelled like every year he had stolen. She fed the fire another page. Another. The candle guttered in the sudden heat, and the little windowless room filled with light that came from the wrong direction, and Maren stood in it with her sleeves rolled back and ash settling on her forearms like gray snow, and she found, to her surprise, that she was not afraid.

She was furious. Fury is braver than fear. It burns hotter and asks fewer questions.

Because she understood now, fully, what she had only suspected when she first found the entries: that Corvane had not merely written a prophecy. He had built a person. He had taken a starving orphan off the street and set her on rails laid a century before she was born, and called it love, and entered it in the books as *paid in full.*

Silas deserved better than to live out someone else's accounting.

So did Veth, probably, though Maren had never prayed to the silent god and did not intend to start now. Still — she glanced at the last unburned sheaf, the one containing the earliest entry, the bargain itself — still, there was something obscene about destroying even a god's death warrant without a moment's silence.

She gave it a moment's silence anyway.

Then the door opened behind her, and the moment ended.

"You're doing it wrong," said Inquisitor Dessa Rook. "Paper burns better if you fan it. May I?"

She did not wait for permission. She crossed the room in four unhurried steps, took the iron poker from beside the cold hearth, and used it to stir the burning pages, breaking up the clumps, feeding air into the heart of the fire until the flames roared and the last of Corvane's century-old handwriting went up in a single bright gasp. Maren stepped back, knife still in her hand, and watched the most dangerous woman in the city save her the trouble of finishing the job.

"Thank you," Maren said, because her mouth had decided that composure was the only weapon left, and committed to it completely.

"Don't thank me." Rook set the poker down. Up close, in the firelight, she looked worse than she had during the raid — thinner, grayer around the eyes, like a woman who had been doing sums in her head for days and disliked the total. "I've been watching this house for six nights. Do you know what I've seen? Deliveries. Appraisals. A man teaching his daughter card tricks. Your guildmaster dining with the tax assessor right now, boring him to death over brandy." Her voice was flat and very quiet. "I came tonight expecting to find seditious texts. Instead I find you destroying them."

"The Guild keeps its accounts private," Maren said. "Even from the Crown."

"No one burns accounts, ledger-keeper. Accounts are money. Money is the one thing the Guild has never pretended to be above." Rook pulled out the room's single chair, turned it, and sat down facing the fire with her hands folded over her knee, unhurried as winter. "You burn evidence. So either you're guilty, or you're protecting someone who is. And I've read your file, Maren Ostrelle. Twenty years of service. Not one late entry. Not one discrepancy. Women like you don't commit crimes. They just decide, once, very carefully, that a crime is the correct answer to a question. I'd like to hear the question."

Maren looked at the fire. Ash floated up past the flue in slow spirals. Somewhere above them, faintly, Corvane's laugh came through the floorboards, warm and practiced, the sound of a man who had been performing charm for a hundred years and had worn the grooves smooth.

"You think we're selling prophecies," Maren said.

"I thought so. Six nights ago." Rook's eyes reflected the fire, two small orange coins. "Now I think something worse. I think you're fulfilling one. There's a difference, you understand. Selling prophecy is greed. Fulfilling it is faith. And faith is what started the war that took Aurelle off the maps. Faith is what killed—" She stopped. The pause lasted half a heartbeat too long, and Maren, who had spent two decades reading other people's ledgers, read the pause like an open book. "Faith killed people," Rook finished, evenly. "A great many people."

"Your sister," Maren said quietly.

Something moved behind Rook's face — not anger. Something older and more exhausted than anger. "You knew that."

"It's in the Guild's files. We keep files on everyone who matters. It's how we stay impossible." Maren held the inquisitor's gaze. "I'm sorry. Truly. But whatever grief brought you to this door, you're holding the wrong people responsible."

"Am I?" Rook rose. She walked to the shelf where the ledger usually sat, ran her finger along the empty space in the dust where it had lived for a century, and turned back. "Here's what I know, Ostrelle. Three weeks ago an anonymous tip named this house. Two weeks ago your cartographer friend paid off a debt no one can find the original entry for. Ten days ago your guildmaster ordered a page burned and a woman watched — I have a witness inside these walls, don't bother asking who. Five days ago Joubert left the city heading east, alone, toward country that doesn't exist. And tonight you're burning the only documents in the world that explain why." She tilted her head. "I'm an arithmetician of crimes. This sum is nearly balanced. All I need is the final figure. Where is Silas going?"

"I don't know," Maren said.

"Yes, you do."

"I don't," Maren said again, and this time she smiled, small and genuine, because it was true in the way that mattered. "I know where she's *going*. I don't know where she'll *arrive*. No one does. That's rather the point of the place."

Rook studied her for a long moment. Then she sighed, and it was the sigh of a woman setting down a weight she had hoped to keep carrying a while longer, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, almost gentle, and infinitely more dangerous for it.

"Here is what happens next, if you keep saying that. I arrest you. Formally. The charge is destruction of Crown-relevant documents, which is a hanging offense, and unlike your guildmaster I don't have a hundred years of bribes banked against the day I'd need them. You will hang, or you will rot in a cell below the Bastion, and either way, tomorrow morning I put every soldier I command on the eastern roads, and I find Silas Joubert within the week, and she hangs too, and everything you burned tonight will have been for nothing." She paused. "Or. You tell me where she went, freely, and I go after her *alone*, quietly, without a garrison, and I give you my word — my actual word, which I have never yet broken and do not intend to start — that she gets a hearing instead of a rope. Maybe she even gets a pardon, if what she finds turns out to serve the Crown. You buy her life with a secret. That's the trade. It's a good price. Most people pay more for less."

The fire had burned down to a bed of orange teeth. In its light, Maren looked at Dessa Rook and saw the truth of her, the thing under the uniform: not a fanatic, not a butcher. A woman who had lost someone to believing, and had spent her whole life since trying to make belief safe by putting it in cages — and who genuinely, desperately believed she was offering mercy.

That was the worst part. That made it hard.

"Your word," Maren said slowly, "against a garrison. Against the Bastion. Against the fact that your own commission orders you to bring her in alive or dead, whichever's cheaper." She shook her head. "Inquisitor, I've kept books for twenty years. I know a bad contract when I see one. The terms look generous and the penalties are written in blood, and I've read enough of those to know whose blood pays them."

"So you choose the rope."

"I choose the correct answer," Maren said, "to the question you asked. You wanted to know why I burned the pages. Here's why. Because somewhere east of here is a woman who has spent her entire life being an entry in other people's ledgers — her guildmaster's, her god's, the Crown's — and every single one of those ledgers says she belongs to them." She lifted her chin. "I burned the only book left that agreed. That's my whole crime. Hang me for it."

For a moment the room was perfectly still, except for the fire settling into itself.

Then Rook moved, and Maren learned how fast a weary woman could be. The inquisitor's hand closed on her wrist — the one holding the knife — twisted, precise, painless in the way that trained painlessness is somehow more frightening, and the blade clicked against the stone floor. Before Maren finished drawing breath, iron was on her other wrist, cold and familiar, closing with a sound like a full stop.

"Maren Ostrelle," Rook said formally, quietly, "you are under arrest for destruction of documents material to the security of the Crown." She straightened, and for just a moment her tired eyes flickered — toward the hearth, toward the ashes — and then back. "You understand I have to take the ashes too. Protocol."

"Of course," Maren said. Her wrists ached pleasantly, honestly. It was done. Whatever happened now, it was done, and Silas was a day further east than she had been an hour ago, and no ledger in the world could call that back. "Mind the hearth corner. The draft catches there. Half of it went up before you arrived."

"I know," Rook said. "I was watching the chimney smoke for six nights. You burn hot, Ostrelle. It's a distinctive signature." She gathered the poker, scooped ash into a leather document case with the brisk care of a woman collecting soil samples, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch. She did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," she said to the doorframe, "I believe you. That's the part that frightens me. Twenty years ago my sister stood in a kitchen much smaller than this and chose the correct answer to a question, and everyone told her she was brave, and she died of it." Now she turned, and her face was the face of a woman doing sums she hated. "Bravery isn't proof of correctness, ledger-keeper. Sometimes it's just the error you refuse to correct. Remember that, wherever they take you."

The lock closed behind her. Boots on stairs. Voices — Corvane's, smooth and startled, performing innocence for an audience of one.

Maren sat alone in the windowless room, in handcuffs, beside a hearth full of lies, and allowed herself exactly thirty seconds of shaking. Then she stopped, because she was who she was, and there was work to do even now — the work of keeping her face blank, her answers dull, her confession worthless, for however many days remained between this cell and whatever came after.

* * *

They took the ashes to the Bastion. Rook worked alone through the small hours, as she always did, in a room with one candle and no witnesses, because what she did next was not procedure. It was prayer, of a kind — the only kind she trusted.

She spread the ash across a sheet of white paper, fine gray words dissolved back toward blankness, and she bent over it the way scholars bend over palimpsests, the way her sister once bent over forbidden pamphlets in that small kitchen, and she read.

Most of it was gone. Fire is thorough where ink is only persistent. But fire has a weakness: it burns unevenly, hungrily, and where paper folds against paper, the inner leaf sometimes survives, charred at the edges but legible at its heart. Rook coaxed fragments apart with tweezers and patience. A phrase here. A figure there.

*A door that opens on remembering,* said one scrap, and her stomach dropped through the floor.

*Item: one keeper, born behind the door,* said another, and the handwriting was unmistakably the guildmaster's, and the date beside it was a hundred years old.

And near the bottom of the tray, half-fused to a curl of carbon, one fragment larger than the rest — the corner of a page bearing the tail of an entry and the head of a marginal note in different ink:

*...the thirteenth turn seals it forever, and the keeper enters and does not ret—*

Beneath it, in cramped, urgent script, as if written by someone else, someone hurried, someone afraid:

*—east hollow. She will remember the road. Veth forgive us both. —C.H.*

Rook sat back. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

East. The hollow. A door. A thirteenth turn — and she counted backward through the almanac in her head, lips moving, and arrived at a date four days from now, and felt the number land in her chest like a dropped stone.

She should send word. Garrison, roads, the whole machine. That was protocol. That was safety. That was the cage, and the cage had kept the Crown safe from believing for a hundred years.

Instead Dessa Rook sat alone with the ashes of a dead man's century, and took out her notebook, and on the first clean page wrote two words in her own careful hand:

*Thirteenth turn.*

Underneath, after a long moment, a third:

*Marguerite.*

Her sister's name, from the margins of a prophecy she had never read, in a war she had never questioned. She looked at it for a long time. Then she closed the notebook, snuffed the candle, and went to find a horse — riding east, alone, ahead of her own soldiers, toward a door that opened on remembering, carrying the one thing she had sworn never to carry again.

Faith.
