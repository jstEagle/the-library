## 16. The Price Written Down

The moon was wrong.

Silas had counted moons her whole life—mercenaries learn to count the dark hours, and ledger-keepers learn to count everything else—and she knew the shape of a waxing gibbous the way she knew the weight of her own pen. But this one hung low over the hollow like a coin pressed into wax, fat and yellowed and slightly off-center, as if the sky had stamped it crooked. The twelfth turn. One night remained.

She stood at the lip of the hollow where Aurelle had been, and below her the grass grew in perfect concentric rings, greener than anything had a right to be this far north in the cold months, and at the center of it all stood the door.

It did not look like much. That was the horror of it. It looked like an ordinary door—a plank-and-iron thing, the kind that might hang on a granary or a poor man's cottage—standing upright in the grass with nothing around it, casting no shadow even though every stone in the hollow cast three. Ivet stood near it, arms wrapped around himself, not looking at it directly. He hadn't managed to look at it directly since they'd arrived at dusk.

"It knows we're here," he said. "I can feel it taking attendance."

"Then let's hope we're all paid up," Silas said, because if she stopped making jokes about ledgers she would have to feel what she actually felt, which was the sensation of standing on the last page of a book whose ending someone else had written before she could walk.

Her hand went to her coat pocket again—the page, folded into eighths, soft as cloth now from handling. *Silas Joubert shall open the door upon the thirteenth turn of the moon, and entering, shall not return.* Her name in ink a century old. She'd read it so many times the letters had begun to look less like prophecy and more like instructions.

Behind them, down the road they had followed from nowhere, came the sound of many boots keeping poor time.

Ivet heard it too. He didn't ask who it was. They both knew. Crown soldiers traveled with a particular rhythm, all heel and no heart. But this wasn't the soldiers' rhythm. This was looser, heavier, punctuated by the creak of leather armor worn soft with use—the tread of men who had walked behind the same master for decades.

"Guild," Ivet said flatly.

"Enforcers." Silas turned to face the road. "Six of them, maybe eight. And one man who doesn't need to hurry."

* * *

Corvane Hale came down into the hollow the way he entered every room: as though the room had been waiting for him and ought to be grateful. He was not a large man. He had never needed to be. A hundred years of being obeyed had given him a gravity that pulled lesser objects toward him, and the six enforcers arranged themselves behind him in a crescent, hands resting easy on their weapons, faces arranged in the careful blankness of men who had been paid very well not to think.

He wore traveling gray, unremarkable, immaculate. His hair was silver-white and his face was sixty years old, which was itself a small impossibility the Guild had long ago agreed not to discuss. His eyes found Silas first. They always found Silas first.

"You brought soldiers," she said.

"I brought my people." He spread his hands. "You brought a cartographer and a stolen page. Between us, I think we know who has been more careful."

"Rook is coming."

"Yes. Two days behind you, give or take. She's good—better than the Crown deserves—but she reads ash, not roads, and roads are what matter tonight." He began to walk toward the door, unhurried, and the enforcers fanned out with him, cutting the hollow into slices. "Though I confess I'm glad she'll arrive after it's done. There's no version of tomorrow morning she'll find satisfying."

"There's no version of tonight *I* find satisfying."

"No," Corvane said, and something crossed his face—fast, quickly buried—that was almost tenderness, and was therefore worse than cruelty. "I imagine not. May I show you something?"

He drew a book from inside his coat. Not the prophecy book—she still had that page, and he knew it, and it didn't seem to trouble him. This was smaller, older, bound in green leather gone black at the corners. She recognized the binding before she recognized the contents, and when she did recognize the contents her stomach dropped through the grass, through the hollow, through whatever lay beneath countries that weren't there.

"The Guild's founding ledger," she said. "That's been missing for eighty years."

"It hasn't been missing. It's been *mine*. There's a difference." He opened it on his palm and turned it so she could see—not the entries, but the back pages, the ones where a careful hand had worked out sums in pencil. "Do you know how I became guildmaster, Silas? Do you know what the Guild of Impossible Things was, before it was appraisals and debt-books and bottled storms?"

"A smuggling ring with pretensions," she said. "Everyone says so."

"Everyone is wrong. It was a rescue operation." He touched the pencil marks gently, the way other men touched relics. "Aurelle did not simply vanish in the war. Aurelle was *hidden*—by its own survivors, in desperation, using a working older than any nation on this continent. A door that opens on remembering. And the price of such a working—" he tapped the page— "is a keeper. Someone bound to hold the memory alive outside the door, generation after generation, so the country doesn't starve in there. The god Veth was Aurelle's keeper. For a thousand years. And then Veth began to die, and a dying keeper cannot keep anything, and the door would have sealed with everyone inside, forever, asleep, forgotten even by themselves."

The wind moved through the hollow and the grass moved with it, all in one direction, toward the door, like water finding a drain.

"So I made a bargain," Corvane said. "Veth dictated the prophecy through me—I was young, and sick, and I had nothing to lose, which is the currency gods prefer. In exchange for writing it down truly, completely, without flinching, Veth would spend the last of its strength buying time. Stretching one year into a hundred. Long enough for the working to be done properly. Long enough for a successor to be prepared."

"And the successor," Silas said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "Let me guess. Born in Aurelle. Carried out as an infant. Raised in your guild. Trained to keep accounts, because accounts are just memories with numbers attached. Taught to pray to a silent god every night for fifteen years so the habit would be bone-deep before she ever learned why."

"Yes."

"You wrote me."

"I *raised* you." For the first time his composure cracked, just a hairline, just enough to see the fire behind it. "Do you think that was simple? Do you think arranging a life is easy? Every posting, every client, every night you spent alone so you'd be strong alone—every one of those was a line item I paid for. I turned down fortunes to keep you in my guild instead of selling you to patrons who would have used you up. I buried two men who learned what you were and might have sold you to the Crown. I have spent a *century*, Silas, building one person, and she is standing in front of me calling herself a forgery."

The enforcers had drifted. She marked their positions without looking—two by the eastern slope, two flanking wide toward the door, one hanging back near Ivet, who had gone very pale and very still. The sixth stood at Corvane's shoulder: Brannic, who had taught her to throw a knife, who had carried her on his back through the flood year when she was nine. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the middle distance, which was what Brannic did when he was ashamed.

"Say the rest," she said quietly. "The part where I go into the door and never come out. Say it the way it's written."

Corvane closed the old ledger. When he spoke again his voice was the voice he used for reading terms aloud over a signed contract—measured, almost gentle, absolutely immovable.

"The thirteenth turn seals the working. After tomorrow night, the door closes on its own, and everything inside is lost—every survivor, every street, every grave of a country that deserved better than the war it got. Unless a keeper enters willingly and takes up the burden. Then the country wakes, lives out its true span, and dies honestly, and its people walk out free. That's what the door is *for*, Silas. Not a prison. An ark. And arks need someone at the tiller who chose the post." He met her eyes. "You were born there. You are the only living soul with blood on both sides of the door. You are the only one who can open it, and the only one who can steer it. The prophecy doesn't demand your death. It demands your *service*. I have served the same bargain for a hundred years. I am asking you to serve it for one."

"One lifetime," Silas said. "Inside. Alone with a dying god and a sleeping country. Never coming back. Never—" she stopped, because the next word was *Maren*, and saying it would cost her more than she wanted anyone in this hollow to see.

"Maren will grieve," Corvane said. "And then she will keep the ledgers, and she will be fine, because Maren is stronger than either of us ever told her. Rook will close her investigation, because the sedition will have ended. The Crown will forget Aurelle again. And you—" his voice softened into something unbearable— "you will finally stop being invisible. Inside that door, you will be the most important person in a whole country. Every eye on you. Every prayer. Isn't that what you've wanted, all these years? To matter?"

The words went into her like knives going into a ledger—precise, between lines, exactly where they'd do the most damage. Because he was right about the wanting. He had built the wanting. That was the monstrous genius of it: he had spent fifteen years cultivating her loneliness the way a gardener cultivates a hedge, pruning it, shaping it, until it grew in exactly the form his prophecy required.

And that was how she knew, finally, past all doubt, that she had to refuse him.

Because a want that someone else planted isn't yours.

"You're doing the thing," she said, "where you call it salvation."

"It *is* salvation."

"It's a ledger entry." She took a step toward him, and the nearest enforcer shifted, and Brannic's hand twitched at his side, and none of it mattered, because she had decided something and her body had already caught up. "I've balanced books since I was six years old. I know every trick there is. Round the numbers down and hide the loss in the noise. Book the expense against a future that hasn't happened yet. Call a debt an investment. You're doing all of them at once, and you're doing it to a whole country, and the worst part—" her voice caught, and she let it— "the worst part is that you love me. I believe that. You love me the way a moneylender loves collateral. Completely. As long as it performs."

Corvane's face went white around the mouth.

"The prophecy requires you to enter at the thirteenth turn," he said. Each word laid down like a stone. "Willingly. That is the letter of it. I wrote the letter. I have had a hundred years to consider the letter."

"Here's what you never understood about letters, old man." She reached into her coat and drew out the page—the stolen prophecy, soft as cloth, and held it up between them in the crooked moonlight. "Letters can be read *against* their author. Ask any inquisitor. Ask any debtor. The letter says I enter at the thirteenth turn. Fine. The letter does not say I stay." She watched him go still in a way she had only ever seen him go still over a forged signature. "The letter says I open the door. It says nothing about locking it behind me. It says nothing about what happens *inside*. You wrote the terms, Corvane. You should have read them the way I read contracts—assuming the other party is looking for the gap. Because I am always looking for the gap. You taught me that."

For a long moment nobody in the hollow breathed.

Then Corvane smiled, and it was the worst thing she had seen in fifteen years of ugly work, because it was fond.

"There it is," he said softly. "There's my girl." He tucked the green ledger away and lifted two fingers, and the enforcers' hands settled fully onto their weapons, and beyond the rim of the hollow, far down the dark road, a point of orange light appeared and multiplied—torches, many torches, moving fast. Too early for Rook. Or perhaps, Silas thought with a lurch, exactly on time for someone else entirely.

"But you've made one error in your accounting," Corvane said, as the torches came on, as the grass leaned toward the door, as somewhere beneath the ground something vast turned over in its sleep and whispered her name in a voice like a dry pen across wet paper. "You think the choice is still yours."

The moon began, impossibly, to rise again.
