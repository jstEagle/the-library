## 17. Betrayal in the Old Tongue

The chains had been Rook's mistake, though Maren doubted the inquisitor would ever see it that way.

They were good chains—warded iron, Guild-grade, the kind Corvane sold to the Crown at a markup and swore were unbreakable. But Maren had inventoried them. She had catalogued their purchase eleven years ago, entry forty-six in the autumn ledger, and she knew what Corvane never let himself learn: that every lock in the Guild's arsenal answered to a ledger-keeper's touch, because Corvane believed books were the only things worth protecting and locks were just books with teeth.

So when the guard outside her cell dozed off at the turn of the hour—a habit she'd mapped during three days of listening to his boots—Maren pressed her thumb to the shackle's seam and said, quietly, in the old tongue of accountants: *Paid in full.*

The lock opened like a sigh.

She took nothing but the page she'd already swallowed once in ink and ash: the transcription she'd made of Corvane's entries in the oldest ledger, copied out in her own careful hand because Silas had taught her that anything worth saying should be legible enough for a stranger to read a hundred years on. The original pages were burned. Maren had burned them herself, in Rook's brazier, with Rook watching—and Rook had read the ashes anyway, because of course she had, because Inquisitor Dessa Rook did not lose things, she only rearranged them.

What Maren hadn't told Rook, what Rook had perhaps guessed and perhaps not, was that Maren had made two copies. One for burning. One for keeping.

She kept it against her ribs now, wrapped in oilcloth, as she went over the wall in the dark before the twelfth moon could set.

* * *

Getting out of the city was harder than getting out of the cell. Getting to the hollow was harder than both, because the roads stopped being roads the closer she came to where Aurelle ought to be, and by the second night she was walking on ground that gave back her footfalls a half-beat late, as if the earth had to think about it first.

Twice she passed shadows standing without owners. Once she saw a bird fly backward, which was either an omen or an argument about direction, and she chose not to settle it.

She was not a brave woman. She wanted that recorded somewhere, in some ledger, in her own hand: *Maren Ostrow, keeper of accounts, is not brave.* She was thorough. Thoroughness had carried her through the raid, through the interrogation, through the moment when Rook had offered her life for Silas's location and Maren had looked at the inquisitor's tired, honest face and understood that refusing would probably kill her—and had refused anyway, not from courage but from arithmetic. Silas was worth more alive than Maren was, full stop. Any competent bookkeeper could balance that equation.

But there was another column in the equation now, one she hadn't shown anyone.

Because Maren had done what she always did when something frightened her: she had read it again. And reading it again, in the lamplight of her cell, with three days to study Corvane's hundred-year-old handwriting pretending to be prophecy pretending to be accounting, she had found the thing that changed everything.

Not the manipulation. Not the arranged meetings, the steered apprenticeships, the orphan girl stolen from Aurelle and delivered into a life shaped like a keyhole. That was monstrous, but it was comprehensible—the desperation of a man bargaining for his own survival, then spending a century dressing the bargain as destiny.

No. It was the marginal note. A single line in Corvane's hand, dated fifty-three years after the rest, written small and cramped as if he'd hoped the page wouldn't notice:

*Veth answers too freely. Silence it until the appointed hour. It must not be permitted to warn her.*

Maren had read that line eleven times. Each time, it cut a little deeper.

Corvane hadn't just used Veth. He had gagged a god. Fifteen years of Silas speaking prayers into silence—fifteen years of Maren watching her friend fold that silence into competence, into loneliness disguised as discipline—and it hadn't been the god's failing or the god's dying or any of the explanations they'd all constructed to bear it. It had been Corvane, holding his hand over a god's mouth so that the girl would walk to the hollow exactly on schedule, obedient to a script she couldn't hear being contradicted.

He had made Silas pray to him, in effect. Every unanswered prayer had been his answer.

*Thorough,* Maren thought, walking through the wrong-colored dark toward the place where the world had a hole in it. *We are both thorough people. The difference is what we keep thorough about.*

* * *

She heard the hollow before she saw it—not sound, exactly, but the absence of it, a hush shaped like a bowl—and then the trees thinned and there it was below her: a ring of ground under a rising moon one night shy of its thirteenth turn, lit by lanterns and wardlight and the cold blue shimmer of a door that wasn't a door, standing open in the air like a wound in the shape of an archway.

And people. More people than she'd expected.

Silas stood near the door—Ivet beside her, gray-faced and swaying like a man who'd walked too far inside his own dreams. On the far slope, torches: Crown soldiers, and at their head a figure in inquisitor's gray who was, impossibly, standing *with* Silas's side rather than against it. Rook. Something had happened between those two that Maren's ledgers didn't cover.

Between them, at the center, stood Corvane Hale—guildmaster, author, murderer of a god's voice—flanked by six Guild enforcers in their sober charcoal coats, and Maren felt her stomach drop, because six enforcers was not a negotiation. Six enforcers was a conclusion.

"—the letter of it," Corvane was saying, and even at this distance his voice carried the patient warmth he used on clients who balked at prices. "It requires her to enter. It doesn't care how. Blood was always the crudest reading. Memory opens it; the thirteenth turn seals it; Silas goes in and does not return. Every term met. Every debt discharged. You've all served the Guild long enough to know that a contract honored is the only holiness there is."

One of the enforcers—Brannic, Maren thought, Brannic who owed Silas for the winter his daughter nearly died—shifted his weight and said nothing, which from Brannic was a sermon.

"She will die," Ivet said hoarsely. "You know that. Inside that door there is no time left. There's only what's left of a country and what's left of your god, and neither has room for a living woman."

"She will be a keeper," Corvane said. "Eternal. Honored." He turned to Silas, and Maren watched him put on tenderness like a coat he'd owned so long he'd mistaken it for skin. "Child. Everything I have done—"

"Everything you have done," Maren said, stepping out of the treeline, "is transcribed."

Every head turned. Six hands went to six weapons, and then hesitated, because she was unarmed, small, mud-splattered, and unmistakably the Guild's own ledger-keeper.

"Master Hale," she said, walking down into the hollow with her heart hammering and her voice, thank all mercies, level. "I've brought the accounts current."

"Maren." Corvane's face did something complicated—the flicker of calculation, the settling into disappointment, the way he looked at everyone as figures in a column he'd been totting for a century. "You were meant to stay burned."

"I know. I wrote two copies. You'd have found that sloppy."

She reached the flat ground at the center, close enough now to see Silas's face—pale, watchful, eyes going wide—and close enough to see the door, which was not really a door at all up close but a depth, a falling-away, a place where the air remembered a country. Her skin prickled. Something on the other side of it was aware of her the way a ledger is aware of a pen.

She unfolded the oilcloth. She held the copy high, and she began, in a clear carrying voice, to read.

* * *

"Entry one," she said. "Dated one hundred and seven years ago. *Received of Veth, last of its name: one prophecy, complete, dictated in the old tongue. Paid in exchange: continuance. Terms: the bearer shall arrange events such that the heir of Aurelle returns to the door at the thirteenth turn of the moon and does not return.*"

"That is private Guild record," Corvane said, and his voice was still warm, still reasonable, but the warmth had gone thin as gilt over rot.

"Entry nine," Maren continued. "*The heir is born. Name entered: Silas Joubert. Note: mother's line confirmed. Delivery arranged.* Delivery arranged, master. The midwife in Aurelle who handed a newborn to a stranger—she was paid. By you. Before the war ended. Before the country vanished. You had her out before Aurelle closed."

"Someone had to ensure the terms could be met—"

"Entry thirty-one." Maren's voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "*Apprenticed the subject to the Guild at age eight, per schedule. Note: she takes to ledgers. Convenient. Ledgers teach a mind to accept what is written.*" She looked up, and she found Silas's eyes across the hollow, and she made herself hold them. "You heard that, Si? He picked you for the books because books make obedience look like sense. Your whole life isn't a life. It's a filing system."

"Enough." Corvane's composure finally slipped—not into rage, worse, into weariness, the tone of a teacher burdened by a slow student. "Maren. Everything she says confirms the design. Yes, I arranged it. Arrangement is survival. The prophecy exists; therefore it must be fulfilled; therefore every step I took was merely the shortest path through a fixed landscape. Would you blame the ferryman for the river?"

"There's no river," Maren said. "That's the point. There's a man with a boat telling everyone the water's deep."

Then she turned the page, and her hands were shaking now, and she let them shake, because the shaking was true and truth was the only weapon she'd brought.

"Marginalia," she said. "Fifty-three years after the main entries. Dated fifteen years ago this spring." She read slowly, so that no syllable could slip away. "*Veth answers too freely. Silence it until the appointed hour. It must not be permitted to warn her.*"

The words dropped into the hollow like stones into a well, and for a moment nobody breathed, and the not-door shimmered.

"Fifteen years ago," Maren said, "a god went quiet. We all told ourselves stories about why. Gods fade. Gods sleep. Gods were never real and Silas's prayers were grief wearing vestments. I helped tell those stories. I held her coat while she prayed, master. I stood outside her door on the bad nights." Her voice hardened. "You reached into the oldest ledger and you closed a god's mouth like clamping a ledger shut, because a god that answered might have told her the truth—that she was born in Aurelle, that the door was hers, that your prophecy was a contract and she was the payment. You didn't silence Veth to save it. You silenced it so your instrument couldn't hear it scream."

"That is a reading," Corvane said.

"It is the entry," Maren said. "In your hand. Witnessed." She turned, deliberately, to the six enforcers. "You all swore to the Guild. The Guild's first law is that ledgers do not lie. Well. Here is the oldest ledger we have, and it says your guildmaster has spent a hundred years forging the biggest entry ever written. It says he muzzled the god the Guild is sworn to serve. Ask yourselves what else in your contracts he wrote himself."

Brannic's hand came off his sword-belt. Not drawn—just lowered, resting, the gesture of a man setting down a tool he no longer trusted himself to hold.

"Master Hale," said a woman enforcer near the end of the line—Sefa, who had trained under Silas—"if the god was silenced... then the night my brother drowned, when we prayed for passage and got nothing—" She stopped. She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Maren watched the arithmetic finish it for her, the same arithmetic working its way down the whole line of charcoal coats: *every prayer that ever went unanswered, answered to whom? To him.*

"You mistake bookkeeping for betrayal," Corvane said, and now there was steel under the warmth, the voice he used when a client tried to renegotiate. "I am the reason any of us survived. I am the reason the god survives at all. Without my bargain, Veth would have died a century ago and none of you would ever have knelt to anything, and Silas would have died in a burning country instead of standing here with a choice."

"She has a choice?" Ivet rasped from beside the door. "You wrote the choice."

"The prophecy required her to enter and not return," Corvane said. "I have spent one hundred years making sure that when the hour came, she would choose it freely, gladly, believing it her own idea. If that is manipulation, then so is teaching a child to read. So is love."

"No," said Silas.

Her voice was quiet. It carried anyway, the way a single true number carries through a column of errors. She stepped forward, past Maren, facing Corvane across ten feet of moonlit ground, and Maren saw that her friend's face was not broken. It had been broken, days ago, maybe hours ago—but it had been remade along different lines, the way a ledger is remade when the audit finds the fraud.

"You never loved me," Silas said. "You *authored* me. And tonight you're discovering the difference, because authors don't get to demand loyalty from their characters." She raised her chin toward the door. "I'm going in. Not because you wrote it. Because it's the only honest ending available, and I intend to edit it. But you—" her finger came up, steady, aimed like an accusation— "you will not profit from it. Maren. Read him the last line."

Maren's throat closed. She looked down at the final entry on the page, the one she'd saved for exactly this moment, the one she'd read twenty times in her cell trying to decide whether deploying it was justice or cruelty, and decided, finally, that Corvane Hale had forfeited mercy somewhere around year sixty.

"Final entry," she read. "*Note to self, appended in confidence:*" she swallowed— "*If she learns the truth before the hour, the bargain fails, and I die with the god. All arrangements void. I have checked this clause eleven times. It is the only part of the prophecy I did not write. Veth insisted.*"

A silence followed that had weight and texture, and into that silence Corvane Hale laughed—one short, awful bark of a laugh, the sound of a man finding the trap in his own snare.

"It negotiated," he said softly. "All those years I thought I was using it. It was using the one thing I couldn't fake—an ending I couldn't control. It built its own door out of my honesty clause." His eyes went to the shimmering archway, and for the first time in a century, guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things looked afraid of the impossible. "It's been waiting for her to refuse me."

The moon cleared the ridge.

It was not yet the thirteenth turn—it was one night short—but the door flared anyway, brightening like an ember breathed upon, and from somewhere inside it, from behind fifteen years of imposed silence, from the bottom of a hundred-year-old bargain, something stirred.

And spoke.

Not in words. In the old tongue of accountants, the language of debts and discharges, in a voice like a pen moving across paper, and every person in the hollow who had ever kept a book felt it write itself directly into their understanding:

*Audit.*

Then the sky above the ridge filled with torches, and the Crown came down the hill.
