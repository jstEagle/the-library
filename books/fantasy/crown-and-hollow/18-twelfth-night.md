## 18. Twelfth Night

The moon came up wrong.

I had watched enough moons rise in fifteen years of ledger-keeping to know how they behaved—slowly, sullenly, dragging their light up over the hills like a debt collector climbing a staircase. This one came up all at once, the way a memory comes: not gradually, but *arriving*, whole and terrible, as if it had been there all along and had only now decided to admit it.

It was the twelfth moon of the turn, swollen and pale as bone, hanging directly above the hollow where Aurelle used to be.

Ivet knelt at the center of it, hands pressed flat against the grass, muttering roads under his breath like prayers. He'd been at it for an hour. The hollow listened to him—I could feel that much. The air had that taut, held-breath quality of a room where someone is about to say your name. But the door did not come.

"It needs two," Ivet said again, without opening his eyes. "Memory in two voices, girl. One is an echo. Two is a choir."

"I don't remember anything," I said, for the fourth time, and for the fourth time it tasted like a lie, because my hands were shaking and my body remembered something my mind refused to: the smell of bread baked in a country that wasn't on any map.

That was when we heard the horses.

* * *

There were three riders coming down through the black pines, and I knew the lead rider before she cleared the tree line, because you do not spend three weeks being hunted by Inquisitor Dessa Rook without learning the shape of her against the sky. Straight back. Grey cloak. A lantern held low so it wouldn't silhouette her—a soldier's habit, not a hunter's vanity.

Ivet scrambled up beside me, his old knees cracking. "Inquisition," he breathed. "Girl, we have to run."

"Where?" I asked, because it was the honest question. Behind us, the hollow. Ahead of us, Rook. And somewhere on the road behind her—less than a day, maybe less than an hour—Corvane Hale with six Guild enforcers who had known me since I was small enough to sleep under the appraisal tables.

I did not run. Not because I was brave. Because I was tired, and because some part of me—the part that had spent a lifetime balancing books—understood that every account comes due in its own time, and this one was due here.

Rook dismounted at the edge of the hollow. She walked toward us the way she must have walked into interrogation rooms: unhurried, deliberate, giving everyone time to decide what kind of person they wanted to be when she arrived. She stopped ten feet away and looked at me, and then she did something no inquisitor had ever done in my presence.

She set her lantern down on the grass. Away from her hand. Unlit by anything but the moon.

"Silas Joubert," she said.

"Inquisitor Rook." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Come to arrest me?"

"No." She reached into her coat, slowly—she made sure I saw every inch of the movement—and drew out a folded page. Old paper. Century-old paper, gone brown at the creases. "I'm going to show you something, and you're going to want to kill me for having it, and I need you to hear me out before you try."

Ivet made a small sound of despair. I stepped forward and took the page.

I recognized it instantly, because I had stolen its twin from an estate sale three weeks ago. Same book. Same hand. The prophecy of Aurelle, written in Corvane Hale's careful script—but this page was from the margins, the narrow columns where the author had worked sums the way I worked them, testing, checking, balancing.

And halfway down the margin, bracketed twice, underlined once, was a name I knew.

*Sessa Rook.*

The world went very quiet. Even the hollow seemed to lean in.

"My sister's name," Dessa Rook said, and her voice—the voice that had dismantled Maren over candles in a stone room, that had read the ashes of burned ledgers like scripture—cracked on the second word. "Sessa died eleven years ago. Fever, they told me. Bad water in a border town. I joined the Inquisition a year later because I needed prophecy to be the enemy of everything, and if prophecy killed her, then hunting it meant something." She took a breath. "Then I find this. Written a hundred years ago. Her name, in the margins of the treason I've spent my life burning. With a note beside it."

"Read it," I said. Though I already could. I had learned to read Corvane's marginalia the way he had taught me—because he taught me everything, didn't he, every habit of mind I owned was borrowed from him—

Rook read it aloud anyway. "'The sister grieves; the grief is useful. Grief makes the hunter certain, and certainty can be aimed.'"

Silence. Ivet sat down heavily in the grass, as if his legs had finally filed their resignation.

"He wrote her death," Rook said. "A hundred years before it happened. He arranged it—don't look at me like that, Joubert, I've spent three weeks inside this man's arithmetic and I know what I'm looking at now. He needed an inquisitor with a dead sister to come looking at the right time. He built me the way he built you." Her jaw tightened. "The difference is you got to live in the dark about it. I got to be *proud* of it."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I burned my commission this morning." She said it flatly, factually, the way she might report the weather or a casualty count. "Because the Crown's founding war—the one that erased Aurelle off the maps—has its own prophecy in that same hand, and I have spent the last two days verifying the ink, and the ink is a hundred years old, and everything I believe is a lie with excellent provenance." She looked past me, at the hollow, at Ivet kneeling in the grass like a man listening to a voice underground. "And because that man—" she pointed back down the road, toward the pines, "—is less than an hour behind me with six enforcers, and whatever he wants done tonight, I no longer wish to help him do it."

"You're helping him," I said. "You've been helping him since the day you were born. We both have. That's what he does. That's what he's *for*."

"Yes," Rook said. "Which is why I'd like to stop. Right now. Tonight. With you." She extended her hand—not to shake, but palm-up, open, the way you offer something rather than demand it. "I don't trust you, Joubert. You don't trust me. Good. Trust is for people with time. We have an hour and a common enemy. Let's work with that."

I looked at her hand. I thought about Maren, sitting somewhere in a Crown cell or walking free on Rook's orders—I didn't know which, and I decided not to ask, because the answer might cost me something I couldn't spare.

"The enforcers," I said. "Six of them. Bramt and Osk are good in a fight. The others will follow whoever seems most certain."

"Then we'll seem certain," Rook said.

I took her hand. It was warm, and callused across the palm in the same places mine were—from pens, not swords. Ledger-keeper's hands. Hunter's hands. The same hands, in the end. Corvane had built us both, and here we stood in the ruins of his design, holding on to each other because there was nothing else left upright to hold.

* * *

We spent forty minutes preparing for a battle, which is to say we spent forty minutes doing what the Guild does best: appraising what we had and finding its hidden value.

The hollow itself was the first asset. Rook paced its circumference while Ivet muttered his roads, and she mapped it in her head the way she must map every room she interrogates in—which corners swallow sound, which approaches are blind. "One road in from the east," she said. "The pines funnel anyone on horseback into single file along the streambed. If they come fast, they come strung out."

"Bramt won't come fast," I said. "Bramt reads contracts. He'll want to know the terms before he draws a blade."

"Then we'll offer terms." Rook smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and I found I liked her enormously for it. "You're the Guild's appraiser. Appraise this: six enforcers sworn to a guildmaster who just learned his hundred-year plan is unraveling. What's their contract worth tonight?"

I thought about it honestly, because I owed honesty to somebody. "Less than he thinks. Enforcers guard the Guild's interests. But Corvane stopped serving the Guild's interests somewhere around the reign of the last king. They just haven't audited him yet."

"Then let's audit him," Rook said.

Ivet, meanwhile, had begun to talk—not to us, exactly, but near us, the way old men do when the truth has gotten too heavy to carry alone. "I dreamed these roads for thirty years," he said. "Drew them. Sold them. Told myself they were fancy. Do you know what it is, girl, to find out your life's work was someone else's memory leaking into you? That a dead country has been using you as a window?"

"Yes," I said quietly. "As it happens."

He laughed, a dry sound with no floor under it. "Then you know the door needs remembering, and I only half-remember. I need someone who was *born* there."

"I wasn't born anywhere," I said. "I was an orphan. Foundlings don't get countries."

"Foundling," Ivet said gently, "is a word grown-ups use so they don't have to say *taken*."

I didn't answer him. There was nothing in my ledgers to answer with. But my hands remembered bread, and the moon climbed another degree, and somewhere east of us, down a funneled road, I heard the first horse.

* * *

They came exactly as predicted: single file along the streambed, torches doused, moving quiet. Corvane rode third, which surprised me until I understood it—he would never ride point, because point is the position that gets killed first, and Corvane Hale had survived a hundred years by never once being the thing that got killed first.

Bramt rode first. Osk rode second. Behind the guildmaster came Hetch, the twins Sorrel and Sarei, and young Pem, who was twenty and still believed the Guild was a family.

We stood in the moonlight at the center of the hollow, four of us—me, Rook, Ivet, and Rook's two soldiers, whom she had freed of their oaths at the treeline with a sentence apiece. Two had chosen to stay. I decided I would learn their names later, if there was a later.

Bramt reined in at the hollow's edge and looked at the tableau for a long moment. Then he swung down, handed his reins to Osk, and walked forward alone, which was Bramt's way: he negotiated in person, face to face, blade sheathed, because a man who opens with a blade has admitted his words are worthless.

"Guildmistress Joubert," he said.

"Don't." The word came out sharper than I intended. "Don't promote me into his scheme. That's what the title's for, Bramt. It's how he makes his arrangements look like inheritances."

Something shifted in Bramt's face—respect, maybe, or relief. "Then what do I call you?"

"Silas. And you can call this what it is: an audit." I gestured at the hollow, the moon, the old man kneeling in the grass. "Ask him what the Guild's oldest ledger says. Ask him whose handwriting dates entries a century before they were written. Ask him why the god he claims to serve hasn't spoken to any living member in fifteen years."

"We know the god went quiet," Osk called from the line, and there was anger in it. Fifteen years of unanswered prayers does something to people who built their lives around the asking.

"Do you know *why*?" I raised my voice to carry. "Because he silenced it! Because Veth started dictating true prophecies, and true prophecies can't be controlled, and Corvane Hale would rather serve a silent god than share a speaking one!"

Corvane dismounted then. Slowly. He was not a large man and never had been; he occupied space the way a signature occupies a page—small, precise, everywhere binding. He walked to within twenty feet of us and stopped, and even now, even knowing everything, some traitor part of me stood straighter when he looked at me. Some orphan reflex. *Sit up straight, child. Attention is the first currency.*

"Silas," he said warmly. "You've brought an inquisitor. How industrious."

"She brought herself. Ask her why."

"I don't need to ask. I read the margins too." He turned to Rook, and his voice softened into something almost kind, which was the cruelest thing about him—that he could make cruelty feel like care, reliably, on demand. "Inquisitor. Your sister. I am sorry for it. Truly. If I could have arranged her survival instead, I would have."

"*Arranged*—" Rook's hand went to her belt, and I moved without deciding to, caught her wrist, held it. She was shaking. So was I.

"He means it," I said. "That's the horror of it, Rook. He always means it. He'll apologize for building your grief while he uses it. Don't give him the satisfaction of a clean death."

Corvane sighed, and glanced at the sky, at the fat pale moon grinding upward toward its zenith. "The thirteenth turn rises in hours. Silas, child. Everything I have done—every entry, every arrangement, every silence—was to bring you to this hollow alive, tonight, with the door within reach. Six enforcers and an ex-inquisitor cannot change the terms. The prophecy is written. Veth himself dictated it."

"Veth dictated it *to you*," I said. "A dying god making a bargain with the only man greedy enough to negotiate. And then you edited it. Maren found the drafts, Corvane. Prophecy doesn't have drafts."

For the first time in the hundred years of him, Corvane Hale looked briefly, genuinely uncertain. Then the signature smoothed itself back over his face. "Maren," he said softly. "Of course. The loyal one. Where is she, I wonder? Still in Rook's cells? Or walking free on some bargain struck in ashes?" His eyes flicked to Rook. "You see how it goes, enforcers. Everyone trades. Everyone has a price. Except, perhaps, our appraiser—whose price, as it happens, I set when she was three days old."

"Then it's overdue," I said, "and accruing interest."

Bramt laughed. Just once, short, involuntary—and I watched it break something in the formation. Because Bramt laughing at the guildmaster was a line item none of the others had budgeted for. Sorrel looked at Sarei. Pem looked at everybody.

"The moon," Ivet said suddenly, urgently, from the grass. "It's nearly there. Girl—*Silas*—it doesn't need blood, I told you, it needs memory, shared, spoken aloud—but it needs to happen before the turn completes, or the door seals forever and everything inside dies unmourned—"

"Everything inside," Corvane repeated smoothly, "including Veth. Sealed away. Contained. Safe at last from prophecy, from change, from *loss*. Think of it, Silas. The god preserved forever. The Guild's purpose fulfilled eternally. Order."

"A museum," I said. "You want to embalm a god and put me in the case next to it."

"I want you to *matter*."

"I matter," I said, and my voice cracked, and I let it. "That's the whole point of tonight, you ancient, cowardly, meticulous monster. I was always going to matter. You just couldn't stand that it wouldn't be *because of you*."

The moon touched the top of the hollow.

And the grass began to remember.

It started at Ivet's knees—a shimmer, like heat-haze in reverse, like frost forming in summer—and spread outward in slow rings. Shapes rose in it. A well. A wall. The ghost of a street lamp burning with light nobody had lit for a hundred years. Aurelle, assembling itself out of two people's memories and a dying moon, brick by borrowed brick.

And beneath it, fainter than breath, I felt it again—the presence that had answered my prayer on the night of the eleventh moon. Faint. Failing. Afraid.

*Silas,* said the god Veth, in the silence between all our hearts at once. *They are here. The door is almost. Hurry, keeper. Please.*

Corvane drew a breath to speak—to command, to arrange, to sign.

Bramt drew steel first. Not at me. At the space between Corvane and the forming door.

"Guild business," Bramt said, in the flat tone of a man reading terms aloud, "ends where the guildmaster's begin to lie. Audit's open, sir. State your accounts."

Corvane looked at his enforcers—at six people he had hired, shaped, and assumed he owned—and I watched him perform the final calculation of his long, long life.

Then the eastern road filled with torchlight, and hooves, and the horns of the Crown, arriving late and armed and answerable to no one he had bribed.

"Silas," Rook said quietly, drawing her blade at last, back to back with me at the heart of the remembering city. "Whatever you're going to do to open that door—do it *fast*."

The thirteenth moon began to rise.
