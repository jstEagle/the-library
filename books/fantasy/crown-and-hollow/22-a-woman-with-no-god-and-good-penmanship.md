## 22. A Woman With No God and Good Penmanship

The shop smelled of beeswax and old paper, and Silas Joubert had come to think of that smell as the smell of being safe.

It was a small place on Cordwainer's Lane, wedged between a cobbler and a woman who sold pickled everything, with a bow window that caught the morning light and a sign she had painted herself in careful block letters: *J. JOUBERT — APPRAISALS & FAIR VALUATION. NOTHING BOUGHT. NOTHING SOLD. ONLY WORTH, HONESTLY TOLD.* The last part had been her idea of a joke, though customers rarely laughed. They came instead because word had spread, quietly the way good things spread, that the appraiser on Cordwainer's Lane did not cheat you, did not lowball widows, and could tell within thirty seconds whether your grandmother's ring was paste or garnet—and would tell you which, gently either way.

She kept meticulous books. That much, at least, seemed to be in her bones.

Silas had discovered, in the eleven months since she'd opened the shop, that she had a talent for numbers and an almost compulsive need for them to balance. She wrote figures in a fine, even hand that the stationer down the street once complimented as "the neatest I've seen outside a guild office," and Silas had smiled and said thank you and felt something turn over inside her, some door swinging on rusted hinges into a room she could not enter.

She did not know why her handwriting looked like that. She did not know why she flinched, very slightly, whenever someone mentioned the moon's phases, or why she could not abide the smell of tallow candles burning low, or why the sight of a blank ledger page made her heart ache in a way that had no story attached to it. The physician in Saltmarket Row called it "a forgetting" and shrugged, which was what physicians did when the body was sound and the trouble lived somewhere they couldn't bleed or bind.

"You were ill," Maren had told her, when Silas first woke and asked who she was. "You were very ill, and it took things from you. But you're well now. And you're Silas."

Maren visited every Thursday. She brought seed cake and gossip and, once a month, a small parcel of money that she insisted was repayment of a debt Silas couldn't remember incurring. Silas had stopped arguing about it around the fourth month. There was something in Maren's face when she handed the parcels over—a fierce, wet-eyed tenderness—that made argument feel like cruelty.

"You keep looking at me like I'm going to die," Silas said once.

"You keep looking at me like you're trying to remember where we met," Maren said. "We're even."

* * *

On the first Tuesday of the new year, a farmer's widow brought in a storm in a bottle.

It sat on Silas's workbench for three hours while she turned it in the light: a squat green glass vessel, stoppered with wax gone amber with age, and inside it a weather all its own—clouds no bigger than thumbnails churning over a sea the size of a thumbnail, lightning flickering without sound, rain falling upward in one corner as if the little world had gotten confused about which way was sky.

"Husband bought it off a peddler forty years back," the widow said. "Told me it was worth a fortune. Told our son the same before he died, so the boy's been sitting on it like a hen on a stone ever since. Tell us the truth, missus. Even if it's nothing."

Silas held the bottle up to the window. The tiny lightning lit her fingers blue-white.

"It's not nothing," she said slowly. She didn't know how she knew what she knew; knowing arrived in her the way weather arrived, unbidden and total. "This is a bottled squall-line, genuine work, not one of the trick bottles with mirrors inside. You can tell by the rain direction—the mirror-bottles get it wrong." She set it down and drew her valuation book toward her, and her pen moved in that fine even hand. "It's worth perhaps eight crowns to a collector. Perhaps twelve if you find one who grew up near the coast and gets homesick easy." She slid the page across. "But I'll tell you what it's actually worth, and this is free of charge. It's worth whatever your husband paid for it, because he bought it to make you smile, and it's been doing that job for forty years without missing a day. Things that do their work that long are worth more than their price. Don't let your son sell it unless the family needs the bread."

The widow cried a little. She left the bottle on the counter anyway—"for the boy to decide"—and Silas stood alone in the shop afterward holding it up to the light again, watching the little storm rage on, and felt an emotion she had no name for. It wasn't grief exactly. It was closer to standing outside a house where a party was happening, hearing the music through the wall, knowing only that the music was lovely and that you were, for reasons unknown, not invited.

She put the bottle on the high shelf behind the counter, where she kept the things people left with her that weren't quite objects anymore. There were four others there: a child's shoe worn through at the toe, a letter sealed and addressed to no one, a brass key that fit no lock in the district (she had tried), and a single pressed flower she could not identify, though she had consulted three books.

She did not know why she kept them. She only knew that throwing them away was unthinkable, the way certain sins are unthinkable—not forbidden, just *wrong*, down in the marrow.

* * *

In the afternoons when trade was slow, Silas read.

Her tastes embarrassed her a little. She liked almanacs. She liked tax rolls and shipping manifests, anything with columns, anything where the truth of the world lay flat on the page in tidy rows. Maren teased her about it—"most women your age read romances, Silas"—and Silas always answered, "Most women your age don't know the pleasure of a column that adds up," and Maren would go quiet and look at her with that wet-eyed tenderness again, and neither of them would say anything else for a while.

There were other gaps. She noticed them the way you notice missing teeth—with your tongue, constantly, involuntarily.

She could not remember her mother's face. This was unremarkable, perhaps; many people lost such things early. But she could not remember *having* a mother at all, nor a father, nor a street, nor a hunger, nor any of the ordinary furniture of a childhood, and when she asked Maren about it Maren said, "You were raised by an institution," in a voice that was smooth as poured glass, and Silas knew—knew the way she knew rain-directions in bottles—that the voice was lying, and knew also that pressing the question would cost her something she could not afford. Some debts you feel without remembering incurring them.

She prayed sometimes. This was the strangest gap of all, because she had no god to pray to—or none she knew of—and yet the habit was there, worn smooth as a stair-stone. She caught herself doing it at dusk most often, hands idle on the counter, lips moving: not words, exactly. More like the shape of words. A report. A tally. *Day accounted for. Nothing owed. No complaints filed.*

And then, always, the same sensation: that she was speaking into a room where someone had recently been, where the chair still bore the warmth of a body, where the silence had the specific quality of a silence that used to be a voice.

She never told anyone about that. Not even Maren. Especially not Maren, whose face went white as chalk whenever the subject of religion came up anywhere nearby, and who had once knocked over an entire shelf of pickled goods at the grocer's when a street preacher started shouting about gods who hear.

* * *

The page had been in the desk drawer since before Silas rented the shop.

She'd found it the first week, tucked beneath the drawer's false bottom along with three crowns and a key she'd assumed belonged to the previous tenant. It was old paper, soft as cloth at the edges, written on both sides in ink gone brown. And the handwriting—

The handwriting was hers.

Not similar. Hers. She knew her own hand the way she knew her own reflection: the particular way she crossed her sevens, the slight forward lean of the letters, the loops that tightened when the writer was hurried. She had held the page up beside fresh entries in her own valuation book and matched them line for line, and then she had sat very still for a long time while the shop filled with evening and the lamplighter passed outside and her own heartbeat sounded loud and wrong in her ears.

She did not remember writing it. She did not remember owning the desk. The landlord swore the desk had come with the property from a deceased estate, contents cleared, and his ledgers—which Silas checked, because of course she checked—were clean and boring and true.

The page itself was mostly accounting. That was what unsettled her most, in those early weeks: not the handwriting, but the content. Columns. Dates. Figures. Terms like *principal*, *interest*, *outstanding balance*. Someone—she—had recorded a debt in this old paper, a debt with no name attached except, at the bottom, in letters pressed hard enough to tear:

**PAID IN FULL.**

Below that, smaller, angled as if written quickly, as if the writer's hand had been shaking or hurried or both:

*Remember me kindly.*

Silas spent the first month trying to solve it. She took rubbings. She compared inks with the stationer. She showed it, once, to Maren—who had gone so pale and so still that Silas hid the page away again immediately and apologized for frightening her, and Maren had said, in that glass-smooth voice, "Some accounts are better closed than audited, love," and had eaten two slices of seed cake without tasting either one.

After that, Silas stopped trying to solve it.

This surprised her more than the page had. She was, by her own settled nature, a solver—of values, of provenances, of the little puzzles objects carry in their grain. But every time she sat down to interrogate the page, something rose in her like floodwater, some instinct older than memory, and it said: *stop*. And underneath the *stop*, faintly, unmistakably, was another feeling, one she finally learned to name in the spring:

Grief. Pre-grieved. Grief already finished grieving itself, left behind like a coat outgrown.

Whatever this page mourned, the mourning had been done. By her. Somewhere. In a life whose shape she could not recall.

So she did the only thing that felt honest. She kept the page. She moved it from the false bottom of the drawer to a flat wooden box lined with felt, the kind collectors use for medals, and she kept the box in the drawer, and once in a great while—on the first Tuesday of a month, say, or on nights when the moon was thin and strange and she woke at some twelfth hour of the night for no reason with salt on her cheeks—she took it out and read it end to end, top to bottom, every dry column and every torn-pressed figure, like a letter from herself to herself.

*Paid in full.*

Whatever I owed, she would think, tracing the words with one finger. Whatever it cost. I settled it. Whoever taught me to write like this, whoever kept my books before I kept them myself—I hope I remembered them kindly.

She always put it away carefully. She always locked the drawer. And she always, on those nights, went to the window and looked at the moon—whatever turn it was on, full or thin or dark—and said the wordless prayer out loud, into the cold, to no one:

*Day accounted for. Nothing owed. No complaints filed.*

*If anyone is listening: I'm well. I'm free. The columns add up.*

*Remember me kindly too, whoever you were.*

* * *

Spring came to the city the way spring comes everywhere—messily, then all at once. The widow's son came for the bottle and, after a long conversation Silas hadn't charged him for, decided to keep it, and brought her a jar of his mother's plum preserves as thanks, which she judged, honestly and generously, to be priceless.

Maren announced, over seed cake in April, that she was taking on an apprentice at her firm—"bookkeeping, nothing glamorous, but the girl has a steady hand"—and that she'd taken the liberty of recommending the apprentice buy her ledgers from a certain stationer on Cordwainer's Lane, because "the stock is good and the owner gives fair value," and Silas laughed and said, "I don't own a stationery shop, Maren," and Maren smiled into her tea and said, "No. Of course not. How silly of me."

There were other moments like that, small and strange, which Silas collected the way she collected the objects on the high shelf. The way strangers of a certain age looked at her in the market—one old soldier had gripped his cap to his chest and bowed, actually *bowed*, before hurrying away. The way the word *Aurelle*, spoken once by a drunk poet outside the Pickled Everything, had landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without hitting bottom. The way she sometimes dreamed of a hollow in a country that wasn't on any map, and a door, and a voice saying *thank you* in a language she somehow understood perfectly and could not, waking, pronounce.

She wrote none of it down. Some instinct told her that her life was better as a balanced column than as an investigation, and Silas Joubert trusted her instincts about bookkeeping above all things.

On a bright morning in late May, with the bow window open and the cobbler hammering next door and the whole lane smelling of leather and brine and bread, a young woman came in carrying a wooden box and asked, shyly, whether Silas could value its contents.

Inside, wrapped in wool, was a ring. Garnet, Silas saw at once—an honest stone, modest cut, the setting worn nearly smooth by decades of a thumb worrying it. The young woman explained that it had been her grandmother's, and her grandmother had died in the winter, and the family couldn't agree whether to sell it or bury it with the last of the linen, and everyone was too raw to decide, so—could she just. Tell them what it was. What it meant. Whether keeping it was foolishness.

Silas lifted the ring into the light. It threw a small red glow across her palm, warm as a coal.

"It's worth six crowns," she said, "to the right buyer. Maybe seven." Then she closed the young woman's fingers around it, gently, and held them shut a moment longer. "And it's worth everything to keep. Grief isn't a debt, love. It doesn't want settling. It just wants carrying, for a while, until it gets light enough to become a keepsake. Bury the linen. Keep the ring. Come back in five years and tell me I was right."

The young woman laughed through tears, thanked her, paid the appraisal fee without haggling, and left the bell over the door jingling.

Silas stood in her shop—in her shop, hers, with its honest sign and its balanced books and its high shelf of almost-objects—and felt the feeling she had finally, after a year, learned to trust: quiet. Earned. Like a ledger closed at day's end with every figure agreeing with every other figure, like a door locked against nothing in particular because there was nothing out there to fear, like a prayer answered not with thunder but with an ordinary morning, sunlight, hammering next door, bread on the wind.

That night, before locking up, she took out the wooden box one last time. She read the columns. She read **PAID IN FULL**. She read the small, shaking, angled line at the bottom, and she found, as she always did, that her eyes stung, and that the sting was not painful—it was the good kind, the kind that means the wound has healed clean and only the weather can reach it.

"Whoever you were," she said to the page, to the handwriting, to herself, "I hope it was worth it. It seems to have been. Look at me—good penmanship, honest work, fair prices. Somebody built that. I intend to live up to the builder."

She put the page away. She locked the drawer. She banked the lamp, and paused at the window to look at the rising moon—full tonight, or nearly, turning through its patient rounds—and gave it the nod she gave all her accounts: acknowledged, closed, no complaints filed.

Then Silas Joubert, a woman with no god and good penmanship, blew out the lamp, and went home through the spring-dark streets to sleep the deep, dreamless, unhaunted sleep of the truly free.

Behind her, in the locked drawer, in the felt-lined box, the ink of an old page dried on undisturbed—every column balanced, every debt discharged, and one small instruction waiting, patient as a promise, for the day she might finally read it aloud:

*Remember me kindly.*
