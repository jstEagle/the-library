## 1. The Gate That Should Stay Shut

The lamp needed trimming at dusk, and Rafael Eriksen had never once let it go past dusk.

They climbed the stone steps of the gatehouse with the oil can in one hand and the brass shears in the other, as they had every evening for twenty years, as their father had before them, as his mother before him, back through a line of keepers so long that the first of them was carved into the lintel with a name nobody could read anymore. The Thornfell gate did not care for tradition. It cared only that someone came, every night, without fail, to feed the wards.

Rafael fed them now. The ritual was simple enough that a child could do it — and once, a child had done it, though Rafael did not let themselves think about that often. You opened the iron cabinet set into the gate's left pillar, you touched your palm to the wardstone inside, and you spoke the words your grandmother's grandmother had spoken: *Keep what passes. Pass what is kept.* The wardstone drank the words like water. It warmed under Rafael's hand, faintly, the way a sleeping animal warms, and then it settled, and the invisible net above the pass hummed so quietly that you could only hear it if you stood very still and very alone, which Rafael always was.

Tonight the stone felt different. Not wrong — Rafael would have known wrong; they had spent two decades learning every mood of this gate the way a shepherd learns the moods of an old dog. But tonight it felt *hungry*. It pulled at their palm a half-second longer than usual. It drank the keeping-words down fast, greedily, and when Rafael lifted their hand away, the humming above the pass came a little ragged, like breath after a long climb.

"Old thing," Rafael murmured to it, because talking to the gate was another part of the routine, one they'd never admitted to anyone. "You've held this pass three hundred years. One more night won't kill you."

The stone said nothing. Stones never did. But Rafael stood there a moment longer than they needed to, hand flat against the cool iron of the cabinet door, listening to the wards hum over the empty road, and told themselves — the way they told themselves every night — that everything was fine.

* * *

Everything was fine. That was the phrase Rafael kept in the pocket of their mind the way other people kept a lucky coin. Everything was fine. The stars were out — all of them, the whole spilled handful of them, wheeling slow over the jagged teeth of the Thornfells. The border wards were lit. The lamp was trimmed. The ledger was current.

The ledger. Rafael carried it down from the gatehouse to the keeper's cottage at the base of the wall, a squat stone building with one room below and one room above, and sat at the desk by the window to make the night's entries. This was the part of the work no songs were written about. Gate-keeping, in the ballads, was all heroism: the keeper standing against the horde, the keeper's blade flashing at the pass. In truth it was ink. Ink and oil and walking. Rafael wrote down the day's crossings — a tinker with a mule, four pilgrims bound for the shrine at Coldwell, a tax courier who'd sneered at the toll and paid it anyway — and initialed each line, and blotted each line, and then sat for a while with the pen dry in their hand, looking at nothing.

Twenty years ago there had been another name in this ledger. Written in a boy's careful hand, in the column marked *Keeper's Second*: **Tomas Eriksen**.

Rafael did not look at that page. They never looked at that page. They knew exactly which page it was — third ledger back, the one with the cracked spine, kept on the bottom shelf where the damp couldn't reach it — and they knew that if they opened it, they would find Tomas's handwriting beside their own for eleven months, two brothers learning the family trade together, arguing about whether the north path or the river path was faster, laughing about the tinker's mule, doing all the ordinary things that ordinary brothers did in an ordinary mountain town where nothing ever happened except weather and trade and the slow turning of seasons.

Tomas had been nineteen. He'd gone up the pass on a bright morning in late autumn to check the high wardline after a storm, and he had not come back. The searchers found his pack. They found one boot. They found, three days later, at the bottom of a crevasse no one could explain him having entered, nothing else at all. No body. The magistrates wrote it down as a fall. Their mother stopped speaking in anything but whispers. Their father took up the gate rounds alone and aged ten years in ten months, and died with the wardstone's warmth still on his palm, and Rafael — who had been the younger brother, the spare, the one who wasn't supposed to matter to the gate at all — inherited a duty and a grief in the same week, and had carried both ever since, the way you carry a pack with a broken strap: constantly adjusting, never setting it down.

Everyone in Thornfell said Rafael Eriksen was the most dutiful soul the town had ever produced. Never missed a feeding. Never missed a round. Never took a holiday longer than a day, and spent that day walking the walls anyway because sitting still made the silence worse. What nobody said — what Rafael made sure nobody could say, by being so reliably fine, so unfailingly present, so perfectly composed behind the gate-lamp — was that duty was the only company they had left. The gate didn't leave. The gate didn't die. The gate asked nothing but the same small faithfulness, every dusk, forever, and Rafael gave it gladly, because the alternative was a quiet cottage and a name in a ledger and a brother-shaped hole that twenty years had not dulled but had instead, like a stone in a stream, worn smooth and deep and permanent.

Everything was fine. Rafael blew out the desk candle and went up to walk the wall.

* * *

The night rounds took two hours if you did them properly, and Rafael always did them properly. Along the curtain wall to the north tower, down the switchback stair cut into the living rock, along the cliff path where the wind came up off the lowlands smelling of pine and distance, and then the long climb to the overlook where the whole pass lay open below like a cupped hand.

From the overlook, on a clear night, you could see everything worth seeing in Thornfell. The official road, pale and winding, safe and dull. And then — because Rafael Eriksen knew these mountains better than any magistrate, better than any soldier, better, probably, than anyone alive — the unofficial ones.

There was the Smuggler's Stair, a seam of hand-holds chiseled into the eastern face, invisible from below unless you knew to look for the place where the lichen grew in a suspiciously convenient ladder-shape. There was the Hollow Under the Old Pine, where contraband had been cached since before the crown drew its pretty maps. There was the ford at Greywater that ran knee-deep by day and shin-deep by midnight, when the upstream dam of fallen rock backed the water up — a trick of timing that Dax Ferrow and his crew had been exploiting for years, slipping kegs and bolts of untaxed silk through the dark while the customs men slept.

Rafael knew all of it. Knew it the way they knew the lines of their own palm. And here was the strange arithmetic of their life, the one they never examined too closely: they had never once reported a single smuggler's path. Not to the magistrate. Not to the garrison. Not to anyone.

It wasn't corruption. Rafael had thought about this many nights, walking the wall, and had finally arrived at something like peace with it. The smugglers hurt no one that the crown itself didn't hurt worse with its tariffs and its levies and its wars fought far away for reasons that arrived in Thornfell only as taxes and occasionally as coffins. The mountains belonged to the mountain folk before they belonged to any kingdom drawn on vellum in a capital Rafael had never seen. And besides — besides — some of those smugglers had helped search for Tomas. Dax Ferrow himself, barely more than a boy then, had spent four days in the crevasse country with ropes and lamps, calling his name into the cold. Rafael had never forgotten. Duty to the crown was written in their oath. Duty to the people who had climbed into the dark for their brother was written somewhere deeper, somewhere the oath's ink had never reached.

So Rafael watched the dark paths, and noted them in a private map that lived under a floorboard and would burn before it was ever surrendered, and let the small crimes of small people pass beneath the great humming wards like fish passing under a bridge. Keep what passes. Pass what is kept. The old words had more than one reading, if you lived with them long enough.

Tonight the paths were empty. Tonight everything was quiet. Rafael leaned on the overlook rail, breath fogging in the mountain cold, and looked up.

The stars were thick tonight. Winter-thick, though it was only early autumn — the sky already behaving like the deep season, the constellations sharp enough to cut. Rafael found the ones their grandmother had named for them: the Keeper's Chain, arcing over the pass like a drawn line; the Lantern, hanging low in the west with its one red star burning at the center like a coal; and there, just clearing the eastern ridge, the Twins, two white stars close enough to touch, rising together or not at all.

Their grandmother had pointed them out when Rafael was six and Tomas was nine, both of them squirming on the cold grass behind the cottage. *Those are yours,* she'd said, tapping each twin in turn against Rafael's shoulder and then Tomas's. *Gate-keepers come in pairs, see. One to hold the door, one to watch the road. Always two. The mountain doesn't trust a lone keeper.*

Rafael had laughed then. Tomas had laughed. Two boys under two stars, and a whole life ahead of them that was supposed to be spent side by side on this wall, growing old the way the gate grew old, together.

One of the twins burned steady above the ridge now. The other was behind cloud. Rafael watched the gap where its light should have been until the watching became unbearable, and then made themselves stop, and finished the round, and went home, and slept badly, as usual, and woke before dawn, as usual, and told themselves everything was fine.

* * *

Three days passed this way. Three days of routine performed flawlessly: the dusk feeding, the ledger entries, the wall walks, the watching of roads that officially did not exist. On the third day a tinker came through southbound with news from the lowland towns — grain prices, a wedding, a rumor of trouble at court that the tinker delivered with the relish of a man selling gossip by the pound. Something about the king's treasurer being arrested. Something about debts. Rafael listened politely, bought a new whetstone, and forgot it by supper. Court troubles were rain falling on some other roof.

On the fourth evening, the wardstone was hungry again.

Hungrier. Rafael noticed it during the dusk feeding — the pull at their palm stronger than before, the words vanishing into the stone almost before they were spoken, the hum above the pass thinner, reedier, a note held too long by a tired throat. Rafael checked the cabinet. Checked the secondary stones in the north tower. All of them warm, all of them working, all of them working *harder*, the way a heart works harder when something is wrong with it.

Rafael fetched the maintenance manual, a leather-bound thing older than the town, and read the section on ward fatigue by lamplight, and found nothing that matched. Wards faded over decades. These had faded in days. Wards failed cleanly, with warning signs listed in careful script. Whatever was happening to the Thornfell wards was failing *sideways* — not dimming but straining, not dying but reaching, as if the net above the pass were trying to hold something it had never been asked to hold before. Or as if whatever fed it were being pulled away from underneath.

Rafael stood in the gatehouse doorway after midnight, unable to sleep, looking up at the pass. The stars wheeled overhead, all of them present, all of them burning. The Keeper's Chain hung over the ridge. The Twins were both visible tonight, for once — both white points clear of the clouds, rising together, as if the sky were offering some small consolation it had no right to offer.

And the wards hummed above the empty road, thin and strained and hungry, holding, holding, holding.

Rafael went inside. Made tea they didn't drink. Took out the private map from under the floorboard and spread it across the desk and stared at it without seeing it, because staring at familiar things was easier than thinking about unfamiliar ones. Somewhere in the middle of that fruitless hour, a memory surfaced unbidden, the way memories do at the worst possible times: Tomas, seventeen, sitting on this exact desk with his boots on the drawer, saying, *Do you ever wonder what the wards actually run on? Father says stone and oath. Grandmother said something else once. She said the old keepers knew a word for it that nobody uses anymore.*

Rafael had asked what word. Tomas had grinned and said he'd find out, he was going to ask the archivist at Coldwell next supply run, he liked knowing things, knowing things was going to be *his* job someday, the watching-the-road job, the pair-of-them job —

He had never made that supply run. Eleven months later he went up the pass instead, and did not come back, and the question he'd been carrying died with him, unanswered, unasked, buried in a crevasse with the rest of him.

*What the wards actually run on.*

Rafael closed the map. Put it back under the floorboard. Sat in the dark with their hands flat on the desk, feeling — actually feeling, through the soles of their feet, through the bones of the chair, through the old stone of the cottage — the wards humming overhead. Thin. Strained. Hungry.

*Stone and oath,* their father had said. But their father had also gone gray in ten months and died with questions in his eyes that he'd never spoken aloud, and their grandmother had used a word for the wards' food that nobody used anymore, and the crown's treasurer had just been arrested over *debts*, and the wards were starving in plain sight of a full sky, and Rafael Eriksen had spent twenty years perfecting the art of not asking questions.

Everything was fine. Everything was fine. Everything was—

Above the pass, so briefly that Rafael would spend the rest of their life unsure whether they had truly seen it, one of the Twins flickered.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Flickered* — the way a lamp flickers when something passes between it and the air that feeds it. A stutter in the white light, a hiccup in the ancient pattern, there and gone in less time than a heartbeat.

Rafael was out the door and up the steps before they'd decided to move, taking the stairs two at a time, coatless in the cold, heart slamming. At the top of the gatehouse they stood gripping the parapet and stared east until their eyes watered.

Both stars burned steady. Both twins rose together over the ridge, white and calm and utterly unchanged, as if the sky had never stumbled in its long recitation. The wards hummed. The lamp burned. The road lay empty and silvered under the starlight, beautiful and benign and completely, perfectly still.

Rafael stayed there a long time. Long enough for the cold to sink through shirt and skin and settle into the marrow. Long enough to talk themselves down twice, three times, four times — *a trick of the eye, exhaustion, the wind, a bird, nothing, nothing, nothing* — and to notice, each time, that the talking-down rang hollow, the way a bell rings hollow when it's cracked.

Because Rafael Eriksen had watched this sky for twenty years. Twenty years of nights, thousands of hours, and the stars above Thornfell had never once — not in storm, not in wildfire season, not in the aurora year when the whole northern sky had rippled like a green sea — never once flickered. The sky was the one thing in their life that had never changed. It was the one thing they had trusted without reservation, the one constant that grief had not been able to take.

And it had just blinked.

Rafael's breath came slow and deliberate, the way it did before hard climbs. They looked at the wards — invisible, humming, hungry — and then at the stars — visible, steady, lying — and felt, for the first time in twenty years of flawless duty, the ground shift under the one certainty they had built their whole life upon.

Something was coming through the dark toward the gate. Or something was already here, underneath, pulling. Either way, the keeping was about to change, and Rafael Eriksen, who had spent two decades making sure that nothing in their world ever changed, stood alone on the gatehouse wall at midnight and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling:

Tomorrow night, everything would not be fine.

Below, in the cottage, the kettle they'd forgotten had begun, very softly, to sing.
