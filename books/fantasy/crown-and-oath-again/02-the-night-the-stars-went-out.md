## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The kettle had still been singing when Rafael finally went down to silence it, and that was the first wrong thing. Rafael Eriksen did not forget kettles. In twenty years of keeping the Thornfell gate, they had never once forgotten a kettle, or a lamp-trim, or the second bolt on the western wicket, or the hour. Their life was a ledger of small duties, each entered nightly and each closed out by morning, and if a kettle could slip through the columns then something was already loose in the machinery of them.

They drank the tea anyway, standing in the cottage doorway with the cold coming off the mountain, and watched the dark where the pass bent out of sight.

The wards were quiet. That was the trouble. For two nights they had been humming under Rafael's skin like a held breath — the deep, mineral note that lived in the stone of the mountains and only showed itself to a keeper, the note that said *the border holds*. Tonight it had gone thin. Not silent — Rafael almost wished it were silent; silence would have meant failure, and failure would have been simple. It had gone *thin*, the way ice goes thin over a stream, so that you could see the black water moving underneath and know that your weight no longer mattered to it.

Rafael set down the cup, took up the lamp, and climbed back to the wall.

This was the shape of their nights: up the outer stair, along the parapet with its stones worn smooth as riverbed pebbles, to the little iron cradle above the arch where the ward-stone sat. The stone was older than the kingdom. Older than any kingdom, probably; the masons who built the gatehouse had found it already in the mountain, humming already, and had done the sensible thing and built around it. Rafael laid a palm flat against it every night, felt for the note, counted the harmonics the way their mother had taught them — *one for the stone, one for the blood, one for the bargain* — and wrote the count in the gate-log with a pencil stub.

One for the stone: present, steady.

One for the blood: present, steady. This was the one that always made Rafael pause, though they could not have said why until later. It hummed at a pitch that felt familiar the way a smell can be familiar — some corridor of the past opened for half a second and shut again before you could walk down it.

One for the bargain: present. Thin.

Rafael wrote *all steady* in the log, which was a lie of omission, and hated themselves for it, and did not cross it out. What would they have written? *Bargain thinned, cause unknown, recommend nothing because there is no one left to recommend it to.* The crown had not sent an inspector to Thornfell in nine years. The last one had yawned through the ceremony, pronounced the wards "adequately maintained," and taken Rafael's good bottle of plum wine as a toll going home.

So Rafael stood on the wall with their hand on the stone and waited for midnight, because that is what keepers do when the ice goes thin. You do not run. You stand where the crack will open and you watch it open, because someone has to see exactly how it happens, or no one will ever know how to mend it.

The valley below was full of small fires tonight. That was the second strange thing, though Rafael hadn't understood it as strange yet. Smugglers' camps in the pass — normal enough; Dax Ferrow's people came through twice a season and lit their cook-fires like honest men, which they were not, but which they were careful about pretending to be. But there were more fires than Ferrow ever brought. Fires up on the shoulder of the mountain where no trail ran. Fires in the pine-dark east of the gate where even goats didn't go. And figures moving between them, unhurried, like people settling in to wait for something everyone knew the hour of.

Rafael had told themselves it was refugees. There had been whispers all autumn — hard winters in the lowlands, tithe-wars in the south, the usual rot — and refugees moved toward passes the way water moves toward drains. But refugees did not arrive and then simply sit. Refugees pushed. Refugees begged at gates. These fires waited with the terrible patience of things that had been told to wait.

At half past eleven, the ward-note changed again.

Not thinner. *Warmer.* That was the word Rafael's mind supplied and immediately rejected, because warmth was the wrong direction entirely, and yet there it was: the third harmonic, the bargain, had stopped straining and begun to — swell. To reach. Rafael pressed their palm harder against the stone and felt something move beneath the note like a fish under ice, close to the surface now, rising, rising toward the place where a hand rested, as if it knew the hand. As if it recognized—

Rafael snatched their hand back.

The stone went on humming without them, sweeter than it had any right to, and Rafael stood on their own wall feeling the way they had felt at their brother's funeral: that the world had swapped a load-bearing piece of itself while no one was looking, and everything built on top of it was about to come down.

Tomas. The name arrived uninvited, as it did perhaps four times a year, usually at dusk, usually when the light did something particular to the mountainside. Twenty years dead. Twenty years, and Rafael still kept his boots oiled in the cottage closet, still couldn't have told you why, except that throwing them out would have been a second death and Rafael had refused to attend the first one properly — had stood at the graveside dry-eyed and furious at the priest, at the mountain, at Tomas himself most of all, for dying stupid and young and out beyond the ward-line where he had been told, *told*, not to go.

Everyone said grief softened. No one mentioned that it also calcified. Rafael had poured theirs into routines and mortar and gate-logs, twenty years of *all steady*, and had believed — truly believed, right up until this evening — that the ledger balanced.

The stars began to die at midnight precisely.

Rafael noticed it first in the reflection in the ward-stone's polished face, which is why keepers polish them: a star over the eastern ridge flickered, guttered, and went out like a pinched wick. Then another. Then the whole sky east of the ridge, in a wave, rolling westward faster than anything in the heavens had any right to move, and Rafael turned around and looked up and the sky was being eaten. Not clouded — *eaten*. Star after star after star, whole constellations folding up like lamps carried down a corridor, and the darkness that came behind them was total and velvet and wrong, because there should have been moonlight, there should have been the moon, and Rafael looked to where the moon rode high over Thornfell and found a hole in the sky shaped exactly like it.

Below, the valley ignited with sound. Every fire-side in the pass began to shout at once. Somewhere a horse screamed. Bells started in the village three miles down-valley — alarm bells, the ones that hadn't been rung since Rafael was a child, rung badly because no one alive remembered the rhythm.

And the ward-stone sang.

Rafael had heard it strain, had heard it thin, had heard it mutter through tremors and the occasional lightning strike. They had never heard it *sing* — a rising arpeggio, bright and desperate and heartbreakingly sweet, the sound of something pouring itself out — and Rafael's hands were moving before their mind caught up, slamming into the old emergency postures their mother had drilled into them at nine years old: both palms flat, weight forward, *hold the line, hold the line, hold the—*

The third harmonic broke.

It did not fade. It broke, clean, like a string parting under too much weight, and Rafael felt the snap travel up their arms and through their teeth and into the floor of their skull, and the singing became a single long descending tone — the sound a struck bell makes as it dies — and then the stone beneath Rafael's hands went cold for the first time in recorded history, and the wards over the Thornfell mountains ended.

The silence afterward was physical. Rafael's ears rang with the absence. Twenty years of a hum they'd stopped hearing, gone, and its absence was louder than the singing had been.

Rafael stayed in the posture. Palms flat, weight forward, holding a line that no longer existed, because the alternative was screaming, and keepers do not scream until the counting is done.

Then the recognition came, and it was worse than the breaking.

In the instant before the third harmonic snapped, it had *turned toward them*. Rafael had felt it lean into their hands with something that was not wind and not resonance but *attention*, warm and eager and terribly familiar, and now, in the ringing silence, their mind replayed it at the speed of grief and matched it against a memory they had spent twenty years not opening: a boy sitting on this same wall, feet swinging, humming while he sharpened a knife. Tomas, sixteen years old, humming a tuneless little three-note figure that drove their mother mad because he never resolved it, never landed the third note, just rose and rose and *left it hanging* —

One for the stone. One for the blood. One for the bargain.

*One for the blood.*

The ward that had held Thornfell's border for a thousand years had been running on a human soul — Rafael had known that much since childhood, the way you know the water in your well comes from somewhere without ever asking where. Keeper's children learned it as theology, not arithmetic: *the mountain takes a toll and keeps it, and the keeping is the price of the light.* Rafael had assumed — everyone assumed — stone souls, mountain souls, some old bargain from the age when such trades were made. Never a person. Never a boy with a knife and an unresolved tune and boots that were still, God help them, oiled in a closet three miles away.

But the harmonic hadn't lied. Blood knew blood. The ward had leaned into Rafael's hands like a dog hearing its name after years, and Rafael had pulled away from it.

"I'm sorry," Rafael said aloud, to a cold stone, on a starless wall, and their voice came out in pieces. "I'm sorry. I didn't know it was you."

The valley was filling with people.

That came back to Rafael slowly, through the grief, the way the world comes back after a blow: shouts resolving into words, words into movement. The pass fires were abandoning their circles and converging on the gate — smugglers with pack-mules, yes, but also men in the mismatched armor of discharged soldiers, a merchant family carrying a trunk between them like a stretcher, a woman herding three children with her coat, shepherds, pilgrims, a whole dark river of humanity flowing uphill through the pass in the absolute black, guided by nothing but memory and terror, because every one of them had looked up and seen the sky emptied and every one of them had reached the same conclusion at the same moment:

*Get behind the gate. Whatever took the stars, get behind the gate.*

Which would have been funny, Rafael thought with a wild edge, if it weren't the exact opposite of the truth. The gate guarded nothing now. The gate was a hole in a fence whose wires had just been cut, and the fence had been cut from the inside, and whatever the wards had been holding back for a thousand years — Rafael had never known what, precisely, only *that* — was free to look at the hole and take an interest.

The first of them reached the gate within the hour.

Rafael did what keepers do, because there was nothing else to be. Went down, unbarred the great leaf of the gate — no use barring it; a crowd in the dark tramples itself, and Rafael had seen the aftermath of a stamped gate-moot once and never wanted to see it again — and stood in the archway with the lamp held high, and called the old words that had not been needed in living memory:

*"The gate is open! Come through slow, come through by the lamp! Children first, wounded second, all others by order of the keeper!"*

Nobody laughed at *by order of the keeper*. That was the thing Rafael would remember afterward, when they tried to reconstruct the night: nobody laughed. A ragged column formed out of pure darkness, as if the word *order* were itself a kind of light, and they came through — a smuggler Rafael knew by his limp and his lies, nodding to them with stripped-away courtesy; a soldier still wearing the sunburst of the Third Border Company, eyes white-rimmed, whispering *they went out, they all went out at once, did you see*; the merchant family with their trunk, which clinked, and which no one asked about; the woman with the children, the smallest of whom was crying without sound, tears just falling out of her face like rain.

"Where's it safest?" the woman asked Rafael, gripping their sleeve. "Tell me where's safe."

"Nowhere," Rafael said, honestly, because the night had burned the habit of comfort out of them, "but here I can see it coming," and the woman nodded as if that were the finest answer in the world and moved on.

For two hours Rafael stood in the archway and fed the darkness through, and the strangest part — the part they would turn over for years — was that it worked. People are made to be managed in small mercies. Give them a lamp and a sequence and a voice that does not shake very much, and they will walk in lines through the end of the world. By the third hour there were hundreds camped inside the ward-line, fires sprouting on the old muster-field like a second, truer constellation, and the pass behind the gate stood empty, and the sky above it stayed a solid, seamless, starless black, and nothing had come down out of the mountains yet.

Nothing had come. But the mountain had begun, very faintly, to hum.

Rafael felt it through the soles of their boots first — the same deep mineral note that had lived in the ward-stone, except now it was everywhere, in the bedrock, in the air, uncontained, a note released from its instrument and roaming. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that Rafael knew to listen, was the tune.

Three notes. Rising, rising, never landing. Sixteen years old, knife in hand, feet swinging off the wall.

Rafael walked back up to the ward-stone and put their palm against it one more time, though they knew what they would find: cold, dead, a mere rock now, its tenant fled or taken elsewhere. The hum wasn't in the stone anymore. It was in the *mountain*, all of it at once, and it was not random. Rafael had spent twenty years learning every voice the pass contained — wind through the eastern notch, water under the glacier, Dax Ferrow's mules, soldiers' drums — and this was none of those. This was directed. This was a signal, or a summons, or a search.

Something beneath the Thornfell mountains was looking for something it had lost. Or something beneath the Thornfell mountains was calling something home.

"Tomas," Rafael said to the dead stone, quietly, and the mountain hummed the hanging third note, and did not resolve it, and Rafael understood that they were being spoken to.

Behind them, down on the muster-field, among five hundred strangers' fires, someone had begun — softly, half-asleep, a child's voice by the sound of it — to hum along.

Rafael turned, lamp raised, and scanned the sea of dark tents and darker faces, trying to find the source of the sound, and could not; the humming seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, threading between the fires like smoke. But as Rafael listened, other voices joined it. A shepherd. One of the soldiers. The merchant woman. Half-asleep, unbidden, dozens of the strangers at the gate humming a three-note figure none of them could have learned anywhere, rising and rising and refusing to land, and Rafael stood on the wall above them with the hair lifting on their arms and understood that the mountain was not searching the valleys.

It was already inside every soul that had come through the gate tonight. It was teaching them the song.

And far down the pass, at the limit of the lamplight, where the road bent into the black, a rider was coming up through the dark at a pace that had given up on stealth entirely — and even before the lamp found the pale flash of a knight's stolen cloak, before the rider reined in below the wall and looked up with a face like a drawn blade, Rafael knew two things with absolute certainty.

The first was that the night had not finished breaking things.

The second was that the woman shouting up at them — *"Gate-keeper! Gate-keeper, in the crown's name, open your books!"* — had come all the way through a starless mountain pass alone, which meant she knew exactly what the wards had been running on.

And so, soon, was going to tell Rafael whether they wanted her to or not.
