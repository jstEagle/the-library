## 3. An Oathbreaker at the Door

The stars did not come back.

Rafael Eriksen stood at the gate through the whole of the next day and most of the following night, watching a sky the color of wet slate, waiting for even one pinprick of light to prove the world had not ended. Nothing came. The wards lay dead beneath their feet — Rafael could feel it now that they knew what to feel, an absence like a tooth pulled from the jaw, a socket where something had lived for twenty years without anyone knowing it was alive at all.

*His soul-mark.* The thought kept returning, patient as tide. *Tomas's mark.*

They had burned his body on the eastern slope in spring, when Rafael was nineteen, and the crown had sent a form letter signed by a clerk who could not spell their family name correctly. *Died in service to the realm.* No one had said what the service was. No one had said the realm had taken more than a body.

Rafael worked because work was the only prayer they trusted. They swept the gatehouse floor. They trimmed the lamp wick and fed it oil it no longer needed to burn against starlight. They checked the smuggler paths from memory, walking the first mile of each in the grey morning: the Widow's Stair, the Hollow Ladder, the Cold Cut where Dax Ferrow's people moved contraband and refugees alike under the mountain's blind stone ribs. All quiet. All dark. Even the smugglers were staying home, as if the absence of stars had made lawbreakers honest out of sheer superstition.

On the second evening, someone knocked on the gate.

Three knocks, spaced like a soldier counting cadence. Then silence. Then three more.

Rafael took down the gate-pike out of habit rather than fear and opened the wicket door a hand's width. Lamplight spilled onto a figure standing in the road: a woman in travel-stained clothes that had once been very fine, a sword wrapped in oilcloth slung across her back the way you carry something you are not permitted to draw. Her hair was cropped short and going grey at the temples. There was a raw, wind-scoured look about her, the look of someone who had ridden hard for days and slept worse.

"I'm looking for the keeper of Thornfell Gate," she said.

"You've found them."

She inclined her head — not quite a bow, but the skeleton of one, the muscle memory of courtesy surviving the loss of everything it used to attach to. "My name is Maren Kael."

Rafael's grip shifted on the pike. That name had crossed their desk two winters ago, inked into a royal decree read aloud in every garrison hall from here to the coast. *Ser Maren Kael, stripped of oath and rank, her name struck from the rolls, her spurs broken before the assembled court.* The charge had been sedition. The specifics had been sealed. Sealed charges were how the crown said *she told the truth and we didn't like it.*

"Gate's closed," Rafael said.

"The gate is broken," she said quietly. "I've come about why."

"We're done talking." Rafael began to swing the wicket shut.

Her hand shot out and caught the edge — fast, unhesitating, the hand of someone who had spent a lifetime catching things. "Keeper. Please." And there it was, under the iron: *please*, cracked down the middle. "I have ridden nine days. I have eaten things I won't describe. Every road south has bailiffs on it looking for me, and I chose this one anyway, because this is the only place in the mountains where what happened might mean something to someone." She let go of the door and stepped back, palms open. "Hear me out. If you still want me gone after, I'll go."

Rafael looked at her a long moment. The lamplight caught the scar across her knuckles, old and clean, a dueling scar or a working one. Behind her the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

"One hour," Rafael said. "Then you're out."

* * *

Ser Maren Kael sat at Rafael's table like a woman who had forgotten how tables worked — perched on the edge of the bench, hands flat on the wood, back straight, as if relaxation were another rank that had been stripped from her. She accepted tea but did not drink it. She watched Rafael the way soldiers watch terrain.

"You know who I am," she said.

"I know what the heralds said."

"And you believe them?"

"I believe the crown doesn't break its own knights for nothing," Rafael said. "And I believe whatever nothing was, it scared them enough to seal the record. Which makes you either the most dangerous traitor in the realm or the most inconvenient honest woman in it. I haven't decided which."

A ghost of a smile touched her mouth and died. "Fair."

"So talk. You have fifty minutes now."

Maren turned her cup slowly on the table, once around, like a compass needle seeking north. "Two years ago I served in the King's Own. Intelligence division. After the war — the Victory War, they call it — I was assigned to audit the old campaign ledgers. Reconciling accounts. Munitions, provisions, casualties. Bureaucratic work. Nobody thought anything of it." She stopped turning the cup. "Except the accounts didn't reconcile. There was a disbursement line running through the entire war. Enormous sums, paid quarterly, to a recipient that had no name. Only a sigil. A mountain with a door in it."

Rafael went very still.

"I followed it," Maren said. "Down through seven layers of clerical obfuscation, because that's what I was good at — following things. And at the bottom of it, Keeper, I found payment records. Not for steel. Not for grain. For *souls*. Purchased souls. Delivered to the Undercourt beneath Thornfell, in exchange for—" her voice caught, steadied— "in exchange for the mountain's armies. The deep gentry marched for us in the Victory War. That's how we won. That's what we paid."

"That's a lie," Rafael said, but their voice came out wrong, thin, and Maren heard it.

"I reported it. In writing, up the full chain, with the ledgers attached." She finally lifted her eyes. "Six days later my oath was struck. My records were burned. The clerk who helped me translate the deep-script was disappeared — his wife was told he ran off with a laundress. And I was given a choice between the block and exile, so long as I never spoke the contents of what I'd found." She spread her hands. "As you can see, I've chosen poorly."

Rafael stood up. Walked to the window. The starless dark looked back at them.

"Why here?" they asked, without turning. "Why my gate?"

"Because your gate broke the same night the stars went out. Because the wards over Thornfell were the oldest in the realm, older than the crown, and the crown's own engineers couldn't explain what fed them." A pause. "Because I think the answer is in those ledgers, Keeper, and I think the ledger line runs through this mountain, and I think you're the only person alive who knows this rock well enough to get me to the bottom of it."

"No."

Maren blinked. "You haven't even—"

"No." Rafael turned. "Whatever the crown did, whatever rot sits under this mountain, it's not mine to dig up. I keep a gate. That's the whole of my oath. I keep the gate, I keep the passes safe, I keep the people on both sides of it alive. I don't march into the Undercourt on the word of a disgraced knight chasing a conspiracy." They heard their own voice harden and could not soften it, because underneath the hardness was terror, and under the terror was Tomas's face at nineteen, laughing, the last time she'd seen him. "Go to the capital. Go to a temple. Go anywhere but through my gate."

"It's not a conspiracy," Maren said, rising too, and now there was iron in her voice, the thing that had made her a knight before it made her an exile. "It's arithmetic. Souls went in. Power came out. And two nights ago the wards died, and the stars went out, and if the wards were fed the way I think they were fed, then they weren't dying, Keeper. They were being *reclaimed*. The debt came due, and nobody paid it, and now the whole sky is collateral."

"Get out."

"Your brother—"

Rafael's hand was on her collar before they knew they'd moved, slamming her back against the wall, the gate-pike's butt cracking against stone beside her head. Maren didn't flinch. She just looked at Rafael with terrible, gentle comprehension, and that was worse than any blow.

"How do you know about my brother," Rafael breathed.

"Because I read the casualty rolls," Maren said softly. "Twenty years ago. One name, Thornfell garrison, cause of death sealed by royal order. The only sealed death in a border skirmish that killed eleven men openly. I didn't know what it meant then." Her eyes held Rafael's, steady and grieving. "I know what it means now. Don't I."

The room tilted. Rafael let go of her and stepped back, and the step back felt like falling.

* * *

*Interlude — the record of Ser Maren Kael, written in exile, unsent*

*I told him the truth in the throne room and I would tell it again with the axe falling, so let me set it down here where ink can hold what mouths may not.*

*We did not win the Victory War. We bought it. Eleven thousand souls, drawn from debtor prisons and plague houses and — the crown's own clerks were careful here, but careful is not the same as clean — from border garrisons, men whose deaths were written up as fever, as fall, as skirmish. Their names went into the deep ledger and their souls went under the mountain, and the gentry of the deep rose up and fought for us, and the historians wrote of valor.*

*I stood before the king with the ledgers in my arms and I said: this is not victory, this is purchase, and the price is still being paid by people who never agreed to pay it. He looked at me the way a man looks at weather. Then he took my oath.*

*I have been two years learning what I actually lost. Not the title. Not the sword. I lost the belief that the realm and the right were the same thing. I am told that is what happens to oathbreakers. I am beginning to think it is what happens to knights.*

*The gate-keeper refused me. I expected it. But when I said his brother's name he looked at me like a man looking into a well he'd drunk from all his life and never known was bottomless. He will say no again tomorrow. And the day after. But he will open the gate. Because he is a keeper, and keepers cannot watch a debt devour the sky and stand idle. It is the one faith I have left.*

* * *

Rafael did not sleep. They sat in the gatehouse with the dead wards humming their silence and the lamp burning oil for no reason, and they thought about Tomas.

Twenty years. Twenty years of lighting the lamps he should have been there to light, of keeping the passes he'd died — supposedly — defending. Twenty years of a form letter and a sealed file and a grave with a sword on it and nothing underneath but the mountain's cold patience. And now a stranger sat in their kitchen saying the word *arithmetic* like a curse, saying *collateral*, saying *your brother* in the tone people use for line items.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stair. Maren appeared in the doorway, armorless, unarmed, hands visible — a soldier's grammar for *I am not your enemy.*

"I'll leave today," she said. "I gave you my word on the hour and I keep what's left of my word. But before I go, I need you to hear one thing, and then you can put me out in the snow."

Rafael said nothing. She took it as permission.

"When the wards failed, the collapse carried a signature. Your instruments would have recorded it — ward-scribes log everything, even the failures. Especially the failures." She reached into her coat and drew out a folded paper, worn soft at the creases. "I pulled the archive copy from the provincial survey office two nights ago, before the bailiffs locked it down. The signature on your collapsed wards, Keeper. It isn't generic. It isn't a pattern-type. It's individual. Souls have marks the way seals have stamps." She laid the paper on the table between them, face up.

Rafael looked down.

The ward-signature glowed faintly off the page, scribe's silver on black paper, and it was shaped like a key turning in a lock — and beneath it, in the survey clerk's neat hand, the catalog notation: *Source: estate of T. Eriksen, Thornfell garrison, deceased. Acquired per Crown Warrant 4471-C. Term: perpetual.*

The room went white at the edges. Rafael heard their own breath, ragged, and Maren's voice coming from very far away:

"They didn't take his life, Keeper. They took everything else. And they've been spending it to hold up your sky for twenty years — until whoever holds his debt decided to call it in." She paused. "The crown knew. The crown has always known. And now the stars are out, and the debt is called, and there is exactly one person in this world with a right and a reason to walk down into the Undercourt and demand him back."

Rafael stared at the signature. At the shape of a key. At their brother's name in a clerk's handwriting, filed like grain.

When they spoke, their voice was quiet and level and colder than the mountain's heart.

"How far down does the road go?"

Maren exhaled — relief and dread in the same breath. "All the way," she said. "But we'll need help. There's a smuggler named Dax Ferrow who owes you a blood debt, if the stories are true. And we'll need him before the bailiffs find us — because as of last night, Keeper, they're not just hunting me anymore." She nodded toward the window, toward the starless east, where on the far ridge a line of lanterns had appeared in the dark, moving in perfect formation, descending toward the gate.

"They're hunting everyone who knows what the wards were fed."
