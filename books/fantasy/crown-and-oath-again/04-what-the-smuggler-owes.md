## 4. What the Smuggler Owes

Dax Ferrow had made a living out of knowing exactly where he stood, and for three days now the ground had stopped answering.

He crouched at the mouth of the Hollow Stair with a cold lantern in one hand and his father's compass in the other. The needle spun slow circles, lazy as a drunk, and every time it slowed Dax felt something in his chest tighten and refuse to let go. Twenty-two years he'd run these passes. He could walk the Thornfells blindfolded, deaf, bleeding, in a whiteout — had done all four at once more than once — because the mountain told him where he was. Not maps. Maps lied. The mountain didn't. The stone had a grain to it, like wood, like muscle, and if you put your palm flat against it long enough you could feel which way the deep water ran, which way the old roads bent, which way home lay.

Now the grain was gone. The stone felt like a corpse.

"Cap." Pellin's voice behind him, pitched low the way everything was pitched low now, in that hush people fell into when the sky went wrong. "Crew's asking again."

"Tell them what I told them last time."

"They're not asking about food this time."

Dax closed the compass. Of course they weren't. Three days of dark, no stars, no moon, nothing above but a black ceiling so complete it pressed on the eyes, and men who'd spent their whole lives reading the heavens for direction had started looking up out of habit and flinching at what they found there. Or didn't find. Brak had stopped talking entirely. Little Sefa, who'd smuggled salt and secrets through six kingdoms without ever once crying, cried in her sleep where she thought no one heard. Everyone heard.

"Then tell them we move at first light," Dax said.

"There is no first light, Cap. That's rather the problem."

Dax almost laughed. Almost. It came out as something thinner. "First light is when I say it is. That hasn't changed." He turned and looked back down into the hollow where his crew had bedded down among the rocks, twelve souls huddled around two small fires fed by peat they'd carried up from the lowlands because even the mountain's own fuel seemed to have gone sullen. Twelve souls he was responsible for. Twelve paybooks he signed. Twelve families who'd get word someday, if word still traveled, that the Ferrow crew went into the Thornfells the same week God turned off the lamps.

The ward-fall hadn't just killed the stars. It had killed the roads. Every path Dax knew ran along or under or through the old wards' reach, because the wards were quiet and predictable and everyone who moved anything worth moving had built their trade around their hum. Now the hum was dead, and the passes had gone strange — rockfalls where no rockfall should be, trails that ended in cliff faces Dax would swear on his mother's grave hadn't been there in autumn. Twice his scouts had come back reporting that a valley was simply missing. Not fogged over. Missing. As if someone had picked it up and put it somewhere else.

And underneath all of it, worse than all of it: the feeling. The mountain was *paying attention* to them now. Dax couldn't explain how he knew, only that walking these stones used to feel like walking past a sleeping bear, and now it felt like the bear had opened one eye.

He pocketed the compass and went down to his crew.

* * *

They were arguing when he arrived, which was better than silence, so he let it go on a moment from the edge of the firelight before he made himself known. Pellin was doing the talking, because Pellin always did the talking, and across from her sat the gate-keeper.

Rafael Eriksen looked like hell. Everyone did, after three days, but on Rafael it sat differently — not fear, not grief exactly, but the look of a person holding a door shut with their whole body while something on the other side knocked politely. They sat wrapped in a travel cloak that had seen forty winters, hands wrapped around an iron cup of tea gone cold, and they were listening to Pellin argue with the patience of a stone wall.

"—and what I'm saying," Pellin was saying, "is that your gate has a garrison road. Garrison roads are kept. Kept means cleared, patrolled, provisioned. We take thirty of your refugees up that road to the lowland forts and—"

"The garrison road runs under the wards' anchor-stones," Rafael said. Their voice was flat and tired. "When the wards fell, the anchor-stones fell with them. Half that road is under a hundred tons of mountain now. I've had runners confirm it."

"Then the river road—"

"Flooded. The sluices failed the same midnight the stars did. Everything fails at once, that's rather the point."

Pellin threw up her hands. "Then we're trapped in a bowl of black rock waiting for—"

"You're not trapped." Dax stepped into the firelight. "You're inconvenienced. There's a difference, and the difference is me."

Pellin's mouth snapped shut. Rafael just looked at him, and Dax felt that look like a hand on the back of his neck. Gate-keeper's eyes. They saw everything and judged quietly, which was worse than judging loudly.

"You want to say something, Ferrow," Rafael said. "Say it."

"I want to say you're sitting in my camp drinking my tea." Dax crouched by the fire and held his palms to it. The heat felt good and fake at the same time, like everything did now. "Twelve crew, plus your sixty-odd strays from the gate, eating my stores. And I'm thinking about what happens when those stores run out, which is four days from now, and I'm thinking about what happens when the mountain does whatever it's building up to do, and I'm thinking that every hour we sit here, we're sitting in a place that used to be safe because of rules that don't exist anymore."

"So you have a way out." Not a question.

"I have a *maybe* of a way out." Dax turned his palms. "There's roads under this mountain that aren't on any crown survey. Roads my family's been keeping for five generations, that we keep by keeping them secret. Old ways. Deep ways. They don't care about wards or anchor-stones or whatever died at midnight three days ago, because they were dug by people who were here before anyone was drawing lines on mountains." He paused. "Problem is, I don't know if they're still where I left them."

"What do you mean, where you left them?"

"I mean the mountain's rearranging itself, gate-keeper. My scout walked the Low Gallery yesterday — walked it, marked it, came back — and today it opens onto a chamber none of us have ever seen, with carvings older than the kingdom, and the gallery's just *gone*. So yes, I have roads. What I don't have is a guarantee the roads haven't decided to be elsewhere."

Silence around the fire. Somewhere above them, impossibly far, wind moved through a pass with nothing to push against.

"And the other problem," Rafael said quietly.

Dax looked at them.

"You know the other problem," Rafael said. "You've known since you climbed up to my gate asking for shelter. You know what's down there. You've known longer than any of us."

Dax stared into the fire. He did know. Every smuggler on the Thornfell side knew the shape of it the way farmers know the shape of a bad harvest year — not the details, never the details, but the dread. The Undercourt. The gentry of the deep. The lamplight markets where you could buy things that had no business being bought. His grandfather had come back from a crossing once with grey in his beard that hadn't been there going in, and had never once, in the remaining thirty years of his life, spoken of what he'd seen. Just one thing, said once, drunk, staring into a fire much like this one: *"Never owe them. Never let them count you."*

"We go over the top, then," Pellin said. "Around the range—"

"In permanent dark, on glaciers nobody can read anymore, with no stars to steer by?" Dax shook his head. "We'd lose half the crew inside a week. Maybe all of us. At least under the mountain there's stone between us and whatever emptied the sky."

Rafael set down the cup. When they spoke, their voice had changed — quieter, more careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice they already knew was thin.

"Dax. I need to go down."

"I know."

"Not out. *Down.* Into the court."

The fire popped. A log settled. Around them, Dax was aware of his crew pretending very hard not to listen, and of the refugees beyond them doing the same, and of the enormous dark pressing in from every side like water against a hull.

"You understand what you're asking," Dax said. "You're asking me to lead sixty civilians and my entire livelihood into the Undercourt. Into the place my grandfather wouldn't name on his deathbed."

"I'm not asking you to lead them. I'm asking you to lead *me*. And Maren Kael." Rafael glanced toward the shadows at the camp's edge, where the disgraced knight sat sharpening a sword by feel, since sight was a luxury now. "The rest stay here with Pellin and the stores until we send word."

"And how exactly does a gate-keeper and a broken knight send word out of the Undercourt?"

"I don't know yet."

"*That's* your plan. 'I don't know yet.'"

"That's my plan," Rafael agreed, and there was something in their face then, some banked and terrible certainty, that made Dax's skin crawl. "I'll find the rest below."

Dax stood up. He paced to the edge of the firelight and back, because standing still felt like agreeing. His grandfather's voice in his head. *Never let them count you.* And underneath that voice, another memory, colder and heavier, the one he'd been trying not to touch since the night the stars went out:

Two winters ago. The White Saddle crossing. The storm that shouldn't have caught them because Dax Ferrow did not get caught. Rafael Eriksen hauling his drowning second cousin out of a meltwater chute with rope burns down both arms to the bone, and then turning around and hauling Dax too, because Dax had frozen at the lip of the chute like a green boy instead of a fifth-generation pass-runner. Rafael hadn't said a word about it after. Hadn't named a price. Hadn't even mentioned it. Just wrapped their arms, drank Dax's terrible tea, and left.

That was the trouble with gate-keepers. They gave you life and called it neighborliness, and then you owed them, and the debt sat in you like a swallowed hook.

"You want a price," Rafael said. It wasn't a question either. "Name it."

"There it is." Dax barked something that wasn't quite a laugh. "See, that's what I respect about you, gate-keeper. No groveling. Straight to accounts."

"Name it."

"Fine." He held up fingers. "One: you swear on your office — on the gate itself — that my crew walks free of this, no debts incurred, no names written down anywhere, by anyone, below. Whatever happens down there, the Ferrow crew was never in the mountain. Two: when this is done, whatever this is, I get first refusal on the new crossing rights. Stars come back, wards rebuilt, whatever replaces them — my routes, my terms, crown law be damned. Three." He hesitated. This one cost him. "Three: when it's over, you tell me what happened at your gate. All of it. The true account, from your own mouth. Because I've got sixty mouths to feed and a crew that deserves to know what world they're living in now, and I'll not raise children of the trade on rumors."

Rafael was quiet a long moment. "The third isn't a price. It's a burden."

"It's the most expensive thing I could think of," Dax said. "Truth always is."

Something moved across the gate-keeper's face — pain, maybe, or its close cousin — and Dax understood suddenly, with the awful instinct of a man who reads cargo manifests and faces for a living, that Rafael Eriksen would have paid the first two prices gladly and would carry the third like a stone.

"Done," Rafael said. "On the gate, on my office, on my brother's grave — all three."

"On your brother's—" Dax stopped. He'd heard things, in the days since the fall. Rumors from the refugees. That the gate-keeper had stood in the ruined ward-light and said a name. "Your brother's been dead twenty years, Eriksen."

"Yes," Rafael said. "Which is why I'm going down."

* * *

They left two hours later, in what passed for morning — Dax's arbitrary declaration of first light, announced to a crew that received it with the grim relief of men handed any schedule at all in an unscheduled world. Pellin took command of the camp with the stores and sixty refugees and instructions to hold the Hollow Stair until sent-for. She shook Dax's hand at the tunnel mouth and gripped it harder than she needed to.

"Come back, Cap."

"Always do."

"Liar." But she smiled when she said it, and that was worth more than the words.

Maren Kael waited at the tunnel entrance with her sword wrapped in sackcloth like something shameful, which, Dax reflected, was roughly what the crown had done to her. The knight nodded to him. He nodded back. Two professionals acknowledging a job neither wanted.

"Ferrow leads," Maren said to Rafael. "In the deep ways, the guide's word is law. Even yours."

"Even mine," Rafael agreed.

Dax unshuttered his lantern and held it up, and the light reached perhaps ten feet into the passage before the dark ate it. Somewhere in that dark was a road his great-grandfather had cut and his grandfather had kept and his father had mapped in his head because writing such things down was how crews died. Somewhere in that dark was the Low Gallery, if it still existed, and past it the deep stairs, and past those the places his family had spent five generations not going.

He thought of his grandfather's voice. *Never let them count you.*

Then he thought of rope burns to the bone, and terrible tea, and a debt that had been sitting in him for two years like a splinter working toward the heart.

"Well," Dax Ferrow said to the dark, in the tone of a man addressing an old enemy he'd finally agreed to visit. "Let's go see what the mountain's done with my roads."

And far below them, faint as a heartbeat through the stone, something answered — a single deep note, like a bell struck underwater, that all three of them felt in their teeth before it faded.

None of them spoke after that. But Dax noticed that Rafael's hand had gone to their chest, the way a person touches a wound, and that Maren had gone very still, and that his own feet — traitorous, fifth-generation, pass-runner's feet — had already started walking.
