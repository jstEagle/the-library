## 5. The Child Who Hears the Mountain

The refugee camp had no name, because the people in it had stopped believing that names would hold.

Maren saw it from the ridge first — a scatter of cookfires burning low and blue in a bowl of scree below the old toll road, hundreds of them, maybe a thousand, like embers someone had tried to bury and failed. No banners. No watchtowers. The camp had grown the way lichen grows, without plan or permission, wherever there was shelter enough to lean against. Tents of waxed canvas. Lean-tos of pine boughs. A chapel made from an overturned grain wagon with a candle in each wheel-spoke, though there was nothing above it now for the candles to address.

Nothing above at all. That was the thing Maren could not stop doing: looking up. Twenty years of habit had trained her eyes to find the stars the way a sailor finds the horizon, and every time she lifted her chin her stomach dropped through the floor of her. The sky was not cloudy. Clouds had edges, movement, the promise of wind behind them. The sky was simply *gone*, black as the inside of a closed fist, and the mountains stood up into it like teeth into a dead mouth.

"You keep looking," Rafael said beside her. They walked with their staff planted like a surveyor's rod, reading the ground the way other people read letters. "You'll fall in a hole."

"I know where the stars are," Maren said. "That's the problem. I keep expecting them."

"Expecting is how you get killed on a mountain." But Rafael looked up too, briefly, the way a man touches a bruise to check if it still hurts. It still hurt. Maren was learning that about the gate-keeper: everything they said about caution, they had learned by bleeding from its absence.

Below them, Dax Ferrow crouched at the edge of the camp's first fire ring, talking to a woman with a fishmonger's apron and a face like a shut door. Maren watched him work — watched the shrug, the open palms, the laugh that came a half-second too late to be honest. He was telling her something comforting and untrue. She'd seen officers do the same before a charge. The difference was that Dax seemed almost sorry about it.

"He's lying to her," she said.

"He's lying *for* her," Rafael said. "There's a trade in it. He thinks he got the bad end."

They went down into the camp together, and the camp swallowed them the way the sea swallows rain.

* * *

It smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular sourness of too many people drinking from too few streams. Children ran between the fires playing a game Maren didn't recognize — blindfolded, all of them, hands outstretched, laughing whenever they collided. She stopped to watch. A boy of perhaps eight felt his way along a rope strung between two tents, grinning under his rag blindfold.

"What's the game?" she asked him.

He turned his covered face toward her voice with unsettling precision. "Starfinding," he said. "You have to find them without looking. Nobody can look anymore, so we practice." He giggled. "Tam lost. Tam always loses. Tam cheats by peeking under the—" He tripped over the rope, went down laughing, and was hauled up by three other blindfolded children who found him by sound alone.

Maren stood there a moment longer than she meant to. In the king's army they had drilled night-fighting until soldiers could load a crossbow by touch, but it had been drill, discipline, fear wearing a uniform. These children had made a game of the dark, which was either the most resilient thing she had ever seen or the most heartbreaking, and she suspected it was both, and she suspected she would see a great deal of both before this was finished.

Rafael had already moved ahead, working the camp the way they worked everything — quietly, thoroughly, asking questions that sounded like small talk and weren't. Where did you come from. Which pass. Did the dark come all at once or did it fade. Was anyone on the high trails when it happened. Maren followed in their wake and listened, because listening was the one skill the crown had never managed to strip from her along with her spurs and her oath.

What she pieced together, fire by fire, was this: the valley towns were emptying. Thornfell's villages, the mining camps, the river hamlets — everyone who could walk was walking, away from the peaks, down toward the lowlands, except that the lowlands were full and the roads were worse than the mountains, so a great many people had done the arithmetic of terror and decided that beneath a mountain with dead stars was safer than beneath a sky with none. They had come here, to the shoulder of the range, and built a town of desperation in four days.

And they all told the same story, in the same words, as if they'd rehearsed it: *the mountain is humming.*

Maren heard it herself around the third hour, standing at the camp's edge where the scree gave way to living rock. At first she thought it was the wind. Then she thought it was her own blood. Then she made Rafael stand next to her and be quiet, and they listened together, and there it was — a note below hearing, or nearly below, felt in the molars and the breastbone more than the ears. Not menacing. Worse than menacing. *Patient.* The hum of something enormous that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

"It started the night the stars went out," Rafael said. "Or I never noticed it before. I don't know which frightens me more."

"I know the feeling," Maren said, and meant more than the mountain.

* * *

They found the child because Dax found her first, and Dax being Dax, he found her stealing.

"Not stealing," the child said, when he caught her wrist in the act of lifting a heel of bread off his crew's supply sledge. "Listening to it."

"It's bread, sprout. It doesn't say anything."

"It does if you hold it right." She said it with such complete sincerity that Dax's grip loosened from confusion alone, and that was when Maren got her first good look at the girl.

Six years old, maybe seven. Small even for it, with the wiry underfed toughness of a camp child, hair cut raggedly short as if someone had taken a knife to lice and given up halfway. Her eyes were the striking thing — pale grey, almost white, the eyes of a much older person, and they did not track the way other eyes tracked. They moved when sounds moved. When a pot clanged fifty feet away, her gaze snapped to it before the echo died.

"Where's your mother?" Maren asked, kneeling to the child's level, because you knelt, that was the first rule of children and the last one anyone remembered.

"Don't know." No grief in it. Fact, filed away. "She went to find my father when the stars went out. She said stay with the Hendersons. The Hendersons left." A shrug, economical. "I stayed with the fire instead. Fires don't leave."

Dax looked at Maren over the child's head with an expression that said, in smuggler's shorthand, *this is your problem now.* Maren ignored him, which was becoming a habit she enjoyed.

"What's your name?"

"Wren." Then, after a pause, as if remembering that names usually came with more: "Like the bird. Ma said I was small and loud and wouldn't stay put."

Dax snorted. "Accurate."

"We're not keeping her," Rafael said, arriving with the flat finality of a gate closing. Maren had learned in two days of travel that Rafael Eriksen issued verdicts the way other people breathed, and that half of them were wrong, and that they knew it, and issued them anyway, because a gate-keeper who hesitated at the wrong moment buried caravans.

"She's alone," Maren said.

"The camp is full of alone. We're not an orphanage. We're three adults and a blood debt walking into a hole in the world, and every one of us is a liability to the others already."

Wren looked up at Rafael with those pale eyes and said, "You're the one the mountain knows."

The fire cracked. Somewhere a tent flap snapped in wind that wasn't blowing.

Rafael went very still. Maren watched it happen — watched the gate-keeper's whole architecture of calm take a hit somewhere below the waterline. "What did you say?"

"You're the one the mountain knows." Wren picked at the stolen bread, unbothered. "It says your name when you walk. Raff-ay-el. Like a bell with a crack in it." She bit the bread. "It likes the crack."

"That's enough." Rafael's voice had gone thin. "Where did you hear—"

"From the mountain." Wren said it the way you'd tell someone water was wet, with a flicker of pity for the question. "It sings all the time. It doesn't sleep good since the stars went out. It keeps counting and counting and it comes up short every time, and it gets scared, and when it gets scared the tunnels move." She took another bite and spoke around it. "It's coming up short by one. It counts everybody and it's short by one and it can't sleep until it finds the one."

Maren felt the hairs rise on her forearms. She had stood in war councils where men described ambushes with less conviction and less detail, and she had learned — expensively, at the cost of her oath and her name — to tell the difference between a lie and a truth nobody wanted. This was the second kind. The girl wasn't performing. She was reporting.

"How do you know its songs?" Maren asked gently. "Who taught you?"

"No one." Wren frowned, as if the question were slightly stupid. "It sings, I hear it. Same as you'd hear a river. You don't need somebody to teach you a river." She cocked her head suddenly, listening to something far under the stone, and her face changed — went older, and frightened, and then carefully blank, the way children go blank when they've decided the adults can't handle what they know. "It's counting again," she said quietly. "It just counted you three. It knows your shapes now. It says—" she paused, lips moving, translating, "—it says the tall one owes it a song and the shadow one owes it a door and the one with the broken bell owes it—"

"Stop." Rafael's staff cracked against stone. Not loud. Just final. "We're leaving."

"She's a child—"

"She's a *mouthpiece*, Ser Kael, and whatever speaks through her has been underground a very long time, and I do not take dictation from things under mountains." Rafael was already walking, fast, the retreat of a person who needed distance more than dignity. "We came for a guide and supplies. We have neither. We leave at dawn."

Dax, to his credit, did not move either. He crouched down by Wren, and Maren watched the smuggler — the man who priced everything, who had negotiated the price of his own loyalty with the shameless openness of a fishmonger — study the girl's face with something that had no price tag on it anywhere.

"Short by one," Dax said. "That's what it says? Every time?"

"Every time." Wren leaned toward him confidingly. "Your song's got a hole in it too. Right in the middle. Something got took out." She patted his arm with sticky bread fingers. "Mine's got a hole too. Everybody's got holes. The mountain can hear the holes."

Dax stood up slowly. He looked at Maren, and for once there was no calculation in his face at all, only a raw and unwelcome recognition. "My brother," he said, barely audible. "Winter crossing, eight years back. The mountain took him." His jaw worked. "It never gave him back."

* * *

They did not leave at dawn.

Maren found Rafael at first light sitting on a rock above the camp, not looking at the dead sky, which for Rafael was a considerable concession. She sat down without asking. Twenty years of soldiering had taught her that silence offered beside a proud person was worth ten times anything you could say to them.

"I refused you at my own door," Rafael said finally. "Do you remember that? Two days ago. You came to my gate with the truth and I told you to get off my mountain."

"I remember."

"Why?" Rafael's voice was rough. "Why did I refuse? I want to hear you say it, because I've been turning it over all night and I can't find the shape of it anymore."

Maren considered. Below them the camp was waking — smoke rising from a thousand fires into a sky that received it without comment, blindfolded children shrieking happily at their impossible game, and somewhere among the tents a small grey-eyed girl waking alone under a blanket that smelled of someone else's family.

"Because the truth cost me everything," Maren said, "and you could see it, and you wanted no part of a debt that large. Because the crown broke me and you still served the crown, and my existence was an accusation. Because refusing me was easier." She shrugged. "All of it. Take your pick. I've picked each one myself on different nights."

"And now?"

"Now there's a child down there who hears our holes." Maren looked at her hands, scarred from twenty years of swordwork, empty of sword. "I spent my whole oath believing the crown held the darkness back. Wards, borders, the king's peace — I thought it was a wall. It's not a wall, Rafael. It's a ledger. And we're entries in it. And something down there is counting, and coming up short, and I will not spend the rest of my life pretending I didn't hear that."

Rafael was quiet a long time. The hum rose through the rock beneath them, patient as tide.

"If we take her," Rafael said at last, "she walks in the middle. Never the rear. And if the mountain speaks through her again, we listen — but *we* decide. Not it. Us." The gate-keeper stood, and something in their face had shifted, some door unbarred by inches. "Go wake Ferrow. Tell him we're buying supplies and he's spending his own coin, because I'm not paying a blood debt *and* a grocer's bill."

Maren allowed herself the first smile in two days. "Yes, gate-keeper."

"And Kael." Rafael didn't turn around. "The child stays with us until we find her mother. After. If there's an after. That's not negotiable and it's not strategy. It's just right, and I'm tired of mistaking the easy thing for the right one."

* * *

Wren took the news the way she took everything — as fact, absorbed without ceremony. She fell in beside them at the camp's edge as they assembled packs, carrying a bundle that contained, as far as Maren could tell, a spare sock, a smooth stone, and half the stolen loaf.

"You're going down," Wren said. Not a question.

"We are," Maren said.

"To give the mountain its missing one."

Maren exchanged a glance with Rafael, who shook their head fractionally: *don't confirm, don't deny.* "We're going to find out what it wants."

"It wants a keeper." Wren said it lightly, terribly, adjusting her bundle in her arms. "But it's got the keeper backwards. It thinks the gate belongs to the mountain." She looked up at Rafael with those pale eyes, and smiled, gap-toothed and sweet, and Maren felt the smile land like ice water. "It's going to be so surprised."

Then she turned and led them toward the tunnels, humming a song no one had taught her, and the mountain hummed it back.
