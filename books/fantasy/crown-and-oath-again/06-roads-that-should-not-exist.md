## 6. Roads That Should Not Exist

Dax Ferrow had a rule about tunnels: you never trust one that's been quiet too long.

A tunnel that hummed, that dripped, that groaned with settling stone — that was a tunnel being honest with you. But the passage below Thornfell had gone silent three hours past, and silence in the deep dark meant something was listening.

He held up a fist and the column behind him stopped. Four shadows and a lantern turned down low, shuttered to a coin of amber on the wet stone. Ahead of him, Rafael Eriksen stood with their hand flat against the tunnel wall like they were taking its pulse.

"Problem?" Ser Maren Kael asked, her voice pitched for his ears alone. The knight still moved like a soldier even stripped of her oath — weight forward, hand never far from her sword hilt, eyes doing that thing soldiers did where they counted exits without meaning to.

"Tunnel's wrong," Dax said.

"It's a tunnel."

"It's *my* tunnel." He kept his voice flat, but the words came out with an edge he hadn't meant to sharpen. "I've run this vein eleven years. The Ferrow route. Three generations of Ferrows before me. My grandmother carved her initials into a support strut at the second junction because she said any road worth walking should know who's walking it." He jerked his chin down the passage. "There's no junction ahead. There should be a junction. There's nothing ahead but rock."

Rafael hadn't turned around. They stood very still, palm against the stone, and when they finally spoke their voice had the careful evenness of someone holding a door shut against a gale. "The wall is warm."

Dax crossed to it and pressed his own hand flat. He'd expected cold — deep rock was always cold, cold enough to ache through gloves, cold enough that smugglers learned to sleep in shifts huddled against each other like dogs. But the stone under his palm was warm as a sleeping animal. Warm as skin.

He took his hand back fast.

"That's not right," he said, and hated how young his voice sounded.

* * *

They'd come down through the Hollow Stair two days after the stars went out, the four of them plus Wren tucked into Maren's cloak like a knife in a sheath. Dax had argued against the descent — loudly, at length, with profanity — and then done it anyway, because that was the shape of his life now. A blood debt didn't care about your opinions. It just sat on your chest at night, patient as a creditor, until you paid or died owing.

The debt was this: two winters back, Rafael Eriksen had pulled him out of a whiteout on the Saddle Pass with frostbite eating his fingers and his crew scattered behind him, and had carried — *carried*, this gate-keeper with their scholar's hands — carried him six miles down to the treeline while the mountain tried to bury them both. And when Dax had mumbled something about paying them back, Rafael had looked at him with those gray, tired eyes and said, "One day I'll ask. That's all."

He'd thought about running. He was good at running; it was practically his profession. But you didn't survive eleven years in the Thornfell trade by breaking faith with the only people who'd ever dragged you out of a snowdrift. Word traveled. And besides — and this was the part that chafed, the part he'd never say aloud — besides, he *liked* them. The stubborn gate-keeper. The disgraced knight with the honest mouth. Even the little ghost-girl who hummed songs that made his teeth itch.

So here he was, guiding the only people in the world foolish enough to walk into the Undercourt on purpose, down roads he'd walked a hundred times that were no longer there.

"The map," Maren said now, in the warm-walled dark. "Rafael. Your map."

Rafael unrolled it by lantern light, and Dax watched their face do something he'd never seen it do. Rafael Eriksen knew the mountains the way priests knew scripture — every pass, every goat track, every smuggler's crawl from here to the Salt Marches. Dax had once heard them recite, from memory, the elevation change of forty-one miles of trail, correcting themselves twice and apologizing for both errors as though geography were a friend they'd failed. Their maps were famous in certain disreputable circles. People had offered real money for copies.

Now Rafael stared at their own inked lines like a man reading a letter from someone dead.

"The Third Junction should be here." Their finger rested on a crossing of passages marked in their own cramped, precise hand. "It's always been here. The junction sits above a fault line — you can feel the cold coming off it in summer. I mapped it when I was nineteen. I've checked it since, four times over twenty years, because—" A breath. "Because my brother and I used to play at the junction when we were children, before I understood what the gate work would cost us. I wanted to be sure I remembered it exactly."

Nobody said anything to that. Nobody knew how. The brother was a door in Rafael that everyone in the party had learned to walk around quietly.

"And now?" Maren asked gently.

"Now there is no junction." Rafael rolled the map closed with hands that weren't quite steady. "There's a wall where the west passage opens. Smooth rock. No tool marks, no collapse rubble. As if the passage never existed at all."

"As if," Dax muttered, "the mountain swallowed it whole and licked the plate clean."

Wren made a small sound. She'd been quiet for hours, which worried Dax more than the warm walls did — the girl was usually a constant low murmur of humming, half-tunes that wormed into your skull and stayed there like burrs. Now she stood clutching Maren's sleeve, her head tipped back, staring up at the tunnel ceiling as if reading something written there.

"She's counting," Maren said softly. "She does that when the mountain's loud."

"Loud?" Dax looked around at the perfect silence. "Kid, I hate to break it to you, but—"

"*Under,*" Wren said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like a child repeating a lesson. "It's louder under. We're walking inside its ear."

Dax decided, firmly, not to think about that.

* * *

They walked on because walking on was the only direction left. Dax led, which was a joke now, a title without a job — but he refused to give up the front position, because if the mountain wanted to eat somebody it could start with the professional criminal. Behind him came Rafael, map furled and useless, and Dax kept glancing back to see how they were bearing it.

Not well, was the answer. Not well at all.

He understood it more than he wanted to. Take away Dax's routes and you'd have taken away everything he was. Eleven years of knowing the mountain better than the crown's own surveyors, of slipping contraband through passes the king's men couldn't find on a clear day with a guide and a prayer — that knowledge was his armor, his income, his pride. Strip it and what remained? A man with a lantern, standing in a hole in the ground.

That was what Rafael looked like now. A woman — person, he corrected himself, Rafael had corrected him once, flatly, and he'd filed it away with all the other things you didn't joke about around gate-keepers — a person with a lantern and no map, walking roads that shouldn't exist through a mountain that had stopped being geography.

"I'm sorry," Rafael said quietly, at some nameless bend. "This is my failing. I promised you safe passage and I can't—"

"Stop that." Dax didn't turn around. "You don't get to apologize for geology."

"Dax—"

"You've spent twenty years learning these rocks like a prayer book. That's not a failure, that's a *life*, and the fact that the mountain's gone senile doesn't make your life worth less." He heard his own voice rise and forced it down. "Besides. You're not useless. You're the only one of us who noticed the wards ran on a soul. I just know where the holes are. You know why they're there. Between us we might stumble into the Undercourt before something eats us."

Behind him, silence, and then, so soft he almost missed it: "Thank you."

"Don't make it weird."

That got him a sound that was almost a laugh, and he counted it a victory, and hated how much he needed the small victories now.

* * *

The tunnel began to change an hour later, and the changes were worse than absence.

First came the carvings. Smuggler tunnels accumulated marks the way taverns accumulated debts — initials, arrows, tally-marks, warnings, the occasional crude drawing that proved smugglers and soldiers were equally idiots. Dax had read these walls his whole career. Now the marks were wrong. His grandmother's initials appeared on a support strut that was in the right place and the wrong century — the stone around them unweathered, fresh-cut, as if she'd carved them yesterday instead of sixty years ago. Farther on, a warning arrow he himself had scratched into the rock three winters ago pointed the opposite way than he'd pointed it.

"Someone's copied our marks," Maren said.

"No one's copied anything." Dax touched his own arrow, feeling the groove of it. Same depth. Same hand. His hand. "Either we've walked backward through time, or the mountain remembers us better than we remember ourselves."

"Or," Rafael said slowly, "it's making them."

They all looked at each other in the lantern light. Nobody finished the thought. Some thoughts you left unfinished, like doors you didn't open.

Then the passages started breathing.

There was no other word for it. The air moved in long slow cycles — out along the corridor, a pause, then back again — and with each cycle came a smell that didn't belong underground: rain, pine, woodsmoke, the green smell of high summer meadows. Surface smells. Memory smells. Dax's chest went tight, because he knew that exact mixture of scents. It was the air outside his mother's house in the valley town he'd run away from at fourteen and never gone back to.

"This is a trap," Maren said. Her hand was on her sword now, properly. "Lures. The court below uses lures — the old stories all say it. Souls wander in following the smell of home."

"It's not a lure." Wren's voice, small and certain. Everyone turned. The girl had let go of Maren's sleeve and was standing in the middle of the passage with her face lifted like a sunflower finding the sun. "It's dreaming out loud. It smells what it misses." She frowned, concentrating. "It misses *up*. It misses sky. It hasn't had sky since—" Her brow furrowed harder. "Since it gave its sky away. To buy something."

"Buy what?" Rafael crouched to her level, gentle as a person approaching a spooked animal.

But Wren's face went slack and distant, the humming rising in her throat, and whatever answer lived behind her eyes stayed locked in the mountain's dream.

* * *

They camped in a chamber that Dax did not recognize and that terrified him more than all the rest combined, because in eleven years there had been no chamber here. The space was round, smooth-floored, sized for six or eight people comfortably — comfortable being a relative term, given that the walls pulsed faintly warm and the ceiling glimmered with veins of pale mineral light like frost trapped in stone.

"We shouldn't stop," Maren said, though she was already easing Wren down onto her bedroll. The girl was asleep before she finished lying down, mid-hum, like a lamp pinched out.

"We shouldn't keep walking either," Dax said. He set the lantern between them and sat with his back to the warm wall, knees up, arms draped over them. "Tired people die stupid in the deep dark. Rule two."

"What's rule one?"

"Never trust a quiet tunnel."

Maren snorted despite herself. Across the chamber, Rafael sat with the map spread open on the floor, staring at it, and Dax watched them trace their own ink lines with one fingertip over and over, like a mourner touching a grave marker.

"It's hiding something," Rafael said finally. Not to anyone. To the map. "All of this. The rearranged roads, the false junctions, the memories in the walls. It's not chaos. Chaos doesn't carve your grandmother's initials fresh. This is deliberate. It's steering us."

"Away from something," Maren said.

"Or toward." Rafael looked up, and the frostlight caught their face and made them look older, carved, like something out of the mountain itself. "My brother's soul is down there. Sold to the Undercourt twenty years ago, feeding the wards from beneath. If the mountain knows we're coming — if it's been dreaming about us, about Wren hearing it — then maybe it's not hiding its secrets. Maybe it's clearing the path."

"To its heart?" Dax asked.

"To its ledger." Rafael folded the map with exaggerated care, the way you handled a thing that had stopped meaning what it meant. "And a ledger is the last place in any world you want to arrive uninvited."

Dax lay back against the warm stone and stared at the frostlit ceiling and did not sleep. Around him the others drifted down by degrees — Maren last, sitting upright with her sword across her knees until exhaustion won, because soldiers never trusted a ceiling either.

Sometime in the black hours, Wren woke screaming.

Not crying — screaming, a raw tearing sound that bounced off the round walls and came back doubled. Dax was up with his knife out before his mind caught up, Maren beside him with her blade drawn, Rafael kneeling by the girl. Wren thrashed and clawed at her own ears, and through the noise Dax realized the humming hadn't stopped. It was coming from the walls now. From everywhere. The mountain's tune, the one she carried, rising through the stone like water through cloth.

"Wren. Wren, wake up, you're dreaming—"

Her eyes opened and fixed on Rafael, and what was in them wasn't a child's fear. It was older and much, much larger.

"He's counting wrong," she gasped. "The keeper under the mountain. He counts and counts and the number won't close, and now he's felt *you* coming, Rafael Eriksen, and he's—" She gulped air. "He's opened the road. He wants you to hurry."

The silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

Dax looked at the others. Maren's knuckles were white on her sword. Rafael had gone the color of old paper, and their lips moved around a word they couldn't quite say — a name, maybe, worn smooth by twenty years of grief.

"Well," Dax said into the ringing dark, because someone had to, and humor was the only weapon he'd ever owned that worked, "good news is we're not lost anymore."

He picked up the lantern and looked down the corridor beyond the chamber, where the tunnel ran straight and true and freshly lit with veins of frost-glow, all the way into the deep.

"Bad news is, something down there just sent us an invitation."
