## 7. The First Toll

The tunnel ended at a mouth.

That was the only word Rafael could find for it. The smuggler passage they had followed for six hours — Dax's beloved "Old Cut," which he swore had run straight to the deep markets for thirty years — opened without warning into a cavern so vast that the lamplight died trying to reach its walls. And spanning that darkness, from where they stood to a ledge somewhere impossibly far below and across, grew a bridge of roots.

They were pale as peeled bone, those roots, thick as a man's waist at their crown and narrowing as they descended in long, swooping arcs, braided together like the fingers of praying hands. They came down out of the ceiling of the cavern — out of stone that should not have been able to grow anything — and plunged into the dark beneath the far ledge. Somewhere along their length, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"Tell me that's on your map," Maren said quietly.

Rafael didn't answer. They were still holding the map. They had been holding it since the third hour, when the passage had bent east where it should have gone west, and Dax had said, too casually, *tunnels do that now,* and Rafael had felt something shift inside them like a keel leaving water. Twenty-two years of gate-keeping. Twenty-two years of knowing every crack in Thornfell the way a priest knows his liturgy. And the mountain had simply — moved.

"No," they said finally. "It's not on my map."

"It wasn't on mine either." Dax crouched at the tunnel mouth, one hand flat against the floor as if he could read tremors through his palm. His crew had stayed behind at the last fork, guarding the supplies with the two lanterns they could spare; he had insisted on coming this far himself, though whether from loyalty or distrust of all of them equally, Rafael couldn't say. "And I don't like what that means. Things grow down here when the mountain wants them to. Things grow *for* a purpose."

Wren stepped past them all.

The child had been silent for hours — not frightened-silent but listening-silent, head tipped, humming under her breath in fragments that rose and fell like breath itself. Now she stopped humming and looked up at the bridge of roots with an expression Rafael had seen once before, years ago, on the face of a man reading his own death warrant: recognition, and a terrible kind of acceptance.

"She's pretty," Wren said.

"The bridge?" Maren asked.

"The *lady* in the bridge." Wren pointed at the thickest root, near its crown, where the pale wood swelled into something almost like a face — knots for closed eyes, a long seam for a mouth. "She's sleeping but she's working. She takes things. That's her job. She's sad about it but she does it anyway." The child turned, and her eyes found Rafael's with an accuracy that made their skin prickle. "She's going to ask you for something. Not me. Not Dax-with-the-bad-teeth. You or the knight-lady. She knows why you're here."

Dax muttered something unrepeatable and made a sign against evil that his grandmother had taught him, which was itself probably illegal in three jurisdictions.

Rafael stepped forward.

They told themselves it was duty. It always was; that was the trick they'd played on themselves for two decades, dressing every hunger and every grief in the gray uniform of obligation. But underneath the duty there was this: the ward-signature burned into their memory, the soul-mark they'd read in the collapse of the wards, the mark that belonged to Tomas Eriksen, dead twenty years and apparently not dead at all but *sold*, and every step deeper into the mountain was a step toward the ledger where his name must be written. Rafael would have crossed a bridge of knives. A bridge of roots that wanted payment was nearly restful by comparison.

The first root took their weight with a groan like an old ship. It was warm. That was the worst part — warmer than stone had any right to be, faintly pulsing, and beneath Rafael's boots they could feel something moving in the wood, slow as sap, patient as debt.

Maren went second. She moved like what she was even stripped of the rank — weight balanced, eyes moving, hand never far from the hilt of the sword she no longer had the right to carry but refused to abandon. Dax came last, walking the bridge the way he did everything, sideways and complaining.

Halfway across, the lady woke up.

The seam of her mouth opened. Her knotted eyes remained shut, which was somehow worse than if they hadn't. And when she spoke, the voice did not come from the root-face alone but from the whole bridge, from the whole cavern, resonating up through Rafael's bones through the soles of their feet.

***Toll.***

The word hung in the dark. Water dripped. Nobody moved.

"I don't have coin," Dax said. "I want that noted for posterity. I am famously poor."

***Not coin.*** The voice was vast and weary and, beneath it all, not unkind. ***Coin is for markets. The bridge takes memory. One crossing, one memory, freely given and truly held. The mountain keeps them. The mountain forgets nothing.*** A pause, and then, with what Rafael would swear was gentleness: ***The child has paid already. In sleep. Children's memories are small and sweet, like berries.***

"What did she pay?" Maren's voice had gone sharp.

***A name-day. A dog called Button. The smell of bread. Small things. She gave them laughing.*** The great mouth curved, or seemed to. ***Now. Who crosses? All who cross must pay.***

Rafael opened their mouth — and found they had been readying the memory all along, the way a man readies coin before he reaches a tollbooth. There was one memory they could afford to lose and knew it, and hated themselves for knowing it: the morning after the funeral, twenty years ago, when they had gone back to the gate because the relief keeper was drunk and someone had to light the lamps. They could give that. They would still remember Tomas alive. They would keep the important grief and lose only the furniture around it.

But Maren stepped forward first.

"I'll cross twice," she said. "I'm carrying the child over. So — two memories."

"Maren—" Rafael started.

"You'll want to save yours," she said, without looking at them. "I've watched you since the gate. You're rationing yourself like a siege town. Don't argue with a knight about logistics."

"You're not a knight."

"No." Something moved through her face, quick and cold as a fish under ice. "No, I'm not. Which is rather the point." She turned back to the root-lady and squared her shoulders the way soldiers do before a wall of arrows. "Ask me. I'll know the memory when you name it."

***The bridge does not choose,*** said the lady. ***The payer chooses, and the bridge weighs. A memory given lightly is a counterfeit. It must cost.*** The knotted eyes did not open, but Rafael felt attention settle on Maren like a hand. ***Choose what undid you.***

The silence stretched. Dax had gone very still, all his flippancy folded away. Even the drip of water seemed to hold itself.

Maren Kael looked at the memory the way Rafael imagined a woman looks at a wound she has kept bandaged for years, knowing the bandage is the only thing holding her together.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I was squire to Ser Aldous Vane, in the king's own retinue. The war with Harrowmere — the war they call the Winter War, the one that lasted eleven weeks — I was there at the signing of the peace. I carried the treaty chest." Her voice was steady. Too steady, the way ice is steady until it isn't. "After the signing, I was sent to fetch the king's countersignature from his field tent, and the flap was half-open, and I saw him counting. Not coin. Souls. Little lights in glass jars, each one labeled with a village name, and a man in gray robes — deep-court robes, I didn't know it then — taking delivery. Payment for victory. Eleven weeks, they said. No army wins a war in eleven weeks. Not honestly."

The bridge hummed. The roots beneath their feet pulsed once, slow, like a heart turning over in its sleep.

"I told the truth about it," Maren went on, and now her voice began to shake, and she let it, deliberately, the way a person opens a fist finger by finger. "At my investiture, before the assembled court, when they asked me to swear my oath by the crown's honor. I said I could not swear it, because I had seen the crown purchase souls to win a war, and I named the villages. Corren's Hollow. Ashmill. The ferry towns on the Sere. Forty-one names. I was twenty years old and I thought truth was a sword and I had just swung it in a throne room." She laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "They didn't execute me. Execution would have been mercy and confirmation both. They stripped my oath, struck my name from the rolls, and buried the record of my knighthood so thoroughly that within a year even I doubted I'd ever worn the white. That's the memory. Take it. Take all of it — the tent, the jars, the faces of the court, the sound my life made when it broke. It's the truest thing I own."

***Given freely?***

"Given *gladly*. It's been sitting in my chest for eleven years like a swallowed stone. If your mountain wants to carry it for a while, it's welcome."

The root-lady's mouth opened wide.

Rafael felt it happen more than saw it — a pressure, a drawing-out, like heat pulled from a fevered brow. Maren swayed. Dax caught her arm without seeming to decide to. And then it was done, and Maren stood blinking in the lamplight with an expression Rafael had never seen on her face: emptiness, but a clean emptiness, like a room after the furniture has been carried out.

"It's gone," she whispered. "I remember that it happened. I remember the shape of it. But I can't — the tent, the jars, I can't see them anymore. They're just words now." She pressed a hand flat to her sternum. "Gods. Is this what forgetting feels like? It's enormous. It's like setting down a pack you forgot you were wearing."

***The memory is kept,*** said the bridge, and there was satisfaction in it, deep and slow. ***And now the other payer. Gate-keeper.***

Rafael's turn. They stepped forward, ready to give the morning-after memory, the lamps, the drunk relief keeper, the affordable grief—

But the bridge was already in their head.

***Ah,*** said the lady, softly, terribly. ***You brought a counterfeit.***

"It's a real memory—"

***It is real and it is cheap and you chose it because it is cheap.*** The voice did not grow angry. It grew *sad*, which was infinitely worse. ***The bridge weighs, gate-keeper. Shall I tell you what I see? You carry a name like a coal in your cupped hands. Tomas. You carry twenty years of keeping a gate in his honor because no one told you the gate ate him. You carry the night the stars went out and you read his mark in the falling wards and you thought: then he is not gone, then there is something left in the world of him, then I can bring him home. That is what you would pay. Not the lamps. Him.***

"Then take it," Rafael said, and their voice cracked down the middle. "Take the grief. Take all of it. I'll walk across with nothing and be lighter for it—"

***No.***

The refusal landed like a dropped beam.

***Grief freely surrendered is not payment; it is escape, and the mountain does not deal in escapes.*** The great mouth softened, or seemed to. ***Listen, keeper. I will tell you what the knight's memory bought, because she cannot hear it anymore and someone must. The crown sold forty-one villages' worth of souls to the Undercourt to end a war that the Undercourt itself had arranged — a war seeded, fed, and harvested, so that desperate kings would come looking to buy. Your crown did not stumble into wickedness. It shopped for it. And the ledger of that purchase sits below us, in the court of bone and lamplight, and in it, among forty-one lines and ten thousand more, is written the name Eriksen.***

The world went very quiet. Even the dripping water seemed to stop.

"How do you know that?" Maren breathed. "How does the *bridge* know?"

***Because the mountain heard the transaction. The mountain hears everything sold beneath its skin. It has been dreaming of the debt for twenty years, and lately its dreams have grown teeth.*** The root-lady's knotted eyes shifted — Rafael would have sworn it — toward Wren, who stood at the bridge's crown watching them all with the calm of someone who had known this speech before anyone spoke it. ***Cross, keeper. Keep your grief. You will need it. Grief is the only fuel that burns hot enough to melt a ledger.***

"And my toll?" Rafael asked. "What do I pay?"

***You will pay it on the way back,*** said the bridge, and the mouth began to close. ***All debts deferred gather interest. Cross quickly. Cross kindly. And when you stand before the Ledger-Keeper, remember what the knight gave me freely: that the truth was spoken once, by one young fool with a sword, and it changed nothing. This time, bring more than truth.***

The roots stilled beneath their feet. The lady slept.

They crossed the rest of the way in silence. On the far ledge, Maren stood apart, testing her own mind like a tongue probing a missing tooth, and when Rafael reached her she said, quietly, "I meant to keep that. I built a whole self around it. And now I can't feel it, and I don't know if I'm free or hollowed."

"You're free," Rafael said. "Hollowed comes later, maybe. Or maybe it doesn't." They looked back at the bridge, at the sleeping face in the wood, and the rage that had been coiling in their chest since the night the stars went out finally found its shape. Not a shape of despair. A shape of *purpose*. Forty-one villages. Ten thousand names. Tomas written in a line among them like a price paid for peace that was never peace at all. The crown had sold its people and called it victory, and history had filed it under *eleven short weeks*, and no one had ever balanced the account.

Someone was going to balance the account.

Below them, the tunnel continued downward, and from its depths came a light that was not lamplight — gold and cold and rhythmic, pulsing like the heart of some enormous patient thing. And rising up out of the dark, faint but unmistakable, Wren began to hum again — the mountain's song, the counting-song — except that now, for the first time, Rafael could hear where it was going.

It wasn't counting anymore.

It was calling names.

* * *

They walked down toward the light, four shadows and a debt, and behind them the bridge of roots drew itself up slowly into the stone, folding away like hands closing over what they'd been given — keeping, the way everything in this mountain kept. Keeping memories. Keeping souls. Keeping count.

Rafael touched the hilt of the lamp they carried and did not look back.

Somewhere below, in a cathedral of bone, a Ledger-Keeper waited with a book that could not be edited, only balanced.

Rafael had begun, quietly and completely, to plan the balancing.
