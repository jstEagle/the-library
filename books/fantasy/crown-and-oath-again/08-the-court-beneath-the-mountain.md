## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The dark changed before the light did.

Maren felt it first in her teeth — a pressure, like biting down on a coin, like the taste of old metal and older law. The tunnel they had followed for two days out of Dax's rearranged labyrinth had been narrowing by degrees, walls sweating with mineral sheen, roots giving way to something smoother, something *worked*. And then the air itself shifted, and Maren knew they were no longer in the mountain.

They were in its house.

"Stop," Wren said, and her voice carried strangely, as if the tunnel had grown ears just for her. The child stood with her head cocked, listening to something beneath hearing. "It knows we're coming. It's setting out cups."

"That's comforting," Dax muttered behind her. He had his knife out, though Maren had told him twice that steel would mean nothing down here. He kept it out anyway. She understood. A blade was a kind of prayer, even when it couldn't be answered.

Rafael said nothing. They hadn't said much since the bridge of roots took their toll — since Maren had fed it the truth of her knighthood and watched Rafael's face change as the memory poured out of her: the war records, the requisitions marked in the king's own hand, the souls sold south beneath the mountains to buy a victory the histories never named. Since then Rafael had walked like someone carrying a load they were afraid to set down, because setting it down might mean looking at it.

Maren knew that walk. She'd worn it for six years.

The tunnel opened without warning.

One moment there was stone; the next, space — vast, vertical, impossible space — and Maren's breath went out of her in a sound she was not proud of.

It was a cathedral. There was no other word for it, though no god she'd ever sworn to would have claimed it. Pillars rose from darkness into darkness, each one carved from what could only be bone — femurs the size of oaks, fused and polished, veined with seams of amber light. Between them hung lamps: thousands upon thousands of them, small glass globes on chains of black iron, each burning with a flame that gave light but no heat. The lamplight fell in soft gold pools across a floor of pale stone, and in those pools moved figures.

People. Hundreds of them. Serving.

They carried trays of food Maren couldn't identify toward tables where tall figures sat robed in colors that hurt to look at — deep violet, bruise-red, a green like pond water over drowned things. The gentry of the deep, if the stories held. They were beautiful the way frost on a corpse is beautiful, their faces too symmetrical, their eyes catching the lamplight and holding it a half-second too long.

And the servants — Maren looked at them properly, and wished she hadn't.

They moved efficiently. Gracefully, even. But there was nothing behind their eyes. Not emptiness exactly — worse. *Patience.* The look of a house waiting for someone to come home who never will. Their faces were smooth with a terrible mildness, and when one of them passed close enough to touch, Maren saw that each wore a collar of woven silver at the throat, and each collar bore a small stamped sigil, and every sigil was different.

Names, she realized. Or what was left of them.

"Don't stare," Dax breathed. "Rule one of any market, any court, any den: don't stare at what you can't afford."

"I can't afford any of this," Maren said.

"No," Dax agreed grimly. "But we're already inside."

Wren had gone very still. She was holding Rafael's hand — had taken it without asking, somewhere back in the tunnels, and Rafael had let her, which told Maren more about Rafael Eriksen than all their maps and gate-lore ever had. Now the child looked up at the lamps, at the bone pillars, at the soulless servants gliding between the tables, and hummed.

Three notes. Low, rising, falling.

A servant stopped.

It was a woman, or had been — gray-robed, middle-aged, her face wearing that awful patient smoothness. At the sound of Wren's humming her head turned, slow as a door swinging, and for one heartbeat something flickered behind her eyes. Recognition. Longing. Grief with nowhere left to live.

Then it was gone, and she continued on her way with her tray, and Maren's hand found her sword hilt without her permission.

"They hear it," Wren whispered. "The songs. They can't remember why, but they hear it." Her grip tightened on Rafael's hand. "The mountain counts them and comes up short, and they feel short. Like a coat with a button missing. Like a word on the tip of your tongue forever."

"Can you un-miss them?" Dax asked quietly. "The buttons?"

Wren shook her head. "Not from outside. You have to go where the ledger is."

"Then we go where the ledger is," Rafael said.

Their voice came out flat and steady, and Maren heard what was under it — the same thing that had been under it since the bridge, since the truth, since the ward-signature that matched a dead brother's soul-mark. Purpose sharpened past grief into something with an edge. Maren had served under officers with that edge. Most of them died badly. A few of them changed the world first.

* * *

Getting across the cathedral floor was Dax's work, and he did it well.

He steered them along the margins, through the shadow between lamp-pools, timing their movements to the gentry's long, distracted conversations — conversations conducted in a language like water running backward over stones. He taught them with gestures: walk when the tall ones laugh, freeze when the lamplight flickers, never look directly at a face. Once, a servant paused three feet away, tray balanced, eyes fixed on some middle distance where nothing lived, and they stood pressed against a bone pillar for the length of two hundred heartbeats while it decided, whatever part of it still decided things, to move on.

Maren used the time to study the collars. Old habit — assess the enemy's restraints. The silver was fine work, seamless, no clasp. Each sigil was struck deep, deliberate, the way a smith strikes a maker's mark. Property, branded at the level of the self.

She thought of the requisitions. *Forty souls, payment rendered, campaign of the northern spring.* She had read those lines aloud in the king's hall six years ago, expecting outrage, expecting horror. She had gotten silence instead, and then the stripping of her spurs, and then a cell, and then exile — because the crown had known. The crown had always known. This cathedral of bone was built on signatures her king had made.

Her rage, which had been banked coals for years, stirred and found new air.

"There," Rafael breathed.

Maren followed their gaze across the floor, past two pillars, to a side alcove near the base of the great dais where the gentry feasted. A stair descended there, half-hidden, guarded by nothing — guarded, Maren suspected, by law rather than steel, which down here was stronger. And beside the stair stood a servant unlike the others.

He was younger. His collar-sigil caught the lamplight as he turned, and even at this distance Maren could see it was worn nearly smooth, as if it had been handled, worried at, touched ten thousand times by fingers seeking an edge of it that didn't exist. He stood facing the stair with his hands folded, patient as all the rest.

But his head was tilted. Just slightly. Toward the distant ceiling, toward the stone overhead, toward — Maren understood with a lurch — the surface. The mountains. The passes.

Toward a gate.

"He's listening," Wren whispered. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word. "That's him. That's the song's beginning. That's—" She pressed her face against Rafael's arm. "That's your brother."

Rafael didn't move. Didn't speak. Maren watched them stand absolutely still, twenty years of grief and guilt and gate-keeping compressed into a body that had forgotten how to breathe, and she did the only thing she could think of: she stepped slightly closer, shoulder almost touching theirs, a knight's presence at a comrade's flank. *You are not alone on this wall,* the posture said. It was the oldest comfort she knew.

The echo — Tomas, it must be Tomas, though how could it be, Tomas had been dead and buried and mourned for two decades — the echo turned.

Slowly. The way the woman had turned to Wren's humming. But this time the thing that surfaced behind the smoothness of his face stayed longer. His eyes found Rafael across the vast floor, across the lamplight and the feasting dead-eyed gentry, and they *focused*, and Maren saw the focus cost him something, like a man hauling a drowning stranger out of deep water.

His lips moved. No sound came. But Maren read the shape of it easily enough; she had spent years reading lips through throne-room distances, watching for treason in the king's council.

*You came.*

Rafael's hand rose — a small motion, helpless, the gesture of someone reaching for a door that has been shut for twenty years.

And the echo smiled.

It was the worst thing Maren had seen in the Undercourt, worse than the bone pillars and the hollow servants and the gentry laughing their backward-water laughter. Because the smile was *his*. It was the smile of a boy on a gate-tower in summer, and it sat on the face of a collared slave in a cathedral of bone, and hope and horror arrived together in Maren's chest so completely entangled she could not have cut them apart with a sword.

Then a bell rang.

It came from everywhere — from the lamps, from the pillars, from the floor itself, a single low tone that made the bone sing in sympathy. Every servant in the cathedral stopped mid-step. Every gentry figure rose. And the echo's face smoothed over like water closing above a sinking man, and he folded his hands, and faced his stair, and was gone — not gone, but *filed away*, put back in his place with a precision that made Maren's stomach turn.

"The changing of some watch," Dax murmured. "Or the ringing of some register. Either way—"

"The Ledger-Keeper," Wren said.

All of them looked at her. The child was staring at the far end of the cathedral, where the dais ended in a great arch of interlocked vertebrae, and beyond it a corridor glowed with a colder, whiter light than the warm gold of the feast.

"He's waking up," Wren said. "The one who keeps the counting. He knows we're here now. He says—" her brow furrowed, translating something vast into something small, "—he says the books are open, and debtors come to him, and we should stop hiding among the cups."

Dax exhaled slowly. "I hate polite monsters."

"It's not politeness," Maren said. She hadn't meant to speak; the words came out of the place where her rage lived. "It's confidence. It thinks the outcome is already written." She looked at Rafael, at the ghost-smile still lingering somewhere behind their eyes. "So do I. Just not the outcome it does."

Rafael turned from the alcove, from the stair, from the brother who had said *you came* with a dead boy's smile, and when they spoke their voice was the gate-keeper's voice, the one that had held a pass alone for twenty years.

"Then we don't keep it waiting."

They crossed the last of the floor together — knight, smuggler, child, keeper — four shadows moving through lamplight toward the white corridor and the cold arithmetic waiting at the end of it. Behind them, in his alcove by the stair, the echo of Tomas Eriksen tilted his head once more toward the distant sky, listening for a gate.

Ahead, the ledger's pages were already turning.
