## 9. Ledger of the Deep

The doors of the Undercourt did not open so much as admit them, the way a wound admits a blade — reluctantly, and with the promise of pain.

Rafael had expected grandeur. What they got was arithmetic.

Beyond the threshold stretched a hall built of vertebrae, each one the size of a chapel arch, stacked in ribs that rose until the lamplight failed and only the suggestion of more remained above. Lamps burned everywhere — small, patient flames cupped in iron hands bolted to the bone, each flame a different color. Some burned white as starlight. Some burned the sickly amber of old tallow. One, near the far end of the hall, burned green as foxfire, and Rafael's eyes kept snagging on it the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

The floor was not stone. It was ledger paper, or something like it — vast sheets of it, pale as skin, laid over the bone and seamed together with lines of ink that moved when Rafael wasn't looking directly at them. Every footstep landed softly, and every footstep left a mark. Rafael looked back after ten paces and saw their own passage written behind them in thin, exact script: *RAFAEL ERIKSEN, GATE-KEEPER, THORNFELL. ARRIVED UNDER LAMPLIGHT THE THIRD. ALONE? NO.*

*No,* indeed. Maren walked at their left shoulder, hand resting on the pommel of an oathless sword she no longer had the right to draw. Dax brought up the rear, and Rafael could hear him cataloguing exits under his breath — a smuggler's habit, a survivor's catechism: *window, drain, dark, dark, window.* And Wren walked in the middle of them all, holding Maren's hand with one of hers and a strip of dried apple in the other, humming under her breath, low and constant, like a kettle not quite boiling.

The soulless were here. Rafael had seen them first in the markets of the deep — men and women who moved with terrible efficiency and nothing else, faces smooth as unscribed paper, carrying trays, trimming lamps, sweeping floors that did not seem capable of dust. Here in the hall they worked in silence among the shelves. And there were *shelves*. They lined the walls from floor to unseen height, thousands of niches, and in each niche sat a small glass vessel, and in each vessel burned a flame.

Every color but one. Not one of them, Rafael realized with a lurch, burned the honest gold of a hearth-fire. Gold belonged to the living. Everything here had been taken out of the living.

"The court keeps its accounts in light," Maren said quietly. She had seen it before, or something like it. "I told you."

"You told me the crown sold souls." Rafael's voice came out flatter than they intended. Twenty years of gate-keeping had taught them to speak evenly into wind and sleet and the faces of desperate travelers; they drew on all of it now. "You didn't tell me they filed them."

At the end of the hall stood a desk.

It was absurdly ordinary — a writing desk of black wood, scarred at the corners, the kind any toll-house or customs station might own. Behind it sat the Ledger-Keeper, and Rafael understood immediately that the desk was not furniture. It was a *restraint*. The Keeper was tall past reason, robed in grey that drank the lamplight, and its face — when it lifted its head — was a smooth oval of pale skin with no features except two closed eyes drawn upon it in ink, delicate as illumination on a saint's book. As they approached, the drawn eyes opened. Ink pupils. Ink lashes. They blinked with a sound like a page turning.

"Four petitioners," said the Ledger-Keeper. Its voice was dry and even, the voice of a clerk reading totals aloud. "One knight, unsworn. One smuggler, indebted. One child, unrecorded." The ink eyes moved to Wren, and for the first time something in the dry voice *hesitated*, a fraction of a beat, like a pen lifting from paper. "Interesting. One gate-keeper." It folded its long hands upon the desk. "State your business."

"You know our business," Rafael said. "You've been reading our footsteps since the door."

"Footsteps record arrival. Petition records intent. The ledger does not assume." A pause. "The ledger never assumes. Assumption is error, and error is debt, and debt is my whole province. State your business."

Rafael set both hands flat on the desk. The wood was warm, faintly, like a body's. "Twenty years ago — thirty-one, if you count from the sale — a soul was taken from Thornfell and sold to this court. Tomas Eriksen. My brother. The border wards have been fed on him ever since, and last week they failed, because whatever bargain held him is breaking. I want him back."

The Ledger-Keeper listened without moving. When Rafael finished, it opened a drawer — the sound was enormous in the bone-hall, a drawer sliding through twenty years — and withdrew a single folio, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling.

"Tomas Eriksen," it read. "Second of his name in this ledger. First was a grandmother; her flame was discharged in full in the reign of the grandfather king. Tomas Eriksen, third child of four, age eleven years at time of conveyance. Sold by warrant of the Crown of Thornfell, sealed by the Lord Treasurer Aldous Crane, payment received: cessation of hostilities along the eastern marches, one war ended, duration guaranteed forty years." The ink eyes lifted. "The guarantee expires in nine days. You are correct. The account is failing."

The words went through Rafael like cold water through a coat. Eleven years old. They had known it, of course — had carried the number for two decades like a stone sewn into the lining of everything they wore — but hearing it read aloud in a clerk's voice, itemized between a grandmother and a war, made it new again, made it *worse*, made eleven years old a figure in a column rather than a boy who had once fallen out of an apple tree laughing.

"He was eleven," Rafael said. "And they paid for a war with him."

"They paid for the *end* of a war," the Ledger-Keeper corrected gently, terribly. "The distinction is material. This court does not make wars. This court balances them."

Behind Rafael, Maren's breathing changed. Rafael heard the knight swallow whatever she was about to say — chapter seven of her own confession, the truth that had stripped her oath — and file it away for later. Later. There was so much later now.

"What happens in nine days," Rafael asked, "when the guarantee ends?"

"The flame gutters. The wards fall entirely. The stars—" the Keeper tilted its featureless head, as though consulting something internal, "—remain extinguished. Indefinitely. The mountain holds the sky's account now, and the sky cannot pay. Your stars will stay dark until the debt is settled." It closed the folio. "You wish to settle it. That is why you are here. All of you. Even the child, though the child does not know yet what she is carrying."

Wren stopped humming.

It was such a small thing, in that vast bone hall. But Rafael felt it land like a dropped lantern. The girl looked up at the Keeper with her chin tucked, the way she looked at things that frightened her, and then she deliberately, defiantly, resumed her song — quieter, altered, some half-tone lower, as if answering a challenge in a language only she spoke. The Keeper watched her for a moment with its ink eyes, and then inclined its head a precise half-inch, and returned to Rafael.

"Very well. The terms of settlement."

"I'm listening."

"The debt is one soul, weight of Tomas Eriksen, eleven years, unspent, unlived, conveyed in good faith and consumed in service of the wards for twenty years. The ledger records the debt as *outstanding*." The Keeper turned the folio around and slid it across the desk, and Rafael made themselves look. There it was: a line of script that moved like something breathing, the name *Tomas Eriksen* written in letters that flickered faintly gold at their edges, and beside it, in red-brown ink that might once have been brighter, the word *OWED*.

"Return the soul," said the Keeper, "and the debt closes. The wards refill. The stars relight. Simple."

"Then give him back."

"That," said the Ledger-Keeper, "is where your simplicity ends." It folded its hands again. "The soul has been *consumed*, gate-keeper. Twenty years of ward-light. Do you understand what that means? A soul burned at the rate his was burned leaves residue, and residue is not a soul. What remains of Tomas Eriksen is an echo — a shape of memory wearing his weight, sufficient to feed the wards, insufficient to be a boy again. We can return you the echo. The echo will burn another twenty years, perhaps forty, and then you will stand in this hall again, and there will be less of him, and eventually there will be nothing, and the debt will reopen forever. Or—" the ink eyes did not blink, but somehow managed to convey patience anyway, the patience of arithmetic itself, "—the debt can be balanced rather than repaid."

"Explain the difference."

"A repayment returns what was taken. A balancing substitutes what is equivalent." The Keeper's drawn gaze swept across them, unhurried, weighing each of them visibly, and Rafael felt it like a hand passing over their chest, taking inventory. "One soul of equal weight, freely offered, entered into the ledger in place of the outstanding name. The wards accept substitution as readily as restoration. The stars do not care whose light burns. Light is light. The mountain is not sentimental."

"And if no one offers?"

"Then the account remains open, and the dark remains permanent, and in nine days the last of your brother gutters out regardless, and the Undercourt will pursue collection through other means." The Keeper said it without malice, without menace, which was somehow the worst part. There was no cruelty in that voice at all. There was only the column, and the total, and the next column. "Gate-keeper, I will tell you the three laws of my office, since you have come so far and deserve honesty, which is rare down here. One: the ledger cannot be edited. No entry may be struck. What is written is written, and I did not write it — I merely keep it. Two: the ledger cannot be cheated. Every attempt to falsify a weight has been tried in ten thousand years, by kings cleverer than yours, and every falsified entry comes due with interest. Three: the ledger can only be balanced. Something for something. Weight for weight. That is all justice is, beneath the mountain. That is all justice has ever been anywhere. The kingdoms simply lie about it more elaborately."

Rafael stared at the word *OWED* and felt the shape of the trap close around the whole journey — the tunnels, the tolls, the bailiffs, Wren's songs, all of it leading here, to a choice dressed up as mercy: give us a soul, any soul, and we will call the books even.

"You're asking us to choose which of us burns," Rafael said slowly.

"I am asking nothing. Asking implies desire. I am *informing* you of the terms." The Keeper spread its long hands over the desk, palms down, and the lamplight of ten thousand little souls bent toward it slightly, like grass toward wind. "But yes. In plain speech: the mountain requires a keeper-flame, and your brother's is nearly spent, and someone must take his place in the book, or everyone in your kingdom lives under a dead sky until the mountain chooses otherwise. Those are the terms. They are not cruel terms. They are *exact* terms. Cruelty would be pretending otherwise — cruelty is what crowns do with ledgers."

Maren stepped forward. Rafael knew that step; had heard it on the road a dozen times, the deliberate planting of feet that preceded an offer she'd already decided on. Rafael put out a hand without looking and caught her forearm. Her arm was rigid under their grip, sword-arm, oath-arm, and trembling very slightly, like a wire under load.

"Not yet," Rafael said.

"Rafael—"

"*Not yet.*"

Dax said nothing at all, which from Dax was a shout. He had gone very still near the door, and his eyes were moving along the shelves, niche by niche, flame by flame, and Rafael saw his lips move and understood he was counting. Counting the vessels. Counting, maybe, against something he knew — a crew, a debt, a winter crossing — looking for faces in the fire. He found none, or he found too many; either way his jaw set like mortar.

Rafael turned back to the Keeper. "Show me the rest of the page."

"There is no rest of the—"

"The folio," Rafael said. "You opened it to the debt. Ledgers have facing pages. Show me the credit side. Where did the payment go? Who received a soul's worth of value for my brother, and what did they do with it?" Their voice was still level, still the gate-keeper's voice, but underneath it something had begun to burn, and it was not the color of any lamp on those shelves. It was hearth-gold. "Because if the ledger cannot be edited, then it also cannot *lie*. And I want to read what actually happened. Every line. Every name."

For the first time, the Ledger-Keeper paused long enough that Rafael could measure the pause.

"That information exists," it said finally. "It is accurate. It is also — how shall I phrase this in terms you will accept — the page your crown spent a great deal to keep unwritten elsewhere." The ink eyes narrowed, just slightly, two strokes of a pen reconsidering themselves. "You should understand, gate-keeper, before you ask to see it: the ledger is honest, but honesty has consequences. There are entries in this book that, once read aloud in this hall, become *active*. Debts called. Obligations enforced. If I turn to the credit side of Tomas Eriksen's account, certain names in your world will find their own balances coming due. Quickly. Possibly tonight."

"Good," said Rafael.

The ink eyes opened fully wide.

"Turn the page," Rafael said. "My brother spent twenty years as a lamp-wick while men in clean robes signed warrants and called it peace. Whatever falls on them when your book tells the truth—" they leaned forward, and the gold feeling climbed up their throat and into their voice, "—can start falling right now."

Maren made a sound beside them, half warning, half prayer. Dax stopped counting flames. And Wren, small and steady in the middle of the bone cathedral, began to hum a new song — one Rafael hadn't heard from her before, older-sounding, deeper, a song with the weight of roots in it — and high above them, in the dark where the vertebrae gave out, something immense shifted in its sleep and began, very slowly, to listen.

The Ledger-Keeper regarded Rafael for a long moment. Then it drew the folio back across the desk, and its long fingers rested on the cover, and the drawn eyes blinked once, like a scale settling.

"So be it," it said. "But know what you ask. On the credit side of this page, gate-keeper, the name written largest — the name that profited most — belongs to a man who is currently sitting in a warm room in your capital city, drinking wine bought with my brother-client's life."

The fingers turned the page.

Somewhere far above, through leagues of stone and root and sleeping mountain, the first bell of Thornfell began to ring — though no hand had touched it.
