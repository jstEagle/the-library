## 10. Wren's Warning

I dream the mountain's dream and it is not a nice dream but it is a true one, and Maren says true things are like medicine, they taste bad and then they help.

In the dream I am small. Smaller than I am now, which is already very small, small enough that the grown-ups forget I am in the room, which is a kind of magic too, the bad kind. I am standing in the dark under everything and the dark is not empty. The dark is full of counting.

*One*, says the mountain. *Two. Three.*

It counts the way I count sheep on the ceiling when the camp is too loud, except it is not counting sheep. It is counting souls. I know that word now. I did not know it a month ago and now I know it the way you know a stone in your shoe.

*Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and twelve.*

The mountain's voice is not a voice. It is more like when you put your ear against the ground and hear the carts far away, except the carts are grief and they are very far away and also very near. It counts and counts and its counting goes up and up through the stone, up through the roots of it, up through the smuggler tunnels where Dax walks with his knife out, up through the lamplight markets where the soulless ones carry trays and do not blink, up through the bone cathedral where the Ledger-Keeper keeps his book, up and up until the counting comes out of the top of the mountain like smoke.

And then the counting stops.

*One short,* says the mountain. *One short, one short, one short.*

That is when I wake up, because even in a dream, when something says *one short* three times, your body knows to run.

* * *

In the morning — I say morning but the stars are still gone so it is only the lamp-time, the time when Dax lights the little lamp and pretends it is the sun — I tell them.

I tell them the way I tell them everything, which is all at once and out of order, because that is how knowing arrives in me. It does not come in lines like Maren's stories. It comes like water into a boot.

"The mountain is counting," I say. "It counted all night. It counted four hundred and twelve and then it stopped and it said one short."

Dax stops chewing. Dax is always chewing something, dried meat or a stick or the inside of his own cheek, and when he stops chewing it means the thing I said has gotten past his teeth and down into the place where he keeps the things he is afraid of. I have learned to watch for it. It is a good trick. Grown-ups' faces lie but their chewing does not.

"One short of what, sprout?" he says.

"Of what it had. Before. It had a number and the number was full and now the number is not full and it is very upset about it. Not angry-upset. The other kind. The kind where you check your pockets over and over."

Maren looks at Rafael. Rafael looks at the lamp. Rafael does that a lot now, looks at the lamp, like the lamp is going to say something helpful. Lamps never say anything helpful. I have checked.

"Four hundred and twelve," Rafael says, quiet, like they are reading it off a page inside their own head. "The Ledger-Keeper's book had — " and then they stop, and their face does the thing where it closes like a door.

"Say it," I say, because I am allowed to say that. Children are allowed to say things grown-ups only think. It is one of the few good things about being small.

"Four hundred and thirteen," Rafael says. "Including the one that was sold. Including Tam." Tam is the brother. Tam is the word Rafael carries in their chest the way I carry the mountain's humming in my teeth. "One soul left the ledger and went into the wards. The wards drank it. So the mountain is short by one, and it has been short for twenty years, and it only *noticed* when the wards broke."

"No," I say, and everyone looks at me, and I wish they would not because what I have to say next is the bad part and I would like to say it to the floor. "It noticed before. It noticed the whole time. It was counting the whole time. It just couldn't *do* anything, because — "

Because. And here is the worst part, the part I have been carrying since the dream, the part that is heavy like a bucket full of well-water, and I have to set it down or my arms will break, only it is not my arms, it is something in the middle of me.

"Because the gate was holding it down," I say. "The gate was a leash. The gate was never for keeping smugglers out or keeping the dark in. It was for keeping the mountain *asleep*. And the leash was tied with Tam's soul, like a knot. And now the knot is loose and the mountain is waking up and it is not waking up angry. It is waking up like someone who has been tied to a bed for a thousand years and is very, very tired, and tired things do not know their own strength."

Nobody says anything. The lamp hisses. Dax starts chewing again, fast, which is worse than not chewing.

"And," I say, because there is an *and*, there is always an *and*, the mountain's dreams are like onions, "it doesn't want the soul back."

Maren frowns. Maren's frowns are famous, I think, in whatever places knights come from. "The Ledger-Keeper said — "

"The Ledger-Keeper reads the book," I say. "The book is not the mountain. The book is a thing people made to sit on top of the mountain, like a lid. The mountain does not care about the book. The mountain does not care about debts." I wrap my arms around my knees. My knees are the only part of me that feels the right size. "It wants a keeper."

* * *

I should tell it in order, Maren says, when she is teaching me letters. First this, then that. But the mountain does not come to me in order, so you will have to have it the way I have it.

After I say the word *keeper* we walk. We always walk now. Walking is what we do instead of being safe. We go down through a tunnel where the walls have little lights in them like drowned stars, and I put my hand against the wall and the mountain tells me things through my hand, the way it tells me things through my teeth when it hums.

This is what it tells me through my hand:

It tells me about a time before the court. Before the Ledger-Keeper and his book, before the gentry with their collars and their lamplight, before the crown up top was even a crown, when it was just a family with sharp teeth and better luck than everyone else. Back then the mountain was awake all the time. It walked. Not like a bear walks. It walked the way a thought walks through a sleeping person, slow and huge, and where it stepped, the world bent a little, and the things that lived on it learned to bend too.

And the things that lived on it were afraid.

That is the whole story, really, if you want it short. Everything else is decoration. The things that lived on the mountain were afraid of it, so they made a leash. They could not tie a rope around a mountain, so they tied it around the *gate* — because the gate was the place where the mountain's dreaming came up to the surface, the soft spot, the fontanelle, Maren's word, the place on a baby's head that is not finished yet. And they fed the gate with souls, one after another, soul after soul after soul, and each soul was a knot, and the knots held the mountain's dreaming down under the stone where it could count and count and never be heard.

Four hundred and twelve souls. I counted with it. I know all their names now. They are in my head like birds in a rafters, and some nights they all shift at once and I cannot sleep for the wingbeats.

Tam is the newest knot. Tam is the last knot. And Tam is loose.

* * *

At the lamp-time meal — dried fish, hard bread, water that tastes like the inside of the mountain, which is to say like pennies and patience — I tell them the rest.

"It doesn't want Tam back," I say. "I know the Ledger-Keeper said the debt has to be paid and the soul has to come home. But the mountain doesn't want a soul in the wards. The wards are the leash. If you put Tam back in the wards, you tie the knot again. You put the mountain back to sleep for another thousand years and it counts and counts and nobody hears it." I look at Rafael. Looking at Rafael is hard because Rafael's face is like the gate, closed and keeping, but I am small and I am used to closed doors. "You'd be doing it again. What the crown did. You'd be feeding the leash."

Rafael's hands go still on the bread. Rafael's hands are the steadiest hands I have ever seen, gate-keeper's hands, hands that have turned the same key ten thousand times, and watching them go still is like watching a river stop.

"Then what does it want?" Rafael asks.

And I almost do not say it. I almost keep it behind my teeth where the humming lives. But Maren catches my eye and nods at me, small, like *go on, soldier*, even though I am not a soldier, I am a girl who hears things, and somehow that nod is enough.

"A keeper," I say. "It wants a keeper. Not a leash. A keeper. Someone to hold the gate not *shut* but *held*. Someone it chooses. Someone who chooses it back." I pull my knees up tighter. "That's why the wards broke, I think. That's why the stars went out. Not because the knot slipped. Because the mountain pulled. On purpose. It's been pulling for twenty years, gentle, gentle, like a horse testing a rope, and the night the stars went out was the night it finally pulled hard enough. It broke its own leash. It broke its own knot. It let Tam's soul go free into the wards so the wards would starve and fail, so the gate would open, so someone would come down."

"Someone," Maren says slowly.

"You," I say. "All of you. But mostly — " and here my voice does the thing where it gets small even though I am already small, "mostly Rafael. The mountain knows Rafael's name. It has known it for twenty years. It has been listening to Rafael walk the wall every night, ten thousand nights, up and down, up and down, checking the lamp, checking the wards, checking and checking, and the mountain thought — " I have to stop and breathe because the next part makes my eyes hot — "the mountain thought, *there. That one. That one knows how to keep things. That one has been keeping a gate with no one to keep it for. That one is keeping a brother who is dead. That one knows what keeping costs.*"

The lamp hisses. Somewhere far off in the tunnels, water drips in a rhythm almost like counting.

"It wants Rafael," I say. "And the court wants Rafael. And those are not the same thing and everyone down here will try to make them look the same, and that is the trap, that is the whole trap, and I dreamed it and I am telling you and nobody ever listens to me until it is too late, so please, please listen *now* — "

"We're listening, sprout." Dax's voice is gentle, which is strange, like a knife being gentle, but he means it. "We hear you."

"The court will offer Rafael a crown," I say. "I saw it in the dream. A crown of the deep, all dark iron and lamplight, and it will look like an ending and it will be a leash. Because a keeper that the court gives you is just a soul the mountain owns. But a keeper that the *mountain* gives you — " I shake my head. "That's different. That's the thing the old law is for. That's the thing underneath the book."

Maren leans forward, elbows on knees, the way she does when the truth is close and she is hunting it. "Wren. The old law. The one older than the Ledger. Tell me exactly what the mountain showed you."

So I close my eyes and I go back into the dream while I am awake, which I can do, which is my one magic besides being forgettable, and I say the words the mountain said, in its voice, the ground-voice, the cart-far-away voice:

*"A debt is a chain. An oath is a hand. A chain holds a prisoner. A hand holds a friend. Let the ledger keep its debts. I will keep my keeper, and my keeper will keep me, and no book shall stand between."*

When I open my eyes, Rafael has gone the color of the lamp-shade, and Dax has stopped chewing again, and Maren is very quietly saying the words back to herself like she is checking them for traps.

"That's not in any law I know," Maren says.

"It's in the law *under* the law," I say. "It's the first law. It's the law the mountain dreamed before anyone wrote anything down. And the court has spent a thousand years hoping nobody would say it out loud." I look at all of them, at the found-family-shaped thing we have become, the knight and the smuggler and the gate-keeper and the girl, and I am so tired of carrying the bucket. "The mountain is waking up. It will not go back to sleep. It will not take Tam back into the leash. And it has chosen its keeper, and the keeper does not know yet, and the court does not know yet, and when they both find out at the same time — "

The lamp gutters. All by itself, no wind down here, no draft, just the flame leaning hard to the left like it is trying to run.

" — something is going to break," I finish. "Something big. A ledger, maybe. Or a world."

Dax relights the lamp with hands that are almost steady. Almost. Gate-keeper's steady is a long way off.

"Then we'd better get to the court before it does," Maren says.

And under us, under everything, soft as a heartbeat, the mountain begins to hum — and this time, for the first time, I hear a word inside the humming, one word, over and over, patient as stone:

*Coming. Coming. Coming.*
