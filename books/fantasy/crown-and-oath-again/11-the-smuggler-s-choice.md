## 11. The Smuggler's Choice

Dax Ferrow had been running from the law since he was nine years old, and he'd learned one thing in all that time: the law always sent someone to talk first. Soldiers came with swords. Bailiffs came with words.

Words were worse.

He crouched at the mouth of the smuggler tunnel his own maps called the Weeping Stair — a name that had made sense once, before the stars went out and the tunnels started rearranging themselves like a drunk rearranging furniture — and watched the lamplight markets of the Undercourt glow far below. Somewhere in that amber haze, three of his crew were being hunted. And somewhere in that haze, a bailiff had found the fourth.

"Say it again," Dax said quietly. "Slow."

The boy — Pip, nineteen, all elbows and bad decisions, the newest of Dax's people — pressed his back to the tunnel wall and shook. "They grabbed Sella at the lamp-oil stalls. Bailiffs, three of them, in the grey coats with the chain-stitch. They didn't hurt her. That's the part that's wrong. They *bought her dinner*."

"Bought her dinner."

"Sat her down at a noodle stand. Paid the man. Asked her about you. About the gate-keeper, and the knight, and the child." Pip's voice dropped to a whisper, as if the tunnel itself might be listening, and after everything Dax had seen in the last week, he wasn't sure it wasn't. "Then they told her the offer. Free passage out. Full pardon, all of us, every name on every manifest the Undercourt keeps on smugglers. Wiped clean. All they want—"

"Is the route." Dax's mouth was dry. "The route we took down. Where we're going. Where the child sleeps."

Pip nodded. "They gave Sella till tomorrow's second bell. Said if she doesn't tell, they'll take the pardon off the table and put the chains on instead. And they said—" the boy swallowed "—they said to tell you it's not a threat, Dax. They said it's *arithmetic*. Said the court doesn't get angry. It just balances."

Dax looked out over the lamplight markets for a long moment. Somewhere down there, a hundred thousand souls without souls served the gentry of the deep, carrying lamps and carrying trays and carrying nothing behind their eyes. Somewhere down there, a girl named Wren was sleeping in a smuggler's hammock, humming songs that made the walls remember what they were. Somewhere down there, Rafael Eriksen was sitting up with their brother's ghost of a soul, and Maren Kael was sharpening a sword she wasn't sure she was allowed to carry anymore.

And somewhere down there, Sella Voss — no relation to anyone, a woman Dax had pulled out of a debtor's cell six years ago — was eating bailiff noodles and doing arithmetic of her own.

"Where is she now?" Dax asked.

"Coming here. They let her go. Said she had until second bell to *decide*, and they wanted her to decide with her crew's faces in front of her." Pip hugged his knees. "Dax, what do we do? The pardon. That's — that's everything. That's you never running again. That's Brann getting to see his mother in Thornfell. That's—"

"That's them finding the child." Dax said it flat, because saying it any other way would have meant screaming. "You know what the court wants with Wren. You saw what the bailiffs did to the lamplighters who wouldn't talk. There's no version of this where we trade the route and the court says *thank you, lovely, off you go*. We'd be trading a little girl's throat for a pardon that expires the moment she stops being useful to them."

"But Sella—"

"Sella knows that better than any of us." Dax stood, and his knees cracked, and he felt every one of his thirty-eight years. "That's why they let her come here instead of dragging her. They're not buying her. They're making *her* buy *us* — so that whatever she chooses, the debt lands on her, not them. That's how the deep court works. That's how every court works. The crown up top taught them well."

He turned back into the tunnel, into the dark, toward the camp where his people waited, and where a gate-keeper who should have been his enemy was keeping watch over a child who hummed the mountain's dreams.

*You owe her a blood debt,* said the old voice in his head, the one that had been keeping his books since the winter crossing three years ago, the one that sounded annoyingly like his mother. *You already paid it. You carried them through the dark. The ledger between you is clean. You could walk away, Ferrow. Take the crew, take the side tunnels, take the pardon if Sella gives them nothing and the court comes for you anyway — you've escaped worse.*

"Yeah," Dax muttered to the empty tunnel. "And look how much good that's ever done me."

The tunnel didn't answer. But somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat, he thought he heard the mountain hum.

* * *

The camp was a hollow the mountain had made and then, apparently, forgotten about — a bubble of stone off the Weeping Stair, big enough for six people and a hammock, dry, warm, and lit by a single lamp Dax had rigged from smuggler's oil and a smuggler's wick. It was the kind of place Dax had spent his whole life finding: the cracks in the world where the law didn't reach.

Rafael Eriksen sat with their back to the wall, a knife across their knees, sharpening it in slow, patient strokes. They'd been doing that for three days. Not because the knife needed it. Because it was what gate-keepers did with their hands when their hearts were breaking, and Dax had learned to read the rhythm of it the way sailors read weather. Fast strokes meant worry. Slow strokes meant grief. Tonight the strokes were slow as a funeral drum.

Wren was asleep in the hammock, humming in her sleep, and the stone hummed back, very quietly, like a mother answering a child across a large house.

Maren Kael stood at the hollow's entrance, on watch, because Maren always took first watch and last watch and any watch nobody else wanted, as if standing guard could burn the disgrace out of her name. Dax had stopped trying to tell her to sleep.

Rafael looked up. "You're back early. That's either very good or very bad."

"Bailiffs found Sella," Dax said.

The knife stopped. Even Maren turned from the entrance. Even the mountain, Dax would have sworn, went quiet — Wren's hum faltered in her sleep, a note held unfinished.

Dax told it all, flat and fast, the way he'd tell a crew about a cave-in: the noodle stand, the grey coats with the chain-stitch, the offer, the pardon, the second bell. He watched their faces as he spoke. Maren's jaw went tight with old, familiar fury — she'd worn that expression the whole time he'd known her, like a scar she'd decided to keep. Rafael didn't move at all, which was worse.

"Second bell tomorrow," Rafael said at last. "That's not a lot of time."

"That's the point. Courts squeeze. Squeeze hard enough and people give up things they'd die before handing over calm."

"They won't get the route from Sella." Maren's voice was iron. "She's loyal."

"She's *smart*," Dax corrected. "Loyal's a feeling. Smart's a survival trait. And smart is going to be looking at that pardon, and at Brann's mother in Thornfell, and at all of us, and doing sums. I don't blame her for whatever number she comes up with. That's the trap of it. They've built it so that every way out costs somebody."

Rafael set the knife down. "Then we don't wait for her to choose. We choose first. We move tonight, take a route even Sella doesn't know, and when the second bell comes, there's nothing for her to sell."

"You don't have a route," Dax said. "That's the problem, Eriksen. Every route in this mountain, I drew. Every route in this mountain is on a map in my pack. And every map I own, the court has a copy of, because the court bought a copy from a man named Hollis three years ago and I've been meaning to kill him about it ever since." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "They don't need Sella for the tunnels. They need her for one thing only. They need her to tell them which of my routes we're on *right now*. And there's only one route that fits the Weeping Stair. She won't even have to guess."

Silence. Wren hummed. The stone answered.

"Then we're cornered," Maren said.

"We're cornered," Dax agreed. "Unless."

Rafael's eyes came up. Gate-keeper's eyes, Dax thought. Eyes that had spent twenty years watching a door and learning everything a person could carry through it. "Unless what, Ferrow?"

Dax reached into his pack.

* * *

His maps were the only beautiful thing he'd ever made.

He'd started drawing them at fourteen, in charcoal on cured hide, because the smugglers who'd taken him in kept dying on routes nobody had written down, and Dax had decided — in the way of children, absolutely and without nuance — that if the routes were written down, nobody would have to die. Twenty-four years later, his maps were legend in three kingdoms and one court beneath them. Every passage through the Thornfells, every hidden stair, every vein of the mountain's underside, inked in his own careful hand, corrected after every crossing, annotated with water sources and watchman rotations and the locations of every trap he'd ever sprung or built. Forty-one maps. He knew them the way priests know prayers. They were, more than his crew, more than his name, the thing that was *Dax Ferrow*.

They were also, as of three years ago, the thing that was going to get a child killed.

He laid them out in the lamp-light, all forty-one, weighted at the corners with stones, and the others gathered around without a word, because even Rafael and Maren understood they were looking at something like a body before burial.

"The court has copies of most of these," Dax said. "Old ones. Hollis sold them a set from before the star-fall, and the tunnels have shifted since — the mountain's been rearranging itself, hiding things, so half of what they bought is wrong now. But the Weeping Stair's on here." He tapped the map in the center. "And the bailiffs know it. And Sella will tell them, because they'll make her, and I won't hate her for it. So."

"So," Rafael said.

"So there's one thing they don't have copies of. The routes I've never written down." He looked at the maps, all that careful ink, all those saved lives. "Every crossing I've made since the star-fall, I've kept in my head. Because the tunnels kept moving and the maps kept lying, and because — I don't know. Some instinct. Some smuggler's superstition. The mountain's hiding something, and I didn't want to be the man who wrote down where."

"You're proposing to burn them," Maren said. Not a question. Her voice had gone strange.

"I'm proposing to burn the ones that matter. The Stair. The approaches. The routes that lead anywhere near where we're going and where we've been. If the maps don't exist, Sella can't sell what she doesn't have, the bailiffs can't follow what they can't read, and the only person in this mountain who knows the way forward is me." He looked up at Rafael. "You're the best route-finder I've ever met, Eriksen. Better than me, up top. But down here, in the dark, with the mountain moving the walls — right now, tonight, I'm the only map there is."

"And if something happens to you?" Rafael asked quietly.

"Then we're all lost together, which is how I prefer it."

Wren stirred in the hammock. For a moment, without waking, she hummed three notes — and the lamp-flame leaned toward her, and the stone walls seemed to lean too, and Dax felt the hair rise on his arms. Then she settled, and the hollow was just a hollow again.

"The pardon," Rafael said. "If you burn your maps, and the court catches your crew anyway — there'll be no deal left to make. No leverage. You'd be burning your people's way out along with your own."

"My people's way out was never mine to sell," Dax said. "That's what the bailiffs don't get. That's what *no* court gets. Sella's not a coin in my purse. Brann's not a line item. They're my people, and my people don't get traded — not for pardons, not for routes, not for anything." He heard his own voice and was surprised by it: not the smuggler's easy drawl, but something harder and older. His father's voice, maybe. His father had been a stonemason before the crown's taxes took the trade, and Dax had spent thirty years trying not to be anything like him, and here he was, talking like him.

Rafael studied him for a long moment. "Three years ago, on the winter crossing, I swore I'd collect your blood debt or die trying. You carried me through a whiteout with two broken ribs and never once complained."

"Complaining's bad for morale."

"You paid that debt, Ferrow. Fully. I told you that at the star-fall." Rafael's voice was low. "You don't owe me this."

"I know." Dax picked up the center map — the Weeping Stair, forty-one routes of a lifetime — and held it over the lamp. "That's what makes it different. A debt, I'd pay because I had to. This—" he looked around the hollow, at the knight and the gate-keeper and the sleeping child who heard the mountain dreaming, at the strange, impossible, found thing they had become in nine days of dark, "—this I'm giving. Freely. There's no law that makes me, no ledger that says I owe it. That's the whole point. The courts think everything's arithmetic. Debts and balances and souls by weight. But this isn't arithmetic. This is the one thing they can't put in a ledger."

He touched the corner of the map to the flame.

The hide caught slowly, the way good leather does — reluctantly, then all at once. Fire walked across the Weeping Stair. Fire walked across twenty-four years of careful ink. Dax fed it map after map, and Maren made a sound low in her throat like grief, and Rafael watched with an expression Dax couldn't read — until he could, and realized it was the same expression Rafael wore at the gate every night, keeping watch over something worth keeping.

The ashes were soft and grey and smelled like every winter crossing Dax had ever survived.

"There," he said, when the last one was cinder. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "Now there's no route to sell. Not for Sella. Not for me. Not for anyone. The only way through is with me, and I don't leave my people behind — and as of nine days ago, Eriksen, whether you like it or not, that includes you."

Rafael stood. For a moment Dax thought they were going to say something grand — gate-keepers were like that, oath-drunk, always reaching for the formal words — but what Rafael did instead was put out their hand, smuggler-fashion, palm sideways, the grip of equals on a job. Dax took it.

"Freely given," Rafael said. "Freely kept."

"Yeah, well." Dax grinned, because if he didn't grin he'd do something embarrassing. "Don't make it weird."

From the hammock, Wren spoke without opening her eyes, in the small, eerie voice she used when the mountain was talking through her sleep: *"The walls remember where the fire went. The walls are sorry."*

Nobody asked her what she meant. They'd learned not to.

Dax was kicking dirt over the ashes when Maren suddenly went rigid at the hollow's mouth, one fist raised — the old knight's signal, *voices, many, closing*. Down the Weeping Stair, far below, lamplight was climbing. Grey coats. Chain-stitch. And at their head, walking with the unhurried stride of someone who has never once been cold or hungry, a bailiff Dax had hoped never to see again: the tall one, the one they called the Tallyman, who carried no weapon and no lamp and no expression at all.

"Second bell came early," Maren breathed, drawing her sword.

"No," Dax said, watching the light rise, feeling the mountain hum beneath his boots like something waking up. "That's not second bell. That's not a hunt at all. Bailiffs don't come up the Stair in force unless they already know where we are." His stomach turned cold. "They're not coming to make Sella talk. They're coming to collect — and I'd bet my name they're not after us."

He looked at the hammock, at the small sleeping shape humming the mountain's dreams.

"They're after the child."
