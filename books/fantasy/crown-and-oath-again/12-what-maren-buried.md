## 12. What Maren Buried

The fire was small. That was the first thing Maren noticed about the way Rafael built it — small, ringed with stones the color of old iron, fed only enough wood to warm hands and not enough to cast their shadows far. A gate-keeper's fire. A fire for someone who had spent twenty years learning that light drew things through passes, and darkness kept them honest.

They were camped in a hollow beneath a shelf of stone that Dax swore had not existed three days ago, which Maren had stopped questioning. The tunnels behind them had rearranged themselves twice since the bridge of roots. Ahead of them, according to Wren's humming, lay the last descent into the Undercourt, and none of them spoke about what waited there except in the sideways language of practicalities: how much lamp-oil remained, whether the bailiffs' hounds could track through stone, who would sleep first.

Maren sat with her back against the cold rock and her sword across her knees, unstrapped, because she had learned in the last week that these three did not relax around an armed knight even when the knight was disgraced. Especially when the knight was disgraced. She had been unbuckling trust from herself piece by piece since the gate, and she knew — she had always known — that there was one buckle left, and it was rusted shut.

Rafael sat across the fire, sharpening a knife they did not need to sharpen. They had been sharpening it since dusk. The rhythm of it had become a question asked over and over into the dark: *what are you not telling me?*

Wren slept curled against Dax's pack, her small hands still moving faintly, conducting some music only she could hear. The smuggler himself was on watch at the hollow's mouth, though his shoulders said he was listening to every word.

"You've been circling it," Rafael said, without looking up from the blade. "Since the roots took your truth. Since before that, if I'm honest. You keep starting sentences and burying them."

Maren watched the fire. "Some truths are like wounds under armor," she said. "You don't open them on the road."

"We're not on a road." Rafael set the knife down at last, and the absence of its sound was louder than any accusation. "We're in the dark under a mountain that eats memories, going to buy back my brother's soul from a court that keeps ledgers instead of laws. If there is anything you know about why he was sold, Ser Kael, I will hear it now or I will spend the rest of this descent imagining it, and I promise you my imagination has been well-fed by twenty years of grief."

There it was. No anger in it. That was the worst part. Rafael Eriksen did not rage; they *kept*, the way they kept the gate, the way they kept the count of every soul that passed, and being kept inside that patient attention felt like standing in a doorway with a storm coming.

Maren pulled off one gauntlet, then the other. Her hands looked strange without them — pale, ordinary, the hands of a woman who had once been trusted with a king's confidence and had spent seven years learning to be no one.

"I told you I was stripped of my oath for speaking a truth the crown buried," she said. "I told you that much at your door."

"You told me that much and then went silent as a tombstone whenever I asked what truth."

"Because the truth has a name attached to it, and the name is yours."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, impossibly far above, the place where stars should have been was a blackness so complete it pressed down like water.

* * *

Maren had been twenty-six when she was sworn into the King's Own, and she had believed — with the whole stupid shining certainty of a woman raised on ballads — that the crown was worth the oath. She described it now in a voice gone flat and careful, the voice of someone reciting a report, because if she let it become a story it would become a wound again, and she needed her hands steady for what came after.

"There was a war," she said. "You won't find it in any chronicle. I've looked. I burned three years of my life looking, after. The histories call it the Reconciliation, or the Pacification of the Marches, or nothing at all — a gap of four years where the scribes simply wrote *the king's peace prevailed*. Four years, Rafael. Kingdoms don't have quiet gaps like that unless someone paid to make them quiet."

Rafael had gone very still. It was the stillness of the gate at midnight, Maren thought. The stillness of a person braced for the ward-line to snap.

"The war was real," she went on. "I fought in its last year before I understood what I was fighting for. The eastern lords had risen — properly risen, with armies and grain and cause, because the crown's taxes had starved three harvests out of them. And we were losing. Not badly. Not dramatically. Just... losing, the way you lose a siege, slowly, by arithmetic. The king's council met every week and the arithmetic got worse every week, and then one morning the arithmetic changed."

"How?" Rafael asked. But Maren saw in their face that they already knew. Had perhaps always known, in the way grief knows things the mind refuses.

"A delegation went under the Thornfells," Maren said. "Nine of them. Ministers, a Ledger-Reader, and the king's own seal. They came back in eleven days, and the eastern lords' armies broke apart within the season — not defeated. *Dissolved.* Their soldiers woke one morning unable to remember why they'd marched. Their generals signed terms they couldn't explain. One of the rebel lords wept through his own surrender and couldn't say why. I was there, Rafael. I held the surrender-standard. I thought it was a miracle." Her mouth twisted. "I wrote a poem about it. Gods help me, I wrote a *poem*."

"And the price?"

"The price was souls." The word came out of her like a splinter coming out of flesh — slow, resistant, leaving a channel of hurt behind it. "Not metaphor. Not tithe or tribute or whatever soft word a priest would dress it in. Souls. Living people, taken sleeping, sold down through the mountain to the Undercourt, and the court gave the crown victory the way a moneylender gives coin — against collateral, against interest, against a debt that doesn't close. The delegation carried back a list. Names. Villages marked in the king's own hand. Places so poor and so far from court that no one would miss them, and if anyone asked, bandits, plague, the usual mercies of history."

The fire had burned low. Neither of them moved to feed it.

"I found the list two years later," Maren said. "I was captain of the king's correspondence guard by then. I read everything that crossed his desk — that was the office, that was the *honor*. And one night there was a ledger page misfiled among grain requisitions, and it wasn't grain, it was names, forty-one names with village marks, and beside each name a weight." She swallowed. "Souls have weights, in the deep ledger. Did you know that? I didn't. I learned it reading a king's shopping list."

Rafael's voice, when it came, was almost nothing. "What kind of weight?"

"Enough to balance a war." Maren met their eyes across the dead fire. "One soul of sufficient weight could anchor a victory. The court priced them like ore. And there was one entry on that page that was different from the others — marked separately, in the king's hand, not the clerks'. A single acquisition. Larger than the rest. Marked *Thornfell gate-line*."

The knife Rafael had been sharpening lay between them on the stone, and Maren watched their hand drift toward it and stop, as if they'd forgotten what hands were for.

"My brother," Rafael said. "Tomas."

"Tomas Eriksen," Maren said quietly. "Age nineteen. Gate-born, gate-blooded — the entry notes it. *Strong ward-affinity.* He wasn't taken for the war, Rafael. He was taken for the *wards*. The crown had been feeding the border wards on purchased souls for years — that was the arrangement, that was the interest on the original loan. When the court called in its collateral, the crown paid in the cheapest strong souls it could reach, and a gate-keeper's son with a bright affinity was worth more than ten farmers. Your brother wasn't a casualty of the war." She made herself say it plainly, because Rafael deserved plainness more than comfort. "He was *infrastructure*. He was bought to hold up the sky."

The sound Rafael made was not a word. It was small and terrible, the sound of a load-bearing wall cracking, and then it stopped, because Rafael Eriksen did not break in front of witnesses — but their hands were shaking, and their eyes had gone somewhere twenty years away and also very close, to a boy on a wall counting travelers, humming to himself, saying *someone has to keep the light.*

"They told us he fell," Rafael whispered. "There was a rockslide on the north path. They brought back—" Their breath caught. "They brought back something to bury. We buried it. My mother never recovered. She used to walk the wall at night saying he was cold, he was cold up there, someone should bring him in out of the cold—"

"They faked the body," Maren said. "It was standard. The crown had a procedure for it. Wax and grave-cloth and a sealed coffin, and a priest paid to look sorrowful. I've read the requisitions, Rafael. *Funerary simulacra, twelve per annum.* Twelve families a year, given a box and a lie, so the mountain could be fed."

Silence filled the hollow like water filling a hull.

Then Rafael stood up. Not fast — that was the frightening thing. They rose the way a tide rises, inevitable, and turned to face the black mouth of the tunnel ahead, and when they spoke their voice had changed. It was still quiet. But it had edges now.

"He was nineteen. He laughed at my counting-songs. He wanted my post someday, and I teased him that he'd have to outlast me first, and he said he would, he'd keep the gate a hundred years." Rafael's fists closed. "And some clerk weighed him like ore and a king signed his name, and the sky stayed up on my brother's neck for twenty years while I swept snow off the steps and *thanked the wards for holding*."

"Maren." Dax's voice from the hollow's mouth, low. He hadn't turned from his watch, but he'd heard everything; of course he had. "Easy."

"No." Rafael's voice cracked like ice shifting. "Not easy. Never easy again." They turned back, and their eyes were wet and blazing at once. "You said the king ordered you silenced. Tell me the rest. All of it. What you said, and what it cost, because I need to know exactly what the truth weighs, Ser Kael. I need to know what I'm carrying down this mountain."

* * *

So Maren told her the rest, and telling it was like opening the last buckle and letting the armor fall.

"I went to the king," she said. "Not a petition. Not a whisper through channels. I put on my white cloak and I walked into the throne room during open court, because I was still naive enough to believe that the oath ran both ways — that I had sworn to protect the realm, and the realm included forty-one villages and a dead boy on a mountain, and the king would *have* to listen. I knelt. I said: *Your Majesty, the peace was purchased with souls, and the debt is still growing, and every year we pay it the crown becomes a thing I cannot protect.* In front of the court. In front of the ambassadors." A ghost of the old fury crossed her face, seven years undimmed. "Do you know what he did? He didn't have me executed. Execution makes martyrs, and I was popular — the Hero of the Surrender-Standard, they called me, gods rot that poem. Instead he had the Lord Confessor declare me *oath-broken by derangement*. Grief-mad, they said. Battle-shock. A knight whose mind had cracked under honor's weight, pitied, pensioned, stripped of cloak and name in the same ceremony where they praised my service. And every record of the Reconciliation's true cost was burned the same week, and every guard who'd ridden with the delegation retired to lands far away, and the mountain went on eating."

She looked at her bare hands.

"I spent seven years proving my sanity to people who preferred me mad. Because madness was safer for everyone. A mad knight is a sad story. A sane knight with evidence is a *crisis*." She laughed, short and bitter. "And here is the joke, Rafael, the part that kept me walking instead of lying down in a ditch: I finally reached the archives of a fallen house last winter — loyalists who kept copies — and I found the original treaty clause. The debt clause. Do you know what it says?"

Rafael shook their head once.

"*The crown shall render unto the court beneath such souls as balance the account, until such time as a keeper of equal standing shall stand surety.*" Maren recited it like a curse. "A keeper of equal standing. I thought it meant a king. I spent months trying to work out which king had defaulted. But I've had a week under this mountain, and I've watched a gate-keeper argue law with a bridge made of roots, and I think I finally understand. It doesn't mean a king. Kings *made* the debt. It means someone the old law recognizes as a keeper — someone who can stand in the ledger's presence and speak with authority over a gate." She leaned forward, firelight catching the scar along her jaw. "Rafael. The clause isn't a trap for the crown. It's a *door*. It's been a door for twenty years, waiting for someone with the standing to walk through it, and the crown has spent those twenty years making sure no keeper ever learned the truth — because a keeper who knew might actually come down here and *end it*."

The hollow was absolutely silent. Even Wren had gone still in her sleep, her fingers curled closed, as if the mountain itself were listening.

Rafael stood motionless for a long moment. Maren watched the rage move through them — watched it seek a target, the king, the court, the clerk who had weighed Tomas like ore, the priest with his wax-and-gravecloth mercy — and watched, with something like awe, as it refused every one. Rafael Eriksen's anger did not want a throat. It wanted a *task*.

"Not vengeance," Rafael said at last, and their voice had settled into something harder and calmer than rage. "The court would love vengeance. Vengeance is just another debt, another entry, another soul weighed against a soul. No." They picked up the knife and slid it home in its sheath with a click that sounded like a lock turning. "My brother was sold to end a war that never needed fighting, by men who will die comfortably in beds I can't reach. Fine. Let them. But the ledger is still open. The wards are still starving. Forty-one villages' worth of souls are still caged under this stone, and somewhere down there is a boy who only remembers keeping a light." Rafael looked at Maren across the ashes, and for the first time since the gate, they smiled — thin, fierce, gate-keeper's steel. "So we're not going down to bargain anymore, Ser Kael. We're going down to *audit*."

From the tunnel mouth, Dax snorted softly. "Audit. Sure. With a smuggler, an oathbreaker, and a child who hums." He paused. "Best raiding crew I ever ran with."

And in her sleep, Wren began — very quietly, in a voice like frost forming — to hum a new line of the mountain's song. None of them knew the melody yet.

But the mountain, far below, had begun to count again.
