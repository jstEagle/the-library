## 13. The Bailiff's Hunt

Dax Ferrow had spent thirty years learning which silences meant trouble. The silence of a guard changing shifts. The silence of a tunnel that had stopped breathing. The silence of a crew that had stopped trusting its captain.

But he had never heard a silence like the one that fell over the lamplight markets when the bailiffs came.

It started with the lamps. The Undercourt's markets ran on lamplight the way surface towns ran on sun — hundreds of little glass globes strung between bone pillars and root-beams, each one burning with a pale, honeyed flame that never guttered and never spread. The light made everything look softer than it was. Made the gentry's long faces look almost kind. Made the soulless servants' blank eyes look almost peaceful.

One by one, the lamps began to go out.

Not all at once. Not like a wind blowing through. One lamp here, two there, a string of six along the eastern colonnade — each flame folding itself closed like a hand, deliberate and unhurried, the way a man snuffs candles in a house he owns.

"They're coming," Wren said.

She was sitting on Dax's shoulders, the way she'd taken to doing in the markets, because from up there she could see the whole cathedral of the place and because, though nobody said it aloud, none of them liked letting her out of arm's reach. Her small hands were knotted in his hair. Her voice was very calm, the way children are calm about things that would break grown men.

"The bailiffs?"

"The ones that walk wrong." She shifted, peering down the colonnade. "Three of them. No. Four."

Dax counted the dying lamps and did his own arithmetic. Four was right. He'd seen bailiffs once before, years ago, from a very safe distance — tall shapes in grey livery that fit them badly, like the livery had been cut for men and the bailiffs had only ever been shaped like men. They didn't carry weapons. They carried writs, long scrolls of pale vellum, and when they unrolled a writ at you, you found yourself standing exactly where they wanted you, and you couldn't remember deciding to stop.

"Maren." He kept his voice level, the captain's voice, the one that didn't carry panic even when it was full of it. "We've got company."

Maren Kael was already turning. She'd been haggling with a lamp-seller over a vial of light that could be carried into the dark places, but her hand had gone to her sword the moment the first lamp died, because she was an oathbreaker, not a fool — there was a difference, and she'd spent years learning where the line ran. "How many?"

"Four. Coming up the colonnade from the court side."

"And behind us?"

Dax glanced back the way they'd come, toward the market's western mouth, the arch of woven root that led down toward the tunnels. Two more lamps had gone out there. He watched the flame of a third fold closed.

"Behind us too," he said. "They've closed the box."

* * *

The thing about being cornered, Dax thought, as the six of them — five, and Wren on his shoulders — moved quickly but not too quickly through the thinning crowd, was that he'd spent his whole life making sure it never happened. That was the smuggler's whole art, really. Not speed, not nerve. It was always having a second door. A third. A tunnel behind the tunnel. He knew the underside of these mountains better than any man living, better than the court's own cartographers, better than the worms that made half the passages.

And every route out of this market, in his head, was already marked with a little red X.

The colonnade route: X. Bailiffs on it. The root-arch west: X. The stair down to the tallow-works: X — he could see the grey shapes converging there, moving with that terrible unhurried patience, like water finding its level. The lamplifters' gallery overhead: X, because bailiffs didn't need stairs.

"Tell me you have a way," Maren said. She'd taken point without anyone assigning it, which was the sort of thing that would have annoyed Dax if she weren't so good at it. Her sword was still sheathed. Swords were worse than useless against bailiffs; he'd heard stories, and the stories all ended the same way, with the sword standing exactly where its owner had left it and the owner standing somewhere else, holding a writ.

"I have six ways," Dax said. "All of them are dead."

"Then make a seventh."

"That's not — " He stopped. Because Wren had gone rigid on his shoulders, her fingers tightening in his hair hard enough to hurt, and she was looking not at the bailiffs but at the lamps themselves.

"They're not hunting us," she said. "They're hunting *him*." She pointed — a small, certain finger — at Rafael.

Rafael Eriksen stood a little apart from the group, as they always did, the way gate-keepers stood, weight settled, eyes moving. They'd been quiet all day, quieter than usual, and Dax had put it down to what they'd seen in the servant's collar — the brother's echo, wearing its patient face. But now Rafael looked up at the closing ring of bailiffs with an expression Dax recognized, because he'd worn it himself once, on a winter crossing he didn't talk about.

It was the face of a man who had already decided what he was willing to trade.

"No," Dax said, before Rafael could say anything. "Whatever you're thinking. No."

"You don't know what I'm thinking."

"I know that face. I've *made* that face. It's the face of a man doing arithmetic, and I'm telling you the sum comes out wrong." He lifted Wren down and set her behind his legs, between him and Maren, which was the closest thing to a vault he could build out of people. "Nobody pays today. Not you, not the knight, not the girl. That's the rule now. I'm making it a rule."

"You don't make rules in my court," said a voice like a door opening in an empty house.

* * *

The bailiff in front of them was taller than the others, or seemed to be, the way a shadow is taller at dusk. Its grey livery fit it worse than the others' fit them. It held its writ already unrolled, and Dax felt the pull of it immediately — a gentle, reasonable tug behind the eyes, a suggestion that standing still would be so much easier, that running was such an ugly word, that really, why not just —

He bit the inside of his cheek until he tasted blood. The tug loosened. Not gone. Never gone. But loosened.

"Rafael Eriksen," the bailiff said. "Keeper of the Thornfell gate. The court acknowledges your claim on the debtor-soul entered in the Ledger of the Deep. The court reminds you that claims are heard in the court's own time, in the court's own hall. The court notes that you have been wandering." The writ unrolled another arm's length. "Wandering is a debt."

"Everything's a debt here," Maren said. "That's the whole trick of the place, isn't it? Make the ledger so long nobody can read it, and call the reading itself a debt."

The bailiff's head turned toward her with the slow smoothness of something that had no neck, only an arrangement. "Ser Maren Kael. Oathbroken. Your name is also in the ledger. Would you like to hear the entry?"

"No," said Wren.

She stepped out from behind Dax's legs before he could grab her — and he *did* grab, and missed, because the child had the uncanny knack of not being where hands went — and stood in the open ground between the party and the bailiff, small and thin and barefoot on the bone-flagged floor, and she began to hum.

Dax had heard her hum before. In the refugee camp, in the tunnels, in the quiet watches of the night when the mountain's dreaming leaked up through the stone. It was always the same music, or music from the same country: slow, looping, wordless, a tune that felt older than tunes. It had made him sleepy, once. It had made him homesick, once, for a home he'd never had.

This was not that humming.

This was the same song played *against* the room. Wren hummed, and the note she held was the exact inverse of the note the bailiff's writ was singing — because the writ was singing, Dax realized, some low subsonic thread under the words, a binding-note, the leash-note the court put in everything it owned — and Wren's voice found that thread and *unwound* it.

The bailiff's writ curled at the edges. Then it curled further. Then, with a sound like a sigh from a dead man, it came apart — not torn, not burned, but *unravelled*, thread by grey thread, until the bailiff was holding a fistful of nothing.

The bailiff looked down at its empty hands.

Dax would carry that look to his grave. Not fear. Bailiffs didn't fear. It was *confusion* — vast, structural, the confusion of a machine encountering a number it had no column for. The court had an entry for everything. Debts, claims, souls, names, wandering. The court had no entry for a child's song.

The other bailiffs stopped converging. All five of them stood in the half-dark of the dying market, and their heads turned toward Wren in unison, and Dax felt the whole weight of the court's attention come down on that one small humming girl like a mountain leaning.

"Wren," Maren said quietly. "Walk backward. Slowly. Keep humming."

"I know," Wren said, and didn't stop humming, and walked backward, and the bailiffs *followed her* — not moving their feet, exactly, but leaning, drifting, drawn along the thread of her song like moths who had forgotten they were supposed to be the predators.

"Right," Dax breathed. "Right. New plan. The song is the seventh door."

"It's not a door," Rafael said. Their voice was strange — tight with something that wasn't fear, or wasn't only fear. "It's a key. Dax, when the song stops — "

"It's not going to stop." Dax was already moving, herding them west, away from the colonnade, toward the one route he'd marked with an X for a different reason — not because it was guarded, but because it was *bad*. The lamplifters' run: a maintenance crawl behind the market's great lamp-strings, a passage of grease and glass-dust and old flame, half-collapsed, that let out into the deep dark below the tallow-works. No bailiff could follow a writ down a passage that narrow. No bailiff, he hoped, could follow at all.

He got them to the hatch. Maren went first, sword drawn now for the crawling, then Rafael, then Wren — still humming, walking backward into the dark, her eyes on the bailiffs until the last second — and Dax went last, because he was the captain, and the captain is always last, and because he wanted one more look at what was behind them.

What was behind them was the bailiffs, all six now, standing in a perfect ring around the space where Wren had been. And as the hatch swung shut, Dax saw them all raise their heads at once, in unison, toward the ceiling of the market — toward the court above, toward the Ledger, toward whatever power had written them — and the look on their borrowed faces was not confusion anymore.

It was hunger.

* * *

The lamplifters' run was exactly as bad as Dax remembered, which was a comfort. He'd have been more worried if it had improved.

They crawled in single file through a passage barely wide enough for his shoulders, the walls slick with tallow-grease, the broken stubs of old lamp-chains hanging down like the ribs of something long dead. Above them, through gaps in the ceiling, they could see the market's lamp-strings passing, globe after globe of honeyed light, and the light threw their shadows forward along the crawl in long, leaping shapes, so that it looked like they were being chased by themselves.

Wren had stopped humming. The silence she left behind her was enormous.

"You did that on purpose," Maren said. Her voice came floating back through the dark, tight with something Dax hadn't heard from her before. Not fear. Ferocity, maybe. Or its first cousin. "The humming. You knew what it would do."

"I knew it would *unmake*," Wren said. "The mountain told me. The bailiffs are made of the same thread as the writs. Same song, different words. My song is the mountain's song, and the court's song is a lie, and lies can't stand up next to it." A pause. "They didn't like it."

"No," Dax said. "They didn't."

"Are they going to tell the court?"

"Oh, they're going to do worse than tell." He pulled himself around a collapsed chain-mass, glass dust crunching under his palms. "You unwound a bailiff's binding in front of five witnesses and the whole market. The court can't unsee that. The court can't unhear *you*. Which means, little songbird, that as of tonight you're not a debt anymore. You're a threat. And the court doesn't have a column for threats either, so it's going to make one, and it's going to make it out of whatever it finds first."

"Meaning us," Rafael said.

"Meaning us."

They crawled on. Behind them, faint through the stone, Dax heard the market's lamps relight one by one, the court reasserting itself, the honeyed order flowing back into the spaces Wren's song had opened. And under the relighting, almost too low to hear, something else: a sound like a great page being turned.

* * *

They came out of the run into the deep dark below the tallow-works, and that was where they found Maren's blood.

It happened fast. Later, Dax could never make the pieces sit still long enough to build a clean memory of it. One moment they were standing in the black, catching their breath, Rafael striking a spark to the lamp-vial they'd bought; the next, the dark *moved* — not a bailiff, not anything with a writ, but something older and dumber, one of the court's lesser leavings, a debt-hound maybe, all sinew and ledger-sense, a thing that could smell an unpaid claim the way a shark smells blood. The court must have loosed it down here before the lamps even went out. A second door, Dax thought wildly. The court had second doors too.

It went for Wren. Of course it did. The smallest debt, the easiest claim.

And Maren Kael — oathbroken, disgraced, a woman who had spent years learning exactly where the line between knight and fool ran — crossed that line without a flicker of hesitation. She got between the hound and the child the way a wall gets between a storm and a window, and her sword came up, and for three seconds the deep dark was full of a sound Dax had only ever heard in the worst stories: steel and sinew and a woman's breath, all arguing.

She killed it. Of course she did. But it got her first — a raking strike across the ribs, four lines of red through her shirt, deep enough to matter, not deep enough to end. She went down to one knee, then up again, waving off Rafael's hands, waving off Dax's, standing over Wren with her sword still up as if there might be more.

"There aren't more," Dax said, listening to the dark. "It was alone. They always hunt alone, the hounds. It's in their contract."

"Everything here is in a contract," Maren said through her teeth. She let Rafael bind the wounds — four gashes, ugly but clean, wrapped with strips of Dax's own shirt, because his were the most expendable and he said so until they let him. She stood through the binding without a sound, which told Dax more about her than any confession ever had.

"You didn't have to do that," Wren said, when it was done. She was standing very close to Maren, looking up at her. "It was coming for me."

"Yes," Maren said.

"It could have killed you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment. The lamp-vial's light was low and gold, and it made the blood on her shirt look like shadow. "Because," she said finally, "nobody gets left behind. Not on my watch. Not ever again." And Dax, who had heard her say a hundred careful things, understood that this was the first true one.

He turned away to give them their privacy and found Rafael watching him, the lamp-vial cupped in their hands, the light climbing their face.

"You made a rule today," Rafael said. "Nobody pays."

"Captain's prerogative."

"It's a good rule." Rafael's voice was quiet. "I'm going to break it. Soon. You should know that."

"I know," Dax said. "That's why I made it a rule. Rules are for breaking, and I wanted to at least be *present* for it when you did, so I could object properly, at volume, in front of witnesses."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through Rafael's eyes and died there. They looked back down the way they'd come, toward the market, toward the court, toward the sound of that great page turning.

"Dax," they said. "That sound. The page. What do you think it was?"

Dax had been asking himself the same question, and he had an answer, and the answer sat in his chest like swallowed ice. He was a smuggler. He knew ledgers the way he knew tunnels — knew their weight, their smell, the sound they made when something heavy was written into them. And he knew what a ledger sounded like when it was being *amended*.

"It was the court," he said, "writing a new entry." He looked at each of them — Maren bleeding and upright, Wren humming softly to herself in the dark, Rafael with their cupped light and their terrible arithmetic face. "It was the court writing down *us*."

Above them, far off, through a mile of stone, the lamps of the market burned on, honeyed and orderly, and somewhere in the deep dark behind them, something began to follow at a walk — unhurried, patient, certain of the debt.

Dax turned them all west, into the black, and set a pace just faster than fear.

"Move," he said. "We've got a soul to steal and a ledger to rob, and I'd very much like to be gone before the court finishes its sentence."
