## 14. The Soul That Remembers

The collar came off easier than Rafael had expected. That was the first cruelty of it — that twenty years of servitude could be undone with a twist of Wren's small hands and a hum that made the bone-white metal split like river ice in spring. The soul-echo had not flinched. It had simply stood there among the lamplight racks of the Undercourt's servant gallery, watching them work the way a doorpost watches someone paint it.

Now it stood at the edge of the lamplight market, where the bailiffs' pursuit had scattered into confused knots behind them, and Rafael faced their brother for the first time in two decades.

It was Tomas. And it was not.

The shape of him was right — the broad shoulders he'd carried even at nineteen, the cowlick that never lay flat, the scar on his knuckles from the time he'd dared Rafael to touch the gate-iron in winter. But the light was wrong. Living people threw shadows; Tomas cast none. His skin had the faint translucence of lamplit vellum, and where his eyes should have held something — mischief, grief, annoyance at being dead — there was only the patient emptiness of a thing kept on a shelf.

"Tomas," Rafael said, and their voice cracked down the middle like old mortar.

The echo tilted its head. "You know my function."

*Function.* The word went through Rafael like a nail through the palm. "I know *you*. I'm Rafael. Rafe. You used to call me Rafe when you wanted to make me furious, because you knew I hated—" They stopped. Breathed. The air of the deep court tasted of tallow and cold stone. "You built me a whistle out of reed when I was seven. It didn't work. You gave it to me anyway and told me the mountain would finish tuning it for me."

Something moved behind the echo's face. Not recognition — Rafael would have known recognition, would have known it anywhere, had spent twenty years imagining it in dreams. It was smaller than that. A flicker, like a candle guttering in a draft, as if some buried sediment of the man had been stirred.

"The gate," the echo said. "The gate needed keeping. I kept it."

"That was my job." Rafael stepped closer. Maren hung back by a shuttered lamp-stall, one hand pressed to her bandaged side, watching the alley mouths. Dax had taken Wren around the corner to keep her from seeing — though Rafael suspected the girl heard everything, whether she wanted to or not. "Yours was the orchard. The south slope. You were going to graft the bitter pears onto sweet stock and prove Father wrong about the soil. Do you remember the orchard?"

"No," said the echo, without cruelty, without anything at all. "There is no orchard here. There is the gate, and the warding, and the counting. I remember the gate."

Rafael's knees nearly went. They caught themselves on a lamp-post, and the iron of it sang faintly against their palm, the same note as the gate-bell back home, and they wondered dimly if the whole mountain was tuned to that one sound, if everything down here rang with the memory of thresholds.

Twenty years. Twenty years of lighting the gate-lamp twice and once more before bed, because Tomas had died in autumn and Rafael had started the habit of an extra flame in the dark months and never stopped. Twenty years of talking to a grave on the south slope, telling it the news of the passes. And all that time, the true weight of their brother — the part of him that was *him*, whatever that was, the whistle-making part, the pear-grafting part, the part that called them Rafe just to watch them scowl — had been down here in the dark, wearing a collar, feeding wards.

"We came to take you home," Rafael said.

The echo considered this the way a scale considers weights. "Home is not in my ledger-entry."

"Your ledger-entry is wrong. Your ledger-entry is a *crime*. You were stolen, Tomas. Sold. The crown sold you before you'd been dead a season, and everything since has been theft compounded on theft, and we are here to unmake it."

"Unmake." The echo tried the word like a coin bitten between teeth. Its voice was Tomas's voice, and that was the worst of it — the exact grain of it, the slight rasp when he spoke low. Rafael had heard that rasp across a kitchen table ten thousand times. Now it came out of a hollow thing under the mountain and every syllable was a small murder. "The wards above are broken. I felt them break. I was the pattern of them, and when the pattern tore, I tore. If I am removed from the ledger, the wards do not mend. If I am removed, the dark stays."

"I know how the wards work."

"You know how the *gate* works." Not sharp — the echo could not be sharp — but precise, in the way of a thing reciting its own instructions. "You keep the door. I *am* the lock. There is a difference, keeper."

Rafael flinched at the title. The echo noticed. Of course it noticed; it had been made into a mechanism, and mechanisms miss nothing in their own workings.

"I dreamed the gate every night for twenty years," the echo went on, and now something crept into the voice that might, in a living man, have been weariness. "That is what they took from me first, the court. Everything else burned away slowly — the names, the faces, the taste of pears. But the gate they fed me every night, because it was useful. I watched your fire through the ward-lattice. Twice a night, then three times. I thought: someone keeps a light for me. I did not know who. I did not need to know who. The knowing burned too, eventually. But the *light* —" the echo's empty eyes turned toward the ceiling of the world, toward a sky with no stars in it — "the light I kept. It was the only thing I owned."

Rafael could not speak. The extra lamp-flame, the winter habit, the stupid superstitious third lighting of the wick — it had crossed the wards. It had reached down through stone and law and theft, and it had been, for a stolen soul, the one possession left.

"They told me to tell you," the echo said. "The gentry. When the wards fell, they said the keeper would come, and I was to say: go home, keeper, the debt is only beginning. But I find I want to say something else instead." It paused, and the pause had Tomas in it, the old hesitation before he said something he knew would start a fight. "I want to say: don't pay it."

"What?"

"Don't balance the ledger. Don't trade. Don't put anyone in my place." The echo's hands — Tomas's hands, the scarred knuckles — opened at its sides, palms up, an old gesture from the kitchen table, *look, no tricks, hear me*. "I have been a thing that is used. I know what it is to be the payment. There is nothing of me left to save, keeper. You cannot rescue what does not remain. But you can *finish* it."

The word landed in Rafael's chest like a dropped anvil.

"No," they said.

"You don't understand—"

"I understand perfectly." Rafael's voice came out low and shaking. "You're asking me to give up. You're asking me to leave you in this hole because it's easier than fighting."

"I am asking you to bury me." For the first time, the echo's voice cracked, and the crack was so exactly, so unbearably Tomas — fifteen years old, voice breaking over the supper stew, everyone pretending not to notice — that Rafael made a sound they'd never made before, somewhere between a sob and a snarl. "I have been *unfinished* for twenty years. A soul in a ledger is not alive and not dead and not at rest. It is a sentence with no end. Every night I dream the gate, and every night I hope the dream will be the last, and it never is, because ledgers do not sleep. You came here thinking rescue and release are the same thing. They are not. Rescue is for the living. I am past its reach. Release is all that's left, and release means *ending*, and ending means someone must hold the door of it open behind me."

"And that someone pays with their own name? Their own soul? That's what the Ledger-Keeper told us. Equal weight. You want me to weigh you out of the scales by weighing myself in?"

"I want you to stop carrying me." The echo stepped closer, into the full lamplight, and Rafael saw that up close its edges frayed like worn cloth, threads of light coming loose and drifting away unregarded. It was unraveling. It had perhaps always been unraveling, patiently, invisibly, all the years Rafael had begged the stars for one more chance. "You have carried me for twenty years. The grave on the south slope. The third lamp. I know — I *felt* every one. You thought you were mourning. You were hauling. Two hundred pounds of grief up every morning and down every night, and it broke your step, and I watched it break, and I could do nothing, because I was a coin in another man's purse." The fraying edges brightened. "Set me down, Rafe."

*Don't call me that,* Rafael almost said, and then realized they were begging for it, aching for it, that hearing it was both the wound and the balm and that they were a coward to want it and a monster to refuse it.

"I can't," they whispered. "Tomas. Little brother. I swore an oath on the gate. Keep what passes, keep what stays, keep what's owed. You're owed. You're mine to keep. If I let the ledger close on you unfinished, then every night for the rest of my life I'll light that third lamp and know it shines on my failure."

"It shines on my rest," the echo said. "There is a difference, keeper. Learn it before the mountain teaches you."

From around the corner came Dax's voice, pitched low and urgent: "Bailiffs regrouping. Two minutes, maybe less. Keeper, whatever you're doing, do it faster."

Maren limped around the stall, pale, jaw set. She looked at the echo, then at Rafael, and Rafael saw in her face the particular knowledge of someone who had also been used as an instrument by powers that never asked — and who had chosen, at terrible cost, to speak the truth anyway.

"It's not lying to him to listen," she said quietly. "We've done enough deciding for other souls in this kingdom."

Rafael rounded on her. "You'd have me finish him? Unmake what's left?"

"I'd have you ask him what's left wants." Maren's voice was steady, but her hand on her wound wasn't. "I spent years wishing someone had asked me before they buried the truth with my oath. Ask him, Rafael. Properly. Then decide."

So Rafael turned back to their brother. The echo stood fraying gently in the lamplight, patient as furniture, and Rafael understood suddenly, wholly, that this was not endurance — it had no self left to endure with. This was a sentence approaching its final word, waiting only for permission to stop speaking.

"When you were little," Rafael said, "and you couldn't sleep, you'd come to my room and stand in the doorway until I woke up. You never came in. You never said anything. You just stood there until I noticed, and then you'd say *good, you're awake, I couldn't sleep either*, as if it was somehow my fault, as if I'd been appointed to it." Their throat closed. "Is that what you're doing now? Standing in the doorway?"

The echo was silent a long moment. The fraying slowed.

"Yes," it said at last, softly. "And you're awake. And I couldn't sleep. And it's yours to decide, keeper, same as it ever was. But I am so tired, Rafe. I have kept the gate in the dark for twenty years and I would like, just once, to be the one who gets to stop."

Rafael reached out — slowly, asking — and touched their brother's hand.

There was no warmth. But there was a flicker, a pulse of cold light, and through the ward-sense Rafael had honed over a lifetime at the gate they felt it: the shape of the wards, the great lattice strung over the Thornfells, and threaded through every strand of it, this soul's pattern, worn thin as the echo's edges, singing the same note as the gate-iron. And beneath the song, fainter than anything, one last ember of the boy with the reed whistle and the bitter pears, holding on not out of hope but out of courtesy, waiting for his sibling to finish the sentence.

"Equal weight," Rafael said hoarsely, to Maren, to the dark, to the ledger waiting somewhere below. "That's their law. Fine. But laws bend to older laws, and Wren says the mountain is older than the court, and I swear before every stone of it — nobody in this company goes in that ledger. Not Maren. Not Dax. Not Wren. If a name goes in beside yours, Tomas, it will be mine, and it won't be as payment. I'll find another way. Do you hear me? I'll find a way to finish you *and* keep my oath, because I'm a keeper, and keeping is what I do."

The echo looked at them. And in its ruined, emptied face, something kindled — small, guttering, unmistakably a smile.

"There he is," it said. "There's my brother."

The lamps down the market-row began to gutter, one after another, in the rhythm of marching boots.

"Bailiffs," Dax called. "Keeper — *now*."

The echo's fraying edges streamed toward Rafael like a tide going out. "Take me to the dreaming heart," it said quickly, urgently, its voice already thinning. "Wren knows the way. The ledger can't be edited — but the mountain wrote the first ledger, before the court, and what the mountain wrote, the mountain may unwrite. Finish me there, keeper. Wake what sleeps below. And Rafe —"

The boots turned the corner. Lamplight glinted on bone-white armor.

"— the third lamp. Keep it. Not for me. For whoever else is standing in a doorway in the dark."

Then the bailiffs were upon them, and Maren drew steel with her left hand because her right wouldn't close, and Dax threw Wren over his shoulder like a sack of smuggled wool, and Rafael seized their brother's dissolving hand and ran — down, past the market, toward the sound Wren had been humming all along, the deep note beneath the deep court, the sound of something vast turning over in its sleep and beginning, at last, to dream of waking.
