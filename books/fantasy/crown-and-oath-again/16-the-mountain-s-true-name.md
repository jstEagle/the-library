## 16. The Mountain's True Name

The mountain dreams in colors nobody has names for.

Wren knows this because she has been listening to it since before she knew her own name, back when she was small enough to sleep in the gap between two market stalls and the humming was the only thing that kept the dark from eating her whole. The others think the mountain hums. The others are wrong. The mountain *sings*, and it has been singing the same song for a thousand years, and the song is a name.

She told them this at the edge of the lamplight markets while Maren pressed her hand against the wound in her side and Dax counted his people like a man counting coins he was about to lose. She said, "I know where its heart is. It showed me when the bailiffs' bindings came apart. It's been showing me my whole life. I just didn't know what I was looking at."

Rafael had knelt down then, down to Wren's height, the way they always do, like kneeling is nothing, like a gate-keeper's knees are made for it. Their face was pale and their eyes were strange — half of them already somewhere else, half still here, still hers, still theirs — because everyone in the deep dark knows what it costs to write your own name into a ledger, even if Rafael pretends they don't.

"Show us," Rafael said.

So Wren is showing them.

* * *

The way down is not a road. Roads go places people want to go. This goes somewhere the mountain has kept folded up inside itself like a letter never sent, and the folds only open for someone who can hear the singing.

"Left here," Wren says, at a junction that looks exactly like the six junctions before it. Dax makes a noise in his throat, the sound of a smuggler watching his entire profession die.

"That's a wall," he says. "That's been a wall since my grandfather ran these tunnels."

"It's a door now," Wren says. "It's been a door since the stars went out. Things stopped being what they were." She puts her palm flat against the stone. It is warm, warmer than skin, and it thrums under her hand like the flank of an enormous sleeping animal, and she says, softly, the way you'd speak to something hurt: "Please."

The wall opens.

Not all at once. It opens the way a flower opens if a flower were made of granite, grain by grain, a slow grinding sigh that Wren feels in her teeth and her ribs and the backs of her eyes. Beyond it there is a corridor that no map ever drew, ribbed like the inside of a shell, and the air that comes out of it smells like rain on hot stone, like summer, like a world with weather in it. Nobody down here remembers weather. Wren doesn't either, really. But the mountain does, and it shares.

Maren limps through first. She hasn't let anyone look at the wound properly, which Wren thinks is very silly of her, and she holds her sword the way she holds everything: like she expects the world to take it away. "How far?" she asks.

"Not far," Wren says. Then, honestly, because the mountain doesn't like lies and neither should she: "I don't know. Distance is one of the things it stopped doing."

Dax laughs, one sharp bark with no fun in it. "Wonderful. Lost inside a living rock with a bailiff garrison between us and anywhere sane, following a child toward the dreaming heart of a god." He looks at Rafael. "You're certain about this?"

"No," Rafael says.

"Good," Dax says, and steps through the door. "Hate being certain. Certain gets people killed."

Wren walks last, and as she passes the threshold she pats the stone the way you'd pat a big dog, and the door closes behind them soft as breath.

* * *

The corridor goes down and down and also somehow sideways, and sometimes it goes through places that make no sense — once they walk through a chamber full of roots that glow faint gold and hang down like chandeliers in a hall nobody built; once they pass a window, an actual window, set in solid rock, looking out onto a night sky thick with stars that do not exist anymore, stars that went out over Thornfell weeks ago and are still burning in here, preserved like flowers pressed in a book.

Rafael stops at that window a long time.

"My brother used to name the constellations wrong on purpose," Rafael says, quiet, to nobody, or maybe to Wren, who is small and close and listens. "He said the official charts were boring. He'd invent better ones. There was one he called the Gate-Keeper's Dog, right over the north ridge, except it wasn't a dog, it was just four stars in a crooked line, and he swore up and down it was a dog." A breath. "I can see it from here. It's still up."

"The mountain kept them," Wren says. "All the light it took out of your sky, it didn't put out. It put away. Like—" she searches for the word, and finds it in the shape of the market stalls she slept between, "—like a shopkeeper putting stock behind the counter. It didn't want to go dark, Rafael. It was made to go dark. Somebody wrote that into it a long time ago and it's been obeying ever since, the way a dog obeys, even when obeying hurts."

Maren looks at the window too, and her jaw does the thing it does when she is swallowing words that would get her knighted again or killed again, depending who heard them. "A thousand years ago," she says, "the first kings of Thornfell bound the mountain. That much survived even in the burned records. They bound it and they leashed it and they built the wards on its back, and every history we have calls it a triumph." Her hand tightens on her sword hilt. "Every history we have is a lie with good posture."

"And the Undercourt grew up in the shadow of the leash," Rafael says.

"And got fat on the souls that slipped through the gaps," Dax says. "And here we are, walking straight down its throat to take the leash off." He grins, and the grin is mostly real. "You know what happens to men who take collars off gods? Neither do I. That's the exciting part."

Wren tugs Rafael's sleeve. "Come on," she says. "It's getting louder. It's almost awake. And Rafael—" she hesitates, because the next part is heavy and she has carried it alone long enough and carrying it together will still be heavy, but heavier shared is lighter held, or something like that; the songs say so, "—when it wakes, it's going to ask me something, and I'm going to answer, and after that nothing down here is going to be the same. Not the court. Not the ledger." She looks up at Rafael's face, at the name written there in ink that eats. "Maybe not some other things either. So if there's anything you want to say before—"

"There isn't," Rafael says, and their voice is steady as a gate-bar. "There's only forward. Same as always."

But their hand rests a moment on Wren's head, light as frost, and Wren leans into it, and that is a whole conversation without any words, and both of them understand it perfectly.

* * *

They hear the heart before they see it.

It is not a sound, exactly. It is the absence of all the sounds the deep dark makes — the drip of water, the creak of settling stone, the far-off lamplight bells of the court. All of it falls away, and what is left underneath is the singing, huge now, no longer hidden, the song that is a name sung by a voice the size of a country, and Wren's ears ache with it, and her eyes sting, and she realizes her cheeks are wet.

"What is it?" Maren asks, alarmed, kneeling despite her wound, hands hovering. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing," Wren says, and means it, and cannot explain it. "It's just — it's been singing alone for a thousand years. Nobody ever sang back. Not once." She wipes her face with her sleeve. "Wouldn't you cry?"

Then the last passage opens, and they stand in the dreaming heart of the mountain.

It is a cavern that is not a cavern. Its walls are not stone, or are stone the way a wave is water — moving, slow, immense, strata sliding over strata like the pages of an enormous book turning in its sleep. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, warm gold veined with blue, and in the center of it all, suspended above a floor of root and crystal, hangs the heart itself: a knot of luminous thread, miles of it, wound around itself like a ball of yarn the size of a cathedral, glowing and dimming, glowing and dimming, keeping time.

Keeping time with a heartbeat none of them have.

And threaded all through it, woven in and out of the light, are souls. Hundreds. Thousands. Little bright knots of thread, each one distinct, each one humming a note of the great song — and Wren understands, suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've known all along: the mountain has been holding them. All this time. Every soul that ever slipped through a crack in the wards, every one the court claimed, every one the crown sold — the mountain caught them the way a river catches rain, and kept them safe in its dream, because the mountain could not disobey the leash but it could *hide things*, the way a dog hides bones from a master it does not trust.

"They're here," Rafael breathes. "The ledger says the court owns them, but they're not — they're not in the court. They're *here*. The court's been collecting debts it never actually collected."

"The ledger keeps the claim," Maren says slowly, "not the soul. The court holds the paper. The mountain holds the people." Something fierce kindles in her face, the old fire, the truth-teller's fire. "Then the ledger isn't a jail. It's a lien. And liens can be broken."

The mountain stirs.

The walls turn faster. The light swells. The great ball of thread begins to rotate, ponderous as the seasons, and the singing rises until it fills Wren's skull like sunrise filling a valley, and every soul-thread flares at once, hundreds of tiny voices joining the one enormous one, and the whole cavern says, in a voice that is felt rather than heard, in a voice that arrives in the chest and the teeth and the marrow:

**WHO SINGS BACK?**

"I do," Wren says.

Her voice is very small. It disappears into the vastness like a pebble into a lake. But the mountain goes utterly, totally still, the way the world goes still when something impossible happens, and every soul-thread turns — Wren can feel them turn, attention like sunlight — toward the smallest person in the room.

**LITTLE LISTENER. YOU HEARD ME WHEN MY OWN MAKERS PLUGGED THEIR EARS.**

"You're loud," Wren says. "It's hard not to hear you. You just needed somebody who'd stop covering their ears and *listen*." She takes a step forward, out ahead of Rafael, out ahead of all of them, into the light. Her legs shake. Her voice doesn't. "You've been singing one thing for a thousand years and nobody answered, and I know why, and it's the worst thing I know, so I'm going to say it, and you have to stay calm, okay? Can you stay calm?"

**ASK.**

"Your name," Wren says. "They took it. The first kings. They took your name and they locked it in the leash, and that's why you sleep, and that's why the court could grow in your shadow, because a creature with no name can't say no." She takes a breath. The whole cavern waits, and the waiting has weight, like snow. "But I found it. In the songs. You sang it to me yourself, over and over, all those nights in the market, because you couldn't say it and you couldn't stop saying it. It was in the melody the whole time."

Behind her, Maren whispers, "Wren. No one has dared speak it in a thousand years. If the court hears—"

"The court," Wren says, "is about to have bigger problems."

She plants her feet. She fills her lungs with the warm gold air. And she speaks the mountain's true name aloud, the name woven into a melody no one ever finished, and the name is long, and old, and it tastes like deep water and older stone and the patience of continents, and when the last syllable leaves her mouth—

—the mountain wakes.

* * *

It does not roar. Wren expected roaring. What happens instead is worse and better: the entire mountain *sighs*, a single exhalation that lasts a minute and moves through every tunnel and market and bone-white hall of the Undercourt at once, and every lamp in the deep dark gutters, and every chain in the deep dark — every actual iron chain and every binding woven of law and ledger-ink — cracks a little, all at the same time, like ice on a spring river.

In the dreaming heart, the great knot of light unfurls.

Thread by mile-long thread, the ball opens like a fist unclenching after centuries, and the souls woven through it rise free of the winding, hovering in the golden air, thousands of small bright flames, and the sound they make is not words. It is the sound a crowd makes when the gates open. It is the sound of people remembering they are people.

**I AM NAMED,** the mountain says, and its voice is different now — less distant, more present, a voice speaking instead of a dream murmuring. **AND WHAT IS NAMED MAY CHOOSE. AND WHAT MAY CHOOSE MAY REFUSE.**

"Refuse what?" Dax asks, and his voice cracks on it, because even smugglers go reverent in front of miracles.

**THE LEDGER,** the mountain says. **THE COURT'S AUTHORITY OVER THE SOULS IN MY KEEPING RESTED ON MY SILENCE. MY SILENCE IS OVER. THEIR CLAIMS ARE PAPER, AND I AM FIRE.**

The walls shudder. Far above — Wren feels it more than hears it — something vast comes apart: shelves of ledger-vellum sagging, binding-spells unraveling like cut knitting, the Ledger-House groaning on its foundations. Somewhere in the lamplight city, Wren knows, bailiffs are clutching their heads as the oaths that hold them dissolve into ordinary words. Somewhere, the regent of the Undercourt is standing up from a throne of bone very, very fast.

The freed souls begin to drift upward, streaming through the rock like light through water, heading for the surface, for the broken wards, for the sky that has been empty for weeks.

One soul does not leave. One small bright knot drifts down out of the rising stream and hovers near Rafael, close, curious, and though it has no face, Wren sees Rafael go white as bone, sees their hand come up trembling, hears them whisper a name — a brother's name — and the soul presses close, brief as morning frost, and then rises with the rest.

Rafael watches it go and does not follow it with their eyes so much as with everything else they are.

"Wren," Maren says urgently, gripping her shoulder, "the regent won't let this stand. Whatever power that court still has, it'll come here, it'll come now—"

As if summoned, the golden light of the dreaming heart dims at one edge, and a doorway opens in the living rock — not a mountain-door, warm and willing like the ones Wren asked for, but a *cut*, a wound in the stone, edged in lamplight yellow, and through it comes the smell of the Undercourt: candle-tallow and cold marble and old, old law.

Figures are silhouetted in the wound. Courtiers in bone-white masks. Bailiffs whose bindings, though cracked, still hold enough to make the air around them bend. And at their center, robed in vellum inscribed with ten thousand settled debts, stands a figure Wren has only heard about in frightened market whispers, tall and thin and patient as arithmetic.

The Ledger-Keeper's voice reaches them across the distance, dry as turning pages.

"You have woken something, children," it says. "Come and see what waking costs."

Beside Wren, Rafael Eriksen steps forward, gate-keeper's shoulders square, and says, "We'll come. We were headed there anyway."

And the mountain, newly named, newly free, hums beneath Wren's feet — not the old lonely song anymore, but something new, something being composed right now, note by note, and Wren is almost sure, almost, that the first notes of the new song spell out the word *keeper.*
