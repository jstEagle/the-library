## 19. Stars Return

Dax Ferrow had spent his whole life learning what light meant.

To a smuggler, light was a liability. Light was a lantern swinging in a patrol's hand, a torch flaring on a ridge where no torch should be, the glint of a bailiff's blade in a lamplit market. Light was the thing that got you caught. He'd taught his crew to read darkness like other men read scripture — to know the weight of a shadow by its smell, to navigate black tunnels by the pull of air against his cheek. He'd made his living in the dark, and he'd told himself he preferred it that way. The dark was honest. The dark never pretended to be anything but what it was.

So when the first star came back, Dax did not feel joy. He felt exposed.

They were standing in the great fissure above the Undercourt — the crack in the world Wren had opened with a name spoken once and never again — and the mountain was still shuddering around them like a dog shaking off water. Dust rained down in silver threads through the shafts of lamplight that leaked up from the broken court below. Maren sat propped against a boulder with her wounded side bound in strips of Dax's own shirt, gray-faced but breathing, her sword across her knees out of habit rather than any expectation of use. Wren stood at the edge of the fissure with her head tipped back, humming, her small hands opening and closing like she was catching something falling from very high up.

And Rafael Eriksen stood at the center of it all, staring at their own name written in the split halves of the Ledger-Keeper's book, which lay open on the stone between them like a carcass.

The ledger had broken with a sound Dax would hear for the rest of his life. Not a tear, not a snap — a *crack*, deep and structural, the sound of something that had held for a thousand years deciding, all at once, to stop. And then the souls had come out.

They were coming out still.

Dax felt them before he saw them — a pressure change, like weather rolling in, like the moment before lightning when your teeth ache and the hairs on your arms stand up. Then the first of them rose through the fissure floor: a pale ribbon of light no thicker than smoke, rising without hurry, as if it had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Then another. Then ten. Then hundreds, thousands, streaming up out of the shattered court like a river reversing itself, pouring upward through the mountain's throat toward the sky.

"Stars," Wren said. She said it the way other children said *look, a butterfly*. "The stars are coming home."

Dax looked up through the fissure, up through the miles of stone the mountain had cracked open for them, and saw the first one arrive.

It happened at the horizon first — a single point of white kindling above the Thornfell peaks, so sudden and so bright that it threw shadows. Then another beside it. Then a handful, then a scatter, then a flood, stars reigniting across the whole bowl of the night like embers breathed back to life, until the sky over the mountains was so thick with them it looked less like a sky than like the inside of a jewel.

Every soul in the party stopped moving. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

"Well," Dax said, because someone had to say something, and silence had never been his strong suit. "That's going to make navigation easier."

Maren laughed — a short, cracked sound, half pain, half wonder. "You absolute wretch. The dead are free and you're thinking about bearings."

"I'm always thinking about bearings. It's why I'm alive." But his voice came out rougher than he intended, and he turned away to hide it, and that was when he saw Rafael's face.

Rafael hadn't looked up at the stars at all.

* * *

Dax had known Rafael Eriksen for eleven years, and in all that time he had catalogued exactly three expressions on that careful gate-keeper's face: duty, grief, and duty wearing grief's clothes. He'd seen them weep exactly once, in a snowdrift above the Ferrow crossing, holding a boy who wasn't going to make it, and even then they'd done it quietly, like a person paying a bill.

What was on Rafael's face now was none of those things. It was nakeder than any of them. It was the face of a person watching twenty years of arithmetic come due.

Because the souls were streaming past them — up through the fissure, past the ledge where they stood, close enough to touch — and each one carried a flicker of what it had been. Dax could see them if he didn't look directly: faces in the ribbons of light, glimpsed and gone. A woman with river-gray eyes. A soldier still wearing the shape of his shield. A child, small and quick, chasing another child-shaped light in circles as they rose. They weren't screaming. That was the thing Dax had braced for and hadn't needed to. They rose the way sleepers rise from a long dream, dazed and gentle, shedding the court's collar as they went, and some of them paused — just for a heartbeat — as if noticing the four small figures below, the ones who had broken the book that held them.

One of them reached down. Just one. A ribbon of light bent like a willow branch, and something that might have been fingers brushed Wren's hair as it passed, and Wren giggled, and the light spiraled upward faster after that, bright as laughter.

But Rafael wasn't watching any of them. Rafael was searching. Dax could see it in the set of their shoulders — that terrible, specific scanning, the look of a person reading a crowd for one face. Twenty years of keeping a gate over a brother's grave, and now the grave had opened, and every soul in the mountain was walking out of it except, perhaps, the one that mattered.

"He'll come," Wren said. She said it matter-of-factly, the way she said everything, her eyes still on the sky. "He's slow because he's heavy."

"Heavy?" Maren asked softly.

Wren nodded. "He carried the wards a long time. Carrying makes you heavy." She frowned, listening to something far away. "He's putting it down now. That's why the stars are coming back one at a time instead of all together. He's handing them out himself. He wants to be sure each one lights."

Dax felt something tighten in his chest. He thought about that — the stolen soul of a boy dead twenty years, spent like coin to hold a border together, and even now, even freed, choosing to climb slowly, choosing to light each star with his own hands rather than let them sputter back unattended. Some people got robbed of everything, Dax thought, and stayed careful anyway. Stayed kind anyway. It was the most obscene thing he'd ever heard, and also the most decent, and he couldn't decide whether it made him want to weep or break something.

Then the stream of souls thinned. Then it slowed. And then, at the back of it, moving against the current of all that rushing light like a man wading upstream, came one soul that moved differently from the rest.

It was dimmer. That was the first thing. All the others blazed with the raw white of released things; this one burned low and steady, banked like a coal, like a lamp kept trimmed in a window for twenty years. It descended rather than rose — drifted down through the fissure, unhurried, past the ledges, past the broken machinery of the court below, until it hung in the air an arm's length from Rafael Eriksen.

Up close, Dax could almost see him. A young man's outline, tall the way Rafael was tall, with the same stubborn set to the shoulders. His edges kept trying to resolve into a face and failing, the way a name does when you haven't spoken it in years.

"Tam," Rafael whispered.

The word came out of them like something that had been lodged in their throat for two decades. Dax watched Rafael's hands open at their sides — the hands of a gate-keeper, scarred and steady, hands that had turned a thousand keys and checked a thousand seals — and watched them shake for the first time in all the years Dax had known them.

The soul-echo drifted closer. When it spoke, its voice was like wind through a gate-lattice, thin and many-noted. *"I know you."*

"It's me." Rafael took a step forward, then stopped themselves, as if afraid of frightening it. "Tam. It's Rafe. I kept the gate. I kept it the whole time, I never—"

*"I remember the gate."* The echo tilted, considering. *"I don't remember much else. There was a war, and then there was a room, and then there was the holding of it. The wards were cold and I was warm inside them. I thought — for a long time I thought the wards were you."*

Dax heard Maren's breath catch. Beside him, Wren had gone very still, her humming stopped, her whole small body attentive as a bird's.

"I'm sorry," Rafael said. The words tore coming out. "I'm sorry it was you. I'm sorry I didn't know. Twenty years I stood on top of you and called it duty, I lit my lamp over your head and slept soundly, I—"

*"You kept it well."* The echo's voice gentled. *"I could feel that. Whoever held the gate held it like a promise. It was the only warm thing in the dark. I used to press myself against it, in the holding. I used to pretend it was home."*

Rafael made a sound Dax had never heard from them, and hoped never to hear again — a small, crushed noise, grief with nowhere left to go, because the person it had been aimed at for twenty years was standing in front of them and was already mostly gone.

"You can go now," Rafael said. "Do you understand? The ledger's broken. No one owns you. You don't have to hold anything ever again. You can go wherever souls go, and I'll keep the gate alone, and I'll be glad, I'll be *glad*—"

*"No."*

The word landed like a dropped stone.

*"Not yet,"* the echo amended, softer. *"There's a thing I have to finish first."* It drifted past Rafael — past all of them — and rose a little way up the fissure, and turned, and looked at the sky. At the last dark patch of it. High above the Thornfell peaks, one region of the heavens still waited, starless, a hole in the rebuilt constellation shaped exactly like the ward-line over the pass.

"The northern reach," Rafael breathed. "The old ward-anchor. It needs—" They stopped. Dax watched them understand, watched the understanding land like a blow. "It needs a soul to light it. That was the design. That was always the design. One soul per anchor, burning forever."

*"Yes,"* the echo said. *"And I'm the only one left who knows how."* It turned back to Rafael, and though it had no face, Dax would have sworn it smiled. *"Don't look like that. It isn't the same thing. Do you see? Before, I was taken. I was sold, and spent, and held. This time I'm choosing. There's a difference. The whole mountain knows there's a difference now — it's the difference your name bought."*

"There has to be another way," Maren said hoarsely, hauling herself upright against the boulder. "Knight's word on it, there's always another way. Let me—"

*"You're wounded and weighted,"* the echo said, not unkindly. *"And it wouldn't take you. The anchors know their own."* It drifted back toward Rafael, close enough now that its light washed over their face, and Dax saw that Rafael was crying without any attempt at quiet, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on their cheeks, and making no move to hide it.

"Twenty years," Rafael whispered. "I lit the lamp every night because I thought — I don't know what I thought. That you'd see it. That somehow you'd know I was still keeping watch."

*"I knew."* The echo raised what might have been a hand. *"Every night, I knew. It was the best part of the holding. Whatever else they took from me, gate-keeper, they never took that."*

And then it touched Rafael's cheek.

Dax had expected cold. What he saw instead, in the way Rafael's whole body eased — shoulders dropping, breath releasing, twenty years of clenched vigilance letting go all at once — was something else entirely. Morning frost, Wren would call it later, and she'd be right: that brief, bright, melting chill of first light on a winter field, cold enough to wake you and gentle enough not to hurt. The touch of a thing saying *I was here, I am going, and both of those are all right.*

"Go," Rafael said. Barely a sound. "Finish it. And then go on, Tam. Go wherever it's warm. Don't wait for me. Don't hold anything for me. Promise me."

*"Gate-keeper,"* the echo said, and its voice was fraying now, stretching thin as it gathered itself for the climb, *"I was never the one holding on."*

Then it rose.

It climbed the way Wren had said it would — slowly, heavily, stopping at each dark point along the old ward-line, pressing itself against the empty anchor-points one by one. Where it touched, stars kindled. Dax counted them without meaning to, smuggler's habit, marking distances: seven stars, then twelve, then the whole northern arc blazing back into being, until only one gap remained, high and solitary, the keystone of the sky.

The soul-echo hung beneath it a long moment. Small and banked-coal dim against all that fresh fire.

Then it spread itself wide — wider than a man, wider than a banner, a slow unfurling like wings or like arms opening — and pressed itself into the last anchor, and the final star ignited, and the sky over Thornfell was whole.

For a heartbeat, Dax could still see him there, woven into the starlight: a tall outline with stubborn shoulders, looking down.

Then morning frost off a winter field, one last time, drifting down through the fissure to touch Rafael's upturned face —

— and gone.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The mountain ticked and settled around them, stones finding new places to rest. Below, the broken court was quiet in a way it had probably never been quiet; Dax could hear, faintly, the sound of the soulless servants of the deep weeping and laughing in the same breath, wandering out of their collars into the lamplight, unsure what to do with hands that belonged to themselves.

Maren broke first. She slid down the boulder onto her knees, wound and all, and pressed her forehead to the stone, and Dax realized with a jolt that the oathbreaker knight was praying — really praying, for the first time since her oath was stripped from her, to whatever listened under a mended sky.

Wren walked to the fissure's edge and hummed, soft and low, a tune Dax now recognized as the mountain's own — except it had changed. The counting-song was gone. What she hummed now sounded, impossibly, like a lullaby. Like a song sung *to* the mountain, by the mountain, for the soul that had just finished its work.

And Rafael Eriksen stood alone in the middle of it, face wet, hands empty, looking up at a star that had been their brother.

Dax Ferrow had owed this person a blood debt for eleven years and had hated every minute of carrying it, because debts were chains and chains were for other people. He understood now, watching Rafael grieve with nothing left to grieve *for* — grief finally allowed to end, the funeral twenty years late and worth every day of the wait — that the debt had died somewhere in the dark below, and what remained was something he'd never had a word for.

Family, maybe. He'd try the word out later, when no one could see his face.

He crossed the fissure floor and stood beside Rafael, and said nothing, because there was nothing a smuggler could say that the sky hadn't already said better. After a while, Rafael's hand found his shoulder and gripped it, hard, and neither of them mentioned it.

It was Wren who broke the silence — Wren, whose head had come up sharply, whose humming had stopped mid-note, whose small face had gone strange in the starlight.

"Rafe," she said. "The mountain's talking again."

Rafael turned. "What's it saying?"

Wren's brow furrowed. She listened the way she listened, head cocked, whole body an ear. And whatever she heard made her take a step backward, and grab Maren's uninjured arm, and look up at Rafael with an expression Dax had never seen on a child before — awe and alarm in equal measure, the look of someone handed a message too large for their hands.

"It's saying thank you," she said. "And it's saying — it's asking—" She swallowed. "Rafe, it's asking for your name. Not the one in the ledger." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "Your real one. It says it wants to give something back."
