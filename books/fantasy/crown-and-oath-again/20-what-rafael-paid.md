## 20. What Rafael Paid

The mountain dreams in colors that are not colors.

Wren sits cross-legged in the hollow where the dreaming heart still beats slow and warm beneath the stone, and she listens. The mountain's dream has changed since it woke — since she said its true name aloud in a voice so small it should not have carried, and yet it carried all the way down to the roots of the world. Now the mountain does not sleepwalk anymore. It dreams on purpose. And its dream is full of souls.

She can see them going up. Little bright threads, thousands of them, streaming out of the broken ledger like smoke from a snuffed candle, rising through the dark veins of the rock toward the surface, toward the wards, toward the sky where the stars are coming back one by one like lamps being lit by someone walking down a long road.

Most of the threads are strangers. She doesn't know their names. But the mountain knows, and the mountain tells her, because she asked it to, and because it likes her. She is the one who woke it, after all. You don't forget the person who says your name for the first time in a thousand years.

*Ilse Marrow,* hums the mountain as a thread goes past, *shepherd, died of fever, sixty-one years kept.*
*Brandt Odo, ferryman, drowned, forty years kept.*
*Little Sefa, little Sefa, died too young, too young, going home, going home.*

Wren hums along. It helps them go faster, she thinks. Names are heavy things when you've been carrying them in a cage for half a century, and someone singing yours back to you makes them lighter.

Then she feels it.

A thread goes by that isn't humming.

It passes through her like cold water through cupped hands, and where it touches her, something *goes away*. Not pain. Pain would be easier. It's more like reaching into your pocket for a stone you've carried so long you forgot which pocket, except the pocket is your head and the stone is —

Wren gasps.

*Rafael Eriksen.*

The name is still there. Barely. Like a word written in breath on cold glass. She can see the shape of it, gate-keeper, keeper-of-Thornfell, brother, the person who held her hand in the lamplight markets when the bailiffs came, who said *stay behind me* in a voice like iron even though Rafael was afraid too, Wren could hear it, everyone is always afraid, but Rafael's fear was shaped like a door and they stood in front of hers.

And now the ledger is eating them.

Because that's what it's doing. Wren understands it the way she understands the mountain's dreams, without learning, without trying. When Rafael wrote their own name into the ledger — not as payment, Maren keeps saying, not as payment, a *claim*, keepers outrank debtors under the old law — they didn't just break the book. They put themselves into the scales. And the ledger, old and cruel and dying, is doing what ledgers do. It is balancing itself against Rafael's name. Erasing it line by line. Taking the payment anyway, out of spite, out of the last cruelty left in a thing built entirely of cruelty.

Out there in the great hall, she can hear the Ledger-Keeper's voice cracking like ice on a pond. The book is split down its spine and it will not stop splitting. Every soul it ever held is pouring out through the crack, and the Ledger-Keeper is screaming about order, about law, about the weight of things, and no one is listening, because the mountain has woken up and the mountain's law is bigger.

But names. Names are different.

The mountain can free every soul in the deep. It cannot hold a name together once the ledger has decided to unmake it. That work belongs to memory. To people. And Rafael's people are few, and far, and some of them are already forgetting.

Wren stands up so fast her knees crack.

She has to hurry. She doesn't know how she knows, but she knows: there isn't much of Rafael left to save.

* * *

She runs through the halls of bone and lamplight, bare feet slapping stone, and what she finds in the great hall stops her like a hand on her chest.

Dax Ferrow is standing over the shattered ledger with his crew behind him, and his face is wet, and he is saying the same words over and over — *Eriksen, Rafael Eriksen, gate-keeper of Thornfell, owes me nothing, I owe them, I owe them, say it back to me* — and his smugglers are saying it back, raggedly, like soldiers reciting an oath they never trained for. Because Dax figured it out first. Of course he did. Smugglers know better than anyone how debts work, and Dax knows that a name forgotten is a debt collected forever.

Maren Kael is on her knees beside Rafael.

Rafael is lying very still on the steps below the broken ledger-dais, and they look *thinner*. Not smaller. Thinner. As if someone were peeling pages off them, one at a time. Their eyes are open. Their mouth moves sometimes, forming words that come out as nothing, as gaps in the air. Wren watches Ser Maren lean close and listen to the silence and then answer it anyway, answering nothing with everything, telling Rafael about the stars coming back, about the wards filling, about the frost-touch of a soul that chose to pass on instead of stay — and Wren realizes with a lurch that Maren is narrating the world to Rafael because soon Rafael will not be able to hear it.

"Where's Wren?" Dax says suddenly. "Someone get Wren—"

"I'm here," she says. "I know what to do."

They all look at her. Maren's face is gray with exhaustion and blood-loss and hope, three things mixed together like paint. "Wren, love, if there's anything—"

"The mountain remembers everything," Wren says. "That's what remembering *is*, underneath. The mountain dreamed every soul it ever held, every night, for a thousand years. That's why they didn't fade down here, even caged. The dream kept them." Her voice comes out steady, which surprises her. Her voice often surprises her. "So I'm going to sing Rafael's name into the dream. All of it. Everything I know. And then the mountain will remember, and mountains don't forget."

The Ledger-Keeper lifts its head from the ruin of its book. Its face is long and gray and cracked down the middle now, mirroring the ledger, and its voice comes out like paper tearing. "You cannot," it says. "The balance is struck. The claim was made. What the ledger takes, the ledger *keeps*. This is law older than your mountain, child. Older than your kind."

Wren looks at it without blinking. She learned that trick from Rafael, actually. Rafael could stare down bailiffs. Wren figures a dead bookkeeper with a cracked face isn't much worse.

"You're forgetting something," she says.

"What?"

"The mountain woke up." She smiles at it, small and sharp. "Your law needed everyone asleep. Nobody agreed to that anymore."

And she kneels down next to Rafael, and she puts both her hands on the stone floor, and she closes her eyes, and she begins.

* * *

Singing into the mountain's dream is like diving into warm black water.

Wren lets herself fall. The dream receives her the way the sea receives rain — everywhere at once, without effort. Down here she is not small. Down here she is a note, and the dream is a song ten thousand years long, and all she has to do is add her note and hold it.

She starts with the easy parts.

*Rafael Eriksen. Gate-keeper of Thornfell Pass.* The words go into the dream and the dream gathers around them, curious, tasting them. *Keeper of the high road. Keeper of the lamp. Twenty years of winters walked the wall in wind and sleet, checking the ward-stones, feeding the fire, logging every traveler honest and dishonest, knowing every smuggler's path better than the smugglers did.*

She feels the dream begin to hum approval. Good. Keep going. Deeper.

*A brother. They had a brother. His name was* — and here Wren falters, because she never knew the brother's name, nobody said it in front of her, it was a wound too fresh-shaped even after twenty years — and then the mountain *helps* her. The dream reaches back into its own vast memory, to a soul it held for twenty years in a servant's collar, and it gives her the name like handing her a lantern.

*Tomas. Tomas Eriksen.* And Rafael grieved him for twenty years, quietly, the way grief gets done when there's a gate to keep and no time given for crying. Rafael learned the shape of every smuggler's lie because Tomas died on a road that shouldn't have been open, and Rafael swore no other road under their watch would fail anybody's brother.

The dream is weeping now. Mountains don't have faces but they have weather, and somewhere above, in the waking world, Wren knows it is beginning to snow softly inside the great hall, warm snow, impossible snow, the mountain's version of tears.

She sings the small things too, because the small things are the load-bearing walls of a person. *Rafael took tea bitter, no honey, and complained about it every morning like a ritual. Rafael talked to the gate-lamp. Rafael counted the stars every night before bed — every single night, twenty years, and on the night the stars went out Rafael stood in the dark and counted nothing and understood exactly what had been stolen, because Rafael had memorized the sky the way other people memorize scripture.*

*Rafael was lonely,* Wren sings, because it's true and because leaving it out would be a smaller kind of forgetting. *Lonely like a lamp is lonely. Burning anyway. On purpose. For whoever might need the light.*

And the dream wraps all of it up, every thread of it, and holds it close, and Wren feels the moment it becomes permanent — feels the mountain take Rafael Eriksen into itself the way it once took the stars into its wards, not as payment, not as property, but as *something kept because keeping is holy*.

Then she opens her eyes, and looks at the ledger's remnant, and waits.

* * *

The Ledger-Keeper is staring at her with its one remaining whole eye, and its expression has gone strange.

"It cannot be," it says. "Memory is not law. Memory has no standing in the—"

"Wrong," says a new voice, rough and dry as gravel, and the floor itself seems to speak it, because the floor *is* speaking it, because the mountain is done letting other people do the talking. The whole hall shudders gently, dust sifting from the bone rafters. "Memory is the oldest law there is, little clerk. Before your ledgers. Before courts. Before crowns. Everything you ever wrote down was only an attempt to do badly what remembering does perfectly." A pause, tectonic and amused. "I remember Rafael Eriksen. Completely. Therefore Rafael Eriksen exists. Your book disagrees? Your book is *broken*."

The ledger's last fragment crumbles to gray ash on the dais. The Ledger-Keeper makes a sound like a closing door and folds down into itself, smaller and smaller, until all that remains is a thin gray thread of a soul — its own, Wren realizes, the only soul it ever owned — which hesitates, and then rises, and follows all the others up toward the light. Even the jailer gets to go home. Wren thinks that might be the most important thing that happened today, and today included the breaking of reality's cruelest book.

At the foot of the dais, Rafael stirs.

It's slow. Like ice melting. First the fingers, curling against the stone. Then breath, deep and real. Then the eyes focusing, finding Maren's face, finding Dax's, finding — last, longest — Wren's.

"Wren," Rafael says. The name comes out hoarse and wondering, like they're testing whether it works. "*Wren.*"

"That's me," she says. Her voice wobbles now. Now that it's safe to wobble. "You know my name."

"I know—" Rafael pushes themselves up on their elbows, staring at their own hands, turning them over, counting fingers like a man checking his pockets after a fall. "I know everything. I thought — it was going quiet, Maren, it was going so quiet, I couldn't find the words, I could feel them being taken, and then—" They look up at the ceiling, at the warm snow falling in the lamplight. "Then something enormous picked me up."

"The mountain," Wren says. "It knows your name now. Forever. I gave it to them." She shrugs, suddenly shy, suddenly seven years old again. "All the parts. Even the tea part."

Rafael laughs. It cracks in the middle and turns into something else, something wetter, and Maren gathers them in, and Dax pretends very hard to inspect his boots while his crew fails equally hard at pretending not to cry, and the warm snow falls on all of them like a blessing that got lost on its way to a church and found a better place instead.

"You gave my name to a mountain," Rafael says into Wren's hair.

"You'd have done it for me," Wren says. "You did do it for me. In the market. *Stay behind me.*" She pulls back and looks at them, and she says the truest thing she knows: "Names are how we hold each other. So I held on."

* * *

Later — hours later, or minutes; time is soft in the deep places now — they climb together toward the surface, toward Thornfell, toward the gate.

Wren walks in the middle of the group, where Dax put her and where she intends to remain indefinitely. Maren limps on Rafael's arm and refuses, with tremendous dignity, to admit she needs it. Above them, through the mountain's opened veins, Wren can see patches of actual night sky, and the stars are *blazing*, all of them, the whole scattered family of them, lit like the mountain lit its own memory.

The mountain walks with them, in its way. It hums the whole road up. And near the top, as the air turns cold and clean and pine-scented, its hum changes, and Wren hears what it's carrying and grabs Rafael's sleeve.

"Listen," she whispers.

The mountain is singing a name. Not hers this time. Not the thousand freed souls.

*Rafael Eriksen,* the stone murmurs, over and over, gentle as falling snow. *Rafael Eriksen. My keeper. My keeper. My keeper.*

And ahead, at the end of the tunnel, there is a square of starlight shaped like a doorway — and standing in it, silhouetted against the returning sky, is the outline of a gate.

Their gate. Open.

Waiting.

"Go on," Wren says, and pushes Rafael gently toward the light.
