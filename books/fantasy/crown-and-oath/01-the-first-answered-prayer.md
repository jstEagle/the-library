## 1. The First Answered Prayer

Greyhallow City woke the way it always did: to the smell of tallow and wet stone, to bells that counted nothing but hours, to the Wardens' boots on the cobblestones like a slow, patient heartbeat.

Odile Okafor had learned to wake with it. Seven years in the charm-market of Lowmarsh Row taught a body to move quietly, to smile correctly, to sell hope in small clay shapes without ever once selling magic. Their stall was the third from the fountain — close enough to catch foot traffic, far enough that no Warden bothered counting their wares twice. Little fired-clay birds for travelers who wanted safe roads. Knot-charms braided from river grass, "for luck," though luck was just another word for attention, and Odile sold attention by the handful.

"Charms against drowning," they called softly, holding up a smooth grey stone with a hole through its heart. "River-stones, blessed by nothing but the river itself."

It was even true. That was the trick of surviving in Greyhallow: never lie about magic, because the Wardens could smell a lie like blood in water. Simply tell the truth so plainly it sounded like nonsense. Blessed by nothing but the river. The customers laughed, bought two, felt clever and safe, and went home. Nobody wanted real miracles anyway. Real miracles had been outlawed forty years ago, back when the last god died screaming in the Worldspine's shadow and the Council declared the age of wonder closed for business.

Odile knew better than anyone how much of the old world still lay under the city's skin. They had mapped it, once. Every unmapped fold of the realms, every hidden spring and sleeping barrow, every place where a god might still be curled up like an ember in ash. Those maps were gone now — burned, mostly, in the years after the Edict. What remained lived folded into false bottoms and sewn hems, inked in a hand Odile no longer let themselves recognize as their own.

Because the gift was not gone. That was the terrible secret they carried beneath the charm-seller's smile. When Odile looked at a street, they still saw its bones — the older road beneath the newer one, the buried stream under the cobbles, the shape of what a place *wanted* to be. Cartography, before the Edict, had been a kind of listening. Now it was only a kind of danger. So every morning Odile bound their own eyes with habit and routine and sold stones with holes in them, and every night they dreamed in lines and elevations, waking with ink-stained fingers they could not explain.

The morning the god came back, it began like any other.

* * *

Fog off the Greywater had settled into the market by midmorning, thick enough that the lamplighters worked an extra hour. Odile was haggling with a ferryman over three knot-charms when they heard the child coughing.

Not an ordinary cough. Everyone on Lowmarsh Row knew that cough; it belonged to Tam, the rag-picker's daughter, eight years old and burning through her second winter fever. Her mother dragged her past the stalls most mornings because sitting still cost more warmth than walking. The child moved like a small grey ghost between the awnings, all elbows and fever-bright eyes.

This morning she stopped at the fountain.

Later, people would swear she simply knelt down at the dry basin — the fountain had been dry since the Edict, since the river beneath Greyhallow was judged too full of old divinity to be trusted — and put her cracked lips to the empty stone. And later still, when it became a story told in taverns with the shutters barred, they would say she prayed. Not to any sanctioned saint, not to any of the Council's permitted dead. She prayed the way children pray, without grammar or permission:

*"Please. I don't want to die. Please, whoever's left."*

Odile heard it. Everyone within ten feet heard it. And then the world did something it had not done in forty years.

It answered.

The sound came first — a low groan of stone, deep under the market, like a door that had been locked so long it had grown into its frame. Then the dry fountain wept. Water rose through cracks that had been sealed with lead and holy law, welling up black and clean and cold, and the fog itself seemed to lean toward the child like a curious animal.

What climbed out of the fountain was not large. That was the first thing Odile noticed, and it undid them more than anything else. The stories had promised gods like mountains, gods like storms. But this one stood waist-high to the ferryman, made of river-water and silt and the memory of reeds, its edges constantly dissolving and reforming, its face a suggestion rather than a fact. It looked *starved*. It looked like something that had been asleep under a city for forty years, dreaming thin.

It looked at Tam.

And Odile's blood caught fire.

There was no gentler way to describe it. Heat bloomed along their veins in branching patterns, exactly — *exactly* — like the tributaries on the old maps, the great river-trees they had once spent three seasons charting in the unmapped north. Light followed the heat. Faint gold-green light, visible through their own skin, tracing the same forked paths across their wrists, up their arms, across their chest. Odile dropped behind their stall as if ducking a thrown knife and pressed their sleeve hard against their forearm, but the light bled through the wool like dawn through a shutter.

In the false bottom of their cart, wrapped in oilcloth, the maps ignited.

Not burned — *lit*. Every line they had ever drawn woke at once, glowing through leather and wood, and Odile felt each one like a finger pressed to their skin. The buried stream beneath Lowmarsh Row. The drowned chapel in the Greywater. The sleeping places, all of them, all across the realms, stirring like beds of coals breathed upon.

*They know I'm here,* Odile thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *Whatever this is, it can see me. It has always been able to see me.*

At the fountain, the little god cupped its leaking hands around Tam's face. Water poured over her, into her mouth, through the fever. Where it touched her, her grey skin flushed pink. The cough broke apart mid-rasp. She gasped — a whole, deep, greedy breath, the breath of someone surfacing — and her mother screamed, and the market screamed, and somewhere down the row a Warden's whistle split the fog in half.

The god raised its head. Its face had no eyes, precisely, but Odile felt its attention land on them with the weight of a falling tree. It came toward the stall through the panicking crowd, and the crowd parted from it the way water parts from a keel, and Odile stood up because there was nowhere left in the world to hide.

"You," the god said. Its voice was the sound of a river deciding which way to go. "You kept the lines. All these years, you drew us, even when they burned the drawings. We felt every stroke like sunlight on closed eyelids."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Odile said. Their voice shook. Their arms blazed with light, and lying now felt obscene, a lie told directly to the thing that owned the rivers.

The god reached up — it had to reach *up*, and there was something almost funny and almost unbearable about that — and laid one wet finger against Odile's glowing wrist. Where it touched, the light steadied instead of flaring, the way a flame stops guttering when cupped.

"Covenant-blood," it said. "Heir-line of Okafor, mapwrights of the Worldspine. We thought your line ended. We hoped it hadn't. We have been praying too, little heir. For forty years, we have prayed to *you*."

The whistle came again, closer now. Boots. More than one pair. A voice shouting the word that emptied markets in Greyhallow faster than fire: *"Manifestation!"*

"The Edict stands," the god said, and there was something in its voice that Odile would spend many nights afterward trying to name — grief, maybe, or apology. "And so they will come for me, and then for you, and then for everyone your blood touches. Unless you run. Unless you run *now*, heir, and take an old, starving god with you, and finish what your family started."

Tam's mother was sobbing over her daughter, who sat up in the wet cobblestones breathing like a bellows, alive, looking around at the crowd with furious, ordinary confusion. Somewhere in that ordinary miracle was everything Odile had spent seven years burying.

Their hands, stained with light where ink used to be, would not stop trembling. It was not entirely fear. That was the worst part. Underneath the terror, in some cellar of themselves they had boarded shut long ago, something was unfolding like a map that had waited years to be opened again.

The first Warden rounded the corner, sword drawn, lantern high.

"Run," the river god said — *Weyl*, some distant, buried part of Odile supplied, the name arriving like water finding its level. "*Now.*"

Odile ran.
