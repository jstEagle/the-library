## 2. A Company of the Unwanted

The maps burned first.

Not all at once — Odile would have preferred that, a clean immolation, everything they had built in Greyhallow going up in one forgiving blaze. Instead the old charts ignited one by one over three sleepless nights, each sheet curling at its edges with a light like dawn seen through water, and each time the light touched their blood it sang along their veins in a language they had not spoken since childhood: *here, and here, and here.* The river god's name was stitched into them now. Weyl. Weyl, who had knelt in the market square with a dying child cradled in arms made of current and silt, and who had looked up at Odile across a crowd of screaming charm-sellers and said, in a voice that tasted of thaw:

*Heir.*

By the fourth night, the Wardens had come to Copperlane with their censers and their iron hooks, asking after the cartographer whose blood answered prayers. Odile left through the roof with a satchel of salvaged charts pressed to their chest like a newborn, and did not look back at the little shop with its harmless charms — the luck-knots and traveler's blessings that were only string and superstition, sold to people who needed to believe in something small.

They ran south along the canal, where the water moved strangely, eagerly, as if it knew them.

* * *

Sefa Vane had stopped believing in her own order somewhere around the third god-child.

That was the arithmetic she carried now, the ledger no pen could balance: two children she had stood aside for and let other Wardens do the burning, and one — small, feathered at the shoulders, laughing at her shadow — whom she had not been able to stand aside from at all. The order called it defection. The order was hunting her with the same diligence it hunted gods, which Sefa supposed was fair, since she was beginning to suspect there wasn't much difference between the categories anymore.

She found the cartographer by accident, or the cartographer found her; afterward neither of them could say which. They collided in the flooded undercroft beneath the tannery district, both fleeing the same cordon, and Odile's blood flared so bright at the sight of Warden-grey that Sefa saw it through their soaked shirt — veins lit gold-green like sunken treasure.

"Don't," Odile gasped, hands raised. "Whatever you're going to do, don't."

Sefa looked at them a long moment. Water dripped from the ceiling into standing pools that glowed faintly where the cartographer's reflection touched them. "I'm done doing," she said at last. "What I'm going to do is get you out of this city before Vane's old squad finds you. Because if they find you, they'll find me, and I am *tired*, mapmaker."

"You're a Warden."

"I was." She unhooked the censer from her belt and dropped it into the floodwater, where it hissed out like a dying animal. "Ask me again when we're past the walls."

* * *

Odile learned the shape of Sefa Vane slowly, the way they'd once learned coastlines — by sailing close and watching what broke against her.

She slept sitting upright, back to whatever wall was nearest, boots on. She ate like someone expecting the meal to be confiscated. And once, crossing a bridge outside Greyhallow's southern gate, she stopped dead at the sight of a child chasing a paper boat down the gutter, and her face did something complicated and grief-stricken before she shuttered it again.

"The third one," Odile said quietly, because they were a cartographer and they read terrain. "The one you couldn't burn."

"Feathered at the shoulders." Sefa didn't look at them. "Laughed at my shadow. Six years old." She started walking again. "The order says the children are vessels. That what answers in them isn't a soul, just a god wearing meat. I held that boy down while the censer worked, and he laughed at my shadow right up until he couldn't anymore." Her jaw worked. "So now I'm an unwanted too. Congratulations on your company."

"It's a terrible company," Odile agreed. "We have one member and a grudge."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped her, rusty as an unused hinge.

* * *

Bramble came to them rather than being found, which was, Bramble explained, the natural way of things when one was half-fae and had spent forty years dodging obligations on both sides of one's blood.

They met at a crossroads shrine — a squat stone thing older than the ban, its carved god worn faceless by weather — where Bramble sat cross-legged on the plinth eating stolen plums and waiting, apparently, with great patience.

"You're the heir," Bramble said. Not a question. They were small and fox-faced, with moth-dust grey hair and ink-stained fingers to the second knuckle. "Word travels strange these days. Down hedgerows and through well-water. The old roads waking up." They hopped off the plinth and circled Odile with frank, unsettling curiosity. "You smell like an atlas that fell in a river. Also, your blood is glowing. You should probably know that."

"It does that now."

"Mm. Terrible for stealth." Bramble produced, from somewhere in their layered rags, a leather folio and set it in Odile's arms with unexpected ceremony. Inside were pages — copies, in a dozen different hands spanning centuries — of fragments. Covenant fragments. "I've been collecting these since before you were born, heir-or-not. Half-fae means half-witnessed, in the old law. Nobody lets me sign anything. But I can *read* anything." Their eyes, amber as resin, glittered. "And I think it's finally time somebody did."

* * *

They decoded the covenant in the cellar of an abandoned mill three days south of the city, by lamplight and by the increasingly inconvenient glow of Odile's own forearms, which Bramble insisted on using as a reading light whenever the oil ran low.

"Three parties," Bramble said, tapping a fragment written in a hand so old the letters leaned like drunks. "Blood-heir. Witness. God — and note the wording, this is important — *willing* god. Assembled at the Worldspine before solstice, when the veil between realms wears thin enough to be sewn."

"Sewn with what?" Sefa asked from the doorway, arms crossed, ever the sentinel.

"With the heir's death, originally." Bramble said it lightly, the way one reports weather, then glanced up and moderated their tone. "Ah. Yes. You didn't know that. The original covenant sealed the realms together with the heir's life poured out at the Spine — blood for borders, death for duration. Every generation, a new heir, a new death. That's why the mortal side broke it. That's why magic got outlawed. Someone decided infinite murder was worse than a world gone quiet."

Odile sat very still. The lamplight trembled. Somewhere under their ribs, their blood pulsed its idiot golden rhythm, and for the first time they understood it not as a gift but as a countdown.

"And the god?" they managed. "Willing. What does willing cost?"

Bramble spread another fragment. "A diminished god, freely bound into the new seal. It's why Weyl answered your prayer instead of staying sunk in his riverbed, I'd wager. He felt you wake. He wants — " the fae hesitated, choosing honesty with visible effort — "he wants to matter again. Willing gods are rare as honest men. You may need to ask him what he's willing *for*."

* * *

Interlude — from the private papers of Sefa Vane, recovered later and preserved against her wishes:

Day nine out of Greyhallow. The mapmaker talks in their sleep in dead languages. The fae steals food but leaves exact change in buttons and promises, which is somehow worse. The river god is the worst company of the four of us and I include myself.

I keep waiting to regret the censer I drowned. I don't. That's the frightening part — how little regret there is, how much room it leaves for other things. I haven't had room since the boy. Room feels like a wound healing wrong. Room feels like it might be the beginning of something.

* * *

Weyl arrived with the rain.

He was smaller than he had been in the market square — Odile noticed immediately, the way a cartographer notices a coastline redrawn. In the square he had filled the plaza, water rising to meet him from every gutter and fountain. Now he came walking up the mill road in a body barely taller than Odile's, dressed in a fisherman's oilskins gone threadbare, his skin the grey-green of deep water and his eyes the flat silver of a sky about to break.

"They took my delta," he said, without greeting, dripping on the mill's threshold. "While I walked away from it. My sister-gods, my brother-storms. They dammed the Weyl-channel and told the water it belonged to them now. This is what the quiet centuries made of us: thieves in a house with no lights." He looked at Odile, and the silver eyes were ancient and furious and, underneath it all, unspeakably tired. "You are the heir. You carry my name in your blood and the covenant's terms in your satchel. So ask me, heir. Ask me the question everyone asks."

"What are you willing for?"

Weyl smiled, and it was not entirely bitter. "To be *asked*. Seven hundred years, and no one has asked." He stepped inside. "Now show me the fragments. I remember more of the old law than your moth-eaten scribe, and less than I wish."

* * *

It happened gradually, the way rivers cut valleys — no single moment you could point to, only the unmistakable fact of changed country afterward.

It was Sefa teaching Bramble to whittle, both of them swearing at the same knife. It was Bramble leaving a plum on Weyl's bedroll every morning until the god, wordlessly, began leaving river-pearls in exchange. It was Odile waking at midnight to find Sefa standing watch over their glowing arms with her sword drawn against nothing, guarding them from the dark out of pure habit, and neither of them mentioning it after.

It was the night the mill's broken sluice gate jammed and the cellar started flooding, and all four of them — god, ex-Warden, fae, heir — stood hip-deep in freezing water hauling on the same rusted lever, laughing breathlessly when it gave, and Odile realized with a lurch that they could not remember the last time they had laughed in front of another person. Seven years of harmless charms and hidden gifts, and it had taken a death sentence and a drowning cellar to make a home.

"We're not ready," Sefa said that night, sharpening her blade by firelight. "Solstice is eighteen days off. The Worldspine is a month's march. The Wardens are behind us, the gods are ahead of us, and the covenant wants your blood on an altar."

"Then we march tomorrow and solve it on the road," Odile said. "We're good at impossible. We've practiced."

But even as they said it, their blood flared — sudden, urgent, answering something far to the north — and Weyl rose from the fire with water standing in his eyes like held-back weather.

"That's not the Spine calling," the god said quietly. "That's my kin. They've taken something. They're demanding terms." His silver gaze fixed on Odile, ancient and afraid. "Heir. They've taken *me* before, and I walked free. This time, I think they mean to keep what they catch — unless you come and claim me first."

Outside, the rain turned suddenly, unmistakably, to falling light.
