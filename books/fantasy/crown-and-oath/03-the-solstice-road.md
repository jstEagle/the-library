## 3. The Solstice Road

The road north from Greyhallow was a scar of old empire, and it remembered being walked by gods.

Odile felt it through the soles of their boots — a hum in the packed earth, like a plucked string still ringing. Seven years they had walked roads without feeling anything but the ache of their own feet, and now the whole world had a pulse. It was worse than any silence.

Bramble rode ahead on a mule named Discretion, her half-fae ears swiveling like weathervanes. "The land's getting louder," she called back. "Every mile north, another octave."

"Then we walk quieter," Sefa said. She had not taken off her boots since Greyhallow, Odile noticed. Not once, even sleeping. As if at any moment she might need to run toward something instead of away.

Weyl did not complain about the pace, which frightened Odile more than anything. The river god traveled as an old man with waterlogged eyes, diminished to a trickle of what he'd been, and he kept his gaze fixed north like a man watching his own funeral procession.

"The other gods know what we carry," Weyl said that night, over a fire no larger than a cupped hand. "The covenant in your blood is a key, little heir. And every lock in the world wants a turn."

* * *

They crossed into the Thornfell highlands on the ninth day, where the road gave up pretending and became goat-track, and that was where the sky broke open.

Not with rain. With presence.

Odile knew it first as pressure behind the eyes, then as Bramble screaming a warning, then as the world simply *changing hands* — one moment standing in grey scree and wind, the next inside a hall built of frozen waterfall, its pillars cascading upward in slow motion, its ceiling alive with trapped lightning. The cold smelled of iron and altitude.

Three figures stood before them. They wore shapes borrowed from human dreams: a woman crowned with avalanche, a man whose skin was slate veined with gold, and something between them that was neither and both, shifting like weather deciding itself.

"The heir," said the crowned woman, and her voice fell like snow off a roof. "And the river-runt who ran from us when the binding broke."

"I didn't run," Weyl said, and for the first time since Odile had known him, his voice carried weight enough to bend the air. "I was *spilled*. There's a difference, sister. Ask your glaciers how much of them used to be me."

The slate-skinned god stepped forward. "Then hear the terms, small ones. The covenant will be reforged — that much is written, and even broken writing has teeth. But it must name a supreme signatory. Us. The high gods. The storm and stone and sky." His gold veins brightened. "Give us the heir's cooperation, and you may all live out your brief mortal lives in comfort. Refuse—"

"You'll do what?" Sefa's hand was already on her knife, which Odile privately thought was the funniest thing she'd ever seen her do. "You're negotiating with four people and a mule."

"We are negotiating," the third god said, its voice like weather deciding, "with time. Yours is short. The solstice does not wait."

And then the hall collapsed inward like a fist closing, and when Odile could see again, Weyl was gone.

Not dead — Odile would have known; some thread of the covenant-bound blood sang between them now, thin and constant as a plucked wire. But gone, pulled through whatever door the gods had opened, and the scree where he'd stood held only a puddle already freezing.

"They took him," Bramble whispered. Her scribe's fingers were shaking. "They took him to make him sign, or to make him *not* sign."

Sefa turned a slow circle, reading the horizon the way she'd once read battlefields. "There's a godhold in the Thornfells. Old Warden garrison, before my order went respectable. If they've caged him anywhere, it's there." She looked at Odile. "Two days to reach it. Four days after that to the Worldspine. The arithmetic is ugly."

Odile stared at the frozen puddle. Somewhere under that ice, metaphorically, was a bitter old river god who had called them *little heir* with something almost like tenderness, and who had taught them, in three weeks of grudging lessons, what their own blood was for.

"Then we don't go to the Worldspine yet," they heard themselves say. "We go get him first."

* * *

It was on the second night of the climb to the godhold, huddled in a shepherd's bothy with the wind trying to peel the roof off, that Bramble found the truth in the old texts — because of course she did, at midnight, by candlelight, with the particular grim joy of a scholar confirming bad news.

"Odile." Her voice was very careful. "You need to read this."

The original covenant, transcribed in a Warden primer Sefa had stolen from her former chapterhouse years ago and never been able to throw away. Odile read it twice. The words were simple. That was the cruelty of them.

*The covenant is sealed in the blood of the heir, freely given unto death, that god and mortal be bound forever.*

*Unto death.*

"Oh," Odile said. Just that. A small sound, like a stitch popping.

"It says *freely given*," Bramble said quickly. "Which means there might be—"

"There isn't always a loophole." Odile set the page down very gently, the way one sets down something that might go off. "That's what people who want loopholes tell themselves."

They walked out into the dark afterward without quite deciding to. The wind was enormous and full of ice crystals that glittered like the light their maps used to make. And Odile stood at the edge of the bothy's little circle of firelight and let the truth settle over them like snowfall:

Seven years hiding. Three weeks running. A child's prayer answered. A god's finger pointed at their chest. And at the end of it, a knife waiting at the Worldspine with their name on it — or rather, their *blood*, freely given, unto death, so that the realms could hold together and children's prayers could be answered by things kinder than chance.

They could stop. That was the terrible clarity of exhaustion: it showed you exits. Walk south. Find a new city, a new name, sell charms that did nothing. Let the gods fight over supremacy among themselves. Let the world stay broken but *intact*, the way it had been for seven years, the way broken things learned to be.

Their feet actually turned south. That was the worst part. Their body, worn down to wire and want, simply began to walk away.

A hand caught their sleeve.

Sefa. Of course. She hadn't slept either; none of them had, really, not since the sky opened. She looked at Odile with those flat grey hunter's eyes that had once sized up god-children for burning, and said nothing at all — just stood there holding their sleeve, breathing steam, waiting.

"I'm going back," Odile said. "To hide. To live. You should come with me. All of you. Let Weyl—" Their voice cracked on the god's name. "Let him sign whatever they put in front of him. He's a god. He'll survive it. I won't survive mine."

"No," Sefa said.

"No?"

"I burned a chapel in Vellmark because the order told me the child inside was half a god and therefore not a person." Her grip tightened. "I believed it for eleven years. Then I met a cartographer who bleeds light, and a scribe with fae blood, and a river god who complains about the food, and I understood that everything I burned was *this*. Exactly this. People who belong to each other." She let go of the sleeve only to plant herself squarely in Odile's path. "So no. We are not walking south. Because if you go, I go with you, and I have spent my whole life following orders, and I refuse to spend what's left of it following *that* one."

Behind her, wrapped in two blankets and radiating indignation, Bramble raised the covenant text like a torch. "Also — *listen* — the phrase is *freely given unto death*. In the oldest recension, 'death' in covenant-law doesn't mean cessation. It means *transformation*. The heir dies to what they were. It's in the marginalia, it's disputed, but it's THERE, and I am a scribe, and my whole miserable life has prepared me to argue marginalia with gods." Her chin trembled. "We are not letting you die. We are letting you *change*. There's a difference, and I intend to litigate it at the top of the world if necessary."

The wind howled. The candle in the bothy guttered. And Odile Okafor, who had spent seven years being no one, stood between a defected god-hunter and a furious half-fae scholar and felt something crack open in their chest that was not despair.

It was the other thing. The thing underneath.

"All right," they said, and their voice came out rougher than intended. "All right. The godhold first. Then the Worldspine. Then we argue marginalia with the divine."

* * *

The godhold squatted on its shoulder of mountain like a toad made of basalt, its walls furred with frost, its windows glowing with a light that was not firelight — too steady, too gold, the light of captive divinity.

They reached it at dusk on the solstice-eve-eve, bellies empty, Discretion the mule left tethered two miles back with a heartfelt apology. Sefa mapped the patrols with her old professional eye: Warden outriders on the lower wall, which meant her former order had arrived ahead of them. Which meant the rival gods and the Wardens were, at minimum, talking.

"Worse," Sefa murmured, glass to her eye. "Cooperating."

Through the great barred gate, down in the flooded cistern that had once been the garrison's water supply, something glimmered. A thread of blue-white light, pulsing slow and stubborn, like a heart refusing to quit.

Like a river that had been dammed but not dried.

"That's him," Odile breathed. Their blood answered the glow, singing along that thin bound wire, and their old maps — sewn into their coat lining, smuggled out of Greyhallow — warmed against their ribs like a living thing.

The gate's bars were old empire iron, but the hinges were newer, and newer meant mortal, and mortal meant Sefa's knife-work. Bramble had already inked a distraction-glamour onto scrap parchment, cursing under her breath in two languages.

Odile flexed their fingers and felt the covenant move in their blood like a tide coming in.

"Hold on, old man," they whispered to the light below. "We're coming to break the dam."

The moon rose over the Thornfells, one night short of full solstice, and Odile led their strange, stubborn family down into the dark.
