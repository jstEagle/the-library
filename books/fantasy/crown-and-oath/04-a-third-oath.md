## 4. A Third Oath

The Worldspine did not look like a mountain. It looked like a sentence that had been interrupted.

Odile felt it before they saw it — the way the road beneath their boots stopped being stone and started being *statement*, each step a syllable of something older than language. The peak rose above them in the last violet light of solstice eve, its flanks veined with channels that might have been rivers once, or script, or both. Snow clung to it like ash on a burnt page.

Bramble walked beside them with her scribe's satchel pressed to her chest as if it were an infant. "It's humming," she whispered. "Do you hear it? It's *humming*."

"I hear it," Sefa said from Odile's other side. Her voice was flat, but her hand had found her sword hilt and stayed there, the way it always did when she was afraid and would not admit it. Seven years hunting gods had taught her that the loudest things were never the dangerous ones. "Save your wonder for after we survive."

Weyl came last, and slowest.

They had cut him out of the rival gods' godhold two nights ago — Sefa's blade through the chains of oath-iron, Bramble's stolen keys, Odile's blood singing so loudly through the walls that every lock had simply forgotten how to be closed. But freedom had not restored him. The river god who had burst into Greyhallow's market square like a flood given a face was now a thin figure in a threadbare coat, his skin the grey-green of water starved of current, his eyes holding the particular exhaustion of something very old that has been told one too many times what it is worth.

"The terms," Weyl said, when they made camp below the final ascent. He spoke to the fire rather than to any of them. "You should hear them properly, cartographer, before you climb. Not the version the old texts softened."

Odile looked up from the map they were not drawing — could not draw; their gift had been screaming since the godhold, ink crawling unbidden across every surface they touched. "Tell me."

"The covenant is a door," Weyl said, "and doors swing both ways. When it was forged, the heir's blood was the hinge. Not spilled over the stone — *spent*. All of it. The first heir gave everything she was so that gods and mortals could speak without devouring each other. That was the price of law." He finally turned his eyes on them, and there was something in them that might have been shame. "I told you I was diminished. Now you know why the covenant chose me to find you. A broken god asks less of a broken heir. We were meant to fail cheaply."

Sefa stood up so fast the fire scattered sparks. "And you waited until *now*—"

"She deserved the whole truth," Weyl said. "All of them do. That's the least the old ones owe."

Bramble had gone very still, quill hovering. "Then what happens at midnight if Odile refuses?"

"Nothing," Weyl said. "That is precisely the horror of it. Nothing answers prayers again. Nothing manifests in market squares to save dying children. The realms drift apart like continents, and everyone pretends the silence is peace." His mouth twisted. "Or the assembled gods take matters into their own hands, and one of *them* becomes the hinge, and mortals learn what divinity looks like when it stops pretending to need us."

Odile closed their eyes.

They thought of the child in the market square, seven years ago and three days ago all at once — the small body going cold while its mother prayed to a name no priest had spoken in a generation, and the river rising out of the cobbles because *someone finally asked*. They thought of their own blood igniting along veins mapped like tributaries. They thought of Greyhallow's charm-sellers, whose harmless trinkets had been the only honest magic left in the city, and of the Wardens' pyres, and of Sefa walking away from all of it with a god-child's name carved into her conscience instead of her report.

"Then we write something new," Odile said.

* * *

The summit of the Worldspine was a bowl of black glass, and it was full of gods.

They had gathered the way weather gathers — some vast and patient as pressure fronts, some small and sharp as hail, some barely visible except where they bent the starlight. Odile counted forty before counting became meaningless. At the bowl's center stood the shattered remnant of the First Altar, and across it, in characters that predated every alphabet Odile had ever charted, the original covenant lay written in a brown-black line they understood, with a lurch, was blood. Dried. Ancient. Waiting for more.

A voice came from everywhere. **THE HEIR ARRIVES.**

"I arrive," Odile said. Their voice cracked on the second word, and they let it. Let these ancient things see a tired person with ink-stained fingers and frostbitten ears. "And before anything else happens, I'd like to read the contract I'm apparently inheriting."

Silence — then something like amusement rippling through the assembly, cold and vast. **YOU HAVE READ IT. YOU KNOW ITS TERMS.**

"I know its terms." Odile stepped onto the altar, and their blood rose to meet the old writing, vein to vein, map to territory. "An heir spends their whole life. A god lends their whole name. A witness burns their whole memory of the event. Three whole things, poured into a hinge, forever." They looked up at the gathered immensities. "You know what I did for seven years? I sold charms. Little brass fish, knotted red thread, nothing. Because magic was outlawed, and I hid, and I hated hiding, and I *still* think those charms were more honest than this." They laid their palm flat against the covenant. "This isn't a covenant. It's a mortgage paid by children."

**IT IS ORDER,** said the voice, and now there was no amusement in it at all.

"It's fear," said Sefa.

She had followed him onto the altar — of course she had; Odile should have known she would — and she stood there in her defector's coat with her hand still on her sword, facing down forty gods with the expression of a woman reading someone the riot act. "Seven years I burned shrines for your silence, and you know what I learned? Gods don't keep order. People keep order. Gods keep *grudges*, and *worship*, and sometimes — if you're lucky — promises." She pulled her hand from the hilt and held it up, empty. "I'll witness. That part of me you can have. My memory of tonight, my certainty, my proof it happened. Take it gladly. But nobody's spending a life on your hinge. Not hers. Find another way or watch us walk down the mountain and let the silence have you."

**AND WHAT WAY,** the voice thundered, **WOULD YOU PROPOSE?**

Odile had been waiting for exactly that question since the fire.

"Bramble," they said. "Now."

The half-fae scribe climbed the altar steps two at a time and opened her satchel with trembling, reverent hands. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was the thing they had carried from Greyhallow: Odile's oldest surviving map, the one drawn before the ban, before the hiding — the map of the Worldspine itself, inked when Odile was nineteen and certain the world wanted knowing. Except it wasn't blank anymore. Since the godhold, it had been filling itself in, lines crawling across the parchment in Odile's own hand though they had never touched pen to it, drafting and redrafting, searching for the right words.

Living ink. Blood's cousin. Covenant's raw material.

"A covenant needs three things," Odile said, taking the map. "Hinge, name, witness. You wrote it that way because you could only imagine binding as *spending*. But I'm a cartographer. Do you know what a map actually is?" They held the parchment high, and the living ink caught the starlight. "It's an agreement. Between the land and the eye. The land agrees to be *there*; the mapmaker agrees to tell the truth about it. Neither is consumed. Both are bound. Every map ever drawn is a tiny covenant, and none of them required a death."

**WORDS ON PARCHMENT CANNOT HOLD REALMS TOGETHER.**

"No," Odile agreed. "But obligation can. So here are my terms." And they read aloud from the self-writing map, their voice steadying with every line: "*Let the realms be joined not by spent blood but by mutual debt. Each realm owes the others its care; each may call upon the others in its need. Gods answer prayers not as owners but as neighbors. Mortals keep shrines not as tribute but as welcome. No heir dies. No god is supreme. Any party may renegotiate at each solstice, in good faith, at this altar.*" They lowered the map. "Sign it, and the door swings open forever. Refuse, and enjoy your silence."

The mountain held its breath.

Then Weyl stepped forward — thin, grey, diminished Weyl, who had been dragged here in chains and freed by thieves and owed nothing to anyone. "I sign," he said, and pressed his hand to the map, and where he touched it the ink ran silver-blue like water finding its channel again. Color flooded back into him from the fingertips upward. "First signature. Willing. *Witnessed.*"

"I witness," Sefa said, and the altar took it — Odile saw the moment leave her eyes, saw her blink and frown slightly, already unable to say what she had just done, only that she had done it gladly.

And Odile Okafor pressed their own palm to the parchment and did not bleed, and did not burn, and did not spend anything except the last of their hiding.

The covenant ignited — not the old brown-black line but the whole summit, the black glass flooding with light, the interrupted sentence of the Worldspine finishing itself at last in a voice like ten thousand pages turning. Far below and far away, in a city of charm-sellers and god-hunters, every Warden's brand went dark, every outlawed prayer found its listener, and Greyhallow's ban shattered like ice on a spring river.

When the light faded, the gods were gone — not vanished, Odile understood, just *elsewhere*: next door now, reachable, neighborly. The stars came back out. Bramble was crying into her satchel. Sefa was staring at her own hands, trying to remember something wonderful.

And Weyl — tall again, green again, laughing — caught Odile as their knees buckled and held them up like a bridge holds a traveler.

"What now, cartographer?" the river god asked. "The world's remade. Where will you map first?"

Odile looked down the mountain toward the distant lights of home — the real one, the one they'd built out of strays and stubbornness — and smiled through the frost and the tears.

"Nowhere," they said. "For once, I already know the way."

* * *

They came down from the Worldspine at dawn, four travelers on a road that hummed faintly underfoot, and found the first shrine already lit — some farmer's humble offering, a candle and a cup of well-water, answered before they passed it by a warmth in the air and a smell like rain. Word would reach Greyhallow within the week. Charm-sellers would become charter-makers. The Wardens' hall would stand empty, or become a school.

At the crossroads below the mountain, where the signpost pointed three ways and all of them led somewhere worth going, Sefa paused — frowning at her empty memory, at the map in Odile's arms where fresh lines were already appearing in a handwriting she almost recognized.

"There's a new road," she said slowly. "On your map. One that isn't on the land."

Odile looked. And there was — a thin silver line, running off the parchment's edge entirely, into the margin, into the unknown country past the edge of everything, and beside it, in their own hand, words they had not written:

*To be surveyed.*

"Well," said Odile Okafor, heiring nothing, hiding nowhere, home already, "we'd better pack."
