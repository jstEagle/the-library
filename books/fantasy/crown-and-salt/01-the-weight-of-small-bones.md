## 1. The Weight of Small Bones

The night market of Saltmere smelled of tar and frying eel and the particular sourness of a city that had learned to keep its secrets behind its teeth. Paloma Zhou set up her stall an hour after sundown, as she always did, when the lanterns along the Fishwife's Row burned low enough to hide her face but bright enough to show her wares. She arranged them with a surgeon's care: whalebone combs, knucklebone dice, charms of carved antler strung on waxed cord, each one smooth as a river stone from hours of her thumb's patient work.

She did not sell magic. Officially.

"Officially," she murmured to herself, testing the edge of her smallest knife against a sliver of rib bone, "I sell trinkets."

The trinkets disagreed. They always disagreed, quietly, in voices only she could hear — which was, of course, the whole problem of her life.

She picked up a whalebone charm she'd finished that morning, a small crescent etched with wave-patterns, and the whisper came through it like water through cloth. *Cold,* said the whale, or what remained of the whale, some sixty years of sea-memory soaked into the bone like salt into timber. *Cold down where the nets don't reach. Cold, and the singing rocks, and—*

Paloma set it down gently, the way one sets down a sleeping child. "Hush," she said under her breath. "You're a luck-charm now. Act like it."

A fishwife named Maren Oduya's cousin twice over — half of Saltmere was related to the Oduyas, or claimed to be — stopped at her stall, hands red and cracked from gutting herring since dawn. Maren had lost a husband to the winter swells three years past and bought a charm every month since, though she'd never once said who the charms were for.

"The usual," Maren said. Her eyes flicked left, right, the reflexive sweep of a woman who lived in a city where informers were paid by the head. "Something for the house. Something... settling."

*Settling.* That word again. Half of Saltmere wanted something for sleeping, something for nightmares, something for the feeling that the drowned were pressing their faces against the underside of the world. Paloma could have told them the truth: that the dead didn't haunt houses, they haunted *bones*, and if you couldn't hear them you were lucky, and if you could hear them you learned to smile while your skull filled with other people's last thoughts.

She chose a piece of antler, pale as milk, already shaped into a sleeping gull. As her fingers closed around it, it woke.

*Small hands,* whispered whatever grandmother had owned this wrist once, long dead, her voice thin as smoke through reeds. *Small hands on my shoulder. The fever took me slow. Tell the girl the loom thread goes under, not over. Tell her. Tell her.*

"I'll tell her," Paloma breathed, barely moving her lips.

"What?" said Maren.

"Nothing. This one's good." Paloma turned the gull in the lantern light, showing off the carving, buying time while she pressed the old woman's voice back down into the bone. "Carved from mountain antler. Mountain things are calm things. Good for a house."

Maren paid four coppers and left clutching the gull like it might fly away without her.

Paloma exhaled slowly and reached for the next piece of work. Around her, the market went on: a boy selling candied kelp, a knife-grinder whose wheel sang against steel, two harbor guards in Voss-blue sashes walking their rounds with the bored menace of men paid to notice things. She kept her shoulders loose and her face pleasant and her gift folded up small inside her, the way she'd learned to fold it five years ago, when she first came to Saltmere with nothing but a knife, a bundle of bones, and a name that wasn't hers.

*Not hers.* Even her own skeleton spoke sometimes, late at night, when she was too tired to quiet it. Her mother's cheekbones. Her father's jaw. A whole graveyard she carried with her everywhere she went, whispering lullabies in a dialect no one else alive remembered.

She picked up a knucklebone die and began to polish it, and the dice — all six of them, in her tray, in every tray she'd ever made — murmured their old game-players' gossip: *loaded, he cheated, she cheated worse, throw again, throw again.*

* * *

Business was good by the second bell of night. Business was always good in Saltmere, because Saltmere was afraid, and fear spent freely.

Five years ago, when Kaelen Voss took the capital and put his blue-sashed Wardens in every port, the edicts had come down like guillotine blades: magic outlawed. Speakers marked. Informers rewarded. In the old kingdoms, people like Paloma had been priestesses, midwives, bone-readers — honored, feared, married into good families. Now they were kindling waiting for a torch, and the only thing that kept the torch away was that nobody knew. Nobody could know.

So Paloma had built herself a careful life, stone by careful stone. A room above a chandler's shop. A stall in the night market. A reputation as a fine carver with a knack for lucky charms — knack being a word safe enough to hang a life on. She never carved anything too obviously powerful. She never took coin from anyone who looked at her wares with hunger instead of superstition. And once a month she walked out to the breakwater at low tide and gave the sea her silence, because the sea was full of bones too, and the drowned spoke to her whether she wanted it or not, and there was nowhere in the world she could go where they wouldn't find her.

That was the thing no one understood about hearing the dead. It wasn't power. Power would have been easier. It was *company* — company she hadn't asked for, company that never left, company that needed things from her. Messages delivered. Promises kept. Griefs carried, one pair of hands' worth at a time, until her own grief had nowhere to sit because every seat was taken.

"Girl." A new customer, an old man with a crab boat and a limp. "You've got any of the storm-bones left?"

"One." She fetched it: a vertebra from a deep-sea fish, carved with a spiral. It hummed faintly against her palm — *storm coming, storm coming, tie down the nets* — an honest warning from something that had died honestly. "For you? Three coppers. But listen to it, old Tobb. When it feels cold in your pocket, don't sail."

He grinned with three teeth. "Superstition."

"The best kind," Paloma said, "is the kind that keeps you alive."

* * *

Third bell. The market thinned. The knife-grinder packed his wheel, the eel-fryer doused his fire, and the guards drifted toward the taverns where their bribes waited. Paloma counted her coppers, wrapped her unsold charms in oilcloth, and let herself do the thing she only allowed at closing time: she listened.

She held a whalebone comb she'd been unable to sell — too heavy, too sad — and let the whale's memory pour through her. Not words, exactly. Whales thought in vast slow shapes. This one remembered its pod, a whole constellation of warm bodies moving through black water; remembered the harpoon line like a lightning bolt through its side; remembered surfacing once, at the end, to look at the moon.

Paloma had heard ten thousand deaths. She could still be undone by a whale looking at the moon.

"You should have been a priestess," her mother used to say, back before that word became a death sentence. "You should have had a temple, students, a hundred hands to help carry it." Instead her mother had taught her to carve, to hide, to smile, to haggle — survival skills dressed up as craft — and then her village had burned anyway, and none of the hiding had mattered, and here she was.

Weary. Competent. Alone in a crowd that paid her to pretend.

She blew out her lantern.

And that was when she felt it — felt it through her boots before she heard it through any bone, a wrongness in the street like a false note in a familiar song. Footsteps. Small ones. Bare feet on wet cobblestone, stopping just beyond the reach of the dying market-lights.

Paloma stood very still. Her hand found her carving knife, more out of habit than hope; a knife against a Warden was a joke with a bloody punchline.

The footsteps came closer. Stopped at the mouth of the alley beside her stall.

In the grey spill of the last lantern she saw a child. Mud to the knees, hair matted with marsh-reed, a smudge of a face maybe seven years old, maybe younger — hollow-cheeked, salt-crusted, staring at her with eyes too old for the face around them. One small fist clutched something against its chest, something dark and angular that caught the light like wet stone.

"Market's closed," Paloma said quietly.

The child opened its mouth. What came out was not crying, not begging, not any language spoken between the Sedge Marshes and the northern ice. It was older than that. Older than the kingdoms. The sound of it went through Paloma like a blade of ice sliding between her ribs, and every bone in her stall — whalebone, knucklebone, antler, rib — *sang*, all at once, a high clear agonized chord, the way tuning forks scream when struck together.

The dead in her wares went silent. All of them. Every whisper, every murmur, every gossiping ghost — silenced, listening, *afraid*.

Paloma's knife-hand shook. Five years of careful stone-by-stone living, and in six seconds a mud-caked child had kicked the whole wall down.

The child took one step forward, held out the dark fragment of what she could now see was temple stone, carved with letters in that same impossible tongue — and collapsed at her feet like a puppet with cut strings.

From somewhere down the Row came the whistles of the night watch, two notes, then three. Patrol change. Minutes, perhaps less.

Paloma looked at the child. Looked at the stone. Felt the silence of ten thousand dead, all of them holding their breath, waiting to see what she would do.

"Oh," she said softly, to no one, to herself, to the careful life she could feel crumbling. "*Oh, no.*"

She knelt, gathered the child up — lighter than driftwood, burning hot with fever — and carried it into the dark.
