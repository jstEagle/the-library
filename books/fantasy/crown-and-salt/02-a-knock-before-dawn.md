## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The knock came in the hour when even the gulls refused to speak.

Paloma Zhou heard it through three inches of oak and a dream she was having about her grandmother's hands — steady, patient hands that had taught her to read the grain of bone before Paloma could read letters at all. In the dream those hands were carving something, and the something was screaming, and then the knocking started and Paloma woke knowing two things at once: that no one knocked on her door before dawn unless they wanted something terrible, and that whatever stood outside was not alone, because the bones in her workshop had begun to sing.

Not whisper. Sing.

She lay still beneath her blanket and listened to it — a thin, high keening from the shelf above her workbench where her finished charms hung on waxed cord. Whalebone charms for the fishwives who wanted their husbands' ghosts kept gentle. Knucklebone dice for the harbor guards who liked to gamble with luck they couldn't name. A rib of some old sea-cow she'd never been able to sell because it held too much grief. All of them vibrating together like plucked strings, and beneath them, faint as a pulse under skin, a sound she had never heard from any relic in five years of listening: a word being spoken over and over in a language that did not belong to any kingdom, any port, any grave she knew.

Paloma sat up in the dark of her attic room and pressed her palm flat against her sternum, the way she did when the dead got loud, as if she could hold her own heartbeat down and keep it separate from theirs.

*No one knocks before dawn,* she thought. *No one knocks at all.*

In Saltmere, people came to her stall in the night market, paid their coppers, took their charms, and left. They did not know where she lived, or told themselves they didn't. The informers knew, of course — informers knew everything in this city, every stairwell and cellar, every crack in every wall where a person might hide a gift the law said did not exist. But informers were paid by the head, and Paloma's head was worth more attached to her shoulders than delivered to the Wardens, because a bone-carver who only ever sold trinkets was useful to everyone and dangerous to no one. That was the bargain she had made with this city without ever speaking it aloud. She carved small. She listened quietly. She gave the dead nothing but her attention and gave the living nothing but what they asked for.

And now someone was knocking.

She dressed in the dark — wool trousers salt-stiff at the knees, a sweater that had belonged to a man whose ghost she'd once helped find his way home, boots with soles worn thin enough that she could feel the temperature of the floorboards through them. From the hook by the door she took her carving knife, not because she expected to use it but because her grandmother's voice, in the one memory of her that wasn't a dream, had said: *A woman alone keeps a blade the way other women keep cats.* Her grandmother had been dead eleven years and still had opinions. That was inheritance, too.

The singing grew louder as she descended the stairs. By the time she reached the bottom step, the whalebone charms were ringing like struck glass, and the sea-cow rib had begun, very softly, to weep.

"I hear you," Paloma murmured to the shelf, which was a foolish thing to say and a habit she'd never broken. "I hear you. Quiet now."

They didn't quiet. If anything, the sound sharpened — focused, directed, the way a dog's barking turns from alarm to pointing. Everything on that shelf was aimed at her front door like a hundred small fingers.

Paloma put her eye to the peephole she'd drilled herself, low and crooked, disguised among the knots of the wood.

Nothing. The street beyond was grey and empty, lamplight guttering in the fog off the harbor, rain beginning again in that fine malicious drizzle that Saltmere specialized in. She let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding and was just deciding that she'd dreamed the knock after all, that the bones were singing at nothing, at weather, at the moon —

— when the knock came again. Lower down. At knee height.

Paloma went very still.

She opened the door.

* * *

The child standing on her threshold could not have been older than seven or eight, though it was hard to tell, because the child was caked head to foot in mud — river mud by the smell of it, silt-brown and reeking of weed and stagnant water, dried in cracks across its face like glaze on badly fired pottery. It wore a coat meant for an adult, sleeves rolled six times over, hem dragging. Its hair was matted flat to its skull. Its feet, bare and blue-white in the cold, were split at the heels and bleeding thinly into the puddle forming beneath it.

It looked up at her with eyes that were startlingly calm. Everyone else in the world, faced with Paloma opening her door at this hour holding a knife half-hidden behind her thigh, would have flinched. The child simply regarded her, the way you regard a door you have been walking toward for a very long time.

"Go away," Paloma said.

The child opened its mouth. What came out was not crying, not begging, not the whine of a beggar-child worked up a speech about charity. It was language — fluid, rhythmic, syllables rising and falling like water over stones, and Paloma understood none of it, and yet her body understood all of it, because the moment the first word crossed the threshold, every bone in her workshop screamed.

Not sang. Screamed. The whalebone charms cracked against each other on their cords. One of them — a charm she'd carved for old Maren Tull, holding the voice of Maren's drowned son — snapped clean in half, and Paloma felt the boy's ghost spill out into her hallway like water from a dropped cup, confused, terrified, reaching for her with hands that had no substance. Behind her eyes, the dead she carried — the ones she'd absorbed over five years of listening, fragments and whispers, passengers she hadn't invited but hadn't turned away — all of them went silent at once, huddled somewhere behind her ribs like animals before a storm.

The child spoke again. Three words this time. And Paloma's own skeleton answered.

It was not pain exactly. It was worse than pain. It was recognition — every bone in her body humming in sympathy, resonating like tuning forks brought near a great bell, and inside that resonance she caught the shape of the words the way you catch the shape of a face through frosted glass. Not meaning. Shape. As if the language itself had a body, and her bones were its distant cousins, and it was calling them by name.

"*Stop,*" Paloma gasped, and grabbed the doorframe because the floor had begun to tilt. "*Stop it — I don't understand you —*"

The child stopped. Looked at her. And then, slowly, deliberately, it raised one mud-crusted fist and uncurled its fingers.

In its palm lay a fragment of stone the size of a hen's egg, black-green and slick, veined with lines that were not natural fractures but carvings — spirals within spirals, worn shallow by centuries of water, still faintly glittering with wet. It should have been ordinary. Broken temple rubble washed up on a thousand shores; Saltmere's children built forts out of less.

But the dead in Paloma's chest leaned toward it the way flowers lean toward light.

"Where did you get that?" she whispered. Then, feeling stupid, because the child clearly spoke no tongue she knew: "Who sent you?"

The child closed its fingers around the stone and spoke again — one word, soft, and this time Paloma's bones did not scream; they *answered*. A single note, deep, sustained, rising from her marrow out through her teeth, and she heard her own voice join it without her permission, a hum she could not stop, and for three heartbeats she and the child stood on opposite sides of the doorway making the same sound in different languages, and the fog on the street seemed to lean in to listen.

Then it ended. The child swayed. Paloma saw, with the particular horror of a person trained to notice such things, how thin the wrists were inside those rolled sleeves, how the child's lips had gone the grey-blue of drowned things, how long it must have walked and swum and hidden to arrive here, at this door, in this hour.

Every instinct Paloma owned — every instinct she had *cultivated*, carefully, for five years, the instincts of a woman who survives by being nobody to everybody — screamed at her to shut the door.

She shut the door.

She stood with her back against it, knife trembling in her hand, listening to the silence on the other side. No footsteps retreating. No crying. Just breathing — small, ragged, patient breathing, the breathing of something that intended to wait.

"You can't be here," she said through the wood. "Do you understand me? There are people — if anyone sees you — there are men who are *paid*—"

The child knocked again. Once. Gently. Like a heartbeat.

Paloma looked down the hall toward her workshop. The sea-cow rib had gone silent. All the charms hung still. Even the spilled ghost of Maren Tull's son had settled, coiled around the fragment of knowledge the child had brought like cats around a fire. And in the quiet, Paloma became aware of a sensation she had never felt in thirty-one years of hearing the dead: they weren't whispering to her anymore.

They were whispering about her.

*Take it in,* said the voice of a drowned sailor she'd never met, the one who lived in the back of her skull like a lodger. *Take it in, carver. Before the ones with boots do.*

"They'll burn my house," she told him. "They'll burn me."

*They will anyway,* said the sailor, with the terrible honesty of the dead, who have nothing left to lose by telling the truth. *Once the stone sings in your walls, they will anyway. Better to choose your burning than wait for it.*

"That's not comforting."

*We do not comfort. We witness.* A pause, and then, almost gently: *You have been witnessing alone a long time, little listener. So has that child. Longer than you know.*

Paloma closed her eyes. She thought about the arithmetic she had performed every day for five years — the careful, constant weighing of risk, the ledger she kept in her head with entries like *smiled at the baker's wife, cost: nothing* and *sold a charm that hummed too sweetly, cost: a Warden's second look, paid off with a lie about whale-song*. Five years of small entries. Five years of staying below the waterline of notice.

And she thought about the child's feet. Blue-white. Bleeding into her doorstep. Whoever this was, whatever this was, it had not come to her by accident. Bones did not sing by accident. The dead did not point like dogs by accident.

"Gods rot it," she said — quietly, to the ceiling, to her grandmother, to whoever kept accounts of such things. "Gods rot it all."

She opened the door.

The child was still there. Of course it was still there. It looked up at her, and Paloma saw now, up close, in the thin pre-dawn light, that under the mud its face was fine-boned and foreign and utterly exhausted, and that its eyes held the specific patience of someone who has been told *no* many times and has stopped expecting anything else.

"Inside," Paloma said. "Before the whole street wakes up and I lose my mind completely."

The child stepped over the threshold. Its bare feet left muddy prints on her floorboards, and Paloma watched those prints and thought, distantly, *those will need scrubbing before market*, which was such an absurdly domestic thought given everything else that she nearly laughed.

Then the child reached up and pressed the fragment of stone into her hand — pressed it there with both small filthy palms, folding her fingers over it, the way you give something precious to someone you trust, and Paloma felt the stone's cold sink straight through her skin into the bones of her hand, and from there outward, a slow ripple, a bell tolling through her whole frame, and in the depths of that tolling she heard — layered beneath the spirals of the carving, beneath the centuries of water, beneath everything —

— a voice. Vast and old and drowning. Singing.

The child watched her face while she heard it. Watched her knees buckle. Caught her arm with surprisingly strong hands and lowered her, gently, to sit on the bottom stair, and then knelt in front of her in the mud-stiff coat and waited, patient as tide, while the great dark song poured through the stone into the hollows of her body and filled places she hadn't known were empty.

When it finally faded, dawn was seeping grey through the window above the door.

"What are you?" Paloma breathed.

The child tilted its head. Opened its mouth. And spoke one last word before exhaustion took it — Paloma saw the small body sway, saw consciousness go out of those calm eyes like a lamp pinched out — and the word, in that ancient rolling language, sounded so much like a name that Paloma's bones rang with it even as the child collapsed forward into her arms, mud and blood and river-water soaking into her sweater, and the stone in her clenched fist sang one final clear note that no one else in the waking city could hear:

Outside, far off across the rooftops toward the harbor citadel, a Warden's horn blew the morning watch.

And somewhere beneath the sea, something that had been sleeping for a very long time began, softly, to listen back.
