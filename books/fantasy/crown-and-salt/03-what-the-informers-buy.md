## 3. What the Informers Buy

The child slept in the corner of my workshop like something washed ashore — curled around the fragment of drowned temple stone as though it were the last warm thing in the world.

I had not slept at all.

I'd told myself, through the small hours, that I would send the child away at first light. That was the sensible thing. The Saltmere thing. You did not take in strays who spoke in tongues that made your bones sing; you pointed them toward the harbor and you locked your door and you counted yourself lucky the wardens hadn't followed them to your step. Five years I had survived this city by being exactly no one's business, and here was a mud-caked child asleep on my floor with a relic in its fists that hummed against my hearing like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

But every time I imagined opening the door and putting the child out into the grey, my hands stopped working. Not metaphorically. I sat at my bench with a half-finished whalebone charm under the awl and my fingers simply refused, the way they refused when a dead voice pressed too close to the surface of a carving and I couldn't tell whose grief was whose.

So instead of sleeping, I worked. And instead of working on charms for the fishwives — little knucklebone hushers to quiet crying infants, rib-bone tokens for safe passage through the marsh road — I found myself carving a new piece entirely: a small hollow bead of gull-wing bone, sized for a child's throat, drilled through so it could be worn on a cord. A listening-charm turned inside out. Instead of letting the wearer hear the dead, it would let me hear *through* the wearer — sense where they were, whether their heart had gone quick or slow. A tether. A coward's compass.

I told myself it was protection. For the child. In case we were separated.

I did not examine too closely who I was protecting myself from losing.

Dawn came up thin and salt-colored through the shutters. The child stirred, sat up, and looked at me with those flat dark eyes that held no fear at all, which frightened me more than tears would have. It touched the stone fragment, then touched its own chest, then pointed at me.

"No," I said, for perhaps the hundredth time since midnight. "You can't stay."

It tilted its head. Then, softly, in that language that was older than any kingdom on the coast — older than Saltmere, older than the crown Voss had stolen, older maybe than the sea wall itself — it said three syllables. My hanging charms all shivered at once on their cords, forty little bones knocking together like teeth, and somewhere behind my sternum the dead went very, very quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it.

I didn't know what the child had said. But every bone in my workshop did, and they approved.

"That," I said, and my voice came out rough, "is exactly the problem."

* * *

I was grinding bone-dust for the morning's glue when the dogs began.

Saltmere's night market had its own soundscape — gulls, gut-cries, the slap of the tide against pilings, fishwives singing out the catch — and underneath all of it, always, the dead murmuring through whatever bone was nearest. What cut through all of it now was barking. Warden hounds. Big brindle things bred in the capital, expensive, and they did not come to Saltmere's low streets for smugglers' scraps. They came when someone had paid.

I killed the glue fire with one motion and crossed the workshop in three strides.

"Up," I said to the child. "Up, come on." I made lifting gestures until it understood, then herded it toward the back wall where my stores stood. Behind the shelf of prepared bone — femur blanks, rib splints, trays of sorted teeth — there was a hatch I'd built into the floor my second year in the city, after the first time a warden knocked on my door over nothing more than a rumor. Beneath it: the bone-dust cellar. Four feet of crawlspace between the joists, lined with sawdust, smelling of calcium and old death, where I kept the stock too valuable to display and, twice before tonight, myself.

The child balked at the edge of the hole. Of course it did. Any sane creature balks at being asked to climb down into a pit full of ground-up dead.

"Listen to me." I crouched, took its narrow shoulders. Its skin was cold even through the rags. "There are men coming who hurt people who talk the way you talk. Do you understand? People who speak your — your language. They will come with dogs and iron and they will ask questions, and if they find you, they will not stop asking." I put my palm flat on the temple stone still clutched in its fist, and the hum of it ran up my arm and settled behind my eyes like a headache of light. "Down. Please."

Somewhere up the street, a door burst open. Wood splitting. A woman's voice, high and furious, and then the particular wet thud of a warden's truncheon doing its work.

The child went down into the dark without another look at me, folding itself into the sawdust like a creature returning to burrow. I pulled the hatch, slid the shelf back into place, scattered dust across the seams, and stood there breathing while my heart tried to climb out through my ribs.

Then I did the only other thing I could think of. I picked up the day's finished charms, hung them openly on the market rack by the door where any searching eye could see them — harmless trinkets, fishwife nonsense, nothing here but commerce — and I opened my shutters to let the grey morning in.

The knock, when it came, was polite. That was worst of all. Wardens who meant to arrest you kicked. Wardens who meant to *search* knocked, because a search required a reason, and a reason required paperwork, and paperwork was how informers got paid.

* * *

There were six of them. Five in salt-grey livery with the crowned eel of House Voss stitched at the collar — the usurper's sigil, worn now even this far from his capital, which told me how far his reach had grown — and the sixth in plain oilskin who moved like a man accustomed to standing slightly behind other men. An informer. I knew the type. Every street in Saltmere had two or three. They sold what they saw, and what they couldn't see, they invented, because invention also paid.

The lead warden was young, square-faced, with the careful blankness of a man following instructions he didn't fully understand.

"Paloma Zhou," he read from a slip of paper. "Bone-carver."

"That's me." I kept my hands visible, dusted white to the wrist, and let my accent do its work — the flattened vowels of a marsh-born woman, unremarkable, unthreatening. "If this is about the Eelmoon tax, I paid it. If it's about Tamsin Corver's claim I shorted her on husher-beads, she's a liar and everyone on this street knows it."

"It's about neither." He folded the paper away. "We're looking for someone. May we see your premises?"

"What does this someone look like?"

"A child," he said.

The word landed in the room and just — stayed there. I heard my own pulse. Behind me, the market rack swayed in the draft, and forty small bones knocked together, and I prayed to every god I'd stopped believing in that the sound read as innocent.

"A child," I repeated. "What child?"

"One that came off the tide-boats yesterday evening. Or out of the marshes. Witnesses disagree." His eyes moved past me, cataloguing: the bench, the tools, the hanging charms, the cot in the corner with its blanket still crumpled from a body that had slept in it. "Mute, they said. Or near enough. Covered in marsh mud. Carrying a stone."

"I'm a bone-carver," I said. "Children come here constantly. Mothers buy hushers, fathers buy luck-teeth. Half this street's brats have been through my shop."

"And none of them mute? None of them muddy?" He smiled without warmth. "None of them carrying stones?"

I shrugged, and the shrug cost me everything I had, and I spent it gladly. "Search if you've paper for it. You're keeping me from an order due at noon."

They searched. I stood by the door with my arms folded and watched five men turn my life inside out with efficient gloved hands, and I will tell you honestly what I felt, because it matters later: I felt nothing about my belongings at all. Not the tools, not the cot, not the jars of dye. All of it — my attention, my breath, my whole self — was four feet below the floor, pressed into sawdust, listening with me. Because here is the thing about the bone-dust cellar nobody knows: I ground that dust myself, over years, from leavings and cast-offs and the honored fragments of the dead I'd carved for, and dust remembers. It isn't hearing, exactly. It's more like standing in a crowd with your eyes closed. And right now the crowd beneath my floorboards was holding its breath around a living child, and every grain of it strained toward the surface, toward me, like grass bending toward sun.

*Quiet,* I thought down at them. *Please. Quiet.*

The dust obeyed. It had never obeyed before. I filed that away in the place where I kept things I wasn't ready to know.

The warden lifted my cot blanket. He opened my dye jars and sniffed each one. He took down the market rack's charms and examined them one by one, turning them in the light, and when he reached the whalebone piece I'd carved at midnight — the hollow listening-bead, the tether, the coward's compass — he paused.

"What's this one?"

"Throat-charm. For stammering children. Gull wing, hollow-bored." I recited it flat, bored. "Superstition. Sells well."

He weighed it in his palm. Something flickered across his face — not suspicion, quite. Recognition of craft. Then he set it back on the rack, and I exhaled through my nose, slow, invisible.

That was when the informer spoke for the first time.

"You should check the floor," he said.

* * *

His voice was nothing special. Dry, unhurried, the voice of a man ordering ale. But it went through me like an awl through soft bone, because I had spent the last quarter hour deciding he was a coward and cowards don't spend other people's coin on guesses.

"The floor," the warden said.

"She's a bone-carver." The informer nodded at my dust-white forearms, the drifts of powder swept into corners, the fine sediment over everything like frost. "Ground bone goes somewhere. Cellar, most likely. Bone-dust cellars are worth looking at anyway — that's where the good relics get hidden. Ask anyone in the trade."

Every eye in the room came to me. I gave them the marsh-woman's shrug again, the one that says *country folk and their storage holes*, and I said, "Cellar's flooded this time of year. Knee-deep in rot-water. You'll ruin your boots."

"We're issued boots," the warden said.

And here is what saved us, and I want to be precise about it, because it was not cleverness and it was not courage. It was the dead.

As the warden crossed toward the shelf, his boot heel rang once on a loose floorboard — and through that board, faint as a whisper under a door, I heard the cellar. Heard it the way I hear through carved bone: the child's heart going fast and shallow, the sawdust holding its shape around a small curled body, and threaded through all of it, rising, the murmur of the dust itself beginning to stir, beginning to *wake*, because the thing lying in it was speaking without speaking, and the dead in my floor wanted to answer.

If they answered aloud — if even one grain of that dust sighed — every warden in the room would hear it, and there are no lies in the world that cover a voice from under the floor.

So I did the thing I had sworn, for five years, never to do in company. I reached — with whatever it is in me that reaches, the thing I called a curse and hid and fed in secret — and I caught the rising voices of my own cellar the way you catch a falling cup. Held them. Poured my will down through the floorboard like water into sand. *Sleep. Sleep. Sleep.*

The dust went silent.

And the price came due immediately, as prices do. The hearing I use is a channel, and channels run both ways, and holding the dead shut meant holding *everything* shut — and for three long seconds, while the warden dragged my shelf aside and stared down at the hatch, I was deaf to the room. Deaf to boots. Deaf to the informer shifting his weight behind me. Blind in the one sense that had kept me alive in this city, and I stood there smiling my dull marsh smile with my whole soul clenched around a silence.

The warden looked at the hatch. He looked at it for a long moment.

Then the woman next door — Widow Corver herself, liar and scold, my least favorite person on the street — began screaming at the top of her lungs about her confiscated herring barrels, and the youngest warden laughed, and the moment broke like ice, and the warden stepped back from the shelf and said, "Flooded cellars aren't worth the paperwork."

They took things anyway. That's how searches end; they must return with something or the informer doesn't get paid. Two pouches of powdered bone. A bundle of blank femurs. And, wrapped in oilcloth and carried out with a care that made my stomach drop, the whalebone listening-bead from my rack — the tether-charm, the one I'd carved at midnight, the only piece in the workshop that was anything other than what it seemed.

"For the captain's review," the warden said. "Standard procedure."

"Of course," I said.

At the door, the informer turned back. He looked at me — really looked, for the first time, a long flat assessing look like a man reading a ledger — and then at the floor, and then at me again.

"Flooded cellars," he said mildly. "Funny thing. Dust stays dry, though, doesn't it. Ground bone. Doesn't rot like wood." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Morning to you, mistress."

* * *

I waited until the dogs had faded three streets off. I waited until Widow Corver had finished shouting and the market noises had reassembled themselves over the wound the raid had left. Then I went to the hatch, and my hands shook so badly I dropped the pin twice, and when the child climbed up out of the dark into my arms — mud-stiff, sawdust-caked, calm as deep water — I held on longer than I meant to.

"They nearly found you," I whispered into its filthy hair. "Do you understand? They nearly—"

It pulled back and looked at me. Then it did something new. It raised one small hand and pressed its palm flat against my sternum, over the place where the dead live loudest in me, and said two syllables in the First Tongue.

My hanging charms sang. All of them, clear and sweet, a chord like a chapel I'd never entered. And beneath the floor, the bone-dust sighed — not in fear, not in hunger, but the way a congregation sighs when the sermon ends and the blessing begins.

I sat down hard on the floor among my ruined shelves and laughed, or cried, or both, because I finally understood what the child had been telling me since it fell through my door. Not *help me*. Not *hide me*.

*I know what you are.*

* * *

I should have sent it away that afternoon. I packed a bundle — I actually packed it, bread and dried fish and the good cloak, and stood with my hand on the latch planning the route to the marsh ferry — and then the tide brought the news, the way the tide brings everything to Saltmere eventually.

Old Maren from the gutting tables came by for her monthly luck-tooth, and gossiping, the way she pays for what she buys: two villages burned on the coast east of here. Not raided. Burned, root and rafter, by crown soldiers with a list of names. Survivors taken in wagons, or not surviving at all. And — she leaned close, delighted and horrified — the soldiers weren't looking for rebels or smugglers. They were asking after *strange words*. After anyone who spoke a language the priests used to call forbidden. A speaker of the First Tongue, Maren said, laughing into her shawl. Imagine. As if such a thing existed anymore. As if the priestesses hadn't been ash these hundred years.

Behind her, at my bench, the child went very still over the temple stone it was holding, and the stone began, very softly, to sing.

I closed my hand around Maren's payment, thanked her, showed her out, and bolted the door.

Two villages. Burned. Hunting a voice.

I looked at the child, and the child looked back at me with those bottomless dark eyes, and I understood that the informer had seen everything this morning — had seen it and walked away, not out of mercy but out of arithmetic. He hadn't been paid enough yet. He was waiting to learn what a speaker of the First Tongue was worth, and he'd be back with the answer, and next time there would be no Widow Corver and no flooded-cellar excuse and no laughter in the street to break the moment.

Five years of being no one's business. One night of being someone's, and already the wolves were at the door.

I set the packed bundle down by the child's feet.

"We leave before dawn," I said, "and you'd better hope, little one, that whoever you belong to is kinder than whoever's hunting you."

The child picked up the bundle. And then, watching me steadily, it said one word in the First Tongue — and this time I didn't need a translation, because every bone in the room translated it for me at once, in the voice of my grandmother, in the voice of the drowned priestesses, in a whisper like tide over old stone:

*Hurry.*
