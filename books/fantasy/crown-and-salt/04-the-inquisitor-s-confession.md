## 4. The Inquisitor's Confession

The smuggler's name was Doss, and he wept before Seren Oduya had finished locking the door of the rented room.

That was usual. They all wept eventually — the ones who were guilty, the ones who weren't, the ones too frightened to know the difference. In eleven years wearing the gray sash of the Order of the Quill and Flame, Seren had learned that tears told him nothing. What told him something was what men did with their hands while they wept. Guilty hands went to their pockets, to knives, to the small hidden things they could not bear to surrender. Innocent hands went to their faces, as if shame were a wound that needed pressing shut.

Doss's hands went to his throat.

"Sit down," Seren said.

The room above the Salt Cellar tavern was the kind smugglers liked: one window facing the alley, one window facing the harbor, two exits if you counted the rot in the floorboards. Seren had taken it three days ago under a false name and a fishmonger's coat, and he had waited, because waiting was the only skill the Order had left him after they took everything else. He was good at waiting. He had been waiting for five years, in one form or another, since the day they stripped his sash and put him on a boat north and told him not to come back until he had learned what obedience cost.

He had learned. That was the bitter joke of it. He had learned exactly what they wanted him to learn, and the lesson had ruined him for anything else.

Doss sat. He was a small man, ropey, with salt-cracked lips and the permanent squint of someone who had spent thirty years reading weather off open water. His eyes went to the door, to the window, to the knife at Seren's belt — a plain working knife, nothing ceremonial; Seren had sold the ceremonial blade years ago along with everything else that burned.

"I don't know what you want," Doss said. "I'm just a hauler. I move cargo."

"You moved cargo out of Merrow's Reach three weeks ago." Seren pulled the second chair from the wall and sat across from him, knees almost touching. Interrogation was proximity work. You didn't loom over a man; you sat close enough that he could smell your breath and couldn't look anywhere but your eyes. "Two nights after the village burned."

Something flickered behind Doss's squint. Not guilt. Fear — a different animal entirely.

"Village burned itself," Doss said. "I heard it was an accident. Lamp oil."

"Lamp oil doesn't leave arrowheads in doorframes." Seren kept his voice level, almost bored. "Lamp oil doesn't kill forty-one people and leave the wells poisoned. I walked Merrow's Reach last month. I counted the graves. Somebody dug them fast and shallow, which means somebody expected more deaths and got fewer, or expected fewer and got more. Either way, somebody was in a hurry, and hurried men make mistakes."

"I don't know anything about—"

"Your boat made port at Greyhaven with a hold full of charred timber and three crates you wouldn't let your own crew touch. Your crew says you slept on deck those three nights rather than go below. Your crew says you paid them double and told them to forget the route." Seren leaned forward. "Men only pay double to be forgotten when they're carrying something worse than contraband. So. What was in the crates, Doss?"

The smuggler's hands left his throat and went to his pockets.

Seren watched them go and felt the old cold settle into his chest, the cold he had carried through every interrogation chamber of his old life, the cold that had once felt like clarity and now felt like drowning. Because he knew what came next. He knew the shape of this confession before Doss opened his mouth, the way you know the shape of a wave before it breaks.

"There was a man," Doss said. "Gray cloak. Order colors under the dye. He met us at the cove with the crates already packed. Said it was salvage. Said the Order would remember me." He laughed, a dry cracked sound with no humor in it. "Remember me. Like that's a blessing."

"What was in the crates?"

"Relics." The word came out of Doss like a splinter coming out of flesh, slow and tearing. "Bone mostly. Carved bone. And stone — one piece of stone, big as a loaf, wrapped in oilcloth, and the whole time we carried it below decks my compass spun. Spun like a drunk. My boy Tavin held it once, just to shift its weight, and afterward he couldn't sleep. Kept saying he could hear singing. Kept saying the singing knew his name." Doss's voice dropped to a whisper. "And there was a woman's voice in it. Old woman. My grandmother died when I was six, ser, and I swear on the brine and the black deep, that stone sounded like her."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

Seren had spent five years telling himself the Order's cruelties were administrative. Necessary. The burning of speakers, the confiscation of relics, the lists of names — all of it filed under *order*, under *safety*, under the edict that magic was a rot and the rot had to come out of the body politic no matter how much healthy flesh came away with it. He had signed orders. He had read the names aloud. He had stood at the edge of pyres with his hands folded, because a Quill-and-Flame man did not flinch, and he had told himself every night that the flinching would come later, when he was old, when it could do no harm.

Then, seven years ago, they had handed him a list with a child's name on it. A girl of nine, in the fishing town of Candlemere, who had been heard talking to her dead mother's bones. And Seren Oduya had looked at that name, and at the child, and at the mother's knucklebone charm the wardens had confiscated, and he had said no. He had refused to sign. He had carried the girl out of Candlemere in the night and put her on a boat with a family heading south, and he had gone back to face the tribunal knowing what it would cost.

They had not burned him. Burning was for speakers. For inquisitors who broke faith, there was only exile and erasure — his name struck from the rolls, his sash burned in front of him, his record sealed. *Go north,* the Grand Inquisitor had said, and there had been something almost like pity in the old man's face. *Go north and forget what you think you know about mercy.*

But here was the thing Seren had never been able to say aloud, the thing that had eaten five hollow years out of him: refusing to burn one child had not absolved him. It had only shown him, in one blinding instant, what all the other signatures had been. Every name he *had* signed. Every pyre he had stood beside with his hands folded and his face composed. The girl in Candlemere hadn't saved him. She had damned him more thoroughly than any tribunal, because now he knew — he knew in his hands, in his gut, in the hour before dawn when sleep gave him up — that the Order was wrong, and that he had served it anyway, for eleven years, and that knowing it late was not the same as being innocent.

"Ser?" Doss was watching him. "You've gone white as gull-meat."

"The stone," Seren said. "Where is it now?"

"Gone. Sold on, in Greyhaven, to a broker with Order coin. But—" Doss hesitated, and Seren saw the last of his resistance crumble, not because of fear but because of something heavier. The need to put it down. To hand the weight to someone else, anyone else, even a stranger who'd broken into his room at knifepoint. "There's more. There's a thing I heard, and I've been drinking myself stupid for three weeks trying to unhear it, and I can't, so you may as well have it too and see how you sleep."

"Tell me."

"The gray-cloak man. Before he left the cove, he stood at the waterline and talked to himself. Or I thought it was to himself. Then Tavin — my boy, the one who hears the singing — Tavin said the man wasn't talking to himself. He was talking *in* a language. Old language. Words like water going over stones." Doss swallowed. "And the stones answered him. I didn't hear it. But Tavin did, and Tavin hasn't spoken a full sentence since. Whatever that language is, ser, the Order wants it bad enough to burn villages for it, and the man who carries it talks to drowned rocks, and I am done. I'm done. Take me to whoever you take people to. Hang me. Just get it out of my head."

Seren sat very still.

*Words like water going over stones.* He knew that description. He had read it once, years ago, in a sealed archive he should never have been cleared to enter — a fragment of pre-Edict text, recovered from a sunken temple, describing the liturgical tongue of the priesthood of the god beneath the sea. The First Tongue, the archivists called it. The language in which, the fragment claimed, true names lived — the name of the god, the names of the dead, the names by which the world could be unmade and remade. The Order had sealed the fragment and burned the scholar who translated it, and Seren had filed the memory away as one more piece of the machinery, one more wheel turning in the dark.

Except now the wheel had surfaced. Now the Order was burning villages — real villages, with wells and graveyards and children in them — hunting for something called the Drowned Name, and a gray-cloak officer spoke the First Tongue to stones that answered, and a child somewhere had heard it all and been struck mute by the hearing.

A child. Mute. Struck silent by words older than kingdoms.

Seren thought of Candlemere. Of course he did. He thought of the girl with the knucklebone charm, and he thought: *somewhere there is another one, right now, running.*

* * *

He found the first thread within the day, because Saltmere was a city built on threads and he had spent five years learning to pull them without snapping them.

Exile had taught him tradecraft the Order never had. As an inquisitor he had walked into rooms with the sash and the seal and the weight of the Flame behind him, and doors opened because they feared what stood behind him. Now he had nothing behind him at all, and it was astonishing how much a man could learn when he was nobody — how much bartenders told a lonely northerner with a purse of southern coin, how much harbor-wives confided in a quiet man who bought their smoked eel and remembered their children's names. Fear opened doors. Loneliness opened wider ones.

By evening he had three threads. A warden captain named Brannic had been asking questions in the night market — not the usual questions about unlicensed charms, but strange ones: *has anyone come to your carvers lately, a stranger, a child, anyone speaking odd?* A reward notice had circulated through the informers' network, unsigned, offering silver by the head for word of "a speaker of the First Tongue." And a fishwife named Maren Cole had mentioned, in the way of women who trade gossip like currency, that Paloma Zhou, the bone-carver off Tanner's Row, had taken in a mud-caked child two days past who hadn't said a word since arriving — except that Maren's sister swore she'd heard the child making sounds through the shutter one night, sounds like nothing human, sounds like *water going over stones.*

Seren stood in the mouth of Tanner's Row in the blue dark before dawn and felt the cold in his chest spread outward into his hands.

He knew what he was supposed to do. Five years ago it would have been reflex: mark the house, send word, let the machinery turn. The Order had built him for exactly this — for finding the hidden ones, the hearth-speakers, the bone-listeners, the whole scattered congregation of the outlawed gift. He had been good at it. That was the worst of it, that he had been *good*, that his goodness at the work was precisely why the work had finally broken him.

And now the machinery wanted this child. Wanted her badly enough to burn villages. Which meant that whatever Paloma Zhou was hiding on Tanner's Row, the Order would find it, and the finding would look like Merrow's Reach: shallow graves, poisoned wells, ash.

Unless someone stood in the way.

*I'm not that man anymore,* Seren told himself. *I burned my sash. I'm a fishmonger with a false name and a knife I use for bread.*

But his feet were already moving down the row, past the shuttered stalls, past the tanning sheds with their reek of lime and salt, toward the narrow house with the carved lintel — little charms nailed up under the eaves, whalebone moons and knucklebone fish, the kind of harmless trinkets every carver sold. Except Seren had spent eleven years learning to read relic-work, and these were not quite harmless. There was a grammar to them. A listening quality. Whoever lived here didn't just carve bone.

She heard through it.

He raised his hand to knock, and stopped, and stood in the dark like a fool with his fist an inch from the wood, understanding suddenly that he had no idea what he was going to say. Every speech he had prepared on the walk over — *I'm not here to arrest you, I'm here to warn you, I know what the Order is doing* — all of it collapsed against one simple fact: he was the Order. Not the sash, not anymore, but the training, the instincts, the interrogator's patience sitting in his hands like a weapon he couldn't put down. She would look at him and see exactly what he was. People always had.

He knocked.

It took a long time. Long enough that he heard movement inside — careful movement, weight distributed, the movement of someone who had practiced not being heard. Long enough that he imagined the interior: the child hidden, the relics buried, a knife or a hot poker within reach. Good, he thought. Be afraid. Fear keeps you alive longer than trust does.

The door opened a hand's width, on a chain. One eye, dark and exhausted, in a face younger than her reputation and older than her years.

"Paloma Zhou?"

Her eye flicked over him with a speed that told him she'd done this assessment before, many times, to many men. Coat: cheap. Knife: visible. Hands: empty. Posture: wrong for a warden, wrong for a thug. Her gaze snagged on something — his stance, maybe, the particular stillness of trained men — and narrowed.

"You're not buying charms," she said.

"No."

"Then you're selling trouble, and I've none to spare." She began to close the door.

"Merrow's Reach," Seren said quietly. "And a village before it, up the coast — Sallow's End, they're calling it. Burned inside a fortnight. Wells poisoned. The Order isn't collecting relics this time, madam. It's collecting a *word*. And there's a child in this city carrying it, and a reward notice with no signature on it, and a warden captain named Brannic asking after strangers in the night market, and when they find what you're hiding — and they will find it, because informers are cheaper than conscience in this town — they will do to your street what they did to Merrow's Reach."

Silence. The chain did not move. The eye did not blink.

"You're Order," she said at last. Flat. Certain. "I can smell it. You stand like them. You wait like them."

"Yes," Seren said. There was no point lying; she'd already read him, and a lie would only confirm the worst reading. "Eleven years. Exiled seven ago for refusing to burn a child. I'm not here to arrest anyone. I'm here because—" and here his prepared speeches failed him entirely, and what came out was the truth, raw and unpolished, the truth he had carried alone for five years and three weeks and could carry no further: "because I heard what was in those crates' description and I haven't slept since, and because there is a child in your house who speaks the First Tongue, and I am the only man in this city who knows what's coming for her, and I cannot carry that alone anymore. I tried. Five years I tried. It doesn't work. Knowing things alone doesn't work."

The eye studied him for a long moment. Behind it, faintly, he heard — or imagined he heard — a small sound. A child shifting. Or perhaps, he thought with a chill that ran down his spine like seawater, something answering from the bone-dust dark of a cellar.

"You say you refused to burn a child," Paloma Zhou said slowly. "Prove it. Tell me her name."

And Seren Oduya, who had not spoken that name aloud in seven years, who kept it written on a scrap of oilcloth inside his shirt where it lay against his heart like a coal that would not cool, reached into his collar and drew out the scrap and read it to her in the blue dark of Tanner's Row:

"Tessaly Briar, of Candlemere. Nine years old. Speaker through her mother's knucklebone. Signed for burning by order of the tribunal of the Eastern Reach — order refused by Inquisitor Seren Oduya, seventh of his name and last of his rank."

The chain slid free.

The door opened, and warm lamplight spilled out onto the step, and standing half-hidden behind Paloma Zhou's skirts was a small mud-haired child with enormous eyes — eyes that fixed on Seren and widened, and the child's mouth opened, and what came out was not a scream but a single syllable of that water-over-stones language, soft and wondering, like a question asked of the sea.

And from the cellar below, faint but unmistakable, something *answered*.
