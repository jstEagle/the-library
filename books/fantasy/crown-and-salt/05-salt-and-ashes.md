## 5. Salt and Ashes

The knock came again at dusk, and this time it was not a child's fist.

Paloma stood in the dark of her workshop with Wren pressed against her side, one hand resting on the girl's shoulder, the other hovering over the poker in the hearth. Through the shutter slats she could see the shape of him on her step: tall, travel-worn, and standing the way soldiers stand when they've stopped being soldiers — weight settled low, hands loose and visible, everything about him arranged to say *I am not a threat* in a language that only worked on people who believed such languages existed.

"I know you're awake," he said through the door. His voice was low, roughened by road dust. "The lamp behind your shutters has been lit since noon. Carvers don't work by lamplight unless they're finishing something or hiding something."

Paloma said nothing. Beside her, Wren had gone rigid as driftwood.

"My name is Seren Oduya," the man said. "Formerly of the Inquisition of the Crown Ascendant. Formerly. I was exiled four years ago, and I have walked a long way to say something to you that I could not carry alone." A pause. "I'm not going to touch the door. I'm going to sit down on your step, and I'm going to talk, and you can listen or not listen. But I think you'll listen."

He sat. She heard his back find the wood, heard the creak of leather and the small metallic whisper of a blade being unbuckled from its sheath and laid flat on the boards where she could see it if she opened the door.

"Damn you," Paloma murmured — not to him, quite, but to the whole arrangement of the world that kept putting strangers on her step.

Wren looked up at her. The child's eyes were enormous in the lamplight, and there was something in them that was not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone who had heard a word spoken correctly for the first time in years.

Paloma blew out the lamp, which was foolish theater since he'd already seen it, then unbarred the door anyway.

* * *

He looked worse inside than he had through the slats. Grey at the temples though he couldn't have been past forty, a scar splitting one eyebrow, and the particular hollowed-out thinness of a man who ate when he remembered to. He sat where she told him to sit — the stool farthest from Wren — and he did not look at the child directly, which Paloma noted and did not decide whether to be grateful for.

"You were at the market," she said. "Two nights ago. You watched me haggle with the fishwives."

"I watch everyone. It's an ugly habit I can't break." He folded his hands. "You sold a woman a whalebone charm against drowning, and when you handed it over you flinched. Not because of her. Because of what the bone said to you when you touched it. And you covered the flinch with a joke about the price, and she laughed, and no one saw. No one but me. Because I spent eleven years learning to see exactly that."

Paloma kept her face still. "You're describing a trick. Charms are tricks. Everyone knows carvers put on a little show."

"Do they." He wasn't smiling. "Then explain why your knucklebone dice always come up the number the buyer needs. Explain why old Marta Corrigan's funeral shroud-bone — carved by you, buried with her last spring — was dug up by gulls' men within a week, and why the warden who confiscated it was overheard saying it *whispered*. Explain why there is a child in this room who speaks a language that was extinct before Saltmere had walls."

The silence stretched. In the hearth, a log shifted and collapsed into embers.

"You've been asking about us," Paloma said quietly. "In the taverns. Don't bother denying it; a man doesn't walk three days to a stranger's door without asking questions first. So you already know what I'd tell you: that you should leave, and that if you speak of this to anyone—"

"I know what informers are paid," he said. "By the head. I helped set the rate." Something moved behind his eyes, some old sediment turning over. "That's part of what I've come to say. But not first. First I need to tell you about Kestrel's Rest."

The name landed strangely. Paloma frowned. "Fishing village. Down the coast past the Sedge. What of it?"

"It isn't there anymore."

She waited for him to go on, and understood after a moment that he was waiting too — waiting to see if she'd make him say it, the way people make surgeons describe wounds.

"Say it plainly," she said.

"Three weeks ago, riders under Voss's black banner came to Kestrel's Rest. Forty men, maybe fifty. They didn't requisition. They didn't conscript. They burned it. Houses, boats, the drying racks, the shrine. They burned the fields while the barley was still green so nothing would grow back. Eleven people died that we know of. Possibly more — they took some away in wagons, and no one knows where the wagons went." He paused. "Two weeks before that, the same thing happened to Gullwatch, further north. Same banner. Same method. Same wagons."

Paloma heard herself say, "Why?" and hated how thin her voice came out.

"That's the question I've been following for a month, from tavern to tavern, from one frightened smuggler to another. And two nights ago, in Brackwater, I got an answer I didn't want." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and now he did look at Wren — briefly, carefully, the way you look at something you might startle. "The smuggler I questioned had carried freight for Voss's quartermasters. He'd seen the orders. He couldn't read them himself, but he had them read aloud by a man he trusted, and he remembered the words because they frightened him so badly he says he'll die with them. The orders named what the riders were looking for. Not grain. Not gold. Not levies." He drew a breath. "Something called the Drowned Name."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

Paloma became aware of her own heartbeat, of the fire's crackle, of the faint high singing that lived in the walls of her skull whenever certain words entered it — and the Drowned Name entered it now like a struck bell. Not a word she knew. A word she *recognized*, the way you recognize a face across a crowded market, someone you loved once and lost.

"The dead have been saying it," she heard herself admit. Her voice sounded far away. "For months. Fragments. In the whalebone especially — deep-water bone, old bone. They don't say it whole. They can't. But they circle around it. Like birds over something in the water." She looked up at him. "What is it?"

"I don't know. Neither does anyone I've found — except that Voss wants it more than he wants the free cities themselves, and he's willing to burn every fishing village between here and the capital to find it. He believes it exists. He believes it can be *found*." Seren's gaze went, deliberately this time, to the shelf above Paloma's workbench — to the fragment of drowned temple stone sitting on a square of cloth where Wren had placed it five days ago, still faintly cold to the touch, still humming against Paloma's teeth when she came near it. "And I believe whoever sent that stone out into the world was trying to get it away from him."

* * *

Wren had climbed onto the bench beside the stone without either of them noticing. The child sat with legs dangling, one small hand resting flat on the fragment's surface, watching Seren Oduya with an expression far too old for the face it lived on.

"She understands you," Paloma said.

"Some of it. Watch her hands." And indeed, Wren's fingers were moving — not randomly, not a child's fidgeting, but in deliberate patterns, pressing and lifting against the stone in rhythms that made the air in the room feel suddenly thick. "That's how the First Tongue works, or so my grandmother claimed, and she was a liar about most things but not, I think, about that. It's a language partly of the hands. Partly of—" he hesitated, "—of elsewhere. Which is why the mute can carry it. Why it survives in places speech doesn't reach."

"How do you know any of this?" Paloma asked, and the question came out sharper than she intended. "An inquisitor. Your whole profession was built on making sure none of it survived."

He took that blow without flinching, which she resented almost more than if he'd flinched.

"Because I signed the papers," he said. "Eleven years of signing papers. Lists of names. Houses to search, relics to burn, families to relocate — that was the polite word, *relocate*, and I used it, and I slept at night, most nights, because the lists never had children on them. That was the rule I built my conscience out of. No children." His jaw worked. "Four years ago they gave me a list with a child on it. Nine years old, caught teaching her little brother a counting rhyme her grandmother had taught her — a rhyme in the old tongue, nothing more, no power in it, probably less power than a nursery rhyme anywhere else in the world. And I refused. I put down my seal and I refused, and they broke me for it. Stripped my rank, took my name off the rolls, put me out of the order with nothing. And I told myself for four years that refusing was enough. That walking away clean was the same as being clean."

"And it isn't," Paloma said.

"And it isn't." He laughed, short and joyless. "Because here's what I learned in Brackwater two nights ago, from a smuggler who talked because he thought I was still someone worth fearing: the rider captain who burned Kestrel's Rest kept a record. They all do — Voss demands documentation of everything, he's a bookkeeper's tyrant, every village, every wagon, every head. And the record of Kestrel's Rest listed what was taken. Grain, salt, boat iron, nets. And *one child, female, approx. seven years, escaped prior to cordon, pursuit unsuccessful.*"

The room went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to hold its breath.

Paloma turned slowly. Wren had stopped moving her hands. The child was looking at Seren with that same terrible composure, and then — only then — the composure cracked, just at the edges, the way ice cracks at the edges of a pond before it goes all the way through.

"They came at night," Seren said softly, and he was not reading from a record now; he was reading from the child's face. "Didn't they. You woke up because the dogs were barking, and someone — your mother, an aunt, the priestess, whoever kept you — put the stone in your hands and pushed you out through the fish-cellar and told you to walk north and not stop. And you walked. Alone. Seven years old, through the marsh edge, alone, until you smelled woodsmoke on a night wind and knocked on the one door in Saltmere that smelled of bone and salt and old prayers." He swallowed. "Did I get it right?"

Wren nodded. Once. Then the child crossed the floor — three steps, no more — and picked up the temple stone, and held it out to Seren Oduya in both hands, the way you offer bread, the way you offer a verdict.

Seren looked at it and did not take it. His hands stayed folded in his lap, and Paloma watched a man look at absolution and refuse to pick it up.

"No," he said hoarsely. "I don't get to hold that. I spent eleven years burning things like it. I've come to warn you, not to be forgiven. There's a difference, and I intend to keep it."

"Then warn us," Paloma said, "and be quick."

* * *

He was quick. He was, she realized, a man who had learned to pack a great deal into few words, the way refugees pack a great deal into few bundles.

Voss's riders had been seen at the mouth of the Sedge Road, a day south. Informers in Saltmere had grown suddenly rich in the past fortnight — paid, the pattern suggested, to listen for exactly the kind of whispers that followed a child who spoke a dead language. And the wagons that left Kestrel's Rest and Gullwatch had gone east, toward the capital, toward whatever place Voss kept for the people he wanted alive.

"He doesn't want the child dead," Seren said. "If he wanted her dead, the cordon would have closed tighter, or the record would have said *killed on sight*. He wants her found. Alive. Which means every soldier on that road has orders to take her gently, and every informer in this city has a price on her curls, and the only reason she's still free is that nobody knows where she is." He rose from the stool. "Which means the clock is running, and we are wasting its time."

"*We*," Paloma repeated.

He stopped at the door and turned. Firelight caught the scar through his eyebrow.

"I can't fix what I did," he said. "There isn't enough road in the world to walk it off. But I know how Voss's riders move, how they cordon, how they think, because I trained half their officers. I know which roads are watched and which captains take bribes and which ones burn the bribers instead. And you—" his eyes went to her workbench, to the bones laid out in their careful rows, "—you can hear the dead, and the dead apparently know what Voss is hunting, which makes you the single most dangerous person on this coast and the only person who might actually understand what we're carrying. So yes. *We.* Unless you'd rather wait for the knock at your own door."

Paloma looked at Wren. The child had come back to her side without seeming to move, and stood with one hand gripping the fabric of her apron — not clinging, exactly. Anchoring. As if Paloma were the fixed point and the rest of the world the weather.

Five years she had survived by belonging to no one. Five years of flinches covered with jokes, of listening to the dead because the living were too dangerous, of a life small enough to hide. And now there was a child with a god's secret in her mouth and a broken inquisitor on her doorstep and a word — the Drowned Name — ringing in her skull like a bell rung underwater, and every instinct that had kept her alive whispered *run alone, run small, run now.*

But the dead had been circling that name for months like birds over something in the water. And the dead, tonight, felt less like whispers and more like a tide coming in.

"We leave before dawn," she said. "North road, then cut west into the Sedge before first light — the marsh hides footprints and the riders hate wet ground. There's a relic-dealer in Brackwater called Mother Quill who's older than sin and discreet as a grave. If anyone can read that stone, it's her." She was already pulling her carving knives from their rack, wrapping them in oilcloth, her hands steadier than she had any right to expect. "And Oduya. Two conditions. You don't touch the child without her leave, and you don't lie to me. Not once. Not even kindly. I carve bone for a living — I will know."

Something flickered across his face at that — fast, quickly buried, a door closing on a lit room. Later, she would remember that flicker and wonder about it. Now she only saw him nod.

"One more thing," he said, buckling his blade back on. "The smuggler in Brackwater. Before I left, he asked me why I cared. I told him the truth — that I'd spent my life helping bury these people and I wanted to dig one thing back up before I died. He laughed at me." Seren's mouth twisted. "He said: *the sea gives back nothing it keeps, inquisitor. It only teaches you how deep you are.*"

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a dog began to bark — and then another, and another, a line of barking moving along the harbor street like a lit fuse.

Paloma blew out the lamp, took Wren's hand, and reached for the cellar door.
