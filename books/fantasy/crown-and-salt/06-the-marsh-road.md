## 6. The Marsh Road

The Sedge Marshes began where the road ended, which Seren Oduya had always taken as the world's way of saying it did not want visitors.

He stood at the edge of the reed sea with the dawn behind him and the water ahead of him — a brown, breathing plain stitched with channels and hummocks, smelling of rot and iron and something older underneath, like a cellar that had been closed for a thousand years. His boots were still crusted with Saltmere's mud. His sword rode his hip out of habit rather than need; in this country it would be useless as a spoon.

Behind him, Paloma Zhou adjusted the strap of her pack and looked at the marsh the way other people looked at an unpaid debt.

"You've crossed it before," she said. It wasn't quite a question. It was the tone she used when she wanted to know something without admitting she'd asked.

"Twice." He kept his eyes on the water. "Once escorting prisoners to the border wardens. Once running." He let that sit. "The second time taught me more."

"And which are we?"

"Neither, if we're careful."

The child stood between them, small hands buried in the folds of Paloma's coat, watching the water with an expression Seren couldn't read — not fear, exactly. Recognition, maybe. As if the marsh were a word in that terrible old language and the child understood every syllable of it.

Paloma crouched. "Wren," she said — and the name came easier now, three days old, worn smooth already with use. "We go through the water. You stay between me and Seren. If I say *still*, you don't move at all. Not a finger. Can you do that?"

Wren looked up at her. Then, deliberately, at Seren. The child's eyes went from his face to his sword and back again, and something passed across that small mud-scrubbed face that Seren had spent ten years learning to see in adults: assessment. Judgment. A verdict being weighed.

He'd been found guilty by better judges than this one. And hanged, in a sense, by worse.

"I'll carry you if the water's deep," he said. "If you'll allow it."

Wren tilted their head. Then held up both arms — a gesture so plainly regal, so absurdly commanding, that Paloma laughed. It was a rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut, and Seren realized he'd never heard her laugh before. He filed the sound away somewhere he didn't examine too closely.

They went in.

* * *

The marsh had its own grammar, and Seren knew enough of it to keep them alive.

Walk on the sedge islands, never the open mud — the mud was a mouth. Follow the water that moved, never the water that sat. When the reeds parted in a straight line, that was a channel, and channels meant boats, and boats meant people, and people in the Sedge Marshes were either smugglers or corpses waiting to be promoted.

By midmorning they had made perhaps two miles. It was slow, brutal going — each hummock a step, each step a negotiation with ground that could not be trusted. Paloma led when the footing was uncertain, her narrow body finding paths Seren's eyes couldn't see, and Seren took the rear, which was where he'd always preferred to be. In his old life, walking behind someone had meant following them to the pyre. He was trying to learn what else it could mean.

It was during one of these stretches — Wren riding his shoulders now, small fingers gripping his hair with surprising authority — that Paloma stopped so suddenly he nearly walked into her.

"Seren."

Her voice was wrong. Flat. Distant. He came around her carefully, Wren's weight shifting on his neck, and saw her standing over a hummock of black earth with her head cocked, the way she did when —

When she was listening.

He'd seen it twice since they'd fled Saltmere. Once at the toll bridge, where she'd frozen beside a fencepost carved with old horse-bone charms and then abruptly changed their route. Once in the night, sitting bolt upright by the fire, staring into the dark until he thought she'd been struck blind. She never explained. He never asked. There was a fragile treaty between them built entirely on questions neither would ask, and he guarded it the way he'd once guarded state secrets — badly, but with great sincerity.

But now she said, quietly: "There are bones here. Under us. A lot of them."

"The battle," Seren said. He knew the histories; everyone did, though no one spoke them aloud anymore. "The Marsh War. Three hundred years ago. The kings' armies drove the god-touched into the Sedge and burned the reeds around them." He hesitated. "That's the story they gave us in the seminary, anyway."

"And what's the story nobody gave us?"

He looked at her. Her eyes were fixed on the ground as though she could see through it — and maybe she could, he reminded himself. Maybe that was exactly what she could do. The realization still landed strangely in his chest, a stone dropped down a well that hadn't finished falling.

"They sang," he heard himself say. "The god-touched. That part wasn't in the sanctioned texts. I found it in a confiscated manuscript when I was young — a soldier's account. He wrote that the drowned god's people stood in the burning reeds and sang, and the water rose to meet them, and his comrades drowned in their armor while the singers walked away dry." He paused. "He also wrote that he heard his dead mother's voice among them, calling him home. The inquisitors cut those pages out of every copy they found. I was supposed to burn the last one." He swallowed. "I kept it instead. That was the first thing I ever stole from the order. It wasn't the last."

Paloma turned her head slowly and looked at him — really looked, the way she almost never allowed herself — and for a moment the whole marsh seemed to hold its breath around them.

Then she knelt, pulled her knife, and cut a plug of black earth from the hummock's side.

"What are you doing?" he asked sharply. "Paloma—"

"Hush." Her voice had gone soft and far away. "I want to hear them properly."

She dug with her fingers now, fast and gentle, the way she handled everything — as though the material might speak if only she touched it right. A moment later she lifted something free: a bone. Long, green-black with age, furred with marsh moss. A femur, or part of one. Small. A youth's leg, Seren thought, and felt sick.

Paloma closed both hands around it and bowed her head.

Wren slid off Seren's shoulders without a sound and knelt beside her, mirroring her posture, small palms pressed flat against the wet earth. Watching them together — the bone-carver and the mute child, heads bent over the same silence — Seren felt like a man eavesdropping at a door he had no key for.

Then Paloma began to speak, and he understood she was translating.

"*...they told us the kings were men,*" she murmured, her voice taking on a cadence that wasn't hers, older, wearier, worn smooth like river stone. "*They told us the god below was hunger. But we were hungry too, and we were not gods. We marched because bread was promised and glory was promised, and the priests put salt on our tongues so we couldn't hear what the water was saying.*"

Seren went cold from the boots up.

"*We heard it anyway.* At night. Through our armor. Through the mud. It said: *come down, come down, the deep is wide enough for everyone.* And some of us did. Some of us just lay down in the shallows and let it take us, because anything was better than burning reeds and a king's promise.*"

Her voice cracked. She steadied it.

"*We were not fighting a war. We were the war's food.*"

Silence. The reeds whispered. Somewhere far off, a bird cried out over the water, thin and lonely.

Paloma opened her eyes. They were wet. She set the bone back into the earth with a tenderness that undid Seren more than any of the words had, and pressed the soil over it like closing a grave properly at last.

"They've been down there three hundred years," she said hoarsely, "and no one ever asked them how they died. Everyone asks *who won*." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, roughly, angrily, as though grief were an informer to be shaken off. "The god below. The kings above. Voss thinks he's inventing something new, burning villages to find the Drowned Name. He isn't inventing anything. He's just the newest king, doing what kings have always done to whatever sings."

"That account I saved," Seren said quietly. "The soldier who heard his mother's voice." He looked down at the hummock, at all the sleepers under it. "I think he's here."

"Then you should listen too someday," Paloma said, rising, brushing mud from her knees. "You have ears, Seren Oduya. You've just spent your whole life pretending they're decorative."

It was the closest thing to forgiveness anyone had offered him in six years. He carried it carefully for the rest of the day, like a coal cupped in bare hands.

* * *

They camped that night on the largest island they'd found — a low crown of sedge and stunted willow, ringed by a moat of dark water. Seren got a fire going with peat and dried reed-fluff, shielding the flame with his body and a canvas sheet, keeping it small. Out here even light was a confession.

Wren sat close to him.

Not to Paloma — to *him*, cross-legged in the firelight barely an arm's length away, watching his hands as he cleaned fish they'd netted from a channel at dusk. This was new. For three days the child had orbited Paloma like a moon, treating Seren with the wary courtesy one gives a loaded crossbow. Now, suddenly, proximity.

He didn't comment on it. He'd learned that trust, like marsh footing, collapsed under direct pressure.

Instead he cleaned the fish and laid the bones aside — spine, skull, the delicate fans of rib — in a neat row on a flat stone. Old habit. Waste nothing; the order had drilled frugality into its novices alongside obedience, and only one of those had ever washed out of him.

Wren reached out and picked up the skull.

Seren tensed. Paloma, across the fire, had gone very still, her carving knife pausing over a half-finished charm.

The child turned the little bone in the firelight. Then looked up at Seren and made a sound — not words, not the First Tongue, just a soft questioning note, and pointed at the fish skeleton, then at their own mouth, then at Seren's.

"You're asking why I keep them," he translated. Wren nodded solemnly. He considered lying, decided against it — lies curdled fast around this child; Seren suspected Wren could taste them. "Because bones remember what they were part of," he said. "And because throwing them away carelessly seems..." He searched for the word. "Disrespectful. Even a fish fed us. The least we owe it is to be tidy about its remains."

Wren studied him for a long moment with those ancient eyes in that small muddy face.

Then the child did something that stopped Seren's heart: reached out, took the half-finished charm from Paloma's kit — she permitted it, watchful — and pressed it into Seren's hand, folding his fingers closed around it.

It was whalebone, he realized. Smooth, warm from her pocket, carved with a spiral pattern that hurt to look at directly, the way bright snow hurts.

"For me?" he asked, stupidly.

Wren nodded. Pointed at his chest. Then at the fire, and made the soft sound again — and this time Seren heard it differently, heard in it something like the word Paloma used when she talked to the child at night, the low soothing syllable that needed no translation in any language:

*Safe.*

Across the fire, Paloma was staring at him with an expression he couldn't parse — suspicion and wonder fighting for the same ground.

"He doesn't give things away," she said slowly. "Three days. He hasn't given anyone anything. Children who've run through what he's run through—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Why you?"

"I don't know," Seren said honestly.

But he thought he did. He thought about the fish bones laid in a neat respectful row. About the confiscated manuscript hidden for twenty years in a false bottom of his trunk. About a girl in a border village, years ago, kneeling in front of an inquisitor's pyre-log pile while her own order waited for his signature — and how he had refused, and ruined himself, and saved no one, because his superior had simply signed in his place and called it mercy.

Children knew. Children always knew the difference between a man who was sorry and a man who was safe, and they forgave the first kind nothing and trusted the second kind everything, and Seren Oduya had spent six years assuming he could never be the second kind.

He looked at the whalebone in his palm until his eyes blurred.

"Thank you," he managed.

Wren smiled — the first true smile Seren had seen on the child's face, gap-toothed and luminous and heartbreakingly ordinary — and curled up against Paloma's side, and was asleep between one breath and the next, the way only children and soldiers can sleep.

* * *

He woke near midnight because the fire was dying and Paloma was gone.

Panic arrived before thought — six years of exile had wired panic into him like sinew — and then he saw her, ten paces off at the island's edge, standing ankle-deep in the black water, motionless. The moon was up, high and merciless, laying silver roads across the marsh in every direction. She looked like a woman standing at the intersection of all of them, unable to choose.

He approached slowly, announcing his steps. She didn't turn.

"There are thousands of them," she said, without preamble. "Under the water. All along this route. I thought it was just the battle site. It isn't. They're everywhere, Seren. Layer on layer. The marsh is *made* of them." Her voice was steady in the way ice is steady. "They talk to each other. Down there in the dark. They've had three hundred years to talk."

"What do they say?"

"All kinds of things. Names they forgot. Bread they were promised." A pause. "But mostly they sing. Fragments of it. The same song, over and over, wearing grooves into each other like water wears stone." She finally turned to look at him, and the moonlight made her eyes strange, pale, almost silver. "It's the god's song, Seren. The one the god-touched sang in the burning reeds. These men died hearing it, and it stayed in them, and now it's in the bones I carve, and it's—" She lifted her hand, and he saw she was holding a fragment of grey stone: Wren's temple-shard, which she must have taken from her pack. It caught the moonlight and threw it back wrong, rippling, like reflection on moving water. "It's in this. It's been singing in my kit for days and I thought it was just my gift. It isn't. The stone is *singing*. And tonight the dead in the marsh started answering it."

The hair rose on Seren's neck.

"Answering how?"

"Calling it by name. Or trying to. They only have pieces — a king broke the name apart, that's what the war was *for*, don't you see? They didn't march to kill the god's people. They marched to take the name so it could never be spoken whole again." She laughed, short and terrible. "And now a mute child walks into Saltmere carrying a piece of it in one fist, and the usurper is burning villages looking for the rest, and every dead soldier in this marsh can feel the fragments waking up."

The water around her ankles shivered. Seren would swear to his grave it wasn't wind.

"Paloma," he said carefully. "What are they saying *to you*?"

She looked back out across the miles of dark water, and when she answered, her voice was very quiet.

"They're saying she's coming. The little singer. They're saying the name is gathering itself, piece by piece, and it chose a child to hide inside because no king thinks to search a child." She closed her fist around the shard. "And they're saying we have to get to Brackwater before the next new moon. Because that's when the tide goes out far enough to show the sunken temple doors. And that's where the rest of the truth is buried."

Far off across the marsh — so far it might have been imagination, except that Wren, asleep by the dead fire, sat up suddenly with eyes wide open and listened too — something vast and patient turned over beneath the water.

In the morning, they would walk north, toward Brackwater, toward relic-dealers and translators and every danger a city holds for people carrying secrets in their pockets.

But that night, none of them slept well. Because the marsh sang, softly, all night long — and for the first time in three hundred years, something in the deep was singing back.
