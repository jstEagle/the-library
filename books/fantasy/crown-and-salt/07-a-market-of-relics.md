## 7. A Market of Relics

Brackwater announced itself by smell before sight: tar and old fish and the green rot of harbor weed, all of it layered over something sweeter — spice smoke from the river markets, cinnamon cut with clove. Paloma stood at the top of the levee road with Wren's small hand fisted in her skirt and let the city wash over her like a tide. Five years since she'd last come here. Five years since she'd had reason to risk it.

Below her, the city spilled down terraces of wet stone toward a harbor crowded with masts. Banners snapped in the river wind — no king's colors here, only the guild flags of the free cities, herring-silver and eel-green, and above them all the plain white pennant of the Concord, symbol of nothing except the stubborn refusal to kneel. For now.

"They'll be watching the roads," Seren said at her shoulder. He'd pulled the gray hood low over the scar that split his eyebrow; even disgraced, even stripped of his office, he had the kind of face that made people look twice and then look away. "Voss's riders were two days behind us at the ford. Word travels faster than horses on the river."

"Then we don't take the main quays." Paloma shifted Wren to her other hip — the child weighed almost nothing, a bundle of sticks wrapped in skin — and studied the terraces. "There's a market under the fish-hall stairs. Relic-dealers. The kind who don't ask where things come from because they don't want to know."

"You know it."

"I sold there. Before." Before Saltmere, before she'd learned to carve small enough and quiet enough that nobody wondered why her charms worked when other carvers' didn't. She started down the levee steps before Seren could ask anything else.

Wren walked between them, one small hand in each of theirs, and did not speak. The child never spoke — not in any tongue Paloma knew. But the temple stone fragment rode in the pouch against Wren's chest, and all morning it had been *humming*, a frequency Paloma felt in her back teeth like a struck tuning fork. The closer they came to water, the louder it sang. Or maybe that was her imagination. Maybe it was everything else — the drowned things beneath the harbor mud, the bones of ships and sailors layered six deep under Brackwater's foundations, whispering in their sleep. She kept her head down and her gift leashed tight, the way she'd learned to do in crowds, gathering the voices close instead of letting them spill.

The market under the fish-hall stairs was exactly as she remembered: a crooked alley roofed in salvaged ship-timber, stalls crammed into alcoves barely wide enough for a body, everything for sale and nothing labeled. Dried seahorses strung like beads. River-glass sorted by color. A woman selling what claimed to be saint's fingerbones and were, if Paloma's ears were right, pig knuckles cured in brine — the dead weren't in those at all, just a faint residue of the butcher's own grandfather, bored and mumbling. Paloma almost smiled. There was comfort in fraud. Frauds couldn't be burned.

She led them past three stalls before she found the one she wanted, tucked at the alley's dead end where the lamplight struggled to reach: a table of true relics laid out with a dealer's careless honesty, each one humming faintly to her ears like separate notes of some discordant chord. And behind the table, an old man mending a net that would never hold water again.

His hair had gone the color of driftwood and his hands shook, but Paloma recognized the tremor — not palsy. Precision. Hands that had done fine work for seventy years and now couldn't stop doing it even when the work was only knots.

"Marek Salt-Row," she said quietly.

The old man's needle stopped. He looked up, and his eyes were milk-pale but sharp underneath, the way old ice is sharp. "Now that's a name I haven't heard called honest in twenty years," he said. "Most folk around here know me as Uncle Fennel, seller of lucky charms, absolutely genuine, no refunds." His gaze slid to Seren, read the sword hidden by the coat, dismissed it, came back to Paloma. "And who's asking?"

"Paloma." She let him see her face. "I used to buy whalebone off a boat out of Sedge's Landing. You gave me fair weight and told me not to come back."

"I remember." Something moved behind the milky eyes. "You've gotten worse at following instructions."

"I've gotten older." She set two copper bits on his table, the old courtesy price, and watched his shoulders drop a fraction when he recognized it. "I need a translator, Marek. Someone who reads First Tongue script. You deal in relics from before the Kingdoms — you must know who still can."

Marek put down his net very slowly. Around them the market went on — haggling, laughing, a child shrieking over a tray of glass beads — but the little dead-end alcove seemed to draw inward, the way a room does when a door closes.

"Who taught you that name?" he asked. "First Tongue. Who told you there was such a thing?"

"A dealer. Years ago." A lie shaped like a truth; a smuggler had said it once, drunk, and she'd carried the phrase like a coin she couldn't spend. "It doesn't matter. What matters is this."

She looked at Wren. The child stared up at her, and after a moment — a hesitation Paloma had learned to read as consent — drew the stone fragment from the pouch and set it on the table among Marek's wares.

The chord of the market changed.

Every relic on that table sang at once, or seemed to; the hum in Paloma's teeth became a bell-stroke, deep and vast, as if something far below the harbor floor had rolled over in its sleep. Marek's hand jerked back from the stone. His milky eyes went wide, then wider, and Paloma watched an old man's composure crack like river ice in spring.

"Where," he said, and his voice had gone thin, "*where* did this child get this?"

"We were hoping you could tell us—"

"*Sit down.*" It was absurd — a shaking old man ordering them in a market stall — but Seren sat, and Paloma realized she was sitting too, pulling Wren onto the bench beside her, because some voices carry command in their bones. Marek glanced up and down the alley, then reached beneath his table and turned something. Above them, a curtain of knotted nets slid across the mouth of the alcove, closing them in with lamplight and dust and the singing stone.

"That," Marek said, "is temple stone. Not a carving of it, not a chip of some ruin sold to tourists. That is dressed granite from the Deephold, the sunken temple below the Tidereach, and it has not been seen above water in four hundred years." He leaned across the table, and this close Paloma could see the pulse jumping in his throat. "Four hundred years, girl. The last time a piece of it surfaced, the man who carried it was flayed in the capital square, and every person who had touched it in passing was drowned in a sack. That was under the old kings. Voss would do worse. Voss would do it to the whole street."

Seren shifted. "You know about Voss."

"Everyone knows about Voss." Marek spat, precise and contemptuous, into a corner jar. "Two villages burned on the coast road. My cousin's grandchildren were in one of them." His eyes came back to the stone, and his voice dropped until Paloma had to lean in. "But you didn't come here for history. You said translator. Which means the child speaks it. Doesn't she? The First Tongue."

Paloma's arm curled around Wren without her permission. "Why does that matter to you?"

"Because—" Marek stopped. Closed his eyes. When he opened them, the sharpness had changed quality; it was grief now, old and banked, the kind that has been carried so long it has become structural, load-bearing. "Because I have been waiting thirty years for someone to walk into this stall and ask me that question, and I swore I'd know it when it happened, and I was wrong, because I thought it would be someone tall." He laughed, wet and short. "Girl. Paloma. How do you carve?"

"What?"

"Your charms. The ones that work. The fishwives' charms, the birthing charms, whatever it is you do that made you good enough at hiding it to survive five years in Saltmere. How do you *do* it?"

The market noise beyond the net-curtain seemed very far away. Paloma heard herself say, "I listen."

"To what?"

"To the bone." Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, flat and distant, like speech carried over water. "There are voices in old material. Whalebone mostly. Sometimes knucklebone, river-glass if it's been buried. I hear them, and I shape the charm around what they say, and it works, and I have spent my whole life making sure no one ever asked me why it works."

Marek nodded slowly, the way a man nods at confirmation of a diagnosis he's suspected for decades. "And your mother? Could she hear?"

"My mother carved charms that didn't work." The words came harder now. "Plain ones. Sold them honestly and starved politely. She thought my grandmother was mad."

"And your grandmother?"

The bell-stroke of the stone was still humming through the floor, through the bench, through Paloma's ribs, and suddenly it seemed less like the stone's voice and more like an accompaniment to something rising inside her own chest. "My grandmother," Paloma said, "talked to the sea. All day. Sat on the rocks at Sedge's Landing and talked to the sea, and the village women brought their sick children to her, and when the king's men came—" She stopped. Swallowed. "When the king's men came, I was already gone. My mother sent me inland with a whalebone comb and told me never to carve where anyone could see. I was nine years old."

Nobody spoke. Beyond the curtain, a fishwife's laugh rose and fell like a gull's cry.

"Come here," Marek said gently. He turned his own wrist over on the table, palm up, and pushed back the frayed sleeve.

Paloma leaned forward. On the inside of the old man's forearm, faded nearly to nothing, inked in the style of a people long scattered: a spiral. Not a decoration — a script, characters wound tight along the curve, and even faded, even inert, Paloma's ears caught the faintest thread of voice coming off the old tattoo like heat off stone at dusk. One word. She couldn't parse it. But she *heard* it.

"What is that?" Seren asked quietly.

"A priestess's mark," Marek said. "Or what's left of one. My grandmother wore the full set — both arms, throat, spine. She was Tidereach-born, of the line of the Deephold, and she married a salt-boiler and kept the marks covered her whole life and taught my father nothing, because teaching got the line exterminated." He looked at Paloma, and his milk-pale eyes were terribly kind. "The order of Voss that hunts speakers now — it isn't new, girl. It's old wine in a new tyrant's skin. Four hundred years ago the priestesses of the drowned god kept the coastal kingdoms alive — tides, storms, harvests of the sea, the speaking of the dead so the living wouldn't fear them. Then the kings decided a power that answered to no crown was a power too dangerous to exist, and the Temple War broke them. Hunted them down coast by coast, village by village. Burned the records. Sank the Deephold itself with the priests inside rather than let it stand." His finger traced the air above the stone fragment without touching it. "And the ones who survived — the ones with the hearing in their blood — they scattered. Married fishermen. Salt-boilers. Bone-carvers. Hid the gift so deep in themselves their own children thought it madness."

The floor seemed to tilt. Paloma gripped the edge of the table.

"The voices," she said. "The ones I hear. They're not random. They're not ghosts happening to haunt the materials."

"They're the choir your blood was built to conduct." Marek spread his trembling hands. "Every speaker alive today is descended from the Deephold line. Every single one. The hearing doesn't skip sideways into strangers, child — it runs in blood, thin as water in some generations, thick as yours in others. Your grandmother wasn't mad. She was untrained. Untrained and powerful and alone, talking to the sea because the sea was the only thing left that answered her." His voice roughened. "Mine went quiet in the end anyway. Took her own life at sixty rather than be taken. Left me a spiral tattoo and a warning: *never carve where anyone can see*. Sound familiar?"

Paloma could not answer. Something enormous was moving through her, slow as a tide, and she could not yet tell whether it was grief or fury or a terrible, vertiginous relief — the feeling of a lock turning after a lifetime of carrying a key without knowing what it fit. All those years. All those years of listening through whalebone and telling herself it was a trick, a curse, a flaw to be managed. *An inheritance.* She looked down at Wren, tucked against her side, and the child looked back with those dark, patient eyes.

"And her?" Seren asked. His voice was careful, controlled, the voice of a man handling something that might go off. "The child. The stone sings for her. She says words that make Paloma's bones ring like bells."

Marek looked at Wren for a long moment. Then he did something Paloma hadn't seen him do: he bowed. Shallow, seated, half-hidden by the table — but unmistakably a bow, the obeisance of a servant to something the servant had believed lost forever.

"I can't read what she carries," he said. "The full script died with the Deephold priests. But I know the *shape* of it. And what she's carrying, child—" his voice gentled further, addressing Wren directly now, though the girl gave no sign of understanding, "—what you're carrying is not a message. Messages are written on paper. That stone comes from the inner sanctum of the Deephold, and the inner sanctum held only one thing, and every story my grandmother ever whispered after midnight agrees on what it was."

"The Name," Paloma whispered. The word came out of her unbidden, and the stone rang in answer, soft and vast, and Marek flinched as though slapped. "Don't—" She pressed her hand flat over her own mouth. "Sorry. I don't even know why I said—"

"Because your blood knows," Marek said hoarsely. "That's what I'm telling you, girl. The hearing isn't a curse. It's an *inheritance*, and inheritances come due." He dragged a rag across the table, not cleaning anything, just needing his hands to move. "Here's what matters tonight. Voss has burned two villages looking for this. He'll burn ten more. And he has scholars, torturers, bone-readers of his own — lesser lines, corrupted lines, but lines. If he learns what that child is, no free city will shelter her, because no free city can stand against what he'd bring. So you cannot stay in Brackwater, and you cannot travel openly, and above all—" his milk-pale eyes fixed on Paloma with sudden ferocity, "—you cannot let anyone else examine that stone. Half the dealers in this market would sell you to Voss's men for the price of a boat, and the other half would sell you cheaper."

"Can you help us?" Seren asked. "Hide us, move us — anything?"

Marek was quiet for a moment. Beyond the net curtain, the market was beginning to close for evening; shutters clattered somewhere down the alley, and the lamplight through the knots was going amber and dim.

"There's a captain," he said slowly. "Runs grain and contraband upriver, past the border stations. Owes me more than he likes to admit. He'll take three passengers and a crate of 'relics' north — for a price." His mouth twisted. "There's always a price. That's the next problem, and it's yours, because I can pay his fee but not what he'll actually want once he sees what's in the crate."

"What will he want?" Paloma asked.

Marek looked at her — at her hands, specifically, the scarred knuckles, the fine white bone-dust ground permanently into the creases — and his expression was pitying and grim.

"Something that speaks," he said. "He collects them. And knowing you, girl—" he tapped the table, right beside the singing stone, "—I'd guess you have at least one thing on you that talks back."

Her hand went to the inner pocket of her coat before she could stop it, and Marek's eyes followed, and she saw him understand, and hated him gently for it.

* * *

They stayed that night in the loft above Marek's stall, behind three locked doors and a wall of false relic crates, and Paloma did not sleep. She sat cross-legged on the bare boards with her coat folded in her lap and the pocket bulging, and listened.

Inside the pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a single carved bead of gray whalebone, no bigger than a plum pit. Her grandmother's voice lived in it. Not a recording — Paloma had never been able to make herself use that word — but a *presence*: the low salt-hoarse murmur she'd heard on the rocks at Sedge's Landing, saying things to the sea that Paloma had been too young to parse and had carried, unread, for twenty years, the way you carry a letter you're afraid to open. She had carved it in the year after the wardens burned her grandmother's memory out of official record. She had never once let it speak.

The stone fragment lay on the boards beside her, ringing its one deep note. Wren slept against Seren's bedroll across the loft, and even asleep the child's fingers were curled toward the stone, and Paloma thought: *the priests hid it in a living line. In blood. In girls on rocks talking to the sea.*

In her.

"Grandmother," she whispered to the bead in the oilcloth, so quietly that even the dead had to strain. "What am I?"

Far off in the harbor, a ship's bell tolled the middle watch. And from the loft's single high window, out over the black water of the river, Paloma heard — faint, layered, unmistakable — the drowned dead of Brackwater begin, for the first time in her life, to sing back.
