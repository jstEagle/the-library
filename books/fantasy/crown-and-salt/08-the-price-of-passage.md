## 8. The Price of Passage

The border at Graywater was a river and a lie.

Seren Oduya had crossed a dozen borders in his years as an inquisitor — some with writs, some with bribes, one with a forged death certificate bearing his own name — and he had learned that every border was the same animal wearing different skins: a narrow place where greed wore a uniform and called itself law. The Sedge Marshes had been three days of sucking mud and dead men whispering, but they had at least been honest about what they were. Graywater was worse. Graywater had a customs house.

It sat on the near bank of the river like a fat spider in its web, all tarred timber and green copper roofing, its pilings sunk into the shallows where the marsh finally gave up and became water. A chain floated across the current, thick as a man's wrist, and beyond it the ferry barges waited under the eyes of four soldiers in Voss's gray-and-gold. Not his soldiers, exactly — the captain here flew the usurper's colors because flying anything else got you hanged from your own roof beam, but Seren could read the difference the way he'd once read guilt in a suspect's hands. These were border-men. Paid too little, bored too long, hungry for whatever a traveler might be persuaded to part with.

He watched them for two hours from the reed bank before he went down to negotiate, because that was the other thing he knew about borders: the watching mattered more than the crossing.

* * *

Paloma waited where he'd left her, back against a leaning alder, with Wren tucked into the shadow of her cloak. She had the child's hand in hers, and Wren — who flinched from Seren still, who flinched from everything that moved too fast or spoke too loud — did not flinch from her. Three days in the marshes had done what three days of kindness always did: it had built something small and load-bearing between them, and Seren found he could not look at it directly without feeling the old ache behind his sternum, the one shaped like everything he'd broken.

"Well?" Paloma said.

"Four guards. Captain's name is Dree Halloway, if the ferryman I bought wine for tells true. He takes coin, but not enough of it — Halloway's real wage is confiscation. Anything interesting crosses his chain, he keeps a piece." Seren crouched, lowering his voice though there was no one within half a mile but gulls and the dead. "Which means we have a problem."

Paloma said nothing. She understood faster than most officers he'd served with. Her eyes went, just for a heartbeat, to the leather roll she carried slung across her body — the one that never left her, that she slept with beneath her head like a soldier sleeps with a knife.

"No," she said.

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"I know what you were going to say." Her jaw set. "You were going to suggest we show him nothing, pay him nothing, and find another way over. And I'm telling you there is no other way. I've listened to this river since dawn." She touched her ear, briefly, an unconscious gesture he'd come to recognize — the bone-carver consulting her ghosts. "Eleven bodies in the mud downstream. Men who tried the ford at night. The river took them, and then Halloway's men fished them out and hung what was left on the pilings as a lesson. There is no other way."

Seren looked out at the customs house, at the gray-and-gold snapping in the wind off the water, and hated how easily the arithmetic came to him. That was the curse of his former trade: once an inquisitor, always able to count the cost of a thing in other people's flesh.

"So we bribe him," he said. "Properly. Enough that he waves us through and forgets our faces."

"With what? You gave the last of your silver to the wine."

"I have my seal ring. It's worth something as metal, nothing more — the order struck me from their rolls, the crest means nothing now except danger to whoever holds it." He turned the ring on his finger; the tarnished silver caught no light at all under the cloud cover. "And you have your charms."

Her whole body went still. Not the stillness of fear — Seren had seen fear enough to know it — but the stillness of a door being barred.

"The charms are for sale," she said carefully. "Some of them. The ones that are only work. Whalebone wind-chimes. Knucklebone luck-pieces. I can part with those."

"But?"

She didn't answer at once. Wren made a small sound — not words, never words anyone but Paloma could parse — and pressed closer to her side. Paloma's hand settled on the child's hair, smoothing it, an anchor thrown both ways.

"There's a roll inside the roll," she said at last. "Bones I don't sell. Bones I won't." Her voice had gone flat in the way voices do when flatness is all that's holding them up. "Speaking-bones. Each one carved from a person I loved, holding what's left of their voice. My mother's knuckle. My father's wrist-bone. My grandmother's—" She stopped. Started again. "My grandmother's finger-bone is in there. It holds her singing. She used to sing while she worked, and when I carve, when I need steadiness, I hold that bone and I hear her sing, and my hands stop shaking. That's what's in the roll, Seren. Not merchandise."

The wind moved the reeds. Somewhere out on the water, a gull screamed once and quit.

"I wasn't going to ask you to sell it," Seren said.

"You were thinking it."

He couldn't deny it. He'd been thinking it since the customs house came into view, the way he'd once thought in evidence and leverage, the way his training reached out its cold hands toward whatever was closest. "I was weighing it," he admitted. "That's what I do. What I did. Weighing what a thing costs against what it buys. It's a filthy habit and I'm not going to pretend I've cured myself of it in one winter of disgrace."

Something flickered across her face — not softness, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman who also carried a filthy habit she hadn't cured.

"What would it buy?" she asked quietly.

"A captain's blindness. Our lives. Wren's life." He let the silence sit. "That's the honest weight of it. I wish it weren't."

* * *

They spent the rest of the daylight preparing, and preparing meant arguing, and arguing meant learning each other, which Seren suspected neither of them wanted and both of them needed.

Paloma laid out her saleable stock on a spread of oilcloth: eleven pieces, each one beautiful in the way that only functional things are beautiful. Wind-chimes that rang warnings when storm-wind rose. Luck-knuckles that grew warm before bad news. A rib-slat charm, whalebone etched with tide-marks, that fishermen swore found the safe channels. Seren watched her handle each one and understood that even these were not nothing — every charm was carved listening to some dead voice, every piece a collaboration with someone gone. Selling them was a kind of introducing strangers into her family.

"They'll want to test one," she said. "Halloway especially, if he's the type I think he is. Informers and confiscators are always half-believers — that's why they're so frightened. So I'll let him test the wind-chime. It'll sing for him. Small magic, harmless magic, the kind that lets a greedy man feel like he's gotten away with something rather than that he's touched something holy."

"And if he wants more than the chime?"

"Then we're past negotiating and into robbing, and you'll do what you do." She glanced up at him. "Can you? Still? Four armed men."

Seren considered lying and decided, for reasons he didn't examine, not to. "Two, maybe. If it starts fast. If it goes long, I'm an old man with a sword against four young ones with crossbows, and you should take Wren and run and not look back."

"We don't leave people," Paloma said. Simply, the way you state a law of nature. "That's the rule now. I decided it in the marshes. Nobody gets left."

He wanted to ask who she'd left, once, that she'd made it a rule. He didn't. There were questions you earned, and he was running a deep deficit.

* * *

They went down at first light, when the fog lay on the river like a drowned thing's shroud, because fog was the ally of anyone buying a blind eye. Seren walked ahead with his hood back, showing his face — an honest face, he hoped, worn honest by five years of exile. Paloma followed with the oilcloth bundle under one arm and Wren's hand gripped tight in the other, the child swathed in wool with the temple-stone fragment hidden in a false bottom of their boot. They'd argued about that too. Paloma had won. Seren was beginning to suspect she usually did.

"Halt." The nearest guard leveled his spear with the lazy menace of a man who'd done this ten thousand times. "Crossing?"

"Paying to cross," Seren said.

"Everyone pays. The question's how much." The guard's eyes slid past him to Paloma, to the bundle, to Wren, and lingered there with the particular appetite of a man calculating percentages. "Captain Halloway sets the rates. In there."

The customs house smelled of pitch, wet rope, and yesterday's fish stew. Halloway sat behind a desk scarred by knives and coins, a heavyset man with a beard going iron-gray and the small, restless eyes of someone who counted everything twice. He looked up, and Seren watched him do what confiscators do: inventory. Two adults. One child. One bundle of obvious value. No horse, no cart — travelers on foot carrying wealth were either desperate or dishonest, and both kinds paid well.

"Names."

"Seren Oduya. My wife, Maren. Our boy's mute — fever took his voice last winter. We're going to family in Brackwater." The lie came smooth, and he hated how smooth. He'd told harder lies to kinder faces and slept fine after. That had been the rot in him all along, he saw now: not cruelty, but ease.

Halloway grunted. "Brackwater. Long road for a family with no goods worth selling." His eyes came back to the bundle. "Open it."

Paloma opened it.

Even Seren, who knew what was coming, felt the air change. The charms lay in their rows, and above them — because she'd chosen to show everything, gambling that honesty read as poverty — the inner roll lay unrolled: six bones resting on faded cloth, each wrapped separately, each humming faintly to senses that shouldn't have been able to hear it. Seren heard nothing. But he saw Halloway hear something. The captain leaned forward despite himself, nostrils flaring, like a man catching woodsmoke from a fire he couldn't see.

"Charms," Paloma said. "Carved bone. I'm a carver by trade. The wrapped ones are commissions — raw stock, nothing finished."

"Raw stock." Halloway picked up the wind-chime and shook it, and it sang — three clear notes that had nothing to do with wind, notes that seemed to arrive slightly after the motion, like an echo traveling backward. The guard by the door made a small involuntary sound. Halloway set the chime down very carefully, the way men set down snakes.

"That's worth something," he said. "That's worth a lot. But it's not worth what you're hiding." His small eyes went to the wrapped bones. "What's in those?"

"Grandmother's finger-bone," Seren said quickly, before Paloma could speak, because he could hear the wrongness gathering in her silence. "Family custom. Keepsake. Worthless to anyone else."

"Everything's worth something to someone." Halloway smiled without warmth. "Here's how it is. Chain toll is five silver a head. That's fifteen, plus two for the boy, seventeen. You don't have seventeen. I can see you don't have seven. So we come to an arrangement instead." He tapped the desk. "One of those wrapped bones. Your choice. Then everyone crosses, and I never saw any of you."

"No," Paloma said.

"Maren," Seren began.

"No." She turned to face him fully, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "You can take my money, my work, my blood if it comes to that. Not those. Those aren't mine to give. They're theirs. They're the only part of them I have left, and I will not hand my grandmother's voice to a man who'll throw it in a drawer."

The room went quiet. The guard by the door shifted. Halloway's smile thinned, and Seren saw the calculation turn ugly — a woman defying a confiscator in front of his own men was a woman inviting an example to be made of her.

But it was Wren who changed the room.

The child stepped forward, out from behind Paloma's cloak, and looked up at Halloway with an expression far too old for that small face. And then Wren spoke — not the First Tongue, not this time, or maybe it was and it simply sounded like this to ears that had never heard it: a single word, low and resonant, that seemed to come from under the floorboards, from under the river, from somewhere beneath the world. Every lamp guttered. The stew pot on the stove went silent mid-boil. And Halloway's face drained to the color of the fog outside, because every man on this border had heard stories about what lived under the sea these days, and none of them had expected the stories to knock on his desk.

Wren pointed one small finger at the wrapped bones, then at Halloway, and shook their head.

Nobody breathed.

Then the child turned, took Paloma's hand again, and stood waiting, and the ordinary world resumed like a held breath released.

Halloway sat down slowly. He looked at the wrapped bones. He looked at the child. Seren watched him weigh it — really weigh it, the greed against the fear — and saw fear win by a margin so narrow it was nearly invisible.

"Take the chime," Halloway said hoarsely. "And get off my river."

* * *

The barge crossed in fog-thick silence, the poleman refusing to meet anyone's eyes, and the chain lifted for them as if by its own accord. On the far bank, when the mist swallowed the customs house and its green copper roof, Paloma finally spoke.

"You lied for me," she said. "In there. 'Maren.'"

"You lied better. 'Our boy.'" Seren managed half a smile. "We make a convincing family."

"Don't." But it wasn't sharp. It was tired, and underneath the tiredness something else — the sound of a wall developing a crack, not falling, but no longer quite solid either.

They walked inland, away from the water, Wren riding Seren's shoulders now — a trust that had arrived sometime during the crossing without either adult witnessing the moment it happened, the way most important things arrive. After a mile, Paloma stopped, unwrapped the inner roll, and looked at the six bones for a long moment.

"He would have taken one," she said quietly. "If Wren hadn't— I would have given it. In the end. I'd have handed my grandmother over to keep this child alive, and I hate that I know that about myself."

"It's not hate-worthy knowledge," Seren said. "It's just love, priced honestly. Everything costs. At least you know the price of yours."

She rolled the bones away and slung them across her body again, and they walked on toward Brackwater through country that grew greener with every mile, freer with every mile, and more dangerous with every mile — because free cities had walls, and walls had gates, and gates had men who asked questions.

It was near dusk, the sky bruising violet over the hills, when Wren stiffened on Seren's shoulders and grabbed a fistful of his hair hard enough to hurt, and pointed back the way they'd come.

Behind them, far off along the riverbank, smoke was rising from the customs house. Thin and black and deliberate. Someone was burning Graywater to the ground — and riding toward the smoke, small with distance but unmistakable, came riders in gray and gold, moving fast, fanning out across the road ahead.

Not chasing them. Sweeping. The way you sweep a field you mean to take.

"Wren," Seren said softly, turning the child toward the hills, toward Brackwater, toward whatever came next, "hold on."
