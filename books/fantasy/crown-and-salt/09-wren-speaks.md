## 9. Wren Speaks

The crossing to Brackwater had cost Paloma her grandmother's voice, and she felt the absence of it the way one feels a pulled tooth — a hollow place the tongue kept returning to. Three days on the border captain's barge, three days of watching Wren sit at the rail with the temple-stone fragment cupped in both hands like a bird that might fly if startled. The child had not spoken since the marsh. Not in the First Tongue, not in anything. Paloma had begun to hope that whatever had been coiled inside those strange syllables had gone back to sleep.

She should have known better. Nothing that sang through bone ever slept for long.

They took a room above a chandler's shop in Brackwater's low town, where the smell of tallow and tar masked them from the neighbors' noses and the landlord asked no questions because his own ledger had lines in it no warden would want read. Seren took the ground-floor room with sightlines to both the alley and the quay — old habits, he'd said, and would not meet her eyes when he said it. Since the marsh road, since she'd come back through the storm to find him keeping watch over a sleeping child with an expression like a man standing trial, something between them had been mended without being healed. A splint, not a set bone.

It was the third night, with rain coming down in ropes off the harbor, that Wren spoke.

* * *

Paloma was working at the little table by the shuttered window, finishing a commission of mourning charms for a fishwife who had lost two sons to Voss's press-gangs. Whalebone, soft as wax under her burin when you knew how to ask it. She did not ask it to speak — she never did anymore, not since Saltmere, not since she'd learned what listening cost — but the dead came anyway, seeping into the carved channels like water into chalk. The fishwife's sons were not in these bones; they had died at sea, and sea-drowned went below, to Him, or so the whispers said. What Paloma heard instead was the usual murmur: a grandmother complaining about a stove, a sailor humming a shanty half-remembered, the long gray static of lives already poured out.

Wren sat cross-legged on the pallet, watching her carve. The child watched everything now — Seren sharpening his knife, gulls fighting over offal, the rain. Watched with the terrible attention of someone memorizing a world they expected to be taken away.

"You can sleep," Paloma said. "Nobody's coming tonight."

Wren tilted their head — Paloma still could not think of the child as anything but *they*, though they looked perhaps nine years old, small for nine, with river-clay still worked so deep into the knuckles it seemed part of the skin. Then Wren opened their mouth, and out came not the ringing syllables that made her bones ache, but words. Common Tongue. Cracked from disuse, accented strangely, pitched wrong, but *words*.

"The stones are talking in your table."

Paloma's burin slipped and gouged a line across the mourning charm. She swore softly, set it down. "What?"

"Under your voice there's another voice." Wren crawled off the pallet and came to stand beside the table, bare feet silent on the boards. "I couldn't say it before. My mouth wouldn't do the shape of your words. But I've been—" the child frowned, hunting for the word, "—practicing. In my head."

"How long have you understood us?"

"Since the door." Since the night they had appeared at her lodgings in Saltmere, mud to the eyebrows, clutching a fragment of drowned temple stone while her carved bones rang like struck bells. Five weeks. Five weeks of hearing everything, understanding everything, saying nothing. Paloma felt the floor tilt gently under her certainties. Every conversation she'd had over the child's head — the arguments with Seren, the whispered calculations about flight and price — all of it had been heard.

"I'm sorry," Wren said, watching her face. "You looked scared when you talked about me. I thought if I was quiet you'd stay less scared."

Something in Paloma's chest folded in half. She crouched, bringing herself level with the child, and saw up close the exhaustion under their eyes, the same exhaustion she carried, worn on a body half her size.

"It's all right," she lied, gently. "You can talk whenever you want now. Even if it scares me. *Especially* then."

Wren considered this with grave seriousness, then nodded once, the way adults nodded, and Paloma thought: *who taught this child to nod like that?* And then, following the thought where it always led, where it had been waiting for her all along: *what exactly did we carry out of those burning villages?*

* * *

Seren came up the stairs an hour past midnight, wet to the shoulders, and stopped dead in the doorway when Wren said, "Your boots are leaking. You should stuff them with wool. The ferryman told you and you didn't listen."

The ex-inquisitor stared at the child for the space of three heartbeats. Then, being Seren, he did the practical thing first: crossed the room, checked the shutters, drew the bolt, and only afterward let himself react. His reaction was small — a slow exhale, a loosening around the eyes — but Paloma had learned to read him the way she read bone, in fine channels and buried grain.

"That's new," he said.

"They practiced," Paloma said. "In their head. For five weeks."

"Five weeks." He lowered himself onto the stool across the table, and she watched him wrestle with what she was wrestling with: every silence re-priced, every moment of the journey retroactively crowded with a witness. He rubbed his jaw, where the gray stubble was coming in silver. "Then maybe they can answer some questions we've been too much of cowards to ask."

"Seren—"

"No." He wasn't looking at Wren. He was looking at his own scarred hands flat on the table, as if checking they hadn't moved on their own. "I burned villages, Paloma. I know how this goes. We tell ourselves the child is safe, is just a child, needs time — and every day we don't ask, we're kinder, yes, and also we're deafer. Voss razed Hollowmere and Grey Anchor looking for something. Your relic-dealer in the market said the stone came from the Drowned Temple. I signed enough orders in my day to know what a search pattern looks like. They're not looking for a thing." He lifted his eyes at last, first to Paloma, then to the child standing solemn beside her chair. "They're looking for a person. Aren't they?"

The rain filled the pause. Wren climbed onto the stool opposite Seren — climbing, settling, arranging themselves with that unnerving adult deliberateness — and put the temple-fragment down between the three of them like a coin paying for something.

"The priests hid Him," Wren said. "Before your kingdoms. Before the salt walls. When the kings came with fire to take His name and wear it like a crown."

"Him," Paloma repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. Through the floorboards, through the chandler's stock of tallow and rendered fat, the dead had begun to stir — she could feel them pressing up like heat through a plank floor, hundreds of faint voices turning toward this small clear voice the way sunflowers turn toward light.

"The god below," Seren said quietly. "The drowned one. I was taught He was a story priests invented to scare sailors into tithes."

"He's not a story." Wren's voice stayed flat, matter-of-fact, which somehow made it worse. "He's old. He's under the water holding the world together, the priests said, like a keel-hold under a boat. And the kings wanted His name, because whoever speaks the name commands Him, and whoever commands Him commands every wave and every drowned soul and every—" the child's careful composure flickered, the first crack, "—every dead person there ever was. So the priests took His name out of the temple. Out of the stones. Stones can be broken open. Words in stones can be read."

"Where do you hide a name," Paloma said, "if not in stones?" But she already knew. She had known since Saltmere, since the first time her bones sang at this child's voice. She knew it the way you know a thing you have spent weeks refusing to know.

Wren looked at her with eyes that were far too old, and said:

"In a mouth. Names live in mouths. That's where they're truest. So the last priests of the Drowned Temple took His name and put it in the deepest safest place left in the world." A breath. "Inside a baby. Inside me."

* * *

Afterward, Paloma could never reconstruct the order of things. There was a stretch of time simply missing, like pages torn out. She remembered the sound of her own pulse. She remembered Seren very carefully not moving, very carefully not reaching for the child, because he had been an inquisitor and he knew what it meant when your body wanted to grab hold of something precious before the world could take it. She remembered the dead below the floor rising in a single tide of attention so dense that her skull ached with it, hundreds of drowned souls straining to hear the mouth that held their god's name, and her clamping down on her gift hard enough to make her nose bleed — a bright thread of copper dripping onto the whalebone charms, and the whalebone drinking it eagerly, greedily, the dead loving her blood the way they loved nothing else.

She remembered wiping her nose with her sleeve and asking, in a voice she did not recognize as hers: "Does it hurt? Having it in you?"

Wren considered this honestly, head tilted. "It's loud. Like standing in a doorway between a storm outside and a fire inside. Most of the time I can't tell which side I'm on." A pause. "When I say the words — the loud words, the ones that made your bones ring — the doorway opens all the way. At the ford, when the soldiers—"

"I know what happened at the ford," Seren said, too quickly, and Paloma saw it pass across his face again: the three riders going under the brown water, dragged down by nothing, by a word, their boots kicking at a surface that had suddenly decided to be hungry. He had written reports about such things, once. Filed them. Ordered pyres for the sources of them.

"Voss wants me alive," Wren said. It was not a question and barely a statement; it had the cadence of something recited, learned young. "The priests' rule was: carry the name until a speaker comes. Someone whose ears are open. Someone descended." The child's gaze moved, deliberate and terrible, from Seren to Paloma and settled there. "The stone dealers in the market said your grandmother's people were priestesses. Didn't they."

Paloma's hands were shaking. She put them in her lap where the child wouldn't see, an old trick, older than her gift. "They said."

"So I was brought to you." Wren said it without accusation, which was the unbearable part. "The ones who ran didn't know where else to run. Everyone who could hear was dead or hiding, except the stories said there was still a carver in Saltmere who talked to bones. So they gave me to the dark and pointed me at your door." The composure finally broke, all at once, like ice going out of a river, and the child was suddenly only a child — small, soaked-eyed, nine years old and carrying a god like a stone in the belly. "I didn't want to be a door. I just want somebody to keep me because of *me*. Not because of what's inside."

Paloma did not remember crossing the room either. But she found herself on her knees with the child crushed against her chest, Wren's face buried in her shoulder, thin arms gripping her back with the desperate strength of drowning things, and over the crown of that damp head she locked eyes with Seren Oduya, who sat motionless with tears running silently down into his beard, making no move to touch either of them, as though he had forfeited long ago the right to comfort anyone and knew it.

"We keep you because of you," she said into Wren's hair, hoarse. "Hear me. Whatever's inside you, whoever wants it — you are a child who leaks boots advice and hates eel porridge and practices words in your head so you won't frighten people. That's who lives in my house. The rest is cargo you never asked to carry, and we'll figure out how to unload it, and if we can't unload it we'll figure out how to guard it. But nobody—" her voice cracked, and she let it, "—nobody is ever going to open you up to get at what's inside. Not Voss. Not the priests. Not even the god Himself. Do you understand me?"

Wren nodded against her shoulder, and hiccuped, and after a moment laughed a little, wetly, at their own crying, and Paloma held on.

But underneath the tenderness, beneath the fierce warm animal relief of holding something living while the rain came down, her gift whispered to her, cold and precise as a burin-line: *the name can be spoken only once, and only by an open ear, and the child cannot carry it forever, and Voss has armies.* The arithmetic assembled itself in her mind unbidden, cruel in its neatness, and she shoved it down into the cellar of herself where she kept all the things she refused to count.

Not tonight. Tonight the child slept at last, truly slept, curled against Paloma's side on the narrow pallet while she sat awake with her hand resting on a small heaving back, feeling under her palm not just breath but something vaster breathing with it, tide-pulled, patient as deep water.

* * *

Near dawn, Seren shook her awake — gently, but his face told her before his words did. He stood in the gray light with a scrap of paper in his fist, and behind him, through the window, the harbor lay flat and strange, every fishing boat still moored, the whole quay empty as a held breath.

"Riders came through the south gate at first light," he said. "A herald, wearing Voss's black-and-gold, posting proclamations on every door." He handed her the paper. His hand was steady; his voice was not.

She read it twice, because the first time the words refused to mean.

*By order of Kaelen Voss, Protector of the Realm: the free city of Brackwater harbors a thief of holy things. Deliver the child to the Protector's custody within three days, and the city will be spared its lawful punishment. Withhold the child, and the Protector shall come and collect what is owed — with interest.*

Below the proclamation, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, a line that turned Paloma's blood to seawater:

*The Protector knows the child has found a Speaker. This changes nothing. Speakers die easily. Names do not.*

Paloma looked up. Down the length of the empty quay, gulls were screaming at something beyond the harbor mouth — and far out, past the breakwater, where the gray sea met the grayer sky, the water was swelling. Once. Slowly. As if something far below had turned over in its sleep.

"Wren," she heard herself say, waking the child with a hand already trembling. "Wren, wake up. I think your god knows we're here."
