## 10. The Usurper's Court

The throne room of Karvath had been built for kings who believed in mercy, which was why Kaelen Voss had the stained glass torn out and replaced with leaded panes of gray. Light came through them now like light through dirty ice — colorless, even, indifferent. He liked it that way. A ruler should never be flattered by his own hall.

He stood at the window with a cup of wine he had no intention of drinking and watched the city below. His city. Three years since he'd taken it, and still the bells rang at the wrong hours, still the fishmongers shouted in accents he hadn't ordered silenced, still somewhere in the warrens a child laughed without permission. Conquest, Voss had learned, was not an act but a condition — like a fever, or a debt. You did not finish it. You only kept paying.

Behind him, chains shifted against stone.

The sound was soft. It was always soft; he'd had the links wrapped in wool so the reader could rest. People assumed cruelty from him because it was simpler than assuming anything else. But Voss did not waste pain on things that served him. Pain was a tool, and tools were maintained, not broken.

"Come to the window, old man," he said. "The light is better."

"I prefer the dark." The voice came dry as chalk dust. "It's more honest about what it is."

Voss smiled without turning. Maeglin Sorrel had been a bone-reader for forty years before Voss conquered the city, and he had been chained in this tower for two of them — not for treason, but for accuracy. The old man read the dead too well. Kings paid readers to tell them their fathers were at peace. Maeglin told them the truth: that the dead were rarely at peace, and rarely quiet, and that some of them remembered exactly who had killed them.

"Your hands are worse," Voss said. "I can hear it when you work. The bones click differently."

"My hands are eighty years old, Kaelen. Everything clicks differently." A pause. The shuffle of slippered feet across flagstone. "You didn't summon me to ask after my health."

"No." Voss set down the untouched wine. "I summoned you because my riders found something in a burned village on the Salt Coast. Two burned villages now, if the reports are true, though I only believe half of what my riders write — they know I pay for urgency." He drew from his coat the thing he'd carried for six days, turning it in the gray light. A fragment of carved bone, no longer than his thumb. Whalebone, pitted with age, worked into the shape of a coiled serpent or perhaps a wave — the carving was old enough that the distinction had eroded. "Tell me what you hear in this."

Maeglin took it with both hands, the way one holds something either holy or dying. For a long moment there was nothing. Then the old man's breath changed — a small hitch, quickly mastered — and Voss watched his knuckles whiten around the fragment.

"Well?" Voss asked.

"Where did you get this?"

"A dead woman's pocket. Or nearly dead. My riders are thorough."

"This is priestess-work." Maeglin's voice had gone very careful, the way a man's voice goes careful when he is walking across ice and listening to it crack. "This is not charm-craft, Kaelen. This is older. This is listening-work." He looked up, and his eyes were milky where they should have been sharp. "There is a voice in here. Faint. A woman. She is singing something, over and over, and I cannot make out the words, but I know the shape of them. They are shaped like a name."

The room seemed to tilt, very slightly, and right itself.

"Say it again," Voss said quietly.

"They are shaped like a name. A long one. Old. The kind of name you don't speak so much as *enter*." Maeglin pressed the bone back into Voss's hand as if it burned him. "Take it away. There are voices in this that have been waiting a long time to be heard, and I don't want to be the door they come through."

Voss turned the fragment over once, twice. He felt nothing. He had always felt nothing — that was the joke of his life, the great iron punchline. The boy who heard the dead had become the man who couldn't hear a whisper.

* * *

He had been nine years old the first time, in the famine winter, in the fishing village of Harrow's End where the sea gave nothing and the king's tax collectors took everything anyway. His mother had died in the third month of that winter. He remembered her death less clearly than he remembered what came after: sitting alone in the cold loft above the salt-room, starving, and hearing her voice come up through the floorboards.

Not through the house. Through the *bones of the house*. Through the whalebone beam his father had set over the doorway when Kaelen was born — a charm-beam, a blessing, the kind of thing every fishing family hung and never spoke about twice. His mother's voice had come up through it thin and strange, like sound through water, saying his name. Saying *Kaelen. Kaelen. Come down, love. It's warm down here.*

She was dead. He knew she was dead; he had helped carry her to the burning ground himself, because there was no one else. And yet her voice had come up through the beam, night after night, patient and warm, and a nine-year-old boy does not ask whether the dead can lie. A nine-year-old boy goes down into the cold cellar where they keep the pickled fish and sits in the dark waiting for his mother, and hears her voice getting closer, and closer, and closer —

— until the village priestess found him there, three days later, half-frozen, talking back to nothing.

They hadn't punished him gently. That was the part he never said aloud, the part that curdled everything after. The priestess had held him down while they burned the beam out of the doorway, and the screaming of the bone — he had heard the bone scream, high and human — had gone through his skull like a nail, and afterward he had never heard the dead again. Never. Not once in thirty years. They called it cleansing. They called it mercy. They told him the voices were demons wearing his mother's face, and that he should thank them.

He had thanked them. That was the worst of it. Nine years old, starving, beaten, and he had knelt on frozen mud and *thanked* them for cutting the only warm thing out of his life.

He had spent thirty years learning what everyone else apparently knew by instinct: how to move among the living without hearing anything underneath. He'd been good at it. He'd been brilliant at it. He'd been so good at being deaf that he'd made himself deaf, and then he'd made himself king of the deafest country in the world, and none of it — none of it — had ever filled the hole where his mother's voice used to be.

So when the rumors began, three years ago — whispers from the coast about a drowned god whose true name could raise the dead and command the tide, about priestesses exterminated generations ago for knowing it, about a power that lived beneath the sea like a coal under ash — Voss had not dismissed them. He had *recognized* them.

Because he understood, better than any living soul, what it meant to have a voice cut out of you.

And he understood something else, something the priestesses and the free cities and all the pious keepers of the old ways refused to admit: that such power should not belong to the sea. It should not sleep in the dark where no one could reach it. It should belong to someone — someone who had earned it, someone who had suffered for it, someone who would never again kneel on frozen mud thanking the people who mutilated him.

It should belong to him.

"You've gone somewhere," Maeglin said. "Your face does a thing when you go somewhere. Like a door closing."

"I'm deciding how many men to send to the coast."

"Ah." The old man lowered himself onto the window bench with a grunt. "And how many is that?"

"All of them."

Silence. Outside, the bells of Karvath rang noon, wrong hours and all.

"You know what I hear in the dead, Kaelen?" Maeglin said at last. "In forty years, do you know the one thing I have heard most often? Not prophecies. Not curses. Regret. The dead are full of regret — it's practically the whole of what they're made of. And do you know what they regret most?"

"Enlighten me."

"Not the terrible things they did. The terrible things they did, they usually did for reasons they could defend." Maeglin folded his ruined hands in his lap. "What they regret is the warm things they refused. The invitations they declined. Every ghost I have ever spoken to, Kaelen, has wanted exactly one thing: to be let back in. To belong somewhere again. And every one of them learned, too late, that belonging cannot be taken. Only given."

Voss looked at him for a long moment. "You think I'm trying to take something."

"I think you're trying to take everything. It's rather your signature."

"The god's power will save these cities." He said it flatly, without heat, because it was true and truth needed no heat. "The free cities are fat and blind and they will fall to the first empire with the stomach to take them, unless someone takes them first and holds them. A god bound to a crown is a wall no army can breach. I am offering them salvation, Maeglin. They simply haven't agreed to it yet."

"And the child?"

The question landed softly and went deep, the way a knife does when it's thrown well.

"What child?" Voss said.

"The reports from the coast mention a survivor. A girl, perhaps. Small. The villagers in the second village — the one that still had living people when your riders arrived — spoke of a mute child who fled north with something precious." Maeglin's milk-pale eyes fixed on him with terrible gentleness. "I hear things, Kaelen. It's what you keep me for. And I hear that whatever you're hunting, it isn't a relic. Relics sit still. You're hunting something that runs."

For a moment Voss considered lying. It would have been easy; he lied beautifully, the way other men sang. But Maeglin read bones, and bones were honest, and there was a certain discipline Voss maintained with the few people he permitted near him: never lie to someone whose craft is truth.

"There may be a child involved," he said. "If the name lives in the blood of the priestesses, then yes — a child might matter. Alive."

"And when you find this child, this small frightened thing that has run from you across marshes and through burned villages — what will you do with her, Kaelen? Gently? Will you explain to her why she must give up what lives inside her?" Maeglin shook his head slowly. "I have read the bones of conquerors before. They all begin the same way. *The world hurt me, therefore I will fix the world.* And they all end the same way, kneeling in the ruins of the thing they built, hearing at last every voice they silenced."

"You describe my future with remarkable confidence for a man in chains."

"I describe it with remarkable confidence *because* I am a man in chains. Chains make one honest." The old man rose, joints crackling like green wood in fire. "Send your armies, Kaelen. Burn your villages. Hunt your child. But understand what you are truly doing. You are not seeking a god. You are seeking the moment when someone, anyone, looks at you the way your mother's voice sounded to you through that beam — warm, and calling your name, and meaning it. And no god can give you that. No crown. No conquest. Believe me. I have asked the dead, and the dead are unanimous."

Voss stood very still by the gray window. Below, the city went on ringing its wrong bells and selling its fish and laughing its unauthorized laughter, ten thousand lives that did not know how close he was to making them safe forever.

"The rider returns from Brackwater in three days," he said. "With names. With descriptions. If a speaker of the First Tongue walks the free cities, I will know her face before the week is out."

"And then?"

"And then," Voss said, "I will finally stop being hungry."

He left the old man chained in the tower with the honest dark, and went down to write orders that would put a thousand horsemen on the road by dawn.

Behind him, faint as breath, the carved bone in his coat began — very softly — to sing.

* * *

That night, in the map room lit by tallow candles, Voss spread the charts of the free cities and weighted their corners with candlesticks and his father's old knife. Brackwater. Sedge Marshes. The Salt Coast with its two black scars where villages used to be. He moved a wooden marker along the coast road, another toward the ford at Greywater, and paused with his finger resting on the marshes — trackless, drowning, useless to armies.

Which meant anything hiding there was hiding well.

A knock. His captain entered, salt-crusted, road-worn, and dropped to one knee.

"The informant from Brackwater, lord. Paid in full, as promised. Says a bone-carver in the night market — one who sells charms too fine for common trade — has taken in a stranger. A child. Mud to the knees, speaking gibberish, carrying temple stone."

Something moved through Voss's chest then, slow and enormous, like a whale turning under a boat. Not joy — he had trained joy out of himself decades ago. Something older than joy. Recognition. The feeling of a lock, rusted thirty years, beginning to give.

"A bone-carver," he repeated. "Who hears too much for her own good."

"Yes, lord."

He looked down at the map, at the little wooden markers, at the whole fat blind world arranged before him like a meal. Somewhere north, a woman who listened to the dead was holding a child who carried a god's name, and neither of them yet knew that the entire machinery of his empire had already begun to turn in their direction.

"Double the reward," Voss said. "For the child alive. And for the carver—" he lifted the candle, and the flame threw his shadow vast and crooked across the map of everything he meant to own, "—bring me her hands. She'll carve for me soon enough, or she'll carve for no one."

The captain bowed and withdrew. Alone in the map room, Voss stood a long time over the charts, listening — as he had listened for thirty years to a silence that never answered — for the sound of the sea.

Three days' ride south, in a cellar that smelled of bone-dust, a child slept clutching a fragment of drowned temple stone, and far beneath the waves something ancient stirred at the edge of a dream, hearing its name spoken for the first time in a generation — not aloud, not yet, but *shaped*, in the dark, by small desperate mouths learning the grammar of gods.

Soon, Voss thought. Soon he would hear his mother's voice again, or he would burn the world down trying — and either way, the hunger would finally end.

He pinched out the candle with his fingers and stood in the dark, unflinching, the way he had stood in the cold cellar at nine years old, waiting to be found.
