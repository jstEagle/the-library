## 12. The Sunken Temple

The dead led them north along the coast for three days, and they were good guides in the way that only the dead can be: patient, tireless, and utterly indifferent to whether you followed.

Paloma heard them first as a pull in the whalebone pendant she wore beneath her shirt — a faint tug, like a fish testing a line, strongest when they strayed from the cliff path and weakest when they turned back toward it. By the second day she had learned to read it the way sailors read wind. *Left here. Down through the black rocks. Not the beach; never the beach.* She did not ask why until the third morning, when the tug grew so insistent it woke her before dawn with her heart hammering and the taste of salt already on her tongue.

*Because the sea takes back what walks on sand,* whispered a voice through the bone, an old woman's voice, dry as cut reed. *We walk where water walked.*

She told no one. She was learning, slowly, which things to carry alone.

Wren, by contrast, had stopped being silent almost entirely — not in any language Paloma knew, but in that other one, the First Tongue, which now lived under Paloma's skin like a splinter working toward the heart. The child talked to gulls. To tide pools. To the driftwood they burned at night, which crackled back in answers only Wren could hear. And sometimes — this was the part that frightened Paloma most, more than wardens or informers or the memory of Brackwater's burning sky — Wren talked to *her* bones. The charms in her pack would chime softly in the dark, and she would wake to find the child sitting cross-legged beside them, listening, nodding at whatever the knucklebones said.

"They like you," Wren had said once, in the trade-tongue, halting but clear. It was the longest sentence the child had yet managed outside the First Tongue. "They say you listen better than most of the living."

Seren, standing watch, had pretended not to hear. But Paloma had seen his face in the firelight — the particular stillness of a man cataloguing evidence he doesn't want to believe.

Now, on the fourth dawn, the dead stopped pulling and began instead to *sing*.

It came through every bone she carried at once, a low harmonic thrum that set her teeth aching, and she stood up in the grey light with her hand pressed flat against her sternum. "Here," she said. Her voice cracked. "It's — we're here."

Seren rose too, hand going to the knife at his hip out of five years of habit he couldn't seem to shed. "Where?"

Paloma didn't answer, because she was looking past him, down the slope of black basalt where the land fell away into a drowned valley. The tide was far out — farther than a tide should go, pulled by some deep breath of the world — and there, revealed in the wet gleam of morning, lay the ruins.

They had been a temple once. You could see it even ruined: the great concentric rings of stone descending terrace by terrace into the sea, each ring carved with figures that time and salt had worn to suggestions — women with their arms raised, women kneeling, women holding up small bright shapes that might have been stars or might have been bones. Half the structure stood above the waterline, barnacled and dark; the rest sank into green shadow below, and through the clear cold water Paloma could see the lowest ring, the deepest one, glowing faintly. Not reflecting light. *Holding* it.

"It's real," Seren said quietly. He had gone pale. "I thought — I read about it in the archives, in my old life. The Temple Below. We were taught it was a story the coastal cults told to justify themselves. A myth." He looked at her, and there was something raw in his face, the look of a man watching his whole education crumble into the sea. "It's not a myth."

"The dead don't lie," Paloma said. "They just whisper." Then she started down the slope, because the singing in her bones had become unbearable, sweet and terrible, and because some part of her — the part that had spent five years pretending to be only a carver of charms — was finally, finally going home.

* * *

The temple's upper rings were treacherous with weed and wet stone, and they climbed down into it carefully, Wren between them. The child's mood had changed entirely since the valley came into view. Where Wren had been skittish on the road — flinching at loud noises, sleeping curled tight as a fist — now there was a steadiness, a gravity, that made the child seem suddenly older than eight years old. Older than anyone.

"This is where they hid me," Wren said, stopping at the top of the second ring. The child's voice was very quiet. "Before. When I was a baby."

"You remember it?" Seren asked gently.

"I remember the water singing." Wren looked down into the drowned depths, unafraid. "It never stops singing. Did you know that? Even under the ground. Even inside people."

Paloma felt the words settle into her like stones into a pond. *Even inside people.* She thought of the name sleeping behind the child's eyes, the Drowned Name, the word that kings burned villages to find, and she understood suddenly why the priests had chosen a living vessel rather than carving it into stone. Stone could be broken. Stone could be read. A child could run. A child could be loved, and hidden inside that love.

"Come," said a voice behind them.

All three spun. On the terrace above, where no one had stood a moment before, were three women.

They were translucent the way heat-shimmer is translucent, the ruined temple visible through their bodies, but they were not wisps, not the threadbare whispers Paloma knew from her charms. These ghosts were *whole* — robed in vestments whose patterns still held color in death, deep blue and bone-white, their faces lined and calm and terribly, unmistakably alive in the way of the dead who have unfinished work.

The oldest stepped forward. She wore a circlet of carved vertebrae, and Paloma's own pendant answered it, ringing like a struck bell.

"A speaker," the ghost said. Her voice was layered, many voices braided into one. Her eyes — pale, sightless-seeming, fixed on some middle distance — found Paloma anyway, precisely, the way the dead always found what mattered. "A daughter of the line. After all these years."

"I'm not—" Paloma began.

"Your grandmother wove charms in Merrow's Rest," the ghost said. "Her mother before her, in the fishing villages of the Sedge. Before that, the temples. Before that, the temples themselves." She raised one shimmering hand. "Do not tell us what you are not, child. We buried your grandmothers' grandmothers. We know our own blood."

Paloma's throat closed. Five years of hiding. Five years of hunching over her workbench with the shutters closed, of lying to fishwives, of flinching from every knock, all of it premised on the belief that she was alone — a single wrong note in a world that had outlawed her kind of music. And here, rising out of the ruin of everything her people had built, was proof that she had never been a wrong note at all. She had been part of a song. A long one. One that others had died to keep singing.

Beside her, she heard Seren exhale slowly. She didn't look at him. She couldn't, not yet, not while something in her chest was breaking open along old seams.

"I hear the dead," she said at last. It was the first time in five years she had said it aloud to a stranger's face — even a stranger without a face, exactly. "Through bone. Carved bone. I don't know why. I never knew why."

"Because bone remembers," the eldest said simply. "Flesh forgets, and stone sleeps, and water scatters — but bone holds the shape of a life the way a cup holds wine. Our order learned to carve it open, gently, and listen." Her gaze drifted to Wren, and every ghost on the terrace went still, the way birds go still before a storm. "And this is the Vessel."

"She's a child," Paloma said. The protectiveness came up in her fast and hot, before thought. "Whatever she's carrying, she didn't choose—"

"No one chooses," the eldest agreed. "That is the first lesson, and the hardest. Come down. The tide will return within the hour, and the lower ring has things to teach you that cannot be taught in air."

* * *

They descended through the rings as the ghosts instructed, and with each descent Paloma felt the world change texture.

The second ring was flooded to the knee, and here the carvings resolved into legible scenes: the raising of fish from the sea by song; the laying of the honored dead into the deep with names spoken over them; a great figure beneath the waves, vast and curved, its face left deliberately blank. Wren touched that blank face with one finger and said a word in the First Tongue, and the water around them shivered though there was no wind.

"What did you say?" Seren asked.

"I said hello," Wren said. "To the god." A pause. "He said hello back. He's been waiting a long time. He gets lonely."

Seren looked at Paloma over the child's head, and Paloma saw her own fear reflected there. They had crossed marshes and forded rivers and buried soldiers together; she had begun, without deciding to, to trust this man. But neither of them had truly reckoned with what walked between them, humming to an empty-faced god in a language older than kingdoms.

The third ring was fully submerged, and here the ghosts taught them to breathe.

Later, Paloma could never adequately explain it. The eldest priestess simply took her hand — cold, but not the dead-cold of grave-things; rather the cool of deep water, of cellars, of stone in shade — and drew her down beneath the surface, and Paloma had braced for drowning and found instead that she could breathe the water the way she breathed air, that it filled her lungs sweet and green-lit, and that the dead did not drown because the dead had nothing left to lose.

*The god's courtesy,* the eldest said, her voice traveling through the water as clearly as through air, through Paloma's bones rather than her ears. *Do not grow fond of it. Borrowed breath is borrowed time.* 

Below, in the deepest ring, the temple opened into a dome like the inside of a shell, and every surface of it was carved with writing. Not glyphs — Paloma had seen glyphs in smuggled books, ordered rows of symbols. This was different. The writing moved. It flowed across the stone like currents, rearranging itself, and as she watched, she realized it was responding to sound — to Wren's soft murmuring, to the groan of the tide beyond, to the pulse of Paloma's own heart.

"The First Tongue is not a language of words," the eldest said. They hung in the green water among the drifting ghosts, dozens of them now, priestesses of every age gathered in a slow orbit like fish. "Your trade-tongue chops the world into pieces and names the pieces. The First Tongue does not chop. In it, to name a thing and to call it and to become it are one act. That is why the name of the god below has power. To speak his true name is not to describe him." Her blind eyes fixed on Paloma. "It is to *open a door*, and stand in the doorway, and let him through you."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry despite the water. "Then I don't want to learn it."

"Liar," said one of the younger ghosts, not unkindly. "You have been starving your whole life, speaker. Do you think we cannot smell hunger?"

They taught her anyway. Perhaps she could have refused; perhaps she only tells herself that afterward. But the grammar of the First Tongue poured into her through the bones of her hands as the ghosts guided her fingers across the moving script, and it was less like learning than like remembering — like a door in her mind that had always been painted shut swinging open onto a room she'd dreamed of all her life. She learned that grief had a root-word and that mercy grew from it, the way a healer's herb grows from poison soil. She learned that the word for *listen* and the word for *love* differed by a single inflection, a turn of the breath, and that getting it wrong changed everything.

Wren floated beside her, translating effortlessly, occasionally correcting her pronunciation with the grave patience of a much older teacher. Once the child laughed — a bright, startling sound in the drowned dome — because Paloma had accidentally said *beloved* instead of *listener*, and the entire dome had flushed warm around them, the god below stirring in what could only be described as embarrassment.

And through it all, Seren waited above. She could see him dimly through the surface, pacing the second ring, a dark smudge against the bright water, keeping watch over a world that had already moved past anything he could guard against.

* * *

They surfaced as the tide turned. The ghosts gathered them on the second ring, water streaming from their clothes, and the eldest priestess stood before Paloma with the tide climbing her translucent robes, and everything in her posture had changed. The warmth of teaching was gone. What remained was the weight of duty.

"You have the grammar now," she said. "You could not unlearn it if you tried. So hear the last lesson, the one we paid for with everything."

Paloma wiped water from her eyes. Beside her, Wren had gone very still. Above, on the rim of the temple, Seren had stopped pacing and was looking down at them.

"The Name may be spoken once," the eldest said. "Not once per speaker. Once. Ever. When the god was bound beneath the waves, our mothers spoke a binding over him, and the binding holds until his true name is spoken aloud by a living voice — after which it can never be spoken again, for the speaking *unmakes* it, draws it up out of the deep like a hook drawing a fish. One voice. One speaking. One choice, made forever." 

"And then?" Paloma asked, though some part of her already knew, the way you know the shape of a wound before you look at it.

"And then the god rises, and the speaker is bound to him as he to her. Power freely accepted corrupts its bearer — that is not a warning, child, it is a law, older than we are, older than him. Every priestess who ever reached for more than listening reached for this, and every one of them was changed, and not one of the changes was kind." The eldest's voice dropped, and all the gathered ghosts leaned close, and the tide surged around them, and for a moment Paloma saw beneath the calm faces — saw centuries of grief, of extermination, of a whole people burned down to whispers in bone. "You are the last daughter of the line. There is no one after you. Whatever you choose, you choose for the world."

"There's another way," Paloma said. Her voice sounded young and thin to her own ears. "There must be. We destroy the name. Silence it. Bury it so deep even the dead can't find it. I've thought about it every night since Wren came to my door. No god rises. No power corrupts anyone. We just — end it."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the tide to climb another step.

"Yes," the eldest said at last, gently, horribly. "That, too, is a choice. But understand what silencing means, speaker. The god below is not merely power. He is *memory*. He is every soul the sea has taken since the world was young, and they live in him as words live in a voice. Destroy the Name, and you do not kill a tyrant. You drown a library. All of them — the drowned fishermen, the lost children, your own honored dead — silent, scattered, gone past even our reaching." Her blind eyes held Paloma's. "We do not tell you this to sway you. We tell you because the choice must be made with both costs visible. That was our failure, long ago. We hid the price, even from ourselves."

The tide was at their knees now. The ghosts were beginning to thin, drawn back toward the deep as the water reclaimed the lower rings, and the eldest was fading fastest, her outline dissolving into green shimmer.

"One more thing," she said, and her voice came from everywhere now, from the water, from the stone, from the pendant at Paloma's throat. "The usurper. He comes. We feel his armies in the roots of the world the way you feel thunder in bad teeth. Days, not weeks. And child—" the voice frayed, caught, reassembled, "—the man who travels with you. Ask him about Merrow's Rest. Ask him what he signed."

The temple emptied of ghosts like a lung emptying of breath.

Paloma stood in the rising water with the First Tongue coiled new-made in her chest, and turned slowly to look up at the rim of the ring, where Seren Oduya stood motionless against the grey sky — and she saw, in the way he would not meet her eyes, that he had heard every word.

"Merrow's Rest," she said.

His face, when he finally looked down at her, was the face of a man standing at the edge of a long fall.

"Paloma," he said. "There's something I have to tell you."
