## 13. What Seren Buried

The temple ghosts had given them a night of grace, and Seren Oduya spent it lying awake beneath a fisherman's overturned boat, listening to Paloma breathe three feet away and rehearsing the words that would end everything.

The boat was beached above the tide line on a shingle spit below the sunken temple, half-buried in dune grass, its keel crusted with barnacles dead so long they had gone to chalk. It was the best shelter the coast offered. It was also, Seren thought, a fitting place for a confession: a thing meant to float, turned belly-up and useless, waiting to be dragged back to the sea or left to rot.

Wren slept between them, curled like a comma, one small fist closed around the fragment of drowned temple stone. Even in sleep the child's lips moved sometimes, shaping syllables of the First Tongue, and when that happened the bone charms in Paloma's pack would chime softly, though no wind touched them. Seren had stopped flinching at such things somewhere in the Sedge Marshes. He had not stopped being afraid of them. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name was everything he had done before the marshes.

*Tell her,* the dead had been saying to him for three days.

That was the cruelty of traveling with Paloma Zhou. He did not need the gift to hear the dead. He had carried them in his own skull for eleven years, and they were not whispers. They were ledgers. Names, ages, the exact phrasing of the orders he had signed. Inquisitors of the Vossian Order were trained to keep records, and Seren Oduya had been the best of his cohort, meticulous, incorruptible, promoted young — and every line he had ever written in that beautiful clerk's hand was still in his head, word for word, the way a man remembers the face of the first person he ever made weep.

*Tell her before the storm comes,* the dead said. *She deserves to hear it from you and not from us.*

The dead, at least, had a sense of mercy. He did not.

He rose in the grey hour before dawn, when the tide was out and the shingle gleamed like the scales of an enormous sleeping fish, and he built a small fire of driftwood because he needed something to do with his hands. Salt smoke. The flames burned green at the edges, the way driftwood did, and he watched them and rehearsed again.

*Paloma. Before the temple. Before you trust what the priestesses told you — you should know who taught me to fear people like you.*

No. Too gentle. She would see through gentle; she had survived five years in Saltmere, and gentle was the language of informers.

*Paloma. I signed the order for Greyharbor.*

Greyharbor. He had not let himself say the name aloud in eleven years, and now that he said it, silently, over the green flames, it opened in him like a wound that had only ever been cauterized, never closed. Greyharbor, the herring village on the cliffs, two hundred and forty souls, a shrine to the old god in its sea-cave that the Order had declared heretical. He had been twenty-six. He had read the survey reports, the testimony of two informers, the sworn statement of the village reeve — and he had found the testimony thin, the informers paid by the head as informers always were, and he had written in the margin of his own report: *evidence insufficient, recommend surveillance.*

And his superior, Inquisitor-Commander Hale, had looked at the margin, and looked at Seren's reputation for incorruptibility, and said: *Then you will go and see for yourself, Oduya. And you will sign it either way. That is what it costs to be certain in this world.*

He had gone. He had walked Greyharbor's one street. He had seen the drying racks, the children playing knucklebones on the harbor wall, an old woman mending a net who had looked at his grey Order's coat and crossed herself with the wrong hand — the old way, the way of the sea — and something in him had known, had *known*, that she was only an old woman mending a net.

And he had signed it anyway. Because Hale was watching. Because his career was watching. Because he had told himself that if he refused, they would simply send someone worse, someone who would burn the shrine and the village both, whereas Seren's signature came with a rider: the shrine sealed, the villagers dispersed, no burnings. He had told himself that right up until the soldiers arrived and the dispersal became a rout and the rout became a fire in the sea-cave, because a young lieutenant had panicked at a sound in the dark — a sound that might have been the tide, might have been chanting, was probably only the tide — and fired the seal-oil, and two hundred and forty souls became a number in a ledger with a footnote in Seren's own hand: *unauthorized escalation; no disciplinary action recommended.*

No disciplinary action recommended. He had written that. He had written it because Hale told him to, and he told himself that too, for eleven years: *Hale told me to.* And then one night in the Order's archive he had found a petition from a Greyharbor survivor, a girl of nine, asking after her mother, and he had signed the denial of that petition too, in the same beautiful clerk's hand, and something in him had finally, quietly, irreversibly broken. Three years later, when they ordered him to burn a smuggler's child in Brackwater, he had refused — not out of the goodness everyone assumed, but because the broken thing in him would not bear the weight of one more signature. They had taken his coat, his seal, his name from the rolls, and put him out of the Order with nothing but the ledgers in his head.

He had thought exile was the punishment. He understood now, watching Paloma sleep, that exile had been the *mercy*. The punishment was this: meeting her. Learning her. Learning that the bone-carver with the tired eyes and the quick hands and the laugh she rationed out like coin in a famine — learning that she had been *born* in Greyharbor, that she was nine years old when the sea-cave burned, that the mother she never spoke of had died in the smoke while a meticulous, incorruptible young inquisitor rode away with a clean conscience and a footnote.

The fire cracked. A gull cried somewhere out over the flats, one raw note.

Paloma woke the way she always woke — all at once, silently, one hand already finding the knife under her cloak. She saw him by the fire, saw Wren still sleeping, and some of the wariness went out of her shoulders. That was the worst part. Every morning she woke a little less wary of him, and every morning it got harder.

"You're up early," she said, voice rough with sleep. She came and sat across the fire, knees drawn up, and looked out at the tide flats where the drowned temple's spire broke the water a half-mile out, black against the lightening east. "The priestesses will call again at dawn. Pal—" She stopped. She had started calling him by some shortened version of his name two days ago, in the marshes, and caught herself, and had not tried again, but the almost was there every time now, and he heard it, and it was a knife.

"Paloma." His voice came out wrong. He cleared his throat. "There's something you need to hear before we go back to the temple. Before you — before you let them teach you anything. Before you trust me with what comes next."

She turned from the water. He watched her read his face the way she read bone — quickly, expertly, looking for the flaw, the fracture line. He watched her find it.

"You're frightening me," she said simply. "Don't do that. Just say it."

*Just say it.* Eleven years, and he had never found a way to just say it. So he did the only thing he could do. He took off his shirt.

She went very still. In the firelight his back was a map of the Order's discipline: the lash-scars from his training years, thin and white and long forgotten. But low on the left side, over the ribs, was a brand. The seal of the Vossian Order — the tower and the flame — pressed into his skin the day they stripped his rank, so that no city, no port, no honest household would ever take him in without knowing what he had been.

"That's not the confession," he said. "That's just the receipt. The confession is this."

And he told her.

He told it the way he had promised himself he would — no softening, no ordering of facts to make himself smaller in the story. He told her about Greyharbor. About the thin testimony and his marginal note. About Hale and the order to sign. About the rider he had attached, the sealed shrine, the *dispersal*. He watched her face while he spoke, because he owed her that — owed her the sight of what his words did, the way she would have had to watch if she had been there — and he watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"Greyharbor," she said. Her voice was very flat. "Say it again. Say the name."

"Greyharbor. The herring village. On the cliffs north of—"

"I know where it is." Still flat. Still terribly calm. "I was born there. My mother dried herring on those racks. I played knucklebones on that wall." Her hands had gone to her lap, and he saw that she was gripping her own fingers hard enough to blanch them, holding herself together the way you'd hold a cracked bowl. "The sea-cave. There was a shrine in a sea-cave. It wasn't a shrine. It was where my grandmother taught the old songs. Where the women went to hear the — " She stopped. Something moved through her face like a fish under ice. "You sealed it. You sealed it and they panicked in the dark, and the oil—"

"The lieutenant fired it. I signed the order that put him there. I signed the report after. I wrote *no disciplinary action recommended*." He made himself say it all. "And three years later a petition came from a survivor asking after her mother, and I denied it, and I have her name in my head, Paloma. I have every name. Two hundred and forty. I say them to myself like a prayer, and it doesn't help, and it doesn't absolve me, and I am not telling you this to be absolved. I'm telling you because you're about to bind yourself to the priestesses' teaching, and to Wren, and maybe to me, and you cannot do it not knowing. You deserve the truth from my mouth and not from the dead."

Silence. The fire burned down to green flames and embers. Behind them, in the boat's shadow, Wren slept on, small and trusting, and Seren thought: *there it is, the last thing I'll lose.*

Paloma stood up. She did it slowly, like an old woman, like someone whose body had aged thirty years in as many minutes. When she spoke, her voice was still flat, and the flatness was worse than screaming.

"Five years," she said. "Five years I hid in Saltmere. Five years I carved charms for fishwives and never once let myself carve what I could really carve, because I knew what happened to people who were heard. I thought I was hiding from the Order. From Voss. From informers." She laughed, one note, terrible. "I was hiding from you. From men like you. From a man with a good margin-note and a clean conscience and a signature."

"Paloma—"

"Don't." The flatness cracked, and under it was something molten. "Don't say my name. You don't — you *signed* — my mother's name was Mei Zhou. She was twenty-nine. She went back into the cave for my grandmother, who was too old to run, and they both — " Her breath hitched and she mastered it, and he had never seen anything so disciplined or so dreadful. "You wrote *no disciplinary action recommended*. I was nine years old, Seren. I dug through the cave rubble with my hands. I found — " She stopped. She looked at the sea. "You knew. In the marshes. When the dead soldiers talked to me, when I told you about the war between the god and the kings, when you held my hand at the ford — you knew, and you let me—"

"I tried to tell you. Every day I—"

"You *rehearsed*." Her eyes came back to him and they were the coldest thing on that cold shore. "I've watched you for weeks, Seren Oduya. I've watched you count your words before you spend them. You didn't fail to tell me. You *scheduled* it. You waited until I needed you. Until Wren needed you. Until the truth would cost me more than the lie."

"That's not—" But it was. He knew it was. He had chosen the grey hour before dawn, the fire, the moment before the temple, because some clerk's part of his mind had calculated the exact price of the confession and picked the moment she could least afford to pay it. The incorruptible inquisitor, meticulous to the last.

Wren stirred. The child sat up in the boat's shadow, hair full of sand, and looked from Seren's branded back to Paloma's face, and whatever the child read there — children of burned villages learn to read fire in people's faces — Wren made a small sound and moved, not to Seren, but to Paloma, pressing close against her side. Paloma's arm came around the child automatically. It was the most natural thing Seren had ever seen, and it was the verdict.

"Get your things," Paloma said. "We have to reach the temple before the tide turns. The priestesses are waiting, and Wren — Wren is the only thing in this world that matters now. Not you. Not me. The child." She picked up her pack. Her hands were steady. That frightened him more than rage would have. "You can walk with us, because three is safer than two on this coast, and because I am not my mother's murderer, Seren, and I will not leave anyone to Voss's riders, not even you. But don't speak to me. Don't touch me. And don't — " her voice finally broke, one clean break, like bone — "don't ever again let me think, even for a moment, that what we were building in the marshes was real. Because I think it was. That's the worst of it. I think it was real, and you knew all along, and you let it grow anyway, because you were *lonely*."

She turned and started up the shingle toward the temple path, Wren's hand in hers, and the child looked back at Seren once — one look, grave and ancient and grieving — and then the two of them were small figures climbing against the brightening sky.

Seren stood by the dead fire. He did not follow, not at once. He looked out at the tide flats, where the temple's black spire stood in the shallows, and he said the names. All two hundred and forty. He said them to the sea, which had taken them, which perhaps had been listening all along.

And the sea answered.

Not with waves. With a voice — vast, low, rising through the shingle under his boots, through the bones of the spit, through the chalk of ten thousand barnacles — a voice that spoke in the First Tongue and yet that he understood perfectly, the way you understand a blow to the chest. And what the voice said, as the sky went from grey to gold behind the drowned temple, was:

*She is not the only one who was born in Greyharbor, inquisitor. Come to the water. There is something buried here you have never confessed — something even you do not remember burying.*

* * *

The tide was turning when he caught up to them on the temple causeway, the black basalt spine of it emerging from the shallows in a long wet arc. Paloma did not look at him. Wren did, and the child's expression had changed — there was something in it now that Seren had never seen directed at him before, something between warning and pity.

The priestesses met them at the threshold, five shapes of sea-light standing in the flooded doorway, and the eldest — the one whose face was Paloma's face grown old — turned her luminous eyes on Seren before he had said a word.

"You heard the voice on the shingle," she said. It was not a question. "Good. Then you know the dead do not only accuse, inquisitor. Sometimes they *summon*." She drifted closer, and the water around her feet glowed. "There is a grave beneath this temple, Seren Oduya. Dug by your own Order, eleven years ago, the week after Greyharbor burned. You were on that boat. You helped them carry it down the steps. And you have forgotten — not forgiven, *forgotten* — because what you buried was not a body." The priestess's voice softened into something almost gentle. "It was a name. The first half of the Drowned Name. And Voss's bone-reader has just found the second half — and the tide will not turn back twice."

Paloma stared at him, and the rage in her face curdled into something far more dangerous: doubt. Because if it was true — if he had stood in this temple and carried that burden and *forgotten* — then the man she had condemned at dawn was not the whole of the man. And if it was false, then the dead were liars, and everything she believed was ash.

"Show me," she said to the priestess, without taking her eyes off Seren. "Show me what he buried."

The water in the doorway began, slowly, to drain away — down, and down, into the dark beneath the temple, opening a stair that no living person had descended in eleven years.
