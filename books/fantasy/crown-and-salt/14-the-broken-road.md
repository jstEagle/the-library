## 14. The Broken Road

The storm found her before she found the road.

Paloma had meant to take the road — the old pilgrim track that wound down from the temple cliffs toward the low country, toward Brackwater, toward anywhere that was not here. But grief made her stupid, and rage made her blind, and by the time the first rain hit her face she had already left the road behind, following instead a goat track that dissolved into mud and bracken and then into nothing at all.

She did not care. That was the truth of it. She did not care if she walked off a cliff. She did not care if the marsh took her the way it had taken the soldiers whose bones she'd heard whispering for three days. She did not care if she drowned, because drowning was only the sea putting its hand over your mouth, and the sea had been putting its hand over her mouth her whole life.

*He signed the order.*

The words kept turning in her like a blade that could not be pulled free. She had made herself walk past the fire, past the temple's broken colonnade, past Seren Oduya sitting in the rain with his head in his hands like a man at his own funeral, because if she had stopped she would have picked up one of the fallen stones and opened his skull with it. She had made herself walk. Now she could not stop walking, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the blade turned again.

*He signed the order. Her name was on the list. Her mother's name. Her grandmother's name. The whole village of Greywater, entered in a fair clerk's hand, and the hand was his.*

She had been a girl of eleven when the inquisitors came. She remembered the smell of the church burning — tallow and thatch and something sweeter underneath, something she had not understood until years later, when she'd learned what burning bone smelled like. She remembered her grandmother pushing her down into the bone-dust cellar, pressing the little carved whalebone charm into her palm, whispering *be quiet, be quiet, be quiet* — and the whisper had been in the charm ever after, and Paloma had heard it every day of her life, and she had thought it was a memory.

It was not a memory. It was a voice. And the man who had made it a voice — who had made her grandmother's last word a *be quiet* instead of a *goodbye* — had walked beside her for nine days. Had shared her fire. Had carried Wren on his shoulders across the Sedge Marshes and let the child fall asleep against his neck. Had looked at Paloma across a dying fire and told her, in that low ruined voice of his, that he understood what it was to carry guilt.

He had never said *I was the one who taught you what guilt was.*

The rain came harder. It came sideways off the sea, needling her cheeks, and the wind tore at her cloak and the dark came down like a lid. Somewhere behind her, thunder rolled along the cliff line, and Paloma thought: *good. Let it roll. Let the whole sky come down. The sky never signed anything.*

She climbed. The goat track became a scramble, the scramble became a ridge of black rock furred with salt-killed heather, and at the top of it the storm opened like a wound and she could see the whole drowned coast below her — the temple's half-submerged colonnade silvered with rain, the sea hammering the shore, the broken road running south into darkness.

She stood there and let the wind try to take her.

*Jump,* said something small and tired in her chest. *Or walk south. Either way, don't go back. Going back is how you die a second time.*

She was so tired. That was the thing no one told you about betrayal — it wasn't sharp, in the end. It was heavy. It was the weight of every kindness reweighed and found counterfeit, every shared meal suddenly a performance, every *I understand* revealed as a hunter's patience. Nine days of trust, and now she had to carry all nine days at once, and they weighed as much as the world.

She thought of her grandmother's charm, back at the temple in her pack. She thought of the voice in it. *Be quiet.*

"No," Paloma said aloud, to the storm, to herself. Her voice came out cracked and small. "I won't be quiet. I'm done being quiet."

And the dead heard her.

They always heard her. That was the curse and the inheritance and the truth of her life, and in that moment, alone on the ridge with the rain hammering down, she felt them gather the way birds gather before a storm turns — a pressure, a presence, a rustling at the edge of her hearing that was not wind.

They came up through the rock beneath her feet. The ridge was old; old things slept in it. She felt them first as cold in her knees, then as sound, then as voices — dozens of them, then hundreds, a chorus rising out of the stone like water rising in a well.

*Daughter.* The word was not spoken so much as remembered at her, the way the dead did everything — not speaking but pressing the shape of a thing against her mind. *Daughter of Greywater. Daughter of the quiet ones. Stop walking.*

"I'm not walking toward anything," she said. "That's rather the point."

*You are walking away from the child.*

The words landed like a slap. Paloma stopped.

"Wren has Seren," she said, and hated how the name came out of her mouth, flat and hard as a coin.

*The child is crying for you.*

"She's a child. Children cry. She'll cry herself to sleep and in the morning Seren will—"

The dead did not argue. That was the terrible thing about them; they never argued, they only showed. The chorus tightened around her, and suddenly she was not on the ridge at all. She was standing in the temple's great hall, in the rain that fell through the broken roof, and she was seeing it as the dead saw it — all at once, from every angle, every shadow lit.

She saw Seren Oduya on his knees in the flooded nave, and she saw that his hands were not in his hands — she saw that he was holding them out, palms up, in the old posture of surrender, as though presenting himself to a court. She saw his lips moving. She could not hear him — the dead could not give her sound, only shape — but she knew the shape of those words. She had seen them on her own lips in the dark, more nights than she would admit.

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

And she saw Wren.

The child stood in the doorway of the priestess cell they had been sleeping in, small and soaked and rigid, staring out at the rain as though it were a wall between her and something she could not cross. Wren's face was the face she had worn the first night on Paloma's doorstep — mud-caked, mute, eyes enormous — but now there was sound, and the sound was the First Tongue, the old language rising out of the child in broken, keening syllables that the storm could not smother because the storm was younger than the language.

Paloma had been learning the grammar of it for three days from the priestess-ghosts, and she knew enough now to be gutted by it.

The child was not crying *help*. The child was not crying for food, or warmth, or safety.

The child was crying a name. *Paloma. Paloma. Paloma.* Over and over, in the tongue older than any kingdom, in the voice of a girl who had carried the name of a drowned god inside her small body and had never once asked why the world was trying to kill her for it — the child stood in the rain calling for the one person who had ever carved her a charm against nightmares, who had braided her hair when the wind got in it, who had taught her the word for *bread* and the word for *safe* and the word, signed on the fingers, for *stay*.

*Stay.*

Paloma's knees went. She sat down hard on the wet rock of the ridge, and the rain fell on her bowed head, and the dead held their silence around her like a congregation waiting.

"She'll be fine," Paloma whispered. "He'll keep her safe. He's — he's good with her. That wasn't a lie. That part wasn't a lie."

*No,* the dead agreed, gentle as falling water. *That part was not a lie. The man is true in the small things and false in the great one. This is the most common shape of a soul, daughter. You have known worse men. You have been a worse woman, some days.*

"That's not the point," she said, and her voice broke on it. "The point is my grandmother. The point is my mother. The point is that I sat across a fire from the man who burned them and I passed him the salt."

The dead were quiet a long moment. When they spoke again, it was not the chorus. It was one voice, old and dry and familiar, and Paloma's whole body went still, because she knew that voice. She had heard it every day of her life through a whalebone charm.

*Little fish,* said her grandmother.

Paloma made a sound that was not quite a word.

*You passed him the salt,* her grandmother said. *And he ate what you cooked, and he carried the child, and he told you the truth at last, which is more than most men do, and less than the child needs. Little fish. Listen to me. I died in a church that a fair clerk's order set on fire. Do you know what I have thought about, all these years, from the other side of the quiet?*

Paloma could not answer.

*I have thought about the inquisitor who lit it,* her grandmother said. *I have hated him, and the hating was warm, like a stove in a cold room, and it kept me for a long time. And then I watched you, little fish. I watched you five years in Saltmere, alone, keeping everyone at the length of a knife, and I saw where the hating leads. It leads to a ridge in a storm, and a woman with no one, telling herself the loneliness is a fortress. The hating is a stove, and it burns the house down.*

"He killed you," Paloma said. The words came out of her like stones from a wound.

*Yes,* her grandmother said, simply. *And the child will die too, if you sit on this ridge until the storm and your pride have finished their conversation. Voss's riders are not sitting on any ridge, little fish. They are moving. The chained reader is dead but the man who chained her has her maps, and the maps point here, and the child carries the name, and you are the only voice in the world learning to speak with her. You can hate him on the road. Hate him walking. Hate him with your boots in the mud. But walk.*

The vision of the temple still hung in her mind's eye — Wren in the doorway, small and streaming, calling her name in the language of the drowned god. *Paloma. Paloma.* The child who had been made a vessel by priests who were dust before Voss was born, who had run from a burning village with a fragment of temple stone clutched in her fist, who had knocked on the door of a stranger because something in the bones had told her *this one, this one hears*.

Paloma had opened the door. That was the whole of it, in the end. Whatever else happened, whatever Seren Oduya had done or been, Paloma had opened the door, and you could not open a door and then pretend the house was empty.

She got up. Her legs shook. The rain had found every seam in her clothing and her hands were blue and she was, she realized, laughing — a small, cracked, terrible laugh that the wind tore away as fast as she made it.

"You're a bossy dead woman," she told her grandmother. "You always were."

*I was right, too,* said the voice, and there was warmth in it, and the warmth nearly undid her more than the storm. *Go down, little fish. The road is broken, but broken roads still go somewhere.*

* * *

The way down took an hour and cost her a strip of skin from her palm and the last of her dignity, which she surrendered cheerfully to a patch of black mud that swallowed her to the hip. She climbed out of it soaked, filthy, and colder than the dead, and she did not stop. The broken road ran south along the cliff, and she took it, because her grandmother had told her to walk and because walking south was walking toward the temple and she could not yet make herself say the word *back*.

The storm began to spend itself as she walked, the way storms do — not ending but exhausting, the thunder moving off along the coast to shout at some other cliff, the rain thinning from needles to threads. By the time the temple's colonnade rose silver-gray out of the dark ahead, the clouds had torn open in the west and a ragged moon was looking down at the flooded nave like a neighbor at a funeral.

Paloma stopped at the edge of the ruins.

Through the broken roof, in the moonlight, she could see the great hall. Seren was where the dead had shown him — still on his knees, or kneeling again; she could not tell how long a man could stay in that posture and she suspected he would tell her *years, if the guilt is heavy enough*. He had not lit a fire. He had not gone into shelter. He sat in the flooded nave with his surrendering hands and his ruined face turned up to the rain, and Paloma understood, with a strange cold clarity, that he would have stayed there all night, and that some part of him hoped the storm would do what his courage could not.

And in the doorway of the priestess cell, Wren.

The child had not gone to sleep either. She stood exactly where the dead had shown her, and when the moonlight reached her Paloma saw that her face was swollen, her eyes rimmed red, her small fists clenched at her sides — and that she was not looking at Seren at all. She was looking down the broken road.

Looking south. Looking for her.

Paloma's chest did something painful and irreversible.

Wren saw her. The child went rigid — one heartbeat, two — and then she was running, splashing through the flooded nave, through the puddles and the moonlight and past the kneeling man as though he were furniture, and she hit Paloma at the knees hard enough to stagger her, and the First Tongue came pouring out of her in a torrent, grief and relief and anger all braided together, and Paloma caught enough of it now to know that some of it was scolding.

"I know," Paloma said, kneeling, gathering the child in, mud and rainwater and all. "I know. I'm here. I'm sorry, little bird. I'm here."

Wren pulled back far enough to look at her, and hit her on the shoulder — a small, furious, soaking blow — and then said something in the old language, slow and deliberate, the way you speak to someone you need to understand.

Paloma knew the words. The priestesses had taught her. *Do not go where I cannot follow.*

"I'm not going anywhere," Paloma said, and found, to her astonishment, that it was true. Whatever she felt about the man kneeling in the nave, this child had reached into her chest five nights ago and taken hold of something, and it did not come loose because the truth was heavy. "I'm here. We go together or not at all."

Wren studied her face with those enormous grave eyes, the way she studied everything — as though memorizing it against loss — and then nodded once, a small queen's nod, and put her forehead against Paloma's, and stayed there, breathing, while the last of the rain ticked down through the broken roof.

Over the child's shoulder, Paloma looked at Seren Oduya.

He had risen. He stood at the edge of the moonlight with water streaming off him, and he did not come closer, and he did not speak, and Paloma understood that he understood: this was not his moment, and it might never be again. His face was the face of a man who had set down the thing he'd carried and found that setting it down had not made him lighter, only empty.

She should hate him. Some part of her did, and that part was right, and it was not going anywhere, and she would not insult her dead by pretending otherwise. Her grandmother's church was still ash. Her mother's name was still ink on a fair clerk's page. Forgiveness was not a door you opened in one night in the rain.

But forgiveness, she thought, was not the same as company.

"Get up off your knees," she said to him. Her voice was hoarse and did not sound like mercy, and she was glad of it. "Both of you — the dead tell me Voss's riders crossed the Sennet Ford at dusk. The chained bone-reader is dead, but he has her maps now, and the maps end here. We have until the storm fully clears. Maybe less."

Seren blinked, as if he had braced for a blow and been handed an order instead. "Paloma—"

"I'm not forgiving you," she said. "I want that said plainly, once, so we never have to say it again. What you did is ash, and the ash doesn't cool because you're sorry. But Wren needs us both, and the dead need us both, and I have decided — *I* have decided, do you hear me — that I am not going to let you take one more thing from my family. Not my grandmother's voice. Not my rage. And not my choice to stand between that child and the man who wants her."

She stood, lifting Wren onto her hip, and the child settled there with the boneless trust of the very young and the very tired, and Paloma looked at Seren Oduya across the flooded nave and felt the blade in her chest turn one more time — and settle.

"Tomorrow we walk," she said. "Tonight, you tell me everything. Every name on every list you ever signed. We start with Greywater."

Seren bowed his head. "There's a ledger in my pack," he said quietly. "I've kept it since the day I left the Order. Every name. I can — I'll recite them. However long it takes."

"However long it takes," Paloma agreed.

And far to the south, past the broken road, past the drowned coast, she felt it — faint as a fish turning in deep water, cold as a chain — the first pressure of an army's dead, thousands of them, moving north with the usurper's banners, whispering one word as they marched.

*The child. The child. The child.*

Paloma turned her face into Wren's hair and began, in the First Tongue, the old word for *hurry*.
