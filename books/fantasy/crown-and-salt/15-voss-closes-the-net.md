## 15. Voss Closes the Net

The war room of the conquered capital had been a temple once. Kaelen Voss had kept the mosaic floor — a sunburst of gold and lapis that some pious mason had laid a century before his birth — because he liked the way it looked beneath his boots, liked the idea of walking on the faces of forgotten gods. Around him, his generals bent over maps of the coast, and the brazier smoke made halos of their helmets, and none of them would meet his eye.

Good, he thought. Fear kept a council honest. Affection never had.

"The vanguard reaches the Sedge Marshes in nine days," said General Corvane, a thick-necked man who had once been a fishmonger and still had the hands for it, hands that could gut anything. "The free cities are mustering. Brackwater has called a council."

"A council." Voss turned the word over like a coin he suspected was clipped. "They will talk for a month and arm for two. Cities always believe that talking is the same as fighting. It is why they lose." He set one finger on the map, on Brackwater's harbor, and pressed until his nail went white. "We do not give them their two months."

"Your Radiance, the marsh roads in the wet season—"

"I know the marsh roads." He had been a starving boy on the marsh roads, once, with mud to his knees and his mother's body going cold behind him, but that was not a thing he said aloud, not ever, not even to himself in the dark hours. He had buried that boy under a general's cloak and a usurper's crown, and if the boy sometimes stirred — if he sometimes woke at night with the taste of salt and old grief in his mouth — Voss pressed him back down with work. There was always work.

"Corvane. Take the coastal road. Burn the granaries at Saltmere's outskirts as you go — I want the free cities hungry before they are afraid. Halvard takes the river. I will follow with the main host." He straightened. "And the reader. Where is the reader?"

The generals' silence had a texture to it, a fine grain of relief. They were glad to be dismissed from whatever came next. Voss watched them go and did not blame them. Even his own soldiers crossed themselves when they passed the door at the end of the east corridor, the one with no guard posted outside it, because everyone knew what was inside and no one wanted to be the one standing nearest when it was opened.

* * *

The bone-reader was dying.

Voss had known it the moment he entered the room — knew it the way he knew weather, the way he knew a lie. The old woman lay on a pallet against the wall, chained at the wrist to an iron ring set deep in the stone, and her skin had gone the color of candle tallow. Her eyes were still fierce, though. That was the trouble with bone-readers. They went out fierce or not at all.

He had bought her in a market town three years ago, from a slaver who had not known what he was selling — a woman who could hold a dead man's knucklebone and tell you where he'd hidden his silver. Voss had recognized the gift the way a one-eyed man recognizes sight. He had chained her within the week. Not out of cruelty. Out of necessity. He had learned early what happened to people like her when they were left unchained: they ran, or they lied, or they remembered things about him that he needed forgotten.

"You sent for me twice yesterday," she said as he crouched beside her. Her voice was gravel and river silt. "I was working. It takes longer now. The dead are... crowded."

"Crowded."

"Every village you burn, every town your soldiers put to the sword — the dead come to me. Do you understand what you have done, Kaelen Voss? You have built me a congregation." She laughed, and the laugh became a cough that bent her double, and when she straightened there was blood on her lips, bright as seal's fat. "They all want to talk to you. Isn't that funny? They all want a word with the king."

He did not flinch. He had learned not to flinch in a cellar in Vhal, at eight years old, while his uncle held his hand against the stove because the neighbors had said the boy heard voices in the walls. *There are no voices,* his uncle had said, pressing flesh to iron. *There is no gift. There is only sickness, and sickness is beaten out or it spreads.* Voss had learned to be silent after that. He had learned so well that for thirty years he heard nothing at all — and then, on the night he took this city, standing in the burning temple with the mosaic sunburst at his feet, he had heard them again. One voice. Faint. A woman's voice, or a priestess's, saying a word he could not hold, a word that slid out of his memory like a fish out of a wet hand.

The Drowned Name. He had chased it across two provinces and through the ashes of six villages, and he was close now. Closer than he had ever been.

"The temple," he said. "The drowned one. You told my captain you had found it."

The old woman studied him for a long time. There was something in her face he had never seen there before, and it took him a moment to name it: pity. Pity, from a chained woman to a king. It made his jaw tighten.

"I found it," she said. "South and west of the Sedge, where the river forgets its name. A temple half-swallowed, older than the kings, older than the kingdoms. The dead there are... organized. They have been waiting a long time for someone who could hear them." She smiled, and her teeth were brown. "Not you, though. Never you. You hear the way a deaf man sees — you know there is sound, and it maddens you, and you will burn the world to make the silence stop. I have read your bones, Kaelen Voss. I read them the first night you chained me. Do you want to know what I saw?"

"No," he said, and then, because some buried boy in him was always hungry, always starving: "Yes."

"I saw a hand reaching into deep water," she said, "and the water closing over it, and the hand still grasping. That is all you are. A hand that will not close, will not open, will not come up for air." She sagged against the wall, spent. "The temple is real. The name is real. And it is already moving toward you, king, faster than your armies move. There is a child—"

"A child." The word came out of him like a blade from a sheath. He had heard the reports. A speaker of the First Tongue, moving north with a bone-carver and a disgraced inquisitor — Oduya, of all people, Seren Oduya, whose name was still spoken in Voss's own order with a kind of horrified reverence. The traitor who would not burn a child. "A mute child. Where?"

"I don't know where." She was fading now; he could see it, the light in her going down like a lamp out of oil. "But I know this, and I will say it while I still have breath: whatever you think you will do with the name, you are wrong. It does not serve. It is not a sword to be taken up. The priestesses learned that, and they were wiser than you, and wiser than me, and they let it sleep. Let it sleep, Voss. Let the dead sleep. There is mercy in—"

She stopped. Not because she chose to stop. Because she was done.

Voss knelt there a while in the lamp-smelling dark, beside the body of the only person in his empire who had ever told him the truth, and felt — nothing. That was the thing no one understood about him. They expected grief, or rage, or some human residue. But the boy in the cellar had burned out of him long ago, and what was left was a hand reaching into deep water, grasping, grasping.

"Send for the quartermaster," he said to the guard at the door. His voice was steady. It was always steady. "The host moves at dawn."

* * *

The village was called Marlow's Rest, and it had the misfortune to sit at the crossroads between the marsh country and the sea, which meant that everyone fleeing the free cities passed through it eventually. Voss rode into it on the fourth day with two hundred riders, and the villagers came out to stand in the road the way they always did, the men with their hats off, the women holding their children a little too tightly, all of them wearing the same expression: *we are no one, we have nothing, please.*

He had seen that expression in a mirror once, long ago. He had hated it then. He hated it now, and hating it made it easy.

"Three days ago," he said, pitching his voice to carry, "a child passed through this village. A child who does not speak — or speaks in a tongue older than any of your grandmothers. With it, a woman, a carver. With them, a man in a traveler's coat who walks like a soldier. Someone in this village sold them bread. Someone sold them a boat, or a mule, or a night under a roof. Someone looked at them." He let the silence stretch. "I want names."

The village headman, a stooped man with a miller's forearms, spread his empty hands. "Your Radiance, we see a hundred travelers a week. We don't ask questions. Asking questions is how you get—"

He stopped himself. Too late.

"Get what?" Voss asked softly. "Get visited?"

No one answered. The wind moved the barley at the field's edge, a long silver shiver, and somewhere a dog began to bark and was hastily silenced.

Voss dismounted. He walked the village street slowly, letting them watch him, letting them feel the arithmetic happening behind his eyes. He paused at the well. He paused at the shrine — a little moss-eaten thing to some river saint, offerings of bread and copper coins gone green. He touched the shrine's lintel and felt, or imagined he felt, the old familiar pressure at the edge of his mind, the sense of voices behind a wall, and his heart began to hammer with something that was not fear and not quite hunger, and he hated it, hated that after everything he was still this, still a boy with his hand on a stove.

"Your Radiance." Corvane had come up beside him, low-voiced. "If they know nothing—"

"Everyone knows something." Voss turned and looked down the street at the gathered villagers, at their held breath. "Corvane. The granary."

Corvane went still. Even he. Even the fishmonger with the gutting hands. "The granary is full. It's their winter store. If we take it, half of them won't see spring."

"I am aware of the arithmetic." Voss began to walk back toward his horse. "I am not taking it. I am burning it. And I am burning the shrine, and the headman's mill, and the house of whoever fed the child — which we will determine, in time, because someone will talk. Someone always talks when the smoke starts." He mounted. "Fire is a question, Corvane. It is the only question some people can hear. We ask it, and the village answers, and the answer travels down every road out of here faster than any rider: *the usurper is coming, and he is asking, and God help you if you have seen her.*"

"Her," Corvane said. "You think it's a woman. The speaker."

"The woman is the door," Voss said. "The child is what's behind it." He thought of the bone-reader's hand closing over his wrist on the first night, her blind-seeming eyes going wide as she read him, and the single word she had said before he had her chained and gagged: *Empty.* She had looked at him and seen a hand reaching into deep water, and she had pitied him, and he had burned six villages since, and the pity had changed nothing, because pity was just another coin, and he had learned at eight years old that no one pays a coin like that for a boy like him. You take what you need. You take it with fire.

"Light it at dusk," he said. "When the light is low and the smoke will show for miles. And leave two of the outbuildings standing. I am not a monster, Corvane. They will need somewhere to sleep."

Corvane looked at him for a long moment with an expression Voss had learned to read in a hundred officers' faces — the flicker of a man deciding, again, that the orders were terrible but the man giving them was necessary, that the world was hard and Kaelen Voss was simply harder. It was a useful expression. Voss had cultivated it the way other men cultivated orchards.

At dusk the granary went up in a column of orange and black, and the village screamed, and the smoke stood over Marlow's Rest like a dark tree growing out of the earth, visible, Voss judged, from ten miles in every direction. He watched it from the crossroads with his reins loose in his hands and felt the pressure at the edge of his mind swell — the dead of the village, or the dead of somewhere else, pressing against the wall of his silence, wanting in, always wanting in.

*Let it sleep,* the old woman had said. *There is mercy in it.*

But mercy was a coin, too, and he had never once been paid in it, and he did not intend to spend what he had never earned.

"Ride," he told his captains, and the column turned south and west, toward the marshes, toward the river that forgot its name, toward the drowned temple where a word waited in the deep like a hand waiting to be taken. Behind him, Marlow's Rest burned against the darkening sky, and the smoke bent east on the wind, toward the sea, toward Brackwater, carrying his question to every ear along the coast:

*Where is the child?*

And somewhere ahead of him — he was sure of it now, sure as he had never been sure of anything — the child heard him asking, and was afraid, and was running.

Good, Voss thought, and put his heels to his horse. Run. Everything that runs, runs toward something. And I will be there when you arrive.
