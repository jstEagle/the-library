## 16. The Council of Free Cities

The Council Hall of Brackwater had been built to impress the sea, and the sea had never once been impressed. It rose on pilings above the harbor like a great white bird with folded wings, its marble floors salt-stained, its banners faded to the color of old bruises. Seren Oduya stood beneath those banners in a coat he'd borrowed from a fishmonger and felt every one of his thirty-eight years settle onto his shoulders like wet rope.

He had sat in halls like this before. He had stood at the right hand of inquisitors-general and watched men and women be condemned by committee, their fates decided between the second course of lunch and the evening's wine. He knew how councils worked. They worked slowly, cowardly, and only under compulsion.

Paloma stood beside him with Wren's small hand buried in hers, and she looked like a woman walking to her own execution who had simply decided not to mention it.

"You don't have to do this," he said for the fourth time that morning.

"Yes, I do." She said it the way she said most things — flat, economical, a carver's economy of motion applied to speech. But her thumb moved against Wren's knuckles, back and forth, back and forth, and Seren knew that gesture now. It was what she did when she was terrified. She had done it through the marshes, through the ford, through the long gray weeks since Brackwater's walls had risen around them. "They won't march for a rumor. They won't even march for me. But they'll march for their dead."

"They'll call you a witch."

"Probably." A ghost of a smile. "I've been called worse by better-dressed people."

Wren tugged her sleeve and signed something quick — the child had learned Paloma's private signs with an aptitude that still startled Seren — and Paloma crouched, listened, nodded. Whatever the child said made her jaw tighten.

"What is it?" Seren asked.

"She says the hall smells like the temple before it drowned." Paloma straightened. "She says the dead here are already listening."

Seren looked up at the vaulted ceiling, at the carved rafters where gulls had nested for two hundred years, and felt the hair rise on his arms. He had spent eleven years as an inquisitor learning to dismiss such feelings as nerves. He had spent four years in exile learning they were never just nerves.

The doors opened. Trumpets — badly played, as trumpets always were in free cities that couldn't afford full companies. And then the voice of a herald, announcing them to the Council of Free Cities: *the bone-carver Paloma Zhou, the exile Seren Oduya, and the child called Wren.*

No titles. No protections. Just names, offered up like coins on a table.

* * *

The council chamber was a half-circle of tiered seats facing a floor of black stone worn smooth by two centuries of shoes. Nine delegates sat for nine cities — Saltmere's seat empty and draped in mourning cloth since Voss's vanguard had taken it, which meant eight delegates, and Seren counted the faces quickly, the way you count exits in a room you might need to leave fast.

He recognized some. Delegate Maren of Brackwater, silver-haired, shrewd, rumored to control half the harbor's grain contracts. Delegate Ossian of Greyharbor, young and ambitious, wearing a sword he clearly wanted someone to notice. And at the center, presiding, First Speaker Aldous Crane — a man whose reputation for caution was so total that sailors joked he'd refuse to board a ship until it had been dry for a week.

Crane looked at Paloma the way a man looks at weather he doesn't trust.

"Paloma Zhou," Crane said. "Carver of charms. Resident of Saltmere, a city currently flying Voss's banner. You come to us claiming the Usurper marches on all the free cities, that he seeks a child, that this child carries—" he consulted a paper with visible distaste, "—*the true name of the drowned god*. And you bring no army, no evidence, no proof. Only a story."

"Not only a story," Paloma said.

"A story and a mute child."

"She isn't mute." Paloma's voice didn't rise. That was the thing about her, Seren thought — she never rose, she only deepened, like water finding its level. "She speaks a language older than any kingdom. She watched Voss's soldiers burn her village. She walked out of the ashes carrying a piece of the god's own temple because someone told her to keep it safe, and she has kept it safe across three hundred miles of marsh and river while grown men with swords failed to do the same."

Murmurs. Ossian of Greyharbor leaned forward, interested in the child the way wolves are interested in lambs — Seren had seen that look before too, in other chambers, over other children, and his stomach turned.

"Stories," Crane repeated. "Vivid ones. I'll grant they're vivid."

"Then let me give you something that isn't a story." Paloma reached into her coat.

Every guard in the room shifted. Two crossbows came up. Seren's hand went to the knife at his belt out of pure reflex before he caught himself and forced it down. *Don't*, he willed her. *Not like this. Not armed.*

But what she drew out was not a weapon. It was a bone — a rib bone, whalebone by the curve of it, carved so finely the lines seemed to move when the light crossed them. Seren had seen her work a hundred times now. He had watched her coax voices out of such bones in dark rooms, in camps, in the bottom of a boat crossing the Sedge. He knew what it cost her. He knew what lived inside them.

"This," Paloma said, holding it up, "is a speaking-bone. I carve them from the remains of the dead, and the dead speak through them. Not metaphorically. Not in poetry. They speak. Their words. Their memories. What they saw before they died."

"That's necromancy," someone said from the upper tiers. "That's outlawed—"

"The ban was repealed last month by vote of this very council," Maren of Brackwater said dryly, "after your delegate argued passionately for it, Ossian, when it suited his trade interests. Do try to keep current."

A ripple of uncomfortable laughter. Seren didn't laugh. He was watching Paloma's hands. They were steady. Her hands were always steady. It was everything behind them that shook.

"I am going to do something now," Paloma continued, "that I have spent five years making sure I would never have to do. I'm going to prove what I am, in front of witnesses, in daylight, in a city full of informers. Because you will not act without proof, and proof is the only thing I have left to give." She paused. "And I want everyone in this chamber to understand exactly what that proof will cost me. After today, every bounty hunter, every Voss agent, every frightened fool with a torch will know my name and my gift. There is no taking it back. So before I do it, I want to hear it from this council's own mouth: if I show you the truth, will you act on it?"

Silence.

"Will you send ships? Will you raise levies? Will you treat with the other cities for a common defense?" Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and Seren's chest ached with how much it cost her to ask. "Or will you sit here and debate until Voss's riders are at your gates?"

Crane's face was a wall. "This council does not commit to action on conditions set by petitioners—"

"No," Paloma said. "It commits to nothing. That's rather the problem."

She turned the bone in her fingers, and Seren saw her close her eyes for half a heartbeat — the same half-heartbeat she took before every carving, before every listening, a small private prayer to whatever gods bone-carvers keep — and then she opened them and held the rib bone high.

"Brackwater keeps its honored dead in the crypts beneath this hall," she said. "Admirals. Founders. The heroes of the Tide War. I know, because I asked permission yesterday, and was refused, and went anyway." A flicker of grim humor. "I took a small cutting. From Admiral Corvane's tomb. The man who broke the blockade two hundred years ago. Your greatest hero."

"That's desecration!" Ossian was on his feet.

"It's a conversation," Paloma said. "One he's been waiting two hundred years to have."

She pressed the bone to her lips. Closed her eyes.

And spoke.

* * *

Later, Seren would try to describe it to himself and fail. He had heard her listening before — the murmured half-voices, the borrowed cadences. This was different. This was a channel opening. When Paloma spoke with the admiral's voice, it was not her voice at all. It was a man's voice, deep and rusted with age, with the flat command of someone who had shouted orders over cannon-fire, and it filled the marble chamber the way cold fills a cellar.

*"Where is my fleet?"*

Delegates flinched. One woman gasped aloud. Crane's composure held, but his knuckles whitened on the armrest.

*"Where is my fleet? The smoke — I see smoke over the harbor. Who is burning? Who gave the order to burn?"* The voice swung, disoriented, furious, grieving. *"Two hundred years. I have been in the dark two hundred years, and the first thing I smell is smoke. Is it Voss? The smugglers used to say a man named Voss would come. We laughed at them. Gods forgive us, we laughed."*

"He knows," Seren breathed. "He already knows about Voss. The dead talk to each other down there—"

*"You above. Whoever holds my bone. Listen to me."* The voice dropped, and the whole chamber leaned toward it without meaning to. *"I died defending this coast. Every man in those crypts died defending this coast. We did not die so that the living could argue about whether the sea is rising. Ask me anything. Ask me, and then ask yourselves why you needed to ask."*

Paloma opened her eyes. Tears ran freely down her face — she wasn't hiding them, wasn't wiping them away; she simply let the dead use her grief as a door. "Ask him," she said, in her own voice, ragged now. "Anyone. Ask him anything only Corvane could know. Test me. Please. It's the only mercy I'm asking for."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Maren of Brackwater rose from her seat, slow and deliberate, an old woman who had clearly decided that if the world had gone mad she would meet it standing.

"Admiral," she said, addressing the bone directly, with the practical courage of a woman who had negotiated with pirates and found this no stranger. "In your final battle, you gave an order that the histories refuse to record. My grandmother served on your flagship. Tell us what it was."

The change in Paloma's face was minute — a tightening around the eyes, the listening posture — and then the dead man's voice rolled out again.

*"I ordered the fleet to scuttle our own ships in the harbor mouth. To drown them — our own ships, our own pride — and choke the channel so the enemy armada could not enter. Forty-one vessels. Good ships. I wept doing it, and I told my officers that any man who repeated what he saw would hang."* A pause, and something almost like humor, ancient and dry as driftwood. *"So the histories are silent because I threatened to hang the historians. Madam delegate, your grandmother was a gunner's mate with a bad knee and a worse temper, and she was the one who convinced me to do it. Tell her family the debt is still owed."*

The silence afterward was absolute. Even the gulls outside seemed to hold off.

Maren sat down slowly. "That order has been debated by scholars for two centuries," she said quietly. "There is no record. There was never any record." She looked at Crane. "First Speaker. With respect. There is your proof."

"There is a trick," Crane said, but his voice had lost its footing. "There is always a trick—"

"Test me again," Paloma said. "All of you. All night if you must. Bring me bones from your own family tombs, from graves only you know, and I will wake them for you one by one. I have nothing left to hide. That is the entire point." She lowered the admiral's bone with both hands, careful as a priest handling relics, and Seren understood suddenly that for her it was exactly that — that she had carried these voices like a burden for five years alone, and today she was setting them down in front of strangers and trusting the strangers not to smash them.

Ossian of Greyharbor stood. "One question," he said, and there was something hungry in it that Seren distrusted immediately. "The child. If the dead can speak through her carvings, what can the child do? The child carries this — name. Can the child prove itself?"

Paloma stepped in front of Wren so fast it was nearly a blur. "No."

"The council asks—"

"The council can ask me. The council will not put that child on display like a relic at market. She is six years old. She watched her village burn. She has earned the right to stand in one room where no one demands anything of her." Paloma's voice finally rose, and the whole chamber heard five years of fear in it. "You want proof of the threat? Look at her. Look at what Voss burned to get near her. A village of fishermen. Two hundred souls. That is what your disbelief costs while you sit here asking a child to perform."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere in the tiers, a delegate began, very quietly, to cry.

* * *

They deliberated for four hours. Seren knew because he counted the bells, standing in the antechamber with Paloma and Wren while the doors stayed shut and the argument inside rose and fell like weather. Paloma sat on a stone bench with Wren asleep against her shoulder, the child worn out from the tension, and stared at nothing.

"You were magnificent," Seren said.

"I was stupid." She said it without heat. "I was stupid and now everyone in this city knows what I am. By tomorrow there will be informers selling my name in three taverns. By next week Voss will know I'm here." She shifted Wren's weight. "I hid for five years, Seren. Five years keeping my head down, carving harmless little charms, lying to every customer about what the bones really did. And I threw it away in ten minutes because a room full of merchants wouldn't believe a burning village was enough."

"It wasn't stupidity. It was the bravest thing I've seen anyone do, and I once watched a woman hold a bridge against forty riders."

"You would think that. You're the man who refused to burn a child." She glanced at him sideways. "We collect each other's worst decisions and call them virtues."

Before he could answer, the doors opened.

Maren of Brackwater came out first, followed by the others, and their faces told the story before the herald could: pale, shaken, divided — but moving. Deciding. Crane brought up the rear looking like a man who had been forced to sign something at knifepoint.

"The council votes at dawn," Maren said. "Levies from four cities, likely five. Ships. Joint command. It passed preliminary discussion nine to none — even Crane, though he'll frame it as provisional." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "You should know what changed their minds. It wasn't the admiral. It was the crying delegate — Ysolde of Lantern Bay. Her father's grave is on a hill only she knows. She's sending for soil tonight. She wants to hear him say goodnight." Maren's voice roughened. "Two hundred years of honored dead, and it took one daughter wanting to say goodnight. That's what unmade them. That's what unmade us all."

"Thank you," Paloma said.

"Don't thank me." Maren's smile vanished. "Thank yourself tomorrow, when the informers start selling your name. Because they will, carver. Tonight. I'd leave the city if I were you — except I suspect you won't, and I suspect I know why." Her eyes went to Wren, sleeping, and softened. "Get the child somewhere safe. That's not council business. That's mine."

* * *

They walked back through Brackwater as the lamps were being lit, and Seren watched the city watch them. Nothing overt. Brackwater was too sophisticated for open staring. But doors closed a beat too soon. Conversations paused as they passed. A woman made a sign against evil, quickly, thinking herself unseen.

Paloma saw it too. She kept walking, chin level, Wren's hand in hers.

"The tide house on Rope Street," Seren said. "The smuggler's safehouse. It's warded, it's got two exits, and Tallow owes me enough to keep his mouth shut for a day or two."

"A day or two." Paloma laughed, short and bitter. "Listen to us. Planning in days now. Last month it was hours."

"That's progress. In the marshes we planned in minutes."

They reached the corner by the chandlery, and Wren stopped. Stopped dead, dragging both of them to a halt, and pointed — not at a person, not at a building, but at the harbor. At the water.

The child's face had gone white as bone-dust. Her lips moved, shaping silent syllables of the First Tongue, and her hand gripped Paloma's so hard the knuckles went bloodless.

"Wren?" Paloma crouched. "What is it? What do you see?"

Wren didn't point again. She pressed both hands flat over her ears, then — worse — pressed them over her heart, and looked up at Paloma with an expression Seren had last seen on soldiers who had heard the horns of an army they couldn't yet see.

Paloma went still. Then, slowly, she drew out the smallest of her bones — a knucklebone, a listener, barely bigger than a coin — and cupped it in her palm.

Her face changed.

"Seren." Her voice came out stripped bare. "The dead in the harbor. The drowned ones, the old ones, the ones under the bay." She swallowed. "They're singing. All of them. They've been silent for centuries and they're singing, and Wren can hear it through the ground, through the stones, through everything." She looked up at him, and in the lamplight her eyes were wide and bright with a terror that had nothing to do with informers or councils or Voss's riders.

"It's a summons. Something down there is calling the dead to gather." Her hand closed around the knucklebone until the carving bit into her skin. "And the song is getting louder."

Behind them, out across the black water, far past the harbor lights, the sea began — very faintly, very deeply — to hum.
