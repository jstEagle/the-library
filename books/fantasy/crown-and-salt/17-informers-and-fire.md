## 17. Informers and Fire

The summons came at the gray hour, when gulls fought over fish guts on the quay and Brackwater's lamps burned low and orange in the mist. A council runner, a boy of perhaps twelve with rain-slicked hair and eyes that would not meet mine.

"The Speaker is asked to attend the Council Hall before the morning tide."

*Speaker*. Two days since I had stood in the great vaulted chamber and let the honored dead of Brackwater speak through my carved whalebone — old admirals and shipwrights and one weeping grandmother whose voice had made three councilors cover their faces. Two days since I had unmade five years of hiding in the space of a single hour, because Seren had stood before those men and said, *Voss is coming, and your walls will not hold*, and they had not believed him until the dead themselves rose up through my bones to say it.

I had thought it a kind of triumph. Now, walking the wet cobbles with the runner's silence stretching ahead of me like a rope, I felt only the particular cold of a trap I could not yet see.

The Council Hall smelled of tallow and salt rot. Half the chairs were empty. Alderman Corvane sat at the head where the High Speaker should have been — Corvane, silver-bearded, generous with his wine, who had wept openly when his father's voice came through my bone and asked forgiveness for a debt unpaid these forty years.

He did not offer me wine this morning. He did not ask me to sit.

"The garrison reports," he said, each word laid down like a coin on a merchant's scale, "that the eastern beacon was not lit last night. That the watch rotation on the marsh road was changed without order. That a rider came through the Sedge Gate at moonrise, was admitted by someone with a council seal, and rode out again before dawn."

My mouth had gone dry. "You think—"

"I think," Corvane said, and there was no silver in his voice now, only iron gone rusty, "that two nights ago a woman stood in this hall and showed us wonders. And I think that this morning, my fishmonger tells me Voss's outriders were seen burning the ferry at Tarn's Crossing, thirty miles east, moving *west*. Toward us." He leaned forward. "Where is the child, Mistress Zhou?"

The question beneath the question bloomed in my chest like a bruise. He was not asking whether Brackwater was betrayed. He was deciding whom to give to Voss if the walls broke.

"She is safe," I said, which was true, and told him nothing else, which was safer.

Corvane studied me for a long moment. Behind him, an empty chair creaked as someone shifted in the shadows of the gallery — I had not noticed anyone there, and my skin prickled with the old market-honed instinct for being watched. Then he waved me out. "Keep her safe," he said to my back. "And keep her hidden. If what you claim is coming comes, no child carrying anything will leave this city alive unless it leaves quietly."

It was only later — much later, too late — standing in the smoke-choked street while the sky over the eastern wall turned the color of a forge, that I understood what I had seen in that gallery. A man rising from a chair that should have been occupied. A council seal gleaming wet at a wrist. The shape of a bargain already struck, the ink still drying while its architect sat above me wearing his dead father's grief like a borrowed coat.

Seren saw it before I did, when I told him. He always saw the shapes of men's betrayals; it was the trade he'd been trained in, before his exile, before me.

"Corvane's seat is mortgaged," he said flatly. We were in the safehouse above the cooper's yard, and he was checking the edges of his knife with a whetstone that whispered like the sea. "Voss has been buying councilmen in every free city for two years. I told you that. You wanted to believe the council because they wept for their dead."

"They *did* weep."

"People weep beautifully right up until they sell you." He set the stone down. His hands were steady; his eyes were not. "Pack everything. We leave within the hour — not out the gate, out through the fen drains. Wren first."

Above us, on the roof where Wren went to be alone, small footsteps crossed the boards and stopped. The child had learned, in these weeks of flight, to read the changes in adult weather better than any barometer. When Seren said *leave*, Wren was already at the ladder, face pale and composed, the temple stone clutched in one fist the way other children clutch a talisman against nightmares.

We almost made it.

* * *

They came at dusk, when the harbor lamps were lit and the fishing fleet was in, which meant they had waited for the tide that trapped the ships inside the mole. I heard them before I saw them — not with my gift, which had been strangely quiet all day, the dead hushed like birds before a storm, but with my body: the deep tremor in the cobblestones, the dogs along the waterfront beginning to scream.

"Drums," Seren said. He was at the window, and whatever was in his face made my stomach drop. "Not riders. Not a raid. That's a vanguard. Three hundred at least, with ladders and firepots." He turned. "He's not besieging. He's making an example. Brackwater opened its gates to a speaker, so Brackwater burns."

The first fireball arced over the eastern wall while the harbor bell was still ringing the alarm. It trailed sparks like a dying comet and burst against a warehouse roof, and the dry shingles caught with a sound like a held breath released, and then the whole street was orange light and running shadows.

What happened next happened in pieces, and I have put the pieces together a thousand times since, and they never fit into an order I can bear.

Seren shouting, driving us down the stairs into the yard. The cooper's family fleeing past us toward the water, faces blank with animal terror. Smoke rolling thick and black along the lane, swallowing the ends of streets. Seren pressing his knife into my hands — *take it, take it* — because he meant to go toward the gate, not away from it, because somewhere in that burning maze the civilians of Brackwater were trying to reach the harbor steps and Voss's soldiers were between them and the water.

"You can't hold a gate with a knife," I said.

"I'm not holding it with a knife." For one moment his face was open, and I saw what lived under the disgraced inquisitor, the exile, the man who had carried his guilt across half the world to my door: a boy who had once refused to burn a child, and had spent twenty years paying for the refusal as if it had been the crime. "I'm holding it with twenty years of knowing exactly how Voss's men are trained. Go to the drains, Paloma. Take Wren. Don't wait."

Wren took my hand. Small fingers, cold with fear, gripping hard.

We went south along the cooper's row, toward the fen-drain culvert Seren had scouted our first night in the city, and we might have reached it. I believe that. I have needed, some nights, to believe that.

But the crowd of fleeing families bottlenecked at the mouth of Salt Lane — a cart overturned, a horse screaming in its traces, a knot of people crushed and pushing — and through the smoke ahead came soldiers. Six of them. Then ten. Voss's regulars, in their lacquered half-armor, moving with the terrible unhurried discipline of men who had done this in a dozen towns and knew precisely how a panicked crowd breaks. They were not hunting. They were herding, driving the fugitives back from the water with spear-butts and torches, and in the middle distance I heard an officer's voice calling names and descriptions over the roar of the fire.

*"...a woman, dark-haired, bone-carver's hands. A girl of nine or ten, mute or near-mute. The girl alive. The girl alive, do you understand me — Voss pays double for her breathing—"*

Wren's hand tightened in mine. The child looked up at me through the smoke, and I saw the calculation happen behind those too-old eyes, the same arithmetic I was doing myself: the crowd could not get through; the soldiers were fanning out; the drain was forty yards away and might as well have been forty miles.

Then Wren let go of my hand.

I want to tell you I was fast enough. I want to tell you I understood what the child was doing in that last half-second, saw the little shoulders square, heard the shape of the word forming in a throat that had been silent for years except in a language older than kingdoms. But the truth is uglier. The truth is that a squad of soldiers swept around the overturned cart, and the crowd surged backward like a wave, and I was thrown against a wall with the breath driven out of me, and when I fought free of the pressing bodies —

Wren was gone. A small figure swallowed by smoke, toward the soldiers, *away* from me — and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that my child had seen the officer pointing at my description and had chosen, with nine years of wisdom bought in fire and flight, to draw the hunt.

I heard the shout go up — *"The girl! There! Alive — ALIVE, you idiots!"* — and the pack of soldiers turning like hounds, and I heard, faint and terrible above the roar of the burning city, Wren's voice rise in the First Tongue.

One word. The ground-shaking word from the ford, the word that had drowned three men in their boots. But Wren was exhausted, half-starved, terrified, and the word came out cracked, a bell struck badly. Two soldiers staggered. One fell into the flooded gutter and did not rise. The rest closed in, and something struck the child — a pommel, a fist — and the voice stopped.

I screamed. I drove into the smoke toward that knot of armored men with Seren's knife in my fist, and a fishwife — a stranger, gods keep her, a broad-shouldered woman named Maren whom I had haggled with exactly once — threw her arms around my waist and dragged me down into an alley as three more soldiers loped past.

"They've got her," she gasped into my ear, holding me with the strength of desperation. "Girl, they've got her, and if you go after six of them with a table knife they'll have you too, and then who's left?"

The arithmetic. The hard, filthy arithmetic that mothers are asked to do in burning cities: one child taken, alive, because Voss wanted her alive. Myself, the second prize, the speaker, worth capturing but apparently not worth the same double bounty. If I died here in this alley, Wren would be alone in Voss's camp with no one coming.

If I lived, I could come.

Maren held me until the soldiers passed. I do not remember struggling. I remember the smell of her wool coat, wet with spray and smoke, and I remember that the dead, silent all day, chose that moment to speak — a chorus of them, the burned and the drowning and the old dead of Brackwater rising through the rubble under my knees, and what they said was not comfort.

*"The catacombs,"* they said, hundreds of voices braided into one. *"Under the eastern hill. The drowned gate. We will show you. Come. COME."*

* * *

I found Seren at the north gate an hour later, and I knew before I reached him.

The gate itself was a ruin — its timbers fired, its portcullis dropped, and before it lay the evidence of what it had cost. Voss's dead in rows. Brackwater's dead among them. And in the center of the breach, propped against a fallen banner-pole with his sword across his knees like a man resting after a long day's work, Seren Oduya.

He was holding his own side together with both hands. His armor — the scavenged brigandine he'd worn since the marshes — was stove in below the ribs, and the blood between his fingers was very dark in the firelight.

Around him, maybe forty civilians huddled, the ones who had made it out through the gate while he stood in it. An old man was binding him with ripped shirts, hands shaking. Nobody spoke.

"Seren." I knelt. My knees hit the stones and I barely felt them. "You absolute fool. You *fool*—"

"Wren?" His voice was gravel. The first thing he asked. Of course it was.

My face answered for me. I watched the light go out of him by degrees, like a lamp running dry.

"They took her," I said. The words tasted of ash. "She gave herself up so they wouldn't take me. She *chose* it, Seren, she's nine years old and she did the sums faster than I did and she—" My voice broke and I hated it and I let it break, because some things should not be carried silently. "They're marching her to Voss's camp. And I know where it is. The dead showed me. There are catacombs under the eastern hill, the old temple drains, they run all the way under the ridge to the flood-meadows where his vanguard is bivouacked, and the ghosts say—"

"Paloma." He caught my wrist. His grip was still strong; that frightened me more than the blood. "Listen to me. You cannot carry her out of an army camp alone."

"Then I won't be carrying her. I'll be guiding her. She knows the way to the water; she's done it before; she escaped them once at Tarn's Crossing—"

"With a night and a head start and no wound in her side." He tried to laugh and it became a cough, wet and bad. "No. No — listen." He pulled me close, so close I could count the lines the last months had carved into his face, and his voice dropped until only I could hear it, and the dead, and perhaps no one else. "There's a tide coming. The spring tide, tomorrow's dawn, the highest of the year — the old harbor-master told me, the day we arrived. The floods will back up every drain and culvert in the city, including yours, including the catacombs. Whatever door you go in by, the sea will follow the same road. You'll have one window, from slack water to the turn. Two hours. Perhaps less."

"And you'll be here," I said, "bleeding."

He smiled then, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen on a living man — tender and finished at once, the smile of someone setting down a weight.

"I held the gate," he said. "Forty-one people through. Count them, they're all here. Twenty years ago I signed an order that killed everyone you loved, and I have been walking toward this gate ever single day since, whether I knew it or not. Let it have meant something." His thumb moved once over my knuckles, gentle as a confession. "Go get our girl."

*I* — I have no words for what I did not say. That forgiveness was not mine to give fully yet, that trust was a broken bowl I did not know how to mend, that none of it mattered tonight and all of it mattered forever. I pressed my forehead to his, blood and smoke and salt between us, and then I stood up, because the tide does not wait, and neither do the dead.

Behind me, Seren Oduya settled back against the banner-pole at the ruined gate of a burning city, and lifted his sword, and faced the road Voss's army would come by — because a man holding a position must look like a position, even alone, even dying — and I ran toward the flooded hills with a hundred ghosts streaming at my heels like foam before a wave.

The dead sang in my bones as I ran, and for the first time since the knock before dawn, I did not wish them quiet.

*Hold,* they sang. *Hold, mother-carver. The sea is rising.*
