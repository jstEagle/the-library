## 18. The Drowned Gate

The dead did not sleep, but they kept hours. After midnight, when the war camp's fires burned low and the sentries' thoughts turned to cold feet and warm memories, the ghosts of the drowned catacombs rose like tide and came to find her.

Paloma felt them before she saw them — a pressure behind her eyes, familiar as an ache in an old wound. She lay in the reed shelter two miles from the camp's edge, listening to Wren's absence the way one listens to a missing tooth. The child had been gone three days. Three days of Seren bleeding in a Brackwater cellar while a bone-setter stitched him closed, three days of Paloma staring at the ceiling and doing arithmetic with her own cowardice.

*She was taken because of you,* the dead had said, over and over, until the words wore grooves in her.

Now they said something else. Now they said *come.*

She sat up in the dark. The reeds around her shivered though there was no wind. Out of the marsh water, pale as moonlight on milk, the shapes gathered — the priestesses of the sunken temple, and with them others, soldiers drowned at the ford, villagers burned from the coast, a thousand small dead drifting toward her like silt toward a drain.

*The catacombs,* said the eldest of the priestesses, a woman whose ghost wore her drowning like a crown, hair floating around a face serene and terrible. *Beneath the black tents. Voss has made his seat over our oldest gate. He does not know what sleeps under his floor.*

*And Wren?*

*Alive. He needs her alive. The name cannot be carved out of a corpse.* A pause, and the ghost's serenity cracked, just slightly, into something like grief. *But he is learning how to make a child wish she were one.*

Paloma stood. Her knees ached. She was forty-one years old and she had never held a sword longer than a kitchen knife, and she was about to walk into the heart of the man who had burned the coast of a nation looking for her.

"Show me the way in," she said.

* * *

The dead made her an army, though not the kind Voss feared.

They could not touch the living — that law was older than the god, older than the kings, older even than the First Tongue. But they could *see*, and they could *speak*, and through her carved bones they could lend her their eyes. Paloma had spent five years hiding what she was, whispering to whalebone in a market stall, pretending the voices were imagination. Now she walked through the night marsh with a necklace of bones — her grandmother's knucklebone, the ford soldier's finger-bone, a rib of a priestess whose name had been lost — and the dead poured through them into her, and she saw the war camp from forty directions at once.

It was like being a flood. She felt the camp the way water feels a hull: the weight of it, the seams, the rotten places. Four thousand soldiers. Four hundred horses. The black tents at the center where the officers slept, and beneath them — she felt it as a coldness in her teeth, a silence like a held breath — the drowned gate.

*There,* said the priestess-ghost. *The old stair. It opens in the marsh, a mile east, where the reeds grow black. We drowned the road over it ourselves, long ago, so that no king could march an army down to wake what sleeps below.*

"And now I'm going to walk down it alone."

*You will not be alone,* the ghost said. *You will be more crowded than any living woman has ever been.*

The old stair was exactly where the dead said it would be, a seam of cut granite hidden under a mat of reeds so dense it had to be cut away with her knife, hand over hand, in water to her waist. The stone was carved. She felt the carvings before she saw them — spirals, wave-patterns, the long looping script of the First Tongue — and when her fingers traced them, the bones at her throat sang, softly, a note like a wet finger on a glass rim.

Below, the dark was absolute, and the dark was full of voices.

She went down.

* * *

The catacombs had been a temple once. She understood that from the way the passages moved — not the cramped logic of storage or burial but the processional sweep of worship, corridors that widened into chambers and narrowed again like a throat singing. The flood had come and never left; the water stood knee-deep and black and utterly still, and in it the reflections of the dead priestesses walked beside her, dozens of them, their faces tilted toward her with an attention that should have been terrible and was instead, strangely, the safest thing she had felt in years.

*Left,* they murmured. *Left, then down three steps. The ceiling falls here — mind your head, daughter. There is a grate. It was locked from the other side, but the lock is iron and iron forgets.*

She heard what they meant. The lock had rusted through decades ago; the dead had simply never been asked to look. She slipped through the broken grate into a passage that smelled of smoke and horse and men, and she understood, with a lurch, that she had passed from the drowned world into the living one — that somewhere above her, Voss's campfires were burning.

*The gate is beneath the great black tent,* the priestesses said. *He has built his command over it. He sleeps above the god's name like a man sleeping on a treasure chest, and he does not know the chest is a mouth.*

"And Wren?"

*Below. There are cells in the old priest quarters, dry ones, where the order once kept its relics. He has put her there with a guard who does not know what he guards.*

*How many?* she almost asked, and then stopped herself, because the answer didn't matter. She could not fight four men. She could not fight one. What she had was the dead, and a knife, and the particular desperation of a woman who had already lost everything she was afraid to lose.

She moved.

The dead were her eyes. Through her grandmother's knucklebone she saw the corridor above — a torchlit passage, two soldiers dicing, bored, their spears leaning against the wall. Through the ford soldier's finger-bone she saw the stair up from the cells, and the guard there, young, chewing something, his mind full of a girl in a village he'd been ordered to leave alone. Through the priestess's rib she felt the shape of the whole understructure, the drowned gate a cold presence below everything, patient as pressure.

She came up through a maintenance stair — the priestesses called it the *ash door*, where the temple's incense had once been carried down — and emerged behind a stack of supply crates in a storeroom that smelled of tar and salted meat. Above her, muffled by planking, men's voices, laughter, the ordinary sounds of an army at night.

*The cells,* the dead whispered. *Down the passage, past the sleeping quartermaster. The guard at the door has a key on a cord.*

She found the passage. She found the quartermaster asleep at his desk, a ledger open under his cheek, and she stood over him for a long moment with her knife drawn, listening to his breathing, feeling the strange tenderness she had always felt for sleeping strangers — the dead were loudest around sleepers, and this man's dreams were full of his mother's kitchen. She did not kill him. She took the key ring from the hook by the door instead, and her hands shook, and the dead steadied them like a current steadies a swimmer.

The guard at the cell stair was the harder problem. He was awake, and young, and when she stepped out of the dark he was reaching for his spear before she had even decided what to do —

— and the dead did it for her. Not violence. The priestess's rib flared cold against her chest, and Paloma spoke without deciding to, one syllable of the First Tongue, a word the ghosts had taught her in the temple that meant *sleep* the way rain means the sea. The guard's eyes rolled white. He sat down where he stood, spear clattering, and began to snore.

Paloma stared at her own hands.

*I'm sorry,* she told him, uselessly, and went down the stairs.

* * *

The cell door was oak, banded in iron, and the key fit. Inside, by the light of a single guttering lamp, Wren sat on a stone shelf with her knees drawn up, and when the door opened the child's head came up like a bird's, and her eyes went wide, and her mouth opened — and no sound came out, because they had taken her voice again, or she had given it up, or fear had simply closed the door the mute years had once closed.

Paloma crossed the cell in three steps and knelt and gathered her in, and Wren's arms locked around her neck with a strength that had nothing childlike in it, and the two of them stayed like that while the lamp guttered and the dead stood in the doorway like an honor guard.

*Later,* the priestess said gently. *Grieve later. The gate is open, daughter, and the man who built his tent above it is already awake.*

Paloma pulled back and looked at Wren's face. There was a bruise along the child's jaw. There was ink on her fingers — they had been making her *write*, Paloma realized, over and over, the sounds she would not speak, hunting the name syllable by syllable the way a torturer hunts a confession. Rage went through Paloma like a blade going into water, silent and total.

"We're leaving," she whispered. "Can you walk?"

Wren nodded. Then her hand shot out and caught Paloma's wrist, and her other hand pointed — down. Through the floor. Her eyes were enormous.

"I know," Paloma said. "The gate. We're not going near it."

Wren shook her head, hard, and pointed again, and then — slowly, watching Paloma's face — she raised her hands and made the sign she had used in the marshes, the one Paloma had learned to read as *the deep one, the one below, the one who waits.* Then she pointed at herself. Then at Paloma. Then she made a shape with her hands that needed no translation: a mouth, opening.

*She wants to go down,* the dead said, and their voices had gone strange, hushed, the hush of a congregation. *She has always wanted to go down. She is the door's key, and keys ache to be turned.*

"No," Paloma said. "No. We go up. We go out. We get to Brackwater and we — "

The lamps in the corridor above went out.

Not one lamp. All of them, in a wave, a darkness rolling down the passage toward the cells like a shutter closing, and with it came a sound Paloma had heard once before, years ago, in the night market, the night the wardens came for her neighbor: the particular silence of men being very careful.

*He knows,* the dead whispered, and for the first time in all the long ages of their death, the priestesses sounded afraid. *Daughter. He has known since the ford. The ambush was not meant to catch you. It was meant to drive you.*

The cell door at the top of the stairs opened. Torchlight poured down, and in it, silhouetted, stood a tall man in a plain black coat with no insignia at all — a man who needed none. He descended the stairs slowly, unhurried, the way a man descends into his own cellar, and behind him came six soldiers and, bound and gray-faced between two of them, a figure Paloma knew.

Seren. Alive. Barely. His left arm hung wrong. His eyes found hers in the torchlight and in them she read everything at once — *I'm sorry, they took me on the road, I told them nothing, I'm sorry, run* — and then a guard's gauntlet closed on his wounded arm and his face went white and he said nothing at all.

Kaelen Voss stopped at the bottom of the stairs.

He was not what she had expected. She had built him out of rumor and rage: a colossus, a butcher, a thing of iron and fire. What stood before her was a lean, tired man in his fifties with gray in his close-cropped hair and eyes that were — that was the worst of it — *familiar*. She had seen those eyes in her own mirror. The eyes of someone who had heard the dead, and never once been allowed to say so.

"You're the carver," he said. His voice was quiet. It carried anyway, the way a blade carries. "Paloma Zhou. Saltmere market, the bone stall, Thursdays and Saturdays. Do you know how long I have had a man watching that stall?"

"Since the ford," she said. Her voice did not shake. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of the pride.

"Since before the ford," Voss said. "Since Brackwater, since the council. You showed the council their honored dead through your little carved bones, and every man in that chamber felt it, and one of them came to me within the hour. People always come to me." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, not because it was cruel but because it was *tired*. "I didn't need to catch you, Mistress Zhou. I needed you to come to me. The drowned gate is beneath my floor. The key was in my cellar. And you — " he looked at her hands, her bone-stained, ordinary hands, with something that was almost reverence — "you are the last priestess, whether you knew it or not. The only voice left that can speak the name and live long enough to finish the sentence."

"You'll never — "

"I don't want to force you." He said it gently, and that was the horror of it, that he meant it. "I was a starving boy in a fishing village once. I heard the dead in the fish-bones my mother threw out, and when I told the priest, they held my hand in a lamp flame until I learned to say I'd heard nothing. I learned very fast." His eyes went distant. "I have spent my whole life learning to say I hear nothing. I am finished with it. Open the gate, Mistress Zhou. Speak the name. And I will give you the one thing no one ever gave either of us."

"Which is?"

"A world where hearing the dead is not a crime."

Behind him, Seren made a small sound, and Wren's hand found Paloma's and gripped it, and down below them all, through the floor, through the stone, through the still black water, Paloma felt the drowned gate waiting — patient, cold, and listening.

* * *

The choice stood in front of her wearing a tired man's face, and Paloma understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Voss had told her the truth — that he had been a boy who heard the dead and was burned for it, that his whole war was a wound that had never closed. She understood, too, what the priestesses had warned her in the sunken temple: *the name changes the speaker forever, and power freely accepted always corrupts its bearer.* She could see her own hunger reflected in him as clearly as her face in the black water. He was what she might become, given a war and thirty years.

"No," she said.

Voss sighed, and the gentleness went out of the room like a tide going out, and what remained beneath it was the thing that had burned the coast.

"Then we do it the hard way," he said, and nodded to his soldiers. "Take the child."

Paloma's hand closed around the priestess's rib at her throat, and every ghost in the drowned dark below rose at once.
