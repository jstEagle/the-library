## 19. Two Voices, One Name

The drowned gate opened into a hall that had once been a temple and was now a throat.

Paloma stood in the flooded nave with water to her knees and the dead all around her like a congregation that had never been dismissed. Torchlight from the catacomb mouth behind her turned the black water to hammered bronze. Ahead, at the far end of the hall where an altar had been torn out and replaced with a campaign table, Kaelen Voss sat waiting on a stolen chair, his boots propped on a map of the free cities as though the map were a footstool made for him by the world.

Around the walls, soldiers. Thirty of them, maybe forty, their spears wet with the same brine that soaked her skirts. They had let her come. They had *escorted* her, she realized now — every ghost-guided turn through the catacombs had been a corridor they had left open, a door they had not locked. She had thought herself clever, slipping through the flooded dark with the dead for eyes. She had been walking down a throat the whole time.

And in the center of the hall, on a low stone plinth that still bore the grooves of old chains, sat Wren.

The child was dry. That was the first thing Paloma noticed, the thing that made her stomach drop through the water beneath her feet. Everyone else in this drowned place was wet — the soldiers, the walls weeping condensation, Voss himself with damp cuffs — but Wren sat on the plinth wrapped in a soldier's cloak, and the cloak was dry, and Wren's hair was dry, and around the child the water lay flat and still as glass, refusing even to ripple.

*They can't touch what carries the Name,* whispered a dead priestess near the wall, one of the voices that had taught Paloma the grammar of grief in the sunken temple. *But they can cage it. Caging is a thing men are very good at.*

"Wren." Paloma's voice came out steadier than she felt. She took a step forward and the water dragged at her legs like hands. "Look at me, little bird."

Wren looked up. The child's eyes were wide and wet and terribly awake, and when they found Paloma's face, something in them broke open — relief and terror in the same breath, the look of someone who has been holding a scream behind their teeth for days and finally sees a person worth screaming at. Wren's mouth moved. No sound came. The First Tongue did not work here, Paloma understood; Voss had seen to that somehow, or the stone of the plinth had, or the god itself had gone quiet to keep from being overheard.

"Ah," said Kaelen Voss, and swung his boots off the map. "The bone-carver."

He rose. In the stories the smugglers told, in the whispers that crossed the marshes ahead of his armies, he was a giant, a shadow, a man made of smoke and fire. He was none of those things. He was a broad, ordinary-looking man in his forties with a soldier's weathered face and a scholar's careful hands, and he walked toward her through the floodwater without hurry, as a host walks toward a guest who has arrived late to dinner.

"You cost me two villages," he said pleasantly. "A ford. Four hundred men, give or take, though I've stopped counting men; they're the cheapest thing I own. And you cost me Seren Oduya, whom I had half forgiven." He stopped six feet from her, close enough that she could see the fine lines around his eyes, the small scar splitting one eyebrow. "I want you to know I'm not angry. Anger is for people who don't understand what they've lost."

"I know what you lost," Paloma said. "I heard it. In Brackwater, your bone-reader died telling your captains where to find us. Do you know what she said, at the end? Through the bone? She said, *he never asked my name.*"

Something flickered across Voss's face — quick, quickly buried. "Maren," he said. "Her name was Maren Ashk. She read bones for me eleven years." He turned away, gestured, and a soldier brought a small chest and set it on the campaign table. "You hear the dead. So did she. So did I."

The last words dropped into the water between them and sank.

"You were a child," Paloma said slowly. The dead had told her this, or hinted it, in the temple — *the kings above punished the hearing once, long ago, before the ban, before the burnings* — but hearing it from his own mouth was different. It landed differently.

"I was a starving boy in a fishing village your precious free cities have forgotten ever existed," Voss said. His voice stayed level, conversational, which somehow made it worse. "My mother drowned when I was seven. And she came back. Not as a ghost — nothing so kind. She came back through the herring bones I cracked for soup. Every night, her voice, in my skull, asking me why I hadn't fed the baby before the baby starved. I told the village priest. Do you know what they do to boys who hear the dead?" He smiled, and it was the smile of a man showing an old wound to prove it no longer hurts. "They hold your head under the well water until the voices stop. It doesn't work. But it teaches you something valuable: that everyone who hears is alone, unless someone powerful decides otherwise."

"And so you decided otherwise."

"I decided to be the powerful one." He opened the chest. Inside, nested in wool, lay bones — dozens of them, whalebone and knucklebone and femur slivers, each carved with the fine spiral work that Paloma knew better than her own signature. Her work. Charms confiscated from Saltmere, from Brackwater, from the burned villages. A museum of everything his informers had bought. "Eleven years, Maren read the dead for me and hated me for it, and I let her hate me because hatred keeps a tool sharp. But tools break. You—" he pointed one careful finger at her, "—you don't break. You hid for five years in a city full of informers and no one sold you until a child dragged you into the light. You crossed my marshes. You drowned three of my riders' brothers at a ford and kept walking. You are the best reader of the dead I have ever encountered, and I am offering you what I offered no one else."

He closed the chest. He stepped closer, and the water between them went strangely warm.

"Not service. Not a chain like Maren wore. Partnership." He spread his hands, and Paloma saw that his palms were calloused in the pattern of a carver's — he had held bones too, once, a starving boy cracking herring skeletons for his mother's voice. "You heard what the priests told you in the sunken temple. The Name can be spoken once, by one voice. One voice, bone-carver. But a voice needs ears, and ears need a voice. Together — you and I, the two people alive who understand what it is to carry the dead in your skull — we could speak it *carefully*. We could command the god instead of serving it. Command it, and command everything after. The sea is the road to every kingdom, and every kingdom knows it. No more burnings. No more well water. No more children held under until the voices stop. We would end the war against speakers forever, because no one wars against the hand that holds the ocean."

He said it beautifully. That was the horror of it. He said it the way she had sometimes, in the worst hours of the night market, thought it to herself: *if only they understood, if only I could make them listen, if only the power were mine to use rightly.*

And there it was. Her own hunger, standing in front of her wearing a usurper's face.

She saw it clearly, the way the dead see things — without mercy and without flattery. She saw the five years of hiding curdling into a wish to be feared instead of hunted. She saw the moment at the Council of Free Cities when she had exposed her gift and felt, underneath the terror, a hot and shameful thrill when four hundred people went silent at the sound of their honored dead speaking through her bones. She had wanted that silence. She had wanted to be the woman who could summon the ancestors of kings. If Voss had never existed, some smaller, kinder version of him would have had to be invented out of her own ribs.

"No," Paloma said.

Voss tilted his head. "Think—"

"I have thought. For five years, in a cellar, with dust in my lungs, I have thought about exactly this." Her voice shook, and she let it. "You're right about me. That's the filthiest trick you could have pulled, being right about me. I *have* wanted what you're offering. I've wanted it the way a starving person wants bread — stupidly, completely, ready to eat it out of the mud." She looked up at him. "But I've also listened to the dead for five years, and do you know what they say? All of them? Every ghost I've ever carved a channel for?"

"Tell me."

"*We were sure, too.*" The words came out of her like water from a split hull. "Every single one. The priestesses were sure. The kings were sure. The inquisitors were sure. Your Maren was sure, right up until she died nameless in your war camp. Certainty is the first thing power gives you and the last thing it takes back. You aren't offering to end the burning, Voss. You're offering to become the fire."

For a long moment the only sound was water dripping somewhere in the dark above, counting seconds like a patient clerk.

Then Voss sighed, and the warmth went out of the air between them, and he became, entirely and simply, the man who burned villages.

"Pity," he said. "Wren!"

The child flinched on the plinth. Around the hall, forty spears came down to point at a nine-year-old sitting dry in the middle of a flood.

"The priests hid the Name inside a living mind because a living mind can refuse to speak it," Voss said, walking back toward the plinth, unhurried again, a man resuming an unpleasant chore. "Clever of them. Foolish too, because minds can be *persuaded*, and children persuade so easily. Fear works. Pain works. But do you know what works best, bone-carver?" He reached the plinth. He put one hand, almost gently, on top of Wren's head. "Hope. Tell the child that everyone lives if only the word gets spoken — tell it often enough, in the right voice — and children will say anything. Children will say *anything* to save the people they love." He looked back at Paloma, and his eyes were the eyes of a boy at the bottom of a well, looking up. "I've spent three days teaching Wren the story where I'm the rescuer. Three days. Children are quick."

Wren's face. Paloma would carry Wren's face to her grave — the dawning, drowning horror of a child realizing the story they'd been telling themselves to survive was the trap itself.

"So here is how tonight ends," Voss said. "Either you take my hand and we speak the Name together, on our terms, with the god leashed before it ever stands up — or my soldiers teach Wren the other story, the one with the well water in it, and the child speaks the Name screaming, and I take it from a broken mouth. Choose. You're good at choosing. You chose to walk into my throat."

The spears did not waver. The water did not move. And in the silence, Paloma heard them — the dead, her dead, all of them at once, the grandmother whose voice she had paid to a captain, the priestesses of the sunken temple, the marsh soldiers, the drowned fishwives of two burned villages, Maren Ashk whose name Voss had spoken like a coin flipped:

*Not for power. Never for power. For the child. Speak for the child.*

She had known, she realized, since the temple. Since the ghosts had told her the rules — *once, one voice, and it changes the speaker forever* — she had known how this ended, and she had walked into the throat anyway, because knowing and accepting are separated by a distance only love can cross.

"One voice," Paloma said aloud. "That's the rule. One voice, once. Not yours. Not ours." She began to walk toward the plinth, water climbing her thighs, her hips, dragging at her like the whole sea reaching up to claim its due in advance. "Mine."

Voss frowned. "What are you—"

"She's nine years old," Paloma said. "Her voice isn't finished. It hasn't got the weight. The Name needs a voice that has already been changed by the dead — a voice that belongs to them as much as to the living." She reached the plinth. She looked down at Wren, and Wren looked up at her, and the child's lips formed a silent word: *run.*

"No," Paloma said gently. "No more running, little bird. This is the place. This is the place we stand."

She climbed onto the plinth. She knelt. She took Wren's small hands in both of hers, and they were dry and warm and trembling, and through the skin of them she felt the Name — not heard, not anymore, but *felt*, vast and slow and patient as pressure at the bottom of the sea, a word the size of a god folded into a body the size of a child.

"Voss," she said, without turning around. "Listen carefully, because I'll only say this once, and then I won't say anything else for a very long time. You wanted to command the god. Here is your lesson, free of charge: you cannot command what you refuse to belong to. The priestesses didn't own the deep. They *married* it. They gave it their lifetimes, and it gave them its strength, and that was the whole bargain, and it was enough." She laced her fingers through Wren's. "I'm not going to leash it. I'm going to hold its hand the way I'm holding hers. And when it rises, it will rise through *me* — my years, my breath, my time — and everything I don't spend saving these cities, it keeps. That's the price. I pay it gladly. Can you say the same? Can anyone who chains a bone-reader to a wall say the same?"

"Stop her," Voss said quietly. "Don't kill the child. Stop the *woman*."

Boots hit water behind her. Forty of them, closing fast, and Paloma bowed her head over Wren's hands and did the last carving of her life — not in bone, but in breath, shaping the air the way she had shaped whalebone for five years, finding the channels, opening them.

The First Tongue was not a language of words, the priestesses had told her. It was a language of *openings*. Every syllable was a door.

She spoke the first door, and the water in the hall went still as a held breath.

She spoke the second, and every torch guttered blue, and the soldiers stopped as if they had walked into a wall of cold.

She felt Wren's hands tighten on hers, and the child's voice — small, clear, unbroken, rising to meet hers like a second rope thrown across a chasm — spoke the third door with her, and the two voices braided, and the Name came up through them from the deep where it had waited since before kingdoms, since before names, since the first thing that drowned drowned.

It was not loud. That was the terrible part. It was quiet, the way the sea is quiet a mile out, the way a wave is quiet in the moment before it stands up.

The fourth door. The fifth. Paloma felt them opening in her — doors in her blood, her bones, the long corridor of her remaining years — and she felt the years begin to pour out through them, silver and cold, and she let them go, she *gave* them, gladly, gladly, the way you pay anything at last for a place to belong.

Behind her, Kaelen Voss screamed — not in fear, she thought distantly, but in recognition, the scream of a boy in a well hearing, at the very bottom, every voice he had ever silenced coming up the shaft together.

The sixth door opened.

The sea stood up.

* * *

Water filled the drowned gate from the wrong side — from the landward side, from the war camp above, from the horizon — pouring into the hall in a black wall that should have killed them all and instead parted around the plinth like a court making way for a queen. The soldiers ran. Some of them made the stairs. The torches died, and in the dark Paloma held Wren's hands and felt the god arrive.

It did not speak. Gods that size do not need to. It simply *was*, all around her, immense and grieving and old beyond the grammar of any tongue, and it knew her — knew her the way the sea knows every river that has ever run into it. *Little voice,* it seemed to say, in the pressure and the pull. *Little mortal voice. You called. I answer. What is your term?*

"My life," Paloma said, into the dark, into the roar of Voss's armies meeting the water outside. "All the rest of it. Take it. Spend it. And when it's gone — sleep. Go back to sleep, and let the world forget, and let my dead rest."

The pause lasted a century or a heartbeat.

*Accepted,* said the god, and the bargain closed over her like water over a dropped stone, and Paloma Zhou, bone-carver of Saltmere, felt the first of her years leave her — felt her hair go white at the roots, felt the price begin — and did not let go of the child's hands.

Above them, through the shattered roof of the ancient temple, the sky turned the color of the inside of a wave.

The sea was coming for the armies. And it was coming, Paloma knew with the last calm corner of her mind, for Kaelen Voss — carrying, in all its million mouths, every voice he had ever silenced, ready at last to introduce them.
