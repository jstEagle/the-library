## 21. Aftermath in Brackwater

The sea took three days to give back its dead.

Seren watched from the harbor wall as the tide laid them out one by one on the shingle below Brackwater — Voss's soldiers, thousands of them, arranged by no human hand but set down with a strange and terrible gentleness, as though the water had carried them home like a mother gathering children who'd wandered too far. The fishwives would not touch them at first. Then an old woman came down alone, knelt beside a boy no older than Wren, and closed his eyes, and after that everyone came, and the work of burying became the work of mourning, which was a mercy, because a city that has been besieged does not know what to do with its hands until grief gives them something to hold.

Seren's hands held little else. His left arm was splinted from wrist to elbow where a horseman's mace had found it at the gate, and beneath the bandages across his ribs the flesh was angry and weeping, and the chirurgeon told him twice a day that he was fortunate, that a blow meant for his skull had caught only his shoulder, that men who hold gates against vanguard cavalry are not usually men who wake up afterward. Seren nodded each time and said nothing. He did not feel fortunate. He felt like a man who had been given back something he had not earned, and the debt of it sat in him heavier than any wound.

On the fourth morning, he went looking for work.

* * *

Brackwater in the weeks after the flood was a city of scaffolding and smoke. Half the eastern quarter had burned when the vanguard stormed it; half the western quarter had drowned when the sea rose up the canals and took Voss's army off its feet. The free cities' council met in a rented warehouse because their hall had been put to the torch by a council member whose name was already being chiseled out of the civic rolls, and into that warehouse every morning streamed carpenters and masons and widows and orphans and soldiers of three defeated militias with nothing now to do but haul timber.

Seren could not haul timber. He tried, on the fifth day, and a foreman with a face like weathered oak looked at the splint, looked at the bandages, and said, "You'll tear those stitches, inquisitor," and Seren said, "I'm not—" and the foreman said, "Everyone knows what you were. Everyone also knows you held the Saltgate for forty minutes so two hundred people could reach the boats. In this city those things weigh about even now. Go count nails."

So he counted nails. He sat in a timber yard with a tally-slate and counted nails by the thousand, and the work was absurdly easy and absurdly healing, because counting nails required nothing of him except that he be present, and being present, he discovered, was a discipline he had never actually practiced. For twenty years as an inquisitor he had been present in body while his mind ran ahead to the next interrogation, the next confession, the next name on the list. Now there was no list. There were nails, and gulls screaming over the yard, and the smell of cut pine and tar, and slowly, nail by nail, his mind came home to his body and sat down inside it like a traveler finally reaching an inn.

He heard the repeal before he read it.

It came through the yard on a market-day morning, passed mouth to mouth faster than any crier could have managed: the council had voted, near unanimously, to strike the magic ban from the laws of all five free cities. Not just the ban on hearing the dead — the whole rotten edifice, the licensing of gifts, the burning registers, the informer tariffs. Gone. A woman in the yard dropped her hammer and began to cry without making any sound, and when Seren asked a carter what was wrong, the carter said, "Nothing, friend. Everything. Her daughter was taken by your order eleven years ago. Burned at Corrow Fen." He said it without malice, the way you'd report the weather, and walked on.

Seren stood among the nails and let that land. Eleven years ago. He had not signed that particular order — he had been exiled by then — but some other man wearing his old coat had, and somewhere in the crowd of Brackwater were hundreds of mothers and fathers and daughters and sons who had lost people to men exactly like him, men who had believed they were keeping order, keeping the world safe from the unquiet dead, and who had instead spent two decades teaching a whole civilization to be afraid of its own grandmothers.

That evening the speakers began to come out of hiding.

* * *

It started small. An herbwoman in the Saltmarket who had always claimed she could tell which remedies a dead patient had favored. A ferryman who swore the river spoke to him at the bends. Then, within a fortnight, it was everywhere — people standing in doorways talking quietly to the air as if greeting an old neighbor, and no one calling for the wardens, because the wardens had been disbanded and reformed as something called simply the Watch, and the Watch's first proclamation was that it would protect all citizens, "including those who hear."

Seren saw the first of them come to Paloma's workshop on a gray Tuesday.

Her workshop — he still thought of it as hers, though she had never once invited him inside — occupied the ground floor of a rebuilt tenement on Coil Street, and its sign was a single carved whalebone vertebra hung above the door, and under it, in fresh paint, the words: *Bones Carved. Voices Heard. All Welcome.* He had walked past it perhaps thirty times since the flood, telling himself he was merely taking the long way to the timber yard, and each time he had seen figures going in or coming out: the grieving, the curious, the frightened, and lately, unmistakably, others like her. People with the look of long concealment wearing off their faces like frost in sun.

On that Tuesday, an old man stood at her door clutching a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and through the open doorway Seren heard Paloma say — warm, unhurried, the voice of someone who had stopped flinching — "Come in, master Aldric. Let's see what you've brought me. Mind the third floorboard; she still complains."

She had named the floorboard. She named everything now — the boards, the beams, the kettle — and Seren understood, watching from across the street with his hands in his pockets, that this was not whimsy. It was the habit of a woman who had spent five years listening to bones speak and had decided that if the world was going to be full of voices, hers would be a voice of welcome. That the naming was itself a kind of carving: shaping a home out of the material of ordinary hours.

Wren came out onto the step while the old man was still unwrapping his bundle, and Seren's chest did the thing it always did now when he saw the child — a tightening, half love and half shame. Wren had grown in the months since the flood, taller, steadier, the mute terror replaced by a quiet watchfulness that was uncannily like Paloma's own. The child saw him across the street. Held his gaze a moment. And then — this was new, this was three days new — raised one small hand in a solemn wave before going back inside.

Seren stood on Coil Street in the gray light and let himself hope, carefully, the way you carry a flame through wind: cupped, guarded, ready to be extinguished.

* * *

He wrote letters that winter. Dozens of them.

They went to every city of the old Order — to the chapterhouses at Vael and Corrow Fen, to the archive at Greywatch, to the surviving inquisitors scattered like ash after Voss consumed the Order's hierarchy and spat it out. Most went unanswered. Some came back marked *addressee fled.* But a handful came back with replies, and the replies were the hardest reading he had ever done, because they were written by men and women who had been what he was, and they wanted absolution, and they wanted it addressed to them, quickly, cheaply, in ink.

*I have prayed,* wrote one, a former colleague named Maren Holt whom Seren remembered as meticulous and kind. *I have fasted. Tell me what more is required, that I may sleep.*

He sat with that letter for a full evening before answering. Every part of him that still missed the Order — the certainty, the structure, the terrible comfort of believing your cruelty was cleanliness — wanted to write back, *Prayer is enough. Fasting is enough. Sleep, sister.* Because he knew the hunger behind the question. He had gone to bed hungry for it himself for twenty years.

Instead he wrote: *There is no penance I can prescribe you, because I am not qualified to grant what I cannot obtain for myself. What I have learned is this: the families of the burned do not want your prayers. They want their dead, and your prayers cannot return them. Go to the living. Find the speakers who hid from us, and serve them. Carry their loads. Fund their workshops. Stand between them and the next mob. Do this for the rest of your life and do not ask whether it is enough, because it will not be, and the asking is itself a form of theft — you are trying to buy back a clean conscience, and clean consciences are not for sale. We spent ours. Now we spend the rest.*

He sent it, and slept badly, and sent another the next night, and another. It was not absolution. It was not peace. But it was work, and work, he was learning, was what hope looked like when hope grew up.

* * *

In the spring, the council asked him to testify.

Not to defend anything — to account. A Truth Commission, they called it, chaired jointly by a Brackwater magistrate and, to universal astonishment, a bone-speaker named Yeva Corrigan who had spent nine years in the marshes disguised as a hermit and emerged when the ban fell. The commission's mandate was to document what the old regime had done: every register, every burning, every informer payment, so that no future government could claim ignorance. They needed witnesses from inside the Order. They needed, specifically, someone who had held rank and could name names.

Seren testified for six days.

He gave them the ledger codes for the informer tariffs. He gave them the routing of the burn orders, the chain of signatures, the doctrine of *preemptive silencing* taught at the Vael chapterhouse. He described, because the commissioners asked and because he had promised himself he would not soften it, the interrogation techniques — the sleeplessness, the isolation, the use of family members as leverage. On the fourth day a commissioner asked him how many executions he had personally authorized, and the warehouse went very quiet, and Seren said, "Forty-one," and then, because the number deserved its weight, "Their names are appended to my written testimony. I memorized them. It seemed the least their deaths owed me."

Afterward, in the corridor, Yeva Corrigan fell into step beside him. She was small and brown and quick-eyed, and the dead, Paloma had told him once, liked her enormously.

"You didn't have to give the names aloud," she said. "The appendix sufficed."

"The appendix is paper. Paper doesn't have to hear itself say them."

"No," she agreed. "But I'll tell you what I hear, inquisitor, since hearing is apparently my trade now. Around you, the dead are quiet. Not absent — quiet. Like dogs settled at a fire. That's rarer than you think. Most of the guilty trail their victims like a stink. You don't. Do you know why?"

"Tell me."

"Because you keep saying their names. Out loud. Regularly. Like watering a grave." She smiled, thin and dry. "The dead forgive less than the living imagine, but they respect attendance. Keep attending, Seren Oduya."

* * *

He kept attending. And in high summer, on an evening when the light came gold through the gaps in Coil Street's new roofs, Paloma opened her workshop door as he passed and said, "You're going to wear a trench in the cobbles, walking past my window twice a day for eight months. Come in or stop lurking."

His heart slammed once, hard, like a fist on a door. He made himself climb the steps.

Inside, the workshop smelled of whalebone and linseed oil and salt. Workbenches lined the walls, and at them sat seven students of wildly different ages — a fishwife, a boy of twelve, the old man Aldric, a woman in the gray dress of a hospital nurse, two sisters who finished each other's sentences literally, and Wren, who sat at the bench nearest the window with a carving knife held in the careful grip Paloma had spent months teaching. Bones lay everywhere in various states: rough vertebrae waiting their first shaping, half-finished charms, finished pieces strung on cords and labeled in Paloma's precise hand. The room hummed — not with sound, exactly, but with the feeling of a place where many quiet conversations were happening at once.

Every head turned to look at him. Seven pairs of eyes, and under them, he could feel it, the attention of others — the dead of Brackwater, maybe, or just the collective wariness of people who had hidden for decades and did not love seeing an inquisitor's frame fill a doorway.

"I don't want to intrude," Seren said. "I'll go."

"You will not." Paloma leaned against her main bench and folded her arms, and her eyes were unreadable, which they almost never were anymore; she had grown easier in every way except, perhaps, the ways that concerned him. "Sit down. There's a stool by the window. You can hold the sandpaper."

"That's not—"

"It's sandpaper, Seren. Even an inquisitor can manage it." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. A gate left unlatched.

He sat. He took the sandpaper she handed him and the rough vertebra she put in his other hand, and he began, clumsily, to smooth it, and around him the lesson resumed — Paloma explaining grain direction, the way bone remembers pressure, the difference between carving to silence a voice and carving to let one through. Wren worked two stools away, close enough that their elbows nearly touched, and after a long while the child slid a finished piece across the gap: a tiny bird, a wren, wings folded, rendered with a delicacy that made Seren's throat ache.

"For you," Wren said. The voice was clear now, unbroken, a child's voice with an ocean underneath it. "Paloma says you're learning patience. So is the bird. It's not finished being a bird yet. Neither are you."

The students laughed — kindly, mostly — and Seren held the small wooden-bright bone bird in his scarred hand and found he could not speak at all.

At the end of the evening, as the students gathered their things and filtered out into the summer dusk, Paloma stopped him at the door. She looked tired in the good way, the way of someone whose day had been full of work that mattered, and outside her window the first star was out over the harbor where the sea lay flat and forgiving and full.

"Same time next week," she said. It was not quite a question. "If you want it."

"I want it," Seren said. "For as long as you'll have me. Longer than that, probably. I'm told I'm stubborn."

"You're told correctly." She studied him for a moment, and whatever she was weighing, she weighed it thoroughly, the way she weighed everything — bone, word, wound. "One week at a time," she said finally. "That's what I can give. No promises past the horizon. Just the week."

"One week at a time," he agreed, and meant it as a vow.

He walked home through the warm streets of a city rebuilding itself, past doorways where people spoke softly to their dead, past the harbor where the water murmured against the pilings in a language he was slowly, patiently learning to hear without fear, and behind him, above the whalebone sign on Coil Street, a lamp was lit in a workshop window, and it stayed lit while he walked, and it was still lit when he turned the corner, and it was, he thought — carefully, cupping the flame against the wind — a beginning.
