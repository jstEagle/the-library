## 22. Crown and Salt

The tide came in over Brackwater's eastern flats at dawn, and with it the smell of salt and old wood and something sweeter underneath — kelp rot, maybe, or the memory of it — that had once made Paloma gag and now felt like the breath of an old friend.

She stood in the doorway of her new workshop and watched the light come up gray-gold across the harbor. The building had been a net-mender's shed before the war, then a granary, then nothing at all; its roof still sagged in one corner where Voss's fire had kissed it, and the city council, in their first act of genuine generosity since repealing the ban, had given it to her for a song and a promise to fix the roof herself. She had fixed the roof herself. Her shoulders still ached from it, three weeks on, and she found she treasured the ache. It was an honest pain. It belonged to her hands and no one else.

Behind her, inside, the workbenches waited: six of them, planed smooth, each set with a lamp and a leather strop and a shallow dish of water for cooling the blades. On the wall above the benches hung her tools — the good knives, the burins, the little hooked scorers her grandmother had used — and beside them, on iron hooks, hung the bones. Whale rib and deer knuckle, cod vertebrae and the long pale finger-bones of something deep-sea that no fisherman could name. They did not whisper anymore. That was the miracle of it, the quiet miracle nobody but her could feel: they hung there like tools, like material, like *things*, and the dead who had lived in them had gone home.

Most of them, anyway.

"You're up early." Wren's voice came from the loft stairs, sleep-roughened, and Paloma turned to see the child standing there in an oversized shirt that had been Seren's, hair sticking up in every direction like gull feathers. Wren was eleven now by Seren's best guess, though the child had stopped counting years somewhere in the marshes and refused to start again. "The sea's not going anywhere."

"The sea goes everywhere," Paloma said. "That's rather the point of it."

Wren grinned and came down the stairs two at a time, the way children do when they have finally decided a place is safe enough to be loud in. That had taken eight months. Paloma had counted every one of them, and would not have traded a single day of the slow thaw for anything in the drowned god's kingdom.

"Apprentices don't get to be late," Wren said, tying on the too-big apron Paloma had left by the door. The apron had been cut down twice already; the child grew like spring grass. "You said so yourself. First rule of the workshop."

"I said apprentices don't get to be late *twice*. You've never been late once. I'm beginning to suspect you sleep in here when I'm not looking."

"I would never." Wren said it with the absolute sincerity of someone who absolutely had.

* * *

The first students arrived as the sun cleared the harbor wall.

They came shyly, in ones and twos, the way people come to anything that has recently been a crime. A herring-girl named Maren whose knucklebones had murmured to her since she was four and who had learned, in the five years of the ban, to hum loudly whenever they started, so no one would hear her answering them under her breath. An old eel-fisher called Doss who had spent forty years pretending he couldn't hear his drowned crewmates calling from the shallows off the Sedge, and who wept the first time he let himself answer. A widow, Tamsin, whose husband's voice had lived in her wedding ring of carved whale ivory and who had worn gloves every day of her marriage's mourning because she could not bear to touch it and could not bear to throw it into the sea.

Paloma taught them the way her grandmother had taught her, before either of them knew there was anything to hide. How to choose bone — dense, clean, unburned, because burned bone held only screaming. How to listen before you cut, holding the piece against your sternum until you felt which dead thing wanted to live in it, if any, and how to honor the ones that wanted nothing, which were the majority, and which were simply bones. How to make the first cut away from yourself, always, not for safety but for humility. How to know when a carving was finished: not when it looked finished, but when the silence inside it went *settled*, the way a room settles after grief has finally done its work.

And she taught them the other thing, the harder thing, the thing the priestess-ghosts in the sunken temple had pressed into her like a seal into wax: that hearing was not power. That the moment it became power — the moment you reached for it, grasped at it, wanted it to make you more than what you were — it began to eat you from the inside out. She showed them her own hands sometimes, steady now, and told them about the year she had almost spoken a word she had no right to, and what it had cost the woman who did speak it.

She did not tell them everything. Some things belonged to the temple floor and the sea above it.

But she told them this: *"The dead are not yours. You are theirs, a little, if they choose you, and the choosing is a trust, and trusts are kept by giving back."*

Maren carved her first listening-charm in the second week — a small rough thing, a gull's wing-bone scored with waves — and when she held it up, her face went white and then radiant, and she said, "It's my gran. It's my gran, she says the herring are running north," and burst into tears, and Paloma had to sit down on her stool for a while and look out the window at the water until her own eyes behaved.

The dead were quiet now, mostly. At peace. But peace, she had learned, was not silence. Peace was a voice that no longer begged. When Maren's grandmother spoke through the little wing-bone, she spoke the way people speak across a kitchen table — unhurried, fond, a little amused at the living. The god's rising had done that, somehow. It had gone through the world's drowned and burned and buried like a tide through a house, and where it receded it had left the doors open. The dead could stay or go. Most of them stayed a while. But they stayed like guests now, not prisoners.

Paloma's own relics had emptied out one by one through that autumn and winter. The whalebone that had carried her mother's voice had gone silent on the night of the rising itself — not lost, she understood somehow, but *arrived*. The knucklebone that had whispered her grandmother's recipes and scoldings and, once, unbearably, a lullaby — that one she had buried beneath the workshop's foundation stone, at the grandmother's own instruction, delivered through the last clear hour the bone ever gave her: *"Put me in the walls, girl. Let me hold up the roof for once instead of talking under it."*

So Paloma lived under her grandmother's voice now, literally, and slept better than she had in thirty years.

* * *

Seren came at midday, as he always did, carrying bread and pretending he hadn't walked past the window three times to check on them.

He filled the doorframe the way he filled every doorframe — too much man, too much history — and Paloma watched him take in the room the way he always did too: the students bent over their benches, the bones on their hooks, Wren at the corner bench with tongue between teeth, working a curl of antler into something small and secret.

"They're making noise," Seren observed quietly, coming to stand beside her at the door. Out in the workshop, Maren was laughing at something Doss had said, and Doss was laughing too, a rusty sound like a winch turning for the first time in years. "Inquisitor in me keeps waiting for someone to report it."

"The inquisitor in you can retire. He's earned it. He took a spear through the shoulder earning it."

"He did." Seren rolled that shoulder experimentally, as he did perhaps forty times a day, and found it still worked, and still seemed faintly surprised. "Council sent word, by the way. The harbor quarter's rebuilding levy passed. They want your workshop exempted from it. Officially. As a — " he grimaced around the word — "*honor.*"

"What did you tell them?"

"That I'd pass it along. And that you'd say yes and hate every minute of it."

Paloma snorted. "I'll say yes. I'll hate every minute of it. Put it on the wall next to the knives — *Paloma Zhou, honored citizen.* My mother would laugh herself sick."

"She might," Seren said, and nodded toward the hooks, gently. "If any of them wanted to."

"No." But she smiled when she said it. "They're resting. That's better than laughing."

He lingered. She knew the lingering. Eight months of it now, since Brackwater's gates, since the blood on the stones and Wren torn from her arms and the flooded dark beneath the war camp — eight months of Seren Oduya showing up with bread and fixing her shutters and asking for nothing, asking for nothing so carefully and so constantly that it had become its own kind of speech.

"Seren."

"Mm?"

"Come to supper tonight. All three of us. Like people."

Something moved through his face — hope trying to get past caution at a narrow gate. "Like people," he repeated.

"Or don't," Paloma said, "and keep walking past my window until you wear a trench in the cobbles, and I'll charge the council for the repairs."

"Supper," he said quickly. "Tonight. I'll bring fish. I can still gut a fish, whatever else I've forgotten how to be."

He left, and Wren, without looking up from the antler, said, "Finally," with the devastating precision of children, and Paloma threw a scrap of whalebone at the child's head, and Wren caught it without looking, grinning.

* * *

That evening, after the students had gone and the lamps were lit, Wren brought the finished carving to her.

It was small — a bird, half-formed, wings folded, carved from the antler tine Paloma had thrown. The cuts were uneven. The proportions were wrong in three places. It was, objectively, the worst piece of work ever produced in that workshop, and Paloma turned it in the lamplight and felt her throat close.

"It's not done," Wren said. "The wings. I can't get them right. Every time I cut them they go wrong."

"They're not wrong. They're folded. It's a bird that's landed."

Wren considered this with the fierce seriousness the child brought to all matters of craft. "Is that allowed? Landing?"

"Wren." Paloma pulled the child onto the bench beside her, into the circle of lamplight. In the light she could see the last traces of the marshes on Wren — the thin wrists filling out, the mute years fading like a scar. "Everything is allowed now. That's what we bought. That's what it cost. Everything is allowed, and most of it is boring, and the boring part is the good part. Supper. Sleep. Carvings that land. Trust me — I spent five years running from boring, and I'd trade every hour of it for one of these."

Wren leaned against her shoulder, bone-thin still but solid, real, warm. Outside, the tide was going out over the flats, and the harbor bells rang the evening hour, and somewhere down the street Seren was probably pacing past the window with a fish.

"Tell me the story again," Wren said. "The whole one. From the door."

"You were there."

"I was there for some of it. Tell me the parts I slept through. Tell me the marsh part. Tell me about the dead soldiers in the mire."

So Paloma told it — the marsh road and the mire-bones murmuring of the war below the world; Brackwater and the relic-dealer's terrible kindness; the captain's price and her grandmother's voice paid out like coin; the ford, and the word Wren had spoken that pulled three men under, and the weeks after when the child hadn't spoken at all; the sunken temple and the priestesses' cold luminous welcome; the storm she had walked into alone, and the dead begging her back, and Wren crying her name in the First Tongue across half a mile of rain. She told the parts she never told the students: Voss's face in the flooded dark, offering her the world; her own face reflected in his hunger, and how nearly she had said yes; the Name rising through her and the child together like water finding its level; the god standing up out of the sea with the whole horizon in its shadow; Voss going under with every silenced voice pouring into him at once, and how even that had not felt like victory, only like weather.

"And then?" Wren always asked. "Then what happened?"

"Then the god looked at me. And I looked at it. And we made a bargain, you and I and it — that it would sleep, and I would age, and both of us would keep our promises." She touched her own hair, white as gull-wing now, white as the foam-line on the flats. The price. She wore it plainly. Children in the street asked her about it and she told them the truth, which they loved, because the truth was rare and strange and free. "And then it went down. And the sea closed. And the next morning there was bread, and a roof to fix, and you, stealing my good knife."

"I wasn't stealing it. I was borrowing it indefinitely."

"Naturally." Paloma set the little folded bird on the windowsill, facing the water, where the morning light would find it first. "Go wash up. He'll be here any minute with more fish than three people can eat, because that's how he apologizes for existing."

Wren slid off the bench, paused at the loft stairs, and looked back — and for a moment the lamplight caught the child's face in a way that made Paloma's chest ache, because it was the face of someone standing in a doorway deciding, again and forever, that this was home.

"Elodie," Wren said — the child's private name for her, learned from a smuggler's slip of paper months ago, never corrected, never explained, kept like the antler bird on the sill. "The dead in the walls. Your grandmother. Does she like the workshop?"

Paloma listened, the way she would listen all her life, to the settled quiet under the floorboards, in the rafters, in the salt wind against the shutters.

"She loves it," Paloma said. "Now go. Wash. The fish won't gut themselves."

Wren thundered up the stairs, and the knock came at the door — three raps, careful, hopeful, the knock of a man who had spent a lifetime breaking down doors learning, at last, to knock — and Paloma Vane-Zhou, bone-carver, teacher, white-haired keeper of a sleeping god, went to let the evening in.
