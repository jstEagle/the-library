## 1. The Last Ledger

The river knew him before he knew it.

That was how August Crane thought of it, anyway — the way the current bent a little gentler around his skiff, the way the reeds parted where his pole touched them, the way every bend of the Thornwater came back to him like an old dog answering to its name. He'd been running this stretch since he was nine years old, small enough to hide in the false bottom of a brandy crate while his mother talked her way past the tollmen. Now he was twenty-three and the tollmen were gone, bought off or frightened off, and there was no one left to talk to but the river itself.

Three crates of Vellish silk beneath his feet. One lantern, shuttered to a knife-slit of amber light. And above him, the sky over Thornfell doing that thing it had done all autumn — bruising purple at the edges, as if the whole realm were a fruit going soft somewhere no one could see.

He poled through the shallows below Miller's Bend without a sound. The water here ran shallow and clear over pale stones, and on any other night he might have paused to watch the moonfish stitching silver threads beneath the surface. Tonight he counted instead. Three hundred paces to the willow snag. Two hundred to the drowned fencepost where the customs men used to moor their skiffs, back when customs men still came this far south. One hundred to the landing where Old Tessa waited with the mule cart, if she'd bothered to come, if she hadn't decided that a guild of three souls wasn't worth the price of her loyalty anymore.

Fifty. Ten. The willows closed overhead like cupped hands, and there she was — a broad shadow in a hooded cloak, mule stamping softly at the midges, and beside her a second shadow that made August's shoulders drop half an inch with relief before he caught himself.

"You're late," Tessa said, which was her way of saying *I'm glad you're alive*.

"The ford was watching for boats." He swung ashore, boots sinking into black mud that smelled of rot and iron. "I went by the reed channel."

"Reed channel's silted."

"It wasn't two weeks ago."

Tessa's silence said everything about what two weeks could do to a place like Thornfell these days. The younger shadow stepped forward and took the first crate without being asked — Wren, sixteen years old, all elbows and earnestness, the last apprentice the guild had taken before the taking stopped. Her hands were steady. That was the only good thing August noticed all night.

They loaded the cart in silence, working by touch and habit, and August stood a moment with the water lapping his ankles and let himself feel the shape of what they were doing. Silk from Vellmark, three weeks' profit, maybe four hundred crowns if the buyer in Harrowgate didn't haggle. Four hundred crowns against a ledger that owed eleven thousand. It was like bailing the sea with a thimble, and he was tired — bone-tired, soul-tired, tired in a way sleep didn't reach — but his hands did not shake as he tied down the last rope, and that was something. That was the thing the Cranes were known for, once. Steady hands.

*The Cranes always land on their feet,* his mother used to say, laughing, her hair wet from some crossing or another. *It's not luck, love. It's just refusing to fall.*

He climbed onto the cart and did not look back at the river. Looking back at rivers was how you got caught.

* * *

The road to Harrowgate ran through country that had gone strange in the way old things go strange — not ruined, exactly, but worn thin, like cloth rubbed at the elbows. August saw it everywhere now that he knew to look. Hedgerows blooming out of season and then dropping their flowers overnight, brown before they ever opened properly. Birds flying in patterns that unraveled halfway across a field, the flock forgetting itself mid-turn and scattering. A chapel outside Dunmore whose bell rang at noon even though the bell had been stolen years ago; the villagers had stopped trying to explain it and simply set out bread beneath the empty belfry, an offering to whatever remembered being a bell.

Wren watched all of it with wide eyes and finally, an hour out, couldn't hold it anymore. "Is it true what they're saying? About the mountains?"

"What are they saying?"

"That the passes have moved. That a shepherd followed the same trail he's walked forty years and came out in a valley he'd never seen, with a river in it that flowed backward." She hugged the crate on her knees a little tighter. "And that the Queen means to do something about it. Something big."

August kept his eyes on the road. "Queens always mean to do something big. It's cheaper than feeding people."

"But the covenant—"

"The covenant is a story grandmothers tell," he said, and heard the lie in his own voice even as he said it. Everyone told it. The covenant bound Thornfell to the fae courts beyond the mountains, an exchange older than any crown — the warmth of human hearths flowing one way, the wild green abundance of fae land flowing the other, and the Sigil Road between them like an artery neither side could survive cutting. Every child learned the rhyme. *Seed for starlight, hearth for bloom, keep the road or lose the room.* It was nursery stuff. It was also, apparently, the reason hedges bloomed wrong and rivers forgot their courses and the sky over Thornfell looked like a bruise.

His mother had believed in it properly. His father had laughed at it gently. Both of them dead twelve years now, drowned in a crossing gone bad, and August had never decided which of them he wanted to be.

Tessa spoke up from the driver's bench, not turning around. "There's soldiers on the Harrowgate road. Two days ago. Asking after cargo."

"Asking politely?"

"Asking with a ledger of their own." She spat over the mule's flank. "Queen's men don't bother with polite much longer. They've got a new tax master in Harrowgate and he counts everything twice."

"Then we don't take the Harrowgate road," August said. "We take the drover's cut."

"The drover's cut is bandit country."

"The drover's cut is *my* country." He heard the edge in his own voice and didn't soften it. Pride was the only coin the Cranes had left in quantity, and he spent it recklessly because the alternative was counting what little real money they had. "Nobody knows those switchbacks but us. Nobody's robbed a Crane on the drover's cut in sixty years."

"And nobody's been a Crane in sixty years either," Tessa said quietly, "except three fools and a ghost."

The cart rolled on through the thinning dark. Wren looked at August, and August looked at the road, and nobody said anything else until the sun came up gray as dishwater and they made the turn into the hills.

* * *

The buyer in the high valley was a wool merchant with soft hands and hard eyes, and he did haggle, and August held the line at three-sixty and hated himself for the eighty crowns he lost doing it. But the money went into a wax-sealed pouch, and the pouch went inside his shirt, against his skin, where the cold of the mountain air couldn't get at it, and by the time they started home he had almost convinced himself that three hundred and sixty was a victory.

Almost. Numbers were honest in a way pride wasn't. Eleven thousand owed. Three hundred and sixty earned. At this rate the debt would be paid sometime after the sun burned out, and the creditors who had already taken the guild's wagons, its warehouse lease, its southern routes, its good name in four separate markets — they would not be waiting on the sun.

They camped that night in a hollow below the drover's road, a fire no bigger than two cupped hands, and August took out the ledger.

He didn't need to. He knew every line of it. But some part of the ritual required the physical weight of it, the cracked leather cover his grandfather had stitched, the pages soft as cloth from a century of thumbs. Debts, all of them, written in his grandmother's neat slanted hand and continued in his uncle Bram's sprawling scrawl and finished, lately, in August's own careful script: *To the Saltmarsh factors, 2,100 crowns. To the Ferryman's Cooperative, 1,400. To Magistrate Vance's court, 900 in seized-goods fines, disputed.* On and on, page after page, a map of everything the guild had borrowed to stay alive and everything it had lost staying that way.

Wren sat close to the fire, sharpening a knife she never used. "We could start over," she said suddenly. "Somewhere else. Sell the hall, pay what we can, walk away clean-ish. Plenty of folk would take me on. Take you on. You're the best runner I've ever seen, and I'm not just saying that because you sign my wages."

"I don't sign your wages. There haven't been wages in a year."

"Then you're the best runner I've ever seen *for free*," she said, undeterred, "which makes you either very good or very stupid, and my gran raised me to believe you can be both."

August almost smiled. Almost. "The hall isn't ours to sell. It's collateral. Has been since the winter we borrowed against it to buy Mother's medicine." He turned a page he didn't need to read. "Besides. A guild isn't the building."

"No," Tessa said from the darkness beyond the fire. "A guild is the name. And the name's the only thing we've got left that nobody's taken, and I'll tell you what, boy — names can be sold too. I've seen it. I've seen better families than ours sell theirs."

"Not ours." He closed the ledger. "Ours holds."

Neither of them argued, which was worse than arguing. The fire burned down. Somewhere far off, too far off to be natural, a bird sang three notes and stopped, as though it had forgotten the rest, and August lay awake a long time listening for whether it would remember.

* * *

They came down out of the hills on the third day, and from the ridge above the river town August saw the smoke first — thin, domestic chimney smoke, nothing alarming — and then the roofs, and then the crooked slate crown of the guildhall rising above the warehouses along the quay, and something in his chest loosened that had been clenched for six days.

Home. Such as it was. A three-story timber building leaning companionably against its neighbors, a painted crane in flight over the door flaking to bare wood, a cellar full of caches and a garret full of memories and, between them, two rooms where three people ate and slept and pretended the world hadn't shrunk to fit them.

He left Tessa and Wren with the mule at the livery and walked down through the streets alone, the way he always did, wanting the last hundred yards to himself. The town smelled of fish and tar and woodsmoke, ordinary smells, good smells, and he let them settle over him like a coat.

The trouble showed at the door.

The guild's door had a lock worth more than most doors — his grandfather's one indulgence, dwarven-made, pick-proof, axe-proof. It hung open. Not broken. *Open*, swinging lazily on its hinges in the river wind, and through it August could see lamplight moving in the front room where lamplight had no business moving at this hour.

He didn't call out. Twenty-three years of smuggling lived in his bones, and his bones said *quiet*, and he went quiet, up the outer stair to the loading gallery, through the window he'd jimmied since he was seven, down the inside stair with each tread memorized for silence.

Two men in the front room. Not thieves — thieves didn't work so slowly, or so lawfully. They had an inventory sheet between them and were checking items off against the room itself: the brass weighing scales, gone. The chart cabinet, open and emptied of its coastwise charts. The mounted crane's-wing fan his mother had won in a card game, wrapped in sacking and leaning against the wall with a dozen other pieces of the family's life. One of the men wore the gray-and-gold sash of a Queen's assessor. The other wore no colors at all, which meant he was the dangerous one.

"Gentlemen," August said from the doorway, and both of them jumped, which restored a measurable fraction of his soul.

The assessor recovered fastest. He was young, pink, sweating, and visibly relieved to have someone to talk to besides his silent partner. "Master Crane? August Crane? Good. Good. We've been expecting—" He consulted a paper. "Ah. The guild's registered principal, yes. I'm Assessor Fenwick, acting under warrant of the Crown debt office. We're executing recovery against outstanding obligations. You'll find everything itemized."

"I can see that." August's voice came out level. Steady hands. He crossed the room, lifted the sacking off the crane's-wing fan, and unwrapped it with exaggerated care while both men watched. "This is a keepsake. Family effect. It's not listed as guild asset."

"Everything within the pledged premises is subject to recovery, Master Crane."

"This building was pledged. Not its contents."

"Premises and contents, per clause nine." Fenwick actually sounded sorry about it, which was somehow more insulting than gloating. Behind him the silent man watched August with the flat professional patience of someone who had evicted many families and expected no trouble worth remembering.

August looked around the room. Really looked. The hooks on the walls where tools should have hung — bare. The corner where the strongbox should have sat — a rectangle of cleaner floorboards. Through the interior arch he could see the great room beyond, the one with the long table where a hundred Cranes had once eaten together, and it was half-dark and half-empty, chairs missing, the guild banner gone from above the hearth. Taken already, then. Days ago. While he was on the river earning three hundred and sixty crowns against the sea.

Something cold moved through him, slower than anger, colder than fear. He set the fan down on the table, gently, and took out the wax-sealed pouch of payment money and set it beside the fan.

"There's three hundred and sixty crowns," he said. "Put it against the oldest debt."

Fenwick blinked. "That's — Master Crane, that's a gesture, but the arrears total—"

"I know what they total. Put it against the oldest anyway." He wrote the figure in the ledger, right there in front of them, in his careful script, and initialed it, and watched Fenwick's silent partner copy the entry with a pencil stub. Then, because pride was the last coin and he intended to spend it: "You'll note the Cranes pay what they owe. Item by item. However long it takes."

"Of course," Fenwick murmured, and gathered his papers, and the two of them withdrew with the fan and the scales and whatever else fit on their handcart, leaving the door swinging behind them.

* * *

Tessa and Wren found him an hour later sitting at the long table in the great room, the ledger open in front of him, a single candle burning. They read his face and didn't ask, which was its own kind of mercy. Wren lit lamps. Tessa put food in front of him — bread, hard cheese, pickled things from the cellar cache that the assessors, thank every god, had never found. They ate in the half-emptied room like survivors of a flood, salvaging a meal from the wreckage.

"They took the banner," Wren said at last. Her voice was very small. "My grandmother wove that banner."

"I know."

"August." She turned her knife over and over on the tabletop. "What happens when there's nothing left to take?"

He looked down the length of the table. Sixteen chairs once. Seven now. Three people. One candle. Outside, the river went on talking to itself in the dark, patient as arithmetic, and somewhere upstream the bruise-colored sky pressed down on mountains that had perhaps, this week, moved.

"We pay," he said. "Item by item. However long it—"

The knock came at the street door.

All three of them went still. No one knocked on the Crane door at night, not in years — the people who needed them knew the signals, whistles and lantern-codes, and the people who didn't need them had stopped coming long ago. This knock was heavy, arrhythmic, the knocking of a man barely standing.

Tessa's hand went under the table to the boat-hook she kept there. Wren snuffed two of the three candles. August crossed the room, put his eye to the seam of the shutters, and looked out.

A man stood in the street, swaying like a mast in weather. Fine clothes, ruined — mud to the knees, blood down one side, a saddle-leather satchel clutched to his chest the way a drowning man clutches driftwood. Behind him, far up the ridge road, a string of lights was coming down through the trees. Lanterns. Many lanterns, moving fast, in formation.

The man lifted his head. Even across the street, even in the dark, August saw that his face was gray as ash and that he was looking directly at the guildhall — at the faded crane over the door — with an expression August had seen once before, on his father, the night he came home knowing he'd lost the *Merryweather* and half his crew.

The man mouthed something. No sound reached across the street, but August read it as easily as print, as easily as a ledger line:

*Crane. Please.*

Every sensible instinct he owned said the same thing at once: *shutters closed, candle out, let the river decide, you owe him nothing, you have nothing left to give.* The guild was three people and a ledger full of ghosts. Taking in a bleeding stranger hunted by a column of lanterns was how guilds died entirely instead of slowly.

August opened the door.

"Inside," he said. "Quickly. And whatever's in that bag — you'd better hope it's worth what it's going to cost us."
