## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The river was low that autumn, the way it always was after a dry summer in Thornfell, and August Crane knew every stone of the ford the way other men knew their mothers' faces.

He had crossed it first at six years old, riding his father's shoulders with a sack of untaxed wool bundled against his chest, and he had crossed it last night with a sack of something considerably more dangerous — salted eel-brandy and three bolts of Vess-blue silk that had never seen a customs house. The run had gone clean. No tollmen, no dogs, no lanterns swinging along the ridge road. Just him, the water talking over its stones, and the long gray walk home to a guildhall that was mostly empty now and getting emptier.

He was thinking about the ledger when he heard the man fall.

Not saw — heard. There is a particular sound a body makes when it gives up standing, a wet collapse of knees and breath, and August had heard it enough times in enough alleys to know it from the sound of a drunk lying down on purpose. He stopped mid-ford, boots planted on the slick crown of a stone, and listened.

The ford lay in a fold of country where the Thorn narrowed between two wooded banks, willows trailing their fingers in the shallows, alders crowding dark and close behind them. Evening was coming down in that slow violet way it did in the borderlands, the sky bruising above the western hills. Mist was already rising off the water in threads. A good place for smuggling. A good place, August thought grimly, for dying.

"Hello?" His voice came out flat, professional. He did not draw attention to himself if he could help it; that was the first rule of the Cranes, older than any written rule. *Be smoke.* But whoever was in the alders on the far bank had already made noise enough for both of them.

No answer. Only a ragged breathing, like a bellows with a split seam.

August stood there a moment longer than he should have, weighing it. The sensible thing — the Crane thing — was to turn around, take the high path home, and let whatever was happening on the far bank happen without him. Three members left in the guild. One ledger full of debts. No room in those numbers for stray wounded men in the dark.

He waded toward the sound anyway, hating himself a little for it, and hated more that the hating didn't change his feet.

* * *

The man had crawled as far as the water's edge and no farther. He lay half in the shallows, one arm stretched out across a flat stone as though he'd been reaching for the river itself, and the mist moved around him in slow coils. He was dressed for travel — dusty riding coat, mud to the knee, a courier's satchel still strapped across his chest with the strap twisted tight, as if someone had tried to tear it from him and failed.

He was also dressed in blood. It had soaked through the back of his coat in a wide dark bloom, and where the water touched him it thinned to pink and drifted downstream in delicate ribbons, unraveling.

August crouched beside him. "Hey. Hey. Can you hear me?"

The man's eyes opened. They were gray, shockingly clear in a face gone the color of tallow, and they fixed on August with an intensity that made the hair rise along his arms. Not the look of a man seeing what was in front of him. The look of a man checking.

"You're not them," the stranger rasped. "You're not one of theirs."

"I'm nobody's," August said. "Who cut you?"

A sound came out of the man that might have been laughter, if laughter could be ground through gravel. "Does it matter? They'll tell you soon enough." His hand moved — slow, trembling, deliberate — to the satchel's buckle. "Listen to me. I don't have time for the part where you say you want nothing to do with this. Nobody ever does. And then it has you anyway."

"I really don't—"

"The road remembers."

August went still.

It wasn't the words themselves, though they were strange enough. It was the way the man said them — not like a riddle, not like delirium, but like a password. Like something he had carried a long way and repeated to himself through all the miles and all the pain to keep it whole, and now that he'd finally said it aloud, some knot inside him loosened and the rest of him started coming apart.

His fingers found the buckle. Worked it. Drew out a scroll wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax the color of dried pomegranates, pressed with a sigil August didn't recognize: a circle broken by a road, a road that passed clean through the break and kept going.

"Take it." The stranger pushed it against August's chest with the last of his strength, and August, off-balance, took it. The wax was warm. That was the detail that would come back to him later, in worse hours — the seal was warm, like something alive underneath. "West. Follow it west. Don't let them have it. Don't let anyone have it. It isn't a map, boy, it's a—" The breath caught. Held. "It's a countdown."

"A countdown to what?"

But the man's eyes had gone past him, up the slope, into the dark under the alders, and what filled them now was not pain. It was fear, pure and cold, the fear of a man who has run out of distance.

"They're here," he whispered. "Go. Go west. Whatever they offer you, whatever they promise — the guilds are sold, all of them, every banner in Thornfell is bought and paid for, and the only thing left that isn't—" His hand found August's wrist, grip astonishingly strong, nails biting through his sleeve. "The road remembers. Tell it I kept faith. Tell it Edmun Vance kept faith to the end."

Then the light in his face guttered like a lamp out of oil, and his hand slid from August's wrist into the water, and the river took up the small work of carrying him away piece by piece while August knelt in the shallows holding a warm sealed scroll and understanding, with the slow horrible clarity of things that cannot be taken back, that he had just been chosen for something.

"Damn it," August said quietly. "Damn it, damn it, damn it."

* * *

He did the practical things first, because practical things were the only kind that kept a man from screaming or running. He closed the stranger's eyes. He checked the satchel — empty now but for the scroll, though the lining had been slit and searched by someone in a hurry who hadn't thought to check the oilcloth against the man's skin. He looked for a ring, a token, anything to send to kin, and found nothing except a worn leather cord around the man's neck with a small wooden bead on it, carved with the same broken-circle sigil as the wax.

Edmun Vance. August said the name once, silently, the way smugglers said the names of the dead — a debt entered, even if he couldn't yet pay it.

And then the light crested the ridge.

Lanterns. Six of them, maybe seven, strung out along the high road above the eastern bank, bobbing as their bearers came down through the trees at a pace that was fast but not frantic. That was worse than frantic. Frantic meant losing the trail. This was the pace of men who knew exactly where their quarry had gone, because quarry that bleeds leaves a trail a child could follow, and because they had done this before, many times, and were very good at it.

August sank down among the reeds at the water's edge, scroll pressed flat against his belly, and watched.

They came into the firelight-spill of their own lanterns one by one, and August's blood went cold in a way the evening chill couldn't account for. They wore no livery. That was the first wrong thing. Every lawful company in Thornfell wore its colors — tollmen in the Queen's blue-and-silver, wardens in forest green, even the free companies wore badges of one grubby sort or another. These men wore dun and gray, unmarked, and moved with a drilled economy that no mob of bandits ever managed. Two fanned out along the bank without being told. One knelt by the body. One held a lantern low over the water, reading the current the way August read it — the way a Crane read it.

The sixth man stood apart, hood back, and turned slowly in place, looking at the trees, the ford, the far bank, the dark.

"We're too late," one of the searchers called softly. "Dead. Cold already, near enough."

"And the cargo?"

"Gone. Satchel's empty. Slit lining — he hid it on himself somewhere, or gave it away."

The standing man said nothing for a long moment. Then he walked down to the water's edge, and August, flattened among the reeds with his heart hammering against the scroll like a second heart, watched him crouch and study the mud. Study the stones. Study, for one endless suspended moment, the exact stretch of shallow water where August had knelt.

"He met someone," the man said. His voice was quiet, pleasant, almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened August more than shouting would have. "Someone crossing west. See how the silt's disturbed — two sets of prints going into the water, one set coming out." He rose. He did not seem angry. He seemed interested, the way a scholar is interested. "A ferryman, perhaps. Or a friend."

"There's no village west for six miles. Nothing but the old guild roads."

"Mm." The man drew off one glove, finger by finger, and looked at his bare hand as if it belonged to someone else. "Then our friend died well after all. He found someone foolish enough to take it." He pulled the glove back on. "Burn nothing. Leave him for the crows — let it be seen. Fear travels faster than we do." And then, turning toward the dark western hills, in a voice pitched not for his men but for the night itself, carrying across the water with terrible clarity:

"Whoever you are, little courier-of-nothing — you're carrying something that eats its keepers. Bring it home. We'll be along."

The lanterns swung back up the ridge, unhurried, and the dark folded over them, and August stayed in the reeds until his legs went numb and the moon rose fat and hazed over the alders, and only then did he move.

* * *

He crossed the ford again, westward this time, because east was where the lanterns had gone and because his feet, traitorous honest feet, had apparently decided the question for him. On the far bank he climbed to one of the old guild caches — a hollow beneath a fallen oak, dry-stone lined, stocked by his grandfather and restocked by his uncle Bram with the grim thoroughness of a man who expected the world to keep getting worse. Blanket. Flint. A twist of dried venison hard enough to chip teeth. He built no fire. He sat wrapped in the blanket with his back against stone, and unrolled the oilcloth by moonlight.

The scroll inside was heavy paper, fine as vellum, covered in ink so dark it seemed wet. It showed Thornfell — the borders, the rivers, the mountain wall in the west drawn in a style older than any map August had ever stolen or sold. Roads spidered across it in silver-gray lines, and one road above all the others ran straight as a struck bell from the Heartland to the mountains, marked at intervals with tiny sigils, and labeled in a script that shifted when he wasn't looking directly at it.

He looked away. Looked back. The label had changed. Where before it had read something like *the Sigil Road*, it now read *the Sigil Road — 214 miles remain*, and the number sat on the page glowing faintly, faintly dimming even as he watched, the way embers dim.

"Not a map," August murmured. "A countdown." To what, the dead man hadn't said. But 214 was fewer than the miles between this ford and the mountains, and the number was falling, and somewhere behind him seven lanterns were coming west at a walking pace because they already knew where he was going.

He thought about the guildhall then. He couldn't help it. He thought about the great room with half the furniture gone, the creditor's chalk-marks on the doorposts like tally-scars, old Marta asleep by a fire built small to save coal, and Uncle Bram upstairs doing sums that never came out ahead. He thought about the Crane name — his father's name, his grandfather's name — worn thin as an old coin, rubbed nearly blank, but still *his*, still the one thing he had ever been given that he hadn't had to steal.

He could go home. Put the scroll in the deepest cache, or better, drop it in the deepest pool of the Thorn and let the river argue with whoever came asking. Go home, run one more route, pay one more debt, keep the ledger balanced another season. Be smoke. Be nobody's. Let the road remember someone else.

The trouble was the man's hand on his wrist. The trouble was *tell it Edmun Vance kept faith*. The trouble was that August Crane, for all his grandfather's rules, had never once in his life managed to walk past a person bleeding in the dark, and everyone who had ever known him knew it, and it was the single worst flaw a smuggler could have, and tonight it had finally found him.

He looked at the map. The number read 213.

"Oh, shut up," he told it.

He rolled the scroll, wrapped the oilcloth, and tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it — warm, faintly, absurdly warm, like a sleeping animal. Then he stood, shook out the blanket, and turned his face west, toward the black line of the mountains and the road that remembered.

Behind him, far back along the eastern ridge, a lantern flickered among the trees. Unhurried. Patient.

August walked, and hated that he wouldn't turn around, and went anyway.
