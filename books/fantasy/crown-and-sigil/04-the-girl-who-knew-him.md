## 4. The Girl Who Knew Him

Isolde Vance had been following the smuggler for two days, and in that time she had learned three things about him.

First: he was careful. He never slept twice in the same hollow, never crossed open ground without a reason, and when he drank from streams he did it downstream of his own boots, as if even the water might be bribed to betray him. Second: he was alone, which was either a sign of great confidence or great desperation, and she had not yet decided which. Third — and this was the thing that kept her awake at night beneath the whispering pines of the Thornfell borderlands — he carried her father's seal.

She had seen it only once, glimpsed through a gap in his coat as he crouched by a fire he was too clever to light properly: the wax-stub of a broken sigil, deep violet, stamped with the Vance mark — a bridge over running water, crowned with three stars. Her father had worn that seal on a chain around his neck for thirty years. Magistrates were buried with their seals; it was law and it was custom and it was, above all, *right*, because a magistrate's word outlived him and the seal was the shape his word took in the world.

Her father had been buried without his.

Isolde knew because she had stood at the graveside in the rain, seventeen years old and shaking, while the undertaker's men lowered a coffin that weighed too little, and her mother's hand on her shoulder had felt less like comfort than like restraint. The coffin should have held her father. It held something else — she had never learned what — and the seal had gone into the ground with whatever lie they'd buried instead, or so everyone believed.

And now it was riding north along a dead man's road in the pocket of a stranger who smelled of river mud and pipe smoke and moved like someone who had spent his whole life being hunted by arithmetic.

*Two days,* she thought, watching from the ridge as he picked his way down toward the ruined mill at Gallowmere's edge. *Two days, and I still don't know your name.*

The autumn light came slantwise through the trees, gilding the fern-bracken and turning the millpond below into a sheet of hammered copper. She had chosen this place last night, walking its bounds in the dark the way her father had taught her to walk a room before an interrogation — noting sightlines, exits, the rotted stair that would take no weight, the old sluice-gate where the millrace narrowed to a throat of stone. He would come through the mill. There was no other way north that didn't add a day, and everything about his pace said he was counting days.

She checked her knife. Checked it again. Her hands wanted to shake and she would not let them.

*You are your father's daughter,* she told herself. *Act like it.*

Then she waited, cold and patient as a stone, and let the forest hold its breath around her.

* * *

He came into the mill just past midday, and he was better than she'd expected.

That was the trouble with watching a man for two days — you started to know his rhythms, and knowing them made you fond of them, and being fond of a target was how magistrates' daughters got themselves killed. August Crane — she'd heard the name whispered at the ford-side inn where the pursuers with lanterns had stopped to drink, hunters asking their soft terrible questions — August Crane entered the ruin the way water enters a cracked jug: finding every gap first, testing every floorboard with his toe before trusting it, his eyes moving in that constant restless sweep she had come to think of as the smuggler's rosary. Left. Right. Up. Behind.

Behind was where she was.

She stepped out of the shadow of the collapsed grain-chute with her knife drawn and her voice pitched low and flat, the way her father had taught her — *never shout, Isolde; shouting is what people do when they've lost control of the room* — and said:

"Don't."

He didn't. That was the first thing in his favor. A lot of men would have spun, lunged, done something brave and stupid. August Crane went very still instead, the way a deer goes still, and let his eyes find her without moving his head, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she'd watched him do at the stream crossings: exits, distances, odds.

"Knife's real," she said. "So is the crossbow bolt I'll put in your back if you run. I don't want to hurt you. I want to talk."

"Talk." His voice was rougher than she'd imagined, sanded down by sleeplessness. "People who want to talk usually wait until a man's inside before they say so."

"People who want to rob you wait outside. People who want to kill you don't announce it." She kept the knife low, unthreatening, honest. "I've been behind you since the ford. If I wanted you dead, you'd have died in your sleep on the first night, and I'd have taken what I came for and been home by now."

Something flickered across his face at *the ford* — a muscle jumping near his jaw, grief or guilt or both wearing each other's clothes. "The ford," he repeated. "You saw that."

"I see most things. It's a family affliction." She took one step closer, deliberately slow, letting him watch her do it. "My name is Isolde Vance. My father was Corwin Vance, magistrate of the Thornfell border marches. And you're carrying his seal."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere up in the broken rafters, a jackdaw shifted and resettled, and the sound of it was enormous.

"That's not possible," August said finally.

"No," Isolde agreed. "It isn't. And yet."

"I found a dying man at the ford. He pressed a map into my hands and told me the road remembers, and then he died, and there wasn't time to ask him anything, least of all whose seal—" He stopped. She watched him stop himself, watched the smuggler's instinct slam shut like a ledger. "Why does it matter to you?"

"Because that seal was buried with my father eight months ago." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Or it was supposed to be. So either the man at the ford robbed a grave — in which case I'll take it back and we can part as strangers — or my father isn't where we buried him, and the man who died holding his seal was carrying something my father gave him with his own hands. And if that's true, then my father was alive after his funeral, doing something secret enough to get other people killed, and I have spent eight months believing I was alone in the world when maybe—"

She cut herself off. Shouting, her father's ghost murmured. Shouting is what people do when they've lost the room.

August Crane looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, keeping his movements exaggerated and visible the way you'd move around a frightened animal — she realized with a jolt that he was treating *her* as the dangerous one — he reached into his coat and drew out the map.

* * *

She had expected paper. What he unwrapped from its oilcloth was something else entirely.

It was leather, or something like leather, tanned thin and supple, and across its surface ran roads rendered in lines of silver that seemed to sit slightly *above* the material rather than upon it — ink raised into relief, or perhaps grown. Mountains marched along its western edge in fine engraved ridges. Rivers glittered. And there, at the map's lower corner, pressed into a seal of deep violet wax that had been broken and resealed with care, sat the Vance mark: the bridge, the water, the three stars.

Isolde's knees nearly went.

She had prepared herself for this moment — she had rehearsed it, actually, standing watches in the dark, telling herself exactly what she would feel — and she had prepared for anger, for suspicion, for vindication. She had not prepared for the sheer physical force of grief arriving on schedule after eight months of pretending otherwise. The mill tilted. The copper light off the pond came through the broken wall and lay across the map, and the seal gleamed like a small drowned star, and her father's hands — big, ink-stained, always warm even in winter — rose up in her memory so vividly she could almost smell the lamp-oil of his study.

"May I?" she managed.

August hesitated, then passed it over. Their fingers brushed on the transfer and neither of them acknowledged it.

Up close, the map was worse. Or better. She couldn't decide. The silver lines weren't static — she was almost sure of it now, sure in the way you're sure of a thing you'll spend years failing to prove — the roads *shifted* when she wasn't looking directly at them, the way stars shift at the edge of vision. One thread of road near the mountains seemed fainter than the others, frayed, like a hem coming undone.

"This is the Sigil Road," she said quietly. "Or a map of it. Nobody maps the Sigil Road. It's not a road, it's a — a covenant. A boundary. The old treaties say it runs between here and the fae court beyond the passes, and that it's maintained by..." She trailed off, frowning. "By the crown. By blood-right. Everyone says that."

"Everyone says a lot of things." August's voice had gone strange. "The map moves. I've watched it. Roads redraw themselves. Whole stretches go dark and then come back wrong. And every time it changes, I get this feeling in my teeth, like a storm coming, except the storm is—" He gestured vaguely at everything. "Farther away than weather."

Isolde turned the map toward the light. And there — she hadn't seen it before, wouldn't have seen it without knowing to look — along the western marches, in handwriting she knew better than her own, small annotations crawled between the silver lines. Her father's script. Tight, precise, the letters leaning forward like a man hurrying.

*"Third crossing failed — ward dark."*
*"Vess's surveyors at the pass. They are measuring the fraying."*
*"If I do not return, trust the road, not the crown."*

The mill swam again. She sat down on a fallen beam without meaning to, the map clutched in both hands like something rescued from a fire.

"He was investigating her," she heard herself say. "Queen Vess. He'd been writing to friends in the capital for a year — coded letters, I thought it was politics, I thought it was *boring* — and then in the spring he rode north and didn't come back, and the crown sent us a sealed letter saying he'd died of fever at a waystation, and there was no body, and when I asked questions the new magistrate — Vess's creature, appointed within the month — told me that grief made girls imaginative." Her laugh came out cracked. "Imaginative. As if I invented him. As if I invented any of this."

August crouched down across from her, slowly, until they were nearly level. Up close she could see how tired he was — shadows under his eyes like bruises, a healing cut along his hairline from the ford, hands that belonged to a working man and eyes that belonged to a worried one.

"The man at the ford," he said. "Describe him."

"Tall. Grey in his beard, but strong — my father swam every morning, he'd have kept it. A scar through his left eyebrow, from a hunting accident when I was small. He'd have been dressed plainly, traveling plain, because that's how he did his real work." She swallowed. "Did he say anything? Before he died?"

"'The road remembers.'" August's jaw worked. "And he said — he looked at me like he was choosing me, or cursing me, I still don't know which — and he said, 'Tell my girl the ledger balances.' I thought it was delirium. Smugglers' talk. I thought he meant money."

Isolde stared at him.

*The ledger balances.* Her father had kept a ledger — an actual one, locked in his study drawer, and she had found it burned in the hearth the week after the funeral, only half-consumed, pages of cipher she'd spent months failing to break. *Tell my girl.* Not *my daughter*, not a name. *My girl.* The way he'd called her across the market square when she was nine. The way he'd woken her before dawn to watch the comet, saying *come on, my girl, the sky won't wait.*

"It was him," she said. Her voice sounded very far away. "It was my father at the ford, and he died in front of you, and you didn't know, and I've been stalking you for two days ready to gut you over a piece of wax." She laughed again, and this time it turned into something else halfway through, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the mill stopped spinning. "I'm sorry. I'm — give me a moment. Magistrates' daughters aren't supposed to cry in front of witnesses."

"For what it's worth," August said quietly, "I've been waiting for someone to come take that map off me since the day I got it. I half hoped you'd gut me. It'd be simpler."

She lowered her hand and looked at him, really looked, and saw it there: the reluctance she recognized from her own mirror. The look of a person carrying something they never asked for, who has already tried to put it down and found it stuck to their hands.

"You could walk away," she said. "Right now. Give me the map, walk south, go back to whatever guild raised you. No one would blame you. I barely blame myself, and I have excellent reasons."

"Could you walk away?" he asked. "If it were reversed?"

She thought of the burned ledger. Of the coffin that weighed too little. Of the letters she'd written to a father who was already dead, addressed to a waystation that had probably never existed, returned unopened by a crown that had already decided what the truth would be.

"No," she said.

"There it is." He exhaled, something between a laugh and surrender. "Crane rule one: you don't leave a debt on the road. Doesn't matter whose debt. Doesn't matter if collecting it kills you. You carry it forward." He rubbed his face. "I hate rule one. I've hated it my whole life. And I've never once managed to break it."

* * *

They talked until the copper light off the pond went grey and then blue, sitting among the ruins of the mill like two conspirators in a country that no longer existed. She told him about her father's investigation — the coded letters, the surveyors in the passes, the way the old stories had begun changing in the border villages. How the fae lights that used to dance on the far hills at midsummer had stopped appearing three years ago. How the wild roses along the covenant stones bloomed later and later each spring, and this year hadn't bloomed at all. How the old people muttered that the *long bargain* was failing, and the young people laughed at them, and the crown taxed harder and smiled wider and built itself a new palace with stone that had come from somewhere no one would name.

And August told her — haltingly, in the flat careful voice of a man reading figures aloud — about the Hollow Guild. About Cranes who had turned their coats, sold their honor to Queen Vess's coin, and were now hunting the map he carried. About lanterns cresting a ridge behind a dying man. About an uncle named Bram who had taught him every rule worth having and might, for all August knew, be one of the hunters by now.

"Hollow Cranes," Isolde said softly, when he finished. "Your own people."

"My family," he corrected, with a bitterness that had gone old and smooth, like a stone handled too long. "Which in Thornfell is apparently the same thing as your father's crown. Everything loyal gets bought. Everything old gets sold." He nodded at the map in her lap. "Except that. Whatever that is, nobody's bought it yet."

"No," Isolde agreed. "But they're trying. And they're close." She smoothed the leather flat across her knees, and the silver roads caught the last of the light, and she saw it again — that frayed thread near the mountains, thinner than it had been at noon. Thinner than it had been an hour ago. "Look. Here. The western line. It's fading."

August leaned in. His breath caught. "It was brighter this morning. I'm sure it was. I'm sure—"

"So am I." She traced the fraying road with one fingertip, not quite touching the silver, the way you'd touch a wound. "My father wrote that Vess's surveyors were measuring the fraying. Measuring implies change. Change implies a clock." She looked up and met his eyes, and saw her own fear reflected there, and hated how much steadier it made her feel to see it shared. "This map isn't just showing us the road, August Crane. It's showing us how much road is left. Whatever your dying stranger handed you at that ford — it isn't a treasure. It's a countdown."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Out in the gathering dusk, the millpond went dark as poured ink, and somewhere far off — so far it might have been imagination, might have been memory, might have been the road itself remembering — a bell tolled once from beyond the mountains, in a register no human throat could make.

August reached out and closed his hand gently over the map, over her father's seal, over the fraying silver thread.

"Then we'd better move," he said. "There's a cache I know, half a day east. Smuggler's hole, dry, defensible. We can read the map properly there — all of it, everything your father wrote. And Isolde—" He paused, and she watched him choose the next words with the care of a man stepping onto ice. "The hunters behind me. They're good. Better than me. If they pick up our trail..."

"Then they'll find two of us instead of one," she said, rising, sliding her knife home, feeling — for the first time in eight months — like a Vance instead of a fugitive. "My father spent his life learning what the crown was hiding. I intend to finish the job. Besides." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile, sharp as her knife. "You've been watching your back for two days straight. You look exhausted. Consider me your relief."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh, shouldered his pack, and led the way out through the mill's broken wall into the blue dark — and neither of them saw, in the deepening shadow under the far arch of the sluice-gate, the glint of a lantern being carefully shuttered, nor heard the soft signal-whistle, three notes descending, that went winging back through the trees toward Gallowmere bridge.

The road remembered. And so, unfortunately, did the Hollow Guild.
