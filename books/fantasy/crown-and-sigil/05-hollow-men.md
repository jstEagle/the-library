## 5. Hollow Men

Gallowmere bridge announced itself long before we saw it — by smell, if you knew what to smell for. Old stone, old moss, and beneath both the sour tang of river water gone stagnant in the bridge's shadow, where the current slowed and thought about things. Every smuggler on the Thornfell side of the mountains knew Gallowmere. It was the only crossing for thirty miles, which meant it was the one place you never crossed.

"Bridge or ford?" Isolde asked, hunched beside me in the bracken on the southern slope. She'd learned to keep her voice low in the three days since we'd left the cache, learned it the hard way, after I'd clapped a hand over her mouth when she'd started humming — humming — on open ground. She had a magistrate's daughter's habits: the certainty that the world was arranged for her convenience, and the stubbornness of someone learning, quickly and furiously, that it wasn't.

"There is no ford," I said. "Not anymore. The river's up."

"Then we cross the bridge."

"We scout the bridge."

"You've been scouting it for an hour."

"An hour's cheap," I said. "A hanging's expensive."

She didn't answer, but I felt her looking at me, the way she'd been looking at me since the cache — that measuring stare, weighing the smuggler against the man her father had apparently trusted enough to die in front of. I didn't know which one she saw. Most days I didn't know which one I was.

The map rode against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from my own skin. Warm, and sometimes — I couldn't prove it, but sometimes — warmer than it should have been, like a bird under a coat. Since the cache I'd stopped pretending it was just ink. The roads on it moved. I'd watched the Gallowmere road redraw itself twice in the night, the bridge's little stroke of charcoal thinning, fraying, the way thread frays before it snaps. Whatever the dying man had pressed into my hands, it wasn't a map of the world. It was the world's memory of itself, and the memory was failing.

Bram crouched on my other side, chewing a stem of coltsfoot like it was a pipe he'd given up but hadn't gotten over. He'd found us at the cache, and he'd been with us since — my uncle, the last of the Crane elders, gray at the temples and quick at the fingers, the man who'd taught me that a lock was just a door with an opinion. He'd gone quiet in the last day. Bram was never quiet. Quiet Bram was a storm glass falling.

"Tell me what you see," he said, without looking up from the coltsfoot.

I looked. The bridge was old work, pre-covenant or early-covenant, its arches furred with ivy gone rust-red in the autumn cold. The far side rose into orchard country, abandoned orchards, their trees gone wild and crooked, fruit rotting black on the boughs because nobody dared pick it. The near side was where we crouched. And on the bridge itself —

"Lanterns," I said. "Two. No — four. They're not moving."

"They're not moving," Bram repeated.

"They're waiting."

* * *

We pulled back off the slope to a hollow where a fallen oak had rotted into a green featherbed, and I laid it out the way Bram had taught me to lay out a route when I was nine: stick-drawn in the dirt, no names, no numbers, everything by shape.

"Four lanterns on the bridge means at least eight men, because nobody posts four lanterns and stands all of them in the open. Which means the rest are off the bridge — in the orchards, most likely, because the orchards have sightlines to both approaches and the trees are bare enough now to see through. They know this is the only crossing. They're not hunting us. They're harvesting us."

"They knew we'd come to Gallowmere," Isolde said. Her jaw was set. She did that when she was frightened — set her jaw like a woman signing something. "How?"

"Because there's one road and they've got horses and we've got feet," Bram said. "You don't need a traitor to do that arithmetic." But his voice had a hitch in it, and I caught the hitch, because I'd grown up catching Bram's hitches the way other children caught colds.

"Uncle," I said. "Who are they?"

He took his time answering. He spat out the coltsfoot. He looked at the sky, which was doing its Thornfell thing — low, bruised, undecided between rain and worse.

"You remember the Hollow men?" he said. "The stories I told you, when you were small? The guild that went into the mountains and came back with no faces?"

"You said they were a story to keep apprentices from wandering."

"I said a lot of things to keep you from wandering." He rubbed his jaw. "The Hollow Guild is real. It was real before you were born. It was the Crane Guild's shadow — the members who decided that smuggling was a poor trade when you could sell the routes themselves. Routes, passes, caches, the names of clients. They sold to whoever paid, and when the old guild cracked down on them, they sold the crackdown too. The last Master Crane burned their charter and struck their names from the rolls, and the ones who survived the purging went north, into the mountains, and something happened to them there. Nobody knows what. They came back wrong. Faces smooth as river stones. They don't talk. They don't take prisoners."

"Then how do you know all this?" Isolde asked, sharp.

"Because I was there when the charter burned, girl, and I was young enough to think it was a festival." Bram's laugh was dry as August. "I know them because they were us. That's what hollow means. It means something that used to have a shape."

I sat with that. The map pressed its strange warmth against my sternum. Somewhere north, men with smooth faces were waiting on a bridge for me to do the stupid, obvious thing.

"So we don't cross," I said. "We go upstream. There's a gorge at Miller's Rest — the water runs through a slot no wider than a cart, and there are stepping stones the millers used before the bridge was built. It's not on any map the guilds have, because my grandmother put the markers in herself and she trusted no one's memory but her own."

"Which you have," Isolde said.

"Which I have."

"Then why are you asking us?"

"Because the stepping stones are underwater this time of year, and the slot runs fast enough to strip the skin off a swimmer, and I wanted one of you to say it out loud so I could pretend I'd been argued out of it."

Nobody said it out loud. Isolde looked at the dirt map. Bram looked at me. And the map against my chest pulsed — I'd swear it pulsed — like something with a heartbeat that had heard the word *upstream* and approved.

We went upstream.

* * *

Miller's Rest was a graveyard that had forgotten it was ever a mill. The wheel housing stood open to the sky like a ribcage, and the gorge below it ran green-black and furious, channeled down to a width I could have spat across, the water inside it moving with the smooth, obscene speed of something that had somewhere to be.

The first marker was where my grandmother's memory said it would be: a notch cut into the rock lip, half a finger deep, worn soft by sixty years of weather. The second was harder — a stone shaped like a kneeling man, and the flood had buried it to its shoulders. I found it with my boot, then with my hand, then with my whole arm in water cold enough to make my bones sing.

"August." Isolde had gone very still on the bank. "August, there are riders on the ridge."

I came up out of the water gasping. The ridge above the mill — the ridge we'd have walked if we'd taken the bridge road — was strung with figures. Six. Eight. More dropping into view as they crested, unhurried, the way you walk when you know the prey is already in the pen. And they were wrong. Even at that distance, even in the failing light, they were wrong — moving too evenly, all of them, like one thing wearing many coats. No lanterns. They didn't need lanterns. Their faces, when the last light caught them, were smooth.

"Down," Bram breathed, and we went down, into the mill's ribcage, into the dark that smelled of wet stone and old grain-rot and, faintly, impossibly, of apples.

"They'll sweep the gorge," I whispered. "It's a funnel. They know it's a funnel. They let us pick it."

"Then why aren't they coming down?" Isolde whispered back.

And I understood, all at once, with the cold river still aching in my arm, why they weren't coming down. They weren't coming down because they didn't have to. They were going to walk the ridge to the far end of the gorge, where the slot opened out, and stand there, and wait, and let the gorge do what funnels do. We could stay in the mill and starve, or go into the water and be stripped and drowned, or come out the far end into their hands. Three doors, and every one of them locked.

Except.

"Uncle," I said. "The sluice."

Bram went white. "No."

"The old flood-sluice. The mill race. Grandmother's maps show it — it runs under the ridge, a cut through the hill, built to carry water to the lower wheel. It comes out past the far end of the gorge. It comes out *behind* them."

"It's been collapsed for forty years."

"Has it?"

"August—"

"Has it, or does everyone just say it has because nobody's had a reason to check?"

Bram stared at me. And I saw it happen in his face — the moment where the uncle who'd taught me caution met the man I'd become, and lost. "The entrance is under the wheel housing," he said finally, quietly. "Under the floor. If the floor's rotted through—"

The floor was rotted through. Forty years of rain through a broken roof had made the mill's heart soft as bread, and when we pried up the planks there was the sluice mouth, a black arch of old brick, breathing cold air that smelled of deep stone and — I put my face to it and breathed again — of moving water. Shallow water. Trickling, not flooding.

"The flood took the top of the hill with it," I said, wonder climbing in my chest like a man up a ladder. "The slide must have opened the sluice to the sky somewhere along its length. It drains now. It drains, Uncle. It's a road."

"It's a coffin with good ventilation."

"It's a smuggler's turn." I was grinning. I couldn't stop grinning. This was the thing, the whole thing, the thing the guild had been for before it was ledgers and debts — the moment when the world says *no* and you say *watch me*. "They built the trap. We're going to use their trap's bones to walk out under their feet."

Isolde was already at the arch, peering in. "It's dark."

"It's a tunnel."

"How far?"

"Four hundred yards, maybe five. Grandmother's map said the lower wheel stood past the orchard wall." I unslung my pack and dug for the candle stubs every Crane carried, and that was when the first stone came down the ridge.

Not a thrown stone. A footfall. One of the smooth-faced men had simply stepped off the ridge line, down the scree, with that terrible even gait, and the scree had answered him, and the sound came rolling into the mill like a verdict.

"They're not waiting," Isolde breathed.

"They're herding. They want us in the water." I lit the candle. Its little flame stood up straight in the sluice's breathing draft — steady air, open channel. "We go now. Uncle, you're last. You're the best in the dark."

"August." Bram's hand closed on my shoulder. In the candlelight his face was old, older than it had been at the cache, older than it had been my whole life. "If that tunnel's blocked past the slide, there's no coming back for the ones behind. You know that. Whoever goes last goes last."

"I know it."

"Then you go first. You're the one with the map and the name and the girl's father's seal. You go first, and you don't stop, no matter what you hear behind you." He smiled, and it was Bram's smile, the one that had taught me locks and ledgers and the difference between a lie and a story. "That's not a request, boy. That's the last order of the Crane Guild, and I'm giving it while there's still a guild to give it in."

"Uncle—"

"Go."

* * *

The sluice was everything Bram feared and everything I'd promised. Low enough that we bent double, tight enough that Isolde's shoulders brushed both walls, dark as the inside of a closed eye except for my candle's trembling coin of light. Water ran shin-deep over brick worn glassy by a century of the mill's hunger. Behind us, our footsteps and the water's voice. Ahead of us, nothing, and then, faintly, a gray thread — the slide's wound, daylight leaking into the tunnel through a crack in the hill.

And behind us, after a hundred yards, another sound.

I heard it first as the water changing — a stutter in its voice — and then as Bram had promised I might: something behind us that wasn't us. A footfall that had no right to be there. Then another. Even. Unhurried. Smooth as river stones.

"They're in the sluice," Isolde whispered, her voice a ghost of a ghost.

"Keep moving."

"August—"

"*Keep moving.*" The candle shook. The gray thread ahead grew. Ten yards of daylight. Five. I could see the slide's scar, a raw half-moon of open sky where the hill had shrugged off its skin, and I scrambled up through it, dragging Isolde after me, out into the orchard's wild twilight — and the orchard was empty. The smooth-faced men were all behind us, in the tunnel, between us and Bram.

"No," Isolde said. "No, no — we go back—"

"We can't. Four hundred yards of a tunnel we can't fight in. If we go back we die in the dark and Bram dies anyway." My own voice was a stranger's, flat and terrible. "We go around. The orchard wall. The lower wheel. If the sluice comes out where Grandmother said, we can be at the exit before they are. We can—"

The sound came up out of the ground under our feet.

Not an explosion. A collapse — the long, grinding sigh of old brick giving up, muffled by the hill's whole weight, felt in the soles of the feet before it was heard. The gray thread of the slide-scar puffed dust. Somewhere below us, the sluice had found its blocked length after all, or the smooth men had brought it down behind them, or the hill had simply chosen that moment to close its throat.

The sound stopped.

Everything stopped. The water in the gorge. The wind in the black fruit. My heart, for one full, screaming second.

Then the orchard wall, forty yards off, cracked open — not fallen, *opened*, brick by brick from the inside, and Bram came through it sideways, coughing, gray with dust, alive, alive, alive — and behind him, filling the breach he'd made, the smooth-faced men came through it too.

"Run," Bram said, cheerfully, horribly, the way he'd said *go*, and turned to meet them.

He didn't meet them with a weapon. Bram Crane had never carried a weapon in his life; he said a knife just told everyone where you kept your fear. He met them with the mill. He went up the lower wheel's broken housing like the old man who'd shinned riggings for forty years, and he cut the brake — the great rotten leather strap that was all that held the wheel's spider-arm in its housing — and forty years of decayed timber and iron came down in a groaning avalanche across the orchard wall's breach, across the gap, across the path.

It did not come down across all of it. There was a gap. There was always a gap, in Bram's work, a door left for family.

"GO, AUGUST!"

I went. Isolde went. We ran through the falling world with the dust of it in our teeth, through the gap, through the orchard, out onto the north road with the mill collapsing behind us like a lung giving out. And at the edge of the trees I turned — I will turn at that edge for the rest of my life — and saw, through the dust, one figure standing on the far side of the ruin, unhurt, unhurried, surrounded by the smooth men who had not, in the end, wanted him dead.

They took his arms. They took his satchel. They took him north, and he did not struggle, and at the last, before the trees closed, he looked back at me and mouthed one word. Not *run*. Not *save me*.

*North.*

* * *

We walked until full dark because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant the word he'd mouthed, and then we stopped anyway, in a hollow under a hawthorn, because legs give out before hearts do.

Isolde built a fire no bigger than a cupped hand. She was furious — I could see it in the set of her shoulders, in the violence with which she fed twigs to the flame — and when she finally spoke it came out low and shaking.

"He bought us twenty minutes with his whole life."

"He bought us the road."

"Is that what you're going to tell yourself?" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "That it was a fair trade? One old man for the covenant? Because that's what *they* would say. That's exactly what Queen Vess would say."

The words went in like a blade finding the seam of armor. Because she was right, and I knew she was right, and I had been doing the arithmetic all day — one uncle, four hundred yards, twenty minutes — the cold smuggler's sums Bram himself had taught me, and I was so tired of them. Tired of doors with opinions. Tired of routes and exits and gaps left in collapsing walls. Tired of a name that meant *get away clean*.

I pulled the map from my chest and unwrapped it, and held it to the firelight.

The Gallowmere road had redrawn itself again. The bridge was gone from it entirely now — not frayed, *gone*, its charcoal stroke lifted clean off the vellum like a scab. And north of the orchards, where nothing had been marked before, a new line had appeared. Faint. Unfinished. A road drawing itself as we walked it, the way a wound draws new skin.

It ran toward the mountains. And on it, small as a flea, a mark I hadn't seen before: a little square of roof, a shrine-shape, a wayshrine of the old covenant, standing alone in the blank vellum like a lit window.

"He said north," I said. "He didn't say *save me*. He said *north*. Bram doesn't waste words. If he's telling us north, it's because north is where this goes — and because he means to be alive when we get wherever it ends." I wrapped the map. I tied the cord. My hands had stopped shaking. "I'm done running exits, Isolde. From here on, we don't take the road that's safest. We take the road that's *right*. Whatever it costs."

She studied me for a long moment, firelight moving on her face. "That's the most foolish thing I've ever heard."

"I know."

"Good," she said, and put another twig on the fire. "Then we'll get along."

North, then. Toward the shrine on the unfinished road, toward whatever waited in the mountains — and toward the man who'd taught me every turn I knew, betting his life that I'd learned enough of them to come for him.
