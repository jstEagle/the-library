## 6. The Knight Without a Court

Isolde had learned to read weather from her father — not the sky's weather, but the world's. The way birds went quiet before soldiers came down a road. The way a village's shutters hung, open or closed, told you whether it had given up hope or merely grown careful. And the way the mist moved through the Thornfell marches these past three days: wrong. It pooled where no water ran and slid uphill when it thought no one watched, as if the land itself had forgotten which direction down was supposed to be.

*The covenant is fraying*, August kept saying, in that flat smuggler's voice he used when he wanted very badly to believe something was true. *The map says so. Look at the edges.*

She looked. She had been looking for days, ever since Gallowmere bridge and the men with the hollow eyes, ever since her father's seal on a dead stranger's map had turned her grief into a weapon with a point she couldn't yet see. The roads on the parchment did shift — she'd stopped doubting that after watching a whole hamlet redraw itself while they ate cold breakfast — but more than shifting now. *Unwriting*. Roads thinned to threads. Threads dissolved to blank vellum. At the far edge of the map, near the mountains, an entire valley had simply ceased to be ink.

Her father had died chasing this. She intended to find out what for.

"The shrine is ahead," August said, low, crouched in the bracken beside her. Beyond him, Crispin — their third shadow, a boy of perhaps sixteen who had attached himself to August somewhere outside Gallowmere with the stubbornness of a burr — peered through the ferns and tried to look like someone who had done this sort of thing before. He had not. His hands shook. Isolde found she minded less than she expected; fear, properly aimed, was honest.

"You're certain?"

"I'm certain it's the only roof between here and the pass." August's mouth twisted. "Smugglers don't pick pretty shelters. We pick dry ones."

The wayshrine had been old when her grandfather was young — she knew its kind. A low stone hall sunk into a hillside, walls furred with moss, roof half-collapsed so that saplings grew from the gutters like green banners of some patient, conquering army. Her father would have called it a border-marker: a place where human masonry and fae craft met and argued politely for centuries. The carvings above the doorway had been worn to suggestions — figures walking in procession, hand to hand to hand, a chain of travelers whose faces the rain had erased.

Someone had tried to deface them recently. Fresh chisel-marks gouged across the linked hands, clumsy and angry.

"Vess's people?" she murmured.

"Hollow Guild, if we're lucky. Queen's engineers, if we're not." August drew the little knife he carried — a smuggler's tool, more hook than blade. "Either way, quiet now."

They went in through the collapsed eastern wall rather than the door, because August trusted no threshold he hadn't tested, and inside the air changed. That was the first strangeness. Outside the night had been raw, the autumn wind carrying woodsmoke and wet rot, but within the shrine the air lay still and faintly warm, smelling of summer hay and candle-wax and something underneath both — a green scent, like a meadow remembered by someone who had never seen one. Crispin sneezed. Isolde's skin prickled.

And in the center of the ruined nave, iron filled her mouth.

Chains. She saw them before she saw anything else, because they were *wrong* — not rusted relics hanging from forgotten hooks but taut, bright-edged links running from floor to broken vault, anchored into the stones themselves, and every link hummed with a light that hurt to look at directly. Cold-white. Starved. They converged on the altar dais, and on the dais knelt a prisoner.

He was kneeling because the chains forced him there, but even so, even starved and shackled and gray as ash, he knelt like something that had chosen the posture once and never unlearned it. Tall, though bent now. Hair white-blond, falling past shoulders that were too sharp under a coat that had once been magnificent — she could see the ghost of silver embroidery, unraveled thread by thread. His wrists bore manacles worked in shapes she almost recognized: interlocking rings, the same pattern as the procession carved above the door.

His ears came to points.

Crispin made a small sound. August's hand found the boy's shoulder without looking.

"That's not a man," Crispin whispered.

"It is," said the prisoner, without raising his head. His voice was cracked from disuse but precise beneath the cracks, each word laid like a mason sets stone. "It has been a great many years since anyone troubled to check. But yes. I am."

Silence. The chained light pulsed, dimmed, pulsed again — and Isolde understood suddenly that it wasn't flickering. It was *beating*. Slowly. Weakly. Like a heart.

"How long have you been here?" she asked.

"A hundred years." A pause. "Give or take a decade. One loses count, and counting was always my weakness."

August had gone very still, the stillness he got when his ledger-brain was running sums too fast for speech. "Who put you here?"

"The Hollow Guild. In the days when they still called themselves Cranes." The prisoner's head lifted then, and Isolde's breath caught. His eyes were the color of deep river ice — pale, pale blue, shot through with veins of silver — and they were *old* in a way no human face was old. They swept over the three of them, cataloguing, and stopped on the roll of parchment at August's belt. On the wax seal, broken but unmistakable, that Isolde had spent two weeks learning to read like scripture.

The prisoner laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as pages turning.

"Well," he said. "The road sends a courier at last. I had begun to think it dead. Or that *I* was."

"We're not couriers," August said carefully. "We're just passing—"

"You are carrying the Sigil Road folded over your heart, boy. I can hear it from here." The ice-silver eyes fixed on him. "It is dying, and it is loud, and you are the only reason it is not already silence. Do not insult us both by pretending otherwise."

---

They cut him free because there was no sensible argument against it, and several insane ones for.

August wanted to leave — Isolde saw it in him, the smuggler's instinct screaming *unknown asset, unknown cost, walk away* — but his hands were already finding the anchor-stones before his mouth caught up, testing which links bore weight, muttering about load-bearing and clever hateful craftsmanship. Crispin held the lantern. Isolde stood before the prisoner with her knife drawn and her questions drawn faster.

"Your name."

"Serahin." He said it like a man laying down a heavy bundle. "Serahin of the Autumn Marches. Knight-Vigilant of the Sigil, once. Now, I suppose, knight of nothing much."

"I've heard that title." Her father's voice, reading aloud by lamplight, three winters ago: *Knight-Vigilant, keeper of the covenant-path, sworn between the courts and commons both.* She had thought it a fairy story then. Everything she'd thought was a fairy story had been following her for weeks. "You defended the old covenant. That's why they exiled you."

Something moved behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, or the ache of being remembered. "Exiled. Imprisoned. Erased. Your human guilds are efficient at all three." He flexed his freed hands slowly, wincing. "I refused to let the Hollow men widen the road's tolls. Refused twice. The second refusal was witnessed, and witnessing is binding among my people. So they bound *me* instead, and left me here to listen to the road die an inch at a time." His gaze went to the ceiling, to the dark beyond it, toward mountains. "A hundred years of listening. You cannot imagine—"

He stopped himself. Drew a breath that shook only slightly.

"No. You can. You've been traveling with it. Tell me how bad."

So they told him. There on the fallen altar-stones, with the lantern guttering and the chained light pulsing slower than any heartbeat should, they laid out everything — the stranger at the ford, the map that moved, the roads unwriting themselves, the Hollow hunters, Bram taken at the bridge, the tollgate that had eaten Isolde's inheritance two nights past. Serahin listened without interruption, and Isolde watched his face do what her own had done at each telling: flinch, harden, grieve.

When August reached the end — *the map is a countdown, and I don't know how many miles are left* — Serahin closed his eyes.

"Then it is worse than I dared hope and better than I feared," he said. "Worse, because the road should not be able to die in a single season. Better, because—" he opened his eyes, and the exhaustion in them had rearranged itself around something fierce and fragile, "—because it is still *fighting*. A dying road does not courier maps to smugglers, child. A dying road does not redraw itself around travelers to keep them alive. It is spending its last strength on you. On whoever will carry it home."

"Home to where?"

"To the Heartstone. Where else?" He said it gently, as if the obviousness were kindness. "The Sigil Road is not a road, boy. Not originally. It is an oath — the oldest one your realm and mine share — given shape enough to walk upon. Every mile of it is a promise somebody made and somebody kept. And promises, unlike stone, require tending. Someone must renew them, generation upon generation, or the path forgets itself and the realms drift apart, and everything the covenant feeds—" he gestured, taking in the walls, the hills, the invisible worlds beyond them, "—starves."

Isolde's heart was pounding. "My father wrote that Vess meant to break it. Sever it entirely."

"She means to. Whether she *can*..." Serahin rose, unsteady, one hand against the pillar, and even half-starved he seemed to fill the ruin. "Severance requires the Heartstone and a ritual older than her crown. I helped draft its counter-warding, a lifetime ago. I know its shape." A bitter pause. "I also, once, helped imagine its shape. That is a confession for daylight, and better roads. Tonight I will say only this: if she completes the severance, Thornfell does not become self-sufficient, whatever her ministers whisper. It becomes a cup with the bottom knocked out. Everything drains. Both realms."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere in the dark rafters, saplings creaked in wind that hadn't reached them outside.

August sat very still on the altar-stone, the map across his knees, and Isolde watched him wrestle with it — the weight settling onto his narrow smuggler's shoulders, the weight he'd been carrying alone since a stranger died in his arms at a ford. *The road remembers.* She had thought those words were for her, proof of her father's last work. Watching August now, she understood they had never been hers at all.

"So it's real," August said at last. Quietly. Not to Serahin, quite. To himself. "All of it. It's actually real, and it's actually me."

"It chose you," Serahin agreed.

"Why? I'm nobody. I run contraband and keep bad books. My guild is three people and a debt column."

"The road has never once in its existence chosen a king." For the first time, the fae knight smiled — a thin, worn thing, but real. "Kings delegate. The road wants keepers."

---

They left the shrine before dawn, because Serahin warned that his prison had been a listening-post as well as a cell, and the Hollow Guild checked on their investments.

But at the threshold Isolde paused, one hand on the defaced carving, and looked back at him in the lantern-light — this gaunt, ancient, impossible creature blinking against open sky for the first time in a century, breathing air that smelled of rain instead of stale enchantment. He swayed. Crispin steadied his elbow, and Serahin allowed it with a grace that suggested he had decided, deliberately, to allow depending on people again.

"Why help us?" she asked him. "You owe none of this. You paid a hundred years already. You could walk into the twilight country and be done with humans forever."

He considered her for a long moment, ice-and-silver eyes unreadable.

"Because I heard the road dying for a hundred years, my lady," he said, "and in all that darkness, do you know what I feared most? Not death. Silence. That when it finally failed, no one would come. No one would even know what had been lost." He touched the broken seal at her belt — lightly, reverently, the way one touches a relic. "And then three children walked in out of the rain carrying its heart in a satchel, arguing about it. Wary, mistrustful, wholly unsuitable children." The thin smile again. "Hope is rarely suitable. That is how you know it is hope and not mere expectation."

Beyond the hill, the east was graying. The mist on the march-lands was moving wrong again — sliding uphill, pooling where no water ran — and Serahin's expression sharpened as he watched it, the knight surfacing through the wreck of the prisoner.

"But we linger," he said softly. "That mist is not weather. It is the fringe of the Sigil Road itself, bleeding into your world where the bounds wear thin — and things other than we three will feel it pulling." He turned to August, and all the gentleness in him became steel. "How fast can you travel, courier? Because by my reckoning, the road has perhaps six days of life left — and I can hear, faint and getting louder, hooves on the northern passes. Whatever comes down off those mountains to meet us..."

He looked toward the peaks, black against the graying sky.

"...it will reach us first."
