## 9. Queen Vess's Bargain

The throne room of Thornfell had been built for a king who loved light. Vess had learned this in her first week upon the seat, when she'd ordered the great south windows shuttered against the glare and her chamberlain had looked at her as though she'd asked him to blind the sun itself. Let him look. The glass was stained with the faces of dead monarchs — Aldric the Wise, Maren of the Long Summer, old Corvane with his crown of antlers — and Vess had no wish to be judged daily by men who had signed away their kingdom's future for the pleasure of a bargain.

She sat now beneath those shuttered windows, in a pool of lamplight that smelled of tallow and cold iron, and read the harvest reports again.

They did not improve with reading.

The eastern granaries: half yield. The river valleys along the Sigil Road: blighted at the borders where fae-touched water bled into human soil. The border towns — Gallowmere, Fenwick, all the little places whose names she was learning to say like prayers — starving by inches, their children hollow-cheeked, their old people walking into the winter woods so as not to burden anyone. And every year, the tithe. Every year, the covenant's price, paid out of human bellies into fae hands, and every year the price grew, because the road was old and hungry and the court beyond the mountains had never once considered lowering its appetite.

Vess set down the reports and pressed her fingertips to her temples.

*The realm bleeds,* her father had written to her, in the last letter he ever sent from the northern front, before the fever took him. *Not from wounds, daughter. From generosity.*

She had been twenty-two years old when she understood what he meant. She was thirty-one now, crowned by acclamation rather than birth — let the gossips sharpen their tongues on that; she had earned the throne the only honest way, by being the one willing to do the work — and she had spent nine years studying the covenant the way a physician studies a tumor. Not with hatred. With precision.

It was not, she reminded herself for the ten-thousandth time, that she hated the fae.

That was the thing her enemies would never understand, the lie they preferred because it was simpler. They called her the Usurper, the Oathbreaker, the Queen Who Would Starve the Otherworld. They painted her in pamphlets with black eyes and a knife made of children's teeth. But Vess had met the fae. She had sat across a table from their emissaries in the early years, before she stopped inviting them, and she had found them beautiful and courteous and utterly without mercy — not cruel the way a wolf is cruel, but cruel the way winter is cruel, which is to say: without any cruelty in them at all, only need, vast and ancient and entitled.

The covenant did not ask. That was the heart of it. It had been sworn three hundred years ago by kings who wanted peace and were willing to pay for it with other people's bread, and it had never once been renegotiated, because the fae did not negotiate and the human kings did not imagine their descendants might want to eat.

*Half the harvest of the borderlands, yearly, in perpetuity.*

*One child in fifty, given to the road at seven years of age, to serve in the twilight court until death or release.*

*First rights to all waters rising east of the mountains.*

In perpetuity. Vess said the words silently, tasting them. In perpetuity meant forever, and forever meant her granddaughter, if she ever had one, standing in this same room reading these same reports, watching the same slow bleed, unless someone — someone unlovely, someone willing to be called usurper and oathbreaker and worse — put an end to it.

A knock at the far door. Her steward, Master Quillon, gray as his own ledger books, bowed low.

"The prisoner, Majesty."

"Ah." Vess rose, smoothing her plain gray gown — she wore gray always in working sessions; gold was for ceremonies, and this was work. "Is he conscious?"

"Barely, Majesty. He refused the physician's draught. Said—" Quillon hesitated, and Vess gestured impatiently for him to continue. "Said he didn't trust anything we handed him. He asked instead for paper. And ink."

Something flickered behind Vess's ribs that she refused to name as amusement. "Paper and ink. For a man in chains."

"He's been writing since they brought him in, Majesty. Lists. He says—" Quillon consulted a slip of notes, looking faintly scandalized, "—he says our supply convoys are routed like a drunkard walks home, and he's marked six points where the Hollow Guild could take them for less than a tenth of what we spend on guards."

Vess went very still.

Then she smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was a real one, and she knew it made her officers nervous, which was sometimes useful.

"Bring me the traitor," she said, "and bring his lists."

* * *

They brought him in through the servants' corridor, which was how she preferred to receive prisoners — no grand entrance, no theater. Theater was for people who needed convincing. Bram Crane needed no convincing; she had read his file twice, and files told you everything theater could not.

Bram Crane. Fifty-three years old. Second-generation guildsman, born in the Crane hall at Millbrook, apprenticed at eight, journeyman at fifteen, master of the western routes at thirty-four. Widower. No children of his own — one nephew, August Crane, last registered member of the parent guild, currently missing somewhere along the Sigil Road with a document Vess wanted very badly.

And, as of eleven days ago, a traitor to the Hollow Guild, which made him — Vess allowed herself the small irony — a traitor to traitors, which in her private taxonomy was nearly a virtue.

He shuffled in between two guards, wrists ironed, and Vess saw immediately why Quillon had described him as barely conscious. His face was a map of the Hollow Guild's displeasure: one eye swollen shut, lip split, knuckles scabbed. But his back was straight. Old smugglers' backs didn't bend for anyone; she'd learned that much in nine years of hunting them.

"Master Crane," she said. "Sit, please."

"I'll stand." His voice was gravel and river-mud. "Chairs are for negotiations, and I'm told I'm past negotiating."

"You're mistaken. You're the most negotiable man in Thornfell." Vess waved the guards back to the wall and remained standing herself, matching him. "You sold your guild's honor to my predecessor's regime, then sold my regime's secrets to your own guild when it turned on me, and now you've sold the Hollow Guild's routes to me. Three betrayals in four years. A lesser man would be ashamed. I suspect you're proud."

Something moved under the ruin of his face. Not quite a smile. "You've read my file."

"I wrote half of it." She let that sit. Then she crossed to the table where his papers lay — the lists, the route-markings, six weeks of a captured man's obsessive cartography — and turned them toward him. "These are good. These are better than good. My quartermasters have been routing those convoys blind for two years, and you found six soft points in an afternoon, chained to a table, half-blind. Why?"

"Because I've walked those roads since before you were crowned, Majesty." He said *Majesty* the way a man spits out a pit. "Roads don't care who wears the crown. Roads care who knows them."

"And yet the Hollow Guild, who know the roads better than anyone alive, chose to hunt a boy down the Sigil Road instead of using that knowledge to feed the country." Vess tilted her head. "Tell me about the boy."

"No."

"No?"

"You can pull my nails, Majesty, and I'll still say no. Not because I love the lad — though I do, more than I ever taught him I did — but because you're asking the wrong question, and I don't answer wrong questions. It's a professional habit."

Vess studied him for a long moment. Lamplight caught the silver in his stubble, the dried blood at his temple. Behind her, the shuttered windows kept out the daylight and the dead kings alike.

"Then let me ask a right one," she said quietly. "Do you know what the covenant costs? Not in the abstract. In numbers."

His good eye narrowed. "Everyone knows the tithe—"

"The tithe is the part they tell children." She pulled the harvest reports from beneath his lists and laid them open, turning each page toward him with something close to reverence. "Half-yield in the eastern granaries. Blight along every waterway the fae touch. Do you know why the blight follows the water? Because the covenant grants them first rights to the headwaters, and everything they draw from grows strange downstream. We plant wheat that comes up gray. We raise cattle that bear dead calves. And once a year, Master Crane, once a year, we gather fifty seven-year-old children at the road's mouth and give them away, and their mothers are told it is an honor, and some of the mothers believe it, because believing it is easier than burying an empty cradle."

The silence stretched. Bram's jaw worked.

"My niece's boy was taken in the spring," he said finally, and his voice had changed — lower, rougher, the gravel gone soft. "My sister's grandson. Little Tam. He waved at the crowd when they led him off. Waved like he was going to a fair."

"Yes," Vess said. "They usually do. That's the cruelest part."

She watched him look at the reports. Watched him read the way a literate man reads — fast, greedy, skipping ahead — and she knew the moment the numbers landed, because his shoulders dropped half an inch, the way a man's shoulders drop when a load he'd been carrying turns out to be heavier than he thought.

"This is real," he said. "This isn't— you're not showing me doctored pages."

"I have no need to doctor pages. The truth is damning enough." Vess stepped closer, and lowered her voice, and gave him the thing she gave almost no one: candor. "I will tell you what I told my council, Master Crane, and I will tell you plainly, because I am tired of being called a monster by people who have never read a harvest report. I did not take this throne for glory. I took it because the man who held it was content to watch Thornfell die politely, one tithe at a time, so long as the songs said he was honorable. The covenant is killing us. Slowly, gently, with beautiful music — but killing us. And I intend to break it."

"Not mend it," Bram said. "Break it."

"Mending is a courtesy extended between equals. The fae do not consider us equals. They consider us livestock that learned to write." She said it without heat, and that was the trick of it — she had rehearsed this speech for years until the heat burned off and only the shape of it remained, hard and clean as forged iron. "So yes. Break it. Sever the road. Seal the mountain passes. Let the otherworld keep its twilight and its riddles and its endless hunger, and let Thornfell learn to feed itself. It will hurt. The first years will be lean, and there will be those who curse my name, and I accept that. I accepted it the day I was crowned. But in fifty years, no child of this realm will be handed to a road like a sack of grain, and no mother will wave her son goodbye to a fair that never lets him home."

Bram was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened her more than defiance would have.

"You've thought about this," he said. "More than thought. You've bled on it."

"Every day for nine years."

"And you genuinely believe you're saving us." He shook his head slowly, wincing at the motion. "God help me, Majesty, I think you do. I think you lie awake nights over it. That's the worst of it. If you were just a tyrant, a body could hate you clean."

"But?"

"But you're counting the wrong ledgers." He lifted his chained hands as best he could, gesturing at the reports. "Those numbers are true. I won't argue them. But the covenant isn't just a tax, Majesty. My grandmother remembered the old stories — the ones from before the kings got their hands on it. The road wasn't built by crowns. It was built by people. Farmers and ferry-men and midwives, swearing to each other across the mountains, freely given, freely kept. The tithe came later. The tithe is rot grown over a living root. You cut the root to kill the rot, and you'll kill both realms doing it. The fae are starving too — did your pretty reports mention that? Did your emissaries ever mention that the twilight court has dimmed two shades in a century? The covenant feeds both directions, or it was meant to. Your way, everyone starves. Just us first."

Vess felt the familiar cold settle into her chest, the cold that lived there always, the cold she had come to think of as the price of clarity.

"That is a story," she said. "Told by people who benefit from the arrangement."

"It's a story told by my grandmother, who gained nothing from the arrangement except a taken son." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the word *son*, and Vess understood suddenly that the little waving boy, Tam, was not the first child his family had lost to the road. "I'm not asking you to weep for the fae, Majesty. I'm asking you to check your arithmetic. You want to save Thornfell. So does the boy you're hunting. The difference is he's carrying a map that might actually do it, and you're carrying an army that can only break things."

"The map," Vess said softly, "is a piece of parchment."

"The map," Bram said, "is the road."

* * *

She dismissed him after that — sent him back to his cell with paper and ink and, after a moment's consideration, a lamp that burned past midnight. Her captain of guards raised an eyebrow at the order, and Vess met the look evenly until it dropped.

Alone again, she stood at the shuttered windows and listened to the wind coming down off the mountains, off the passes she intended to seal, off the country beyond them that she had seen exactly once, at nineteen, from a ridge at dawn: towers of ash-gray stone wrapped in ivy that glowed faintly, streets full of tall figures moving like candle flames in a draft, beautiful and dimming, dimming even then.

*Starving,* Bram had said. She had known it. Of course she had known it. The emissaries' hands had grown thinner each decade; the music they brought to treaty-days had slowed, the way a heart slows. She had known, and she had folded the knowledge small and put it away, because a queen who pities her enemy is a queen who hesitates, and hesitation had killed kinder rulers than she.

But the knowledge would not stay folded tonight.

*You're counting the wrong ledgers.*

She thought of the boy. August Crane. Nineteen years old, by the guild rolls. Last apprentice of a dying house, running one final route to pay his family's debts — she had read the intercepts, had felt something uncomfortably like recognition reading them. A young person trying to hold up a collapsing inheritance alone. She knew that weight. She had carried it up the steps of a throne.

Under other stars, she thought — and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, tender as a bruise — under other stars, he might have stood in this room. Might have read her reports and understood them, smuggler's arithmetic, the arithmetic of margins and survival. Might have been her ally. Her wardens-general. The kind of sharp, road-wise, unbought mind that Thornfell would need in the lean years after the severance, when the roads went dark and only the people who truly knew them could keep the country fed.

Instead he was out there in the dark with a dying map, chasing a fairy tale about freely given oaths, while the Hollow Guild closed around him like a hand.

Vess turned from the window. On the table, among Bram Crane's obsessive lists, one page lay apart from the others — the page he had written last, before she summoned him. She had read it three times already. She read it again now.

It was not a list of convoy routes. It was a single line, in a cramped, stubborn hand:

*She'll listen to reason, if anyone ever gives her a reason. Try the truth. It's the one weapon nobody's used on her yet.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*Tam's birthday is midwinter. If the road still stands by then, maybe he waves at us coming home.*

Vess stood over the page for a long time, lamplight trembling, the cold in her chest and the warmth behind it at war like two armies meeting on a bridge.

Then she rang for Quillon, and when he came she heard her own voice go out crisp and clear and queenly, ordering the Hollow Guild's hunters doubled along the Sigil Road, ordering every pass watched, every ford netted, every lantern lit — because the map was moving, her spies reported, redrawing itself mile by mile, and whatever the boy carried was no longer merely parchment.

It was the road. And roads, her grandmother had told her once, long ago, in a kitchen that smelled of bread — roads remember.

"Find him," Vess said to the darkness beyond her steward's shoulder, and did not let herself hear how much the words cost. "Before the road remembers him first."
