## 10. Bram's Choice

They took his boots first.

Bram Crane noticed that — of all things, that. Not the manacles, not the hood soaked in river-water and smelling of the Gallowmere mud they'd dragged him through, but the boots. Twenty years of smuggling had taught him that a man stripped of his boots was a man who wasn't going anywhere, and whoever ran this place understood the language of practical cruelty. They set the boots by the door where he could see them, laces tucked neatly inside, so tidy it was almost kind.

The cell was a cellar, or had been once. Now it was a room with a table, two chairs, and a lantern that burned something pale and slow. Fae-light, he thought. It gave off no heat at all, and Bram had been cold since the bridge.

He worked at the ropes on his wrists without much hope. The Cranes taught knots to children before letters; he'd slipped a dozen bindings in his life and been caught by exactly none of them until now. These were different. Every time he flexed a tendon, the rope *answered*, tightening like something breathing in its sleep. Hollow Guild work, then. Or fae work. He was beginning to suspect there was less difference than he'd spent a lifetime pretending.

He thought about August the way you think about a debt you've been avoiding: sidelong, with one eye, flinching from the full sum.

*Go left at the split, boy. Always count your lanterns twice. Never sell what you can't carry.*

That was the catechism he'd raised the boy on, after his sister and her husband went into the river and came out only as names on a ledger line marked *survivors: one.* Pragmatism. That was the word Bram had built his whole guardianship around, the way other men built theirs around faith. Keep your head down. Keep your purse shut. Keep your name clean enough to trade on and never let it cost you more than it earns. When the guild began to die — when the routes closed one by one and the old families sold out to Vess's agents with their soft voices and softer coin — Bram had called it pragmatism too. Adaptation. A Crane who couldn't bend was just kindling.

And then he'd stood on Gallowmere bridge with six Hollow hunters coming up the bank, and he'd looked at his nephew's face — that stubborn, exhausted, *hopeful* face, gods help them all — and he'd heard himself say, *Run, lad,* and stepped the wrong direction on purpose.

Not pragmatic. His knees still ached from where they'd brought him down. But not pragmatic.

The door opened without sound. The woman who entered was not Queen Vess — Bram had seen the queen once, from a rooftop, at a distance, and remembered the stillness of her the way you remember a statue that might have blinked. This was someone else: grey-cloaked, grey-eyed, carrying a leather folder and a cup of something steaming. She set the cup in front of him. Broth. Real broth, with marrow and thyme.

"Eat," she said. "You'll want your strength for listening."

"I've listened to better offers in worse rooms."

"I don't imagine you have." She sat, opened the folder, and turned it so he could read. His own handwriting stared back at him. Ledger pages. Route notes. The cache inventories from three counties — things he had written in cipher, in code, in smuggler's shorthand that hadn't been broken in twenty years, copied out fair and translated as if they were recipes. "Your guild's accounts, Master Crane. Reconstructed. You'll notice we didn't need your help for any of it. We needed it for one thing only."

"August."

"The route." She said it the way a surgeon names the limb. "The map he carries is the last living thread of the Sigil covenant, and it will reach the Heartstone if no one stands between it and the mountain. The queen has no wish to harm the boy. She has read your file, Master Crane. She knows what you sacrificed at the bridge. She finds it — her word — *admirable*. Misplaced, but admirable. Men like you are wasted on dying guilds."

"And if I tell you where he's going?"

"You walk out of this room. Your boots go home with you. And the Crane Guild is restored — charter, hall, routes, the name cleared of every debt Vess's clerks bought up these ten years. Your nephew's inheritance, whole again. Better than whole. He'd be the youngest guildmaster in Thornfell's history." She slid a pen across the table. "All it costs is a road. One road, drawn on paper. The boy loses nothing today. Perhaps nothing ever. The queen simply chooses which fork he takes."

Bram looked at the broth. It smelled like his sister's kitchen, which was either coincidence or research, and he suspected nothing about this woman was coincidence. That frightened him more than the ropes did. Torture he could plan against. Kindness with a ledger behind it was another animal entirely.

"You know the funny thing," he said. "I taught the boy that a deal like this is the only honest kind. No violence, no lies. Just price. Clean arithmetic." His hands were steady. He made himself notice that; whatever else happened tonight, he wanted to remember his hands being steady. "I told him the world doesn't run on honor. It runs on ledgers, and honor is just the story men tell when the sums come out even."

"Yes," she said. "So sign."

"The sums don't come out even." He pushed the pen back with one knuckle. "You're asking me to spend my nephew. I know what my debts are worth. I've carried them forty years. He isn't one of them."

Something moved behind the grey eyes — disappointment, maybe, or respect, filed away for later. "Master Crane. Do you understand what happens in rooms like this when the arithmetic fails?"

"I raised a smuggler," Bram said. "I assume it involves arithmetic."

She rose, gathered the folder, and paused at the door. "The queen will see you herself, I think. You've earned that much. Very few do." Then, quieter, almost gently: "For what it's worth, she meant the offer. She always means her offers. That's what makes her dangerous."

The door closed. Bram drank the broth. It seemed important, somehow, not to leave anything on the table that they'd given him — waste nothing, want nothing, the first rule of every starving house. It tasted like his sister's kitchen after all, and he wept a little while he ate, quietly, the way he hadn't let himself at the funeral, because there had been a child to raise and grief doesn't keep books.

* * *

Queen Vess came at what might have been dawn. There was no window, but the guards changed, and the pale lantern guttered low, and when it relit she was standing in the corner of the room as though she'd always been there, watching him with the patience of weather.

She was smaller than he expected. That was the trick of her, he decided within the first minute — everything about Vess was smaller than it should be, her voice, her gestures, her crown, which sat on her brow thin as a circlet of fence-wire. She spoke softly enough that he had to lean in, and he caught himself doing it and hated how natural it felt.

"Master Crane. Tell me about the ledgers."

"Your clerk already showed me my own handwriting. I'm not sure what's left to tell."

"Not yours." She drew a stool from the corner — she carried no chair, or perhaps the room provided one, the fae-light bending toward her like grass toward wind — and sat with her hands folded. "The covenant's ledgers. Thornfell owes the far court for every good harvest, every mild winter, every plague that turned aside at the border. Three hundred years of payments in luck and years and firstborn chances, deducted from the realm's own account. Do you know what our treasury looks like, Master Crane? What the land itself looks like, near the Sigil Road? Borrowed country. We are farming on credit, and the note comes due in my reign, and I am expected to hand my grandchildren's harvest to a court that calls us *the mortal tenants*." Her voice stayed level. That was the terrible thing about it. "I am not breaking faith. I am refusing to pass a debt I never signed."

Bram thought of the flooded fields along the Thorn, the blight-year when the guild lost four routes and two cousins, the strange barren miles around the old wayshrines where nothing sang anymore. He'd blamed the covenant for half the miseries of his life without ever once seeing its face.

"Might even be true," he said. "What you believe. I'm not the man to judge cosmology. I judge exits." He shifted his bound wrists onto the table, deliberately, letting her see them. "But here's my trouble, Majesty. You're offering me a restored guild built on a road you mean to sever. My family hauled goods along that covenant-path before it had a name. My sister paid tithe-tolls at wayshrines that predate your crown. If the road dies, the guild you're handing me is a coat of arms on a wall. Pretty. Worthless. You're not restoring my inheritance. You're embalming it and asking me to call it life."

"Or you preserve a memory of it," Vess said, "instead of watching your nephew die in the mountains for a covenant neither of you ever chose."

"There it is." Bram nodded slowly, almost grateful. "There's the real price. Thank you for saying it plain. Your clerk dressed it up in boots and broth." He met her eyes, and found, to his surprise, that she let him. "You want to know what I'd tell him if he were sitting here? I'd tell him: take the deal, boy. Always take the deal. That's what I taught him. Forty years of taking deals, and here's my account balance: a dead guild, an empty hall, a sister under the river, and a nephew I taught to distrust every soul who ever offered him help." He laughed — a short, ugly sound that surprised them both. "Pragmatism, Majesty. I wrote the book. It's a very short book, and the last page is this room."

"Then you understand there is no virtue in refusing me."

"No." He leaned forward, and the ropes creaked, and he found he didn't care. "But here's the thing about ledgers nobody tells you when you're young and clever. Every entry balances *eventually*. Every shortcut gets collected. I shorted honor my whole life thinking I was beating the market, and the market came due at Gallowmere bridge, where I got to watch my nephew run because I told him to, and hate me for it, and be *right* to hate me for it — and still look back. Still look back, Majesty. Boy's got no sense. Boy's got no pragmatism at all." His voice cracked and he let it crack; there was no one left in the world whose opinion of him could get lower, so he might as well spend what remained on the truth. "He's heading for the Split. The stone cairn above the Drowned Chapel valley, where the road forks north and south."

It was a lie. He watched it leave his mouth like a coin leaving a cut purse, and he watched her receive it, and he understood in that instant exactly what kind of gambler he had finally become: the sort who bets the only thing left, which is the chance that the people hunting his nephew trust him precisely as far as he deserves.

Vess studied him for a long moment. "The Split."

"Cairn's a marker. Old smugglers' signal-cache beneath it. He'll take the south fork — the low road, through the fraying ground. It's slower but the Hollow patrols can't ride it." Every word was false and every word was smooth, because Bram Crane had told lies to magistrates, tax-men, wives, and bishops, and he had one performance left in him and by every god named and unnamed he would make it worthy of the boy who'd inherited his mother's stubbornness instead of his uncle's sense. "If you want him stopped, stop him there. Anywhere past the Split and you'll need the fae roads, and I don't think," he added softly, "you want to owe the far court *anything* else."

Something flickered across the queen's face — the first expression she'd worn that looked like it belonged to a person. Respect, maybe. Or recognition. Two gamblers across a table, each holding a hand built out of other people's lives.

"You love him," she said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't kindness. It was inventory.

"He's all that's left on my books," Bram said.

Queen Vess stood. The lantern light leaned after her and she gathered it back with a gesture, and at the door she stopped, her back to him, thin crown catching the pale flame.

"My clerk will return with a map," she said. "Draw the route. Accurately, as best your conscience permits. If the ambush succeeds, the guild is restored and the boy lives — I keep my offers, Master Crane, that is the whole point of making them. If it fails..." She did not finish it. She didn't need to. Rooms like this one finished sentences for themselves.

* * *

The map was waiting when they brought him back from wherever they took him the second time.

Bram lay on the cellar floor and contemplated it from a distance of three feet, because three feet was as far as his body would currently travel in any direction. The clerk — the grey woman, methodical even in this — had laid it open beside him with the pen, and turned his face with two fingers at his jaw so he could see it, and left the lantern burning, and gone away. Even the tortures here were tidy. Boots by the door. Broth at intervals. Pain administered on a schedule, precise as tide-tables, designed by someone who had read his file and understood that the Cranes broke on meaning, not on meat.

His right hand wouldn't close properly anymore. So he used the left, and it took him most of an hour to draw the south fork in careful, confident strokes, and while he drew he recited the catechism in his head like a prayer, because his sister had taught it to *him* first, long before he taught it to her son:

*Count your lanterns twice.*
*Never sell what you can't carry.*
*A name is the only cargo you can't buy back.*

He'd gotten the order wrong his whole life. Carried the name like freight, sold it by the ounce whenever the price was fair, told himself the ledger would forgive him because ledgers forgive everything that balances.

Except they don't. That was the joke. Ledgers only record. Forgiveness was some other document entirely, kept in some other office, by clerks he'd never believed in until a wounded stranger died at a ford and put a dying world in a smuggler's boy hands and the boy — no sense, no pragmatism, all heart — picked it up and started walking.

*Let them come to the Split,* Bram thought, and laughed until the laughing hurt, which didn't take long. *Let them wait at my beautiful lie with their lanterns lit, counting twice.*

Because August wouldn't take the south fork. August had never once in his life taken the sensible route Bram mapped for him — not in trade, not in grief, not at Gallowmere where any rational boy would have kept running instead of turning back for the flare of his uncle's lantern. The boy would go north. The boy would go straight through the worst of it, because someone would need him to, because that was the whole broken glorious architecture of him.

Which meant Bram had one job left, and it was this: survive the next interrogation convincingly enough that when the ambush failed, they believed the failure was the road's fault, not the liar's. Hold the lie together with his body if his voice gave out. Buy days. Buy hours. A smuggler knows the truest currency is time, and Bram Crane intended to die solvent.

Footsteps on the stair. The grey woman, and two others, and the pale lantern already brightening in anticipation.

Bram Crane rolled onto his side, reached the table's leg with his ruined hand, and pulled himself upright, inch by inch, toward the map he had betrayed beautifully.

"Right," he said to the door, and to the queen beyond it, and to the nephew he'd failed in every way except the one that mattered tonight. "Where were we?"

Far to the north, under a sky that was starting to fray at its seams, the road remembered where he'd sent it — and went the other way.
