## 11. The Drowned Chapel

The rain found them an hour before the chapel did.

It came down the mountainside in gray sheets, erasing the path beneath Isolde's boots, turning the world into water and guesswork. Serahin walked with his weight against August's shoulder, his wounded side bound in strips of Isolde's petticoat, and every few steps he made a sound so faint she might have imagined it — except that fae did not imagine pain, the knight had told her once, they simply declined to perform it. What she heard now, then, was real. What she saw in his face was real: the silver of his skin gone dull as old pewter, his eyes half-lidded, tracking nothing.

"We cannot stop," August said, for the third time in an hour. He said it the way people say prayers — not because it will change anything, but because silence is worse.

"And we cannot go on," Isolde answered, for the third time too. She had stopped softening her voice somewhere around the tollgate. "He'll die on his feet and we'll all watch it happen. There." She pointed through the deluge, where a shape crouched at the valley's floor like a sleeping animal. A bell tower, its spire snapped off mid-height, jagged against the bruised sky. "A chapel."

"Consecrated ground won't hold the Hollow Guild if they truly want us," Serahin murmured, without opening his eyes.

"Nothing holds the Hollow Guild," August said. "But rain slows even traitors. Come on."

They descended together, three figures roped by necessity, sliding down slopes that had given up being soil and become rivers instead. Isolde thought, absurdly, of her father's study — how he had kept a map of this very valley behind glass, and how she had once traced the little drawn chapel with her finger and asked what it was called, and he had said, *Gospell-on-the-Mere, though there hasn't been a mere in a hundred years, sweetling; the water left the same year the roads began to move.* She had been seven. She had not thought of it again until now, wading shin-deep across what had once been the mere's bed, and she understood suddenly that her father had known about the covenant's decay decades before anyone else dared speak of it. That he had known, and stayed at his post on the border anyway, filing reports no one read, keeping faith with a thing already dying.

She missed him so violently in that moment that she stumbled, and August caught her elbow without comment, and she hated him a little for how easily he did it.

* * *

The chapel doors stood open to the flood. Inside, the water lay black and still, knee-deep and mirror-flat, holding the ruined ceiling upside down in perfect reflection so that Isolde could not tell, for a moment, whether they entered a chapel or sank into one. Rain dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a metronome. Wooden pews floated in loose flotillas, bumped softly against one another like cattle.

"Careful," August said. "Smugglers loved drowned chapels. Where there's water, there's usually a cache, and where there's a cache—"

"There are often traps," Serahin finished. "Yes. You've mentioned your family's philosophy of architecture."

"It's served me alive so far."

Isolde went in first anyway, because she could not bear the sight of both of them laboring, and because — though she would not have said it aloud — she wanted to be the one who found something. It was a small, ugly want, and she examined it as she waded between the pews, turning it over like a coin of dubious minting. Since Gallowmere bridge, since Bram's hands had been torn from August's on the far bank, she had watched August carry his guilt like a second wound. She could not carry it for him. But she could find dry tinder. She could find a safe corner. She could do *something* that was not simply enduring, which was all her grief had ever qualified her for.

The water deepened toward the altar. The chapel had been built on a slope, she realized, and the sanctuary end had settled over the years, sinking below the floodline so that the altar itself stood submerged to its knees in black glass. Candles long since melted to stubs clung to iron prickets like pale fungi. And beneath the water, faint as a watermark, she could see stone flags carved with writing.

"August." Her voice came out strange. "Come here. Bring the lantern."

He came, holding the shuttered lamp low over the water, and the light spread across the flooded floor like honey poured on slate. The carvings resolved out of the dark: lines of text cut into flagstones, worn by centuries of feet and flood, but legible. Not scripture. Not any liturgy she knew from her father's books.

*I, Marta of the Ford, bind my word to the road's keeping. Let my hand stay honest while the way stands.*

Below it, another hand, another century:

*I, Aldous Wick, tanner, bind my word. My children after me, and theirs.*

And another. And another. Dozens of them, hundreds perhaps, each flag a single oath, names of farmers and ferrymen and midwives and one entry that was only *the miller's boy, who could not write his name, and marked it thus* — followed by a crude pressed thumbprint chiseled into the stone.

Isolde knelt down in the floodwater without feeling the cold of it. "These aren't royal records," she whispered. "This isn't a coronation oath. This is—"

"People," August said quietly. "Just people."

Serahin had reached the edge of the sanctuary. He stood with one hand braced on a pew-back, staring down at the flags, and his expression was one Isolde would remember for the rest of her life: a man looking at the face of someone he had loved in a crowd two hundred years after the funeral. "I never knew," he said. "We were taught — I was *taught* — that the covenant was sworn between the first human king and the Twilight Regent. Blood of thrones to blood of thrones. The whole architecture of the courts rests on that teaching. The regents claim custodianship because—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Because custodianship is power. And these people gave away their oath to whoever held it next, generation after generation, never asking anything back but the road."

"The Heartstone," Isolde said slowly. The thought was assembling itself in her chest with the terrible click of a lock finding its key. "If the covenant is a web of common oaths, then the Heartstone isn't a throne's anchor. It's a — a keystone. It holds because thousands of small promises press against it from underneath. Vess thinks breaking it frees Thornfell from the fae. But if the oaths were never the crown's to begin with—"

"Then severing the road doesn't free her kingdom," August finished. "It orphans it. Both realms starve, exactly like the map says. And nobody — no queen, no guild, no usurper — has the right to break promises other people made freely." He looked down at the drowned names, and Isolde watched something move through his face, grief and recognition braided tight. "That's what the Cranes were, originally. Did you know that? Before we were smugglers, before we were debts and ledgers — my grandmother said we started as road-wardens on the Sigil Road. Oathkeepers. We fell so far we forgot what we fell from."

"You haven't fallen as far as you think," Serahin said.

Before August could answer, Isolde's boot struck something under the water — wood, heavy, square-edged. She reached down into the black cold and pulled it up streaming: an iron-banded chest, small enough to carry, its lock long since rusted open. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work faithfully for a century, lay books.

Not one book. Eleven of them. Ledgers, she saw immediately, in the way she recognized ledgers the way August did — by their spine-cracks and thumb-worn corners. But when she unwrapped the first volume and opened it on a floating pew-lid, the pages were not accounts of contraband. They were registers. Names, dates, parishes. *Sworn at the spring equinox, witnessed by two keepers.* Page after page after page, centuries thick, of ordinary people binding themselves to the road's keeping.

"The chapel kept the rolls," she breathed. "Father always said there was a mother-archive for the border oaths, somewhere in the lowlands, lost to the flood. Everyone assumed the crown archives in Corvenne held the true record. But this — this is it. This is the actual covenant, written down in farmhands' hands." Her eyes were burning and she realized distantly that she was crying, that she had been crying since the miller's boy's thumbprint, and she did not care. "August. Do you understand what this means? The answer was never blood. It was never some lost royal line, never a king's sacrifice, never any of the things Vess is afraid of or the regent demands. It's *this*. Ordinary people keeping faith. It always was."

* * *

They made camp in the gallery above the floodline, where a rotted choir loft still held enough floor to sleep on, and August coaxed a fire from pew-wood and the last of their dry tinder. The rain played its endless patient music on the broken roof. Serahin slept — truly slept, for the first time since the wayshrine — with his head against a hymnal stand, his breathing shallow but even, color creeping back into his skin by firelight degrees.

Isolde sat with the ledgers spread around her and read.

She read for hours. She could not have said why it mattered so much to touch every page, only that it did — that these were her father's people too, in a way her father had never explained and she had never asked about. Magistrates swore the same oaths as ostlers. In the oldest volume the ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, and the handwriting wandered and looped, and once, beside a name dated four hundred years past, someone had written a small annotation: *swore with a broken arm and would not wait for it to heal.* Isolde laughed aloud at that, a startled, wet sound, and August looked up from the watch he was pretending to keep.

"What?"

"Someone four centuries ago broke their arm, walked through a storm, and insisted on swearing their oath anyway rather than reschedule." She turned the book so he could see, careful of the wet-dark pages. "There's no note about who they were. No title. Just that someone wrote down that they came."

August was quiet a long moment. Firelight moved in his eyes. "My uncle always said inheritance was what you could carry," he said finally. "Coin, routes, the name. He never once said it might be something like this." He nodded at the ledgers, at the drowned floor below with its thousand small promises. "Something you're trusted to continue rather than entitled to own."

"That frightens you," Isolde said. It was not a question. She had learned to hear the fear under his pragmatism; it sounded like inventory, like listing what could be lost.

"Yes," he said simply, and she loved him — not romantically, or not only, but in the harder way — for not dressing it up. "A name you inherit can fail and it's not quite your fault. Faith you choose? If I take this seriously, then everything Bram is suffering right now is because of a promise I decided to keep. Everything Serahin's lost. Everything you gave up at the tollgate." His hands curled closed. "Crowns make wars over blood and it's almost clean, in its way. This is messier. This says every person who ever kept a small promise helped hold up the sky, and every person who breaks one helps pull it down, and there's nowhere to hide in the middle."

"Good," Isolde said.

He blinked at her.

"I spent my whole life watching powerful people explain why their promises didn't apply to them," she said. "My father was the only magistrate on the border who filed honest reports, and they buried those reports in a drawer for thirty years, and he kept writing them anyway. I used to think he was a fool. Now—" Her voice caught. She let it. "Now I think he was one flagstone in a floor. And the floor held for a thousand years because of people exactly like him, and it's failing now because everyone forgot the floor was ever made of anything but kings." She looked at the sleeping knight, at the exhausted smuggler, at the eleven drowned-and-rescued books that proved mercy older than memory. "Let the messiness win. It's the only thing that ever actually worked."

August smiled — a real one, small and tired, the first she'd seen reach his eyes since Gallowmere. "You'd have made a terrifying magistrate."

"I intend to be a terrifying something."

Outside, the rain changed its note.

It was a subtle thing — the metronome drip from the roof stuttering, then stopping altogether, though the downpour beyond the walls continued. Isolde felt it before she understood it: a pressure in her ears, like the moment before thunder. August was already on his feet, dousing the fire with a kicked-over water flask, and Serahin's eyes were open, silver and fully awake, which frightened her more than any sound could have.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"The road remembers," Serahin said, and there was something in his voice she had never heard there before — awe, or dread, or both wearing the same face. "Those words your stranger died saying. I thought they were a warning about the decay. But the road is not only dying, Isolde. It is *searching*. It has found its rolls again, its sworn keepers, all in one room." He rose unsteadily, and pointed down through the dark at the flooded nave, where the black water had begun — impossibly, gently — to glow, threadlines of light spreading along the carved flagstones like veins remembering blood. Every name. Every oath. Lit from below.

And rising from the water's surface, faint as breath on a window, came a sound none of them could mistake for the rain: footsteps, many footsteps, walking a road that ran straight through the drowned chapel floor — and among them, unmistakable, a voice calling a name.

Calling *Isolde*, in her father's voice.
