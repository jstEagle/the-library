## 12. Ambush at the Split

The Sigil Road forked at a place the old maps called the Weeping Stones, and the map in August's hands had stopped pretending to be a map.

It had become something closer to weather. The ink shifted like cloud-shadow over hills; roads appeared where no road could be and vanished before you could commit them to memory. For three days now it had shown two paths leading out of the high pass — one descending west toward the river valleys, broad and easy, the kind of road built for armies and supply carts. The other climbed east into the teeth of the mountains, narrow as a knife-cut, half-swallowed by scree.

The map wanted them to go east. August was sure of it, the way he was sure of a current under still water. The eastern path pulsed faintly when he looked at it, like a vein.

The problem was that Bram's message said otherwise.

*Take the western fork,* his uncle had written, in a hand that shook only slightly more than usual. *Vess's column is massing at Gallowmere. They'll expect you to climb. Go low, go fast, reach the Heartstone road by dark.* It had come by way of a tinker, a tinker who owed the Cranes from thirty years back, sealed with the guild's broken sigil pressed into grey wax.

Isolde hadn't trusted it. Serahin hadn't either — though the fae knight expressed distrust differently, standing at the fork with his head tilted as if listening to something beneath the stones, his grey eyes gone distant and cold.

"He is alive," Serahin said quietly. "Your uncle. I can tell you that much. But the letter—" He touched the wax seal with one long finger, and flinched. "There is iron in this seal. Iron ground fine, mixed into the wax. Someone wanted to hurt me for touching it."

August stared at him. "Bram wouldn't—"

"Bram might not know what he carries," Serahin said. "Or he might know exactly." He turned his face east, toward the climbing dark. "The road itself wants the high path. And the road, whatever else it is, has never once lied to us."

They argued for an hour in the wind. Isolde wanted to trust Bram because trusting Bram meant her father's friend was still fighting. August wanted to trust Bram because Bram had taught him every pass in Thornfell, had carried him on his shoulders through the marsh country, had been the last warm thing left of his childhood. Trusting the letter was easier than believing his uncle might be a hook baited with love.

In the end they split the difference, which is how smugglers say *we are about to do something stupid*. They would take the eastern road — but slowly, carefully, watching their backs.

Which meant, August would think later, that they walked into the trap with their eyes open, and walked in anyway, because the map said so and because hope is its own kind of blindness.

* * *

The eastern path climbed through a country of black stone and white lichen, where the wind came down off the peaks carrying a smell like struck flint. The covenant's fraying was worse here. August felt it in his teeth. Whole stretches of the mountainside flickered — not vanished, but *hesitated*, as if the world couldn't quite decide to keep existing. A stream they crossed ran backward for ten paces, then thought better of it. Once, Isolde's shadow detached from her boots entirely and lay pointing the wrong direction for a full minute before slinking back, embarrassed.

"Serahin." She'd been quiet most of the day. Now she fell into step beside the knight. "When we get through this. When the covenant holds again. What happens to you? To your exile?"

Serahin walked a few paces before answering. "If the old court endures, my sentence stands. If the covenant reforges, perhaps the law that exiled me reforges with it." A pause. "I have not let myself want that. Wanting is a wound you give yourself in advance."

"And if it doesn't hold?" August asked. "If we fail?"

"Then wanting will be the least of anyone's wounds."

That was when August heard the sound.

At first he took it for wind in the scree — that dry whispering rush of loose stone settling. But stone doesn't settle uphill. He raised a hand, and the other two stilled, and for a moment there was nothing but the wind and his own heartbeat counting out the silence.

Then the ridge above them bloomed with lantern-light.

Not lanterns. *Eyes.* Hollow Guild hunters wore masks of lacquered bone, and behind each mask burned a small cold light, and there were a dozen of them at least, spilling over the crest of the slope like water finding every crack at once. They didn't shout. That was the worst part. Hollow men never shouted. They came down the mountain in perfect silence, ropes and hooks and blades, closing off the path ahead and behind with the unhurried efficiency of people who had done this many times and enjoyed none of it and all of it.

"Hollow Guild," Isolde breathed. "But Vess's forces took the western fork—"

"They sent their own hounds on the high road anyway." Serahin drew his blade, and the steel sang a note that made the flickering air around them steady, just slightly, as if the world leaned toward the sound. "A hedge against failure. Or a leash on their own queen's ambitions. Either way—" His gaze swept the narrowing ground. "Either way, here they are."

August's mind did what it always did under pressure: it went flat and practical. Three of them. A dozen hunters. Ground that funneled — the path here ran between a wall of black stone and a drop into cloud, maybe eight feet wide, narrowing further up where a spur of rock jutted out like a prow.

Eight feet wide. One defender could hold eight feet wide, briefly, if the defender were very good or very willing to die.

He looked at Serahin. Serahin was already looking at the spur.

"No," August said.

"You haven't heard the plan yet."

"I don't need to hear it. I can see you building it behind your eyes. No."

"The road narrows at the spur," Serahin said, calm as a man discussing the price of oats. "One sword can make that ground cost more than a dozen lives can pay. If all three of us stand there, we die together in about four minutes. If one stands, the other two live past four minutes, and living past four minutes is the entire strategy of everything we have done since the ford." He rolled his shoulders once, and August saw — really saw — the old discipline settle over him like armor going on plate by plate. "I am the best swordsman in this company by a margin that would embarrass us all to discuss. Therefore I am the one who stands."

"We could run," Isolde said. Her voice cracked on it. "Back down—"

"They've cut the lower path already. Look." And she looked, and August looked, and yes — far below, threading through the mist, more lights. Not hurrying. Hollow men never hurried. They knew the shape of a trap they'd built.

"There is a crevice," Serahin went on, "forty paces beyond the spur. Big enough for two, too small for armored men to follow quickly. You take Isolde, you take the map, you get into it, and you do not come out. Whatever you hear. Whatever you—" His voice did something almost imperceptible. "Whatever you hear me do, you do not come out."

"I'm not leaving you," August said, and hated how young his voice sounded.

"Yes, you are." Serahin smiled then, and it was the first time August had ever seen him do it, and it was terrible and bright as frost. "You're leaving me because in a flooded chapel you read records older than any kingdom, and you understood what they said. Faith freely given. Nobody ordered me to stand on that spur, Warden-to-be. I am giving this. Let it cost me what it costs. That is the whole of the covenant, isn't it? What we learned in the drowned dark?"

The hunters were fifty yards away and closing, silent as falling snow.

"August." Isolde's hand found his sleeve. Her knuckles were white. "We can't win this fight. We can only lose it slower."

Everything in him screamed otherwise. Everything in him wanted to plant his feet beside the knight and spend himself gloriously and stupidly, because that was easier than walking away, easier than carrying the weight of someone else's sacrifice up a mountain in the dark. Running away from his family name had nearly killed him once. Running toward a found family, he discovered, was harder still — because family means having things you cannot afford to lose, and he was learning, right there on the black stone with death coming down the slope, exactly what his was worth.

He took the map from inside his coat and pressed it into Serahin's hands.

"What—"

"So they take it off your body and follow it into the fraying," August said. "It shows false roads to people it doesn't trust. It trusts you. Make it lie for us."

For a heartbeat Serahin simply looked at him. Then he laughed — a short, startled sound, genuine as a struck bell — and took the map. "Smuggler's cunning. Your uncle taught you well after all." He stepped back, blade coming up into a guard August didn't recognize, something old and formal, a stance from before Thornfell had a name. "Forty paces. Run. Don't look back."

August ran. He looked back anyway. He would carry it forever, and he knew it even then.

* * *

What happened on the spur, August saw in pieces, between lungfuls of thin air and the hammering of his own cowardice and courage fighting for control of his legs.

He saw Serahin meet the first three hunters at once, and the world seemed to contract around the knight's blade — each parry ringing like a chapel bell, each counterstroke precise as a signature. A hunter went off the edge without a sound. Another folded around the sword's point and slid down the rock wall, leaving a long dark smear.

He saw the fourth hunter get past, and Isolde turn on it with her father's hunting knife, screaming — actually screaming, all her magistrate's-daughter composure burned away — driving the man back with pure fury until August reached them and put a smuggler's sap behind the hunter's ear and the man dropped like wet rope.

He saw the crevice, a black seam in the mountain's flank, and shoved Isolde into it, and squeezed in after her, into air that smelled of deep stone and old ice.

And then he saw the rest through a gap the width of two fingers, and he could not stop seeing it, ever, for the rest of his life.

They stopped trying to duel him. Six of them converged at once, and Serahin gave ground along the spur, giving it inch by inch, making every inch cost. A blade opened his thigh. He killed the man who'd done it. A hook caught his cloak and hauled him half off balance and he turned the stumble into a strike that took another hunter's mask — and the light behind it — clean away. Blood sheeted down his sword arm. He shifted the grip. Kept fighting left-handed. August had seen soldiers train all their lives and never once look like that: like the sword was not a tool but a sentence being finished.

"Go," Serahin shouted, to the mountain, to them, to no one. "The road remembers! *Make it remember you!*"

The seventh hunter used a crossbow.

No honor in it. No drama. Just a bolt from twelve feet, punching through the knight's side below the ribs, and Serahin staggered, and the spur's edge was right there, and for one endless moment he teetered on it, blood-dark, upright, refusing.

Then he went over — deliberately, August realized later; he *chose* the fall rather than be taken — dropping into the cloud below with the map clutched to his chest, and the wind came up out of the gorge a moment afterward carrying a sound that might have been the fae battle-cry of a dead court, or might have been the mountain itself grieving.

August's knees hit stone. He didn't remember deciding to fall. Isolde's arms were around him, her mouth against his ear saying *no, no, no*, except it wasn't words anymore, just grief with the language boiled off it.

Above them, the hunters gathered at the spur's edge. Peered down into the white. Waited.

Then one of them — the tallest, with a crack running through his bone mask — held up a fist, and they turned, and began to descend the mountain after the fallen knight instead of searching the crevice. After the body. After the map.

Because the map mattered more than two fugitives who might freeze in a crack in the world.

August held Isolde and shook, and the shaking went on a long time, and somewhere in the middle of it he understood something with a clarity that felt like a wound opening: this was the price. This was what the drowned chapel's records had promised in their careful old script. *Faith freely given costs what it costs.* Found family was not a soft thing, not a shelter, not a warm fire to replace the cold hearth of blood and inheritance. It was a debt you entered knowing the interest. It was a knight choosing a cliff so that a smuggler and a runaway could live to finish a road.

And it was worth it. God help him, even kneeling in the dark with his friend's blood still bright in his eyes, some stubborn coal in his chest insisted: *worth it.*

"August." Isolde's voice, raw and small. "The bolt. The fall. He can't have—"

"He chose the fall," August said. His voice came out sanded flat. "He chose where to fall. Serahin doesn't do anything without choosing it." He pulled back and looked at her, and made himself say the thing he believed rather than the thing he feared. "The gorge feeds the river system. Smuggler's country. I know those waters. If anyone could survive that drop, it's a man who's already survived his whole court giving up on him."

She searched his face for a lie and didn't find one, mostly because it wasn't one. Hope, in the crevice's freezing dark, was a decision they made together, deliberately, the way you decide to keep breathing.

* * *

They climbed through the night.

Above the crevice the path narrowed to a goat-track, then to less than that, and twice the fraying nearly ended them — a stretch of trail simply absent, hanging in a shimmer of unreality, forcing August to lead them across on footholds he remembered from a route he'd run at seventeen, praying the mountain's memory matched his own. The covenant's sickness was visible now to the naked eye: stars flickering overhead like guttering lamps, the moon showing two different phases depending on which eye you closed.

Near dawn they came over a shoulder of the mountain and saw, far below and behind, the western fork — and the smoke.

Vess's column, thousands strong, marching confidently down the valley road that led, according to a map that lied to everyone it distrusted, directly to the Heartstone. Directly into forty miles of terrain that no longer existed. August watched the distant dust of the usurper's army committing itself, wholly and proudly, to a road made of wishful thinking.

"It worked," Isolde whispered. "Bram's tip, the false roads, all of it. It actually worked."

"It worked," August agreed. And then, because grief makes honest men of us all: "It cost a knight."

They descended toward a high tarn where the map showed water and, near the water, a thread of silver that might have been a road and might have been a promise. August told himself stories the whole way down — that Serahin had washed ashore, that fae knights healed like fae knights, that the map had chosen him for a reason and the road did not spend its chosen lightly. He told the stories because Isolde needed to hear them, and because he needed to say them aloud to make them true enough to walk on.

They rounded the tarn as the sun came up, gilding the black water, and August saw the tracks first — boot prints in the mud, uneven, dragging one leg. Faun-blooded prints, too long for human feet, leading away from the shoreline toward a cleft in the rocks where something pale glinted.

Armor. Discarded breastplate, dented, dark with dried blood. And beside it, propped against the stone with a broken arrow still jutting from below his ribs and his skin grey as ash, sat Serahin — alive, barely, conscious by what looked like sheer refusal to do otherwise.

His eyes found August's. The ghost of the frost-smile crossed his lips.

"Took you long enough," the knight rasped. "I've been holding the door."
