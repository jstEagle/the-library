## 15. Court of Ash and Ivy

The door in the twilight had no hinges.

Isolde understood that first, before she understood anything else about the fae court — that the threshold they had earned with their spoken debts was not a door at all, but a decision the world had made long ago and simply never unmade. It stood alone in a meadow of grey grass, an archway of black stone furred with ivy that moved against the wind, or perhaps *was* the wind. Beyond it there was no path, only a slow deepening of dusk, as though someone had poured evening into the land like water into a cup and let it settle.

August went through first. Of course he did. Isolde had learned enough of him by now to know he would never ask anyone to walk into danger he hadn't tested with his own boots, though he'd sooner swallow his own tongue than admit that was why. Serahin followed, moving stiffly, his wounded side bound in linen Isolde had cut from her own underskirt two days ago. The knight's glamour had thinned to almost nothing; she could see now what she had only glimpsed in the wayshrine — the ash-grey cast of his skin, the way his eyes caught light like a cat's but duller, banked, a fire burned down to its last coals.

Then it was her turn, and Isolde Vance, who had run from her father's house with nothing but a stolen horse and a head full of accusations, stepped out of her own world for the first time in her life.

She had expected splendor.

That was the humiliating part, afterward — how much she had expected splendor. She had grown up on border stories, magistrate's daughter that she was, tales of the twilight court told by nurses and nervous farmers: halls of living glass, thrones of woven moonlight, ladies whose gowns were made of moth-wing and rumor. Her father had believed none of it, of course. He had believed in ledgers and boundary stones and the dull arithmetic of tolls. But some childish corner of her had kept faith with the stories, and so when the archway released them into the court itself, she was not prepared for what she found.

Ash. And ivy.

The court was a garden that had given up. Terraces of pale stone climbed a hillside under a sky the color of old pewter, and every terrace was overgrown — not wildly, not riotously, but *tiredly*, the way a house goes to seed when the last servant has stopped caring. Ivy everywhere, yes, but ivy gone woody and sparse, its leaves grey-edged. Fountains stood dry, their basins cracked along fault lines too fine to be weather. Lanterns hung from wrought-iron branches along the walks, and each one held a flame no larger than a thumbnail, burning without warmth, without smoke, without any apparent will to go on.

And the people — the *folk* — were worse than the garden. They lined the terraces as the three travelers climbed, silent, watching. Some were beautiful in ways that hurt to look at directly: cheekbones like cut glass, hair like poured ink or spun frost. Others had let themselves go strange, bark showing through the skin of their forearms, antlers bleached soft as chalk, mouths that opened a half-second behind their words. None of them were young. That was the thing Isolde couldn't stop seeing. Fae were supposed to be ageless, and these were — technically, horribly ageless, faces frozen at whatever age they'd chosen centuries ago — but frozen is not the same as preserved, and everything here had the look of fruit left too long on the branch. Beauty going over. Elegance curdling into endurance.

"They're starving," Serahin said quietly. It was the first time Isolde had heard him sound anything other than guarded since the wayshrine. "Not for bread. For the covenant's flow. The road fed this place, all the small crossings and exchanges between realms — milk for songs, iron for blessings, name for name. Cut the flow, and..." He gestured, a small bitter motion at the whole grey hillside. "This."

A herald came down to meet them. Isolde would have called him a man if pressed, tall and silver-robed, but his shadow fell the wrong way, leaning east when the light came from the east, and when he spoke his voice arrived slightly before his mouth moved.

"The regent will see the debt-bearers," he said. His gaze passed over August, then Serahin — and lingered there, and something like distaste flickered across his perfect face. "All three. Even the exile." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "How brave of him to come home."

"I didn't come home," Serahin said. "I came to work."

The herald smiled without any muscle below his eyes participating. "They all say that, at the end."

* * *

The regent received them in a hall that had once been magnificent and now merely insisted on it.

Pillars of white wood rose to a vaulted ceiling where constellations had been set like jewels — real stars, Isolde realized with a lurch, actual captured lights, though half of them had gone dark and the rest flickered like guttering candles. The throne at the hall's end was living oak, roots plunging through the floor into whatever lay beneath, branches grown up and around into a high-backed seat. Moss grew thick along one armrest and had been trimmed away from the other, recently, by hand, by someone who still cared about appearances if nothing else.

The regent sat upon it sideways, one leg crossed over the other, which somehow made the whole throne look less like a seat of power and more like a piece of furniture someone was waiting to be rid of.

She was old. Not elderly — fae did not do elderly — but *old* the way a river is old, the way winter is old. Her hair was white as birchwood and fell to her waist unbound, and her face had the terrible smoothness of something worn down rather than aged. She wore grey silk shot through with threads of green so dark they read as black, ivy embroidered at hem and collar in a pattern that, Isolde noticed, was fraying. Someone had tried to re-stitch it. The new thread was a slightly wrong shade of green.

"Serahin of the Ninth Gate." The regent's voice filled the hall without effort, the way cold fills a valley. "My knights broke their oaths for lesser causes and were merely maimed. You defended a dying covenant against your own queen's design, and you got exile. Tell me, little knight — does the injustice of it keep you warm at night?"

"It keeps me moving, lady."

"Mm." Her eyes — pale as frost on a window, and just as easy to see through and just as hard to open — slid to August. "And this is the human. The map-bearer. We felt him coming for six days. Like a pebble in a shoe." She rose, unhurried, and descended the three steps from the throne, circling them slowly, and Isolde felt the weight of her attention pass over each of them like a searchlight. When it settled on Isolde herself, it stopped.

"You," the regent said. "You have a magistrate's jaw. Thornfell border stock. Your people drew the first boundary stones, four hundred years ago, and charged the fae a toll to cross them. Tolls." Something moved in her face that was not quite a smile. "Your kind has always been very good at counting what you owe us and very bad at paying it."

"That isn't fair," Isolde began.

"No," the regent agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Fairness is a human invention. We had covenants instead, and yours broke them." She turned away, back toward the throne, trailing birch-white hair. "So. You have walked my dying road, you carry its living map, and you have come to ask the Court of Ash and Ivy to march its last able bodies to the Heartstone to defend a bond your queens have spent three generations sawing through. Have I summarized your petition correctly, smuggler?"

August didn't flinch. Isolde watched him not-flinch and marveled at it, because she could see what it cost — the set of his shoulders, the careful evenness of his breathing, the smuggler's trick of looking relaxed while calculating exits. There were no exits here. He knew it. He stood in the middle of a starving court's hall with his hands loose at his sides anyway.

"You've summarized it," August said. "Though I'd argue one point. It wasn't your covenant they broke. It was everyone's. The drowned chapel records—"

"Yes, the chapel." The regent waved a hand, and the gesture carried a wind that smelled of cold stone. "We know of it. Common folk swearing freely, oaths between neighbors, milk and salt and song. Very touching. Do you know what we call those oaths here?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Kindling. Small warm things that burn fast and leave nothing. A covenant needs *weight*, human. Blood-weight. Crown-weight. Generations of binding so heavy neither side can lift it and walk away. Your grandmother's promise of honey at midwinter could not hold two realms together if both realms *wanted* to be held. And they don't. Yours wants cheap iron and full granaries. Mine—" her voice frayed, just barely, just for a word — "mine wants to stop being hungry. Why should I spend what's left of my court on a bond both sides have already abandoned?"

"Because it hasn't been abandoned." Isolde hadn't planned to speak. The words came out of her the way her father's had come out of him in the magistrate's hall — flat, border-country blunt, the tone that ended arguments in her household. Every eye in the room turned to her, and she discovered she didn't care. "I've read the records. I've walked the road. People are *still* keeping faith out there — hedge-wives leaving cream at the boundary stones, ferry-men singing the crossing-songs to no one, my own father spending his last year investigating Queen Vess because he thought the covenant mattered more than his career. It cost him his life." Her voice caught on that, and she let it, deliberately, because the truth of it deserved to be heard raw. "You want crown-weight? Crowns are exactly the problem. Vess is a crown. Hollow Guild gold is a crown's money. Everything that's killing the road wears a coronet. Maybe the answer was never up there." She pointed, absurdly, at the ceiling, at the dying captive stars. "Maybe it was always down at the ford and the fence-line and the kitchen door. Ordinary people keeping ordinary faith. Which is apparently *kindling* to you, except kindling is what actually catches fire, my lady. Thrones don't burn. They rot."

Silence. Somewhere above, a star guttered and went out, and the darkness of it seemed to spread like ink.

The regent looked at her for a long moment. "A runaway noble girl lecturing a regent on the dignity of commoners," she said at last. "Thornfell must be stranger than I remembered." But something in her voice had shifted — not warmth, nothing so generous, but the pause of a chess player who has just realized the board contains a piece she hadn't counted.

It was August who ended it.

He reached into his coat and drew out the map.

Isolde had seen it a hundred times by now — the wax seal broken, the vellum soft at the folds from handling, the roads that crawled and redrew themselves like veins under skin. But she had never seen August hold it the way he held it now: out before him, both hands, the way a man offers something on an altar. Or a blade.

"This is the Sigil Road," he said. "Not a copy of it. Not a record. The living thing itself, or the last living piece of it. It moves because the covenant moves. It dies because the covenant dies. If we fail at the Heartstone, this goes grey forever — and so does everything it touched." He turned slowly, letting the whole court see it, and Isolde watched the watching folk press closer along the walls despite themselves, drawn like moths, like mourners. "You asked why you should spend what's left of your court on a bond both sides abandoned. Here's my answer: don't. Don't spend it on the bond. Spend it as collateral."

The regent's frost-pale eyes narrowed. "Collateral."

"A wager." August's voice stayed level, but Isolde, standing close enough to see his hands, saw the tremor he was holding still through sheer stubbornness. "If we reach the Heartstone and fail, the covenant breaks, and this map dies with it — you lose nothing, because there'll be nothing left to lose. But if we succeed — if the covenant holds — then the road lives, and the road belongs to whoever reforged it. So I'm offering it to you. The Court of Ash and Ivy becomes co-warden of the renewed Sigil Road. First claim on every crossing, every toll, every exchange between realms. Not charity. Investment." He paused, and then he did the thing Isolde would remember for the rest of her life, the moment she would retell to wardens not yet born until it wore smooth as a river stone:

He held the map out toward the regent. Toward her actual hands.

"And if you think I'm lying," August Crane said, "take it now. Hold the road itself in trust until we prove ourselves. If we die on the way, your court keeps it. If we break our word, your court keeps it. I'm betting everything my family ever built on a promise no one on either side of the mountains has kept in three hundred years." A breath. "Seems only fair the other side puts up something too. March with us."

The silence that followed had texture. Isolde felt it press against her eardrums. Along the walls, the fae had gone utterly still — and she understood, suddenly, with the clarity that arrives only in the worst moments, what August had actually done. He hadn't offered collateral. He had offered *insult wrapped in gift*. No human in the history of the border had ever put something this precious into fae hands and trusted them with it, because trusting fae with treasure was the oldest fool's story in Thornfell, the cautionary tale mothers used to keep children from the woods. He had stood in their starving hall and bet his inheritance on *them*. On their honor. On the honor everyone said they'd lost.

And if the regent refused — if she sneered, or took the map and gave nothing — then every soul in this hall would know, forever, that the humans had offered faith and the fae had been the ones afraid to accept it.

It was brilliant. It was insane. Isolde wanted to kiss him and shake him in equal measure.

The regent descended the steps again. Slowly. She stopped before August, close enough that Isolde could see the fine grain of her skin like paper held too near a flame, and she looked down at the map in his outstretched hands for a long, long moment.

"Do you know," she said softly, "what the last human who stood in this hall offered us?"

"No, lady."

"An apology. Three hundred years ago. King Aldous the Third sent an ambassador to *apologize* for the toll-stones, with gifts and fine words, and asked in return only that we forgive the debt and forget the insult." Her lips thinned. "We would have forgiven it. That was the horror of us, then — we were still young enough to be generous. But the ambassador took fright at our faces and fled before dawn, and the apology never got spoken, and the forgetting never happened, and everything after followed from that night like water following a crack." She lifted her eyes to August's. "You stand where he stood. You offer more than he offered. And you offer it to a court that has been hungry so long we have forgotten what it is to be trusted."

"Lady," August said quietly, "I'm not offering because I'm sure of you. I'm offering because somebody has to go first, and I'm tired of waiting for a better person to show up."

Something broke in the regent's face. Not visibly — her composure held like ice holds, all the way to the instant it doesn't — but Isolde saw it, the fracture line running through three hundred years of grievance, and she saw the regent choose, deliberately, not to look at it.

"If you fail," the regent said, "your name becomes a lesson taught to fae children. *The smuggler who gambled the road.*"

"Fair," August said.

"And if you succeed, the Court of Ash and Ivy will hold first claim on the crossings of the new Sigil, and your heirs' heirs will curse the day you bargained away their birthright."

"Probably," August agreed. "But I'm not planning to have heirs. I'm planning to have *people*." He shrugged, that smuggler's shrug Isolde had come to know, the one that made light of things that weren't light. "Different ledger."

For the first time — Isolde would swear to this, later, to anyone who doubted her — the Regent of Ash and Ivy laughed. It was a dry sound, rust and paper, a laugh that had not been used in years and had to be dug out like a tool from a shed. But it was real.

"Serahin of the Ninth Gate," she said, turning. "You will guide the human's host through the Fraying, since you wrote half its maps before your treason. The muster leaves when the second dusk falls — we have two dusks here, and only one of them is fit for marching. Ration what strength you have left, knight. You will need all of it." Her gaze swung to Isolde, and the frost in it had thawed exactly one degree. "Magistrate's daughter. You will ride beside me. Anyone reckless enough to lecture a regent in her own hall is either a fool or an advisor, and I am short on advisors."

Isolde bowed — shallow, border-style, the bow her father had taught her for equals — and had the rare satisfaction of watching the regent *notice* it.

"And smuggler." The regent paused at the foot of the throne, her hand resting on the living oak as if steadying an old friend. "Keep your map. A wager isn't a wager if the stake changes hands early. But understand what you have done tonight." She looked around her hall — the dead lanterns, the darkened stars, the beautiful starving faces of her people — and her voice dropped low enough that only the four of them could hear it.

"You have made hope a matter of contract. If you break it now, it will not merely fail. It will *owe*."

Outside the hall, through high windows gone grey with the coming of the second dusk, Isolde saw the first banners unfurling — ragged banners, ivy-wound, borne by skeletons of honor guard — and beneath them, gathering in the ruined courtyards, the last army of the fae mustering for a war they believed they could not win.

Two dusks, she thought, until the Fraying. And somewhere beyond the mountains, at the Heartstone, Queen Vess was already counting down faster than they were.
