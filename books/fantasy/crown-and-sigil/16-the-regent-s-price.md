## 16. The Regent's Price

I had forgotten what it was to kneel in a court that still had a ceiling.

The throne room of the twilight court had no ceiling anymore — that was the first thing the fraying had taken from them, or perhaps the last thing they had given up willingly, so that their queen of a thousand years ago could watch the stars as she died. What remained was ash-colored ivy climbing pillars of white wood, and banners gone gray as old spider silk, and the regent herself: Lady Maelis of the Autumn Gate, seated not on a throne but on a living stump that had once, I suspected, been a throne tree. Her antlers were crooked with age. Her eyes were the color of the sky an hour past sunset, and they were fixed on August Crane with the patient hunger of a creature deciding whether something was food or threat.

We stood in the center of the hall, the three of us, on a floor of pale roots worn smooth by ten centuries of courtiers who no longer came. Isolde had washed the road's dust from her face at some point I'd missed; her chin was high, her hands folded like a magistrate's daughter delivering a petition. August stood beside her with the map held out in both hands — *offered*, in the oldest grammar of courts, though he probably didn't know he was speaking it. And I knelt, because I had been a knight once, and because kneeling here felt like pressing a bruise to see if it still hurt.

It did.

"Collateral," Maelis said again, tasting the word. Around her, the thin remnant of the court — perhaps forty souls where ten thousand had walked these floors — murmured like wind through dead reeds. "You offer us the Sigil Road itself. The dying covenant-path. As *collateral*."

"It's not dying," August said. "It's starving. There's a difference. Dying things can't be fed. Starving ones can." He turned the map slightly, and I saw what only its bearer could see: the roads beneath the vellum's surface, moving like veins under skin, dimming at the far end near Thornfell and flaring faintly gold near the Heartstone. "Your side of it is still alive. It's the human side that's being cut. Queen Vess has been severing anchor-stones for two years. When she cuts the last one, the road doesn't just die — it tears, and everything on both sides starves together. You know this. Your people feel it every time they try to work magic."

A fae near the pillars made a sound that might have been grief or agreement. Maelis lifted one hand, and the hall went silent.

"I know it," she said. "We have known it while your kind built your smoke-towns over our wellsprings and called our bargains tricks when they proved costly. We have known it since the last Warden died and no human came to swear a successor. Three hundred years, Serahin." Her eyes flicked to me, and I felt the weight of them like a hand on the back of my neck. "Three hundred years since the order that guarded the road let itself dwindle to nothing, and the man who should have kept faith—" she did not say *you*, she did not need to, "—chose instead to help draft its murder."

The hall's murmur turned sharp. Isolde's shoulder pressed against mine — deliberately, I think, a wall offered where a blade would have been justified.

"I have answered for that," I said. "I will answer for more if it comes to it. But the girl is right about what happens if the covenant snaps, and the boy is right about how to save it. My failures don't change the arithmetic, my lady. They only make me the last person who has any right to ask you for anything. So I am asking anyway. Kneeling, even."

"Kneeling suits you poorly," she said, and there was, buried deep, something almost like the old dry humor of the Autumn Gate. "Very well. Let us speak plainly, human, since you have offered collateral. Here is my price."

She rose from the stump. She was not tall, but the hall seemed to rearrange itself around her as she moved, roots shifting to lay a path from dais to floor, and I remembered — with a jolt like cold water — that Maelis had been old when the covenant was young.

"The road cannot be fed by armies. It cannot be fed by blood spilled in battle — we tried that in the Sundering War, and all it bought was graves. The Sigil Road runs on *oath*. Freely given, binding given, sworn by mortal voices into mortal stone. That is what your ancestors understood and your queens forgot. It was never a treaty between crowns. It was ten thousand small promises, kept by farmers and ferrymen and midwives, renewed each generation by ordinary people who chose to keep faith." She stopped before August. Close enough that I could see him fight the instinct to step back, and win. "To renew it now, at this late hour, requires a single oath of unusual weight. The Oath of the Wardens. The first oath, the founding oath, the one that binds the swearer's line to the road until the road ends or the world does."

"His *line*," Isolde said sharply. "Meaning his children. His children's children."

"Meaning everyone who comes after him, forever," Maelis said, without softening it. "They will hear the road in their sleep. They will feel it fray. They will walk its passes in storms and wars and famines, and they will die keeping it more often than not, and no crown will thank them and no song will remember them. That is the price of my march, human child. Not his life tonight — his life, and every life that flows from his. One name, given to the road, until the road releases no one ever again."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on the shoulders.

I looked at August and thought: *he doesn't understand yet.* How could he? He was twenty years old, maybe twenty-one, raised in a guildhall full of debts and lantern-smoke, taught by his uncle that everything has a price and the trick is paying less than the other fellow thinks. An inheritance meant a ledger and a roof and a name. He was hearing *duty* the way a landsman hears *sea* — as a word, not as the thing that gets into your bones and never leaves.

"He hasn't agreed," Isolde said, stepping half in front of him. "There are others. Me. I've claim enough — my father—"

"Your father was a magistrate who kept faith privately, which honors him, but the road knows the difference between a private heart and a public vow," Maelis said. "And you have already paid once today, girl. The tollgate took your estate. The road keeps honest books; it will not demand twice from the same wound." Her gaze returned to August. "Besides. It wants him. It has wanted him since the ford, I think. Roads choose their wardens the way rivers choose their valleys. Slowly. Inevitably."

August hadn't spoken. He was looking down at the map in his hands, and I watched his thumb move across the vellum — over the little inked towns of Thornfell, the river fords, the smuggler's paths he knew better than any tax map — and I saw the moment the word stopped being a word.

I knew that moment. I had lived it, three hundred years ago, standing in a different court with a different offer in my hands, and I had chosen wrong. I had weighed my oath against my fear and found my fear heavier, and I had told myself for three centuries afterward that the scales had been honest.

"August," Isolde said quietly. "You don't have to. Whatever the regent says about roads choosing, you get to choose. That's the whole point of what we're trying to build."

"I know," he said.

And then he laughed — short, cracked, entirely human — and looked up, and his eyes were wet and steady at once.

"My family kept a ledger for six generations," he said. "Every debt owed, every debt owing. My grandfather used to say the ledger was the only honest member of the guild. Bram burned half of it to keep the Hollow Guild from reading our routes, and the creditors took the rest, and I spent this whole road thinking if I could just get back the ledger, get back the hall, get back the *name*, I'd have won something." He turned the map over in his hands. "But this thing's been teaching me the whole way that inheritance isn't what you're given. It's what you take responsibility for. Bram taught me that too, in his way, I just wasn't listening." His jaw tightened. "You want my line bound to the road forever. Fine. But I'm not swearing it as a Crane."

Maelis tilted her head, antlers catching the gray light. "Explain."

"Cranes pay debts. Cranes run cargo, dodge tolls, keep their own skins whole. That's the name I was born to, and it's a good name for what it was, but it isn't what this needs." He drew a breath, and I heard the tremor in it and admired him for breathing through it anyway. "You said the covenant was ten thousand small promises from ordinary people who chose to keep faith. Then that's the line I'll found. No heirloom, no guild seal. Just — whoever comes after me, and chooses, the way I'm choosing now. They inherit the choice. That's all. That's the whole inheritance."

Something moved through the hall then that I had not felt since before my exile: not applause, fae do not applaud, but a settling, like snow sliding off branches — the sound of a very old place deciding to believe something new.

Isolde had gone very still beside me. When I glanced at her, her eyes were bright, and her mouth was pressed into the particular hard line of someone refusing to cry in public, and I understood suddenly why the road had gathered these three specific lost children to itself. We were all of us people whose names had failed us. And he had just done the one thing none of us had managed: refused to be defined by the failure without abandoning the people who shared it.

"You understand what you're giving up," Maelis said. It was not quite a question.

"Everything I was going to get back," August said. "Yeah."

"And what you're taking on?"

"Everything that comes after me." A pause. "Which is either terrifying or the best trade I've ever been offered, and I've made some genuinely terrible trades, so."

Someone in the court — one of the ivy-woven elders — made a sound that was unmistakably, anciently, a laugh.

Maelis studied him a long moment. Then she extended her hand, palm open, and in it lay a knife of white bone with a sigil worked into the flat: the original mark of the road, the interlocking path-and-star that had been carved above every wayshrine between the realms. I had not seen it since the day they struck it from my shield.

"The Oath of the Wardens is not words," she said. "Words are cheap and mortals waste them. The oath is a cutting and a giving. Your blood into the root, your name into the road, freely offered, irrevocably. Once done, it cannot be undone — not by you, not by me, not by regret. Do you hear me, August Crane?"

"I hear you."

"Say what you give."

He knelt. That undid me more than anything else — the boy who had swaggered into a drowned chapel with a stolen chalice and a smuggler's grin, going down onto the root-floor of a fading court with his spine straight and the map laid flat before him like an altar cloth.

"I give my name to the road," he said. "Not the one I was born with — the one I'm building. I give my line, whatever it turns out to be. Everyone who comes after me inherits the keeping of this path, freely, the same way I'm giving it. Freely." His voice caught on the word and he pushed through. "No crown ordered this. No debt forced it. Nobody sold it to me and nobody's buying. I'm choosing. Let the road remember that part especially."

"*Especially*," Maelis repeated softly, and I saw something in her ancient face shift — approval, or relief, or grief for the three hundred years this offer had gone unmade.

She took his hand, turned it palm-up, and set the bone knife's edge against the base of his thumb.

Isolde's breath stopped. My own hand moved toward a sword hilt I no longer carried, uselessly, the old reflex of a bodyguard with nothing left to guard but promises.

"Last chance, warden," Maelis said.

"Do it before I get smart about it," August said.

She cut.

The blood that welled was shockingly red against the bone-white floor, and when it touched the root, the root *drank* — and the drinking traveled. I felt it pass under my knees like a wave under a boat's hull, out along the pale floor-roots, up the ash-ivy pillars, into the gray banners which stirred though there was no wind. Far away — miles, realms away — I felt the Sigil Road shudder and turn over in its long sleep like a sleeper smelling bread.

And the map.

The map in August's bleeding hand lit. Not the dim vein-glow of the past weeks but true gold, lines kindling town by town, ford by ford, the whole living length of it flushing with light like a limb waking from numbness — except at the far western edge, where Thornfell's anchor-stones hung dark, where Vess was working even now. Where we had to go.

"It accepts," Maelis said, and her voice carried to every corner of the ruined hall, and the forty remaining souls of the twilight court bowed as one, antlers and bare heads alike. "The road has its warden. The court marches at dawn."

Then she did something I did not expect. She helped him up. Took his bleeding hand in both of hers, careful of the cut, and raised him like raising a banner.

"I am sorry for the price," she said, quietly enough that only our four ears held it. "I have asked it of mortals before, long ago. I swore I never would again. But the hour is past mercy, and you are the first in three centuries to offer before I finished asking."

"That's the smuggler in me," August said, a little gray around the mouth, cradling his hand. "Always close the deal before the buyer talks themselves out of it."

Isolde took his arm. She didn't say anything — she didn't need to; I watched her press her forehead briefly to his shoulder, one heartbeat's worth of shelter, before stepping back and becoming practical again, asking about supply lines and the western passes. But I saw August hold that heartbeat like a coin he'd never spend.

And I stood apart, as exiles do, and looked at my own empty hands.

Three hundred years ago I had been offered a version of what he had just accepted, and I had run. Tonight I had watched a twenty-year-old debtor with burned hands and no guild and no name left to lose reach out and *take* the weight anyway, freely, laughing at the terror of it — and I finally understood what my failure had cost, because now I had seen the shape of what it could have been.

"Dawn," Maelis said aloud, to the hall. "Rest. Eat. Those of you who remember how to sharpen things — sharpen things." The ghost of the old humor again, and this time several of the fae smiled, and the sound of a court remembering how to hope was the most beautiful and frightening thing I had heard in three hundred years.

Later — much later, in a side chamber lit by lamps of living moths — I found August alone, staring at his bandaged hand and the map glowing on his knee.

"It doesn't feel different," he said, when he saw me. "Shouldn't it feel different? I just married a road."

"It feels different in thirty years," I said. "Or in a storm. Or when someone you love is on the far side of it and you can feel them through the stone." I hesitated. "Or so I'm told. I ran before I learned."

He looked up at me. And there was no judgment in his face, which was somehow worse than judgment, and kinder too.

"You didn't run this time," he said.

"No," I agreed. "This time I get to watch what I chose instead."

He almost smiled. Then his eyes went west, toward the dark edge of the glowing map, where the black patch around the Heartstone had grown visibly since morning — a hole in the light, widening, patient.

"Dawn, then," he said. "Let's go take it back."

Outside, in the ruined court, the horns of the Autumn Gate began to sound — low, cracked, out of practice after three hundred years of silence — calling the host to war.
