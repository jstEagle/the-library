## 17. March Through the Fraying

The Sigil Road had never been meant for an army.

I understood that the moment I saw them gathered at the threshold of the twilight court: three hundred fae in ash-grey mail laced with living ivy, their banners stitched from moth-wing and memory, and behind them perhaps sixty humans — border-folk, smugglers, a handful of Isolde's father's old magistrates who had come when word went out on roads only the desperate traveled. Sixty. It looked like nothing against the dark rolling down from the mountains.

The regent stood at the head of her host, tall as a winter tree, and she did not look at me as she gave the order to march. She looked at the map in my hands, at the way its ink was bleeding off the edges like a wound that wouldn't clot.

"Warden," she said, and the title sat on me like wet wool. "You will walk first. The road knows you now. Where you falter, we all fall."

No pressure there.

Serahin came up beside me, moving stiffly. The wounds from the Split were three days closed and still wrong somehow — his left arm didn't swing when he walked, and the ivy that had been knitted into his skin by the court's healers had gone grey at the edges, like frost creeping across a window. He'd stopped pretending it didn't hurt around the second day. That was worse, somehow. Serahin admitting pain was a barometer reading, and the needle kept falling.

"The road is thinning," he said quietly. "Can you feel it?"

I could. For two weeks the map had been more than paper to me — since the drowned chapel, since I'd learned what it actually was, I'd felt it the way you feel a rope you're holding over someone else's drop. And the rope was fraying. Not evenly. In patches. Like frost on glass, like rot in a beam, like every other kind of ruin I'd ever watched creep through something I loved.

"Yeah," I said. "Let's go before it gets worse."

Isolde fell in on my left, Bram's old knife at her belt because she refused to carry anything of her father's into a fight anymore — too many things of her father's had already been taken. She didn't say anything. None of us did. There's a particular silence that falls between people who've each confessed their worst thing to the others, and ours had settled in somewhere past the Fae Court's Door and hadn't lifted since. It wasn't an unfriendly silence. It was just honest, and honesty is heavy luggage.

We marched.

* * *

For the first hour, the Sigil Road behaved.

It ran east along the spine of the mountains, a causeway of pale stone that no cart had worn and no rain had touched in a thousand years, floating — there's no better word for it — above a valley full of morning mist. On either side, the land it remembered grew up around it like a hedge: orchards that fruited out of season, hedges blooming in frost, little waystones carved with oaths so old the names on them had worn to whispers. This was what the covenant had built. Not walls. Not armies. *Hedges.* A thousand years of ordinary people promising small things to each other — this field to this family, this ford to this ferryman — and the promises piling up until they held the world together.

And then we crossed the first tear.

There was no warning. One step the road was whole; the next, the hedge beside me simply wasn't. Not destroyed — *unwritten*. The orchard trees were still there but they weren't growing anything, weren't doing anything, standing like actors who'd forgotten their lines. An apple hung in the air where a branch should have been holding it. When one of the fae soldiers reached out to touch it, her fingers passed through, and she made a sound I'll hear until I die. Small. Almost polite. The sound of someone finding their own hand has stopped belonging to them.

"Keep moving," Serahin said, loud enough to carry. "Do not touch the unwritten things. Do not speak your name aloud past a tear. The road takes what it can reach."

Behind us, I heard the tear spread. Not fast — patient, like water finding the low places in a floor. I made myself not look back. Looking back was how you lost the rhythm, and the rhythm mattered. I'd figured that out within the first mile: the road responded to us, to our attention, our intention. Walk like you know where you're going and it held firm under your feet. Hesitate, and it softened like wet bread.

So I walked like I knew where I was going, which was the funniest thing about this whole cursed march, because I didn't. Nobody did. The map redrew itself under my thumbs faster than I could read it — the route to the Heartstone bending, doubling back, occasionally erasing itself entirely in long grey strokes like someone dragging a sleeve across wet ink.

"August." Isolde's voice, tight. "The valley."

I looked. Below us, in the mist, a village. Real enough — smoke rising from chimneys, a dog barking, a woman hanging washing. Except the smoke rose sideways. The dog barked without opening its mouth. And the washing on the line was hanging there in perfect stillness while the wind moved everything else — grass, trees, the woman's hair — around it.

"It's caught," Isolde whispered. "Between the old covenant and whatever Vess's breaking has done. It's—"

The village flickered. Once. Twice. Like a lantern running dry.

Then the mist took it, gently, the way you'd pull a sheet over someone sleeping, and when it cleared there was nothing below us at all except a valley that had never held anyone, empty and green and utterly indifferent.

Nobody spoke. Three hundred fae and sixty humans walked on in silence, and every single one of us was counting. I knew they were because I was. Every step forward, another piece of the world going quiet behind us. We weren't racing to save Thornfell. We were racing ahead of the saving, and the world was paying the toll for every yard of lead we gained.

My hands were shaking on the map. I gripped harder.

"You feel responsible," Isolde said. Not a question. She'd gotten good at that — saying the true thing flatly, without pity, because pity would have cracked me open and we couldn't afford cracks. Not today.

"I feel like the man holding the bucket during a fire," I said. "Doesn't matter how fast I run. Just matters how much burns before I get there."

"And if you stop to grieve every house—"

"I know." I did know. Bram taught me that lesson young, in his cold pragmatic way: a courier who stops for every beggar delivers nothing. But Bram's version of the lesson had always had a hole in it, a place where the heart was supposed to go, and I'd spent this whole wretched journey learning that the hole was the point. You couldn't carry a covenant you didn't grieve for. The drowned chapel records had said it plain as scripture: the oath held because people *cared* it held. Take away the caring and you had Vess — efficient, logical, starving her own magic out of the world.

So I let myself grieve the village for exactly eleven steps, and then I put it down and walked, and hated how easy the putting-down got, and kept walking.

* * *

The second tear was wider than the first, and the third was a canyon.

By midday we were climbing over gaps where the road had simply ceased, stone and memory both, and the fae soldiers were laying planks of solidified twilight across the voids — the court's last magic, spent like coins from a nearly-empty purse. Each plank cost them. I watched the regent's face as her soldiers paid it, and for the first time I saw what Serahin must have seen when he defected for these people: not coldness. Arithmetic. She was spending her nation to buy mine an afternoon, and she was doing the sums without blinking because blinking wasted time.

That was when we heard the fighting behind us.

Not battle — skirmish. Short, sharp, over almost before the sound reached the front of the column. Horns, a scream with too many harmonics in it, the crack of Hollow Guild crossbows. My blood went to ice water. The Hollow hunters had found us again, or Vess's outriders, or—

"Hold the column!" Serahin barked, and broke from my side before I could grab him.

I didn't hold the column. I'm not proud of it. I shoved through ranks of startled fae, map crushed against my chest, Isolde swearing and following, and burst through the rear guard into a scene I will carry to whatever grave gets me:

Hollow Guild soldiers, six of them, black-liveried, retreating. And in the middle of the road, on his knees, one-armed — *one-armed*, his left sleeve pinned and soaked through, his face grey as old tallow —

Bram.

They'd left him. Or lost him, in some scuffle at the rear, and he'd crawled and staggered and god-knows-what-else along the road after us, and now he was kneeling on the causeway with a stolen hunter's blade in his good hand and blood sheeting down his chin, laughing — actually laughing — at the retreating backs of the men who used to be his brothers.

"Took you long enough, boy," he rasped, and pitched forward.

I caught him. Of course I caught him. He weighed half what he should have. Up close I could see what Vess's interrogators had left of him: the burn scars roping his forearm, the fingers that bent wrong, the eye that didn't track right anymore. They'd had him for nine days. Nine days, and he'd fed them false routes the whole time, and they'd finally started checking their prize's work against reality and found the lies, and apparently decided to leave the lying corpse for the crows rather than waste a bolt on him.

"You absolute idiot," I said, and my voice broke in the middle of it, and I didn't care who heard. "You stupid, stubborn, brilliant old bastard."

"Aye," Bram agreed, sagging against me. "All three. Write it in the ledger."

* * *

We carried him. There was no question of leaving him, and there was no question — not even a flicker of one — of whether he'd earned carrying. Whatever debts sat in the old Crane ledger between us, he'd paid them nine times over in a Vessian cell, feeding lies to torturers with his teeth kicked loose. Isolde bound his wounds with strips of her own cloak. Serahin walked beside the litter and spoke to him in low tones, and I caught fragments — guild cant, old smuggling signs, a language of trust the two of them had apparently built in the weeks I'd been too busy to notice.

Bram drifted in and out. When he surfaced, he talked. Bram always talked; pain had never once shut him up, and maybe that was its own kind of courage.

"Saw it from their camp, you know," he murmured, somewhere past the fifth gap we bridged. "The tears. Watched 'em spread for days through a slit in a tent wall. Started keeping count." His laugh was mostly blood and gravel. "Forty-one villages unwritten before they marched me anywhere. I did the sums, Aug. Same sums the regent does. And I thought — that clever bitch Vess, she's right, it can't be stopped, best play is to be on the winning side." His ruined hand found my sleeve. "Then I remembered you. Stubborn little ox. Never once took the winning side in your life. Always took the *right* one, slow as Christmas, and I thought — well. If the boy can bet on impossible, so can his uncle."

"That's not a plan, Bram."

"No. It's better. It's a *reason*." His eye closed. "Plans fail. Reasons don't. Reason walks."

I looked back down the road. Behind us, the afternoon light was coming apart at the seams — literally, soft golden rags drifting off the horizon like the sky itself was one of the unwritten things. Forty-one villages. How many now? How many since dawn?

Every step forward costs a piece of the world behind us. I'd been thinking it all day like a curse. But Bram's voice turned it over, showed me the other face: every step forward *buys* a piece of the world ahead of us. The Heartstone. The reforging. The chance — thin as a plank over a void, but real — that everything being unwritten might yet be written back.

Reason walks. So we walked.

* * *

Dusk found us at the mouth of the Vale of Hours, and the map told me what waited there, and I wished it hadn't.

Beyond the vale lay the Heartstone — one day's hard march, maybe less if the road held. And the road was not going to hold. I could see it now with naked eyes, no map needed: ahead of us the Sigil Road narrowed, whitened, thinned like ice on a spring pond, and beyond it the sky above the Heartstone was *wrong*, bruise-colored, pulsing faintly like something with a heartbeat that wasn't a heart.

Vess was there. I knew it the way you know weather coming. She'd reached the Stone ahead of us, exactly as Bram's false intelligence was supposed to prevent and hadn't, because there are limits to what a broken man can lie convincingly about. Her ritual would be beginning. The final severance. Everything — the hedges, the waystones, the drowned chapel's humble records, the thousand-year pile of small promises — would come undone at midnight or thereabouts, unless we arrived first.

The regent called a halt. Her officers gathered. I stood among them holding a map that was mostly blank now, the ink exhausted, the road's last memories spent drawing us this far.

"One night's march remains," the regent said. Her voice carried the way winter carries. "The Warden believes we can cross the Vale before the ritual completes. I have spent three centuries not believing humans." She paused, and her ancient eyes found mine. "Tonight I will spend belief instead. Warden Crane — walk first."

Bram, propped against a waystone with Isolde holding a cup of water to his lips, managed a crooked grin. "First time anyone's called you Warden and meant it as a compliment."

"It's not a compliment," I said. "It's a bill."

"Same thing, in this family."

I turned to face the Vale of Hours, and the bruised sky, and the dying road, and behind me I heard the host rise to its feet — three hundred fae and sixty humans and one broken smuggler insisting he could walk, damn it, unhand me, girl — and the whole ragged weight of them waiting on my next step.

The road hummed under my boots, faint and failing, like a voice remembering a song.

I stepped.

* * *

Somewhere behind us, far to the south, the last light left the world in the places we'd already passed — I felt it go, each extinguishing, like doors closing down a long corridor. Ahead, the bruise over the Heartstone pulsed faster.

Midnight was coming. We had one night to outwalk the end of everything, and the man beside me had one arm, and the knight beside me had half his grace, and the girl beside me had given up her name at a tollgate for mine, and none of us were anywhere near enough.

But reason walks.

So we walked — faster now, faster, the road groaning beneath four hundred feet, the world tearing softly at our backs — toward the light that shouldn't be there, the sick gold glow rising over the ridge like a second dawn.

A dawn that belonged to Queen Vess. And to her ritual, already begun.
