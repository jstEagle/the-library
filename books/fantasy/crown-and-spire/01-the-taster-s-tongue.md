## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There are three ways to die at King Aldous's table, and I have survived all of them before the second bell.

The first is poison, which is my profession. The second is boredom, which is my punishment. The third is being noticed by the king himself, which would be fatal in a way no antidote can touch.

I arrived that morning as I arrive every morning—through the servants' stair, past the scullery boys who have worked beside me for six years and could not pick me out of a lineup of one. This is not an accident. Being forgettable is a craft like any other. It requires discipline, practice, and a certain willingness to be nobody, which most people find unbearable and I find restful.

"Morning, Quentin," said Cook Marta, who says it every morning and remembers it only until I've passed the door.

"Morning," I said, because even ghosts can be polite.

The king's breakfast was poached eggs in cream, white bread, honeyed pears, and a small cup of bitter tonic that the royal alchemist, Master Corvane, sends down each night with instructions written in his cramped, precise hand. I tasted everything in order, as prescribed by the Office of the Taster: eggs first, then bread, then fruit, then tonic last, so that if something killed me, the physicians could at least narrow down the culprit by which course I died on. A tidy system. I have never once been grateful for it.

The eggs were fine. The bread was fine. The pears were excellent, which made me suspicious out of habit, but suspicion is free and I indulge it cheaply. The tonic was bitter with gentian and valerian and something underneath I couldn't name—a green, cold note, like the smell of a cellar where something has been growing in the dark for a long time.

I swallowed it anyway. That's the job. My tongue has been trained on sixty-one poisons and their antidotes until it flinches at nothing, and if there is a slow poison hiding under the valerian, well. Slow poisons are someone else's emergency.

King Aldous ate his breakfast behind three feet of silver and a wall of paranoia older than his crown. He watched me taste from across the room, the way he watches everyone, with the flat gray stare of a man who has already decided you are guilty and is merely waiting for the evidence to arrange itself politely.

"You," he said, when I announced the meal clean. "You're the taster."

"Eleven years, Your Majesty."

"Hm." He chewed a pear with the wariness of a man defusing it. "You don't look like much."

"No, Your Majesty."

"That's why you're still alive, probably." He pointed the pear knife at me, briefly, the closest thing to warmth his face could manage. "Nobody bothers killing furniture."

I bowed. Furniture does not bow, technically, but I have found that exceeding expectations is the surest way to remain beneath them.

* * *

By day I keep the king alive. By night I keep the books of the Gilded Spoon, which is a guild the way a knife is a spoon—technically true, and misleading in every way that matters.

The Gilded Spoon trades in impossible things. Bottled laughter. Borrowed luck. A winter, once, sold on credit to a farmer who swore he'd pay us back in spring, and did, though what he paid us back I am not permitted to write here. We deal in the intangible made tangible, the priceless given a price, and my job—the part nobody knows about, the part that fits me like a glove sewn for a ghost—is to sit in the back office with a green lamp and a ledger bound in eel-skin, and write down what everything cost.

That night's entries, for the record:

*One bottle of laughter, medium grade, harvested from a wedding in Lower Harrow. Sold to a widow on Candlemaker's Row. She wept when she opened it. She paid double. Note: laughter keeps better than grief; grief ferments.*

*One first kiss, slightly used, traded against two hours of perfect silence. Both parties satisfied. Neither party present, which is how we prefer it.*

*One winter, sold on credit. Interest to accrue at one memory per month of sunshine. Client signed in blood, then complained about the ink. Note: farmers are terrible negotiators and wonderful debtors.*

*One small song, purchased from an unnamed source, pending valuation.*

I stopped at that last line and read it twice.

Songs came through the Spoon now and then—there was a whole drawer of them downstairs, filed between the bottled dreams and the confiscated curses—but this one had come in without a seller's name attached, which meant someone had dropped it off anonymously, which meant either shame or fear, and both of those make for interesting bookkeeping.

I went downstairs to look at it.

The guildhall at night is a different animal than the guildhall by day. By day it's all brass scales and hushed negotiation, clients in good coats pretending they aren't desperate. By night the lamps burn low and the impossible inventory hums to itself on its shelves—laughter fizzing faintly in its bottles, storms sulking in their jars, luck ticking like a clock that knows something you don't. Ovelia says you get used to it. Ovelia lies professionally, so I take her at her word carefully.

The song sat in a shallow dish on the valuation bench, and it was doing something songs shouldn't do. It was leaning. Toward the door. Like a plant toward a window.

I bent over it, and it leaned harder, and somewhere in its small glass prison it made a sound—not heard so much as remembered—a few notes of something bright and unfinished, like a child humming in another room.

I knew that tune.

I didn't know how I knew that tune. But I knew it the way you know your own front step in the dark, and my chest did something complicated and unwelcome, and I stood up very fast and told myself it was the lamp smoke.

Because I did know it. It was Wren's song. The one she hums when she thinks she's alone, the one she's been humming since the winter she arrived at the Spoon half-frozen with her dead mother's shawl and nothing else, the one I have heard approximately four hundred times while pretending not to listen.

Someone had bottled Wren's song and sold it to my guild without her name on it.

* * *

Wren was in the kitchen when I got home, which is to say she was in the kitchen, because Wren is always in the kitchen, occupying it the way weather occupies a sky.

She's fifteen, mostly elbows and opinions, with a smudge of ink on her cheek that has been there so long I suspect it's structural. She came to the Spoon as a client's orphaned daughter three years ago and simply never left, and Ovelia—whose heart is a locked drawer in a locked cabinet in a locked room—took her in with the words "she can sweep," which in Ovelia means *I have decided to love you and will deny it under torture.*

"There you are!" She said it the way other people say *finally*, as though my arrival were a scheduled event she'd been waiting on. "Quentin, tell Ovelia the ledger needs a new column for—"

"The ledger needs nothing. The ledger is perfect."

"The ledger is a tyranny." She hopped onto the counter, which Ovelia forbids, which is why she does it. "You know what you are? You're the kind of person who alphabetizes other people's feelings."

"I do not alphabetize feelings."

"You absolutely do. Last week Marta from the scullery was crying and you handed her a handkerchief *folded into eighths.*"

"Folding is care."

"Folding is *filing.*" She grinned at me, and the grin did the thing it always does, which is reach inside my chest and rearrange the furniture. "Anyway. You're late. I kept your supper warm. Well. I kept your supper. Warm is aspirational."

She'd made noodles. Bad ones. She makes them badly on purpose because the first time she made them well, I said "adequate," and she declared she would never cook well for me again until I learned to say something nice, and I have been eating punitive noodles for two years rather than learn.

They were terrible. I ate every bite.

"You know," she said, chin on her fists, watching me eat, "you're basically family."

"I'm your landlord's bookkeeper."

"You're *basically family.* You taste-test for a paranoid king, you file impossible things, and you pretend you don't like my noodles. That's family. That's the definition."

"It is not the definition."

"Look it up. I'll write it in the ledger." She slid off the counter and stole a noodle off my plate, which is theft, which she knows, which is why she does it slowly, maintaining eye contact. "Quentin. Say the noodles are nice."

"They're—" The word stuck, the way the important words always stick in me, rusted shut from disuse. "Adequate-plus."

"*Quentin.*"

"Adequate-plus," I said firmly, and she threw a dish towel at my head, and I caught it, and neither of us mentioned that I held it a moment too long before handing it back.

This is the arrangement. She insists on family; I insist on terminology. She calls me *basically my brother*; I call myself *staff adjacent*. She draws little pictures of the two of us in the margins of her practice ledgers—stick figures, holding hands, labeled *W + Q*—and I pretend I haven't seen them, and then I go home and lie awake cataloguing all the reasons attachment is a poison with no antidote.

I have dosed myself against everything else. Why not that?

Because here is the truth, which I will set down once, quietly, in the margin of this account where hopefully no one reads it: I have spent eleven years making myself unnoticeable, unmemorable, un-*there*, and it has worked so well that the person I most want to be seen by looks straight through me every single day, and calls me family anyway, out of sheer stubborn generosity, the way you'd feed a stray cat that won't let itself be touched.

And some nights—I want to be touched. Some nights the loneliness sits on my chest like a stone with excellent posture, and I think: *one word. Just say the word. Say 'yes.'*

And then morning comes, and the king needs tasting, and the ledger needs balancing, and I fold my handkerchief into eighths and put the word back on its shelf with all the other impossible things I refuse to price.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when I noticed the second strange thing, and by then the first should have warned me.

I was closing the ledger when I saw it: an entry in my own handwriting that I had no memory of writing.

*Received: one small song, source unnamed. Valuation pending. NOTE — buyer enquired after the singer. Buyer enquired twice.*

Buyer enquired twice.

No buyer enquires twice. Buyers at the Spoon pay for their impossibilities and leave, grateful and anonymous. Curiosity is a luxury our clients cannot afford and our business cannot survive. And no entry writes itself, least of all in my own neat, cramped, eighths-folded hand.

I sat very still in the green lamplight and listened to the guildhall around me—the laughter fizzing, the storms sulking, the luck ticking—and beneath all of it, faint and far away, I heard humming.

A few notes. Bright and unfinished. Leaning toward the door.

Wren's song. Except Wren was asleep upstairs, and had been for hours, and the song in the dish downstairs was supposed to be *in* the dish, and yet—

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and told myself it was the building settling. Old buildings settle. Old buildings groan and lean and hum, and old bookkeepers imagine things, and tomorrow there would be eggs to taste and a king to survive and a ledger to balance, and whatever had written in my hand and hummed in my dark would turn out to be nothing, because things are usually nothing, because I have arranged my entire life around the principle that attention is danger and noticing is the first step toward caring and caring is the first step toward losing.

But I did not sleep. And in the morning, when I walked past the valuation bench, the dish was empty.

And Wren, at breakfast, humming her bright unfinished tune, paused mid-note, frowned at nothing, and said:

"Weird. I can't remember how the ending goes."
