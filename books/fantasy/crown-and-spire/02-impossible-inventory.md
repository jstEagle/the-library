## 2. Impossible Inventory

The Gilded Spoon does not, as you might reasonably assume, sell spoons.

It sells everything else. Bottled laughter, mostly secondhand and slightly flat. A jar of last winter, sold on credit to a widow who wanted to feel cold again for reasons she declined to share. A thunderstorm, vintage uncertain, currently sulking in the back room and rattling its shelf every time someone walks past. And once — this was before my time, but Ovelia keeps the receipt framed behind the counter like a trophy or a warning — a Tuesday. An actual Tuesday. Nobody remembers what the buyer wanted it for, which is fitting, because Tuesdays are like that.

My name is Quentin Eriksen, and I keep the books for all of it.

By day I taste the king's food and survive it, which is its own kind of ledger entry. By night I sit in the back office of the Gilded Spoon with a candle, a pot of ink, and the collected absurdities of a city that has learned to treat the impossible as a commodity. I write down what came in, what went out, what was promised, what was paid, and what was owed. I am very good at it. Numbers do not ask you questions about yourself. Numbers do not look at you with hopeful orphan eyes and say things like *you're basically family, Quentin.*

More on that later. Or never, if I can manage it.

* * *

The bell above the guild's front door has a voice like a cat stepping on a harp, and it announced our client at half past nine, which is late for commerce and early for regret.

Ovelia ran the floor that evening. She always runs the floor when a client is likely to be difficult, and she can smell a difficult client the way I can smell arsenic in a soup — faintly, sadly, with a certain professional resignation. I was at my desk in the back, door open a crack, because Ovelia's rule is that the ledger-keeper hears every transaction. *You cannot price what you have not listened to,* she told me my first week. It is the closest thing the guild has to scripture.

The client was a woman of perhaps sixty, dressed in the good wool of someone who has sold the better wool already. She carried a small glass jar wrapped in a shawl, the way other people carry an infant, or a bomb.

"Mistress Ovelia," she said. Her voice was steady in the way of someone who has rehearsed steadiness in front of a mirror. "I'm told you buy things that can't be bought."

"We rent, mostly," Ovelia said. She has a way of saying things like that — pleasant, precise, and faintly like a knife being laid on a table. "Buying implies the seller is finished with the item. Few people are finished with anything, truly. Sit, madam. Tea?"

"No tea." The woman sat. She set the jar on the counter between them with the care of someone laying down a wager she could not afford. Inside was nothing, or rather, it was the particular nothing that isn't empty. The glass held a color that isn't a color — a blue-green that made my teeth ache to look at, that smelled of salt even from the back room, even through a closed door and a crack in it. "I want to sell a memory. The sea. All of it. Every day of it."

Ovelia's fingers went still on the counter. In the back room, I sat up straighter.

"May I ask," Ovelia said, "what you intend to buy with the proceeds?"

The woman's jaw tightened. "That's my business."

"It is," Ovelia agreed. "And it is also the guild's business, because we are required by our own charter to know what a client intends to purchase. It prevents certain... recidivisms." She smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "We once sold a man his own courage back three times in one year. He kept spending it on the same terrible marriage."

The woman looked at the jar. When she spoke again, the steadiness had cracked down the middle. "There was a boy. On the coast. Forty years ago. He used to mend nets with his teeth when his hands were full, which was disgusting, and I loved him the way you love things when you're seventeen and stupid. My father moved us inland. I married a cooper. He was a good man. He's dead now, and I find that I can't remember the sea without remembering him, the boy, and I can't remember the boy without the sea, and I am *tired*, mistress. I am tired of standing at my window on windy days feeling like something was taken from me. So. I sell you the sea. And with what you pay me, I buy—" her voice did something complicated, "—I want a second chance at a first love. I'm told you broker those."

The back room was very quiet. So was I, which is different, and requires more effort than you'd think.

Ovelia was quiet too, which was worse. Ovelia is never quiet. Silence from Ovelia is the sound of a scale finding its balance, and it usually means the weight on one side is something that should not be weighed at all.

"Madam," she said at last, gently — and Ovelia being gentle is another sound you learn to fear — "the sea and the boy are the same memory. You know that. You said it yourself. If we buy one, we buy both."

"Then buy both."

"And if you buy him back — a second chance — you will buy him without the sea. A boy on dry land, mending nothing, with no salt in him at all. He will be a stranger wearing a beloved face. You will spend everything you have, meet him, and feel nothing, because the feeling *was* the sea, and we will be holding it in a jar."

The woman's hands closed around the glass. "You're saying no."

"I'm saying the price is wrong," Ovelia said. "Not the number. The *price*. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire guild." She leaned forward. "Sell me the storm you carry instead. The one under your ribs. I'll pay fairly. You'll sleep well. And you'll keep the sea, and the boy, and the ache, which is not nothing, madam. The ache is the receipt. It proves you were there."

For a long moment the woman said nothing. Then, in a voice like a door closing: "I was told the Gilded Spoon would take anything."

"We take anything," Ovelia agreed. "We do not *buy* anything. There's a difference, and the difference is also the entire guild."

The woman stood, wrapped the jar back in its shawl, and left. The bell made its harp-cat noise. The door shut.

I let out a breath I'd been holding for approximately four minutes.

"Ledger," Ovelia called, without turning around.

I came out with the book. "No transaction," I said. "Declined. Reason: the item and its price were the same object."

"Write it exactly like that." She rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Add: client referred to the Quiet Sisters, who deal in grief and are better equipped. And Quentin—" she looked at me, and for a second something moved behind her face like a fish under ice, "—the under-court bought a memory last week. The sea, as it happens. A different sea, a different seller. They paid triple the market rate."

"Triple."

"Triple, and they didn't haggle. The under-court *always* haggles. Haggling is how they feed. When they stop haggling, it's because they've stopped being picky." She took the ledger from me and looked at the blank line where the transaction would have gone. "Hungry buyers are like hungry animals, Quentin. The question is never whether they'll eat. It's what's left in the pen when they're done."

* * *

I should explain the under-court, insofar as anyone explains it.

Beneath the mountain — the same mountain the palace is built into, which the architects insist is a coincidence and the architects are lying — there is a court. It has a king, or something shaped like the idea of a king. It has nobles, or things wearing the shapes of nobles. It trades, as we do. It has excellent manners and no bottom to it. Every guild has a relationship with it the way every village has a relationship with the wolf in the woods: carefully, and with an eye on the fences.

The Gilded Spoon's relationship is older than Ovelia and conducted through intermediaries and a treaty nobody under forty has read. We do not ask what the under-court does with what it buys. It does not ask what we do with what we buy. Commerce, Ovelia says, is the only peace that has ever lasted, because both sides are too busy counting to fight.

I wrote the day's entries. *One memory of the sea, declined. One jar of bottled laughter, returned by purchaser, cited as "flat since Tuesday." One winter, payment overdue, second notice.* My handwriting is the only honest thing about me: small, even, unremarkable. A hand like everyone's hand. I have spent thirty years making sure of it.

There is a trick to being unnoticed, and the trick is that it isn't a trick. It's a discipline. You keep your voice in the middle of its range. You keep your clothes one shade off memorable. You answer questions with answers, never with stories. You do not let anyone learn the shape of your silences, because silences are how people recognize you across a room. The king has tasted ten thousand meals I have tasted first, and if you asked him to describe his taster he would say *small man, ink on his fingers, always there* and then his eyes would slide off the memory like rain off glass.

I used to think this was survival. I have begun, lately, to suspect it is something else. But suspicion is expensive, and I have never been a rich man.

* * *

Wren arrived at ten, which is late, which is usual, which is a whole conversation I am not having with her because every version of it ends with me saying something true.

She comes in through the kitchen door like weather, all at once and everywhere. Fifteen years old, orphaned twice over — once by plague, once by the workhouse — with a voice that does things to candles. I mean that literally. She sings and the flames lean toward her, all of them, every candle in the room, like flowers after a sun that isn't there. Ovelia took her on as an apprentice two years ago over the objections of exactly no one, because even the guild's objections were charmed.

"Evening, Quentin." She dropped onto the corner of my desk, which is against at least four of Ovelia's rules, and started reading the ledger upside down, which is against the fifth. "Ooh, someone tried to sell the sea."

"You heard that?"

"I heard all of it. The walls in this place are more honest than the people." She spun a spare quill between her fingers. "Do you think Ovelia was right? About the ache being the receipt?"

"Ovelia is always right. It's infuriating."

"She wasn't right about the storm in the back room. She said it was sulking. It's not sulking, it's *grieving*. Storms grieve when they're bottled. I can hear it." She said this with the absolute certainty of the young, and the worst part is she's usually correct about the things I've decided not to believe in. "Also I'm late because I was singing to the lamplighter's wife. She's been low since the baby. I fixed her. No charge. Don't write it down."

"Wren."

"Don't write it down, Quentin."

I wrote it down. *One lullaby, gifted. Value: unpriced.* She watched me do it and grinned, and the candle on my desk leaned toward her, and I pretended not to notice, and she pretended not to notice me pretending, and that is the whole architecture of us.

Then, because she is fifteen and the world is a door she hasn't learned is locked: "You know you're basically family, right? Me and you and Ovelia. That's a thing. That's a real thing."

"Wren, I keep your employment records. That's not family. That's payroll."

"You keep *everyone's* payroll and you don't call the clients family. You fixed the clasp on my coat last month without being asked. You leave the good ink where I can reach it and pretend it rolled there. You tasted the king's soup on my birthday and then came here and ate noodles with me at midnight and told me—" she dropped her voice into a truly slanderous impression of me, "—'this is efficient, we were both going to eat anyway.'"

"That is efficient."

"That is *family*, you disaster." She hopped off the desk, snatched an apple from the bowl Ovelia keeps for exactly this kind of raid, and headed for the stairs to the apprentice loft. At the landing she paused. "One day you're going to have to admit you love us. It's going to be so embarrassing for you. I can't wait."

The candle straightened up as she went. The room got colder, or I did.

* * *

Ovelia found me at the ledger near midnight, which she does not usually do, which meant something.

She set down a single sheet of paper. On it, in a hand too fine to be human — the letters all leaning the same direction, like reeds in a current — was a list. An order list. The under-court's standing purchases for the season.

"Bottled laughter," Ovelia read, though I was already reading it. "Bottled storms. First snows. Last words. Lullabies." She tapped the line. "Lullabies, Quentin. They've never bought lullabies. Lullabies are the cheapest thing in the building. You can't do anything with a lullaby except remember being safe."

"Unless you're buying them from someone," I said slowly, "who's selling the memory of being safe."

"Yes." She sat down across from me. In the candlelight she looked, for the first time since I'd known her, old. Not aged. Old the way institutions are old, the way debts are old. "The under-court has been hungry all season. Triple rates. No haggling. Buying things they've never wanted before. And last month they paid for something I have never in thirty years seen them pay for."

"What?"

"An apprentice's song. Unfinished." She looked at me, and I felt the floor tilt a half-degree, the way it does when the scale tips. "A girl with a voice that makes candles lean. The intermediary asked — casually, in passing, the way they ask — whether the Gilded Spoon held any *claim* on her. Whether she was inventory. Whether she was spoken for."

The candle between us guttered, though there was no draft.

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them she was spoken for." Ovelia's voice was iron, and under the iron, something that had not been allowed to be afraid in thirty years, being afraid. "I told them the guild claims its own. And Quentin — I need you to hear this, and I need you to not be clever about it — the under-court does not ask questions it doesn't already know the answers to. They asked because they are *shopping*. And they have developed a taste for the one thing in this city we cannot restock."

"Which is?"

She looked toward the stairs, toward the loft, toward the small sleeping weight of a girl who sang to candles.

"Light," Ovelia said. "They're buying light. And we are nearly out."

I did not write the entry that night. Some things, I am learning, the ledger should not hold — because paper remembers, and paper can be read, and there are buyers in this city who pay triple and never haggle.

I blew out the candle myself, before it could lean toward anything.
