## 3. Wren Sings to the Dark

The trouble with being an orphan is that everyone assumes you don't know anything. The second trouble is that they're usually right about the important things and wrong about everything else.

I knew, for instance, that Master Quentin kept his heart in a locked box somewhere behind his ribs, and that the key had been lost so long ago he'd stopped looking for it. I knew he pretended not to like my singing while standing in the doorway of the kitchen every single time I did it, which is a very particular kind of pretending. And I knew — this was the thing I was wrong about, though it took me a long while to learn how wrong — that the courteous stranger who came to the well at midnight paid too fairly to be trusted.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Quentin says I do that. He says it the way he says most things, which is quietly, to his ledgers, as if the ledgers were listening better than people do.

Here is how the night began: with me on the back step of the Gilded Spoon, singing to the dark, because the dark had asked.

* * *

I should explain the voice thing, because everyone always wants it explained and nobody ever believes me until the candles move.

My mother — whoever she was; the Sisters of the Lantern found me in a basket with nothing but a name stitched into the lining — left me a voice that does things. Not magic, exactly. Or if it's magic, it's the shy kind, the kind that doesn't announce itself. When I sing low, candles lean toward me like sunflowers after the sun. When I sing high, they straighten up as if embarrassed. Once, at the orphanage, I sang old Mother Cassia's headache away entirely, and she gave me an extra heel of bread and told me never to tell anyone, because things that work are more frightening than things that don't.

I have mostly followed her advice. Mostly.

Except for the stranger.

He came the first time three weeks ago, when I couldn't sleep and went down to the well behind the Spoon to sing where no one could hear me and no candles would be made to behave strangely. The well is deep and old and echoes beautifully, and I'd learned all its favorite songs the way you learn a friend's laugh.

"You have a voice that makes the dark remember itself," he said.

I nearly fell in. He was standing by the wall — I hadn't heard him come, which should have been the first warning, because Quentin says anyone you can't hear coming is either a cat or a problem — and he was tall and gray-cloaked and courteous in a way I can only describe as *old-fashioned*, like a portrait that had gotten bored of hanging still.

"I wasn't singing for company," I said, with the enormous dignity available only to small people caught doing something private.

"No," he agreed. "That's what made it worth hearing."

And then he held out his hand, and in his palm was a starlight.

Not a metaphor. Not a firefly. A starlight — a little bead of white-silver light, warm as a fresh egg, humming faintly the way a struck bell hums after the sound has technically ended. I'd seen starlight twice before, once in Ovelia's strongroom (through a door I absolutely was not supposed to be near) and once in a dream so vivid I woke up homesick for a sky I'd invented.

"What is it?" I asked, which was stupid, because I knew what it was, the way you know your own name.

"Payment," he said, "for one small song."

Now. Any sensible person would have run. But here is the third trouble with being an orphan: nobody sensible ever taught you what to run *from*. The Sisters taught me prayers and bread-baking and how to make my body small in a crowded room. Nobody ever said *when a beautiful stranger offers you a piece of the actual sky, that is the moment to leave.*

So I sang. Something small, something my mother might have sung — a lullaby about a boat, though I've never seen a boat. The well took it up and turned it over and gave it back deeper than I'd made it, and when I finished, the whole courtyard smelled like the minute just before snowfall.

The stranger bowed. He set the starlight on the well's rim, next to my hand.

"Same hour, if you're willing," he said. "No obligation. Songs cannot truly be bought — only *thanked*."

Then he walked into the shadow by the wall the way you or I would walk through a doorway, and the shadow closed behind him like water.

I sat there a long time holding the starlight. It didn't burn. It hummed against my palm like something alive and patient, and I thought — and I want to be honest about this, even now, even knowing everything — I thought: *someone finally thinks what I have is worth something real.*

You can judge me for that. Quentin would. That's rather the point.

* * *

It became a habit, the way lovely dangerous things do. Every few nights, the same hour, the same song or a new one. He never asked for sad songs or grand songs. Small ones, always. Songs about bread. Songs about rain on a specific roof. Once, embarrassingly, a song about a cat named Duchess whom I had invented at age six and still missed.

And every time, payment: one starlight, laid gently on the stone.

I kept them in a tin under my floorboard — eleven of them by the end, twelve, thirteen. They lit my corner of the attic at night with a glow like moonlight through water, and I'd lie there watching them and feel rich in a way that had nothing to do with coin. The guild dealt in impossible things; I'd grown up around bottled laughter and winters sold on credit. But those were other people's impossibilities, priced and shelved. This was mine. This was *mine*.

Which is why I should have known it would cost.

* * *

Master Quentin found out on a Tuesday, because of course he did. The man misses nothing; it's his entire personality. He tastes poison for a paranoid king and keeps books for a guild that trades in bottled thunderstorms, and his superpower — his actual, honest-to-the-gods gift — is being so unremarkable that the world slides off him like rain off wax. I used to think it must be peaceful, being forgettable. Lately I'd started to suspect it was just lonely with extra steps.

He caught me coming in over the back wall at half past midnight, starlight tin in my apron pocket, humming.

"Wren."

Just that. My name, in his flat ledger-keeper voice, which is the voice he uses when the numbers have come out wrong.

"Evening, Master Quentin," I said brightly. "Couldn't sleep. Went for a walk. Very normal walk. Walked extensively."

"Show me your pocket."

Now, I want it on the record that I considered lying. I considered it seriously. But Quentin Eriksen has a face like a closed account, and lying to him felt like trying to counterfeit a coin he minted himself.

I opened the tin.

Thirteen starlights looked up at us from the bottom of it, and the alley went soft and silver, and Quentin — Quentin went very, very still. Stiller than still. I've seen him taste soup that could kill a horse and not blink, and this was worse than that.

"Where," he said, and then stopped, and started again, quieter, which was somehow scarier: "Wren. Who gave you these?"

"A friend."

"Wren."

"A *courteous* friend," I said. "He's very polite. He never asks for anything except small songs. He thanks me. He *pays* me." My voice was climbing and I couldn't seem to get it down. "Nobody pays me, Quentin. Nobody thinks anything I do is worth actual sky. You taste kings' food and keep impossible books and you're *important*, and I scrub pots and sing to wells, and for once somebody treated me like—"

"Like what? Like merchandise?" He crouched down to my level, and his eyes in the starlight-glow were doing something complicated. "Wren. Souls are traded down in the dark places of this city. I know, because I keep their receipts. There is no version of a stranger who appears at midnight, pays a child in *stars*, and wants nothing. That trade doesn't exist. Everything in this city has a price tag, and I am telling you, as the person who writes the price tags: whatever he's buying from you, the bill hasn't arrived yet."

"He's *kind* to me."

"I'm kind to you."

"No, you're *careful* with me," I shot back, and the words came out with all their edges showing. "There's a difference. You check my hands for burns and you count the spoons after market day and you leave bread on my sill like some kind of — some kind of ghost of a person, but you won't actually—" I stopped, breathless, furious, tears arriving without any invitation whatsoever.

Quentin stood up. Slowly. Like an old door.

"You will not meet him again," he said. "That's not a request. If he comes to the well, you come get me. Do you understand what the under-court is? Do you understand what's been happening to the wards, to the—"

"The *under*-court," I repeated. "You sound like Ovelia when she's frightened and pretending to be annoyed. 'The under-court.' Everyone whispers about it like it's weather. Nobody tells me anything, because I'm the orphan, the pot-scrubber, the little songbird, and gods forbid anyone treat me like a person who could understand a—"

"*Because understanding it won't protect you!*"

He never shouts. In two years, I had never heard Quentin Eriksen raise his voice, and it came out of him cracked and raw, echoing off the walls of the alley, startling pigeons out of the gutter three roofs away. And underneath the shout, plain as a bruise, was fear. Real fear. For me.

For one whole heartbeat, I almost softened. Almost reached for his sleeve. That's the awful trick of loving someone difficult — they show you the wound for half a second and then button the coat, and you spend years wondering if you imagined it.

But he'd already buttoned it. His face went flat again, ledger-flat, and he said, in the quiet voice, the worst voice: "Give me the tin. You'll stay away from the well. We'll speak no more of it tonight."

And something in me — the part that had spent eight years in an orphanage learning that adults take things and call it protection — snapped clean through.

"No," I said.

"Wren."

"They're mine. He *gave* them. For my songs. My songs, Quentin — the one thing in this world that's actually mine — and you want to confiscate them like I'm a child who found a knife." I hugged the tin to my chest, thirteen little stars humming against my ribs like a second heartbeat. "You know what I think? I think you don't care about the danger. I think you just can't stand that somebody else got to me first. Somebody who isn't afraid to be *seen* wanting good things for me."

The words landed. I watched them land. I watched them go into him exactly where they'd hurt most, because I have my own small talents, and cruelty aimed true is apparently one of them.

"That's not fair," he said. Very quietly.

"Neither is keeping everyone at arm's length and calling it love!" And there it was — the true thing, the ugly true thing, out in the open air between us, silver-lit and irrevocable. "You dose yourself against everything, Quentin. Poison, sure, fine, that's your job. But attachment, too. Family, too. You keep a locked box where your heart should be and you hand out bread through the keyhole and you expect me to be *grateful*—"

"Wren." His voice had gone strange. Thin. "Please."

Nobody had ever said *please* to me like that. Not begging-for-a-favor please. *Please stop, because I have nothing armored left in that spot.* And I saw it then, clear as the starlight between us: the loneliness under the invisibility. The man who had made himself unnoticeable so thoroughly that even the people who loved him had to squint.

I should have stopped. A kinder person would have stopped.

"You know what the funny thing is?" I said instead, because hurt sixteen-year-olds are not kind, they are thorough. "Your stranger-friend — mine, I mean, my courteous stranger — he *listens*. He asks for songs about my cat. About bread. About rain. He remembers what I say. When's the last time you let anybody close enough to remember anything about you?"

The silence afterward was the longest of my life.

Quentin looked at me for a moment longer — and I swear, I swear on Duchess's memory, that his eyes were wet, though he blinked it away so fast I could pretend forever that I'd imagined it. Then he stepped back, and the wall seemed to come up around him, brick by invisible brick, and he said:

"Keep your stars, then. Keep them all. But hear me, Wren, because I'll only say this once, and I'll say it in the flat voice you hate, because it's the only voice I trust not to break." He pulled his coat straight. "Whatever he is paying you for — however small the songs, however fair the price — the Court Beneath does not buy. It *collects*. And when the bill comes due, it will not send a receipt. It will send for *you*."

"Then I'll pay it," I said, chin up, tin clutched tight, every inch of me trembling and certain. "At least my debts are honest ones."

Something flickered across his face — I've replayed it ten thousand times since, trying to decide if it was anger or grief or agreement, and I still don't know — and then he turned and walked up the steps into the Spoon, and the door closed behind him soft as a book shutting.

* * *

I cried for an hour, if you must know. Into the tin, which was foolish, because starlights probably shouldn't get saltwater on them, but they didn't seem to mind. They glowed up at me through my fingers, warm and patient, and eventually I fell asleep in the stairwell with my cheek against the cold metal, humming the boat song without meaning to.

And here is the part I have never told anyone, not Ovelia, not Quentin, not the Sisters, not the priest who took my last confession before everything happened:

When I woke near dawn, the tin was open.

All thirteen starlights were still there. Counted them twice, three times. Nothing taken.

But laid on top of them, neat as a merchant's card, was a slip of paper in handwriting I didn't recognize — elegant, old-fashioned, courteous even on paper — and it said:

*Thank you for the songs. Thirteen is a fair beginning. The rest of you sounds lovelier still.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost tender:

*Tell no one. Especially not him. He, of all people, knows what a fair price looks like — he simply cannot bear to watch one be paid.*

I burned the note in the kitchen stove before the maids came down, and I told myself the shiver up my spine was just the cold.

Three nights later, the stars went out.
