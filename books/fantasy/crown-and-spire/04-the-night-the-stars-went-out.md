## 4. The Night the Stars Went Out

The king's soup that evening was pheasant and chestnut, finished with a cream that had cost the crown eleven marks a pint because the cows were fed on clover grown over a buried saint. I know this because I keep the accounts as well as I keep my tongue, and because in the palace of King Aldous the Paranoid, nothing is too small to be priced, weighed, and suspected.

I dipped the silver spoon. I held it under my tongue for the count of thirty — long enough for most venoms to announce themselves with heat, numbness, or a sudden conviction that I could fly. Nothing. Then I chewed the garnish of fried sage, waited another hundred heartbeats, and nodded to the steward.

"The taster finds no fault," I said, which is the longest sentence I am generally permitted before the court, and some nights not even that.

What I did not say: *and it is very good soup, and I wish someone would hand me a bowl of my own instead of making me stand at the sideboard like a piece of furniture that occasionally swallows.*

Eleven years I have stood at that sideboard. Eleven years of being seen by everyone and noticed by no one, which is exactly how I built myself, plank by careful plank, into the man I am. A food-taster is supposed to be forgettable. A man who is forgotten can walk through any door in the palace, including, on Thursdays, the one behind the tapestry of the Huntress, down the stair to the Gilded Spoon, where I keep books for things that have no business existing.

I had ledgers waiting for me tonight. I always do. It is the only place in either of my lives where I feel competent rather than merely useful.

But first there was a feast to survive.

* * *

King Aldous was celebrating the anniversary of his own coronation, an occasion he observed twice a year because once did not seem to satisfy him. The great hall blazed with two hundred candles and the chandeliers were hung with polished mirrors so the light doubled itself, and above it all, through the high glass of the clerestory, the stars came out one by one like guests arriving fashionably late.

I stood where I always stand, tasting what I was given, declining what I wasn't, and letting the noise wash over me. Courtiers lie beautifully when they are full of wine. I collect their lies the way other men collect coins.

"—swear on my mother's grave, the eastern wards hummed all week, like a struck bell—"

"Your mother is alive, Corvel."

"Then on her future grave."

That one went in the ledger of my head under *ward troubles, third report this month*. The border wards had been humming for weeks if half the drunk men in this room were to be believed, and the other half reported dreams of falling upward. Ovelia had gone quiet when I mentioned it at the Spoon, and Ovelia going quiet is louder than most people screaming. She'd only said, "The under-court has been buying. Heavily." And then she'd looked at me the way you look at a door you hope stays shut.

Wren found me between courses, as she always does, materializing at my elbow with the specific grace of a girl who learned early that being small and quick gets you fed.

"You look like a spoon," she said.

"I look like a professional."

"You look like a spoon that's been left out in the rain." She stole a honeyed fig off my tasting tray with the impunity of family. "Save me the almond torte. The kitchen boy owes me a favor and he'll cut the corner piece if you distract the pastry cook."

"How does a kitchen boy come to owe *you* favors?"

"Because I sang his grandmother's cat down from the roof," Wren said, as though this explained everything, and honestly, it did. Her voice does things to animals and candles and, once, to a locked door that I have chosen not to investigate. "Quentin. Look up. The stars are out proper tonight. All of them."

I looked up. Through the clerestory glass, past the candle-glare, the sky was salted thick with them — more than usual, it seemed to me, though I am no astronomer. Bright enough that even here, drowned in lamplight, they made themselves known.

"Make a wish," Wren said.

"Wishes are trade goods," I said. "I've priced them. They're worth less than you'd think."

"You're worth less than you think," she said cheerfully, "and yet here we both are." She bumped her shoulder against my arm — the closest thing to affection the palace allows in public — and went off toward the pastry table like a small determined comet.

I should have followed her. I want that written somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps track of such debts: *at the ninth hour of the feast, Quentin Eriksen should have followed the girl.*

Instead I tasted the almond torte. It was excellent. It needed nothing.

* * *

It happened between the toast and the dancing, in that slack moment when the wine has done its work and the music is deciding what to be.

Later, everyone would describe it differently. Lord Corvel swore the lights flickered first. The queen's cousin insisted there was a smell, like hot metal or struck flint. But what I remember — what I will always remember, because I am cursed with exact recall in precisely the moments I'd prefer to forget — is the sound.

A harp string snapping. One clean note, high and wrong, felt in the teeth more than heard. Every dog in the city began to howl at once.

And then the light changed.

Not the candles. The candles guttered and steadied and burned on, oblivious. It was the *other* light — the faint silver wash that had been coming through the clerestory all evening, the light I hadn't known I was seeing until it stopped. I looked up and the sky through the glass was black. Not clouded. Not overcast. Black the way the inside of a closed book is black. Every star in the heavens, gone, as if a hand had reached up and pinched them out like wicks.

The hall noticed in stages. First the astronomers' table by the east windows went silent. Then a woman screamed, briefly and musically, and stopped herself out of etiquette. Then everyone spoke at once.

King Aldous rose from his chair with the expression of a man who has trained his whole life for catastrophes and resents each one for arriving during his party. "Calm," he announced. "There will be an explanation. There is always an explanation. Master Eriksen—"

And here is the thing about being invisible: when the world breaks, invisible men get handed the pieces anyway, because everyone assumes someone else already claimed them.

"Master Eriksen, go to the alchemist. Tell him the king requires the night-tonic prepared *immediately* — the calming draught, the blue vials. If the stars have been stolen, I will not have my court panicking about it."

"Stolen, Majesty?"

"Everything is stolen or bought," Aldous said, with the weary authority of a man who has purchased many things, "and I want names."

I bowed and went, because obedience is the best disguise there is, and because a small cold voice in me was already whispering *the wards, the humming wards, the snapped-string sound* — and the other voice, the older one, was whispering something worse: *where is Wren.*

I told myself she'd gone to the kitchens. Girls who sing cats off roofs do not vanish; they simply relocate toward food. I told myself the cold crawling up my spine was the draft from the doors.

The corridor outside the hall was chaos in miniature — servants running with candles, guards forming squares around nothing in particular, a chamberlain weeping into a topiary. I moved through all of it the way water moves through cloth, unnoticed, unhindered, a skill I had spent eleven years perfecting and now, for the first time, hated.

At the turn by the armory, a young guard grabbed my sleeve — actually grabbed it, which hadn't happened since my second year — and stared into my face with the wild eyes of a man looking for anyone at all.

"You. You're the taster, aren't you? Did you see it? The string — I *heard* it, everyone heard it, it came from below, from *underneath*, like something broke down in the mountain—"

"Go back to your post," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, and he let go, which didn't. Men don't remember my face. That has saved my life perhaps four times.

Tonight it felt less like armor and more like being buried alive with the lid politely closed.

* * *

The alchemist's tower stank of sulfur and ambition. Fennick — royal alchemist, a soft-spoken man with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that never quite met anything — was already grinding something at his bench, and he did not seem surprised to see me, and he did not seem surprised by the sky, and I filed both of those facts away in the drawer where I keep things I'm not ready to open.

"The king wants the calming draught. Blue vials. Immediately."

"Of course." Fennick smiled his paper smile. "Strange night for it. Though I expect you of all people understand, Master Eriksen, that the heavens are simply another market. Things rise. Things fall. Things are... harvested."

"Harvested," I repeated.

"A figure of speech." He pressed three blue vials into my hands, warm from his pocket, which I noted — potions ought to cool in glass, not ride in wool — and waved me out. "Give the king my regards. And Master Eriksen — do give the little songbird my regards as well, if you see her."

I stopped in the doorway.

"What songbird?"

"The apprentice. The one who visits the mountain road in the evenings. Everyone hears her, you know. She has quite the gift." His smile did not change at all. "The dark appreciates it more than the daylight does."

I have been poisoned eleven times in my career. I know exactly what fear tastes like — bitter almonds, copper, the metallic tang of a body preparing to die. This was not that. This was colder and slower, a frost moving through the blood, and it said, with terrible clarity: *he knows about Wren. Something knows about Wren. Run.*

I ran. I abandoned the blue vials on a window ledge without a thought for the king or my post or eleven years of careful professionalism, and I took the servants' stairs three at a time, and I tore through the kitchens — empty, everyone at the feast or fled — and out into the courtyard where the cold hit like a wall and the sky was a wound with no stars in it.

"Wren!"

The word bounced off stone and came back alone.

She wasn't in the kitchen yard. She wasn't in the stable loft where she liked to sit with the mouser. She wasn't at the well, or under the eaves, or anywhere in the spreading circle of torchlight and shouting that the palace was becoming. I checked places I had no reason to check. I called her name until my voice cracked, and every time I called it, the dark swallowed it faster, as if the night had gotten *hungrier* since the stars went out.

It was nearly midnight, by the chapel bell, when I finally went back inside — back to my post, because a mad idea had lodged in me like a splinter: *her things.* Wren kept things. Buttons, ribbons, a sparrow's feather, the receipt-stubs I gave her when she ran errands for the guild, which I pretended were mere accounting and which she kept like love letters.

Her cloak was still on its peg in the taster's antechamber. Her shoes — she owned one good pair, and wore them only for feasts — were still beneath it.

On the floor, square in the middle of the room, laid flat as if presented, was a single sheet of paper.

I knew the hand immediately. Of course I did. It was mine.

Not similar. Mine. My slanting figures, my habit of crossing sevens, the little hook I put on the terminal r that no scrivener in the city could counterfeit because I invented it out of laziness twenty years ago. Written in my own ledger hand, in ink I did not own, on paper I did not recognize, was this:

---

*Received, of Wren (no surname), one (1) soul, entire, freely given in settlement of account.*

*Consideration rendered: forty-one songs, paid in starlight, plus balance owed against kindnesses uncounted.*

*Buyer: the Court Beneath.*

*This transaction is final. No refunds. No exceptions. Signed and sealed—*

---

And then a seal I had never seen and knew instantly: a spiral, drawn inward, like a staircase seen from above, pressed into black wax that was still, impossibly, warm.

I read it four times. My hands were steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have. Somewhere above us the sky sat empty, and somewhere below us a snapped harp string lay quiet in the dark, and somewhere between the two, a girl who called me family had signed away the thing I hadn't known how to protect because I'd spent eleven years teaching myself that nothing could be protected, only priced.

*Kindnesses uncounted,* the receipt said.

They had counted them anyway. Someone had been keeping my ledger better than I kept it — recording every fig she stole, every night I walked her home, every time I said nothing and meant everything — and they had turned all of it into a debt, and she had paid it, gladly, in the only coin she had, because I had taught her that everything has a price.

No. That last part wasn't fair. She'd learned generosity on her own. What she'd learned from me was that I would never ask for help, never offer it plainly, never once in eleven years say the words *you matter to me* without wrapping them in arithmetic.

The wax seal cooled under my thumb. In the distance, beyond the walls, the mountains made their long black shape against a starless sky, and I thought of Ovelia saying *the under-court has been buying* in a voice like a closing door.

I folded the receipt along its creases and put it inside my shirt, against the skin, where I keep the things I cannot afford to lose and cannot bear to look at.

Then I did the only thing I have ever been genuinely gifted at.

I became no one — and went downstairs.
