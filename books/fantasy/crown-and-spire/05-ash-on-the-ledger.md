## 5. Ash on the Ledger

I have kept books for forty years, and in that time I have learned that a ledger never lies—but it will happily hold its tongue.

The morning after the stars went out, my ledgers began to scream.

Not literally. I want to be clear about that, because Quentin will read this account someday—assuming there is a someday, and assuming Quentin is still Quentin and not some nameless wisp of regret drifting through the under-court—and he will accuse me of melodrama. He does that. He thinks I don't notice him noticing things. Forty years of running a guild that trades in impossible things teaches you exactly one useful skill: spotting the quiet ones who think they're invisible.

But I digress, which is what fear does to a well-ordered mind.

The ledgers screamed in the only way ledgers can. Entries going flat. Ink fading where it should hold. I sat at my desk at dawn with the Gilded Spoon's master ledger open before me, watching a bottle of bottled laughter—one dozen units, sold to the Widow Marsh of Cobble Lane, paid in full—watching the entry *unwrite itself*, the letters going grey, then pale, then gone, leaving behind a ghost of an indentation like a scar on the page.

I dipped my pen and rewrote it. The ink slid off the paper like water off oiled cloth.

"Well," I said, to no one, because my office was empty at dawn and had been empty for thirty years, "that's not good."

* * *

By noon, they came to me. Clients, I mean. They always come to me; I am the face the guild shows when it wants to look respectable, which is most of the time, and I have the sort of face that looks respectable even while it's calculating how much your grandmother's recipe for forgiveness might fetch on the open market. But these were not selling clients. These were *refund* clients.

A cooper from Tanner's Row whose purchased luck—a modest thing, three years of favorable weather for drying wood—had turned sour overnight. Every plank he cut split. Every nail bent. His apprentice had been struck by lightning under a cloudless sky, which even I admitted was showing off.

A gentleman collector—I use the term loosely; he was a fop with money and taste in roughly equal, insufficient measures—who had bought a bottled storm off us two summers past, intending to unleash it on his rival's garden party. He'd kept it on his mantel as a conversation piece. Now he set it on my desk, and I confess I flinched.

It had been, at purchase, a magnificent thing: a squat glass bottle with a thunderhead swirling inside it, purple-dark, spitting the occasional miniature bolt that cracked against the glass like a fly against a window. Now it held nothing but fog. Flat, grey, apologetic fog. A storm that had forgotten it was ever angry.

"You'll refund me, of course," said the fop.

"I'll do no such thing," I said. "You signed the standard waiver. Clause nine. 'All impossible goods are sold as-is, the impossible being notoriously resistant to guarantees.'"

"The storm is *dead*, madam."

"The storm is *flat*. Dead implies we could bury it." I picked up the bottle and swirled it. The fog obeyed the motion sluggishly, like a dog too old to chase sticks. Something about it made my chest hurt, and I am not a woman whose chest hurts easily. "Leave it. Guild policy on failed impossibles: we buy them back at half price and study them."

"Half? It's worthless."

"Everything's worth something to someone who knows what went wrong in it." I counted out the coin because arguing took longer, and because I wanted the bottle. When he'd gone, I held it up to the light and looked into that dead grey nothing, and I thought: *this didn't fail. This was spent. Something drank it.*

I have been in this trade long enough to know the difference between a thing breaking and a thing being used up. A broken lock has splinters. An opened one just hangs there, swinging, embarrassed.

* * *

Emergency session was called for sundown, which is to say I called it, in the guild's oldest chamber—the Coin Room, where the walls are lined with the first currencies we ever traded: teeth, promises, a jar containing somebody's last words. The senior members assembled: Master Corvin of acquisitions, who looks like a heron that has learned to wear clothes; the twins Esme and Estha of appraisal, who finish each other's silences; old Harrow, who handles the darker end of our inventory and has not blinked since the reign of Aldous's grandfather; and Quentin.

Quentin arrived last, as usual, slipping in as though the door had merely happened to be near him. That is his gift, you understand—or his curse, or his craft, I've never fully decided which. Some men work hard to be overlooked. Quentin *is* overlooked, the way stone is grey and water is wet. In forty years I have watched kings forget his face mid-sentence, watched guards wave through the man carrying their own poison, watched his own name slide off people's tongues like a fish. I once introduced him to a client twice in one evening and the client found nothing strange in it.

I have often wondered if it was a talent he found or a talent that found him. That night I stopped wondering, because I finally understood what such a talent must cost, and I felt, briefly and inconveniently, the urge to put my hand on the boy's shoulder. I did not. We are a guild, not a family.

Though God help me, sometimes I wonder about that too.

"Report," I said.

Corvin reported. Bottled courage gone watery across three districts. A sold memory of flying that its owner now recalled as a bad fall. The winter we sold on credit—the famous one, the beautiful one, the sculptor's winter that half the north side had walked through on a warm spring day—was melting in its buyer's cellar, and not into water but into *nothing*, the snow vanishing before it touched the ground, as though being called home.

"Failures city-wide," Corvin finished. "Since the night of—"

"Don't," said Esme.

"—since the stars went out," said Estha, because twins are contractually obligated to be unbearable.

Harrow spoke without opening his eyes. "Impossible things are star-stuff. Always have been. Bottled laughter is a laugh that was too true for one throat, caught at the moment it touched something older than the laugher. A sold luck is a thread of pattern pulled loose from the weave. And the weave—" he tapped the table with one yellow nail, "—is woven from the same light as the sky. Take away the stars, and everything made of starlight begins to starve."

"That's the official explanation, then," I said. "Comforting. Wrong, but comforting."

Every head turned to me. Even Harrow's eyes opened, which I took as a compliment.

"The failures aren't starvation," I said. "Starvation is slow. These are withdrawals. Someone is *spending* the impossible inventory of this city, quickly and in bulk, and whatever they're buying with it requires a great deal of fuel." I set the flat storm-bottle on the table with a click. "So I did what one does. I checked our accounts against our debts. And I found that for eleven months, a single buyer has been acquiring, through intermediaries, more product than any entity has ever purchased from us or any guild like us."

"What product?" Quentin asked. Quietly. Of course quietly.

I let the silence sit, because a guild mistress learns to make silence do her shouting.

"Souls," I said.

The word dropped into the room like a coin into a deep well.

"Not whole ones—not yet. Slivers. Echoes. The part of a person that remembers a song after they've forgotten the words. The ambition of a man who no longer has any. First loves sold back for cash by people who wanted to stop hurting." I spread my hands. "We don't deal in those, as a rule. Too risky, too heavy, and the paperwork is ghastly. But other channels do, and the trail leads down. All of it leads down. To the Court Beneath the mountain."

Esme and Estha swore in unison, which was the first time all evening they agreed on anything audible.

"They're old stories," Corvin protested. "Nursery tales. Nobles under the hill who buy your shadow if you're careless at crossroads—"

"Nursery tales are inventory lists that got soft over the telling," I snapped. "The under-court is real, it is awake, and it has been shopping. Eleven months of purchases, accelerating every week. And now the stars go dark in a single night, every impossible thing in the city goes flat within hours, and none of you can tell me those facts are strangers to each other." I looked around the table, letting each of them feel the weight of what I said next. "Here is my theory, and I pray someone in this room can improve upon it. Souls burn. Starlight burns. The under-court has been buying souls as fuel—kindling, tinder—to drag the stars down out of the sky. Not to extinguish them. To *harvest* them. You don't blow out a lamp you intend to carry home."

The room was very still. Old Harrow laughed, once, a sound like a shovel striking gravel.

"And the wards?" he said. "The border wards fell the same hour. Those were sealed with dragon-fire. Nothing buys dragon-fire."

"Nothing has to buy what it can simply wait out," I said. "Brammel is the last. Fires gutter, Harrow. Even his."

Quentin had not moved through any of this. That was typical. What was less typical was the quality of his stillness. Ordinary Quentin-stillness is a stone's stillness, a piece of furniture's stillness. This was the stillness of a drawn bowstring, and I realized, with the particular dread of a woman who has spent decades reading faces professionally, that he had gone very quiet indeed about something that mattered very much to him.

"Wren," I said.

He looked at me. For a moment—for the first time in all the years I have known him—he let me see all the way in, and what I saw in there was so raw and so young that it frightened me worse than anything on my list of theories.

"She's gone," he said. "Her bed wasn't slept in. There was a receipt in my ledger. Her soul. Sold. My handwriting." His voice stayed level; that was the terrible part. "It's my handwriting, Ovelia."

"I know," I said gently. "The under-court forges well. Or it borrows hands. Either way—" and here I made the mistake I will regret until they lay me in the ground, either way I chose the ledger over the boy, "—you are not going after her."

The moment I said it, I knew it was the wrong sentence, said in the wrong voice, at the wrong volume. Because Quentin's face closed like a book, politely, and I watched him file the sentence away in whatever drawer he keeps the world's small betrayals.

"It isn't safe," I pressed on, because a captain cannot un-order the charge halfway through. "The guild forbids it. The treaty—"

"The treaty," said Harrow softly, and even he had the grace not to look at me directly.

"Yes," I said. "The treaty."

"What treaty?" Quentin asked.

And there it was: the question I had spent thirty years hoping never to answer in this room, in this lifetime, to this particular young man.

"The guild," I said, and my mouth tasted like ash on a ledger page, "has certain arrangements. Old ones. We do not trade below a certain stair, and in exchange, the Court Beneath does not trade above it. We honor their territory; they honor ours. It was signed before the palace had its present foundations, and it has one clause relevant to tonight: the Spoon does not pursue reclaimed goods into the under-court. Ever. Whatever is taken below stays below."

"So Wren stays below," Quentin said.

"The guild's survival depends on that clause holding."

"Then let the guild survive," he said, standing, gathering his coat, and his voice was so mild, so reasonable, so perfectly *Quentin*, that it took me a heartbeat to hear the door slamming inside it. "I'll note the abstention in the minutes."

"Quentin—"

"Goodnight, guild mistress."

And he was gone, the way only he can leave a room—not dramatically, not with a slammed door, but with the simple, unnerving impression that he had never been in it at all.

* * *

Afterward, the others drifted out. Harrow lingered at the door.

"You told him no," he said.

"I did."

"He was going to go anyway."

"I know," I said.

"You could have told him yes. Given him backup. Given him the map of the lower tunnels we definitely don't keep."

"Harrow."

"He reminds you of someone," Harrow said, and left before I could find something sharp enough to throw.

I sat alone in the Coin Room with the flat storm and the ledgers, and I did something I had not done in thirty years. I unlocked the bottom drawer of my desk and took out the first ledger page I ever wrote, when I was young and honest and had not yet learned that institutions survive by eating their own conscience a bite at a time.

I won't tell you what's on it. Not tonight. Tonight I will only say that I looked at that page, and then I looked at the empty chair where Quentin sits when he thinks no one counts how often he sits there, and I understood with terrible clarity that I had just made the same choice the guild has been making for three hundred years: the clause over the child. The treaty over the truth. The books balanced against a girl's soul.

The storm-bottle sat on the table between my hands, grey and spent. I picked it up and shook it, harder than dignity allows, the way you shake a lantern that has gone out on a dark road.

And—perhaps I imagined it; I had drunk no wine; but perhaps the glass was thinner in places where hope had worn it—from somewhere deep in the fog, faint as a memory of thunder, something growled.

I set the bottle down very carefully, took a fresh page, and began to write.

The entry read: *One debt, unpaid. One girl, taken. One clause, burned.*

I underlined nothing. Underlining is for people who believe their own resolutions.

Then I blew out my candle, sat in the dark, and admitted what I had known since dawn: Quentin Eriksen was already descending the mountain, and he would not stop, and every step he took toward that court would be a step the treaty could not survive—which meant that if I wanted to save both the guild and the boy, I had perhaps four days to decide which one I was willing to burn.

Somewhere far beneath me, I swear the mountain swallowed.
