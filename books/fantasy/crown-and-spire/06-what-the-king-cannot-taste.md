## 6. What the King Cannot Taste

There is a particular art to tasting for a paranoid king, and I have mastered it the way other men master the lute or the sword: obsessively, at the cost of everything else a normal life might contain.

King Aldous does not trust his cooks, his brewers, his wife's family, his brother, his brother's wife, or the concept of soup in general. He trusts me, insofar as a man like Aldous can trust anything—meaning he trusts my fear of dying more than he trusts my loyalty, which is, frankly, the soundest basis for trust I've ever encountered. Every dish passes my tongue before it passes his. Every cup. Every crumb of marzipan shaped like his own face, which the confectioner insists is flattery and which I insist on tasting anyway, because if someone were going to poison the king, they'd hardly do it in the turnip stew. They'd do it in the sugar.

So it was that I knelt at the king's elbow that evening, as the court dined beneath chandeliers that burned ordinary candle-oil because there was nothing else to burn, and took my sip of the nightly tonic.

The tonic comes last. It has come last for eleven years, prepared by Master Corvane the royal alchemist, delivered in a grey glass vial with a wax seal, and taken by the king with the grim devotion of a man swallowing his own mortality on schedule. Bitter root and silverleaf, everyone says. Good for the humors. The king drinks it and sleeps like the dead, which everyone agrees is a great blessing, since His Majesty's dreams used to be legendary things, full of shouting and prophecy and once, memorably, an attempt to knight a chair.

I held the vial, broke the seal, sipped.

And stopped.

Now. A food-taster who spits is a food-taster who hangs, so I did not spit. I swallowed, kept my face arranged into its usual expression of professional blankness—the one I've worn so long it has likely fossilized—and passed the vial up with a nod. *Safe.* The king drank. The court relaxed by exactly the degree it always relaxes when the tonic goes down, and dinner conversation resumed its usual murmur: grain tariffs, a scandal involving a countess and a huntsman, complaints about the dark.

Everyone was complaining about the dark. Six nights now without stars, and the city had run out of explanations. Priests said it was penance. Astronomers said it was distance. My lord chamberlain said it was weather, which is what people say when they have given up saying anything true.

But the tonic tasted wrong.

Not poisoned-wrong. Nothing so kind. There was a note under the bitterness I knew from the guild's back room—a cold, mineral thing, like licking a frost-covered window, like the smell of the air off the glacier lake where Ovelia once sent me to appraise a frozen mermaid. I had catalogued it myself, three years ago, in ledger entry forty-something: *essence of forgetting, one dram, sold to buyer unknown, paid in old coins.*

The Gilded Spoon trades in impossible things, and I keep its books, and somewhere in those books was the recipe of the king's good night's sleep.

* * *

I want to be honest about what I did next, because honesty is not my native tongue and this part shames me.

Nothing. I did nothing. I finished the service, bowed, walked out through the servants' corridor counting flagstones the way I do when my thoughts need herding, and went home to my narrow room above the guildhall, and sat down at my desk, and pulled the ledgers toward me, and began to look.

Here is what you must understand about me: invisibility has been my life's work. Not the magic kind—or so I believed until very recently—but the practiced kind. I am the man whose name servers forget mid-sentence. I am the taster kneeling three feet from the throne whom no courtier has ever described afterward. For years I told myself this was craft. Caution. Wisdom. *The mouse survives by being boring.* And underneath that story, in the cellar of myself where I keep everything true, I knew it was simpler than that: I did not want to be seen because to be seen is to be losable, and I had done enough losing before Wren ever called me family.

Wren. Six nights gone. One soul, sold, in my own hand.

I had not slept since. I mention this not for sympathy but because it explains why, when I found the entries, my hands were steady and my heart was not, and why the wry voice in my head—the one that usually narrates my life like a disappointed accountant—had gone very quiet.

The Gilded Spoon's ledgers record everything we trade, and I have kept them faithfully for nine years, including the years when keeping them meant writing down things no decent person should price. I turned back through the volumes, night by night, season by season, and laid the purchases end to end like beads on a string.

*Essence of forgetting, two drams, sold to buyer unknown.*
*Essence of forgetting, four drams, winter quarter.*
*A memory of the night sky over the western sea, entire, purchased from a retired admiral; disposition: sold south, buyer unknown.*
*One lullaby about stars, bought from a washerwoman for the price of a coffin; disposition: sold, buyer unknown.*

Buyer unknown. Buyer unknown. Buyer unknown. But I had been the one to carry some of those parcels to the drop point, a stairwell door in the Salt Quarter that opened onto cold air and a courteous voice that never showed its face. *The Court Beneath,* Ovelia had said, warning me weeks ago that it had grown hungry. And now I traced its appetite backward through my own neat handwriting and found it fed at the highest table in the kingdom.

The king's tonic. Eleven years of the king's tonic.

I sat back. Candle guttering. My breath loud in the little room.

It wasn't poison. That was the elegant horror of it. Corvane had brewed something that worked exactly as advertised—it *was* good for the humors, in the way that cutting out a man's tongue is good for his manners. Silverleaf and bitter root and, threaded through them, essence of forgetting, dosed nightly, not to the king alone but through him to the court. Because the king shares his tonics with his favorites when they look peaky. Because the alchemist attends every banquet and "refreshes" the wine of anyone who seems troubled. Because for eleven years the royal court of Aldmere had been drinking a slow, gentle erasure, and nobody noticed the shape of what went missing, because the shape of it was *the stars*, and the stars were gone.

That was why the panic had faded. That was why six nights of empty sky had curdled into tariff talk within days. Grief needs a memory to grip, and Corvane had been sanding theirs smooth, dose by dose. The wards fell, the stars went out, the under-court reached up through the gap—and the court above simply... forgot to be afraid.

And me? I taste everything. I have spent twenty years dosing my own body against every poison in the catalogue, building tolerances layer by layer the way a mason builds a wall. Whatever else the tonic carried, it could not touch me. I had made myself immune to everything except, apparently, understanding my own complicity.

I had sipped it. I had nodded. *Safe.* I had handed the king his forgetting and watched him swallow it with the contentment of a man doing something healthy.

* * *

Morning found me in the alchemist's tower, because I am invisible, and invisible men go where they like.

Master Corvane received the king's taster the way he always did—with the vague courtesy one shows a piece of furniture that has learned to bow. He was at his workbench, a spare grey man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles he polished when he didn't want to meet your eyes, surrounded by the usual apparatus: retorts, mortars, jars of things labeled in a cipher I happened to know, having priced several of their contents.

"Taster Eriksen," he said. Actually, he said "taster," the way everyone says it, my first name having been sanded off me years ago by my own careful hand. "Something for His Majesty?"

"His Majesty slept poorly," I said. This was even true; I had heard him shouting at the chair again. "He asks whether the tonic might be strengthened."

Corvane's spectacles flashed. "Strengthened, hm. I'll prepare a fresh batch." And he turned to his shelf, and he took down a jar, and here is where nine years of bookkeeping earned their keep: I recognized the jar. Lot number 114, essence of forgetting, the very consignment I had carried down to the Salt Quarter stairwell in autumn, wrapped in oilcloth, humming faintly against my ribs like a caged moth.

I watched him measure it with a pipette, steady as a surgeon. I watched him murmur the counter-words that bind a volatile essence into syrup. And then, because the workbench stood between us and his back was turned and I have spent my whole life becoming the thing nobody watches, I let my eyes travel where they wanted: to the corner shelf, where five empty grey vials stood in a rack, and beside them a sixth, sealed, waiting. And beneath the rack, half-hidden by a ledger of Corvane's own—

A receipt. Written in a hand I knew better than my own heartbeat, because it was my own hand. Or nearly. Copied. Copied perfectly, down to my habit of crossing sevens, down to the little hook I put on the letter *Q* that no one else writes that way.

*Received of the Court Beneath: forty crowns' weight of old gold. Rendered: access schedule of the royal kitchens, the seal-signature of the master of wine, and continued silence of the king's taster.*

Continued silence of the king's taster.

He had bought me. Not my body, not my loyalty—those were never for sale and never needed to be. He had bought my *invisibility*. All those years I thought I was hiding, and some careful creature beneath the mountain had looked at my hiding and seen a commodity. My unnoticeability, itemized and invoiced. The reason no guard ever questioned me. The reason no clerk ever checked my credentials. The reason Corvane had measured essence of forgetting with his back to a witness and never once worried about it.

I had hoarded my smallness all my life like a miser hoards coins, and it turns out the coin was counterfeit, minted elsewhere, circulating at someone else's direction.

Corvane turned around, fresh vial in hand, and smiled his grey smile. "Tell His Majesty this batch is stronger. He'll dream of nothing at all."

"I'm sure," I said, and bowed, and took the vial in both hands like the sacred duty it was supposed to be.

* * *

I did not deliver it.

I want that recorded plainly, in my own hand, in whatever ledger will have it: I walked out of the tower carrying the king's tonic, and I turned left instead of right, and I did not deliver it.

Instead I climbed to my room and set the vial on my desk next to my ledgers, grey glass against black binding, and looked at it for a long time. The wry voice came back eventually. *Well,* it observed. *You've finally found a poison you can't build a tolerance to. It's called being an accessory.*

Because that was the arithmetic, wasn't it? Every morning I had knelt and tasted and nodded while a kingdom forgot the sky. Staying invisible had been my comfort, my craft, my whole personality—and now it was a line item in someone else's purchase order. There is no neutral corner anymore. There is no flagstone small enough to stand on. Either I was the king's taster, or I was the under-court's instrument, and the difference between those things had narrowed, tonight, to the width of a single undelivered vial.

Wren would have known what to do immediately. Wren would have done something loud and warm and probably foolish, and it would have been *right* in the way that matters. That was the thing she'd thrown at me our last evening together, hurt turning her voice sharp: *You keep everyone at arm's length, Quentin. You keep the whole world at arm's length. What are you saving yourself for?*

For this, apparently. For the moment when staying unseen becomes the same as helping them take her.

I uncorked the vial and smelled it: frost on a window, the glacier lake, the smell of the vault where my soul's receipt had been filed. Then I stoppered it again. Not poured out—I am not a fool, and evidence destroyed is leverage lost. Instead I wrapped it in oilcloth, the same oilcloth, if the gods have any sense of humor at all, that had once carried lot number 114 down to the Salt Quarter. Into my coat pocket. Heavy as a sin.

Then I took out a fresh sheet of paper and, for the first time in nine years, wrote something in the ledger that was not a transaction:

*Tonight I stopped keeping accounts and started settling them.*

Underneath, I drew the only map I needed: the Salt Quarter stairwell, the courteous door that opens onto cold air, the drop schedule I knew by heart because I had been the courier. They had bought my silence and my unnoticeability, and they had been right to, because those were real and valuable things. But they had made one error, the same error everyone makes about mice.

They assumed a mouse stays a mouse because it cannot be anything else.

I put out the candle, pocketed the vial, and went looking for a dragon.
