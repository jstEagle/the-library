## 7. A Debt Owed in Dragon-Fire

The mountain did not want visitors. I could tell because it kept trying to kill me.

First there was the scree slope that shifted under my boots like a ledger being balanced by a drunk. Then the wind, which came off the high passes with the particular malice of something that had been paid to do this. By the time I reached the treeline's last stubborn pines, bent sideways like courtiers bowing to a king they hated, my hands were cut in four places and my pride was bleeding somewhere internal.

I had told no one where I was going. That was easy. Telling no one was the one skill I'd perfected over thirty years of tasting other people's food and keeping other people's secrets. Ovelia had forbidden me from going after Wren — *the guild does not chase debts into the dark, Quentin* — and so I had done what any sensible man does when his family is stolen and his institution fails him: I stole a horse, forged a travel writ in the hand of a minor customs official whose handwriting I'd memorized years ago for exactly this sort of occasion, and left before dawn.

The horse was named Turnip. He deserved better than me.

"You know," I said to him, as we climbed the switchbacks above the ash-line, "in a fair world, this would be someone else's errand. Someone brave. Someone with a sword and a jawline."

Turnip snorted, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

The wards were supposed to be up here. That was the whole point of the mountain: the old stories said dragon-fire had sealed the borders against the things beneath, back when there were dragons enough to spare, back when the sky still had its stars sewn on properly. Four nights ago every star in the heavens had gone out at once, and the wards had fallen with them, and Wren had vanished leaving nothing but a receipt in my own handwriting. One soul. Sold. Paid in full.

I had read that receipt perhaps two hundred times. I knew the way the ink sat slightly wrong, as if written by someone wearing my hands like gloves. I knew the little flourish I always put on the final digit, the one habit I couldn't break even when forging myself.

Whoever wrote it had studied me carefully. That frightened me more than the dark did.

* * *

I found the cave by smell before I found it by sight. Sulfur, yes, but underneath it something older — hot stone and old smoke and a sweetness like resin weeping from a wound. The entrance was a black seam in the mountainside, half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, and when I ducked through it with Turnip tethered outside (I apologized to him; he bit my sleeve; we understood each other), the air changed.

It went warm. Not comfortable-warm. *Attended*-warm. The warmth of a room where something enormous is breathing slowly and has been for a very long time.

The tunnel descended in wide spirals, the walls smooth as poured glass in places, rough as torn bread in others. My eyes adjusted to a light that didn't come from any torch. It came from below, a deep amber glow, pulsing.

Slowly. Like a lamp running out of oil.

I have stood in rooms with kings. I have watched men die of poison and pretend to enjoy the soup beforehand. I am not easily awed. But when the tunnel opened into the great hollow heart of the mountain, I stopped walking, and I confess without shame that my breath left me and took its time coming back.

She filled the cavern the way weather fills a valley.

Brammel. Last of the dragons. I had expected — I don't know what. A monster out of a woodcut, all teeth and menace. Instead she lay coiled around a floor of fused glass her own body heat had made, and she was *dim*. Her scales, which must once have thrown back light like hammered gold, had gone the color of old pewter, of ash pressed under a grate. Along her spine ran a ridge of embers, and those embers guttered as I watched, flaring and failing, flaring and failing, in rhythm with her breath.

A dying lamp. That was the thought that came, unbidden and cruel. The last fire in the world, and it was burning down to its wick.

"Either state your business," she said, without opening her eyes, "or stand there gawking until you freeze. Both are traditional."

Her voice was not what I expected either. It was dry and precise, an old schoolmistress's voice, the voice of someone who had graded ten thousand disappointing essays and remembered each one.

"I'm looking for the fire that sealed the wards," I said.

"Mm. And I'm looking for the stars to come home. We are both likely to be disappointed." One eye opened then — a slit of molten copper, banked but not dead — and considered me. "You're the taster."

I went very still. Being known is a thing I have spent a lifetime avoiding; being *recognized*, in a cave at the top of the world by a creature who should have had no use for palace gossip, set every alarm I owned ringing.

"How—"

"The wards fell," Brammel said. "You felt it. Everyone felt it. And now the mountain is full of small noises climbing toward me — mice, men, memories. You are the quietest of them. That's how I know what you are." The copper eye narrowed. "Or rather, that's how I know what you *aren't*. You aren't anything, taster. You're a hole shaped like a man. Walking up my mountain in a hole shaped like a story."

"That sounds insulting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be diagnostic." Her head shifted, and the whole cavern groaned like a ship changing tack. Embers along her spine flared, dimmed. "Ask your question. My patience is shorter than it used to be. Most of me is."

"Why did the wards fall?"

"Because they were never stone, boy. They were light. Dragon-fire braided with starlight, woven into the skin of the world, and it held for nine hundred years because both ends of the braid held." Her breath came out slow, and with it a wash of heat that smelled of every hearth that had ever been lit. "Then your clever court beneath the mountain began buying souls and burning them for fuel, and the stars above began to gutter, and the braid frayed. When the last star went dark, the wards didn't fall. They *starved*."

"And they can be relit."

"With fire, yes. Mine." A sound came out of her that might have been laughter in a younger throat — a dry rasp, like a page turning. "There's the joke of it, taster. I am the last ember of the first age, and everyone wants me for something. The guild wants me catalogued. The under-court wants me devoured. The crown wants me stuffed and mounted in a museum. And now a hole-shaped man climbs my mountain to ask me to spend what's left of myself relighting a fence around a kingdom that forgot I exist."

"I haven't forgotten."

"No," she agreed, and something in the copper eye softened by a degree I might have imagined. "You haven't. That's why you're here instead of someone useful."

I should have turned around then. I want that noted, wherever ledgers are kept. I should have turned around, because I heard the price coming the way a taster hears almond in wine — that faint bitter note under the sweetness, the note that means *stop*.

"What's your price?" I asked anyway.

Her head lowered until the great blunt wedge of her face hung above me, close enough that I could see my own reflection in the copper eye, warped and small. Close enough to feel the heat of her breath press my hair flat.

"Fuel," she said. "Fire needs feeding, taster. I burned nine hundred years of hoard sealing those wards, and I have been eating my own tail ever since. To relight them — truly relight them, bright enough to hold against what's coming — I need something that burns long and burns true." The embers along her spine pulsed. "Not wood. Not coal. Not souls; I've seen what souls burn like, and it's obscene. Something rarer."

"Name it."

"A self," Brammel said. "A whole one. Freely given. A life's worth of being — memory, name, face, the lot — fed into the flame. It would keep the wards lit for another age." She drew back, and the cavern cooled by a single degree, and I understood she had given me the truth precisely because it was unbearable. "That is my price. Now you know it. Go home and grow old."

I stood there doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. A self. I had spent thirty years building a self out of absence — out of small doses and smaller gestures, out of being the man nobody looked at twice. And here was a creature offering to take even that.

And yet some traitorous part of me whispered: *you were going to give yourself away anyway, down the stair that isn't there, to buy back a girl who calls you family.*

"I can't pay that," I said. "Not yet."

"Not yet," Brammel repeated. She tasted the words like I taste wine. "Interesting. Most men say *never*. You say *not yet*, which means you've already decided, and you're only waiting for the accounting to balance." The copper eye closed again. "Go away, taster. Come back when you've stopped pretending you have a choice."

* * *

I made it eleven steps toward the tunnel before she spoke again.

"One more thing. A professional courtesy." The glow behind me deepened; she had raised her head. "Your invisibility. Your careful, cultivated, thirty-years-in-the-making unnoticeability. You dose yourself against poisons. What do you dose yourself against *memory*?"

I turned. "I don't understand the question."

"No," she said. "You wouldn't. Let an old creature check her sums." The copper eye fixed on me, and I felt something pass over me like a draft under a door — not painful, not invasive, just *attentive*, the way a jeweler's loupe is attentive. Whatever she saw made her go quiet for a long moment.

"Well," she said finally. "Well, well."

"Well *what*?"

"You think you taught yourself to be unseen. Practice, discipline, dull clothes, a forgettable face." She laughed her dry page-turning laugh, and this time there was no humor in it at all. "Taster. You didn't learn that. You *are* that. It's not a habit. It's a gift — the rarest kind, the kind that gets its bearer killed in every age that remembers it exists. The gift of being unremembered. People look at you and their minds slide off, like rain off oiled leather. You don't make yourself invisible. You *are* invisibility, wearing a man's coat."

The cavern was very warm and very quiet. Somewhere far above, wind worried at the frozen waterfall.

"That's absurd," I said. "People remember me. Ovelia remembers me. Wren—"

"Wren," Brammel said softly, "is a child who sings to the dark, and children who sing to the dark see true. Ask yourself why she's the only one who ever called you family unprompted. Ask yourself why the guild keeps a taster on the books who no client can describe a week later. Ask yourself why your parents—" She stopped. Her mouth closed with a click like a bank vault.

"My parents *what*?" I crossed three steps toward her before I caught myself. "My parents were tasters. Ordinary. That's all anyone's ever said."

"Anyone who's allowed to say anything," Brammel said. "Yes. I imagine it is." She settled her head back onto the glass floor, and the embers along her spine guttered low, and for a moment I thought she was done with me. Then, quietly, almost gently, in the voice of a schoolmistress handing back a failed exam:

"They made the same bargain you're circling, boy. The ones who came before you always do. The gift runs in lines, and the line always ends the same way — a hole-shaped person trades the hole, or fills it, and either way the line stops." Her eye closed. "Now get out of my mountain. I'm dying, and you're late for whatever terrible thing you're going to do next."

* * *

Turnip was waiting where I'd left him, steaming faintly in the cold, wearing the expression of an animal who has been asked to believe in me too many times already. I untied him and we started down through the blue dusk of the ash-line, and I did not speak, because for once I had nothing wry to say.

Unremembered. Not unnoticed — *unremembered*. Every conversation I'd ever had, filed away in other people's heads under a shape that slid out overnight. Every kindness evaporating by morning. Thirty years of loneliness I had blamed on my own caution, and it hadn't been caution at all. It had been a nature. A magic. A curse with good posture.

Except Wren. Wren remembered. Wren called me *family* across dinner tables and ledger desks, stubborn as a weed in a crack, and I had thought it was because she was young and foolish and loved easily.

Maybe it was because she saw true. Maybe I was the only thing she'd ever looked at and *kept*.

And maybe that was why the under-court had wanted her soul specifically — no. No, I wasn't ready to follow that thread yet. Some threads you follow when you have rope enough.

What I knew, riding down through the freezing dark with a stolen horse and a head full of broken arithmetic, was this: I had walked up the mountain asking a dragon to relight the wards, and I had come down owning a debt I couldn't pay, a nature I couldn't deny, and a question I couldn't put down.

*My parents. What did they trade? Who did they trade it to?*

There was exactly one place in the city where impossible transactions got recorded, and exactly one woman ruthless enough to have buried the answer in her ledgers.

Ovelia was going to be furious.

Good. Furious people tell the truth faster.
