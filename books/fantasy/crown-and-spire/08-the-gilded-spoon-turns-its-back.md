## 8. The Gilded Spoon Turns Its Back

I have expelled eleven members from the Gilded Spoon in my thirty years as guild mistress. I remember each of them the way a surgeon remembers incisions: precisely, without flinching, and with a private tally of how much blood I spilled.

Quentin Eriksen would be the twelfth. He was also the only one whose expulsion I rehearsed in front of a mirror, which is a thing I will deny to my grave, if I am ever granted one, which is doubtful, given the state of my accounts.

He came when I summoned him—of course he did. Quentin is the sort of man who arrives exactly on time for his own execution and apologizes for tracking in rain. He stood in my office among the shelves of impossible inventory—the bottled laughter humming faintly on its top shelf, the winter in its crate breathing frost through the slats—and he had the decency not to look at the ledger already open on my desk to the page that damned him.

"You went through my books," he said.

"I go through everyone's books. It's called bookkeeping." I turned the ledger toward him. "Chapter and verse, taster. Three nights ago you accessed the sealed archive. You copied the Treaty of Hollow Light. And this morning a courier who answers to no guild I know delivered a message from beneath the mountain, addressed to you, which my ward-keeper intercepted because my ward-keeper does her job, unlike some people who used to do their jobs."

His face did what it always does, which is nothing. Twenty years I have watched that face refuse to have expressions, and I have come to think of it less as a mask than as a locked room where furniture occasionally thumps. But I've kept ledgers long enough to read entries others can't see. The thumping was loud this morning.

"Say it plainly," he said.

"Fine. You stole guild secrets to bargain with the Court Beneath. For the girl."

"For Wren."

"Don't correct me," I snapped, and immediately regretted the volume, because the bottled laughter on the shelf giggled nervously, and there is nothing more unsettling than inventory taking sides. I lowered my voice. "For the girl. Yes."

* * *

Here is what I did not say, and what I will now say to no one but this page, since pages don't gossip:

The treaty is real. The Treaty of Hollow Light, sealed four hundred years ago by a guild mistress considerably braver than me or considerably stupider—I've read her handwriting and honestly it could go either way. In exchange for the under-court's leaving the surface alone, we agreed to three things. We would never trade upward—that is, nothing bought below could be resold above. We would never speak of the treaty outside these walls. And once a generation, when the Court Beneath grew hungry enough to come looking, we would... facilitate. Point them toward someone expendable. Someone unremembered. Someone whose absence would cost the world nothing.

Do you understand now why a guild that trades in impossible things keeps its oldest entry under lock? Not because it's valuable. Because it's shameful. Because every fifty years or so, some guild mistress has signed off on feeding a person to the dark so the rest of us could keep selling bottled weather at a comfortable margin.

And Quentin Eriksen—the most unremembered man I have ever employed, the man whose own mother once forgot his birthday while he sat across the table—walked into my archive and read that document knowing, *knowing*, that his entire life had been an inventory item waiting for a buyer.

He didn't steal the treaty to sell it. He stole it to learn the price list. He wanted to know what the under-court pays, so he could outbid them for a child's soul.

That is either the most loyal thing anyone has ever done in this building or the stupidest, and after thirty years I can tell you those are frequently the same thing.

* * *

"You understand what you've done," I said, when he'd read enough. "Not the theft. The theft I could forgive; half my best traders are thieves, it's practically a qualification. You've done something worse. You've made yourself *interesting* to them."

"They were already interested. They took Wren."

"And they'll take you next, and then they'll have a taster's palate, a ledger-keeper's memory, and a gift for being forgotten, all in one purchase, and they will use all three to walk up our stairs some night and buy the whole guild out from under us." I stood. My knees complained. Everything complains at my age except my temper, which has remained disgustingly youthful. "You didn't just risk yourself, Quentin. You risked the treaty. You risked every soul I'm obligated to protect, including ones who haven't been born yet."

"The treaty protects no one," he said quietly. "It just decides who gets eaten first."

And there it was. The sentence I'd been building defenses against for thirty years, delivered flat as an invoice total. I had prepared rebuttals. I had, God help me, prepared *anecdotes*. None of them survived contact with that sentence, because he was right, and both of us knew it, and the knowing sat between us like a third party at the negotiation.

"So," he said. "Am I expelled?"

* * *

Here is where I should describe the ceremony. There is one. The bell over the door rings backward, the expelled member's name is struck from the rolls in ink mixed with ash, and the guild seal is pressed to a notice that goes up in the hall. It's meant to be dignified. Mostly it's meant to warn the others.

Instead I found myself doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. Eleven expulsions. In every case, I slept fine afterward. In ten cases, the expelled member had wronged the guild. In the eleventh, she'd wronged me personally, which made it easier, if anything.

Quentin had wronged no one. He had, in fact, done the single most selfless act in the guild's recent history, and I was about to punish him for it, because the alternative was admitting that the institution I'd given my life to runs on a conveyor belt of sacrificed nobodies—and that I, Ovelia Vess, keeper of the rolls, have been the hand on the lever.

"Before you answer," he said, "you should know I'm going anyway. Whether you expel me or not."

"I know," I said. "That's rather the problem. A member who disobeys is a problem. An ex-member who disobeys is a stranger trespassing, and strangers get treated differently." I let that land. He didn't flinch, which I expected; he'd spent a lifetime practicing not flinching. "If you're caught down there, no one comes for you. No ransom. No rescue. The guild's hands are clean, officially."

"Understood."

"*Unofficially*—" and here my voice did something traitorous, cracked slightly, and I hated myself for it— "unofficially, the kitchen maid Marta knows the under-stair, and she owes me a favor older than you are, and if a certain ex-member happened to be standing near the scullery door at midnight, certain documents might find their way into his pocket. Maps. In a child's handwriting. Which I certainly did not obtain, because obtaining them would violate the treaty, and I am a woman of strict principle."

He looked at me then. Really looked, the way he almost never lets himself, and I saw the locked room open one inch, and inside it—not gratitude, worse than gratitude, *trust*—and I nearly rang the backward bell right then just to end the conversation before I did something unforgivable, like hug him.

"Ovelia," he said. "Why are you helping me?"

"Because you're the best ledger-keeper I've ever had, and watching you mangle double-entry from below the mountain would be unbearable."

"That's not—"

"It is entirely the reason. Don't make it strange." I pulled the ash-ink toward me. "Name?"

"You know my name."

"I need to hear you say it. For the record. The record requires it."

A pause. Something moved behind his eyes—hesitation, maybe, or the beginning of understanding about what striking a name really means, what it costs, what it forecloses. Then, quietly: "Quentin Eriksen."

I wrote it. My hand shook. I blamed the ink.

* * *

The bell rang backward. The sound is supposed to be solemn—a cracked, reversed peal, like time swallowing. Instead it sounded cheap and small in the empty hall, and Quentin stood under it with his coat already buttoned, because of course he'd packed before coming, because he plans everything, because planning is how a man makes himself invisible even to his own feelings.

The notice went up. I wrote it myself: *Expelled for theft of guild secrets and trafficking with the Court Beneath.* Both true. Neither the reason.

The other members drifted past the notice all morning, reading it, murmuring. Old Corvin the luck-broker crossed himself, which is impressive given his profession. The apprentices whispered. And Wren's empty chair in the corner of the common room stayed empty, and nobody mentioned it, and everybody thought it, and the silence around that chair was louder than the backward bell.

One person did more than murmur. Little Sefi, the youngest apprentice, planted herself in front of my desk at closing and demanded, with the fearlessness of the very small, "Is it true Mr. Quentin sold secrets?"

"Mr. Quentin broke rules," I said. "Rules and secrets aren't always the same thing."

"Then why'd you expel him?"

"Because institutions survive by being cruel in predictable ways. If I forgive him publicly, every member learns that conviction is negotiable, and within a year we're a charity, and within five we're dead, and then who protects the people who need protecting?" I said it smoothly. I've had thirty years of practice saying smooth things that taste like ash.

Sefi considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Wren says he's family."

"Wren," I said, "is currently the subject of a transaction I am legally forbidden to discuss."

"Wren says *you're* family too. She says everybody's family, you just keep yours in different rooms."

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I gave her a copper for the honesty and sent her to bed, and then I sat alone with my ledgers for a long time, listening to the bottled laughter hum on its shelf like it knew something I didn't.

* * *

Midnight. Scullery door. I watched from the upstairs window as a figure in a plain coat paused at the threshold, checked the street both ways with a taster's habitual caution, and retrieved the packet I'd left in the flour bin. He held it a moment—weighing it, probably calculating what accepting it would cost us both—and then he tucked it inside his coat, against his heart, which is where fools carry maps, and walked into the dark toward the mountain.

I went back downstairs and opened the ledger to the current page. Guild law requires that an expelled member's final entry be closed and balanced. So I balanced it: assets seized, none; debts outstanding, one, payable to the Court Beneath, collateral, one soul, appraised value—

My pen stopped.

Appraised value. Thirty years of pricing impossible things, and I could not put a number on Quentin Eriksen. Not because he was worthless. Because the ledger's whole grammar assumes everything can be exchanged, and somewhere between the backward bell and the flour bin, I had stopped believing that anything about this particular entry was exchangeable at all.

So I did what any honest bookkeeper does with an entry she cannot complete.

I left it open.

Then I poured myself the good brandy—the one I save for funerals and audits, which are frequently the same event—and I drank to the twelfth expulsion, and I told the empty room, in a voice I would deny under torture: "Come back, you stubborn, invisible fool. Come back, and I'll burn the treaty myself, and damn the consequences, and you can yell at me about proper archival procedure while I do it."

The bottled laughter giggled.

"Yes, yes," I told it. "Laugh while you can. If the stars stay gone, you'll be flat as the storms by winter."

Outside, the sky hung starless and black as a closed ledger, and somewhere beneath the mountain, a man who had spent his whole life learning not to be seen was walking straight into the one place that sees everything.

I blew out my candle, sat in the dark I was becoming accustomed to, and began, very quietly, to plan treason.
