## 9. Down the Stair That Isn't There

The palace guard has looked at me every day for eleven years, and not once has he seen me.

This is the trick of it, and I want to be honest with you, because honesty is about all I have left: I did not build my invisibility. I only ever thought I did. I thought it was craft—muted colors, a soft tread, a face you forget before you finish looking at it. A taster's survival kit. Stand near the king's elbow, be furniture, live long enough to die in bed. I wore unremarkableness like armor and told myself I'd forged it plate by plate.

Brammel took that from me in one conversation, the old liar, guttering in his cave like a lamp left out in the rain. *You are not careful, boy. You are a hole in the world's attention.* The gift of being unremembered. Rare, he said. Corruptible, he said. The under-court craves it, he said—and then named a price for relighting the wards that I couldn't pay, which was convenient of him, because I'd already decided what I could pay instead.

So now, walking past Sergeant Hallow at the east post—Hallow, who once shared a meat pie with me during a snowstorm and complained about his wife's singing—I test it deliberately. I stop. Directly in front of him. Close enough to count the grey hairs in his mustache.

His eyes slide off me like rain off wax.

"Evening, Sergeant," I say.

He frowns at the empty corridor. Scratches his jaw. Goes back to counting torch-brackets or whatever sergeants count when the night is long.

Eleven years of being furniture, and it turns out I was never trying at all. There is something obscene about how easy this is. It should cost something. Everything costs something; I've priced enough impossible things to know. But the gift spends itself like a drunkard's coin, freely and without memory of the spending, and I am beginning to suspect that by the time I reach Wren, there will be less of me left to bring home.

I keep walking anyway. Guilt is a fine traveling companion. It never asks to rest.

* * *

The map came to me an hour ago, folded inside a hollowed loaf of bread, and I have been tasting fear ever since like a flavor I can't spit out.

Marta brought it. Kitchen maid, second shift, the one who slips Wren honey-cakes when she thinks no one notices—which is funny, because nobody notices anything I do either, and between the two of us we've built a small invisible nation in the palace undercrofts. She found me in the guild's back room, where I was doing a very poor job of packing, and she pressed the loaf into my hands with flour still on her knuckles.

"He drew her down," Marta said. "The courteous one. She wrote it all after, every night, in case—" Her voice did something complicated and stopped. "In case anyone ever needed to come."

Wren. My Wren, who sings to candles until they lean toward her like sunflowers, who calls me family with the casual cruelty of children who don't know they're aiming knives. She'd known she might need rescuing, and she'd written instructions, because even half-stolen she was still the most practical person I know.

The map is drawn in her round, determined hand, ink smudged where her pen hesitated:

*Start at the cold hearth in the old kitchens—the one that's bricked up. Knock twice, wait, knock once. Don't knock three times. He counts.*

Then a stair drawn as a dotted line going down through what should be solid mountain, past little annotations in her voice: *cold here, bring socks. A door that breathes—don't touch it, just say thank you and walk through. The chandelier room—don't look up too long, Q, I mean it. Then the long gallery, and him.*

She'd signed it with a small wren, wings spread, and beneath it: *If you're reading this, you absolute stubborn ledger-keeper, I'm sorry. Also you owe me a song.*

I had to sit down on a sack of turnips for a while after reading that last line. A man can only be so loved before it becomes unbearable.

* * *

The cold hearth is exactly where she said, in the disused kitchen behind the wine cellars, its arch bricked over sometime before my grandfather's grandfather learned to taste for kings. The bricks are black with old smoke. Someone has left a dead pigeon on the hearthstone, which feels like an omen, though of what I couldn't say.

I knock twice. Wait. Knock once.

Silence. Long enough that I start composing my own eulogy—*he died as he lived, unnoticed*—and then the bricks make a sound like a held breath released. They don't move. They simply become the sort of thing you can walk through, the way a rumor becomes true if enough people stop questioning it.

Cold pours out. Not winter-cold. Older than winter. The cold of places that were dark before dark had a name.

I stand at the threshold longer than I'd like to admit. Here is what I know, and knowing it changes nothing: Ovelia forbade this. The guild forbade this. Every sensible instinct I've spent a lifetime sharpening forbids this. And here is what I also know: somewhere below me, a girl with a voice that makes candles lean is being kept in a glass case, her soul measured out in installments, and the ledger of my life will not balance unless I go down and correct the entry.

Ovelia would say I'm being reckless. Ovelia, who taught me that nothing is free, who prices grief by the ounce. And she'd be right. That's the joke of it. I finally understand that I have a rare and corruptible magic, and my first act upon learning it is to spend it all in one place, on one person, without haggling.

A good guildsman would negotiate. I am apparently not a good guildsman anymore. I stole the alchemist's vial, I lied to my mistress, and tomorrow—or never—there will be an accounting. Tonight there is only the stair.

"Thank you," I tell the breathing doorway, because Wren said to, and because manners cost nothing, and because I have a sudden superstitious horror of being rude to things older than language.

It sighs around me as I pass. Warm, briefly. Like approval. Like pity.

* * *

The stairs go down farther than the mountain is deep.

I don't mean that poetically. I counted. Two hundred steps should put me beneath the foundations, four hundred beneath the bedrock, and at six hundred I stopped counting because the numbers started feeling like a dare. The stone changes as I descend—palace granite giving way to something veined and pale, then to a smooth black material that takes the light from my lantern and gives back only a portion of it, keeping the rest as tax.

Wren's annotations appear at intervals, chalked directly onto the walls in her handwriting, fresher than the map. She must have written these on her way down, or—worse—on her way *up*, escorted, leaving messages like breadcrumbs for a rescue she wasn't sure would come.

*COLD HERE. BRING SOCKS.* (I did bring socks. I am wearing them. This is somehow comforting.)

*A door that breathes—don't touch it, just say thank you.*

The door arrives at step seven hundred and change. It stands alone in the passage, freestanding, oak and iron, and it moves. In. Out. Slowly, patiently, the way a sleeping thing breathes, and the wood is warm when I hold my palm a respectful inch above it.

Every instinct says run. My instincts have been wrong about everything lately, so I ignore them.

"Thank you," I say.

The door exhales—a long, shuddering sigh that stirs my hair—and swings open onto darkness. As I pass through, I feel it consider me. Weigh me. Find me, perhaps, lighter than expected. There is less of me to weigh every hour, and the door knows it, and I try not to think about what that means.

On the other side, chalked small and low, almost hidden: *Q — if you get this far, turn back. I mean it. Turn back. — W*

And beneath it, in different ink, different hand, elegant and patient: *But he won't. They never do.*

I look at that second line for a long time. The courteous stranger. The one who paid a child in starlight for songs. He knew I'd come. Perhaps he counted on it. Perhaps my whole descent is a delivery, a package arriving as scheduled: one invisible man, gently used, addressed to the Court Beneath.

I keep walking. Recklessness, I'm discovering, feels a great deal like resolve if you don't examine it too closely. So I don't examine it. I examine the walls instead, and my lantern, and my socks, and anything except the growing suspicion that I am not the hunter on this stair but the goods.

* * *

The chandelier room stops my heart for a full three seconds. I counted those, too. Some numbers you keep.

There is no floor I can see—only a bridge of black stone across a vast dark, and hanging above it, hundreds of them, chandeliers. Not crystal. Cages. Each one a lattice of that pale veined stone, and inside each lattice, light. Moving light. Light that presses against its bars like a moth, like a face, like fingers.

Starlight. Harvested. Hundreds of them, thousands maybe, receding into the dark overhead, each cage holding a stolen piece of sky, and the whole chamber humming with a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it—a vast, slow, starving chord.

*Don't look up too long, Q, I mean it.*

I look anyway. I'm bad at obedience; it's why Ovelia is expelling me, presumably, though I haven't stayed to read the letter. I look, and among the caged lights I find them—the ones I know. The Shepherd. The Sisters Three. The old names the court forgot buying peace, the stars whose absence no one above remembers mourning because the memory itself was sold. They hang here, dimmed and desperate, fuel for a court that was exiled from the sky and has been eating its inheritance ever since.

And threaded through the hum, faint, from far ahead—from a vault, from a glass case—

Wren is singing.

Not the whole song. Fragments. The way a dreamer talks. A phrase of the lullaby she used to sing in the guild kitchen while Ovelia pretended not to listen, then silence, then another phrase, thinner each time. Being sung by someone whose soul is being poured out in installments, and each installment buys the under-court another hour of borrowed splendor.

My hands are shaking. I note this the way I note everything, in the flat voice of the ledger-keeper: *Subject exhibits tremor. Subject's resolve, however, hardening. Recommend immediate intervention, consequences to be assessed never.*

I cross the bridge. I do not look up again, mostly. Once, near the far side, something in the nearest cage turned toward me—all the lights did, a slow ripple, hundreds of faces of light pressing to their bars—and I felt myself being *noticed*, truly noticed, for the first time in my life, by things that have been starved of noticing.

They saw me. Me. The hole in the world's attention.

And their hunger turned toward me like a compass needle, and I understood, with the clarity of a man stepping off a ledge, exactly what Brammel meant by *corruptible*. My gift isn't invisibility. It's an appetite of its own—it eats attention, it eats memory, it eats *me*, and the under-court wants it the way the starving want bread.

I walked faster.

* * *

The long gallery at the end of the bridge is lined with mirrors, and none of them work properly.

They show rooms. Other rooms. A feast hall where nobles wear faces like borrowed coats, laughing at jokes made centuries ago. A garden where the roses are made of frost and someone is always just leaving. A throne room I recognize with a lurch—the throne room above, our throne room, King Aldous picking at his supper while I stand forgotten at his elbow—but wrong, dimmer, seen through water.

And in the nearest mirror, a figure walks the gallery: a thin man in muted colors, lantern in hand, socks visible below unlaced boots because Wren said to bring them and I obey exactly one person.

That's me. That's the first mirror all night that shows me plainly, and I stop and stare at myself like a man meeting a stranger, because in here, beneath the mountain, where attention is currency and hunger is law, my gift doesn't work. The dark sees me. The dark has always been able to see me. I was only ever invisible to the light.

"You're late," says a voice behind me, courteous as a closed door. "Though I suppose punctuality is difficult, when no one expects you anywhere."

I turn. At the end of the gallery, where Wren's map ends in a small drawing of a wren with its wings spread, stands the Under-King's herald—the courteous stranger, the one who bought songs from a child with coins of starlight. He is beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and he is smiling, and behind him the doors of the Court Beneath stand open, spilling music and light that hurts to look at.

"We received your application some time ago," he says. "Your gift. Your *self*. We do hope you've come to discuss terms."

My hand finds the vial in my pocket—the alchemist's vial, the king's dulled tonic, my one piece of evidence, my one weapon. My other hand finds the memory of a girl calling me family like it was nothing, like it was obvious, like I was worth claiming.

"I've come about a debt," I say, and my voice doesn't shake, which surprises us both. "One soul. Sold. I'm here to buy her back."

The herald's smile widens by a single, terrible degree.

"Ah," he says. "Then you'd better come in. The King does love a negotiation."

And the doors close behind me like a ledger balancing shut.
