## 10. The Court Beneath

The stair went down for a very long time.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last dignity available to a man descending into a hole that officially does not exist. The stair was cut from stone the color of dried blood, and it spiraled, and it had no railing, and at some point—I couldn't tell you where—the torchlight I'd brought stopped mattering, because the walls themselves began to glow. Not firelight. Starlight. Old starlight, dim and bruise-colored, seeping out of the rock like marrow through bone.

I counted my steps for something to do with my fear. At four hundred I stopped counting, because four hundred felt like a number that would only embarrass us both.

The map in Wren's hand ended at the bottom of the stair with a single line: *Don't let them hear you hesitate.* She'd drawn a small bird next to it. She drew birds on everything. It is difficult to march toward your own destruction when the marching orders are decorated like a child's primer, and yet here I was.

Here, in fact, being the operative word, because the stair ended, and the Court Beneath began.

* * *

I had expected darkness. I had expected, if I'm honest, something crude—caves, bones, the theatrical squalor of things that live under other things. What I got instead was a ballroom.

It was vast beyond sense, carved not built, every pillar a single rib of black stone polished until it held reflections that were slightly too honest. The floor was obsidian, or something wearing obsidian's face, and my boots made no sound on it at all. Chandeliers hung overhead—not candles, never candles—and each one held dozens of small glass cages, and inside each cage a fragment of light turned slowly, restlessly, like a moth that had been told about windows all its life and never shown one.

Stars. They were stars. Pieces of stars, caged and hung for decoration.

My stomach did something complicated. Part of it wanted to weep; part of it, the guild-trained part, the part that prices bottled laughter and winters on credit, immediately began calculating what a chandelier like that would fetch above ground, and then hated itself with real enthusiasm.

And the court itself—

They were dancing. Of course they were dancing. There was music coming from somewhere, thin and sweet, though I could see no musicians, and the dancers moved through figures so old and elaborate that watching them was like watching arithmetic performed by people who found it beautiful.

They wore faces.

That's the only way to put it. Not masks—masks sit *on* a face. These faces sat *in*, the way a key sits in a lock, and you could see the seams if you looked, faint lines at the jaw and temple like the joins of fine porcelain. Some of the faces were beautiful. Some were merely kind. One old woman's face was so perfectly ordinary, so exactly like the baker's widow two streets from the Gilded Spoon, that my skin crawled worse than any horror could have managed.

Borrowed faces. Bought faces. Faces that belonged to someone upstairs who was, presumably, going about their morning wondering why the mirror felt slightly like a stranger.

"Quentin Eriksen."

The voice came from everywhere, which is to say it came from the throne, and the throne came from nowhere. It stood at the far end of the hall on a dais of unpolished stone—the only rough thing in the room, and I understood immediately that this was deliberate. Everything else gleamed. The throne refused to. It was the one object in the Court Beneath that had never bothered to lie about what it was.

"You know my name," I said. My voice came out steadier than I'd earned. Years of tasting for a paranoid king will teach you control of your face; apparently it extends to control of your terror, within tolerances.

"We know everything that walks down our stair." The Under-King rose.

He was tall the way a spire is tall, and thin the way hunger is thin, and he wore no borrowed face at all. His face was his own, which somehow made it worse—a long grey countenance with eyes like the space between stars, patient and enormous and absolutely empty. When he smiled, it was with genuine warmth, and I have never been more frightened by warmth in my life. I price impossible things for a living. Warmth from a thing like him was the most expensive item in the room.

"Come," he said. "Dance first. Or drink. We have wine pressed from the last vintage before the sky closed." He gestured, and a table appeared—or rather, I noticed a table that had clearly been there all along, set with two cups, because that is how his magic worked: not creation, but attention. "You've come a long way to bargain. Bargains go better unhurried."

"I don't drink on the job," I said. "Occupational habit."

"Ah. Yes. The taster." He said it like an endearment. "The man who poisons himself daily so that others needn't be poisoned. Do you know, we find that the most obscene profession in your city? Above ground they call it loyalty." His empty eyes glittered. "Down here we call it practice."

"Practice for what?"

"For tonight," he said pleasantly. "Sit."

I sat. Refusing would have been hesitation, and Wren's map had been clear about hesitation. The cup before me was full of something dark and still, and I looked into it and saw my own reflection looking back with an expression I didn't recognize—which was strange, because I have studied my own face in mirrors for years with the diligence of a man checking for poison symptoms, and I know every expression it owns. This one wasn't mine. This one was the face of a man who belonged here.

I pushed the cup away an inch. The Under-King laughed, and the sound was genuinely delighted, and the chandeliers trembled, and somewhere far off in the dark behind the throne, something answered his laugh with a sound I will not describe except to say it was many voices and none of them willing.

"Now," he said. "You want the girl."

"Wren. Her name is Wren."

"The songbird." He said her name the way a miser says a coin's weight. "Yes. She sings beautifully. She sings *nutritiously*. Do you know what her voice does to our chandeliers? The light turns toward her. Even caged, even starving, it turns." He leaned forward, and his face filled with something almost tender, and I wanted to break every tooth in it. "She has been singing to the dark since she was small enough to fit in a soup pot. Did she never tell you why? Children sing to the dark because the dark listens. Everyone else has stopped listening by then. We never stop."

"She sold her soul to you," I said. "There's a receipt. In my ledger. In my handwriting, which is a trick I'd admire if it hadn't been used to rob me."

"A receipt makes it legitimate," the Under-King said serenely. "Legitimacy is the whole art. Your guild understands this—we learned it from them, generations ago. A thing taken is theft. A thing traded is commerce. A thing receipted is *law*." He spread his long grey hands. "So. You are here to make commerce. Speak your offer."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about bargaining with the dark: it isn't hard. That's the trap.

Above ground, every negotiation I've ever run for Ovelia involved two parties pretending they needed the thing less than they did. But the Under-King didn't pretend anything. He wanted Quentin Eriksen's invisibility—that was written all over the court's attention, in the way a hundred borrowed faces had gone still and turned toward me the moment I entered, the way conversation had died into a silence with the texture of appetite—and he knew I knew he wanted it, and he let me know he knew, and there was a horrible comfort in that honesty. Like trading with Ovelia. Like family.

"There's a catch," I said. "There's always a catch. So let's have it up front, because I keep ledgers, and I hate discovering entries late."

"No catch," said the Under-King. "Only terms. Your gift—your *unremembering*, as our last dragon so clumsily named it—for the songbird's soul, whole and returned. Payment due in seven nights. During those seven nights you keep the gift; you may spend it as you like; some of our clients enjoy a farewell tour." He smiled again, warmly, terribly. "At the end of seven nights, you return here, and you give it up, and you walk out of here with the girl, and everyone lives. Where is the catch in that?"

"The catch," I said, "is that nothing down here is free, and the price is never the price on the table."

"Then you've been paying attention." He sounded proud. Actually proud, the way Ovelia sounds when I catch an error in my own ledger before she does. "Very well. The true price. Your gift is rare, taster. Do you understand how rare? Souls are common. We buy souls by the sack, upstairs, from people who barely notice the weight leaving them. But a *native* unremembering—one grown in a living body over thirty years, fed daily, disciplined, hoarded—" his voice actually shook, and the chandeliers rattled, and every caged star strained toward me like plants toward sun— "that is a vintage. That is the vintage of vintages. With it, we could walk up your stairs. Into your palace. Your guild hall. We could stand in any room in your city and no ward, no sentry, no ledger-line would mark our passing."

"And that's why the wards fell," I said. "You didn't break them to get in. You broke them so there'd be nothing left between you and a shopping trip."

"The border wards fell," the Under-King corrected gently, "because the king's grandfather sold them. Piece by piece, across forty years, to pay for a quiet kingdom. Your crown has been selling you to us since before your grandparents met. We are simply the party holding the receipts." He folded his hands. "But yes. Your gift opens doors that even bought wards cannot. And so, taster, the true price is not merely the gift. It is the gift *given freely, gladly, with witness*. A coerced surrender sours in the vault. We have had... spoilage." Something behind the throne moaned agreement, many-voiced and miserable. "You must want to give it."

"I want to save Wren."

"Yes," he said softly. "That will do beautifully. Wanting one thing badly enough is wanting to give everything else. We find it always works out the same."

* * *

While we spoke, the music changed.

I noticed it the way you notice a smell—you're halfway into it before you realize it arrived. The thin sweet dance-tune had become something else. A melody I knew. Simple, looping, a street song, a kitchen song, the song Wren hummed while she swept the guild's front room, off-key in the same three places every time because she refused to learn the tune properly on principle.

Her song. Coming from behind the throne. From the vault.

Every hair on my body stood up and pointed at the sound.

"She's here," I said. "She's awake."

"She is *present*," the Under-King said, with a pedant's care. "Present, and fading. A soul removed from its body keeps its habits for a while. Singing is a deep habit. Then the habits thin, and the soul goes quiet, and quiet souls keep beautifully." He tilted his head. "Would you like to hear her?"

"That's not a question anyone answers no."

He raised one grey hand, and the great doors behind the throne—doors I hadn't seen until he wanted them seen—swung open onto a corridor lined with more chandeliers, and at its end, a vault door of black iron banded in silver, and through the iron, muffled but unmistakable, Wren's voice, singing to the dark, because the dark listened, because the dark had always listened, and no one had ever told her what listening costs.

I was on my feet. I don't remember standing. The nearest dancer—a noblewoman wearing a young man's face, which told me everything about how carefully these creatures chose—reached out to steady me, and her fingers passed through my arm like smoke, and she withdrew them with an expression of profound disappointment.

"You see," the Under-King murmured. "Already half ours. Things cannot touch you here unless we permit it. You are a guest, taster. Guests are precious. Guests are *kept*."

"Seven nights," I said. My voice came out low and ugly and I didn't fix it. "I bring you my gift, freely and gladly, with witness. You release Wren's soul, whole, back into her body, upstairs, alive. Receipted. In writing."

"In your handwriting," he agreed, eyes glittering with private humor, "which you'll find so convenient."

"And if you renege—"

"Taster." He rose, and his shadow rose with him, and his shadow was taller than the hall. "Look around you. Every light in this room is a promise someone above once made and we collected. We do not renege. We are the *other* party. Reneging is an above-ground art." He extended his hand—long, grey, cool as a cellar, and when I took it, his grip was careful, the way you hold something you intend to keep for a very long time. "Seven nights. Spend them well. We recommend ordinary things. Our clients always say the ordinary things are what they miss."

The handshake sealed it. I felt it seal—felt something settle over me like a coat tailored to a man slightly larger than myself, a contract wearing my skin. Somewhere in my chest, an entry wrote itself in a ledger I don't keep and can't close.

"One more thing," I said, pulling my hand back.

"You are in no position for more things."

"Humor me. I'm told I'm memorable company."

His smile widened. "Ask."

"When you harvest a gift like mine—unremembering—what happens to everyone who's already remembered me?" I kept my voice flat, professional, a taster describing flavor notes. "Wren calls me family. Ovelia—" I stopped. "People upstairs know my name. Does giving it to you take it out of them?"

The Under-King considered me for a long moment, and in that moment I saw, briefly, honestly, the thing underneath the courtesy: a starved creature looking at a meal, doing sums.

"No one knows, taster," he said gently. "That is rather the point of you. Think carefully. Who, above ground, could speak your name right now with any certainty of being answered? Who looks at you and sees anything but a useful piece of furniture?" He spread his hands, and the kindness in his voice was the cruelest thing in a room full of stolen stars. "You have spent thirty years teaching the world to forget you. All we ask is that you finish the lesson."

The music behind the iron door faltered—one bar of silence, as if the singer had heard her name spoken somewhere very far away—and then picked up again, stubborn, off-key in the same three places.

I walked back up the stair that isn't there with a contract on my shoulders and seven nights in my pocket, and I did not let myself hesitate even once, because Wren's map had warned me, and because if I hesitated—if I let myself feel even one inch of what I'd just signed away—I would have sat down on that bloody-colored stone and stayed there until the dark got hungry enough to come collect me early.

Four hundred steps. I counted every one.

At the top, night air. Guttering stars—no. No stars at all. Just the black sky over the mountain like a page torn out of a ledger.

Seven nights. I had spent years making myself unnoticeable, and now the bill had finally come, and the terrible joke of it—the joke I would laugh at later, when I could afford laughter—was that the Under-King hadn't lied about a single term.

He hadn't needed to.
