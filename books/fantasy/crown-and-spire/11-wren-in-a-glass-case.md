## 11. Wren in a Glass Case

Here is what I know about being a soul in a jar: it is very quiet, and the quiet is not empty. It is full of other people's wanting.

I used to think hunger was a simple thing. You're hungry, you eat, you steal an apple from Quentin's satchel because he pretends not to notice and always packs two anyway. That was my whole philosophy of appetite, aged eleven to fifteen, and I stand by it. But the Court Beneath is hungry the way a hole in the ground is hungry — no mouth, no belly, just an endless falling-inward, and everything near the edge goes over eventually.

Including me.

* * *

Let me tell you how it happened, or at least how much of it I can still hold.

There was a feast. The king had that look he gets when he suspects his own soup, and Quentin was doing his tasting face — which is not really a face at all, just a stillness, like a heron deciding whether the pond is worth the trouble. I remember thinking, *he looks tired*, and meaning to say something about it later. Maybe bring him noodles from the stall on Tanner's Row. He pretends street food is beneath him and then eats twice what I do.

Then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*, all at once, like a hand closing over the sky, and the border wards made a sound I felt in my back teeth — a harp string snapping somewhere enormous — and everyone in the great hall started shouting at once, and I—

I sang.

That's the shameful part. The court was screaming and the guards were running and I stood there in my apprentice's coat and *sang*, because when the world breaks, my voice does the only thing it knows how to do. It tries to mend. Candles lean toward me. They always have. And something in the dark leaned too.

The courteous stranger was there. Of course he was there. He'd been there for weeks, paying me in starlight for small songs — a coin of cold white light for a ballad, two for a lament — and I'd spent them on sweets and ribbon and one truly ridiculous hat, and I'd told myself it was harmless, because Quentin forbade it and everything Quentin forbids is usually just him being frightened in a coat.

He held out his hand and said, "One more song, little wick. A big one. Enough to light your way home."

And I thought: enough for a whole year of hats.

I don't remember signing. That's the trick of it, I think. You remember the music, and then you remember the glass.

* * *

The vault is a long room, and the ceiling of it is wrong. There are chandeliers down here — caged starlight, hundreds of little prisons of gold and glass, each one holding a knot of light that used to be free — but above them, where a ceiling should be, there is only dark that goes up and up and never arrives anywhere. I have stared at it for what feels like years. It might be years. Time down here is measured in songs, and nobody asks me to sing anymore.

My case sits on a shelf with the others. Yes, others. I am not special, which is somehow worse than being stolen. There are jars of laughter gone flat. There is a winter in a bottle — I can see its breath fogging the glass. And there are souls. Dozens. Hundreds, maybe, if the shelves go back far enough into the dark, which they do. Each one is a soft glow, and each one flickers in a different rhythm, and if you listen — and I have nothing to do but listen — you can almost hear names inside them.

Mine flickers fastest. Ovelia would say that means poor quality control. I choose to believe it means stubbornness.

Because here is the thing about having half a soul: the half that's left is *louder*. Whatever they took, they took the smooth parts, the agreeable parts, the parts that would have let me drift. What remains is all edges. All Wren. And Wren has decided — the way you decide anything when you're fifteen and furious — that she is not going to be a lamp.

* * *

The Under-King visits sometimes.

He doesn't look the way stories say. Stories give their monsters teeth and shadow, and this man — if he is a man — has neither. He is beautiful the way an empty house is beautiful, all clean lines and nothing living behind the windows. When he walks past the shelves, the souls lean toward him. Mine leans too. I hate that. I hate that some traitor piece of me still wants his attention, the way a plant wants the sun even when the sun is the thing burning it.

"Little wick," he said last time — or last song; I've stopped counting — "you shine brighter than the rest."

"It's called being angry," I said. "You wouldn't know. You've never been anything."

Most prisoners scream. I insult royalty. It's a gift.

But he didn't punish me. That's the part I didn't expect, the part that keeps me up — well, keeps me *alert*; sleep is another thing they take. He sat down beside my case, folded his hands like a grandfather at a bedside, and told me a story. And the horrible thing, the thing I keep turning over like a stone looking for the rot underneath, is that it wasn't a lie. I know lies. I grew up on the streets until Quentin found me picking his pocket outside the guildhall. Lies have a smell. This had none.

"We were keepers once," he said. "Before there was a mountain over our heads. Before there was an *above*. We tended the stars — do you understand? Not worshipped them. Tended. Pruned them. Turned them in their courses like lamps in a great hall, trimmed their wicks, kept their fires fed. Every light in your sky was a duty someone loved."

"And then?"

"And then the ones above us decided heaven needed tidying." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it. Grief that doesn't rise is grief that has given up expecting rescue. "They built their crown and their spire on the backs of our duties. They wrote us out of the ledgers — oh, yes, little wick, we were *written out*, struck through in a fine careful hand — and the doors closed, and the stars went on burning without anyone to feed them, and we went hungry. Have you ever been hungry for four thousand years? Not for food. For light. For purpose. For one single thing that remembers your name."

I wanted to say something cruel. I had a whole speech ready, something about how stealing children's souls doesn't un-exile anybody. But I looked at him sitting there in his beautiful borrowed suit, hands folded, patient as a stone, and what came out instead was:

"That's not fair."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

And then he stood and walked away, and I sat in my glass case feeling like I'd just comforted the thing that ate me, and I hated myself for exactly eleven songs, and then I stopped hating myself, because self-hatred is a luxury for people with whole souls, and I need every scrap I've got left.

Here is what I settled on, after the eleven songs: both things are true. He is starving, and he is a thief. Being hurt does not earn you the right to hurt. If it did, every orphan in the city would be entitled to burn the palace down, and honestly? Some days I might sign that ledger. But not this one. Because the difference between me and him — the difference I intend to keep, with my last flickering scrap of light — is that when I'm hungry, I want company at the table. He wants the table itself, and the chairs, and the house around it, and everyone who ever ate there forgotten.

* * *

Quentin comes to me in pieces.

That's how memory works now. Half a soul holds half a life, and the halves come loose. So he arrives in fragments:

— Quentin's hands, ink-stained, turning a ledger page with the exaggerated care of a man pretending the book is more interesting than the conversation.

— Quentin saying, "Don't call me family," the first week I knew him, in exactly the tone of a man daring the universe to make it true. And me, thirteen years old, missing a front tooth and all my fear, saying "Too late, already did," and watching him lose that argument to himself in real time.

— The taste of the noodles on Tanner's Row, which I am suddenly terrified of forgetting, so I recite it like a prayer: pepper-broth, charred pork, the cheap vinegar they cut it with, the way he always said *this is a mistake* and then finished mine when I got full.

— Him teaching me to taste poison without swallowing it. Hold it on the tongue. Let it tell you what it is. Never let it in. I thought he was teaching me about food. He was teaching me about people, about himself, about the whole careful architecture of a heart with the drawbridge permanently raised.

I hold onto those. When the dark gets close — and it does get close; there are songs when my edges go soft and I nearly dissolve into the general glow of the shelf — I grab the fragments and I say them out loud to the glass. The maid-of-all-work who smuggled me a crust once. The hat. The tooth. *Pepper-broth, charred pork, cheap vinegar.* A name is mostly a list of small things, I think. Lose the list, lose the person.

So I keep the list.

* * *

Tonight — tonight-song, whatever — the vault is restless. The nobles are whispering in the galleries, their borrowed faces sliding slightly out of true when they think no one is looking, mouths a half-inch from where the faces end. Something has happened above. Something has *changed*. The chandeliers burn a little lower than yesterday, and the Under-King hasn't come to gloat, and gloating is practically his hobby, so whatever it is, it isn't good for him.

Good, I think. Then: please let it be Quentin. Then, immediately: *don't be stupid, if it's Quentin that means he came down here, into this place, for you, which is the worst possible outcome, because Quentin will trade anything, Quentin has been practicing trading things away his whole entire life, he'll give them the one thing he's got—*

His invisibility. His not-being-noticed. I figured it out before he did, probably. You don't become that easy to overlook by accident. Nobody's forgettable on purpose unless forgetfulness is a door they're holding shut from the inside.

Oh, no, I think, in my little glass box, with my little leftover spark. Oh, no, you absolute ledger-keeping idiot. Don't you dare.

And the dark at the top of the vault shifts, the way it does when the stair opens — the stair that isn't there, the stair I drew a map of once for a stranger I thought was kind — and footsteps start coming down, unhurried, familiar, the particular careful tread of a man who has spent thirty years making sure no one hears him arrive.

I press my palms against the glass.

"Quentin," I whisper — or I try to; half a voice doesn't carry — "Quentin, turn around, go home, don't you *dare*—"

The glow in my case flares bright, bright, brightest it's been since the sky went out.

Stubbornness, I told you.
