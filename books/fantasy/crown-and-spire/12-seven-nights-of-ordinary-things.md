## 12. Seven Nights of Ordinary Things

Here is what nobody tells you about having seven days left to be a person: the days do not cooperate. They refuse to be solemn. They arrive smelling like bread, or rain, or the tallow-drip of the palace kitchens, and they ask nothing of you but that you live in them, which is suddenly the hardest thing I have ever been asked to do.

I have tasted poison for a paranoid king for eleven years. I once priced a bottled thunderstorm. I sat across a table from a crowned thing made of hunger and did not flinch until it was polite to do so. And I cannot get through a bowl of noodles without my hands shaking.

Let me start at the beginning, which is to say, the morning after.

* * *

I woke in my own narrow bed above my own narrow rooms with Wren's receipt folded in my breast pocket like a splinter I'd decided to keep. *One soul, sold, to the Court Beneath.* My own handwriting. The Under-King had been considerate enough to forge it in my hand, which told me something about how thoroughly he'd studied me, and how thoroughly he intended me to understand the terms: this was my debt, signed by me, before I'd ever signed anything.

Six nights remained after that one.

I got up. I shaved. I looked at myself in the little mirror with the cracked corner and thought, with the detachment I've spent a lifetime cultivating, *so that's what he wants. Not your magic. You.*

Then I went downstairs and made tea, because ritual is what the body does when the mind has resigned, and because the tea was already there.

The first ordinary thing I did was eat breakfast sitting at the table instead of standing at the window like a man casing his own life. It felt absurd. It felt necessary. I chewed each bite the way I taste for the king—attentive, suspicious, cataloguing—and discovered that I have spent eleven years eating like an executioner and calling it caution. Bread is not a threat. Butter is not a threat. I put down the knife and ate with my hands, which is barbaric, and which made me laugh, alone, at seven in the morning, in a way that alarmed the neighbor's cat.

The second ordinary thing was harder. I went to see Wren's room.

She lodges with the kitchen maid Marta now—I arranged that years ago without ever explaining why, the way I arrange everything, silently, deniably, so that no one would owe me anything and I would owe no one. Her pallet is unmade. There are song fragments on scraps of paper everywhere, most of them about food, two of them about me, which I read four times and then folded into my pocket next to the receipt, because apparently I collect wounds now.

One scrap said: *Q. says family is a word for people you can't fire. I am keeping him anyway.*

I sat on her floor among the paper birds she folds when she's thinking, and I understood, finally, what the Under-King had actually purchased from me. Not invisibility. Not the gift. He'd bought the man who heard *family is a word* and never once said the other half out loud. Six nights to say it. To her, if she came back. That was the whole commission. Everything else was haggling.

* * *

On the third night I went to the Gilded Spoon.

Not inside. Inside was closed to me; Ovelia's expulsion still rang in my ears with the terrible tenderness she'd tried to hammer flat. But the guild-house has a loading door on Cordwainer Lane where the impossible inventory comes and goes in plain crates marked FLOUR and BUTTONS, and I have balanced those ledgers long enough to know its schedule.

I waited until the porter stepped inside, and then I did something I'd been doing anonymously for years, only now with an expiration date: I mended their books.

There's a copying alcove off the loading bay, left unlocked because who would dare, and I sat down at Ovelia's spare desk with her spare ink and began repairing the week's entries in a hand she would not recognize because it is a hand I have practiced specifically so she would not recognize it. A bottled giggle whose stopper had failed—reclassified from *laughter, vintage* to *laughter, spilled,* with a note on salvage value. Three feet of yesterday, returned by a dissatisfied customer—restocked, discounted, flagged *do not sell to the Ferrymen's Guild, they will know.* A winter sold on credit to a widow in the Lowmarsh, payments overdue not from malice but from grief, and I quietly moved the due date and initialed nothing, because debts should breathe.

This is what I know how to do. I cannot relight stars. I cannot forge keys out of dying dragons. But I can make a ledger tell the truth gently, and somewhere in that cold guild-house there is a woman who reads every page, and I wanted—God, I wanted—one of the last things I did on earth to be an kindness she'd never trace.

Around midnight she came through the loading bay. I heard her voice giving orders, clipped and steady, and then I heard it falter at the alcove doorway, and I flattened myself against the wall in the dark and used the gift without deciding to, the way you duck a thrown cup—instinct, old habit, the talent for being unremarked that I now knew was never a talent at all but a wound dressed up as one.

She stood in the alcove a long time. She touched the page I'd just finished. She said, very quietly, to no one, "You absolute fool," in the voice other people use for *come home,* and I stood in the dark with my hand over my mouth and let her believe the books had repaired themselves.

That was the third night. I count them because they're all I have, and because counting is a form of prayer when you don't believe in anything gentler.

* * *

Fourth night, noodles.

There is a stall on Tanner's Bridge run by a woman named Suki who has sold noodles to the city for forty years and has never once learned my name, despite my eating there perhaps three hundred times. This used to feel like a triumph. Now I stood in line behind a drunk tanner and a courting couple and listened to Suki call everyone *love* and realized I had engineered my own anonymity with the same care another man might engineer a legacy.

"One copper," she said, holding out the bowl, steam climbing into the cold. "You look terrible, love. Extra chili."

"I didn't order extra chili."

"You look like a man who needs to feel something. Sit down."

So I sat down on her one stool, which I have never done, always taking the bowl away, always eating walking, as if being seen stationary were dangerous. And the chili did make me feel something. My eyes streamed. A dockhand slapped my back and laughed at my face. Suki refilled my water without asking. And I sat there weeping gently into a five-copper bowl of noodles while strangers assumed it was the spice, and it was the spice, and it wasn't the spice at all.

A life, I thought, is worth approximately one bowl of noodles plus tax. I had been pricing things wrong my entire career.

An old woman at the next table was telling her granddaughter about her late husband—how he'd whistled off-key, how she'd hated it, how she'd give her teeth to hear it again. The girl rolled her eyes with the magnificent cruelty of the young, and the old woman laughed, and I sat very still and thought: *that. Write that down. That's the exchange rate.*

Wren's soul against mine. A soul for a name. I kept trying to find the trick in the accounting, some hidden fee, some entry mispriced. There wasn't one. Some things you buy by letting go of the ledger entirely. Ovelia would faint.

* * *

Fifth night, I did the thing I'd been avoiding.

I went back to the palace. Not to the tasting chamber—to my own desk, in the little records room off the kitchens where the household accounts live. If I had six nights left as Quentin Eriksen, I wanted to spend part of one doing the only work I've ever been praised for, quietly, where no one could thank me for it.

I rebalanced the spice ledgers. I found two entries where the under-steward had padded the saffron costs and corrected them in pencil rather than ink, so he could fix it himself without shame—a courtesy I learned from watching Ovelia, though I'd never told her that either. There's a version of a good life that is just this: correcting small wrongs in pencil. I'd had it within reach for years and treated it like a consolation prize instead of the prize.

Halfway through the wine cellar inventory, the new alchemist came past the open door.

He stopped. Which no one does. No one stops at doors I'm behind; that is the entire architecture of my life. He was a young man, hollow-cheeked, ink-stained, with the harried look of someone promoted over a corpse—the old alchemist had not been seen since the night the stars went out, though officially he was "indisposed." The new one stood in the doorway holding a mortar like a shield and looked directly at me.

"Taster," he said. Uncertain. As if checking.

"That's me." It isn't, anymore, quite. But names are going out of fashion in my vicinity.

"Have you—" He glanced down the corridor, both directions, the gesture of a man checking for watchers, and something in my spine went cold and attentive, the old poison-sense waking. "Have you tasted the king's tonic lately? The evening one."

"Every night. Why?"

"No reason," he said, too fast, and then, quieter, with the desperate courage of the very frightened: "It's only—if you ever needed to speak to someone about tonics. About what's *in* them. Officially I couldn't help you. Unofficially—" and here he set the mortar down on my desk, and in the bottom of it, half-ground, was a grey crystal I recognized, because I'd found traces of it in the king's nightly draught weeks ago and filed the finding where I file all my findings, which is nowhere, which is the problem with being invisible: you can witness everything and report nothing, and it curdles into complicity so smoothly you mistake it for discretion. "Unofficially, the dispensary keeps records the last master never burned. Someone should burn them. Or read them."

He left before I could answer. Coward, I thought, and then, fairly: no. He'd just done the bravest thing available to him, which was to stand in a doorway and look at the man nobody looks at and say a sentence out loud.

I turned the crystal in the mortar under the lamp. Grey, faintly warm, and when I held it near the flame it threw shadows the wrong direction—toward the light instead of away. Under-court stuff. The same dull starlight-starved mineral that the wards had been leached of. The king's tonic had been drinking *him*, drop by drop, memory by memory, for years. The court above hadn't lost the stars by accident. They'd been dosed.

And I had tasted it. Every night. And said nothing, because saying something meant being somebody, and being somebody meant being blamable.

I sat in that records room for a long time with the little crystal warming my palm like a coal, and I understood that the six remaining nights weren't a gift I was spending on noodles and mended ledgers. They were a sentence I was serving, and the crime was eleven years long.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I would follow the tonic to its source, and the alchemist, old or new, would answer for what was in it—or I would die with ink on my fingers asking questions, which, come to think of it, is how I'd want the ledger to close anyway.

But tonight—tonight I walked home the long way, over Tanner's Bridge, past Suki's shuttered stall, and stood in the middle of the bridge looking up.

Five stars back. Only five, small and stubborn, hanging in a sky that had been sewn shut. One of them, the low red one, seemed to pulse, very slightly, like a candle leaning toward a voice.

"Sing, Wren," I told it. "Whoever's listening down there—keep singing. I'm coming with the accounts settled. All of them. Even mine."

The star did not answer, because stars are honest. But it stayed lit, and I counted that as a reply, and went home to spend my fifth night doing the most ordinary thing left to me: sleeping like a man with something left to lose, which is the only kind of sleep worth the name.

Two nights. One confession to shake loose beneath the palace, one name to spend at the bottom of the mountain. I fell asleep composing the entry, the way I compose all entries, precise and unflinching:

*Received: six ordinary evenings, fair condition, lightly wept upon. Paid out: everything. Balance owed to the world: one apology, delivered in person.*
