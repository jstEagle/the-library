## 13. The Alchemist's Confession

The royal alchemist's tower smelled of burnt sugar and old lies.

I had been in it exactly twice before—once to deliver a sample of the king's spittle for analysis (a task I would not wish on my worst enemy, and I have tasted things that would make my worst enemy weep), and once to return a ladle I had borrowed during the Incident with the Boar's Head, which is a story I will take to my grave, or at least to whatever grave a man without a name gets.

Both times, Master Corvane had been pleasant in the way that well-oiled machinery is pleasant: smooth, efficient, and entirely without warmth. He was a narrow man, gray as unsalted porridge, with spectacles so thick they made his eyes look like two startled coins trapped in glass. He had served three kings. Nobody could quite remember how long he'd been there. That should have been my first clue, years ago. A man who outlasts kings is either very good at his job or very good at something else.

Tonight I climbed his stairs knowing which.

I still had the vial in my pocket. The king's nightly tonic—the one I tasted every evening, the one that tasted of honey and chamomile and nothing else, because *nothing else* was precisely what it did. It didn't dull poison. It dulled memory. One sip a night, and King Aldous could stand on his balcony under an empty sky and feel only a vague, comfortable sense that the stars had always been optional.

Three nights since Wren vanished. Three nights since the wards fell with that harp-string snap I still heard when I closed my eyes. Two days since Brammel told me what I was. And one day since Ovelia expelled me from the Gilded Spoon with a face like a locked door.

So yes. I was not in a patient mood. But I knocked politely anyway, because old habits are the last thing to die in a man, and mine were specifically bred for longevity.

"Enter," said Corvane.

* * *

He was grinding something at his workbench—valerian root, by the smell—and he did not look up when I came in. That was its own small confirmation. Everyone looked up at me. Not because I was memorable; because I was a body in a doorway, and bodies in doorways are how assassins arrive. But Corvane kept grinding, unhurried, as though the door opening meant nothing at all.

Either he genuinely hadn't noticed me, which was impossible, or he knew exactly who I was and considered me beneath the effort of surprise.

"Master Eriksen," he said, to the mortar. "Or should I say, former guildsman. Word travels. Slowly, in your case, but it travels."

"You know about the expulsion."

"I know about everything that happens in this palace. It is my job to know." He set down the pestle and finally turned, folding his hands atop the bench. "You've come about the tonic."

Not *how may I help you*. Not *what brings you here at midnight*. Straight to it, like a man flipping to the last page of a book to see if the dog dies.

"The tonic," I agreed. "Let's talk about the tonic."

"What would you like to know? Its composition? Honey, chamomile, milk of the poppy—" 

"Don't." I took the vial from my pocket and set it on his bench between us, upright, deliberate, the way you place a card you've been holding all night. "Don't insult both of us. I've been tasting for eleven years, Master Corvane. I know what forgetting tastes like. It tastes like chamomile that's trying too hard."

For a long moment he simply looked at me, those coin-eyes catching the lamplight. Then, to my genuine astonishment, he laughed—a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge remembering motion.

"They told me you were unremarkable," he said. "Eleven years of reports, and every single one says the same thing. *Quiet. Competent. Forgettable.* I begin to suspect the reports were written by someone who never actually looked."

"Most people don't."

"No," he said softly. "They don't. And that, Master Eriksen, is the entire architecture of our peace."

* * *

He poured two cups of tea. I want that noted in whatever ledger governs my life: the man who helped gut the sky offered me tea, and it was good tea, oolong, properly steeped, and I drank it because refusing seemed somehow more obscene than accepting.

"The court above," he began, "did not lose the stars. It sold them."

I set down my cup. "Explain that sentence slowly, because I'm going to spend the rest of my life repeating it to people who won't believe me."

"Generations ago." He settled into the chair across from me, and for the first time he looked old—not gray-porridge old, but tired in the marrow, the way Brammel looked, the way anything looks when it has been burning far longer than it was built to. "There was a war. You'll find no record of it; records were part of the price. The Court Beneath rose against the Crown, and the Crown was losing. Badly. The wards were failing, the dragon-fire was guttering, and the Under-King offered terms."

"Terms."

"He would withdraw. End the war. Seal himself beneath the mountain and trouble the surface never again—in exchange for one thing." Corvane's hands were very still around his cup. "The court above would surrender its *memory* of the stars. All of it. Every song, every chart, every name the starkeepers had given them. A people who cannot remember the light will never miss it. Will never reach for it. Will never wonder what's keeping it from them."

"And they agreed."

"The alternative was burning." He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "Kings are not poets, Master Eriksen. When given the choice between their children and their sky, most choose their children. It is difficult even now to call it wrong."

"But the wards—"

"Were maintained by the dragon, alone, for two hundred years. Until he too grew tired. And the Court Beneath, patient as debt, began to collect interest." He gestured upward, toward the ceiling, toward the empty night above it. "Souls, Master Eriksen. Starlight is made of attention, of witness, of *mattering*. They cannot make their own anymore—they traded that away long before we did, though that is another story. So they buy souls to burn. And the tonic ensures that the court above watches the sky go dark and feels nothing but a mild nostalgia for weather."

I thought of the feast. Of nobles laughing under a starless dark, raising cups to a king who couldn't remember why the balconies felt so strangely bare. I thought of Wren's voice making candles lean, and a courteous stranger paying her in light.

"How long have you been brewing it?"

"My family has brewed it for four generations." He said it the way other men say *we have farmed this land for four generations*, with a terrible inherited pride. "It is not a betrayal, whatever your face is doing. It is a *duty*. The most important duty in the palace. More important than the king's life, frankly, which is why the taster's office has always reported to me rather than the other way round."

Something cold walked down my spine. "Say that again."

"The taster's office answers to the alchemist. It has always been so. Why do you think the position pays so poorly and attracts so few?" He smiled, thin as a paper cut. "We do not need a man to catch poisons, Master Eriksen. We need a man whose tongue can verify the tonic's dose, night after night, year after year, and who will never once think to ask why the sky is empty. Your office was designed by my grandfather. You are the last ingredient in the recipe."

* * *

I want to tell you I reacted with dignity. That I stood, or shouted, or overturned his workbench in a satisfying clatter of glassware. What I actually did was sit very still and feel eleven years rearrange themselves behind my eyes like furniture in a house I'd never truly lived in.

They chose me because I was invisible. I had thought it was luck, then skill, then fate. It was none of those. It was *specification*. Somewhere in a dead man's notes, my particular affliction—my gift, Brammel called it, my curse, I called it—had been circled as ideal. A taster who cannot be remembered cannot be questioned. Cannot be suspected. Cannot, in the end, *matter enough to matter*.

And then a second thought arrived, colder than the first, wearing familiar shoes.

Tasters. Plural. My parents had been tasters. That was the whole of what I knew of them—a profession, a death by poison when I was six, and a grief I had spent my life dosing into silence. My mother's hands. My father's habit of humming while he worked. Nothing else. Memory is a poor archivist when it starts so young.

"Corvane," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "What were their names?"

"Whose?"

"My parents. Erik and—my mother. What was my mother's name?"

He looked at me over his spectacles, and I watched him decide whether to lie. I watched him decide against it, not from kindness but from something worse: the weariness of a man too tired for one more secret.

"I never knew your mother's name," he said. "But I knew your father. Tomas. Tomas Eriksen, taster to Aldous's father. A careful man. Quiet. Competent." He paused. "Forgettable."

The room tilted slightly. I gripped the arm of the chair.

"Tomas came to me once," Corvane went on, quieter now, "near the end. He had begun to notice. The empty sky, the missing years, the way the court flinched from nothing and remembered nothing. Tasters notice things—that is the whole of the trade. And he asked me what was in the tonic. I told him. All of it. The war, the bargain, the souls." His coin-eyes held mine. "Do you know what he did?"

"Tell me."

"He asked for a double dose."

The words landed like a dropped stone in a well—long silence, then the deep cold splash of understanding.

"He had a son," Corvane said. "A boy of six. And Tomas understood that if he let himself remember—if he let himself *know*—he would do something reckless and noble and fatal, and his son would grow up fatherless in a palace that eats orphans for breakfast. So he drank deeper. He chose to forget the sky, the war, the crime, all of it, so that he could stay alive and unnoticed and *present*. For you." He spread his hands. "Two months later he died of poison anyway. The universe has a sense of humor. I have never found it funny."

I sat there. Somewhere in the tower a clock ticked, indifferent, doing its job the way we all did ours.

My father had known. Had known everything I now knew, had sat in this same chair drinking this same tea, and had chosen the tonic over the truth because he had a boy to feed. And I had spent thirty years believing myself an orphan of accident, when really I was an orphan of arithmetic—the same arithmetic now sitting in front of me, offering me the same cup.

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. That was the pattern laid out plain as a recipe. *Be small. Be safe. Swallow the forgetting. Stay alive for the ones you love.* My father had followed it to the letter and lost everything anyway, including the memory of why he'd done it. And here I was, following him step for step down a stair I could finally see the bottom of: hoard yourself, hide yourself, dose your heart against attachment—and lose Wren regardless. Lose her to a court that collected souls the way my guild collected bottled laughter, patiently, in trade, from people who never read the fine print.

Generational doom, Ovelia would have called it, if she'd deigned to put it in a ledger. *Inherited liability. Recurring charge.*

Well. I had been expelled from the guild. Nobody was auditing me anymore.

I stood up.

"Master Eriksen." Corvane didn't rise. He looked, suddenly, very old and very tired, a man at the end of a four-generation shift. "Sit down. Think about what you're doing. You are one man. You are, by your own nature, a man no one will remember fighting beside, mourning, or avenging. Whatever you intend—you will do it alone, and it will leave no mark, and the tonic will go on being poured."

"That's where you're wrong," I said. "It already left a mark. You just told me about my father. That's a mark. That's the first mark anyone's ever left on me, and you did it by confessing, which means somewhere under four generations of duty there's a man who wanted to be stopped." I picked up the vial from his bench and weighed it in my palm. "So here's what happens now. Tomorrow night, the king's tonic comes to my table as usual. And I taste it as usual. And I report—as I'm required to report, to you, the alchemist, my superior—that the dose is correct."

His brow creased. "That is... what you already do."

"Yes. Except tomorrow I'll be lying about it, because tomorrow I'm going to start diluting it. A little more each night. Slowly. Chamomile that tries a little less hard." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a key turning in a lock that hadn't been opened in thirty years. "You taught the whole court to forget gently, Corvane. I'm going to teach it to remember the same way. One spoonful at a time. By the time anyone notices the sky, it'll be too late to stop it."

"You'll be caught."

"By whom? I'm not memorable, remember? You said so yourself. Eleven years of reports." I tucked the vial away. "Turns out everyone's favorite flaw of mine is also the perfect smuggling route. I've been contraband my whole life. Time I acted like it."

He stared at me for a long moment. And then—quietly, so quietly I almost missed it—he nodded. Once. Whether it was surrender, blessing, or simply the nod of a tired man handing off a shift, I couldn't say. Maybe he didn't know either.

At the door I paused. "One more thing. The kitchen maid, Sera. She smuggled out a map for me. If anyone asks—"

"No one will ask," Corvane said. "No one ever asks about the invisible ones." A pause. "That was always the tragedy of your office, Master Eriksen. And tonight, perhaps, it becomes the hope of someone else's."

I went down the stairs two at a time, out into the starless dark, and I did not feel afraid.

I felt *counted*.

Behind me, in the tower, I heard the alchemist begin, very softly, to hum—an old tune, half-remembered, the kind of tune a man might have learned from a fellow taster with a habit of humming while he worked. My father's tune.

I carried it down the mountain with me, toward the stair that isn't there, toward the vault where a girl's soul burned in a cage of glass. Seven nights, the Under-King had said. Five remained.

I had a debt to pay, a pattern to break, and—for the first time in my life—a song in my head that wasn't mine.

I intended to use all three.
