## 14. Ovelia's Oldest Entry

I have kept the Gilded Spoon's ledgers for forty-one years, and in that time I have learned one iron law of the trade: *nothing is ever truly lost, only misfiled.*

Souls, years, first kisses, the last words of dying mothers—clients bring them to us because they cannot bear to hold them, and we hold them because no one else will. We file grief by weight and joy by rarity. We keep receipts for things that have no business existing, and we are never, ever sentimental about it, because sentiment is how a guild dies. Sentiment is how an Ovelia dies.

Which is why it took me three days to admit what I was looking at.

It started with the ledgers themselves—the ones Quentin left behind when I threw him out. He'd been my bookkeeper for nine years, and he had a hand like copperplate come to life, every entry ruled straight as a plumb line. When I sat down to reconstruct his accounts, I found errors.

Quentin did not make errors. In nine years, I could count his mistakes on one hand, and two of those were mine and he'd corrected them quietly without telling me.

But here was a ledger with a smudged total. Here was a column that summed wrong by exactly four coppers—a deliberate error, the kind you plant so someone checking your work feels clever and stops looking. And here, on page after page, were repairs. Small ones. A water-damaged folio re-sewn with thread a shade too fine for guild stock. A margin note in a different ink, correcting a valuation made thirty years before Quentin was born. A pressed sprig of star-thyme tucked against an entry for *one memory of a garden, sold under duress*, as if he were paying respect.

He hadn't just kept our books. He'd been tending them. Anonymously, patiently, the way a man tends a grave.

Or an apology.

"Damn you, Eriksen," I said to the empty office, and my voice cracked on the second word, which was unacceptable, so I poured myself the medicinal brandy and pretended it was for the ledger's health.

* * *

Merrin found me at midnight still surrounded by paper. My junior clerk is a sharp-nosed boy with the moral flexibility of a wet reed and the loyalty of a mastiff, which is a combination I have exploited shamelessly for six years.

"You've been down here since yesterday," he said.

"I'm auditing."

"You're brooding over old ledgers with a candle burning at both ends because you lit it wrong." He dropped into the chair across from me, uninvited, as is his habit. "The council wants to know if you're going to report Quentin's movements to the Court Beneath. Per the treaty."

"The treaty," I said.

"That's what I said."

"Merrin, fetch me the founding box. The real one, not the one we show auditors."

He went pale in the way young men do when they discover their employer has depths, most of them unpleasant. "There's a *fake* founding box?"

"There's a fake everything. Go."

While he was gone, I looked at the oldest repair again. It was the one that had been bothering me all evening, the reason I'd sent for the box. Deep in Ledger One, on a page so old the vellum had gone the color of weak tea, there was an entry in a hand I knew better than my own—because it was my own, forty-one years younger, all uncertain loops and ink-blots.

My first entry. Written my first night as a junior clerk, when I was Merrin's age and twice as arrogant, copying from the master ledger while the old mistress watched me with her hooded eyes.

*Received into holding: one debt, unresolved. Owed by the Gilded Spoon to Eriksen, taster, and Eriksen-born wife, for services rendered in the matter of the Stair. Value: unassessed. Status: OPEN.*

I had copied it without understanding it, as juniors do. And then I had filed it, and the guild had filed it, and forty-one years is a long time for a thing to sit in the dark getting heavier.

*Eriksen.* Taster.

Quentin Eriksen. Who never once, in nine years, asked the guild for anything. Who took his wage in coin when everyone else took it in favors and trades. Who kept himself small and unsalted and unremarkable, who dosed his own heart the way he dosed his tongue—

Who was the son of tasters. Who had grown up in a house where the word *Stair* meant something. Who must have found this entry years ago, deep in our books, and said nothing, and asked nothing, and instead mended our ledgers in secret like penance for a sin that wasn't his.

The founding box arrived. Merrin set it down and hovered.

"Go to bed, Merrin."

"No," he said, and sat back down.

* * *

The true founding box smells of cedar and something older, something like the air in a tomb. Inside: the guild charter, signed by seven thieves and a saint; the original seal matrix, which we claim was lost; and the treaty.

The Treaty of Quiet Neighbors. Sixty-two years old. I had read summaries of it my whole career. I had never read it whole, because reading it whole required the mistress's key, and mistresses do not hand down keys to clerks who ask questions.

I read it whole now, by candlelight, while Merrin pretended not to read over my shoulder and failed completely.

It was worse than I'd feared and simpler, too. In exchange for the under-court's forbearance—no raids on our vaults, no claims on our holdings—we had agreed to three things. First: neutrality. The Gilded Spoon would trade with the Court Beneath on the same terms as any client. Second: silence. We would not warn, inform, or assist the surface courts against it.

And third.

Third was written in the same hand as the entry in Ledger One. The old mistress's hand. A codicil, added eleven years after the treaty itself, initialed by every mistress since:

*Should the Court Beneath purchase souls toward the end of drawing down the stars, the Gilded Spoon shall raise no obstacle, neither by act nor omission, until such time as the debt owed to Eriksen, taster, be paid in full.*

They'd known. For sixty years, the guild had known what the under-court wanted souls *for*, and we had sold our silence with a rider attached—a rider designed, I realized with a cold spreading through my chest, to ensure it would never be honored. Because the debt was *unassessed*. No value. No terms. And a debt with no assessed value cannot be paid, cannot be canceled, cannot expire. It just sits there, open forever, a lock on our conscience that we ourselves had thrown away the key to.

Except someone had kept a key. Someone had spent nine years quietly repairing our ledgers, pricing the unpriceable, learning the exact grammar of value—and had never once invoked the debt. Not when it could have saved him exile. Not when it could have bought him allies. Quentin had found the guild's shame and chosen to carry it rather than spend it.

Because spending it would mean being seen. Being owed. Being *known*.

"Oh, you absolute fool," I whispered, and I wasn't sure which of us I meant.

"What does it say?" Merrin asked. "Ovelia. What does it say?"

I told him. All of it. He listened the way the young listen to bad news—too fast, already three steps ahead, already angry. "So the guild has been feeding people to the dark for sixty years to keep its vaults safe, and the one man who could have ended it carried the receipt around in his head and never used it because—" He stopped. "Because of what the dragon told him. Because being remembered costs him something the rest of us get free."

"Yes."

"Then the treaty's void, isn't it? If the debt can be assessed and paid—"

"It can't be voided, Merrin. It can only be broken. By me. Knowingly, formally, with the seal." I turned the parchment toward him so he could see the clause beneath the codicil, the one every apprentice learns: *No master of the Spoon shall break this treaty save at cost of her office and all things held in trust thereby.*

If I broke it, I lost the guild. The vaults, the holdings, the charter, the name. Everything the Spoon had spent two centuries accumulating, everything I had spent forty-one years protecting.

"They'll take Wren's soul anyway," Merrin said quietly. "And Quentin's. Whether you burn that paper or not. The only question is whether the guild stands with them or stands on the treaty."

I have made perhaps four decisions in my life that I knew were irreversible at the moment I made them. Marrying Tomas. Taking the mistress's chair. Selling the winter, God forgive me. This was the fifth, and it was the easiest.

* * *

We did it properly, because if you're going to commit institutional suicide, you should commit it with good bookkeeping.

I convened the senior partners at dawn—all seven of them, gray faces around the long table, and I laid out the treaty and the codicil and the entry in Ledger One, and I watched them each arrive at the same place I had arrived, and I watched most of them flinch away.

"The debt could ruin us," said Half-mask Vey, whose actual face no one remembers, which in hindsight should have been informative. "An unassessed obligation to a dead family—if we acknowledge it, every client with a grudge will invent a grievance—"

"The debt isn't the issue," I said. "The issue is that we have been selling children to the dark for sixty years and calling it prudence."

"We've been calling it *survival*," said Corvane, gently, the way you speak to someone grieving. "Ovelia. The court beneath cannot be fought. You know what happened to the houses that tried. The treaty keeps the lights on. It keeps *us*—"

"It keeps us complicit. There's a difference between surviving a famine and running the kitchen for it."

"Then you'll end your career for one boy and one apprentice?" Corvane spread his hands. "For sentiment?"

"For honor," I said, "which is the only inventory item this guild has never once held in stock."

I stood. I took my copy of the treaty—it is part of the office that each mistress holds a copy, so that no single burning can undo the thing, a redundancy I suddenly understood as a horror rather than a safeguard—and I carried it to the hearth where the accounting-fires were already laid for the morning's work.

Vey moved first. "Ovelia, wait—"

"If any partner wishes to invoke the law against me," I said, without turning, "do it now, while my hands are empty."

Nobody did. Cowards, all of them, and thank God for it.

The treaty caught slowly, the way old parchment does—reluctantly, then all at once, edges curling black, the codicil in the dead mistress's hand glowing ember-red before it went to ash. Sixty-two years of quiet neighbors. Sixty years of *neither by act nor omission*. I watched it become smoke and felt lighter than I had since the night the stars went out.

"Well," said Corvane, into the silence. "You've killed us."

"No," I said. "You killed us decades ago. I've just done the paperwork." I looked around the table at seven faces I had trusted and been betrayed by in equal measure. "The Spoon is yours to administer per the charter's dissolution clauses. I resign my office. Merrin—he's coming with me. Anyone else who wishes to join us may, provided they understand the terms: we are going below, we are going armed with nothing but a ledger, and we intend to collect a debt."

* * *

By full light we were three—myself, Merrin, and old Sefa from the vaults, who appeared at the door with a pack on her shoulder and said only, "Someone has to carry the candles," and I nearly wept, and didn't, because there was no time.

I stopped at my desk only once. I opened Ledger One to the first page I ever wrote, and beneath the old entry, in my own hand, forty-one years steadier than it had been, I wrote the assessment:

*Debt owed by the Gilded Spoon to the family Eriksen, for services rendered in the matter of the Stair, heretofore concealed: ASSESSED IN FULL. Terms of payment: the soul of Wren, returned; the person of Quentin Eriksen, restored; the treaty, void. Paid by: Ovelia, mistress, against the whole holdings of the guild.*

*A debt this size requires everything. So be it.*

Then I closed the book, and gave it to Sefa, and we went out into a starless morning to find the stair that isn't there.

Quentin, you impossible, invisible fool. You spent your whole life making sure no one would come after you.

You should have priced that better.
