## 15. The Under-King's Offer

The Under-King did not gloat.

I want to be clear about that, because I had spent three days in his court expecting gloating—the long villain's speech, the velvet threat, the monologue delivered while pacing before a fireplace that burned something it shouldn't. Kings above do that sort of thing. King Aldous once kept me standing for forty minutes while he explained at length why he suspected the duck.

The Under-King poured tea instead.

It was real tea—surface tea, mountain-grown, the kind the Gilded Spoon sold by the memory and I had tasted exactly twice in my life—and he poured it with the unshowy competence of a man who had done nothing else for ten thousand years and had made his peace with it.

"You look tired," he said.

"I've been underground for two days," I said. "There's no morning here. My body doesn't know when to be anything."

"That will pass." He set the cup before me on a table of black glass that reflected not my face but my hands—young hands, hands I hadn't owned since I was twenty. "Drink. It isn't drugged. I know you'll check anyway."

I checked. Of course I checked. Twenty years of tasting for a paranoid king leaves a man with habits deeper than his bones. Bitterness of willowbark, sweetness of honey, a mineral edge I couldn't name and didn't trust—but no nightshade, no dullthorn, none of the alchemist's little library of quiet poisons.

"Good?" he asked.

"It's tea," I said. "You don't get points for not poisoning someone."

"Ah, but I do." He settled across from me, folding himself down like smoke deciding to be a man. His borrowed face was handsome in an unmemorable way—I realized after a moment that this was deliberate, that the face was designed to slip out of the mind the way mine slipped out of rooms—and his eyes were the only honest thing about him: grey as starlight going out. "Everything in my court is transactional, Master Eriksen. Even courtesy. You should appreciate that. You keep books."

"I used to."

"You still do." He smiled faintly. "You're keeping one now, whether you admit it or not. Seven nights remaining. Six, by the time we finish our tea. Every kindness I show you is an entry against what you owe me. You're a careful man. You'll have noticed."

I had noticed. The chamber they'd given me had a bed too soft, food too good, a window that showed stars—stars, down here beneath the mountain, caged in crystal like moths. Everything offered. Nothing given. I was a taster; I knew the difference between hospitality and bait.

"Why am I still here?" I asked. "We struck the bargain. Your gift for Wren's soul. Payment due in seven nights. There's nothing to negotiate."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Nothing at all. I merely wondered if you'd like to see what you're buying."

"Buying?"

"With your life." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Your gift, Master Eriksen. The one you've been calling caution since you were old enough to know what you were doing. The art of being passed over, overlooked, forgotten in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Do you know what it actually is?"

"Brammel says it's magic."

"Brammel is a lamp running out of oil and entitled to his poetry." The Under-King leaned forward, and the chandeliers of caged light dimmed toward him, the way candles lean toward Wren's singing. I hated that I noticed. He noticed me noticing. "It is *unremembering*, Quentin Eriksen. Not invisibility—that's a trick of the eyes, cheap, common, any hedge-witch can sell you a salve. What you have is rarer. You are a hole in the world's attention. And there is exactly one place where such a thing has value beyond price."

"The vault," I said.

"The vault," he agreed, and for the first time his voice lost its pleasantness and became simply, terribly, sincere.

* * *

He took me to see it.

Of course he did. That's the thing about the under-court that the stories never tell you—they aren't liars. Liars are cheap. They are *precise*. Every horror they show you is true, accurately rendered, honestly labeled. It's the labels that kill you.

The vault sat at the bottom of everything, past the ballrooms where nobles wore the faces of people they'd eaten, past the gardens growing in soil made of ground-up promises. A door of plain iron, which surprised me until I understood: you don't decorate the thing you can't afford to lose.

Inside, the dark was not empty. It was full. Cages upon cages of light, ranked into the distance farther than my eyes could follow, each one humming at a different pitch, and woven through them all—a sound I knew before I knew why I knew it. A voice, thin as thread, singing something small and stubborn about a baker's cart and a stolen roll.

Wren.

"She doesn't suffer," the Under-King said beside me, and I flinched because I hadn't heard him move. Of course I hadn't. "That word gets misused down here. She is *held*. Her soul is in the third rank, fourth column. Her body sleeps in a room upstairs with fresh linens. When the payment comes due, the two are rejoined and she walks out of my mountain whole, minus seven days she won't remember. You have my word."

"Your word."

"I keep my bargains. Ask anyone I've ever bought." Something moved behind his face that might, in a better-lit place, have been called grief. "It's the one law I have left. But that isn't what I brought you to see."

He walked forward into the ranks of caged light, and the light leaned away from him, and I followed because the alternative was following later.

"Do you know how the vault works?" he asked. "No—of course you don't. Your guild sells the fruit without teaching anyone the orchard. Souls are fuel, yes, but fuel for what? Heat? No. *Attention.* A soul is the part of a person that notices things. That remembers. That insists, against all evidence, that it matters. We harvest that insistence, Master Eriksen, and we feed it to the sky." He gestured up, though up meant only more mountain. "Or rather—we fed it to the sky, once. Before your king's grandfather sold the memory of us to buy his borders. Now we feed it to ourselves, and we starve, and every hundred years we grow a little smaller, and a little hungrier, and a little less able to remember why hunger is shameful."

"And you need a keeper."

"I need a *warden*." He stopped before a cage that held, instead of light, a darkness so complete it seemed to have weight. "Someone must tend the vault. Count the stock. Feed the fires. Keep the souls from guttering. For ten thousand years I have done it myself, and I am—look at me. I am what ten thousand years of tending looks like. I need someone who can walk these ranks unseen by the light itself. Someone whose presence doesn't disturb what's held here. Someone who is, in the most literal sense possible, *no one*." He turned to me, and the grey eyes were almost kind. "I'm not asking for your gift, Quentin. Gifts are taken; this would be chosen. I'm asking for *you*. All of you. Your unremembering, your ledger-keeping, your careful tongue, your habit of loving people at arm's length because arm's length is safe. Bring all of it down here and aim it at the only work that will never betray you."

"And what happens to Quentin Eriksen?"

"He becomes warden of the vault," said the Under-King gently, "and no one above ever misses him."

* * *

Here is what he did next, and I want it recorded precisely, because precision is the only weapon I've ever carried.

He showed me my life.

Not a vision. Not a dream. He opened a cage—one of the middle ones, holding light the color of a kitchen fire—and he said, "Look," and I looked, and I was *in* it.

I stood in the Gilded Spoon's back room at evening, watching myself ink figures into a ledger by lamplight. Ovelia across the desk, sharpening a quill with the grim concentration she gave to everything. The other-me glanced up, said something dry about a client who'd tried to pay in nostalgia, and Ovelia laughed—that short, startled laugh she probably thought nobody had ever heard.

"Watch," the Under-King murmured, though his voice came from nowhere, everywhere, the way voices do in memories.

And then the scene continued, but I was no longer in it.

Other-me finished the entry. Ovelia looked up to make some remark—her mouth shaped the beginning of it—and then her gaze slid off, the way gazes slide off me in hallways, at feasts, everywhere, always. She frowned slightly, as if she'd misplaced something small, and went back to her quills. Other-me spoke again and she answered the air, cheerfully, to no one.

"They adjust," the Under-King said softly. "That's the mercy of it. Minds hate gaps. Around you, they fill you in with nothing and carry on. Look further."

Further. Weeks compressed into breaths. I watched myself eat dinner alone at the corner table of the Dented Kettle, and the serving girl who liked me—Tamsin, who saved me the heel ends of bread because I'd once told her they made the best toast—set the plate down at an empty chair, puzzled, and shrugged, and gave the heel ends to someone else the next day. I watched Wren come looking for me after supper, call my name in the stairwell, hear it echo unanswered, and stand very still for a long moment with her fists clenched, and finally say to the empty air, in a small furious voice, "*Fine,*" and stomp off—and then, two scenes later, watch her not think of me at all, because there was nothing left to think *with*.

"This is what you already live," the Under-King said. "This is your gift, working exactly as designed. I'm offering you nothing you haven't had. Only honesty about it. Above, you are a ghost pretending to be a man and paying for the pretense daily. Below—" he spread his hands, and the vault lights bent around him like water around a stone, "—you would be a ghost among ghosts, and the work would be *true*. Tending light that cannot see you. Keeping accounts that no one will falsify, because no one will remember you exist to bribe. Isolation, yes. But isolation with *purpose*. Tell me that isn't more than you have now."

And the worst part—the part I have not put in any ledger, the part I am putting here because this account may outlive me—is that for one full breath, I wanted it.

Not the loneliness. I know loneliness the way a fish knows water. I wanted the *cleanliness* of it. A job with no faces. A duty with no attachments to fail. Twenty years I have dosed my heart like I dose my stomach, small regular measures of distance, and here was a bargain that said: *stop pretending. You were built for the dark. Come home.*

"Say it," the Under-King urged, and his borrowed face was gentle, and somewhere far above us Wren's song threaded through the cage-ranks, thin and stubborn. "Say yes, and the debt dissolves. Wren goes free tonight. No seven nights. No waiting. You keep your word, I keep mine, everyone eats."

"*Everyone*," I repeated.

His smile flickered—just a hairline crack, a taster's eye catching the wrong bitterness in a soup. "Pardon?"

"'Everyone eats,'" I said. "That's what you said. But it's not true, is it? Because if I stay, the stars stay dead. The wards stay broken. Whatever's pressing in on your borders keeps pressing. Wren goes free into a world with no sky worth freeing her into." I looked at him, and I let him see me look, which is the one thing no one in this entire mountain has been able to bear. "You didn't offer me a position. You offered me anesthesia. Same drug the alchemist puts in the king's tonic. Same drug the guild deals in bottled. *Forget, and be comfortable.*"

The silence stretched.

Then the Under-King laughed—not cruelly, which frightened me more than cruelty would have. He laughed like a man hearing an old song he'd written himself.

"Ohh," he said. "Oh, you *are* your parents' child."

The floor did not go cold. The lights did not gutter. Nothing dramatic happened at all. But every hair on my arms stood up, and the tea in my stomach turned to ice, and the Under-King watched me understand it with the patient satisfaction of a man who has finally gotten a client to read the fine print.

"Did you truly think," he said, "that a rare and corruptible magic appears from nowhere? That the royal family employs *tasters*—a hereditary post, boy, a *guild* post—as a matter of tradition? Your mother stood where you stand. Your father signed what you nearly signed. They chose the same anesthesia, and they were *good* wardens, and they were forgotten by everyone including themselves, and their son grew up thinking his emptiness was a personality." He straightened, and the borrowed face began to slip at last, features sliding like wax, unable to hold shape against whatever was underneath. "I have been buying your family for four generations. You were never going to be the taster who broke the pattern. You were going to be the taster who *completed* it."

I should have run. I should have argued. Instead I heard myself ask the bookkeeper's question, the only question that matters when the numbers turn against you:

"How much time is left on my seven nights?"

"Five," said the Under-King, wearing now half my father's face and half no face at all. "But do hurry. I've just received word that an old woman with a ledger is asking questions at the mountain's gate—and unlike you, Quentin Eriksen, *she* is very hard to forget."
