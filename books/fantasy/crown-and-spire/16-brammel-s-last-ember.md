## 16. Brammel's Last Ember

I have watched ten thousand bargains struck in my cave, and I will tell you the truth of them: no one ever trades what they say they are trading.

The merchant who came to buy my fire for his forges was really buying the right to tell his neighbors he owned a dragon's flame. The queen who begged a scale from me to wear at her coronation was buying courage she had not earned. The pilgrims who left offerings of honey-wine and asked nothing in return — ah, but they were buying the sweetest thing of all, which is the story they would tell of their own generosity until they died.

And Quentin Eriksen. The taster. The ledger-keeper. The man built out of small careful absences.

He came to my cave twice now, and both times he traded without knowing it. The first time he bought a truth he did not want: that his invisibility was never caution, never discipline, never the clever armor of a sensible man. It was magic. The gift of being unremembered, worn so long and so well that he had mistaken it for his personality. I watched him carry that truth up the mountain like a stone in his mouth, and I confess I laughed after he left. Not cruelly. But dragons are old, and old things find a certain humor in watching someone discover the shape of their own cage only when they finally decide to leave it.

The second time, he came asking about the wards. About relighting them. And I told him the truth again, because that is apparently my function in this story: I am the creature who tells truths at inconvenient prices.

Here is the truth I did not tell him, because he did not ask, and because even a dragon grows tired of being the bearer of ruin:

I cannot relight the wards. Not because I will not. Because there is almost nothing left of me to light them with.

* * *

You should understand what a dragon's fire is, since the stories get it wrong. It is not fuel. It is not breath and belly-fat and sulfur, the way the scholars write in their dry little books. A dragon's fire is memory. Every flame we spend is a remembering — of the first sunrise we saw, of the weight of our mother's wing over us, of the smell of the world before men put roads on it. We burn what we are to make heat, and when a dragon gutters, it is because he has spent himself down to the last few recollections, and those he guards like a miser guards his final coin.

I am down to three.

I will not tell you what they are. Some things stay mine. But I will tell you this: one of them is warm, one of them smells of rain, and one of them I have been saving for two thousand years against an emergency exactly like this one, though I could not have named it until tonight.

* * *

The boy came back at dusk. Not Quentin — the other one. Wren. Or rather, the shape of Wren, which is worse. She walked up the mountain path with her feet not quite touching the ground, and her voice came ahead of her into my cave like smoke under a door, singing something small and sweet about bread, of all things, bread and butter and somebody's name she couldn't finish saying.

The under-court sent her. Or sent something wearing her, the way they wear everything. They stood her in the mouth of my cave with the last light behind her, and the thing wearing her face said, in her voice, in a voice that made my bones lean toward it the way candles lean toward her true voice, "Brammel, last of your kind. The Court Beneath offers you rest. You have spent so much. Let us carry what remains."

They offered me sleep. Do you understand? Two thousand years of carrying the sky's errands, and they offered me the one thing I cannot take, which is permission to stop while the world still needs the opposite.

I said, "Tell your king that a dragon does not hand his embers to beggars."

The thing wearing Wren smiled with her smile, and it was the worst thing I have seen in a century, and I have seen continents drown. Then it bowed — courteously, the way everything in that court is courteous — and went back down the mountain, singing.

I sat in the dark after that and did arithmetic. Dragons do arithmetic at the end. How many years of dark if the wards stay dead. How many souls in that vault, caged and dimming. How much of me is left, and what it would buy, and whether any of it would be enough.

The answer kept coming back the same: not enough. Not enough to relight the wards. Not enough to burn down the under-court. Not enough to matter, if I spent myself the way dragons have always spent themselves, in great roaring conflagrations, in wars and wardings and the sealing of borders against the hungry dark.

Not enough to do anything *big*.

Which is when the thought arrived, the way important thoughts always arrive — sideways, quietly, wearing plain clothes.

* * *

Every bargain I have witnessed, and I told you I have watched ten thousand, and every one of them was a trade of something for something less. Men trade true things for pretty things. Kings trade tomorrow for today. The court above traded its memory of the stars for peace, which is like trading your eyes for a painting of the view. And the under-court trades courtesy for souls, payment always deferred, interest always compounding.

But in ten thousand bargains, do you know how many were *true* trades? How many times did someone give a real thing for a real thing, full price, no trick, no deferral, no fine print?

Once.

A woman came up the mountain four hundred years ago with her firstborn son asleep on her shoulder, and she said, "Dragon, I have brought you a gift, and I want nothing." And I said, "Everyone wants something," and she said, "Yes, but not from you," and she set down a loaf of bread still warm from her oven, and she sang to me — badly, off-key, with love — and she went home.

She wanted nothing from me. She gave anyway. That was the only true trade I ever saw, and I have hoarded the memory of it the way other dragons hoard gold, and I begin to suspect — sitting here in the dark, doing my end-of-things arithmetic — that I have been saving it all this time without knowing why.

Now I know why.

* * *

Quentin will come back. He is coming back even now; I can feel his particular quality of absence approaching up the mountain, the way you can feel a draft from a door opened in a far room. He has been to the court beneath. He has made his bargain. Seven nights, he thinks, and then he pays with his gift, or with himself, whichever the Under-King decides the fine print says. He does not know yet which. That is the cruelty of that place: you sign first and read after.

He will come to me desperate, and he will ask me for fire for the wards, because the wards are the big thing, the obvious thing, the thing a hero asks a dragon for. And here is where I must decide, here in the dark, with three memories left and one of them warm and one of them smelling of rain and one of them a loaf of bread and a bad song four hundred years gone.

Do I spend my last ember on the wards? The old duty. The ancient charge. The thing every dragon before me would have done without hesitation, because it was *the work*, and the work was what we were for.

Or do I do the thing no dragon has ever done?

Do I forge instead of burn?

* * *

Here is what I know that the courts above and below have both forgotten: fire can be shaped as well as spent. My ancestors sealed the wards with broad strokes, whole horizons of flame poured across the borders of the world. Magnificent. Unrepeatable. And useless for what is needed now, because the problem is no longer a border. The problem is a vault. One vault, deep beneath the mountain, holding a stolen girl's soul and a thousand others besides, behind a lock that has never been picked because no one ever had a key — because keys are *made*, and making is not burning, and no dragon has ever been small enough and precise enough and humble enough to try.

No dragon has ever been nearly out.

That is the joke, if you want it. The old world's fire was too vast for small works. It could seal borders and boil seas and light the lamps of heaven, but it could not fit through a keyhole. Only a guttering remnant, a dragon down to his last three memories, could ever make a flame small enough, fine enough, *true* enough to open one door once.

I am not the last dragon because the world failed me. I am the last dragon because the world finally needs exactly what I have become.

* * *

So I have decided. I will tell him no. When Quentin comes up the mountain with his desperate eyes and his careful invisible heart, and asks me for fire to relight the wards, I will tell him no — and then I will show him what I will do instead.

I will take the memory of the rain first, because rain forgives everything, and pour it into the forge of my chest. Then the warm one, because a key that opens a soul-vault ought to be warm, so that whatever soul passes through knows, in the instant of its opening, that it was loved. And last — last, when the key is nearly formed and needs only its tempering — the bread. The woman's off-key song. The only true trade I ever witnessed.

I will spend all of it. Not on the wards. Not on war. On one small object, one turn of one lock, one use and then slag. The old world's last light, spent not on preserving itself but on setting something free.

It is not what dragons are for. That is precisely why it must be me.

* * *

His footsteps now. Careful ones, on the scree, the walk of a man who has spent a lifetime not being heard. In a moment he will round the corner and see me — see me truly, perhaps, for the first time, since seeing is not his habit — and he will see a dying lamp of a creature, and he will ask his question, and I will give him my answer.

Let him think, for one more moment, that the old bargains still hold. Let him climb the last stretch believing he has come to negotiate.

Because I have already decided, and I find — this surprises even me, an old lizard who thought himself past surprise — that I am glad. Two thousand years of guarding, spending, sealing, sacrificing for the *work*. And at the end, the finest thing I will ever do is make a key.

The stars went out because the world forgot how to give without taking. Very well. Watch, then, as the last dragon gives everything and takes nothing, and forges the one true thing that will open the dark.

He rounds the corner. Our eyes meet — mine banked coals, his careful gray.

"Dragon," Quentin Eriksen says, "I need your fire."

And I, Brammel, last of my kind, keeper of three memories and one true trade, open my mouth to say the word no dragon has ever said to a supplicant before:

"No. But come inside. I'm going to teach you what fire is *for*."
