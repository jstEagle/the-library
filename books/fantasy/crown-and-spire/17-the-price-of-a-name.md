## 17. The Price of a Name

The vault door was not a door.

That was the first thing I understood, standing before it with Brammel's key burning a hole through my coat pocket like a coal that had opinions. Doors are things you open and close. This was a *seal*—a slab of dark stone set into the mountain's living root, and across its face moved lines of light like veins in a leaf, except the light was starlight, caged and dim and flickering the way a candle flickers when someone is crying behind it.

I could hear Wren through it.

Not her voice, exactly. The memory of her voice, worn down to a thread. She was singing something small—the noodle-seller's song from the night market, the one about the boy who trades his shadow for soup—and every few bars the melody would fray and restart, like a singer who keeps forgetting where she is.

Seven nights I'd had to prepare for this moment. Seven nights of street noodles and mended ledgers and sitting on Ovelia's step at dawn watching the city pretend nothing was wrong with its skyless dark. Seven nights of practicing what I would say to her, all of it lies or apologies, which in my experience amount to the same thing said with different posture.

I took out the key.

Brammel had forged it from his last true fire, and it did not look like a key. It looked like a splinter of dawn—a sliver of gold-white light that hummed against my palm and made the shadows lean away from me as if embarrassed. When I pressed it to the seal, the vein-light parted like a curtain drawn by a careful hand.

The vault opened onto a room that should not have existed inside a mountain: a long gallery, ceiling lost in dark, and along both walls, shelves. Not shelves of books. Shelves of glass—jars, bells, lanterns, cases—each one holding a soft glow. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Some burned steady. Some guttered. One, near the far end, flickered in time with a half-remembered song.

Wren stood—or stood the way an echo stands—in front of a glass case taller than she was. Her outline kept going slightly wrong at the edges, the way ink goes wrong in water. Inside the case, floating like a captive sunfish, was a knot of light the size of a heart. It pulsed. It reached toward her. She didn't seem to see it anymore, and that was the worst thing I had seen in seven nights, including the Under-King's smile.

"Wren," I said.

She turned. For a moment her eyes found me—really found me, the way they used to over the guild's ledgers when she'd catch me smiling at some absurd entry and refuse to let it go until I admitted the world was funny. Then the finding slipped.

"You're—" She frowned, sweet and lost. "You're the man who tastes things. You come to the market. You always stand where no one looks."

"That's me," I said, and my voice held, which I counted as the finest act of my career. "Quentin."

"Quentin." She tried it like a word in a foreign language. "Why does that taste like salt?"

Because your soul remembers what your mind has been sold out of, I wanted to say. Because names are the oldest seasoning there is. Instead I crossed the gallery, and the caged lights dimmed as I passed—not afraid of me, I realized. *Reaching.* Starlight, even imprisoned, remembered warmth and leaned toward any body that carried it.

I stopped in front of the case. Up close, Wren's soul was not abstract at all. It was warm. It smelled, impossibly, of the guild kitchen on bread day—of yeast and scorched honey and the particular smoke of Ovelia's temper. My eyes stung, and I decided the vault air was simply very dry.

"I'm here to buy you back," I told her.

"You can't afford me," she said, with the ghost of her old grin. "I'm expensive. I sing."

"I know," I said. "I've heard."

And then, because there was nothing left to do but the thing itself, I turned to face the dark at the end of the gallery, and I spoke the words I'd been carrying for seven nights like a stone under my tongue.

"I've come to settle the account."

* * *

He arrived the way cold arrives—not walking, just suddenly being everywhere at once. The Under-King wore his borrowed beauty again: a tall shape of courtly grace, a face like a portrait of a man rather than a man, a crown of frozen sparks. Behind him, out of the gallery's shadows, came others—nobles wearing faces I almost recognized, courtiers of the deep places, their borrowed features slipping now and then into something older and hungrier underneath.

"The taster," he said, and his voice was velvet over a well. "You return ahead of schedule. Debts delight me most when paid early."

"Then delight," I said, and drew out the contract.

It had been waiting in my coat since the third night, folded around Brammel's ember-key, because I am a ledger-keeper before I am anything else and I do not conduct business without paper. The Under-King's own scribe had drafted it, in ink mixed with grave-dust and patience. I unfolded it and read the terms aloud, because contracts only bind when spoken in the presence of both parties and at least one witness, and my witness was a girl who currently couldn't remember mine.

"'For consideration received,'" I read, "'the undersigned conveys to the Court Beneath his name entire—Quentin Eriksen—together with all claims, memories, and recognitions attaching thereto, in perpetuity, throughout the upper world and the lower.'"

The Under-King's portrait-face brightened by a degree. "You understand what you surrender. No one above will know you. Your mother's lullabies will have no son to sing them to. Your grave, when you earn one, will read *here lies*—and nothing after. You will be the space where a man used to be."

"I'm familiar with the merchandise," I said. "I've been selling samples of it my whole life."

"And the girl?"

"Released entire. Soul returned, unharmed, unspent. All songs she sold you voided and returned."

"All songs," he echoed softly, and I watched hunger move behind his beautiful borrowed face like a fish under ice. He wanted more than my name—I'd known that since the seventh night, when he'd shown me how beloved I'd become and how fast I'd be forgotten, when he'd offered me a position instead of a purchase, warden of this vault, invisible keeper of caged light. I had refused. He had smiled the way creditors smile. "Very well, taster. Sign."

I took up the pen. Grave-dust ink, cold as a promise. I set the nib to the line—

—and the vault door slammed open behind us with a sound like a ledger dropping from a great height.

* * *

Ovelia stood in the entrance, and I have seen Ovelia angry, and Ovelia frightened, and Ovelia confronting a client trying to return a used miracle, but I had never seen her like this. She looked like winter deciding to be personal about it. Her gray braid had come half loose. Her coat was torn at the shoulder. In her arms she carried the Gilded Spoon's master ledger, the oldest book in the guild, its cover scarred with two hundred years of impossible accounting, and she carried it the way soldiers carry flags.

Behind her, breathing hard in the doorway, stood Marta the kitchen maid, holding a candle stub like a weapon, and beside Marta—gods help me—was a cat. I never learned whose cat. It simply belonged to the scene, the way cats do.

"You're late," I said, because what else does one say.

"I'm *punctual*," Ovelia snapped. "There's a difference, and it's measured in lives. Quentin Eriksen, put down that pen."

"He can't," the Under-King said pleasantly. "The contract is struck. Witnessed. Binding."

"It is *not* binding," Ovelia said, "because the seller cannot convey clear title to goods encumbered by prior debt."

The gallery went quiet enough that I could hear the caged starlight humming. Even the Under-King's courtiers stopped pretending to breathe.

Ovelia opened the master ledger across her forearm with the flourish of a duelist, turned to a page near the front—paper gone amber, ink gone brown, handwriting I did not know—and read aloud:

"'Received into the Gilded Spoon, this ninth night of Deepwinter, from Edda and Tomas Eriksen, tasters to the crown: one favor, unlimited in kind, redeemable by themselves or their heirs unto the last generation, valued beyond price.'" She looked up. "Your parents, Quentin. They bought their bargain with the under-court the same way everyone does—with something precious. But they were guild members first, and they paid the guild a retainer. A debt owed *to* them. Unredeemed. Until now."

"I invoke it," I said. My voice came out strange. "I invoke my parents' favor."

"You don't have to ask," Ovelia said. "That's what makes it a favor and not a loan." She turned to the Under-King, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for clients who thought the guild's rules were suggestions. "By the oldest law of the Gilded Spoon, older than your treaty, older than your exile: no member's debt may be collected while a prior claim stands against the estate. The Spoon holds first lien on Quentin Eriksen. Your contract is void. Void as a bottle of storm. Void as a king with no kingdom."

The Under-King did not move for a long moment. When he did, it was slow, and the borrowed beauty began to slide off him like wax off a candle left too near a flame. Underneath the portrait-face was something with too many joints and a mouth that opened the wrong way, and it was still smiling.

"Clever," he said. "Clever little bookkeepers. But ledgers are only words, mistress, and words require *enforcement*. My court wrote your treaty. My court remembers when your guild was a stall in our market. Come. Give me the girl, the taster, and the old woman, and I will let the maid and the cat go free. That is generosity. That is *terms*."

The courtiers began to move. The caged lights flared—all of them at once, thousands of imprisoned stars pressing against their glass, and I understood suddenly that they weren't flaring in fear.

They were flaring in hope.

My hand went to my pocket, to the ember-key, Brammel's last fire, still warm, still waiting. Wren, beside me, had gone rigid—and then her hand found mine, and though her eyes were still fogged with sold memory, her fingers knew exactly what they were holding onto.

"Quentin," she whispered. "That's your name. Quentin. Why do I keep forgetting it? Why do I keep—"

"Hold onto it," I said. "Just for a minute longer. Hold onto it for me."

The Under-King raised one too-jointed hand, and every glass case in the gallery began, very softly, to sing—a sound like the sky being unplugged—and Ovelia stepped forward to stand at my shoulder, ledger open, pen drawn, ready to write the guild's next entry in whatever ink survival required.

"Now would be the moment," she murmured, "for whatever reckless thing you brought in that pocket."

I took out Brammel's key, and it blazed, and the dark leaned in to see.
