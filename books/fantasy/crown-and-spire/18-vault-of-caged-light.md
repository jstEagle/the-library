## 18. Vault of Caged Light

The key of flame weighed nothing and everything.

That's the trouble with true things, I've learned. They never weigh what they should. A spoonful of arsenic weighs less than a spoonful of sugar, and a key forged from a dragon's last ember weighs less than a thought — and yet my hand shook holding it the way it had never shaken holding poison, or ledgers, or the Under-King's contract with its ink that moved when I wasn't looking.

Brammel's fire didn't burn me. It burned *for* me, which is a different thing entirely, and one I intend to think about for the rest of a life I was no longer entirely sure I would have.

"Quentin." Ovelia's voice behind me, low and steady in the way she only got when she was terrified. She'd arrived like weather — all at once, with the smell of smoke and old paper, her copy of the guild's oldest ledger clutched to her chest like a shield or an apology. "The debt stands. The sale is void. Say the word and we walk out of here."

"The word," I said, "is no."

I could feel the vault before I saw it. Every soul in this mountain pulled at me faintly, the way a full pantry pulls at a hungry man standing in the street outside. And beneath that pull, threaded through it like a voice through a wall, came singing.

Wren's song.

Except it wasn't quite Wren anymore. It was Wren stretched thin, Wren as heard through water, Wren with the middle of her scooped out and replaced by something cold and borrowed. I had spent six years teaching myself not to care about people — dosing my heart the way I dosed my tongue against the king's dinner — and every lesson failed me at once, all together, like guards deserting a losing post.

The stair opened into a chamber that made my teeth ache.

I want to be fair about this. The under-court knew beauty the way a leech knows blood: intimately, and only as food. The vault was a sphere of black stone veined with silver, and set into its walls were cases — glass, or something pretending to be glass — and inside each case burned a soul. Not souls as the priests describe them, all wings and white light. Souls as they actually are: small stubborn fires, each one shaped like the life it had come from. A baker's soul smelled of morning bread. A soldier's flickered in drill formation. One near the door pulsed in slow, patient rhythm, and I understood without being told that it had belonged to someone who had spent decades rocking children to sleep.

And above them all, suspended on chains of shadow, hung the chandeliers of caged starlight — the harvest of generations, the reason the sky over the kingdom had gone dark as a closed eye.

Wren's case stood at the center.

She was smaller than I remembered, which was absurd, because I'd seen her three days ago and she hadn't shrunk since. But souls take up more room than bodies do, and hers was half-gone from her body, half-here and half-in-the-glass, so that the girl slumped against the case looked like a sketch of herself drawn by someone in a hurry. Her mouth was still moving. Still singing. Even hollowed out, even harvested, some stubborn spark in her kept singing to the dark, because that was the first thing she'd ever owned and the last thing she'd ever give up.

"Wren," I said.

Her eyes found mine. For a moment there was nothing in them — the polite blankness of the under-court's merchandise — and I felt something in my chest tear along a seam I hadn't known was there.

Then her brow furrowed. Just slightly. The way it did when she was doing sums.

"You're..." she said, and her voice came out rusted. "You're the one who salts things."

"I'm the one who *under*-salts things, thank you very much. There's a difference. It's a matter of professional pride."

A ghost of a smile crossed the sketch of her face. Behind me, I heard Ovelia make a sound she would deny making until her dying day.

I raised Brammel's key.

Here is what nobody tells you about opening a vault of stolen light: it opens like a confession. The key didn't turn so much as *admit* — the black stone recognized the fire that had once sealed the wards above, the fire that had been betrayed and banked and left guttering for centuries, and the whole vault shuddered like a thing caught in a lie.

The first case cracked with a sound like dawn.

Light came out the way grief comes out — all at once, after years of being held wrong. The baker's soul rose through the broken glass and went up, up through the ceiling of the world, home to whatever waits above the sky. Then the soldier's. Then the rocker-of-children's, pulsing its patient lullaby-rhythm all the way into the dark overhead. Case after case, crack after crack, and the chamber filled with light the way a drowned man's lungs fill with air, painfully, gloriously, too much and exactly enough.

Wren's case shattered last, because the vault wanted to keep her longest, and because Wren had always been worth keeping.

Her soul came back to her mid-song.

I mean that literally. She was singing — that thin, rusted thread of a melody — and the light poured back into her between one note and the next, so that the note she'd begun as a hollow girl she finished as a whole one, and the difference rang through the chamber like a bell struck in a cathedral. Color flooded her face. Her hands flexed. She looked down at herself, then up at me, and her eyes were *hers*, furious and bright and eleven-going-on-eternal.

"Quentin Eriksen," she said, "you are *late*."

"Traffic," I said. "Also I sold my name to a monster, so technically you'll have to introduce me to myself later."

"Oh good," said Ovelia behind me, "she's fine. She's absolutely fine. Wonderful."

And then the under-court arrived, and everything got worse.

They came through the walls, because doors are for people who believe in consent. The nobles in their borrowed faces — a duchess wearing a dead woman's smile, a general wearing a hero's jaw — crowded the edges of the blazing chamber, and I watched them do something I hadn't seen them do before.

I watched them *starve*.

It was there in the way they moved, the terrible grace gone jerky, the borrowed faces slipping like masks in wind. The freed light blazed around them and they couldn't touch it. Souls that had been theirs to sip from cases now flew free, and freedom, it turns out, has a taste the starving cannot bear. The Under-King stood among them, and he was no longer beautiful. He was a tall shape of hunger wearing a crown, and his court pressed in around him like beggars around a bakery window, and the whole vault hummed with their wanting.

"Thief," the Under-King said. His voice had lost its music. What remained was older and worse. "That light was bought. Paid for. Contracted across generations. You have robbed us of a *century of meals*."

"Funny," I said. "I was about to accuse you of the same thing. Except your victims signed nothing, and mine—" I held up the contract, the one with my invisibility written into it in moving ink, "—this is void. Guild law. Oldest entry on the books. Ask her; she'll show you the page."

Ovelia stepped forward and did something I will treasure forever: she held the ledger open toward the King Beneath the Mountain and read him a line item in the tone of a woman disputing a restaurant bill.

The Under-King ignored her, which was his mistake, though not yet his fatal one. His attention was on the light — on the flood of freed starlight still rising through the broken ceiling, streaming up toward the sky where the stars waited, dark and empty, to be refilled.

"There is enough there," he said softly, "to feed us for ten thousand years. Enough to drag the heavens down and wear them. And you stand in the middle of it, taster, with your little key and your little friends, and you have not yet taken a single drop for yourself."

He was right. That was the horrible part. He was right the way a knife is right.

Because here is what I hadn't said aloud, even to myself: the escape route ran through the light. The stair that isn't there opens only for the unremembered, and I had sold my unremembering, but the freed starlight would carry us — carry Wren, carry Ovelia, carry me — up through the mountain's bones and out, if we took enough of it with us. A handful each. A satchel's worth. Light enough to buy our passage past the court's grasping hands, and yes, light enough that the under-court would remain below, starving, in the dark we'd made darker.

Hoard, and live. That was the arithmetic. I had done arithmetic my whole life. It had never once asked me to bleed.

"Take it," the Under-King murmured, and his voice was almost kind, which is how you know a bargain is rotten. "You hoarded yourself for thirty years, taster. Hoarding is your native tongue. Take your handful and go, and let us keep what remains. Everyone profits. That is what your guild believes, isn't it? That everything has a price, and the price is always payable by someone else."

Wren's hand found mine. Small, warm, whole.

"You're doing the face," she whispered.

"What face?"

"The ledger face. The one where you're counting something and hating yourself for the total."

"It's a very professional face."

"Quentin." She squeezed my fingers. "You taught me that some things don't go in the ledger. You just don't know you taught me. You used to write off debts in other people's names when they couldn't pay. Little ones. You thought nobody noticed."

I had. Three coppers here, a memory-fee there, quietly absorbed into the guild's margins where even Ovelia's sharp eyes wouldn't catch them. I'd told myself it was bookkeeping hygiene.

It turned out I'd been leaving a trail of breadcrumbs my whole life, and a child had followed them to who I was.

The Under-King's court crept closer. The light rose. My hand hovered over the nearest stream of it, cupped, ready to grab our passage out — and I thought about my parents, two tasters who had made this same bargain in miniature, trading pieces of themselves for safety, for quiet, for the privilege of being overlooked. I thought about the alchemist's confession. I thought about how every generation of my family had done the math and chosen survival, and how the sky had gone dark anyway, because survival hoarded is just hunger wearing a different coat.

"Ovelia," I said. "How much does the guild owe my parents?"

"Everything," she said simply. "Unpaid. With interest."

"Then let's start paying it down."

I opened my hands and let the light pour through them.

Not grabbed. Not satcheled. *Given.* I turned my cupped palms outward and the stream of freed starlight broke over my fingers and kept going — up, out, away — and I fed it, deliberately, wastefully, magnificently, feeding every last current of it upward toward the empty sky. Wren laughed, a real laugh, the kind that makes candles lean. Ovelia swore in three dead languages. And the under-court *screamed*.

They lunged. Of course they lunged. Starving things seeing the meal pass over them forever — they came at us like a tide of beautiful, borrowed faces, and the light streamed past their grasping fingers and rose and rose, and the vault's broken ceiling opened onto the roots of the mountain, and beyond that—

Beyond that, the first star lit.

Far above, through a thousand feet of stone, I felt it happen rather than saw it: a single point of returned fire kindling in the dark socket of the sky. Then another. Then a constellation's worth, the freed souls of the vault pouring home into their ancient houses, and the light of them came down through the mountain like dawn through a keyhole.

But the Under-King was fast, and desperate, and desperate things get one good lunge in them.

He didn't reach for the starlight. He'd already lost that, and he knew it. Instead he turned — all his hunger narrowing to a point, a needle of want — toward the one flame left in the chamber that he could still seize.

Brammel's key. The last dragon-fire in the world, still warm in my hand, spent now, guttering, its work done — but a work done is not the same as a fire dead, and the last ember of the last dragon was still, even dying, the most precious thing in the dark.

"Give it to me," the Under-King said, and his court surged forward behind him, and the needle of his hunger came straight for my hand.

* * *

The strange thing about having your soul half-removed is that you remember in pieces.

I remembered Quentin in fragments, while I was in the case. A smell of ink. Someone humming off-key on purpose to make a child laugh. The particular way a person stands when they're trying to be furniture — shoulders in, chin down, *please don't see me* — and the way that same person forgot to do it whenever I sang.

When my soul came back mid-song, all the fragments came home too, clicking together like a finished puzzle, and the picture they made was so obvious I almost laughed. *Him. It was always him. The man who salted nothing and gave away everything.*

Now the vault was breaking open around us and the light was going home and Quentin — the man who called himself a taster the way a king might call himself a gardener — was standing in the middle of it with his hands open, giving away the only exit any of us had.

I've met brave people. The kitchen maid Marta is brave; she smuggled me a map under a tray of eels. Ovelia is brave the way a fortress is brave. But Quentin's bravery is a different species. It's the bravery of a man who has spent his whole life believing he doesn't matter, choosing, over and over, to spend the only currency he thinks he has.

"Quentin!" I shouted, because the Under-King was moving, and even newly-whole I could feel the shape of his hunger the way you feel a storm in your teeth. "The key! He wants the key!"

"I *know*, Wren!"

"Well, *do something*! You're the one with the plans! You plan everything! You planned how to sneak me extra pudding for two years without anyone noticing!"

"That was one plan, and it was flawless—"

"*QUENTIN.*"

The Under-King reached for the last ember of Brammel's fire, and the whole dark of the world leaned in to watch.
