## 19. The Last Dragon-Fire

Here is a thing they don't tell you about miracles: they smell like burnt sugar.

The vault of caged light had opened the way Brammel's key promised—one true thing, turned once—and now the starlight was everywhere, pouring out of its glass prisons like water from a dropped pitcher. It didn't fall so much as *remember falling*, streaming upward through the vault's high dark and out through whatever cracks the mountain had been keeping secret all these centuries. Wren stood in the middle of it with her soul back where it belonged, singing, because of course she was. The girl could not be handed a miracle without turning it into an encore.

I should have been watching her. I was watching the Under-King instead.

He had not moved. That was the worst part. When the light went free he simply stood at the head of his long black table, hands folded, face tilted up toward the escaping radiance the way a starving man tilts toward the smell of bread—not with hope, exactly. With arithmetic. I had spent my life around hungry courts, and I knew that look. It was the look of a man recalculating what was left on the table.

And what was left, I realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the under-court's chill, was Brammel's flame.

It sat in my breast pocket where I'd carried it since the dragon pressed it into my hands—a coal no bigger than a walnut, warm as a sleeping bird, heavy as a sin. The last fire of the last dragon. Fire enough to have relit the border wards, if wards were what we'd chosen to spend it on. Fire enough to burn for a hundred years in the right hands.

Or fire enough to feed a starving court, if a starving court got there first.

"Quentin." Ovelia's voice came from somewhere behind me, tight as a ledger line. She still held the old book open to the page with my parents' debt written in faded ink, as if the accounting itself might hold the Under-King at bay. Maybe it already had. "Quentin, the light is going. We need to go with it."

"In a moment."

"Quentin—"

"I said *in a moment*, Ovelia." I never raised my voice. Raising your voice is how invisible men get noticed, and I had spent thirty years perfecting the art of being furniture. But this time the words came out loud enough to echo off the vault's glass cases, and every borrowed face in the under-court turned toward me, and I found—to my considerable surprise—that I did not care.

The Under-King smiled. Even diminished, even with his chandeliers emptying above him like taverns at closing time, he smiled the way creditors smile: patiently, certain the debt will come due.

"You hold something of mine," he said.

"I hold nothing of yours." I took the coal out of my pocket. It pulsed against my palm, faintly offended at the damp air of the place. "This belongs to a dragon who gave it freely, which is more than can be said for anything you've ever owned."

"It is the last warmth in the world below," he said softly. "You cannot mean to waste it on the sky."

"Waste it?" I laughed, and the laugh came out strange, cracked down the middle. "Your Majesty, I am a food-taster. Waste is the one thing I have never once been permitted. Do you know what my whole life has been? A study in small portions. One sip here. One bite there. Never enough of anything to be poisoned by, never enough of anything to be full. I hoarded myself the way you hoard starlight—in little sealed jars, for fear someone would take it." I looked at the coal. It looked back, in the way that very old fires do. "Brammel told me something before he gave me this. He said every bargain he ever witnessed came down to one question: what are you willing to spend? And everyone always spent someone else."

"Then spend yourself," the Under-King said. "That is the trade I offered. Your name. Your selfhood. An eternity of useful invisibility. It stands. It *still* stands, taster. Give me the flame and keep your anonymity, your safety, your careful little life—"

"No."

"—your obscurity, your unremarkable face, your freedom from being *known*—"

"I said no." My voice did something I'd never heard it do. It shook. Not from fear. From the effort of holding the rest of what I wanted to say behind my teeth until I could say it properly. "You misunderstand the transaction entirely. I'm not selling. I'm not trading. I'm not bargaining, bartering, or booking it into any ledger anywhere. For the first time in my life, Your Majesty, I am giving something away for nothing at all, and I would appreciate it if you stopped interrupting while I enjoy it."

Behind me, Wren's song caught—just for a beat—and then rose, and I swear the freed starlight rose with it.

* * *

Let me explain what I understood in that moment, because I want it on the record, in proper ledger-hand, dated and witnessed.

Starlight is not fuel. That was the under-court's great error, the error of every court that has ever mistaken treasure for sustenance. You can cage starlight. You can bottle it, sell it, spend it on chandeliers and borrowed faces and the slow drag of heaven down a mountainside. But it does not nourish you, because starlight was never meant to be eaten. It was meant to be *given*. Every star in the sky is a thing burning itself outward without condition or invoice, and that—only that—is why looking up has always made people feel less alone.

The under-court had been trying to drink the sea for ten thousand years.

But dragon-fire—dragon-fire was made to be given too. Brammel had told me that much, guttering in his cave like a lamp left on for someone who might still come home. *Fire wants nothing,* he'd said. *That's the tragedy of us. We only know how to give warmth, and the world keeps asking for weapons.*

So I did the arithmetic the Under-King could not. I had a coal that wanted to give. I had a sky-full of starlight pouring past me, homeless, bound for a heaven that had forgotten it. And I had, thanks to a lifetime of careful practice, absolutely no instinct whatsoever for keeping things.

"Ovelia," I said. "Check my math."

She came to stand beside me. She looked at the coal. She looked at the light. I watched thirty years of guild discipline war across her face—the discipline that says *never give, only trade; never burn, only bank*—and I watched it lose.

"The rate of exchange," she said finally, dry as dust, "is catastrophic."

"Catastrophic," I agreed.

"There is no precedent in the ledgers."

"Noted for the record."

"If you do this, the guild's books will never balance again."

"Good," said Wren, arriving at my other side mid-verse, because the girl could not bear to let anyone else have the last word, even musically. "Balanced books are boring."

And so the three of us stood together in the vault beneath the mountain—an exile bookkeeper, a disgraced guild mistress, and a soul freshly returned to its body—and we committed the single most unprofitable act in the history of commerce.

I opened my hand and fed the last dragon-fire to the stars.

* * *

I wish I could tell you it exploded. It would make a better story. There should have been thunder, some grand detonation, the mountain shaking on its roots. Instead what happened was quieter and infinitely worse for the Under-King: the coal lifted off my palm like a spark off a campfire, drifted up into the stream of freed starlight, and *caught*.

Fire met light the way a voice meets a song it knows.

The flame spread through the starlight not like burning but like memory—here a constellation rekindling, there a whole river of heaven relighting, and I felt rather than saw the sky above the mountain flare back into existence, star after star after star, the ones the court above had sold their memories of, the ones Wren had sung to in the dark, the ones whose names nobody living remembered except a dragon who had just spent his last breath making sure somebody would. The light came down through the vault like a benediction and went out through the mountain like a rumor, and somewhere far above I knew the border wards were snapping back into place with a sound like a harp string being retuned—whole, this time, and singing.

And the under-court's stolen splendor began to collapse.

It went gently, which surprised me. The chandeliers dimmed first, their caged remnants drawn upward into the general resurrection like rain returning to a cloud. Then the borrowed faces. The nobles of the Court Beneath wore faces they had bought or stolen over millennia, and as the true light touched them, each face peeled away—not cruelly, the way a bandage comes off clean when the wound has finally healed—and underneath there was nothing but pale, smooth, ancient hunger. They did not scream. They looked almost relieved, the way exhausted people look when someone finally tells them they can stop pretending.

The palace of glass and shadow softened around its edges. Columns became mist. The long black table thinned to a suggestion. Ten thousand years of accumulated magnificence, and it all came apart like sugar in warm water, because it had never been built—it had only been *held*, clutched, kept, and keeping is not the same as having.

The Under-King shrank with it.

I watched him diminish, and I will be honest with you, since honesty is apparently the theme of my retirement: part of me wanted him to suffer. Part of me—the part that had tasted poison for a paranoid king, the part that had found a receipt for Wren's soul in its own handwriting—wanted him ground down to ash and scattered. He had taken children's souls. He had taken my parents' names, though I hadn't known it yet. He had taken the stars themselves and called it survival.

But what was left of him, when the last of the splendor drained away, was too small to hate.

He stood—or rather, he persisted—in a puddle of fading glamour, a cold little thing no bigger than a child, grey as a snuffed wick, with two eyes that had once reflected constellations and now reflected nothing at all. The hunger was still there. That was the horror of it. Everything else had been stripped away—the crown, the court, the beauty, the terror—and the hunger remained, entire, undiminished, a hunger with nothing left to be hungry *for*, because the thing it had starved for all these ages had just been given away to the whole sky at once, free of charge, no receipt required.

He looked up at me. Small. Cold. Starving.

"You could have ruled beside me," he whispered.

"I couldn't have," I said, and I meant it kindly, which somehow made it worse. "Ruling requires being seen."

He flinched. Then he turned, and the last of the under-court's dark folded around him like a coat, and he walked away into the deep places of the world—a small cold hungry thing, going to find some new darkness to wait in. I did not follow. Some hungers you cannot fill. You can only refuse to feed them.

Wren's hand found mine. Her grip was warm and slightly sticky, because she had apparently eaten a pastry at some point during her own soul-liberation, and I decided in that moment that it was the finest hand I had ever held.

"Quentin," she said. "Look up."

Above us, through the vault's broken ceiling, through the mountain's secret throat, the night sky was *full*. More than full. It was lit the way I imagined it must have been lit before any court sold anything to anybody—dense with stars, reckless with them, spilling over, a treasury that had finally learned to open its doors.

"They're brighter than the stories," Wren whispered.

"They're brighter than the stories," Ovelia agreed, "because the stories were written by people who'd only seen the discount version." She closed the old ledger with a snap. "Someone will have to write new ones."

"Someone," said Wren, with the particular sweetness she reserved for schemes, "could pay well for new stories. If a person had a guild. With new laws. About what may and may not be traded."

"I am not reopening the Gilded Spoon in a collapsed cavern beneath a mountain, Wren."

"You could call it the Gilded Spoon *and Star*."

"I will end your apprenticeship before it begins."

They bickered their way toward the stair, and I followed, and the freed light followed me, settling into my clothes, my hair, the creases of my palms, the way woodsmoke follows you home from a bonfire. I would carry it for years, people told me later. On dark nights, they said, you could find Quentin Eriksen by the faint gold at his collar, like a man forever walking out of a room where something wonderful had just happened.

Except, of course, that wasn't quite my name anymore. I had signed it away. The ink on that contract had burned with everything else, but magic keeps its books better than Ovelia ever did, and a name given to the dark stays given.

I climbed the stair that wasn't there, nameless, luminous, lighter than I had ever been in my life.

And when we came out onto the mountainside, into the cold honest wind, into a night ablaze with returned stars—I heard, drifting up from the city below, the first church bell of dawn, and beneath it, fainter, stranger, the sound of thousands of people opening their doors and stepping outside to look up at a sky they didn't remember losing, and finding it more beautiful than anything they'd ever been told to expect.

Ovelia stopped at the mouth of the cave. She looked at the stars. She looked at me—at the nameless man who had cost her a treaty, a fortune in caged inventory, and, judging by her expression, approximately ten years of composure.

"Well," she said. "The ledger's going to need rewriting."

"Every page," I agreed.

"Starting," she said, pulling out her pen, "with yours."
