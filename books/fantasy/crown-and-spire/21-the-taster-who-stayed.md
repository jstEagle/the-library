## 21. The Taster Who Stayed

The strange thing about being known, I discovered, is how loud the mornings become.

For eleven years I had moved through the palace like a draft under a door. Cooks nodded through me. Guards looked at a point slightly to my left and found nothing worth arresting there. The king himself, paranoid enough to taste-test his own shadow, had never once been able to hold my face in his memory longer than it took me to swallow his soup. That was the gift—or the curse, depending on which of us you asked, and nobody had ever asked me anything, so the question remained theoretical.

Now the scullery maid Marta greeted me by name before the sun had fully cleared the kitchen wall.

"Morning, Master Eriksen," she said, and beamed, and handed me a heel of bread still warm from the oven, unasked.

I stood in the doorway holding it like an artifact from a dead civilization.

"You don't have to do that," I said.

"I know." She was already turning back to her kettles. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Ovelia told us all about it. The guild mistress herself came down here, if you can believe that, in those boots. Said there's a new law at the Gilded Spoon now: nothing with a soul gets traded, and anyone who feeds a taster without asking first has committed no crime at all."

"That's not—" I began.

"It's on a plaque," Marta said. "She had it made. Brass."

I ate the bread. It was very good. I want to report that some part of me recoiled from warmth freely given, that the old instincts flared up and warned me away from attachment as they had for decades. They did flare. But they sounded, I noticed, fainter than before. Like a bell rung in another room. Like a habit whose owner had died and left the house to someone who actually lived there.

The palace had changed overnight, or rather, over the week since the stars came back. People remembered them now—remembered losing them, remembered the feast where the sky went black, remembered Wren's song echoing out of nowhere the moment the light returned. The king's chroniclers were working themselves into a froth trying to decide whether the Starless Night had been a punishment, a miracle, or an administrative error. The alchemist's tower had been emptied of its previous occupant, along with most of his equipment and, I'm told, his dignity. His replacement was a sharp-eyed woman named Serah who had once worked the border wards and who looked at everyone, including the king, as though calculating their half-life.

Serah saw me in the corridor outside the royal apartments and stopped.

"Taster," she said.

"Eriksen," I said. "Quentin Eriksen."

"I know." She held up a small glass vial, amber liquid catching the morning light. "Your king's tonic. New recipe. Willow-bark, honey, a pinch of iron for the blood. No starlight, no forgetting, nothing that isn't exactly what it says on the label. I thought you'd want to test it yourself before he drinks it."

"You could just tell me what's in it."

"I could." She pressed the vial into my hand. "But you're the one who caught the last one, aren't you? Ovelia says you tasted something wrong in a tonic that three separate assayists certified clean."

"It was more of a hunch with a aftertaste."

"A hunch with an aftertaste saved the court's memory of the sky." She shrugged. "Around here, we've decided to trust your tongue. It's a strange feeling, I imagine. Being trusted."

It was. It was like wearing a coat tailored to a man I'd only seen in mirrors, and the fit was close but not exact, and every seam reminded me it was there.

* * *

King Aldous received me at midmorning, as he always did, in the small dining room with the barred windows he'd insisted on after the Night. He was older than his portraits, thinner than his reputation, and he watched me cross the room with the flat, patient attention of a man who has survived four assassination attempts and one apocalypse and has opinions about both.

"You're the taster," he said.

"Yes, Majesty."

"They told me your name this time." He frowned, as though the information had arrived in damaged packaging. "Quentin. Eriksen. Odd. I feel as though I should have known it years ago. You've been at my table a long while, haven't you?"

"Eleven years, Majesty."

"Eleven years." He said it the way other men said *eleven stab wounds*. "And in all that time, I never once wondered about you. Isn't that remarkable? A man standing between me and death daily, and he might as well have been the furniture. My new alchemist tells me that wasn't entirely your doing."

"No, Majesty."

"And that you gave up whatever it was." He gestured vaguely, a king's gesture encompassing magic, mystery, and the general inconvenience of other people's souls. "Gave it up to save a child. And the stars. In that order, I'm told, though the order is disputed."

"The order is disputed," I agreed.

He studied me for a long moment. Kings are not good at studying people—they're good at weighing them, which is different—but Aldous had survived by learning the difference. Whatever he weighed me against, apparently I balanced.

"Well," he said. "Sit down, then. If you're going to die for my lunch, the least I can do is learn your name first."

So I sat. At the king's table. In a chair. With a name.

* * *

The soup arrived at noon: a white bean and smoked ham affair, the kind of honest food that survives even royal kitchens because no cook has the heart to ruin it. I took the tasting spoon. Old ritual, older than the guild, older probably than the crown: one swallow, wait, watch the king watch me, and if I didn't turn colors or start composing my own elegy, he ate.

I swallowed. Waited. Felt nothing but bean.

And then—and this is the part I keep returning to, the part that feels like the actual ending of a story that technically has one more chapter—I reached for the salt.

Understand what this meant. A taster does not season the king's food. A taster tastes; he does not *improve*; improvement implies opinion, opinion implies investment, investment implies a self with preferences, and a taster's entire craft is the erasure of self. For eleven years I had eaten like a lock being tested. I did not eat like a man.

I picked up the salt cellar—the good one, silver, shaped like a little fortressed tower because everything in this palace is a metaphor if you squint—and I shook a modest pinch into the bowl, and I stirred, and I tasted again.

Better. Objectively better. The ham sang. The beans lost their bureaucratic blandness. Someone in the kitchen, I would later learn, had been under-seasoning the king's food for years out of terror, and the whole court had been eating like penitents as a result.

"What are you doing?" the king asked.

Not *what are you doing to my food*, mind you. Just: *what are you doing*. Curious. Almost gentle.

"Improving your soup, Majesty."

"You're allowed to do that?"

"I am now." I set down the spoon. "New policy."

He looked at me for a long moment, and then King Aldous, breaker of sieges, survivor of the Starless Night, did something I had never seen him do in eleven years of service.

He laughed.

"Salt," he said, shaking his head. "Eleven years, and the revolution turns out to be salt." He picked up his own spoon. "Very well, Quentin Eriksen. From now on, season my food. Heaven knows the court could use it. Metaphorically," he added, with the caution of a man who has recently learned that metaphors in his palace sometimes come true.

* * *

Wren found me in the guildhall that evening. Not the old guildhall—the Gilded Spoon had moved, post-reckoning, into a narrow building near the river with good light and bad stairs, because Ovelia had declared that any organization built on impossible things should at least occupy plausible premises.

She came up the stairs two at a time, fifteen years old and lit from inside like a paper lantern, and she dropped onto the bench across from me without asking, which is a thing family does.

"You salted the king's soup," she said. "Marta told Serah, Serah told the laundress, the laundress told literally everyone. By sundown they'll have songs about it."

"They will not."

"'The Taster's Pinch,'" Wren said, ignoring me, eyes shining. "I could write it. My voice makes candles lean, you know. I could make the chandeliers weep."

"Wren."

"Verse one: *In the hall of the paranoid king, a humble servant dared—*"

"*Wren.*"

She grinned and stole a piece of the cheese I'd been saving, which is also a thing family does. "You're famous," she said, chewing. "You're *known*. Do you understand how funny that is? The man whose whole magic was being forgotten, and now the palace can't stop talking about him. Ovelia says the universe has a sense of humor. She says it bitterly, but she says it."

"How is she?"

"Downstairs, terrorizing the apprentices. We have three now. Actual paying apprentices. Learning to price things honestly." She rolled her eyes with tremendous affection. "Yesterday Ovelia spent an hour explaining why bottled laughter is exploitative, and then sold a jar of it anyway, to a widow, at cost, because the widow's husband used to laugh like that and she just wanted to hear it once more. Ovelia cried. She'll deny it. She's already denied it twice."

"That tracks."

Wren's grin softened into something smaller and truer. She turned the cheese rind over in her fingers.

"Hey," she said. "Quentin."

Nobody had called me that before the stars came back. Now it was everywhere, worn smooth already, like a coin passed through too many hands. It still startled me a little each time. I suspected it always would. I suspected I wanted it to.

"Yeah?"

"Thank you. For the vault. For the—" She gestured at herself, at all of herself, the soul-shaped entirety of her sitting there stealing my cheese. "I remember pieces. Of down there. The case, and the dark, and the song I couldn't finish. And I remember that when the light came, it came because you opened your hand instead of closing it. I don't remember everything. But I remember that."

"You'd have done the same."

"No," she said cheerfully. "I'd have sung at it until it surrendered, which is a different strategy and frankly less expensive." She leaned across the table and bumped her shoulder against mine, deliberately, the way you'd ring a bell to prove it still sounds. "Family doesn't keep score, though. That's the whole point of it. Ovelia explained it to me. There's a ledger entry and everything."

"There's a ledger entry?"

"Line item: *family, miscellaneous, no charge.*"

I laughed—out loud, in a room with other people in it, a thing I had spent eleven years training out of myself—and the sound surprised us both, and Wren looked so triumphant about it that I resolved to laugh less often, purely in self-defense.

* * *

Later, after she'd gone, I climbed to the spire. Old habit, or new one—I'd started going up there in the weeks after the Night, when sleep proved reluctant and the ceiling felt dishonestly low. The city spread below, lamplit and ordinary, and above it, the sky did the thing it had done every night since: burned.

All of it. Every star. The astronomers were beside themselves; several had resigned on principle, unable to cope with a sky that now exceeded their catalogs. The wards along the border hummed steady as a held breath, fed on nothing but their own old strength and Brammel's sacrifice, and the mountain where the under-court had been sat quiet, cold, and small—a hunger with nothing left to eat but itself.

I stood at the parapet with my elbows on the stone, and I let myself feel it, the way Ovelia kept telling me to, in the tone of a woman prescribing medicine: *feel it, Quentin, it's good for you, I don't care if it stings.*

I had given up my name to buy a child's soul. I had given up my gift—my beautiful, terrible talent for being overlooked—to free a sky. And in exchange I had received, by any rational accounting, a catastrophic loss of privacy: a maid who fed me, an alchemist who trusted me, a king who knew my name, a dragon-shrine tended by strangers who left flowers and honest questions, a girl who called me family in front of witnesses, a guildmistress who had written my name back into her books by hand because no spell would do it.

By any rational accounting, I had traded everything for nothing, and come out ahead.

The wind off the river smelled like bread from the night ovens and, faintly, impossibly, like the sea—someone in the city, I'd heard, had finally traded back a memory of the sea, returned it to the guild, unpaid and unasked, saying the world had enough wonders in it now without hoarding. Ovelia had entered it in the ledger under *gifts, inbound, unprecedented.*

Below me, a lamp flickered in a window I knew. Ovelia's office, late as always. Through the glass, tiny with distance, I could see her bent over the great ledger, writing. Keeping accounts. Believing, stubbornly, in the arithmetic of decent things.

Tomorrow I would taste the king's breakfast, and I would salt it, and he would complain about the portions and mean nothing by it, and Marta would feed me again, and Wren would compose verses, and Ovelia would deny crying, and the stars would burn, and I would be seen—thoroughly, inconveniently, permanently seen—by everyone whose seeing mattered.

I found, testing the thought the way I once tested tonics, that it had no bitterness at all.

Just salt.

Just enough.
