## 22. Crown and Spire

A year ago I learned that stars are not distant things. They are close things. Hungry-close. They lean down when you sing the way candles used to lean toward me before I knew what my voice could do, and if you sing to them long enough they will tell you secrets in a language made of pressure and old light.

Tonight's secret was a small one: *the third star from the spire's tip is showing off.*

I laughed out loud on the guild roof, alone except for a jar of bottled laughter I was supposed to be cataloguing, and the star flared like it had heard me. Maybe it had. Since the night Quentin fed the last dragon-fire into the freed starlight and the sky came back blazing, the stars have been vain as peacocks. Who could blame them? They'd been caged in chandeliers beneath a mountain for longer than anyone could remember, eaten bite by bite by a court of exiled keepers who had forgotten they were keepers at all.

I had been one of their meals. Half of one, anyway. There is a scar where my soul was pried loose, and it aches on cold nights, and on warm ones too if I'm honest, but it also means I can hear them better than anyone else alive. Ovelia says that makes me valuable. Quentin says it makes me *Wren*, which is his way of saying the same thing while pretending he isn't being sentimental.

Quentin. Right. Let's do this properly, because there is a rule about how you say his name now, and I'm the one who made the rule, so I should follow it.

You don't say it. Not because it's forbidden — the Under-King took his name in the signing, and no ledger, no law, no amount of Ovelia's furious ink has ever called it back — but because saying *Quentin* around him makes him go quiet in a particular way, the way a person goes quiet when they're listening for something they've been told they lost. So we call him other things. The Taster. Old Salt-Lips, which is mine and he hates it. The Man Who Added Salt, which is the kitchen maid Marta's, because she was there the first time he seasoned the king's soup instead of merely surviving it, and she swears the whole palace changed key.

But in my head, where nobody can audit me, he is still Quentin. Names are like souls. You can sell one and still keep the other.

* * *

The Gilded Spoon reopened eleven months ago under new law, and the new law is painted on the door in letters tall enough that even drunk clients read them twice: **NOTHING WITH A SOUL MAY BE TRADED.**

Ovelia wrote it herself. Well — she dictated it to me, then made me write it three times because my first attempt was "too pretty." Guild law should look like guild law, she said. Ugly, legible, and slightly threatening. The third version had a blot on the *soul*, and she declared it perfect.

Business has been strange since. We still trade impossible things — bottled laughter, winters on credit, second chances at first loves — but every contract now comes with a question Ovelia asks personally, leaning across her desk with her spectacles down her nose: *"Does it have a soul?"* And clients, who came in expecting to haggle over a memory of the sea or a luck-charm, find themselves actually thinking about it. Some of them cry. One man sold us his grief instead, which is allowed, apparently, because grief is what's left over after love and love stays home. Ovelia priced it fairly and then quietly gave it back to him at closing time, and if you tell her I told you, I will deny it with the full weight of the guild behind me.

I am apprenticed now. Officially. There's a paper and everything, though my handwriting on it is still "too pretty," and my duties include catalogue work, client interviews, and — this is the part that matters — singing.

Every clear night, I climb somewhere high and I sing to the relit stars. It started as maintenance. Brammel's fire had gone into them, dragon-flame braided through starlight, and dragon-fire needs tending the way a hearth does; a song keeps the braid tight. But somewhere over the winter it stopped being maintenance and became conversation. The stars remember being kept. That was the terrible secret of the Court Beneath: they hadn't been monsters originally, just wardens, exiled and starving, who forgot the difference between keeping a light and eating it. The stars remember all of it — the exile, the hunger, the fall. When I sing, sometimes I sing *for* them, the way you'd sit with someone grieving, and sometimes I sing *at* them, the way you'd scold a child who won't share, and both work equally well.

The city doesn't remember losing them. That's the strangest part. The court above sold its memory of the stars generations ago to buy peace, and when the sky came back, people woke to it the way you wake to furniture: it had always been there, obviously, why would anyone make a fuss. Only a handful of us know there was ever a hole in the world. Sometimes I want to grab strangers in the market and shout about it. Mostly I've learned that remembering is its own quiet job, and somebody has to do it without shouting.

That's the job, really. That's what the guild is now. We remember things so they can't be unmade again.

* * *

Brammel's cave is a shrine now, which would have appalled him so thoroughly that I suspect he'd have secretly loved it.

Strangers tend it. That's the part that gets me every time. Nobody remembers the dragon — his fire went out entirely, spent on forging the key that opened the soul-vault, one true thing made from an old world's last light — and yet people come anyway, drawn by something they can't name. A shepherd leaves wool. A miner leaves a lamp he no longer needs. Marta leaves bread, because Marta believes every shrine runs better with bread, and honestly she might be right. There's a stone at the cave mouth where visitors scratch wishes, and the wishes have worn a groove into it, a long smooth line like a signature.

I go once a month. I bring nothing, because Brammel and I had an arrangement in life that didn't involve offerings — mostly him telling me I sang like a kettle with ambitions, and me telling him he smelled like a banked oven, and both of us meaning *I'm glad you're here*. At the mouth of the cave I sing the song he hated most, the one about the miller's daughter and the moon, and the mountain hums it back in a register no living throat could manage, and I choose to believe that's him. Ovelia says it's resonance in the stone. Ovelia says a lot of things in the exact tone of a woman protecting herself from hope.

Inside, the shrine-keepers have hung lanterns. Real ones, candle-lit, dozens of them, climbing the walls toward the dark hollow where a dragon used to gutter like a dying lamp. He spent two hundred years watching bargains come up the mountain path, weighing every one, and in the end he made the only free gift of his existence and it cost him everything. The lanterns say what the city can't remember to say: *we noticed.* Somebody noticed.

I lit one for him last month. Then I lit a second one and didn't tell anyone whose it was.

Some things you don't put in ledgers.

* * *

Which brings me, circling like a moth that's given up pretending, to the Taster.

He lives in the little room off the kitchens still, because he says the king's paranoia requires proximity and because — though he'd deny this under torture, and he'd know, he's been tortured adjacent his whole career — because it's near us. Near Marta, who saves him the end piece of the bread. Near me, when I'm not on a roof or in the vaults. Near the guild, a ten-minute walk he makes every evening at dusk, punctual as tide, ostensibly to review accounts and actually to sit in Ovelia's office arguing about interest rates on bottled courage until she throws an inkwell at him, which is their love language, and everyone knows it, including them, especially them.

Here is what the Under-King took, and here is what he couldn't:

He took the name. Every record of it — guild rolls, palace registries, the baptism book in whatever parish claims him, the memories written in ink and magic — gone, blanked, smoothed over like a word scratched off wet paper. Ovelia tried for months. She bought back names from three different brokers, tested each against him like keys in a lock, and none turned. Finally she did the thing that defines her better than any treaty-burning ever did: she sat down at her desk, opened the master ledger to a fresh page, and wrote his name by hand. *Quentin Eriksen. Restored by act of guild, this date.* It has no power. Magic slides off it. It's just ink.

It hangs framed behind her desk. Clients ask about it sometimes. She tells them it's a debt record. Which is true, if you understand what kind.

Because here's what the Under-King couldn't take: Marta remembers him saving her from the alchemist's creature, remembers it in her bones even if the details have gone soft. Ovelia remembers expelling him and remembers that it was the worst thing she ever did right. The king — paranoid, poisoned-slow King Aldous — remembers that his food-taster is the only honest man in the palace, and cannot say why he knows it, and trusts him completely anyway, which may be the single most miraculous event in the history of that court. And I remember everything. My soul was half-removed once; it came back with the seams showing, and seams hold memories the way cloth holds stitches. I remember a man dosing himself against poison and against attachment, failing at the second one every single day, buying my songs with sternness and my safety with his name.

The Under-King wanted a warden no one could remember, invisible even in absence. He got exactly that. He just never asked what people do with a shape left behind. They fill it in. Stubbornly, inaccurately, lovingly, they fill it in.

* * *

Tonight we eat on the spire.

This was Wren's idea, and yes, I'm allowed to refer to myself in the third person when the idea was good enough. The spire above the palace — the old watch-spire, unused since the wards fell and were rebuilt on dragon-fire and song — has a flat crown of stone with a view of the whole city and most of the sky, and I decided a year of endings deserved one beginning that happened up where the light gets in.

Marta cooked, which means there is enough food for eight people and there are four of us. Ovelia brought wine she officially requisitioned as "inventory" and unofficially stole from her own cellar. Quentin brought salt.

He brings salt everywhere now. It started as a joke the week after — Marta handed him the dish at the king's table and said, *go on then, make history* — and it has become a talisman he pretends is a habit. A small clay pot of salt, worn smooth at the edges from handling. When he seasons food, he seasons it deliberately, visibly, a small public act of having opinions about things. A man who spent his whole life making himself unnoticeable, now announcing himself to a bowl of soup. If you don't understand why that wrecks me a little, you haven't been paying attention, and I've been paying attention for two chapters' worth of years.

We spread the meal on a cloak. Roast fowl, bread with the good crust, pickled things, a honey-cake Marta absolutely did not make for the king first and divert en route. Below us the city glitters, real glitter, lamplight and window-light, and above us — well.

Above us, the third star from the spire's tip is showing off.

"Look at that," Ovelia says, pointing with the wine bottle because glasses are for people with patience. "Vain as a broker."

"It heard you," I say. "They hear everything now. You should be careful what you say about interest rates."

"I say nothing about interest rates that isn't defensible in guild court."

"You said the moon was 'overleveraged.'"

"The moon," Ovelia says with dignity, "is carrying more reflected debt than any celestial body should."

Quentin laughs. It's a real laugh, unguarded, the kind he rationed like poison antidote for years and now spends freely, and the sound goes up past us and the stars lean in — I feel it, the faint pressure-shift, the sky tilting its ear — and I think: *they're learning his laugh. Filing it away. Somewhere in the braid of fire and light there is now a permanent record of the way he laughs on a spire in autumn,* and no Under-King can sign that away, because it was never written anywhere it could be erased.

He catches me staring. "What?"

"Nothing," I say. "Old Salt-Lips."

"Wren."

"Taster."

"*Wren.*"

"Fine. Quentin," I say, quietly, just for me, just to keep the muscle strong, and he goes soft-eyed for exactly one heartbeat before covering it with the honey-cake, and I let him.

Marta raises her cup. "To the shrine-keepers," she says, because she always toasts the forgotten ones first; it's her liturgy. "May their bread stay fresh."

"To Brammel," Ovelia says, "who never once honored a contract and was therefore the only honest party in every deal he made."

"To the stars," I say, "may they never learn modesty."

And Quentin lifts his cup last, looks around at us — the maid, the mistress, the apprentice, the found and finding family of it — and says, "To the ledger," which startles all of us until he adds, "the one page it can't hold," and drinks.

Below, the city hums. Above, the sky leans close. And on the flat crown of a spire above a palace, four people share a meal under stars that remember being saved, and I think about crowns — real ones, the heavy gold kind sitting on a paranoid king's head two floors beneath our feet — and I think: wrong metal entirely. Wrong shape. A crown is whoever passes you the bread. A crown is a name written by hand in ugly ink because magic wouldn't carry it. A crown is a lantern lit in a cold cave by a stranger who'll never know why it felt necessary.

The wind picks up. The stars flare, gossiping among themselves, and I catch the edge of what they're saying, because I always catch the edge now, and it stops me mid-chew.

They're saying something is coming up the mountain road. Something old, walking slowly, patient as interest. Something that smells — the stars say, in their language of pressure and light — like the space where a star used to be.

I lower my cup. Quentin sees my face change, because he always sees, because noticing people is the one magic he never sold.

"What is it?" he asks.

I look north, toward the mountain, toward the dark road winding up through the pass, and somewhere far below us, deep under stone, I'd swear I hear a very small, very cold thing begin to sing.
