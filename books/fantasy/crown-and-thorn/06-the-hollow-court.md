## 6. The Hollow Court

*From the private report of Serah Vane, Knight-Captain (stripped of rank), to the Crown Prosecutor's Office, filed under seal, never answered.*

---

I was summoned to the capital on the fourth day of the month of Reaping. I record the date because the date matters. Everything in this report matters, and no one will read it, and I am writing it anyway, because a knight who keeps silent becomes the thing she serves.

The summons came through channels that should not have known I existed. My name was struck from the rolls eleven years ago, after Thornhollow. Anyone who wished to find me would have had to dig — and someone dug. The letter bore the king's own seal, pressed so hard into the wax that it tore clean through.

I went. I want that recorded too. A disgraced knight does not refuse the crown twice.

* * *

Aldenholt in autumn smelled the way I remembered: woodsmoke and horse dung and, beneath both, something sweeter that I could not name until much later. Incense from the temple quarter. The Order of the Quiet Flame had always kept a presence near the palace — debtors' offices, mostly, rows of clerks counting what people owed down to the candle-hour. I passed their windows on the way to the citadel. Behind the glass, scribes bent over ledgers like rowers at oars.

One window was dark. I remember thinking nothing of it.

At the citadel gate they did not ask my name. They looked at a slate, then at me, then waved me through as though I were expected freight. The guards stood oddly still. Not relaxed-still. Held-still, the way a dog holds when it has been told to wait and is not certain why.

The throne room doors opened without heralds.

I had seen King Aldric once before, eleven years ago, from three hundred feet away, at his coronation. He had been thirty then, broad-shouldered, famous already for pardoning a whole village's debts after the flood year. They called him Aldric the Generous behind his back, which for a king is the highest compliment there is.

The man on the throne was not that man.

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left. He was not ill. He was not changed by grief or age or the long weight of ruling. Those things hollow a face gradually, and you can trace the lines of them. This was different. This was a face with the lines erased. His skin sat smooth over his skull like fresh paint over old wood, and his eyes —

His eyes were open. That is where I must start, because everything else follows from it.

He never blinked. Not once. I stood in that throne room for the better part of an hour, close enough to see the grain of the lacquer on his crown, and his eyelids never came down. Not to blink, not to narrow, not to soften. Eyes are supposed to move. They flick toward sound. They drop when a person thinks. His did none of these things. They pointed forward the way a lantern points, and whatever light was in them was not coming from behind.

"Serah Vane." His voice was his voice. I recognized it from proclamations read aloud in market squares. But it came out level and unbroken, every word the same length, like coins counted out one by one. "You refused an order once."

"I did," I said.

"You will not refuse this one."

Behind him, chamberlains and clerks arranged themselves along the walls, and I realized with a slow cold turn of the stomach that none of them were looking at the king. They looked at their hands, at the floor, at the middle distance. Men who spend their lives near a throne learn to watch the throne. These men watched nothing.

A clerk approached with a scroll and held it out to the king. The clerk's hands shook. The king took the scroll and did not look at it — did not need to, I understood later; he had written it himself weeks ago, or something wearing him had — and he broke the seal.

"The realm carries a sickness of accounts," he said. "Debt is rot. Rot is cut out."

He signed. I watched his hand. It moved smoothly, confidently, the signature of a man signing his own name ten thousand times — except that he paused before the final letter, just barely, the way a hand pauses when the arm holding it belongs to someone else learning the shape of it.

Then he handed the scroll back and looked at me — pointed his eyes at me — and said:

"Read it aloud."

I read it aloud. I will reproduce its substance here, stripped of the legal language, because the legal language exists to hide it. Effective at solstice: all persons carried on the ledgers of the Order of the Quiet Flame in default beyond ninety days are declared forfeit. Forfeit means forfeit of life. Their debts die with them. Their names are struck from every ledger in the realm, and the accounts are closed.

There were — the clerk whispered the number to the king before the audience began, and I heard it — four hundred and six thousand souls in default across the nine provinces.

Four hundred and six thousand. Scribes and farmers and ferry-men and widows. People who owed a winter's worth of candle-hours for a father's medicine, a roof mended after a storm, seed-grain borrowed back from the temple that had taken their harvest in the first place.

And among them — I did not know her name yet, then. I know it now. Zephyrine Dunmore. A temple scribe of the Thornfell chapter, debtor of some fourteen years, entered in the rolls as reliable, punctual, *valuable*. I have since seen the ledger page. Someone had annotated it in the margin, in a hand I now recognize as belonging to High Cantor Mavelin herself: *retain if possible.*

Retain if possible. While the rest of the four hundred thousand were marked for the axe.

I asked the king — I will write exactly what I asked, because I have thought about it every day since — "Your Majesty, what crime have these people committed?"

And the king smiled.

It was a correct smile. Every muscle engaged in the right order, the corners lifting to precisely the height a smile lifts. It reached his eyes the way light reaches the surface of a well — stopping there, going no deeper.

"They owe," he said. "Owing is enough."

I have served soldiers who loved killing, and I know what appetite looks like when it speaks. It is eager. It leans forward. It makes excuses. Whatever wore the king's face did none of those things. It stated the fact of our owing the way a stomach states hunger: not cruelly, not kindly, simply as the truth of its nature. And I understood, standing on cold marble with a death warrant in my hands, that I was not looking at tyranny.

Tyrants want. Tyrants fear rivals, hoard gold, sleep badly. This — this only *fed*, patiently, everywhere at once, through ledgers and clerks and candle-hours, through a system so old and so ordinary that no one thought to call it a mouth.

Somewhere in the provinces, a scribe was copying out her own death warrant in fair hand, and the hand holding the pen belonged, in every way that mattered, to the thing on that throne.

* * *

They gave me quarters in the citadel and told me I would ride within the week. Captain's commission restored, retroactive pardon pending — pending, always pending, the carrot ground fine — in exchange for one task. There were fugitives in the Thornfell passes. A scribe. A child. I was to bring them back alive. Only alive. The emphasis was repeated three times, by three different officials, in three different rooms, and each time the same odd care in the wording, as if *alive* were the difficult part, the precious part, the part worth a knight's pardon.

I did not ask why. Asking questions in that place felt like tapping the ice over deep water.

That night I could not sleep, and near the second bell I gave up and walked the corridors, as I used to when the nightmares from Thornhollow got loud. The citadel at night was wrong in ways I could not immediately count. Then I counted.

No dogs. A garrison that size keeps hounds, and hounds bark at night, and I remembered the barking from my last visit eleven years ago. Now silence. As if even animals had decided, in the wordless way animals decide, that this place was a throat and not a home.

No laughter. Servants moved through the halls at a walk so uniform it looked rehearsed. I passed two of them scrubbing a floor that was already clean, over and over, the brush making small circles, their faces empty as unbaked clay.

And the candles. Every corridor burned candles in brackets every ten paces, hundreds of them, and not one guttered. Air moves in old buildings. Flames dance. These burned straight as nails, all of them, all night, as if the building itself were holding its breath.

Near dawn I found myself at the doors of the throne room again. I do not know why I went. I tell myself I was confirming a suspicion. The truth is that some part of me — the part that refused orders at Thornhollow, the part I have spent eleven years trying to drown — wanted to see it again, alone, without ceremony, to prove I had imagined it.

The doors were unlocked. They swung at my touch without a sound.

He was there. At that hour, with no court, no clerks, no one. Sitting on the throne in full regalia in the dark, lit only by those nail-straight candles, eyes open, pointing forward into nothing.

"Serah Vane," he said, without turning his head. He could not have heard my boots; I walk silently; it is the first thing they teach you and the last thing you keep. "You did not sleep."

"No, Your Majesty."

"You dreamt of fire." A pause of exact length. "Everyone dreams of fire now."

I said nothing. My hand had gone to my sword without my permission, and I made myself take it away, because I suddenly understood that drawing steel in that room would be like striking a match inside a granary.

"What happened at Thornhollow?" the king asked. Eleven years, and no record, no inquiry, no mention — and now this thing asked me directly, in the dark, in that counted-out voice.

"I refused an order," I said. "My commander ordered a village burned with the people inside. Debt-refusers. I refused, and my own men—" I stopped.

"Say it."

"My own men obeyed. I survived by running."

The thing on the throne considered this. I use the word loosely. It was more like watching a mill wheel decide whether a log will pass.

"And now," it said, "you hunt debtors again. The Order is pleased. The Order is always pleased when the hungry are gathered in." Its head turned — the first movement it had made that was not required of it — and the lantern-eyes fixed on me, and for one moment, one flickering moment, I saw something else underneath. Something small and drowning. A man far down in a dark water, palms pressed flat against the underside of the surface, mouth open in a scream that made no sound because sound does not carry through glass.

*Help me,* the eyes said, or I dreamed they said it, and I have not slept properly since.

Then the surface closed over, smooth as paint, and the smile returned, correct to the last degree.

"Ride soon," the king said. "The children of the old tongue are ripening. Bring me the small one. Bring me the scribe. Bring them breathing."

I bowed. I walked backward to the doors, because turning my back on that throne felt like a mistake I would not live to correct, and in the doorway I looked back one last time.

He was still smiling. And in the stillness I finally named the sweet smell beneath the woodsmoke of Aldenholt, the one I had been unable to place since I rode through the gates.

It was the smell of a greenhouse. Warm earth and growing things and sap — the smell of a garden kept in the dark, tended by something patient, something that had learned that the sweetest fruit takes years on the vine.

The realm was not being ruled. It was being *ripened*.

* * *

I rode out through the south gate at first light, carrying sealed orders I had no intention of honoring in the manner intended, and I set down this report at the first waystation because if I do not write it now I never will, and because somewhere in the mountains there is a scribe and a child who believe the men hunting them are merely men.

They are not. Nothing in Aldenholt is merely anything anymore.

Let whoever finds this understand: the decree of the month of Reaping is not law. It is feeding. Four hundred and six thousand names, and the king does not blink, and the candles do not waver, and the Order of the Quiet Flame counts its candle-hours with the tenderness of a farmer counting days to harvest.

I am riding to Thornfell to find them. Whether I bring them back alive depends entirely on what I find when I get there — and whether what I find lets me leave at all.

If I do not send a second report, burn this one. And then run.

— S.V.
