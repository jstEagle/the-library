## 7. Thorn and Root

*What follows is Zephyrine's rendering. The child's tongue has no written form that survives — or rather, its written form is the speaking of it, ink and voice being the same act in a language older than kingdoms. I have set down what Wren showed me in the firelight over three nights, translating as faithfully as I am able. Where the old words have no honest equivalent in ours, I have chosen the closest wound.*

*— Z.D., marginal note, smuggler's atlas of Thornfell, folio 41*

* * *

Wren did not dream the way children dream.

There were no monsters in the dark corners of sleep, because for Wren there had never been a dark corner that was not already occupied by something with a name older than fear. What came instead was memory — not Wren's own, at first, but memory the way rain belongs to a river: gathered drop by drop from a thousand years upstream and carried forward whether the river wished it or not.

In the morning after the third night on the smuggler's road, Wren woke with frost in their hair and a word in their mouth that made the nearest pine tree shudder though there was no wind.

Zephyrine was awake instantly. They had learned to wake that way — the scribe had told Wren this, later, in the careful trade-language they built between them out of gesture and charcoal sketches and patience. A person who owes money learns to sleep like a deer. Every sound might be a creditor.

But it was only the tree, shivering off its frost, and Wren sitting up small and solemn in the hollow of a fallen log, saying the word again.

Zephyrine wrote it down. Zephyrine always wrote it down. That was the first thing Wren had understood about the tall one with ink-stained fingers who had opened the door on the night of the storm: this one *keeps*. Everything given to Zephyrine was kept — every word, every name, every grief spoken aloud into a cottage too small for griefs that size. Wren had been sent to many keepers in the long walking. Most of them had died or fled or sold what they were given. This one kept.

So Wren said the word again, slower, and pointed at the tree, and then — because some things cannot be pointed at — began to show.

* * *

Here is what the showing was:

A valley. Not Thornfell as it is now, all scree and switchback and hidden passes, but green to the summits, terraced with gardens that climbed like staircases into the clouds. Rivers ran through it in channels cut so precisely they looked drawn rather than dug, and where the rivers met, there were stones — standing stones, thousands of them, each one carved with words that moved.

That was the thing Zephyrine underlined three times when they translated it back to Wren, tapping the charcoal sketch, asking with raised eyebrows: *the words moved?*

Yes. In the first garden, language had never been still. To write a word was to plant it, and planted words grew. A binding spoken over stone would live as long as the stone lived. A promise spoken over a child would hold until the child broke it or was broken by it. The people of the valley — Wren's people, though Wren had never seen the valley except from inside this memory that was not theirs — were called in their own tongue something that translates most honestly as *the woven*, because they understood that everything alive is threads, and that a people who can speak to thread can mend what tears.

They mended rivers. They mended quarrels. They mended, once, with terrible cost, something that should never have been touched at all.

Wren could not show Zephyrine the Thorn directly. Not yet. When Wren tried, the memory went white at the edges, like paper held too close to flame, and Wren's nose bled, thin and bright, two drops on the frost. Zephyrine bound the nose with a strip of clean linen and did not ask again that day. But that night, by the fire, Zephyrine laid out the pages of transcription — weeks of work now, whole folios of the old tongue rendered phonetically into trade-script — and asked a different question. Asked it carefully, the way you approach a wounded animal.

*Where are the others?*

And Wren, who had been carrying the answer since the night of the storm, finally set it down.

* * *

The first kings came out of the east with iron and a new kind of word.

Not woven words. *Cut* words. Words meant to end things rather than grow them. The woven had met conquerors before; the mountains had swallowed three armies whole in the centuries before memory, their stones turning against any force that came in malice. But these easterners had learned. They came slowly. They married in. They traded. And above all they listened, and wrote down, and waited — because they had noticed the one truth about the woven that no army had ever exploited:

Their power lived in agreement. A binding held because both the speaker and the bound consented to the shape of it. Even the great bindings — the river-mendings, the mountain-wakings — required the mountain's leave. The woven had spent so many centuries being trusted that they had forgotten how to be feared.

The first king — Aldric's ancestor, eleven generations of crown between them, though the crown itself was far, far older than either line — did not attack the valley. He asked for help.

His kingdom was starving. His winters were cruel. Would the woven teach his scribes a little of the weaving? Just enough to coax grain from stubborn soil?

And the woven, who mended quarrels, said yes.

For thirty years the two peoples worked side by side. Eastern scribes learned the grammar of growing things. Woven teachers learned, without noticing, the eastern habit of writing things down in books that could be stolen. The bindings deepened. The harvests swelled. And beneath the temple the easterners built — as thanks, they said — a vault, and in the vault they assembled, piece by piece, purchased page by purchased page, a complete copy of the woven tongue.

Every binding in it. Including the ones no teacher had ever offered to share. The ones the woven kept only in memory, passed mouth to mouth, because some words are too dangerous to plant.

The vault was the theft. But the theft was only the beginning.

* * *

Here Wren's memory turned a corner even Zephyrine's steady hand hesitated at. The charcoal stopped. For a long moment the fire cracked and spat, and Serah Vane, sitting watch at the mouth of the hollow with her sword across her knees, glanced back at the two of them bent over the pages and said nothing, because she had learned by then that whatever passed between scribe and child was not hers to interrupt — though she listened, Wren noticed. She always listened. She pretended not to, and her pretending was terrible.

What happened next, Wren showed in fragments, because whole was unbearable:

The first king took the throne of his fathers to the vault, and with him the finest of his borrowed scribes, and together they spoke the greatest binding ever stolen.

They did not invent the Thorn. Nothing so old can be invented. The Thorn was simply *there* — the hunger at the root of things, the appetite that eats forests from the inside, that wears rot like a coat. The woven had known of it forever and had one law concerning it, the oldest law: *do not feed it a shape.* Hunger without form starves. Hunger given a crown becomes a king of hunger.

The easterners fed it a shape.

They bound it — with the woven's own grammar, spoken backward, corrupted, cut — into an object small enough to steal and heavy enough to sink a nation. A circlet. A crown. And then they set it on a head, because a binding needs an anchor, and what anchor is more stable than a throne, handed down intact from wearer to wearer, generation to generation?

The first host lasted nine years before the hunger wore him to lace. The second lasted four. By the fifth, the kings had learned what the Order of the Quiet Flame was quietly founded to manage: that the Thorn must be *fed* to be contained. Fed justice, fed judgment, fed the slow drip of other people's suffering — and above all fed debt, because debt was the perfect food. Debt made the hungry accept their hunger. Debt made chains feel like charity.

And the weavers? The ones whose grammar made the cage?

Zephyrine wrote the translation of this part very slowly, and Wren watched the ink-stained hand begin to shake, and felt something twist in the own small chest — not Wren's grief, exactly. Older than Wren. But living in Wren now, rent-free, the way grief does when it has nowhere else left to go.

The first king needed the woven one last time. A binding that strong needed living thread to anchor it — blood of the blood that spoke the tongue. So he invited the elders of the valley to the capital for a feast of thanksgiving, and he sealed the doors, and what happened in that hall became the reason the old tongue has no word for *forgive* but fourteen words for *ash*.

Almost all of them died. The valley was burned. The standing stones were pulled down and ground into mortar for eastern walls — and this, Wren showed Zephyrine with a flat, cold clarity that made the scribe look away, was deliberate, because mortar remembers. Every wall in the old quarter of the capital stands on ground-up binding-words. The city itself is a mute hostage.

But almost is not all.

One grandmother escaped into the high passes with an infant. One line survived, hidden in Thornfell, keeping the true tongue alive in secret — teaching it to one child per generation, no more, because a language spread widely is a language that can be found. For eleven generations the hidden weavers dwindled: married out, died young, gave up the burden. Each generation taught the next a little less, until the tongue lived in a single throat at a time.

Wren's mother's throat. Then Wren's.

The hunters found them in the autumn. Wren showed this last part only as sound and color and running — no faces, faces were too much — the burning house, the mother pressing a bundle into small arms and speaking the sending-word, the one that plants a purpose in a child and grows toward whoever the child finds. *Find a keeper,* the word said, in the mother's voice, in the storm, in the dark. *Find one who keeps.*

Wren finished, and the fire burned low, and Zephyrine sat motionless for so long that Wren wondered if the translation had broken the scribe somehow, the way using the old tongue too hard could break a throat.

Then Zephyrine picked up the charcoal and wrote one sentence at the bottom of the folio and turned it around to face Wren, because by now they had enough shared script for this:

*You are the key. They didn't send you to hide. They sent you to finish it.*

Wren nodded. Yes. That was the purpose planted under the skin, growing all this time toward this fireside, this keeper. The binding on the king — on every king, the whole rotten chain of them — was woven in the true tongue, and only the true tongue could unweave it. Wren was the last thread. The last person alive who could speak the Thorn loose from the crown.

It would cost everything. Wren's mother had known that too. The sending-word carried the knowledge the way a seed carries the tree: *unbinding requires a vessel. The Thorn does not die. It moves.*

But there would be time to tell the keeper that part later. There had to be. Some purposes need a while to grow before they can be harvested.

* * *

Serah Vane chose that moment to swear softly, which was unusual; she mostly saved her swearing for fords and weather.

She had come back from her watch without either of them hearing her — a knight's trick — and she stood looking down at the folios spread across the bedroll, at the sketches of standing stones and burning valleys, at the last line in Zephyrine's hand. Her jaw worked. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough as bark.

"The Order knows the old tongue exists. Mavelin called it heresy of the first age — *first age*, Zeph. You don't call a dead language by its era unless you've studied its era." She crouched, tapping the sketch of the vault. "Which means the Quiet Flame isn't ignorant. It's *complicit*. It's been managing the feeding for — how long did you say?"

Zephyrine held up fingers. Eleven reigns. Three hundred years.

"Three hundred years of debtor ledgers," Serah said slowly. "Three hundred years of the temple taking people's lives as collateral and shipping the desperate wherever the crown points. I always thought it was greed." She looked at Wren, and her gray eyes were not unkind, which from Serah Vane was its own kind of earthquake. "It's husbandry."

Outside the hollow, far below in the black folds of the mountain, a horn sounded. Once. Then again — and the second horn answered the first from a different direction, which meant coordination, which meant more than one hunting party, which meant the Order's enforcers or the Thorn's hunters or, worst of all, both, working the same pass from opposite ends.

Wren felt the old words rise in their chest unbidden, ready, hungry to be used — and remembered what every use cost, and pressed them back down.

Zephyrine was already gathering the folios, moving fast, hands sure. But before snuffing the fire, the scribe paused, looked at Wren across the embers, and wrote one final line in the dirt with a bare finger, quick and fierce:

*You're not cargo. You're not even just the key. From tonight you're mine to keep, and I keep what's mine.*

Then the light went out, and the horns came closer, and the three of them moved into the dark of Thornfell with the last thread of the woven tucked between them like a coal that must not go out — while behind them, patient as winter, the hunters closed the pass.
