## 8. Debt Collectors

The cache was where every smuggler's map said it would be and no honest road ever went: a seam in the mountain's flank above the frozen falls at Hollowmere, hidden behind a curtain of icicles that had been growing over the door for thirty years. Zephyrine had drawn this entrance themselves, four years ago, in exchange for a reduction of eleven candle-hours. They remembered the exact figure because it had felt like theft — theirs or the Order's, they'd never decided.

Now they stood before their own ink made real, chipping ice with a boot heel while Wren watched the treeline and Serah watched Wren.

"Inside," Zephyrine said. "Quickly. The cold's been guarding it better than any lock, but once we open it—"

"Once you open it," Serah said, "anyone following us can smell woodsmoke for a mile."

Zephyrine paused. "You've been to one of these before."

"I've burned three of them." Serah said it flatly, the way she said everything that cost her something. "Order caches. Smuggler caches. There isn't a difference worth the words."

Wren tugged Zephyrine's sleeve and signed something quick — *cold, small one cold* — and that settled the argument the way Wren's hands always settled arguments: by making the abstract suddenly flesh. Zephyrine struck the last shell of ice away and shouldered the door inward.

The cache was a smuggler's whole philosophy built in timber and stone. A low room cut into the mountain's root, a stove vented through a crack that breathed but didn't glow, blankets waxed against damp, dried meat hanging in strips like pale flags. And on the far wall, shelved behind oilcloth, the thing that made Zephyrine's chest tighten with an old, complicated shame: maps. Dozens of them. Thornfell rendered in their own careful hand, every pass, every ford, every hidden stair — sold to the Order so the Order could hunt people exactly like the ones now sheltering behind them.

"You drew all of these?" Serah had followed their gaze.

"I drew all of these."

"And now we're hiding inside your own snare."

"That," Zephyrine said, kneeling at the stove with numb fingers and a striker, "is either irony or justice. I've stopped trying to tell them apart."

* * *

They were two hours warm when the horns came.

Not the Order's horns — Zephyrine knew those, low and double-toned, a sound like a ledger closing. These were higher, thinner, wrong, a note that seemed to bend in the middle as if the horn itself were breathing. Wren heard it first. The child went rigid mid-bite, dried meat falling from their hand, and made a sound Zephyrine had never heard from them: not a word, not a cry, but a click deep in the throat, like a lock testing its own mechanism.

*Hunters,* Wren signed. Then again, harder, fingers stabbing the air. *Thorn-hunters. Not men.*

"They're men," Serah said, already moving, pulling her sword from the oilcloth where they'd wrapped it. "I've seen them. Men who used to be something else." She glanced at Zephyrine, and for once there was no armor in her expression at all. "After Hollowmere. After I let you go. They came through the ford an hour behind you, and they weren't tracking footprints, Dunmore. They were tracking *you*. Like blood in water."

"How many?"

"Four came through the pass behind me. I killed none of them. I want that understood — I ran, and they let me run, because I think they wanted me to lead them somewhere." Her jaw tightened. "Somewhere like here."

The second horn sounded closer. Below the falls. Zephyrine looked at the single door, at the vent-crack too narrow for a child, at the wall of their own maps, and did the arithmetic they were best at: candle-hours, escape routes, debts. It didn't balance. It never balanced. That was the whole design.

"There's a back way," they said slowly. "Older than the cache. The smugglers who built this found a fissure that goes up through the mountain's spine — my maps show it, but I never walked it. Nobody walks it. It's a chimney of stone for forty feet and then a crawl."

"Forty feet up a stone chimney with a child, pursued by things that used to be men." Serah tested her blade's edge with her thumb. "Fine. I've had worse Tuesdays."

Wren pulled at Zephyrine's sleeve again. Signed something long, urgent, and Zephyrine caught only fragments — *they smell the words, the words on you, the ink* — before the child gave up on signs and pressed both palms flat against Zephyrine's chest, over the satchel where the transcriptions lived.

"The pages," Zephyrine translated aloud, cold spreading through them that had nothing to do with the mountain. "They're tracking the transcriptions. Not me. Not Wren. The *writing*."

"Then burn them," Serah said.

"I can't—" Zephyrine heard their own voice crack and hated it. "Serah, these are the only living record of a language older than every kingdom. This is the binding. If the binding can be undone — if Aldric can be *saved* — it's in these pages. Burning them is burning him."

"Then carry them and run faster."

The third horn was close enough now that Zephyrine could hear the wet undertone beneath it, the sound of breath being drawn through something that wasn't quite a throat. Through the vent-crack, the faint smell arrived: sap and rot, pine pitch gone sweet with decay. The smell of the Thorn's garden in high summer, the smell from Wren's nightmares, which Zephyrine knew because lately they'd been sharing them.

"Up," Zephyrine said. "Now. Wren first, then you, then me with the satchel. I'll drop the lamp and kill the light."

Serah boosted Wren to the fissure's mouth. The child climbed like the mountain owed them money — hand, knee, hand, flowing upward through stone that should have been impossible, and Zephyrine thought, not for the first time, that whatever Wren's people had woven into the world, some of it had been woven into Wren's bones.

Boots hit snow outside. Not four sets. Six.

"Go," Serah hissed, shoving Zephyrine toward the chimney. "I'm right behind you."

Zephyrine got three feet up the stone before understanding arrived, cold and complete. They looked down. Serah stood alone at the fissure's mouth, sword drawn, lamp still lit, deliberately the brightest thing in the room.

"No," Zephyrine said. "No — Vane, get *up here*—"

"Someone holds the door," Serah said, "or nobody gets out of it. Climb, scribe. That's an order."

"You don't have the rank to order me."

A smile crossed her face, quick and crooked and gone. "No. I really don't."

Then the door burst inward, and there was no more time for arguing, because the hunters came through it.

* * *

Zephyrine climbed. It was the hardest thing they had ever done, and they had done hard things — they had copied ledgers by the thousand-hour, lied to a High Cantor's face, carried a mute child across a frozen kingdom. But climbing away from the sound of Serah Vane fighting was a different category of pain entirely, and their body shook so badly on the stone that twice they nearly fell.

Below them: steel. Shouting. Then sounds that were neither. The wet click of Wren's fear-sound, echoed from above, where the child clung to the chimney's throat listening to the same battle. And beneath it all, threading up through the stone like smoke through a floorboard, the old language — Wren whispering it, over and over, a word Zephyrine hadn't yet decoded but whose shape they were beginning to know. A word that sounded like *hold*.

The fight lasted ninety seconds. Zephyrine counted, because counting was what they did instead of screaming.

Then silence, and Serah's voice, ragged: "Climb! Both of you, *climb*, there are more coming around the falls—"

Zephyrine climbed. At the top of the chimney the stone opened into a crawl-space that smelled of clean ice and nothing dead, and they hauled themselves through it dragging the satchel, and came out into starlight on a ledge above the frozen falls with Wren pressed against them, shaking, and no knight.

"We go back down," Zephyrine said.

"She told us—"

"I don't care what she told us." Zephyrine was already turning for the chimney. "She held the door for us. You don't leave that unpaid. I have spent my entire life watching debts get collected, Wren. I am *not* going to become someone who leaves one standing."

Wren looked at them for a long moment. Then the child did something extraordinary: they took Zephyrine's hand, turned it palm-up, and traced a character in it — slow, deliberate, the old script written in skin. Zephyrine didn't know the word. But they felt its shape, and the shape felt like *together*, and they decided to believe that was what it meant.

They went back down.

* * *

They found Serah at the cache's threshold, backed against the doorframe, bleeding from a gash along her scalp and holding her sword in her left hand because her right hung wrong at the shoulder. Three hunters lay around her. Two were dead in ways Zephyrine's eyes kept sliding off of, the way eyes slide off a page of bad handwriting. The third was pinned under her knee and still moving, still clicking, and its face —

Its face had been a face. Under the sap-black veins and the bark-ridged skin, under the thornwork lacing its jaw shut, there had been a person. A tattoo on its throat: the Quiet Flame's sigil, the little coiled wick.

"It's a temple enforcer," Serah said. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not. "It was a temple enforcer. I trained beside two of its squad at Vell's Crossing. I *know* its face." She pressed her blade harder. "Dunmore. Tell me what I'm looking at."

Zephyrine crouched, keeping just out of reach, and looked — truly looked — at the thing wearing a brother of the Order. Up close, the thornwork wasn't decoration. It was infrastructure. Veins of black sap ran from its throat down into its chest and vanished, and where they vanished the skin bulged slightly, rhythmically, like something feeding.

"It's grafted," Zephyrine said quietly. "The way the king is grafted. Smaller. Cheaper. The Thorn grows hunters the way the Order grows debtors — it takes people who are already owned and puts them to work." They met Serah's eyes. "This is what the debt is *for*, Serah. This is where the candle-hours go."

The pinned hunter clicked. And then, horribly, it spoke — around the thorns, through them, a voice like sap dripping onto hot iron:

"*Ssscribe.* The Cantor offers amnesty. Return the vessel. Return the *pages*. Your debt is forgiven. Your debt is *forgiven*." The words repeated, looping, a phrase worn smooth by use. "Your debt is forgiven. Your debt is—"

"Shut up," Serah said.

"—forgiven. Forgiven. For—"

Serah killed it. Cleanly, professionally, without hatred — Zephyrine saw that, and understood what it cost, because mercy executed well is still execution. She stayed kneeling afterward, sword tip on stone, breathing.

"My orders," she said finally, "were to retrieve you alive and the child however convenient. Those orders came through the capital chain. Which means they came through the crown. Which means—" She laughed, short and awful. "Which means I've been hunting for the Thorn since before I knew the Thorn existed. Every fugitive I dragged back. Every debtor. I was a *collector*."

"Serah—"

"Don't." She stood, swaying, caught herself on the doorframe. "Don't absolve me, Dunmore. I'm not asking for it. I'm telling you what I am, so you can decide what you're carrying down this mountain." She looked at Wren, who had come silently down the chimney and stood at its mouth, watching her with those old, old eyes. "I burned my name off the rolls at Hollowmere when I let you pass. Tonight I burned the rest. There's no garrison that will have me, no Order that won't hang me, no road home. All I have is a sword, a bad shoulder, and—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "And wherever you two are going."

Zephyrine should have said something wise. Something about trust, or forgiveness, or the difference between a debt and a bond. What they actually said was:

"Your shoulder's dislocated. Sit down before you fall down."

And Serah Vane — disgraced knight, oath-breaker, collector of debts — sat down on the floor of a smuggler's cache among the dead, and let a debtor's hands set her shoulder, and made a sound through her teeth that might have been pain and might have been relief, and did not pull away when Zephyrine's hands stayed a moment longer than splinting required.

* * *

They left before dawn, taking nothing from the cache but food, blankets, and one map — Zephyrine's own, the oldest one, the one with the fissure route marked in a younger hand and annotated, in faded ink: *never walked; possibly myth; possibly death.*

"Cheerful," Serah said, reading over their shoulder as they climbed past the falls into the high passes.

"I was twenty-two when I wrote that. I was very committed to accuracy."

Above the tree line the world simplified to white rock and black sky, and the wind took everything except the essentials: move, breathe, keep the child warm. They walked in a rhythm Zephyrine had learned in the weeks since the ford — Serah on point despite her arm, Wren tucked between them in the lee of her cloak, Zephyrine navigating by stars and memory. Twice the thin horns sounded, far below and behind, hunting the empty cache. Once Wren stopped, gripped Zephyrine's hand, and wrote a character against their palm: the same one as before. *Together.* Or whatever it was. Zephyrine was starting to suspect it meant something larger, and that they'd spend the rest of their life learning how large.

At midmorning they crested a shoulder of the mountain and looked back, and Zephyrine saw it: smoke rising from the cache's vent-crack, thin and deliberate. The Order, or the Thorn's people, or both — it hardly mattered anymore, if Mavelin's enforcers wore grafts — burning what they couldn't use. Somewhere in that smoke were Zephyrine's maps. Twenty years of careful, complicit, beautiful work, curling to ash.

They expected grief. What came instead was lighter. Like setting down a ledger.

"Goodbye," Zephyrine murmured.

"What?" Serah said.

"Nothing. Keep walking."

But Wren looked up at them, and smiled — the first true smile since the child had arrived mud-caked at a cottage door — and signed one-handed against Zephyrine's arm, quick and certain: *new pages now.*

"Yes," Zephyrine said. "New pages now."

They walked north, into the white heart of Thornfell, three fugitives with one sword, one satchel of impossible language, and no debt left but each other. Behind them the smoke thinned and vanished. Ahead, half-buried in the pass, Zephyrine could just make out stones that were too regular to be natural — a line of them, marching up the ridge like teeth.

Wren saw them too. The child stopped walking.

"Wren?" Serah said. "What is it?"

Wren's hands moved slowly, reluctantly, spelling out the old script in the air between them, and Zephyrine — decoding as they went, heart dropping syllable by syllable — translated:

*"That is where my people died. And the mountain remembers their names."*
