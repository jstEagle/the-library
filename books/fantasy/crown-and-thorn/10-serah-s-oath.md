## 10. Serah's Oath

The fire had burned down to coals by the time Serah took the watch, and she was grateful for it. Darkness made lying to herself easier.

Zephyrine slept curled against Wren on the far side of the pit, the scribe's cloak thrown over them both, one hand resting — even in sleep, even now — near the child's shoulder, as if a hand could be a wall. The scribe's face had gone slack and young in the firelight. Unarmed. That was the word that kept coming back to Serah all night, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: *unarmed.* Zephyrine Dunmore, wanted by an Order and a crown, carrying nothing sharper than a stylus.

Serah had killed men for less than what Zephyrine carried in their head.

She turned her cup of snowmelt in her hands and let the memory come, because it always came when she stopped moving, and she had learned years ago that fighting it only meant dreaming it instead.

* * *

Nine years ago. The village of Harrowmere, which no longer existed.

She had been twenty-two, newly sworn, still wearing her oath like armor that fit. Her commander — Captain-Justiciar Edran Voss, who had taught her swordwork and scripture in equal measure and never once confused the two — rode at the head of twelve knights into a valley where the Crown claimed rebels were harbored.

Serah knew before they crested the ridge that there were no rebels. She could count cooksmoke. She could see children driving geese along a stone wall. Twelve knights against a village of farmers, and the writ in Voss's gauntlet said *cleanse*, and the seal on it was fresh, and the seal was the king's.

"Captain," she had said, riding up beside him. "There are children down there."

"There are always children," Voss said. His voice was gentle. It had always been gentle; that was the horror of him, that he did this work gently. "That is not the question I trained you to ask."

"What is the question?"

He looked at her then, and his eyes were tired in a way she would not understand until much later, until she wore the same tiredness herself. "The question is whether you believe the seal or your own eyes. Choose now, Serah. It is the last easy choice you will ever make."

She chose her eyes. She said so. She said it loudly enough for eleven other knights to hear.

Voss did not rage. He dismounted, unhurried, and drew her aside behind a stand of birches, and he spoke to her the way a father speaks to a daughter about a fever.

"You will stand down," he said. "You will ride back to the garrison and report that you fell ill. I will write the report myself. No one will ever know you refused." He paused. "And tomorrow night, I will be forced to name the knight who refused, because the writ requires a full accounting, and if I do not name you, I will hang beside you. Do you understand what I am telling you? I am telling you that I will betray you, and that I will hate myself for it, and that both of those things can be true while I am still trying to save your life."

"Then don't do it," she said. "Don't go down there."

"I have a wife," Voss said simply. "I have two sons. And down there is a village that the Crown has decided is guilty, and my refusal will not save it — it will only add my corpse to the pyre and yours beside it. Obedience is a cage, Serah. But it is a cage with food in it, and outside the cage is a longer fall than you can imagine."

She stood in the birches and hated him, and understood him, and hated herself most of all for understanding.

She rode back to the garrison. She reported ill. At dusk she watched the smoke rise from Harrowmere from three miles off, a black column standing straight up in the windless air like a pillar holding up nothing, and she ate the supper they gave her, and she slept, because her body was young and young bodies sleep.

They named her anyway. Not as a refuser — Voss kept his word, mostly — but in the accounting afterward, some clerk noted that Knight-Serjeant Vane had been absent from the action without recorded cause, and absence without cause was its own small crime, and small crimes accumulate. Within a year she was passed over twice. Within three she was given duties no one wanted: escorting debt-collectors, guarding ledger-houses, standing watch over clerks who burned names. The Order of the Quiet Flame liked her, actually. They said she had a talent for grim work. They said it as praise.

Voss retired with honors. She saw him once more, at his retirement feast, gray and heavy and laughing, and he caught her eye across the hall and raised his cup a fraction of an inch — a salute, or an apology, or just a man acknowledging another man's cage — and she turned away first.

That was her oath, then. Not the one spoken over the altar. The real one, the one written in smoke over Harrowmere: *I will survive, and I will remember, and the remembering will not save anyone.*

* * *

"Serah."

She came out of it with her knife half-drawn, which embarrassed her. Zephyrine was sitting up, hair loose, watching her with those ink-stained fingers laced around one knee.

"You twitch," the scribe said. "In your sleep, too. You fight someone every night. You always win, but it looks close."

"Go back to sleep."

"Wren's fever broke an hour ago. We're past the worst of it." Zephyrine nodded at the child, still dead asleep, breath slow and even. "You've been up since midnight. I counted your circuits of the camp. Eleven."

"Twelve," Serah said. "You missed one."

Something moved across the scribe's face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lived. "Sit down. Five minutes. The mountain won't fall while you rest."

Serah sat. She told herself it was because standing invited conversation and sitting ended it. She was getting good at lying to herself; nine years of practice.

Zephyrine fed the fire a stick and watched it catch. "You dream about a village," they said quietly. "I hear you say a name sometimes. Harrowmere."

The cold went through Serah like a blade finding the gap in armor. Her hand went to her sword hilt — old reflex, useless here — and then dropped. "You read my reports once? Before you ran?"

"No. You talk in your sleep. I told you." Zephyrine's voice stayed soft. "I'm sorry. I wasn't trying to learn your secrets. Secrets leak out of people at night; any archivist knows that. Half the Order's intelligence comes from guards who talk in their sleep."

"Is that why you're awake? Gathering intelligence?"

"I'm awake because you're awake." The scribe looked at her across the fire, and the honesty in it was worse than any interrogation. "Someone should keep watch with you. Even if you won't let anyone take the watch itself."

*You won't let anyone take the watch.* Because taking the watch was trust, and trust was a door, and Serah had nailed her doors shut so long ago she'd forgotten where the nails were. She stared into the fire and said nothing, and Zephyrine, mercifully, let the silence stand.

But silence, Serah was learning, was its own kind of confession. In the quiet she could hear everything she wasn't saying: that she had crossed a ford three weeks ago with orders in her pocket and a fugitive in her sights, and had let them go, and had told herself it was strategy. That she had drawn steel against her own brothers-in-arms in a smuggler's cache and left two of them bleeding, and had told herself they'd live. That every mile deeper into Thornfell was a mile further from the person the oath had been supposed to make her, and that she was not dragging her feet anymore, and that frightened her more than the hunters' horns.

Because here was the thing she could not say aloud, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal:

She loved them.

Not the way she'd loved duty — duty had been clean, a shape to pour yourself into. This was not clean. This was Zephyrine teaching Wren which moss held water, laughing — actually laughing, a sound Serah had heard maybe four times in six weeks and hoarded each one like contraband. This was the scribe falling asleep mid-sentence over a translation and Serah standing guard over them not because it was her turn but because she wanted to, and wanting was the crime. Wanting was the second oath broken. First Harrowmere, where she'd chosen survival over the people in the valley. Now this, where she was choosing — what? A person. Two people. Choosing them over the shape she'd been poured into, and knowing that someday, probably soon, probably at the worst possible moment, the choice would come due again, and someone would pay, and it would be her fault in a new and inventive way.

Love felt like another broken oath. That was the truth of it. Every time Zephyrine smiled at her, Serah heard Voss's gentle voice: *Choose now. It is the last easy choice you will ever make.* And she had already chosen, hadn't she? Back at the cache, blade wet with her own order's blood. She just hadn't had the courage to say the words, even to herself, until now, alone, at a dying fire, in the dark where lies were easier.

Except the dark wasn't working tonight.

"You're doing it again," Zephyrine said.

"Doing what."

"The thing where you look at the fire like it owes you money." The scribe shifted closer — not touching, never touching, but near enough that Serah could feel the warmth of them, which was somehow worse. "You know what I did, before all this? Before the temple bought my debt?"

"No."

"I copied love letters. For money, on the side. Merchants' wives, soldiers, a bishop's cousin — people who couldn't write, paying a scribe to say what they couldn't say. And do you know what I learned?" Zephyrine poked the fire. "Everyone thinks the hard part is the feeling. It isn't. The feeling is free. It arrives whether you want it or not, like weather. The hard part is saying it. People paid me good coin just to write down words they already knew, because writing them down made them real, and real things can be refused."

Serah's throat was tight. "Is that what you're doing? Selling me a letter?"

"I'm telling you I recognize the weather." Zephyrine looked at her then, straight on, firelight in their eyes. "Whatever it is you're circling. Whatever you think loving us costs you. I'm not asking you to say it. I'm telling you I already read it. I'm a scribe, Serah. I read everything."

Wren stirred between them, murmured something in the old language — a soft syllable, rising at the end, almost a question — and settled again. Serah found she had moved without deciding to, her hand hovering over the child's blanket, tucking it, a gesture so domestic it terrified her more than any hunter's horn.

"I broke my oath," she said. The words came out low, gravelly, like something excavated. "Years ago. There was a village. I could have — it wouldn't have mattered, twelve knights, one refusing — but I could have tried, and I didn't, and everyone in it died, and I survived, and surviving became the whole of me. Everything after was just... managing the shame. Duty was easier than thinking. Then you came through that ford, and—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Every day since, I've been waiting for the moment this turns out like Harrowmere. Where staying means burning, and leaving means living with the smoke. And I need you to understand that when that moment comes, I don't know which way I'll—"

"You'll stay," Zephyrine said.

"You don't know that."

"I read everything," the scribe repeated, gently. "You've stayed every single day. You're staying right now. Whatever you're afraid you are, the evidence says otherwise." A pause. "Harrowmere wasn't your oath breaking. It was other people's oaths breaking around you, and you being young enough to believe theirs were stronger than yours. There's a difference. I spend my life reading the difference."

Serah wanted to argue. She had spent nine years building the argument, stone by stone, and it was a fine argument, load-bearing, the kind of argument a person lives inside. And Zephyrine had walked up to it in the dark and put one hand against it, the way you test a wall you intend to live without.

She didn't argue. She sat with the scribe and the sleeping child and watched the coals breathe, and somewhere in the next hour, without noticing the exact moment, she stopped keeping watch and started keeping company, and the difference between those two things was larger than anything she'd learned in the order, larger than anything in any oath.

Near dawn, Zephyrine slept again, head tipping sideways until it rested — lightly, questioningly, easily denied — against Serah's shoulder. Serah did not deny it. She sat very still, like a woman holding a bird, and looked east where the sky was going gray over the pass, and thought: *this is the thing I betrayed. Not the crown. Not the order. This. I betrayed this before I ever had it, and now I have it, and I will burn down whatever comes to take it.*

It didn't feel like peace. It felt like standing in the birches again, choosing, knowing the choice would cost.

* * *

The horns woke them an hour past sunrise.

Two notes, then three — not the Order's call, which Serah knew bone-deep, but something older, uglier, a sound like a saw dragged through wet wood. Thorn-signals. She'd heard them once before, at the cache, and her body remembered before her mind did: the spike of cold, the taste of iron.

"How many?" Zephyrine was already up, shaking Wren awake, hands steady, voice steady, and Serah loved them for the steadiness and hated how much room it took in her chest.

"Close. Below the ridge line, moving fast." She scrambled to the overlook, glassed the valley through the morning mist, and her stomach dropped. "Too many to fight. Too spread out to ambush. They're not hunting us — they're herding us. Driving us up."

"Up where?" Wren signed, then caught themself and spoke it aloud, the words still clumsy, precious as eggs.

Serah followed the line of the ridges with her eyes until it arrived where she'd known it would: the high notch to the north, white-toothed even in summer, the gap the smugglers called by a name nobody said above a whisper.

"The Pass of Teeth," she said. "It's the only way they're leaving open. Which means it's exactly where they want us."
