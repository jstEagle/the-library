## 11. The Pass of Teeth

The mountain did not want us to cross. I understood that within the first hour, in the way you come to understand a person's mood from the set of their shoulders. Thornfell had shoulders, and they were squared against us.

We had climbed since dawn out of the valley where the hunters' horns had found us two nights past, up through pine and then through pine's ghost — bare gray spikes, wind-stripped, bent all one direction like a congregation frozen mid-flee. Above that, nothing but stone and the white scree fields called the Teeth by every smuggler whose maps I had ever copied for coin. I had drawn this pass a dozen times without ever seeing it. Ink is kinder than rock. My maps showed a clean line threading between the summits. The truth was a spine of shattered granite barely wide enough for one traveler, canted outward over a drop so deep the clouds passed below it like slow white rivers.

"Charming," Serah said, looking up at it. She had Wren on her back, the child's arms looped around her neck, and she said it the way soldiers say such things — flat, almost fond, reserving her real fear for things that deserved it.

"How far?" Wren asked. The child's voice had grown more ordinary these last weeks, less like wind through a ruined temple, more like an actual child who was very tired. But some words still came out wrong-shaped, older than they should be. *Far* came out sounding like a word I hadn't learned yet.

"Half a day," I said. "If the weather holds."

Serah looked at the sky, which was the color of old pewter and moving in two directions at once, and did not dignify that with an answer. She adjusted Wren's weight and started climbing.

I went last. That was my place in our small procession, and not only because I was the slowest. Someone had to watch the back trail. All morning I had been watching it, and all morning it had been empty, and I no longer believed in empty back trails. The Thorn's hunters did not leave horn-signals in valleys and then politely give up. They circled. They climbed where climbing should kill them. I had seen one once, three nights ago across a ravine, and I still dreamed about it: a shape that had been man-sized but wrong in its joints, moving with the patient, terrible economy of something that had never once been tired. It wore the tattered remnant of a temple pilgrim's robe. That was the detail my mind kept returning to. The Order's cloth, on the Thorn's creature. As if the two had always been the same thing and we had only lately noticed.

By midday we were on the spine itself.

It was worse than it looked from below, which I would not have believed possible. The wind came up the southern face in long shoves that arrived without warning; you would be setting your foot on a shelf of rock, trusting it, and the air would lean on you like a hand. To the left the drop fell away into cloud. To the right rose a wall of frost-cracked stone hung with ancient ice, and threaded through the ice, dark as veins, ran lines of something that was not quite mineral and not quite wood. I had seen its like before — in the deep stacks of the Temple library, in the petrified core of a log brought down from Thornfell's high country four hundred years ago. Blackthorn root. Up here, at the roof of the world, frozen inside the mountain itself.

The Thorn was everywhere. I was beginning to understand that it had never been only in the king. It was in the land the way rot is in a beam — the crown merely the visible knot of it.

"Talk to me, Zephyrine," Serah called back. Her voice carried oddly in the thin air, flattened of everything but the words.

"You're doing fine."

"That's not what I meant."

She wanted distraction, so I gave her what I had: geography, the scribe's consolation. "The pass narrows ahead, maybe half a mile, then opens onto the saddle. From the saddle there's a descent route the smugglers called the Stair — cut steps in the east face, sheltered from the wind. There's supposed to be a cache near the bottom. Rope, dried meat, if the cache hasn't been robbed."

"Supposed to be," Serah repeated. "Your maps are full of *supposed to be*."

"My maps are full of things people survived," I said. "That's what a map is."

Ahead of me, too quietly for anyone but me to catch, Wren laughed.

We made the narrow stretch with the clouds rising to meet us. That was worse than the drop, in its way — the world shrinking to ten feet of wet stone with white nothing on either side, sound gone soft and close, every footfall loud as a breaking branch. I kept my eyes on Serah's back and counted my breaths the way the Quiet Flame taught: ink in, ink out. Even now, even after everything Mavelin and her Order had revealed themselves to be, the old disciplines held me together. Perhaps especially now. You cannot wholly unlearn the thing that kept you alive.

We were nearly through the narrows when Wren stopped.

I felt it before I saw it — the child's weight shifting on Serah's back, a small stiffening, like a bird dog scenting. Then Wren turned their head, there on the knight's shoulders, and looked back along the way we had come, into the fog, and their whole body went taut as a bowstring.

"They're coming," Wren said. "Fast ones. Many feet."

"How many?" Serah's voice had gone to the flat register again, the soldier's voice.

Wren was quiet a moment, head tilted, listening to something that was not sound. "More than horns. Less than a war." A pause. "They are not walking the trail. They are coming up the south face."

I looked at Serah. She looked at me. Nothing climbed the south face. The south face was why this pass was safe — sheer black ice, eight hundred feet of it. Which meant the things behind us could climb anything, and every map I had ever drawn was a map of illusions, and the only safety left in the world was whatever we could make with our own hands in the next few minutes.

"Run, then," Serah said, and set Wren down, and ran.

* * *

We broke out of the narrows onto the saddle with the fog boiling off us like steam, and I saw the Stair below us — and saw, at the same moment, that we would not reach it.

The saddle was a bowl of snow perhaps two hundred paces across, ringed by the highest teeth of the pass. And across it, flowing up out of the eastern col like water finding its level, came the hunters.

There were nine of them. I counted because counting is what I do when I am afraid; numbers are my prayer. Nine shapes in pilgrim rags, wrong-jointed, faces smooth as river stones where faces should have folded and lined. They moved across the snow without sinking into it. Where they stepped, the snow did not melt or mark. It *grayed*, as though all the color were being drawn down into them, and I understood with a sick lurch that even this high and cold, the Thorn fed constantly, on light, on warmth, on the small quantum of justice that keeps snow white.

"Behind us too," Serah said. She had put herself between Wren and the creatures, sword drawn, and I saw her do the arithmetic every soldier does — distances, numbers, the width of her blade against nine unburiable bodies — and arrive at zero.

"Zephyrine." Wren tugged my sleeve. The child's face was very pale, and their eyes had that doubled look I had begun to dread, pupils flickering as though something ancient were reading the world over the child's shoulder. "The mountain here is not dead. It is only sleeping very hard. The cold puts it to sleep. Like the temple put you to sleep, with the ledgers. Counting instead of living."

"What are you saying?"

"I can wake it." Wren looked up at me, and for a moment all the strangeness fell away and there was just a child, exhausted and frightened, asking an adult to confirm that a terrible idea was permitted. "You have to hold my hands. So I know where the shore is. Otherwise I go too far out."

Behind us, gravel skittered. The first of the hunters had reached the rim of the saddle.

"Wren—"

"You told me once," the child said, "that a word written in the right order is stronger than a sword. You said it about the ledgers. About the debt. You were sad when you said it. But you said it."

I had said it. Some weary night in the cottage, transcribing smugglers' routes, I had said exactly that, never imagining the child memorized my bitterness like scripture.

So I knelt in the snow and took both of Wren's hands in mine, and the child began to speak.

* * *

I am a scribe. I have spent my life believing that language is the truest thing humans make. I have copied contracts that ruined families and prayers that comforted them, and I thought I knew what words weighed.

I knew nothing.

What Wren spoke on the saddle of the Pass of Teeth was not a sentence. It was a geology. The sounds came up out of the child like roots pulling themselves out of soil, each syllable vast and slow and layered on the last, and I felt them through my palms before I heard them through my ears — a vibration like the deepest bell in the world being rung once, under the mountain, by the mountain.

And the mountain woke.

I do not have the vocabulary for what happened next, and I am a person who owns vocabulary professionally. The ice-veined wall on the western side of the saddle *shivered*. Not cracked — shivered, the way a horse shivers off flies, the whole hundred-foot sheet flexing. The snow beneath our feet stopped being dead matter and became, briefly, skin — I felt it prickle, felt a vast attention turn toward us through the ground like sunlight moving across a floor. Below the cloud-line, somewhere far down in the bones of Thornfell, something groaned, a sound with a geological patience in it, the sound of a creature turning over in a sleep of ten thousand years.

The hunters stopped.

All nine of them stood still in the snow, heads tilting in unison, and for the first time I saw something like hesitation in their wrong geometry. They were the Thorn's hunger given legs. And the mountain rising awake around them was the one thing in the world older than their master's appetite.

Wren spoke another of those impossible syllables, and the slope above the hunters moved.

It was not an avalanche. Avalanches are chaotic; this was deliberate. A shelf of wind-loaded snow perhaps forty feet wide simply let go, all at once, and slid — carrying the hunters off the saddle and down the eastern face in total silence, nine ragged shapes tumbling into the white, and then the mountain shrugged its shoulder and a second slide followed the first, and then a third, and the cloud below swallowed everything.

The vibration faded. The snow under my knees went back to being snow. The wind resumed, indifferent.

And Wren collapsed.

* * *

I caught the child before the ground did. What I felt in my arms frightened me more than the hunters had — not the limpness, children faint, children are made of falling — but the lightness. It was as though something had been subtracted. As though Wren weighed measurably less than the child who had woken the mountain thirty seconds before.

Then I saw the hair.

At the left temple, a patch of white — not gray, not frost. White as fresh paper, pure and absolute, a hand-span of color drained out of the world. And spreading from it, faint as watermark, lines across the child's cheek like cracks in fired clay. They might have been mistaken for dirt, for windburn. I did not mistake them.

"Wren." I pulled off my glove, pressed my bare fingers to the small face. Cold. Not mountain-cold. The cold of a room where a fire has just gone out. "Wren, look at me."

The eyes opened. Both pupils were steady now, ordinary, human-dark, and terribly tired.

"Did we win?" the child whispered.

"The hunters are gone. You did it. You—" My voice caught on something I refused to let it finish. I swallowed it down and made my voice into a scribe's voice, level, functional. "You need to tell me what that cost. Now. All of it."

Serah crouched beside us, snow blowing past her shoulders, sword still bare in her fist. She said nothing. She had learned, in the mountains, when to let me do the talking.

Wren looked at our hands, still clasped around the child's small ones. "Every word of the old language is a thread out of me," the child said slowly, as if translating from something larger. "The first weavers spun big things. Rivers. Borders. A binding strong enough to hold the Thorn for a thousand years. They spent whole lives on single sentences. That is why the kings killed them all — not because they were dangerous. Because they were *expensive*." A ghost of the terrible child-laugh. "I am the last spool. Every time I speak it, there is less of me left to speak with."

The wind shoved at us. Serah stripped off her cloak and wrapped it around Wren without a word, and lifted the child onto her back again, and I watched her hands check the grip with the same automatic tenderness she used checking a saddle girth, and I thought: *she has already decided. She decided weeks ago and neither of them knows it yet.*

"And the cracks?" I asked. "On your face."

Wren touched the white patch of hair, exploring it, unsurprised. "Those happen when the thread runs short. The weaving shows." The child met my eyes then, and it was the oldest I had ever seen those eyes, older than any kingdom, calm as ledger-lines. "There is enough of me left for the big word. The one that matters. But Zephyrine — not much more than that. One, maybe two, smaller spells after. And then the spool is empty."

I nodded, as though this were information like any other, distance to the Stair, pounds in the pack. Inside me something had begun, quietly and methodically, to rewrite every plan I owned.

Below us the Stair descended into cloud. Somewhere beyond it lay the capital, the hollow king, the crown, the end of this road. I had thought we were running *from* something. Standing on that saddle, holding a lighter child than the one I had caught, I understood at last that we had been running *toward* something all along, and that the something was going to ask a price, and that I had already begun, in the secret ledger-room of my heart, to calculate how to pay it alone.

Three days later, in the shelter of the Stair's cut steps, with Wren sleeping under Serah's cloak and Serah watching the darkness below with her sword across her knees, I took out my journal and by candlelight drew the sigil Wren had spoken into my palms on the mountain. My hand shook. Not from the cold.

Because beneath the sigil, clear as anything I had ever copied, I wrote the words I had sworn I would never write: *There may be a way to take the binding myself.*

And it was then, hearing the wind shift, that Serah looked up from the trail below and said, quietly, "Zephyrine. There are lights in the valley. Torch columns. Marching in ranks."

Not hunters. Hunters did not march in ranks.

"It's the Order," she said. "Mavelin has reached the mountains."
