## 12. Cantors in the Snow

The snow came down like ash, and Mavelin thought: *good*. Ash was honest. Ash meant something had burned.

She stood at the head of the column, forty enforcers behind her in grey wool and iron-shod boots, and watched the pass swallow the light. Thornfell did not want them here. The mountain made that plain the way a locked door makes it plain — not with malice, just with refusal. The wind came off the heights carrying a sound she had not heard in sixty years of service, a low harmonic under the storm, like a choir humming through stone.

The old language. Faint. Getting stronger.

"High Cantor," said Brother Ossik, at her shoulder. He was young for an enforcer-cantor, broad and careful, the kind of man who checked his knots twice. "The trackers say they're two days ahead. The child slows them."

"The child *is* them," Mavelin said. "Dunmore is incidental. Vane is incidental. Remember what we're actually hunting."

Ossik hesitated. She could feel him deciding whether to ask. He asked. "What are we actually hunting?"

Mavelin looked north, toward the capital, invisible beneath the storm but present anyway — she always felt it now, a tug behind her sternum, like a fishhook set deep. Sixty years she had felt it and called it devotion.

"A debt," she said. "The oldest one on our books."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a shrine — one of the first-age waymarkers the Order had re-consecrated centuries ago, its original carvings chiseled flat and replaced with the Quiet Flame's spiral. Mavelin ran her bare hand over the flattened stone while the enforcers pitched tents. Beneath the chisel marks, if you knew where to press, you could still feel grooves. Letters. A language older than any kingdom, older than the Flame's first candle.

*We bound it,* the stone had once said. *We paid.*

Her order had spent three hundred years teaching that the first age was myth. That the old tongue was heresy. That debt was sacred because the Flame said so, and the Flame said so because —

Because.

She withdrew her hand and flexed her fingers against the cold. There had been a time, early in her novitiate, when she'd found one of the unchiseled stones out past the terraced gardens, half-buried, and copied its script into the margin of a psalter as a joke. Her mentor, old Cantor Heskel, had gone white as tallow when he saw it. Had burned the page in front of her without a word. Had gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *Never write what you cannot unwrite, girl.*

She hadn't understood then. She understood now. You couldn't unwrite a thing that was already listening.

"High Cantor." Ossik again, holding out a tin cup of broth. She took it more for the warmth than the food. "May I speak freely?"

"You never do. Consider it a novelty."

He almost smiled. "The men are asking why we're chasing a scribe across a mountain in winter. Dunmore's debt was twelve thousand candle-hours. We've spent more than that already." He paused. "Some of them are asking whether the debt is really the point."

Smart boy. Dangerous boy. The dangerous ones were useful, right up until they weren't.

"Sit," Mavelin said.

* * *

She told him by the fire, quietly, while the sentries stamped circles beyond the tent-flaps and the snow hissed itself into drifts against the shrine stone. Not all of it. Never all of it. But enough.

"When the Order was founded," she began, "it was nothing. A scriptorium and a begging bowl. Do you know how a minor cult becomes the keeper of every ledger in the realm? How we come to hold paper on half the land and a claim on nearly every soul who can't pay?"

"Faith," Ossik said. "And lending."

"Lending," she agreed. "But who do you think taught us the arithmetic? Who showed the first cantors how to structure a debt so it could never be discharged — only transferred, inherited, compounded?" She turned the cup in her hands. "There is a power under the capital, brother. Older than the crown. It sits beneath the throne like water under ice, and it is hungry in a way flesh cannot be hungry, and three hundred years ago it made our founders an offer."

Ossik's face had gone carefully blank. The blankness of a man rebuilding his world.

"It wanted feeding," Mavelin said. "Not bodies — it gets those from kings, who have always been generous with other people's blood. It wanted something else. Something it could taste through the ledgers themselves. Obligation. The flavor of a soul that belongs to someone else. Every debtor we bind, every candle-hour we tally, every family ground down under compound interest — the essence of that drains downhill, brother, all of it, into the dark under the throne room. And in exchange—"

"In exchange the temple keeps its power," Ossik said slowly.

"The temple *is* the farm," Mavelin said. "The rest is barn paint."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, faint as breath, the harmonic sang again, and this time Ossik heard it too. She watched him hear it. Watched the color leave his face.

"That's the old tongue," he whispered.

"That's the binding, fraying," Mavelin said. "It has been fraying for a century. The king stopped blinking eleven years ago; I have the reports. And now a mute child has walked out of Thornfell speaking the language of the people who wove the binding, and a temple scribe has run off into the mountains with it, and somewhere in that collision is either the end of everything or the last chance to keep the arrangement intact." She drank the cold broth. "Guess which outcome the Order prefers."

"And Dunmore?"

"What about Dunmore?"

"They were a good scribe," Ossik said. "I audited their ledgers once. Perfect hand. Never a missed hour." He looked into the fire. "They counted down their own slavery in neat little columns. And we're going to kill them for walking away from it."

"We're going to kill them for what they're carrying," Mavelin corrected. "The difference matters. To me, at least." She set down the cup. "Sleep, brother. Tomorrow we climb."

He rose, paused at the tent flap, and asked the question she'd been waiting for since the fire started. "Do *you* prefer it? Keeping the arrangement?"

Mavelin looked at him for a long moment. Sixty years of candle-hours. Sixty years of believing the noose around the realm's neck was a collar of office.

"I prefer to be the one holding the leash," she said. "Go to sleep."

* * *

The dream came, as it always did on this mountain, and she let it.

She stood in a throne room made of roots. The walls were living wood, grown dense and dark, and on the throne sat a shape wearing a crown, and the shape was not a man, though it wore Aldric's face the way a glove wears a hand. It turned its head toward her without moving its body, the way a plant turns toward sun.

*You brought me so few this year,* it said. Its voice was the sound a ledger makes when it closes. *The harvest thins, High Cantor. Debts forgiven. Reformers on my councils. They speak of abolishing the candle-tithe.* The mouth smiled without any participation from the eyes. *You know what happens to my appetite when it starves. You remember the famine year.*

"I remember," Mavelin said, in the dream, in the voice of a much younger woman. "I was there. I helped bury them."

*Then feed me,* said the Thorn, *or learn what I eat instead.*

She woke with her heart slamming and her hands numb, and lay listening to the storm, and permitted herself, as she permitted herself perhaps twice a decade, the full weight of the truth: she was not the shepherd of the Quiet Flame. She was the foreman of a slaughterhouse that had learned to call itself a church, and she had known it for fifty years, and she had stayed, because the alternative to feeding it was being fed on, and she had never once in her long gray life been brave enough to find out which death was worse.

Zephyrine Dunmore had found out in a single night, over a knock at a door.

That was the part that galled. Not the fugitive maps, not the stolen child, not even Serah Vane's treason. It was that a debt-scribe with ink-stained fingers had done in one evening what Mavelin had lacked the spine to do in six decades: chosen.

"High Cantor?" The sentry at the flap. "Scouts report movement below the eastern ridge. Small party. Three figures."

Mavelin sat up. Outside, the storm had begun to thin, and through the rag in the clouds the peaks showed white and vast and patient, and beneath her feet, faint through the stone, the old harmonic swelled — closer now, urgent, like a voice calling a name.

Three figures. Two days ahead, the trackers had said. Now below the ridge.

Either the fugitives had doubled back, or something on this mountain was herding all of them — hunter and hunted alike — toward the same place, the same hour, the same choice.

"Break camp," Mavelin said. "All of it. We move now."

She pulled on her gloves and stepped out into the snow, and high overhead, where the clouds tore open, she saw the peak the locals named the Crown of Teeth, and she saw — or told herself she saw — a thread of green light crawling down its face like sap down bark.

The binding was failing faster than the reports said.

And whatever reached the top of that mountain first would decide what the realm woke up to at solstice.
