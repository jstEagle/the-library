## 13. What Wren Remembers

Wren did not know how to give a dream to someone else until the night the fire burned low and Zephyrine fell asleep sitting up, the translation pages sliding from their fingers into the ash-gray coals.

The child had been watching them for hours. Watching the way Zephyrine's mouth moved in sleep, shaping words from a language that had died before their grandmother's grandmother was born — or would have died, if Wren's people had not hidden it inside a child's skull like a seed inside winter fruit. The words were not lost. The words were waiting. They had always been waiting.

*You want to know,* Wren thought at the sleeping scribe, the way they had learned to think at the mountain, at the river, at the small stubborn fires of the world. *You have been asking with your whole body for weeks.*

So Wren gave.

It was not difficult. That was the terrible part. It was like opening a door that had always been there, painted to look like wall. Wren stepped through, took Zephyrine's sleeping hand, and pulled them both down into the oldest memory anyone alive still carried.

* * *

The dream began with snow that fell upward.

Zephyrine stood on a mountainside that Wren knew better than their own heartbeat — Thornfell, but younger, its teeth unbroken, its passes unscarred by smuggler roads. The sky above was wrong: green-gold, bruised at the edges, as if the world were an egg something had begun to peck open from within.

"Where are we?" Zephyrine asked, and their voice sounded strange here, doubled, half-echo. "When are we?"

Wren could not answer in words. Words were heavy things; they had cost Wren too much already, every syllable spoken aloud a coin scraped off a soul already thin. So instead Wren did what their mother had done, and her mother before her, back and back down a line of teachers who had all paid the same price for knowing: Wren *showed*.

They opened the memory like a hand opening.

And Zephyrine saw.

A queen, dying, on a throne of black stone at the heart of the mountain. Not old — young, terribly young, her belly round with child, her hands stained to the wrist with blood that was not entirely her own. Around her stood nine figures in robes the color of wet ash, and Wren felt Zephyrine flinch beside them, because the robes were familiar. The robes had never gone out of fashion. The Order of the Quiet Flame wore their descendants still.

"That's—" Zephyrine began.

*Watch,* said the memory, in a voice made of every voice Wren had ever inherited. *All of it. You asked. Watch all of it.*

The queen's name had been Serevanne, and she had been the last of a line that had ruled these mountains since before kingdoms had names. She was dying because she had chosen to die — because the thing beneath her mountain had broken loose of its first prison, a pit sealed by her great-great-grandmother, and the only way to cage it again was a life freely given, poured out like wine into the seal.

But Serevanne had been afraid. That was the first sin, and Wren showed it without mercy, because mercy was not what this memory was for.

She had been afraid, and so instead of giving her own life entire, she had bargained. She had taken the thing — the Thorn, though no one called it that yet; then it had only been *the Hunger Under the Hill*, the *Root That Drinks*, the nameless gnawing dark — and she had done something worse than killing it and less than dying. She had bound it. Bound it into a shape small enough to hold, cold enough to wear.

She had bound it into a crown.

*Why a crown?* Zephyrine's dream-self whispered, and even in the dream their scribe's mind was working, turning the question like a page. *Of all shapes, why—*

Because a crown is worn by kings, the memory answered. Because a king sits at the center of justice, where the Hunger most wanted to feed. Because a prisoner locked in a cell grows weak, but a prisoner locked in power grows *patient*. Serevanne believed she had found a perfect cage: the monster imprisoned in the very seat of law, forced to spend eternity watching justice done properly, starving on the sight of fairness, unable to touch it.

She believed she had built a lock.

She had actually built a larder.

* * *

The dream shifted, and years poured past like water through fingers.

Wren showed Zephyrine the coronation, Serevanne's son kneeling to receive the crown his mother had forged with her dying breath. Showed how the boy wept when it touched his head — not from awe. From *pain*. A flash of it, white-hot, a needle finding the soft place behind the eyes.

Showed him standing at his first judgment three days later, sentencing a poacher to hang for stealing a deer to feed his starving children. Showed the small smile the boy wore afterward and did not remember wearing.

Showed the next king. And the next. Each one bending a little further. Each one blinking a little less.

"They don't know," Zephyrine breathed. In the dream their face kept trying to settle into one expression and failing — horror, pity, recognition, each displacing the last. "None of them ever knew. They think the cruelty is theirs."

Some of it is theirs, the memory said, and Wren felt the old grief move through them like weather. The Thorn does not make men wicked. It only makes wickedness *easy*, and then it eats what grows. A king who hesitates feels the crown grow warm. A king who shows mercy wakes with nosebleeds and nightmares. Year by year, reign by reign, the Thorn prunes the tree of justice down to the stump that feeds it best. It has had eleven hundred years of pruning, scribe. Look what's left.

The dream gave them Aldric.

Not the hollow king of the capital — a younger man, a decade ago, laughing in a garden with a woman who was not yet dead, dirt under his fingernails from planting an orchard he had promised his daughter they'd harvest together. Then the coronation ring pressing against the crown's edge. Then the same man, five years on, signing a tax decree with a face like a closed door, while somewhere behind his eyes something ancient leaned forward to read over his shoulder, taking notes.

"He's still in there," Zephyrine said. It was not a question. Their voice cracked on it anyway. "He's been in there the whole time. Watching."

Watching, yes. And sometimes, in the deep watches of the night, screaming. But the Thorn had learned patience from its prison, and patience is a kind of cruelty all its own.

* * *

Then came the part Wren had never shown anyone, because there had never been anyone left to show.

The dream turned, and Zephyrine cried out — a small sound, involuntary, the sound of someone touching a stove — because the memory turned toward *them*, toward the line that had made the binding possible. The nine robed figures had not worked alone. Binding a god-shaped hunger required a language with roots deeper than any living tongue, a speech that could tell a thing what it was and make it believe. The weavers of that language had lived in the high valleys of Thornfell since the melting of the first ice.

Wren's people.

The dream showed the villages. Terraced fields cut into slopes so steep the goats wore bells to be found. Longhouses with carved lintels, every beam singing a different word of the old tongue, so that a house was also a sentence, and a village was a paragraph, and the whole valley read itself aloud in wind and creak and hearth-smoke. Children learning letters by tracing them in ash. Grandmothers who could argue a river into changing course, politely, over tea.

And then the dream showed the soldiers.

Because Serevanne's heirs had understood, within three generations, what their ancestor had perhaps known all along: that the binding needed keepers. That the weavers' language was the only key to the crown, and keys are dangerous things to leave lying about. And so a king — Wren showed his face, a soft-faced man with a scholar's squint, which somehow made it worse — had declared the valley's speech a heresy of the first age, and sent the Order of the Quiet Flame, whose robes had by then changed from wet ash to dove gray, to correct it.

*Heresy of the first age.* Zephyrine flinched again. Mavelin's words, word for word. Some phrases outlive their reasons.

The dream did not soften it. Wren had promised themselves, in whatever remained of themselves that could make promises, that if they ever showed someone, they would show everything. So the dream showed the longhouses burning with their sentences still carved in the beams, the words shrieking as they charred. Showed the grandmothers who went to the soldiers singing, so that the last thing the valley said was not a plea. Showed the terraced fields running red, then running empty, then growing nothing but nettles for two hundred years.

Showed a handful of survivors fleeing higher, and higher, into the teeth of the pass, keeping the language alive the only way left — by hiding it in children, generation after generation, each child taught in secret, each child carrying more than a child should hold, each child paying interest on a debt they had never borrowed.

"How many?" Zephyrine whispered. "How many children?"

The memory did not answer with numbers. Numbers were a temple way of counting grief, and Wren's people had never counted that way. Instead the dream simply opened, and Wren let Zephyrine feel it: the weight of every carrier before them, stacked like stones, like sediment, like the mountain itself. Names without faces. A girl who hid the language inside lullabies and was caught humming one. A boy who swallowed the words rather than speak them under torture, and choked. An old man who taught the whole tongue to his granddaughter in a single winter because he could feel the hunters closing in, and who smiled at the end and said, in the old speech, *now you are heavier than I am, little bird, and I am finally light.*

Little bird. *Wren.*

"I didn't choose my own name," Wren said aloud, in the dream, in the old tongue, and heard it translated inside Zephyrine's mind the moment it left their lips. "My mother named me for the last thing my grandfather ever carried."

* * *

The dream was ending. Wren could feel it fraying at the edges, and worse, could feel the cost of it settling into their bones — showing was not free either, not for the last thread. Their hands, asleep by the fire in the waking world, had gone numb to the elbow. Somewhere far away, Serah Vane was shaking Zephyrine's shoulder, saying their name in a voice pitched tight with fear.

But there was one thing left, and Wren spent their last strength in the dream to say it.

They brought Zephyrine to the binding-stones — the circle on the high mountain, the place where Wren had first appeared out of the storm, mud-caked and mute, at a scribe's door. In the dream the stones stood new-cut, gleaming, and around them knelt the nine robed figures and the dying queen, and Serevanne was speaking the words of the binding for the last time, teaching them to the stone, and her voice was ragged with something the dream refused to hide:

Regret.

*"I bind thee, Hunger, into gold and grief,"* the dying queen said, *"and I bind my line to thy keeping, and my line's line, unto the last of the blood —"* and there she stopped, coughing blood of her own, and the nine waited, and she finished in a whisper: *"unto the last of the blood, who shall pay what I would not."*

"She knew," Zephyrine said. Their dream-face was wet. "She knew what she was doing. She wrote the bill and signed it with her descendants' names."

Yes, said the memory. And the Thorn heard it too. Every word spoken over a binding becomes part of the binding. The Thorn has been listening for eleven hundred years for the last of the blood. It knows the debt comes due. It has been *waiting* for me, scribe, the way winter waits under the snow.

The dream was going gray now, dissolving, and through the gray Wren felt Zephyrine seize them — not physically, not in any way that mattered to the waking world, but with the whole desperate grip of a mind that had spent its life copying other people's debts into ledgers and had just seen the largest one of all.

"What happens when the last of the blood pays?" Zephyrine demanded. "What does paying *mean*, Wren? What happens to you?"

And Wren, who had known the answer since before they could walk, who had been raised on the answer the way other children were raised on milk, looked at the scribe with the storm-gray eyes and told the truth, because they had promised themselves truth, everything, if they ever showed anyone:

*I don't know. My grandfather didn't know. No one has ever come back to say. But the binding says the debt transfers, never breaks. Someone must wear what the queen wore. Someone must take the Thorn willingly, or the crown takes the last of the blood unwillingly, and either way—*

The dream collapsed.

* * *

Wren woke to firelight and Serah's sword half-drawn and Zephyrine bolt upright, gasping, both hands pressed flat against the floor of the smuggler cache as if to confirm the world still held.

"You were both—" Serah started. Stopped. Her eyes moved between them, reading what knights are trained to read: the aftermath of violence. "You were both somewhere else. Both of you. At the same time."

"Wren showed me something." Zephyrine's voice came out scraped raw. They looked at their hands, ink-stained, trembling, and Wren watched them flex their fingers slowly, as if checking whether the dream had left anything written on the skin. "Eleven hundred years, Serah. The crown, the kings, the Order — the Order was *there*, at the first binding, they've been feeding it debtors since before the temple had a name — and the child, the child is—"

Wren watched the scribe stop. Watched them look at the mute child by the fire, really look, and saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished behind their eyes: the last of the blood, the debt coming due, the bill signed by a dying queen and countersigned by ten centuries of kings.

"The child is what," Serah said quietly.

Zephyrine opened their mouth. Closed it. And Wren, who had shown them everything, watched them decide — visibly, painfully, in the space of a single breath — to lie.

"The child is tired," Zephyrine said. "We should all sleep."

Serah's hand stayed near her sword a moment longer. Then she nodded slowly, unconvinced, and banked the fire, and lay down facing the entrance with her back to them both, the way she always did, guarding a door that led nowhere safe.

Wren did not sleep. Wren sat in the dark and watched Zephyrine not-sleep either, watched the scribe's eyes reflect the coals, and understood, with the terrible clarity the old language gave them, exactly what Zephyrine was doing behind that careful face.

They were planning something. Something they did not mean to tell the knight.

And Wren, the last thread, the final payment on a debt older than kingdoms, pulled the thread of that silence taut — and waited to see whether the scribe would break, or lie forever, or finally learn the one word their whole life had been missing.

*Enough.*

Three days south, beyond the pass, the capital waited with its hollow-eyed crowds and its unblinking king. And Wren dreamed, alone this time, of a garden planted over binding-stones, and could not tell if it was memory or prophecy — only that in the dream, Zephyrine's hands held the trowel, and Serah's shadow fell across the soil like a vow, and the earth gave up its old grief one stone at a time.
