## 15. An Audience with the King

The seal still worked.

Serah hadn't believed it would. Three years since they'd struck her name from the rolls, three years since Commander Ashe had looked through her at the tribunal and said *knight no longer* like he was reading a market price. But the palace guard didn't read names. They read the silver thorn-and-tower stamped in wax, and the wax was old, and old things in this city were treated gently — as if they might break, or bite.

She wore it on a cord under her shirt, against her sternum, where she'd once worn her oath.

"Two visitors," she told the gate captain. "Retainers of House Vane, petitioning on private family business." She put just enough boredom into it. Boredom was the uniform of people who belonged here. "My aunt's will is contested. You know how the courts crawl."

The captain glanced at Zephyrine — travel-stained, hood up, ink stains permanent as birthmarks now — and at Wren, who stood close against Zephyrine's side with their face hidden. The child had been coached: keep your eyes down, don't speak, not one word of the old language. Not even to count.

"The aunt," the captain said, "is dead, I take it."

"Bitterly."

He waved them through, and Serah felt the gate swallow them, walls rising on either side, and understood that she had just used the last shred of her old life as a lockpick. There would be nothing after this. If they were caught inside, the seal wouldn't save her. It wasn't meant for treason.

The city was worse than the mountains had promised. From the high passes they'd looked down on Corvane and seen smoke, commerce, the ordinary noise of ten thousand lives. Inside the walls, the noise was there but wrong — spaced out, like gaps in a heartbeat. Market stalls open with no one minding them. A baker standing in his doorway watching the street with his eyes half-lidded, flour drying on his hands. Two children playing a game with rules Serah couldn't follow, laughing at intervals that came too evenly, like bells.

Nobody jostled. Nobody argued. Nobody ran.

"They're all half-asleep," Zephyrine murmured. They kept their voice below the level of the fountains, which sang everywhere in this district, which suddenly seemed less like ornament than anesthesia. "Look at their hands."

Serah looked. The baker's hands hung loose. A woman crossing the square paused mid-stride when the fountain peaked, then resumed. Everyone moved the way debtors moved through the temple halls on collection day — heads bowed not in humility but in conservation, saving every scrap of attention for something unseen.

*Feeding,* Zephyrine had said, two nights ago, over a map drawn by firelight. *It doesn't eat flesh. It eats judgment. Every decision made slowly, every grievance swallowed, every choice surrendered — that's what goes into the crown.*

And the king sat at the top of it all, drinking from the whole realm at once.

The throne room doors were bronze, worked with the first king's line, and the guards there were not tranced. That frightened Serah more than the sleepwalkers. These four stood alert, hands near swords, eyes clear and cold — and none of them blinked when she gave her name, and none of them asked why retainers of a dissolved house wanted an audience, because one of them had already been told to expect her.

*Mavelin knows we're coming,* she thought, and made herself walk forward anyway. There was no other direction left.

* * *

They waited an hour in an antechamber hung with portraits of dead monarchs. Serah passed the time counting exits — two, plus the windows if she broke the glass and didn't mind a forty-foot drop onto marble. Zephyrine spent it writing. They'd filled half a notebook on the road south, transcribing everything Wren said, and the child had stopped resisting it somewhere past the Pass of Teeth, speaking deliberately now, slowly, like someone rationing coins.

Wren sat between them with eyes closed, lips moving faintly. Practicing, or praying. Serah had learned you couldn't tell the difference in the old language.

"I should say it before we go in," Zephyrine said quietly. "If it goes wrong in there — if Aldric isn't reachable, if the Thorn takes the room — you take Wren and go. West corridor, service stairs, the route I mapped. You don't come back for me."

"No."

"You don't—"

"No." Serah watched the doors instead of them. "I've broken one oath already. I'm not practicing."

Zephyrine's mouth curved, small and tired, and Serah hated how much she loved seeing it, how much of her heart she'd staked on a scribe who lied beautifully and planned to die politely. But that argument was for another night. The doors opened without a hand touching them.

The throne room was enormous and dim, lit by high windows gone grey with afternoon, and it smelled — Serah's stomach turned before her mind caught up — it smelled like the temple's incense, the same heavy resin the Quiet Flame burned over its ledgers, and beneath it something older and wetter, loam and sap, a forest rotting in slow motion under the stone.

King Aldric sat on his throne the way a coat sits on a hook.

He was not what she remembered. She'd seen him twice from a distance at tournaments — a broad man, loud, famous for laughing longer than any joke deserved. Now he was thinner than his clothes, and very still, and his eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance where the portraits' eyes would have been fixed, if portraits hung in this room. He did not blink. Zephyrine had told her. She still wasn't ready for it — eyes dry as old varnish, reflecting the light without taking any of it in.

Guards lined the walls. Six of them, awake, expressionless. Behind the throne, half in shadow, stood a tall figure in cantor grey whom Serah recognized from wanted notices: High Cantor Mavelin herself, hands folded, watching them with the mild patience of a woman watching weather arrive on schedule.

"Your Majesty," Serah said, and knelt, because the body remembered protocol even now, and pulled Zephyrine down beside her. "Serah Vane, formerly of the King's Third Lance. These are my — retainers. We bring word of matters touching the crown's welfare. Private matters."

Nothing moved. The fountains outside sang on.

Then Aldric's head turned. It was a small motion, mechanical, a puppet testing its strings — and then, all at once, it wasn't. His gaze came into focus like a lens being ground, like something surfacing through deep water, and his face changed, and Serah felt Zephyrine go rigid beside her because the change was *human*. Painfully, terribly human. His eyes welled. His mouth trembled around words that took visible effort, each one dragged up from somewhere far down.

"—still in here." His voice was a ruin, rusted from disuse. "There's still — I can feel it loosening. When they come close—" his eyes fixed on Wren, and something in his face cracked open with hope so naked it hurt to witness, "—it loosens. What is that? What is *that?*"

Wren stepped forward before anyone could stop them. Of course they did. Serah grabbed for their shoulder and got air.

"It's the language," Wren said aloud, and the old syllables rang strangely in the vaulted room, off the bronze, off the stone, and every guard flinched as if struck. "You're still bound. We came to unbind you."

"Don't." The king lurched forward against nothing, straining at his own body like a man strapped down. Spittle at the corner of his mouth. Tears cutting clean lines through the grime of years. "Listen to me. Listen. Whatever it costs — whatever you think you can trade — *don't pay it.* It only ever asks for one coin." His voice dropped, ragged, urgent. "Kill me. While it's loose like this. While I can still want it. Please. I have signed death for thousands and felt my hand move and known it wasn't mine, and I cannot — I cannot live in this thing one more—"

"Aldric." Mavelin's voice, from behind the throne, was gentle. Gently, she stepped into the light. "You're tired."

The change was instantaneous and obscene.

It happened between one breath and the next: the king's face smoothed over like water closing above a drowned man. The tears stopped — not dried, *stopped*, mid-track. His spine straightened. And when his eyes found them again they were the varnish-eyes, the unblinking ones, and his mouth smiled with perfect warmth, and Serah's sword was somehow in her hand though she had no memory of drawing it.

"Visitors," the king said pleasantly. "How good. We receive so few."

Mavelin sighed, and it was the sigh of a woman disappointed in children who wouldn't eat their supper. "You brought it here," she said to Zephyrine, conversationally, walking down the steps from the dais as if the whole room weren't tightening around them like a fist. "All this way, and you carried it straight to the crown. The child. Did you truly think the Order hunted you out of piety?" She stopped at the base of the steps. Up close her face was older than her voice, and very calm. "We have fed this kingdom's keeper for six hundred years, child. Debtors, heretics, the troublesome and the bright — do you imagine the ledgers were about *money?*"

The doors slammed. All of them — antechamber, side entrances, the bronze behind — boom, boom, boom, like a heartbeat closing its fist. The guards drew in unison, six blades catching grey light.

"Wren," Zephyrine said, very quietly. "West corridor."

The trap sprang.

* * *

Later, Serah could never assemble the fight into order. Memory gave it back in pieces, the way bad dreams do.

The guards moving faster than men should, in perfect silence, six swords as one instrument — and her meeting them, backpedaling, giving ground she couldn't afford, shouting for Zephyrine to *run, gods, run.* The king descending from the throne at a glacial pace, unhurried, smiling, while the room itself seemed to lean inward; the pressure of it, the sleepy heavy weight pressing on her mind like warm hands, *put the sword down, kneel, rest, rest* — and her biting through her own lip until she tasted blood, because pain was a rope and she hauled herself along it hand over hand.

Zephyrine hurling the notebook — the transcription, months of work — at Mavelin's face and dragging Wren toward the west door, hammering the bar, screaming words in the old language now, abandoning caution entirely because caution was dead in this room.

And Wren turning around.

That was the piece that would stay. The child pulling free of Zephyrine's grip and facing the throne, feet planted, small body rigid, and speaking — not loudly. Almost conversationally. But the old language filled the room anyway, poured out of a throat too small for it, syllables stacking on syllables, and every guard dropped as if unplugged. The pressure lifted. The fountains outside went silent, every one, all at once, which should have been impossible.

The king screamed. It was not entirely a human sound, but it was not entirely not one, either, and Serah understood in one lurching instant that both the prisoner and the thing wearing him were feeling the same blade: the binding, the original binding, being *pulled taut* by the last person alive who knew how.

"Wren!" Zephyrine's voice, raw. "*Stop — you're unraveling—*"

The child wasn't stopping. Couldn't, maybe. Their eyes had gone white as birchwood, their hair lifting as though in water, and threads — actual threads, light or memory or something with no name — were coming away from them, drifting upward, dissolving. Each word cost one. Serah could see it happening. Could see the child paying out their own self, strand by strand, buying seconds.

Serah did the only thing left. She sheathed her sword, crossed the killing floor of the throne room under Mavelin's astonished eyes, and picked Wren up.

The words died in the child's mouth. The white drained from their eyes. They weighed almost nothing, and that was the worst of it — that whatever was leaving them was leaving fast, and what remained in her arms was lighter than a child should be.

"Got you," she said. "I've got you. Sleep if you have to. I've got you."

The west door hung open on darkness and stairs. Zephyrine stared at her across the wreckage of the room — at the sleeping guards, at Mavelin frozen mid-step with her hand pressed to her mouth, at the king slumped sideways on his throne like a marionette with cut strings, breathing, weeping without sound, *free* for one shuddering moment of whatever fraction the binding had loosened — and Serah saw the look on her love's face and felt the floor drop out of the world.

It wasn't relief. It was calculation. It was grief doing arithmetic.

"Go," Serah said. "Zeph. *Go.*"

They went — down the service stairs, through the underbelly of the palace, Serah carrying fifty pounds of fading child while Zephyrine ran ahead whispering the map to themself like a prayer. Behind them, distantly, alarms began, real ones, human ones, bells and shouts. Somewhere above, Mavelin was screaming orders in a voice cracked down the middle.

In an alley behind the tanneries, with the city still sleepwalking around them, Serah set Wren down against a wall and finally let herself shake.

The child's eyes opened. They were Wren's eyes again — but dimmed, somehow, like rooms with the lamps carried out. They looked up at Serah, and then past her, to Zephyrine, and their small mouth shaped words in the old language, soft, deliberate.

Zephyrine translated in a whisper, and their voice broke on it:

"*Now you understand. Someone has to hold it. And I am almost too thin to carry anything anymore.*"

Serah looked from the child to Zephyrine — to that terrible gentle face, already composing, already planning, already lying — and knew, with the certainty of a blade going in, that whatever Zephyrine was about to decide, they had decided it in the throne room, in the second it took to watch a king beg.

They would not tell her. That was the worst part. She could already see it beginning.
