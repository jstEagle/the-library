## 2. A Knock in Dead Tongues

The winter solstice came down over the city like a lid.

By late afternoon the snow had thickened to that particular grey wool that made the streets seem to end a few feet beyond the lamplight, and the Guild of Impossible Things drew in around its hearths the way old animals do—shoulders hunched, eyes half-closed, pretending not to count the hours until the year's darkest turning had passed. Quentin Sterling liked this night better than any other in the guild calendar, precisely because nothing was supposed to happen on it. The solstice was the one evening the ledgers closed early, the trade-floor went dark, and even Master Aldery's bell of small brass hung silent on its hook. On the longest night, the guild's founding charter promised, *no bargain shall be struck*, because bargains made in darkness bind twice as deep.

Quentin had spent forty-odd years being grateful for that clause.

She spent it now as she spent most evenings: in the map-room at the top of the guildhouse, with a brazier ticking and a pot of tea going bitter at her elbow, doing work no one had asked her to do because work no one asked for could not be traced back to anyone. Before her lay the great table, oak scarred with decades of compass-points, and across it she had unrolled the newest of her private commissions—a survey of the Amberreach, a coastline that existed only on Tuesdays and had lately begun drifting north. Her pen moved in long sure strokes. Coastlines were easy. Coastlines forgave you their impossibility so long as you drew them honestly.

Below her, the guild settled into its solstice quiet. She could hear the muffled economy of it: the archivist locking the trade-cabinets two floors down, each lock giving its small definitive click like a period at the end of a sentence; the apprentice choir attempting, badly, an old midwinter hymn in the stairwell; someone laughing in the kitchens. The Guild of Impossible Things wore its noise like a coat. Quentin had learned, years ago, to hear through it—to listen past the surface sounds to what the building itself was saying, the creak of its bones, the way its doors spoke when they opened for something they shouldn't have.

That was why, when the knock came, she heard it before anyone else did.

Not heard, exactly. *Felt.* It arrived in her back teeth first, a wrongness like a cracked bell rung once, and then traveled up through the floorboards and into the soles of her feet, and only afterward resolved itself into sound: three knocks at the guild's outer door, spaced with terrible patience. Not the urgent hammering of someone pursued. Not the shy tapping of someone selling grief or shadow or secondhand luck. This was the knocking of something that knew it would be answered eventually and saw no reason to hurry.

Quentin set down her pen.

The trouble with a long life built on careful invisibility, she thought, standing, is that you become very good at noticing when it ends.

* * *

The guildhouse door was black yew, older than the guild itself, carved with the eight symbols of impossible trades—and it opened, on ordinary nights, only to those who already knew how to ask. Quentin descended the stairs slowly, one hand trailing the wall, listening to the building below her hold its breath. The apprentice choir had stopped mid-hymn. The kitchen laughter had cut off like a snuffed candle. Even the fire in the entry hall grate seemed to lean sideways, flames bending toward the door as if drawn by a draft that wasn't there.

Three of the night-porters stood in a knot at the foot of the last staircase, and none of them had moved toward the door. That was wrong too. Answering the door was the porters' whole profession; they took pride in it, in the way men take pride in anything when pride is all the pay they get.

"Who is it?" Quentin asked.

Tammas, the eldest porter, turned to her with a face gone the color of tallow. "We don't know, mistress. There's nobody there." He swallowed. "There's nobody *there*, but it keeps knocking."

Beyond him, through the leaded glass of the door's narrow window, Quentin saw the snow falling in the courtyard, the lamp burning above the gate, the wheel-ruts filled white—and, small and still at the base of the steps, a shape that Tammas's eyes slid over the way water slides over stone.

"It isn't nobody," she said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "It's a child."

She crossed the entry hall. Her own footsteps sounded loud and mortal. When she laid her hand on the yew door's iron ring, the metal bit cold enough to burn, cold enough that she felt it later in her knuckles for days—but she pulled anyway, and the door swung open on the hush of falling snow.

The child stood where the lamplight ended. Seven years old, perhaps eight, small for either. She wore a coat that had been good once, grey wool gone greenish with age, buttoned to the throat, and beneath it a hem of finer cloth that had been cut down to fit her—cut down clumsily, by hands that understood cloth but not children. Her hair was dark and snow-dusted. Her face was pale and watchful and utterly calm, which was somehow worse than tears would have been. Children who knock on strange doors crying can be sent away. Children who knock waiting do not leave until they are let in.

And she was speaking.

She had been speaking, Quentin realized, since before the door opened—that was the wrongness in the knock, that was why it had arrived in the teeth before the ears. The words and the knocks were one thing, syllables struck in wood instead of breath, and the language coming out of her small mouth was one that Quentin had heard exactly once before in her life, thirty-one years ago, in a place where hearing it meant you had already gone too far.

It had no living scholars. It had never needed any. The ones who might have studied it had all, in one way or another, been sold.

"*Serra ven ath,*" the child said. "*Serra ven ath, i lorume.*"

The snow kept falling between them. Behind Quentin, Tammas made a small involuntary sound, the noise a man makes when his skin crawls ahead of his understanding.

"I know," Quentin said, before she could stop herself. "I hear you."

The child stopped. Something moved through her stillness—not surprise, exactly. Recognition, maybe. Or relief arriving so long delayed it had forgotten how to look like anything but exhaustion.

"*Ath serra?*" she said again, slower, and held out one mittened hand, palm up. In it lay a coin. Quentin knew coins; she had catalogued four hundred varieties in the guild's numismatic ledgers, from clipped silver of dead kings to the glass tokens the ferry-ghosts accepted below the Weep. This was none of them. It was black, and warm despite the snow, and shaped like nothing so much as a tooth.

"*I lorume,*" the child repeated, and pressed the coin gently into Quentin's bare hand, and the warmth of it went up Quentin's arm like a memory of summer, like sunlight on water, like—

Like nothing. Like nothing at all. Quentin shut that door inside herself with the ease of long practice.

"You'd better come in," she said in the common tongue, and stepped aside, and the child walked past her into the light.

* * *

They put her in the small room off the kitchens that served for foundlings and difficult guests, and the guild's housekeeper fed her bread and honey and hot milk laced with something mild and dreamless, and the child ate with perfect manners and perfect silence and watched everything. The apprentices came to gawk in twos and threes until Quentin shooed them off. The Guildmaster sent word that he had been informed and would be informed further in the morning, which was Master Aldery's way of saying he wanted no part of it while the year was still dark.

The problem—the only problem that mattered, though the guild would spend the night failing to see it—was the language.

By midnight, half the scholars in the building had trooped through the little room. They brought dictionaries. They brought the lexicon of Old Vessarine, the grammar-sheets of pre-Dynastic Hallow, the fragmentary wordlists salvaged from the drowned archive of Sarn. One enthusiastic fellow fetched the guild's prized possession, a palimpsest three centuries older than the guildhouse itself, whose under-text was said to preserve the liturgy of a faith with no surviving name. The child listened politely to all of it, head tilted, the way one listens to birdsong—pleasant, meaningless.

Nothing fit. Nothing came close. Old Master Corvane, who had spent fifty years proving that every tongue south of the ice could be traced to a common root, sat with the child for an hour and left looking ten years older. "There is no root," he told Quentin in the corridor, quietly, as if the walls were listening. Which, in this building, they sometimes were. "Every language I have ever catalogued descends from something. This one doesn't descend. It just *is*. It's as if—" He stopped himself, and looked at her, and didn't finish, and Quentin was grateful, because she knew exactly how that sentence ended. *As if it came from somewhere that has no ancestors. As if it came up out of the ground.*

Only Quentin could half-follow her.

She did not announce this. She sat with the child after the scholars had given up, in the circle of the little room's stove-warmth, and let the girl talk—because talk she clearly needed to, pouring out that old dark music in a steady stream, patient with Quentin's fumbling answers, correcting her gently with a gesture or a repetition when Quentin guessed wrong. Half-understanding was generous. Quentin caught perhaps one phrase in five, and those mostly by shape rather than sense, the way you recognize a melody heard once, decades ago, in a place you've spent decades refusing to think about.

But some phrases needed no scholarship.

When the child touched her own chest and said a word that rolled low and resonant, like stones settling in deep water, Quentin understood it meant *home*, and understood also that home, for this child, was not anywhere the sun reached.

When the child pointed down—at the floor, through the floor, through the foundations and the cellars and whatever lay beneath the cellars, an unwavering small finger aimed at the heart of the earth—and said "*i lorume*" with an intonation that made it not a place but a summons, a debt called due, Quentin's hands went cold around her teacup and stayed cold.

And when the child, near dawn, took the stub of charcoal from the ledger-tray and drew on the back of a discarded inventory sheet—quick, certain strokes, the unhesitating lines of someone copying from sight rather than imagination—and pushed the paper across to Quentin, Quentin looked at it for a long moment and then rose, calmly, and walked to the window, and opened it, and stood breathing the frozen air until she trusted her voice again.

The drawing showed a mountain, seen from within. A hall of pale stone ribbed like the inside of a shell. And descending into it, small and precise in charcoal, a staircase of ninety-one steps—and at the bottom, rendered with a child's careless accuracy, a veiled figure seated on a throne, and beside the throne, unmistakable even at a stick-figure's scale, a drafting table with a lamp upon it.

A cartographer's station. Waiting.

"Who told you to draw that?" Quentin asked, in the common tongue, though she knew the answer would come in the other one.

The child pointed down again. Through the floor. Through everything.

Then she touched Quentin's wrist—small fingers, terribly gentle—and said a word Quentin had not heard in thirty-one years, and had never once, in all that time, been able to forget. She did not know its full meaning. She knew only the context in which she'd first heard it: spoken softly, almost tenderly, by something vast behind a veil, in a hall of pale stone, to a young woman with ink-stained hands who had just been told the price of walking out alive.

Later—much later, in another life, in the last chapter of all this—Quentin would learn what the word meant. That night, she knew only that it was a name, and that it was hers.

* * *

She did not sleep. She went up to the map-room instead, and lit the brazier, and sat before the great table among her beautiful impossible coastlines, and listened to the guild wake around her into the year's first morning without feeling any of it.

The child slept in the little room downstairs, exhausted at last, clutching the empty teacup like a talisman. The scholars were already composing their theories. By noon there would be papers. By evening, arguments. The Guild of Impossible Things would spend weeks debating the linguistic provenance of the mute-foundling-turned-speaking-child who had knocked on a sealed door on the solstice, and every theory would be wrong, and Quentin would let them all be wrong, because the truth was not a thing this guild survived knowing.

Because Quentin knew the language. Knew it the way you know a scar—by ache, by weather, by the tug of old tissue in cold. Thirty-one years ago she had sworn, on her oath and her craft and the only thing she had ever valued more than her own skin, that she would never map the court beneath the mountain. Never find it. Never speak of it. Never, above all, remember it louder than necessary. She had buried that tongue in the deepest drawer of herself, under layers of ledgers and coastlines and careful, practiced invisibility, and she had lived forty uneventful years on top of the grave.

And now the grave had climbed out, put on a grey coat too fine for it, and knocked three times at the guild door.

*A soul sold to the court beneath the mountain,* she thought, watching snowmelt crawl down the windowpane. The knowledge sat in her chest like swallowed stone. She didn't know yet how she knew it—knew it only the way she half-knew the child's language, by shape and by scar—but she would know it properly soon enough. She would go down to the forbidden back-ledgers, the ones locked behind the archivist's third cabinet, the ones written in the guild's oldest hand. She would search forty years back, to a winter she could not remember ever living through, and she would find the transaction.

And she was afraid—she was afraid down to the marrow, in the old scar-deep way—of whose signature would be waiting at the bottom of the page.
