## 3. What the Guild Sells

The back-ledgers lived behind a door that pretended not to be a door.

Quentin had known this since her first year at the Guild, when she was still young enough to believe that every secret was a gift waiting to be unwrapped. Old Ferrow had shown her the trick of it once, drunk on plum wine and nostalgia, tracing his finger along the seam where the archive wall met the floor. *Everything in this building is a contract, girl. Even the architecture. You just have to read the terms.* The wall opened for those who asked it correctly—not in words, but in intention. You had to want to know something the Guild did not want known, and you had to be willing to carry the weight of it afterward. The door could taste the difference between curiosity and need.

For nineteen years, Quentin had walked past that wall every morning with her need folded small and buried deep, the way one carries a knife through a market crowd.

Tonight she stopped walking.

The guildhouse at midnight was a different animal than the guildhouse at noon. By day it bustled with brokers and scriveners, with clients clutching jars of bottled grief and receipts for borrowed shadows, with the ordinary commerce of impossible things. By night it settled into its true shape: a great sleeping ledger, its corridors lined with shelves that breathed faintly in the dark, paper and vellum and vellum's stranger cousins—hide, bark, slate, one shelf of thin hammered gold that hummed when the moon was full. Quentin moved through it now in her stocking feet, lamp unlit, navigating by the memory of a map she had drawn of these halls in her second year and then burned, because a map of the Guild of Impossible Things was itself an impossible thing, and the Guild punished its own cartography.

The child's voice followed her down the corridor. Not aloud—Wren was asleep upstairs in the infirmary cot, wrapped in two blankets and watched by Sister Anselm, who had been told the girl was a foundling, deaf-mute, shell-shocked by the winter road. A kind lie. Anselm was good at absorbing kind lies; it was half her function.

But the voice followed Quentin anyway, in the way that a language follows the one person who can half-hear it. She had lain awake for two nights turning the syllables over, that old tongue with its consonants like stones dropped down a well, its vowels like the space between heartbeats. The court beneath the mountain. She had not heard that language spoken since she was nineteen years old, and she had never heard it from a living mouth—only from ledgers, from contracts, from the one document she had sworn an apprentice's oath never to seek out.

*You will not map the Undercourt,* she had sworn, hand on the Guild's iron seal, ink still wet on her thumb. *You will not seek its borders, chart its roads, or read its transactions. What the mountain buys, the mountain keeps.*

She had kept that oath for nineteen years. She had kept it through the plague-winter, through Ferrow's death, through every solstice when the guildhouse walls grew cold enough to sweat and the shelves of gold hummed their low moonlit note. She had kept it because keeping it was the price of being allowed to stay, and staying was the price of being someone at all. Quentin Sterling, cartographer. The name was a small thing, a coat she wore. But a coat, worn long enough, grows into the skin.

The child had no coat at all. The child had been sold.

She had not known that for certain until tonight. She had suspected it from the first moment, from the way the girl's eyes had gone to the guildhouse door and *recognized* it—not the wonder of a stranger, but the flat, weary familiarity of something returning to a place it had been taken from. And suspicion, in a cartographer, is a disease. It spreads. It demands surveying.

Quentin stood before the false wall and let her need unfold.

* * *

The door tasted her for a long time. She felt it the way one feels a hand hovering an inch from one's cheek—not touch, but the promise of touch, the held breath. The archive was old beyond the Guild's own founding, older than the charter, older than the name. Nobody knew who had built it. The prevailing theory among the senior brokers was that the Guild had not founded the archive; the archive had founded the Guild, the way a spider does not found a web so much as become the center of one.

Then the seam sighed, and stone became shadow, and Quentin stepped through into the dark that smelled of dust and iron and, beneath both, something sweeter and far worse: the smell of a memory being kept too long, like fruit in a sealed jar.

She lit her lamp.

The back-ledgers were arranged not by date or by client but by weight. This was the first thing they taught you, if they taught you at all: that transactions had mass, and the mass varied. A bottled grief weighed less than a stolen name. A borrowed shadow weighed less than a grief. And a soul—she had never seen the shelf where souls were kept, had never let herself look, though she had known since her oath that the shelf existed, somewhere in the deepest dark, that it was not a shelf at all but something structural, load-bearing, that the whole Guild of Impossible Things rested upon the commerce in souls the way a cathedral rests on the bones of its saints.

She walked past the light transactions. Past griefs and shadows, past a case of secondhand courage and a rack of unspent regrets. Past the ledgers of names, each volume bound in the skin of the name it contained—don't ask whose skin, Ferrow had said, and she never had. The lamps along the aisle burned lower as she went, not for want of oil but for want of permission, until she reached the boundary where the ordinary dark ended and the dark that *watched* began.

There was a gate. Of course there was a gate. It was iron, and it was low—waist-height, absurdly low, a gate a child could step over—and that was the whole trick of it. The gate was not a barrier. It was a question. *What are you willing to step over?* Quentin had stood before it once, at nineteen, with Ferrow's hand heavy on her shoulder, and she had turned back, and Ferrow had said, *Good girl,* and bought her dinner, and she had spent nineteen years telling herself the turning-back was wisdom.

She stepped over it.

The air changed the way air changes when a storm that has been deciding finally decides. Her lamp flame stood up straight and went the color of old honey. Ahead of her, the shelves changed too: no longer wood but something darker, denser, grown rather than built, and the volumes on them were not bound in skin but in *absence*—she could not have said how she knew, only that her eyes kept sliding off their spines, refusing to report what they saw, the way the eye refuses to look at the sun.

And at the end of the aisle, on a lectern of black stone, open to the air as though it had been waiting, lay a ledger.

She had not been led here. She was almost sure she had not been led here. But the lectern stood in the aisle's end the way an answer stands at the end of a question, and Quentin was too much a cartographer to pretend she hadn't noticed the geography of it: that the archive had arranged itself so that the thing she sought was the thing she would find.

*Everything in this building is a contract. You just have to read the terms.*

She put her hands on the ledger and read.

* * *

The script was old Guild-hand, the formal clerk's cursive that had fallen out of use two decades before Quentin's apprenticeship, and the transaction it recorded was dated in the autumn of a year forty-one years gone. The entry was brief, as the worst entries always are. Quentin had spent her career reading contracts, and she knew the grammar of cruelty: the crueller the trade, the fewer the words.

*Received: one soul, juvenile, female, approximately seven years of age. Condition: intact. Language: pre-regnal. Delivered by broker of this Guild under seal. Consideration rendered: see attached.*

*See attached.*

There was no attachment. There never was. The attachment was the kind of thing the Guild kept in the place where the gold shelves hummed, or the place where the dark watched, or the place that was not a place at all. Quentin read the entry three times, and each time her eye snagged on the same detail, the one that was not the child's age or the child's language or the cold economy of *condition: intact*—a phrase that would live behind her eyes for the rest of her life, she was sure of it, the way a splinter lives under skin.

The snag was the signature.

Every transaction in the back-ledgers bore two marks: the broker's signature and the client's mark, since most who sold such things could not or would not write. The client's mark on this entry was a simple pressed thumbprint, long faded to the brown of old tea. The broker's signature, though—

Quentin knew handwriting the way other people knew faces. It was her trade and her obsession; she had authenticated more documents than she could count, had traced the evolution of a forger's tremor across a decade of contracts, had once identified a dead man by a signature alone. Handwriting was a map of the hand that made it, and the hand was a map of the person, and she had built her whole careful invisible life on the principle that maps do not lie, only mappers do.

The signature read *M. Sterling.*

She did not move for a long time. The lamp flame stood in its honey-colored stillness. Somewhere far above, the guildhouse settled on its foundations with a sound like a sigh, or a laugh.

It was not her signature. That was the first thought, the sane thought, the thought she clung to with both hands. It could not be. She was thirty-eight years old; the transaction predated her birth by three years. Whatever quirk of ink or coincidence had produced those letters, it was not—

But she was already reading it the way she read everything, professionally, mercilessly, and the reading was killing her by inches. The *M* with its deep initial stroke and the little hesitation at the crown, where the writer's hand always paused—she paused there too, had paused there her whole life, had been teased about it in her apprenticeship by a boy who forged seals and called it her *thinking-hook*. The *S* that leaned too far forward, eager, overreaching, then corrected itself with a small guilty loop. The *g* that descended in a straight, unornamented tail because she had never had patience for flourishes, had been told a hundred times that her hand was too plain for a Guild scrivener, too honest, too legible, as if legibility were a sin.

And the *t* in *Sterling*, crossed not once but with a second, fainter stroke above the first—a double-cross, a habit she had never seen in another hand in her life, had never thought about, had never *had* to think about, because who thinks about the crossing of their own t's? Who thinks about breathing? Who thinks about the shape of their own name until the shape of it is staring up at them from a ledger of the sold, dated three years before they were born?

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange, floating detachment, as though they belonged to someone else—someone standing in a dark archive reading a document that could not exist.

*It's a copy,* she told herself. *It's a forgery. Someone signed my name—someone who knew my hand, who—*

Who? Who had known her hand well enough to counterfeit its most private habits? Who had access to the deep archive? Who would bother, and why, and to what end—to frame a woman who did not yet exist? The thought collapsed under its own weight even as she thought it. Forgeries were made to deceive someone. This signature deceived no one but her, and it did not deceive; it *accused*. It sat at the bottom of the entry like a verdict, like a mirror held up to a face she suddenly could not be sure was her own.

Because here was the thing she could not stop thinking, the thing that turned her blood to well-water, cold and slow: the handwriting was not *like* hers. It was not an imitation, not a cousin, not a forgery with the tells that all forgeries have—the tremor of effort, the over-careful letterforms. It was hers. It was hers the way her reflection was hers. The pen-pressure matched. The spacing matched. The small, unlovely honesty of it matched. If she had sat down at this lectern forty years ago, in this dark, with this ledger open before her, and written her own name—

She would have written exactly this.

The vertigo came then, the true vertigo, not of heights but of ground that proves to be no ground. She gripped the lectern's edge. The black stone was cold as a riverbed. Forty years. A soul sold to the court beneath the mountain, forty years ago, in her hand. A child of seven, sold, and the child who had come knocking on the solstice door was—what? Seven still. Unaged. *Condition: intact.* The mountain kept what it bought, and it kept it whole, and it kept it *young*, and the arithmetic of that was a door opening in the floor of everything Quentin believed about her own life.

She had not done this. She could not have done this. And yet her name—her actual, particular, unrepeatable name, in her actual, particular, unrepeatable hand—sat at the bottom of the sale like a stain that would not wash.

Unless.

Unless the name was not a signature at all. Unless it was something older and stranger. She was a cartographer of impossible things; she knew that in the deep trades, names were not labels but *debts*, that a name could be written in a ledger by a hand that did not yet know it owned the name, that time in the back-archive did not run in one direction any more than a river runs in one direction once it reaches the sea. She had seen contracts dated before their signing. She had seen receipts for things not yet delivered. The Guild's oldest ledgers were not records of the past; they were *maps* of it, and maps, she had sworn on the iron seal, could show places the walker had not reached.

If that was true—if the ledger was not accusing her of a crime but *forecasting* a debt—then somewhere ahead of her, or beside her, or in a year that had not happened yet, Quentin Sterling would stand in this dark and sell a child to the mountain.

Or had already. Or was, in some sense that made her teeth ache, doing it now.

*No.* She said it aloud, and the archive swallowed the word without echo, the way it swallowed everything. *No. I came here to help her. I came here to find who sold her.*

She looked down at the entry again, and this time she made herself read the consideration line, the *see attached*, and made herself look past it to the final line of the transaction, the one she had been avoiding, the clerk's closing formula that appeared at the end of every deep trade:

*Let the seller forget the price, and the sold forget the sale, until the mountain calls the debt.*

The mountain had called the debt. It had called it in a dead language, through a child's mouth, on the winter solstice, at the door of the very guild that had sold her. And it had called it, Quentin now understood with a certainty that felt like falling, to the one person in the world whose name was already written in the ledger of the sale.

She closed the ledger. The sound was very small and very final, a door shutting in a house with no other doors.

Then she stood in the watching dark with her honey-colored lamp and did the only thing a cartographer could do with ground that would not hold still: she began, against every oath, every instinct, every scar, to survey it. The child. The mountain. The signature that was and was not hers. And threaded through all of it, faint as a watermark, the question she had been avoiding since the solstice night, the question the ledger had now made unavoidable:

If her name was in this book—what else about her was for sale?

She turned back up the aisle, past the gate she had stepped over, past the shelves of griefs and shadows and unspent regrets, and she did not see the lectern behind her, in the dark, as the ledger opened itself again—quietly, patiently, to a page near the end, where a newer entry waited in fresh ink, undated, its consideration line reading only: *see attached.*

Its signature had not yet dried.
