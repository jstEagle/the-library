## 5. The Child Who Remembers Forward

The child would not sleep.

Quentin had given her the cot in the map-room's back alcove, the one reserved for apprentices too exhausted to walk home, and had layered her with two blankets and a quilt that smelled of cedar and old paper. The guild was quiet around them, that particular hush of a building full of sleeping impossibilities—bottled grief settling like sediment, the borrowed shadow in the third-floor closet rustling against its moth-eaten walls. Quentin sat at her desk with a lamp turned low and pretended to work on a survey of the Fenmarsh causeway while she listened to the small sounds of a child who was not sleeping.

She had named her Wren because of the sound she made when she finally did speak—not words, not yet, but a soft chittering note of alarm when Quentin first reached toward her, like a bird startled from hedgerow cover. The name fit. Small bones, quick eyes, a stillness that was never really still.

The language she spoke had no name that any living scholar knew. But Quentin half-understood it—the way one half-understands a song heard once in childhood, the melody intact and the meaning just out of reach. *Beneath,* the child said, over and over that first night. *Cold. Counting.* And then a word that made the hair on Quentin's arms rise: a word she could not translate but whose shape her mouth remembered, as though her tongue had been built to pronounce it long before she learned to fear it.

*Undercourt.*

Now, past midnight, the scratching started.

At first Quentin mistook it for mice. Then she recognized it: charcoal on paper. She rose without letting the floorboard sing—thirty years of practice—and crossed to the alcove.

Wren was sitting up in the dark, blankets pooled around her waist, drawing by no light at all. Her hand moved with the certainty of someone copying, not creating. Charcoal strokes laid themselves down in swift, sure lines, and Quentin felt her cartographer's heart clench with an ugly, involuntary envy: the child drew the way Quentin had always dreamed of drawing, without hesitation, without correction, as if the image already existed somewhere and needed only to be let out.

"What are you making?" Quentin asked softly, in Guild-tongue, and then, feeling foolish, tried the shapeless syllables of the child's own dead speech.

Wren looked up. In the darkness her eyes caught what little light there was and held it, the way certain stones do. She turned the paper toward Quentin.

It was this room. The map-room, rendered in a child's unskilled but unnervingly accurate hand—the desk, the alcove, the cot, even the lamp with its low-turned flame. And standing where Quentin stood now, drawn in charcoal so fresh it smudged at the edge of the page, was a figure Quentin knew intimately: herself, hunched slightly at the left shoulder, ink stains on the right cuff.

But the figure was wrong. The figure was *lying down*, on the floor of the map-room, and Wren had drawn the shadow beneath it spreading outward like spilled water, and above the prone shape she had drawn a mountain.

"Where did you learn to draw?" Quentin asked, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

Wren answered in her dead tongue, three syllables, patient, the tone adults use with the slow-witted. Then she pointed at the picture. Then at the window, through which, far off, past the rooftops of the lower city, the mountain called the Crown stood against the stars, snowlit, silent.

Then she pointed at Quentin.

---

In the morning Quentin told herself it was coincidence. She was very good at telling herself things; she had been doing it for decades. Children drew strange pictures. Children drew death all the time—it was how they practiced their powerlessness. A prone figure, a mountain. Any grief-counselor in the lower wards could have explained it in terms of displacement and borrowed anxiety.

But she was a cartographer. She dealt in exactitudes. And the room in the drawing was exact: the crack in the plaster above the alcove shaped like a river delta; the desk leg mended with wire; the ink stain on the right cuff, which Wren could only have seen if she had been studying Quentin for days, cataloguing her the way Quentin catalogued everything else.

Quentin spent the morning doing what she always did when frightened: she worked. She corrected the Fenmarsh survey. She logged a trade—a widow trading forty years of Sunday serenity for one hour of her husband's voice, a fair exchange, both parties weeping and satisfied. She filed the receipt. She was invisible. She was safe.

Wren followed her.

Not obviously. The child had a talent for being unobtrusive that Quentin recognized with a lurch of kinship—here was another creature who had survived by being overlooked. Wren drifted through the guild's corridors like dust motes drift through light: present everywhere, noticed nowhere. The other clerks glanced at her and away, the way people glance at something they've agreed not to see. Only Old Maren, who kept the bottled-grief cellar, stopped once and looked at Wren for a long moment, and then looked at Quentin, and said nothing, which from Maren was a paragraph.

At midday Quentin set paper and charcoal in front of the child at the long worktable, partly as kindness, partly as experiment, mostly as trap. "Draw me something," she said. "Anything. Something you saw yesterday."

Wren drew the widow's trade. The bottle, the two figures, the weeping—accurate down to the widow's crooked second finger, which Quentin had noted in the ledger and no one else would have remarked.

"Now draw me tomorrow."

Wren's hand paused. For the first time since arriving at the guild, the child looked afraid—not of Quentin, Quentin realized with a cold drop in her stomach, but of what her own hand might do. She looked at Quentin's face for permission, or absolution, or warning. Quentin gave none of these. She only nodded, because a part of her already knew, the way a cartographer knows the contour lines are going to close into a peak before the survey confirms it.

Wren drew the guild's front door, open. Snow coming in sideways. A tall figure in the doorway shaking snow from a coat, faceless because Wren hadn't seen the face yet—but wearing the guildmaster's chain of office, unmistakable, the links drawn link by link with a child's obsessive care.

Tomorrow, the guildmaster would come to the map-room. Tomorrow, snow.

Outside the window, the sky over the lower city hung grey and heavy, undecided.

Quentin looked at the drawing for a long time. "And the day after tomorrow?" she asked, very quietly.

Wren shook her head slowly and put down the charcoal. Then she picked it up again, and drew one more thing, quickly, as if to get it over with: a small bird, round-bodied, blunt-beaked, flying toward the door of the drawing rather than away from it.

A wren, coming in.

Quenting laughed—one short, cracked syllable that surprised them both. The child watched her laugh with grave attention, head tilted, as though filing the sound away in whatever ledger children keep instead of memories.

"All right," Quentin said. "All right. Wren. That's your name now. Wren." She pointed at the little bird on the page, and then at the girl.

Wren considered this. Then she did something that undid Quentin more thoroughly than the drawings had: she smiled. Not a child's easy smile—this one was careful, rationed, the smile of someone who has learned smiles cost something. But she spent it anyway, on Quentin, freely.

And Quentin, who had spent thirty years ensuring that nothing in the world had a claim on her, felt the claim settle over her like a yoke fitted to shoulders that had forgotten they were made for bearing.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You know what happens to things you keep.*

* * *

That night Wren dreamed aloud.

Quentin woke to the sound of it—small voice moving through the dark in the old language, rising and falling in patterns that were almost prayer, almost inventory. She lay in her own narrow bed in the clerks' quarters and listened, and understood fragments, and hated every fragment.

*Beneath. Cold. Counting.* The same words as the first night. But now, threaded among them, new ones. One meant—or seemed to mean—*the scales.* Another, *the veil.* Another came back to Quentin half-translated and wholly terrible: *she draws us out.*

Quentin got up. She lit the lamp. She went to the locked cabinet where she kept the maps that were not maps—the ones the guild pretended didn't exist, the surveys of places that refused to be surveyed—and she took out the oldest folder, the one with no label, and she did not open it. She held it. She held it the way you hold a wound you've decided not to look at.

Through the wall, faintly, Wren's dreaming voice said a word Quentin's mouth had been built to pronounce and her mind refused to house.

She put the folder back. She locked the cabinet. She went and stood in the doorway of the alcove and watched the child sleep, curled tight, fists closed, and thought about the drawing on the desk: the prone figure, the mountain, the shadow spreading like spilled water.

*She draws us out,* the dream-voice had said. As if someone beneath the mountain were waiting to be drawn. As if the child had been sent up not to escape but to fetch.

"No," Quentin said aloud, to the sleeping child, to the mountain beyond the glass, to whoever or whatever kept ledgers older than kingdoms. "Whatever you're fishing for, I'm not biting."

The child turned over in her sleep and, without waking, reached one small hand out of the blankets—an unconscious gesture, blind, trusting, the gesture of someone reaching for a rope thrown into dark water.

Quentin stood in the doorway a long time, not taking it, not leaving.

Then she took it.

The hand was cold as the underside of a stone. It closed around Quentin's fingers with surprising strength, and Wren sighed once, deeply, the sigh of someone arriving safely after a long journey, and did not let go until dawn.

Quentin did not sleep. She sat on the edge of the cot with a child's hand wrapped around hers and stared at the mountain through the window, and made lists in her head the way she always did when terror needed organizing: *the ledger, the back-ledgers, the transaction forty years gone, the signature in my own hand.* She had not yet dared to search the forbidden volumes—that was tomorrow's cowardice, or tomorrow's courage, depending on how she framed it when the lamp was lit.

For now there was only this: warmth, weight, trust, and a picture drying on the desk of her own body on the floor of her own room with a mountain standing over it like a judge.

*Tenderness,* Quentin thought, *is just terror with better manners.*

She stayed until the sky went grey. When Wren woke, the first thing the child did was check the drawing of the guildmaster's arrival—still accurate, still waiting—then look up at Quentin and say, clearly, in the dead tongue, pointing at the mountain:

Two words. Quentin understood neither. Understood both.

The next day, the guildmaster came early, shaking snow from his coat, and asked Quentin why there was a child in his guild, and Quentin opened her mouth to lie—

and found, to her horror, that she had already decided to tell the truth.
