## 9. The Ferryman's Toll

The river appeared on the third morning, exactly where the road said it wouldn't be.

That was the trouble with roads that unwrote themselves: they kept their own counsel. Quentin had mapped this stretch once, years ago, in a fever of ambition she now recognized as her younger self's particular madness—a survey of the Thornmarch hinterlands, all grey wash and careful hatching, every ford and ferry crossing noted in her neat, upright hand. That map was ash now, or worse than ash, because the road had eaten the landmarks behind them one by one, and Quentin had learned to stop looking back. A cartographer who looks back on this road goes mad, the ferryman's cousin had told her once, in another life, in another ledger. Or worse: she starts drawing what isn't there anymore.

So she didn't look back. She looked at Wren instead, who was walking ahead with her arms out along a fallen log like a tightrope artist, utterly unafraid of the drop into the ravine, and Quentin felt that familiar tightening in her chest that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with how much a person could come to matter in six days.

"Careful," she said, in the old tongue, the one that lived under kingdoms. *Slow-heart. The stones are lying about their footing.*

Wren glanced back and grinned, and answered in the same language, her hands flying as much as her mouth. The child spoke with her whole body; the words were only the half of it. *Stones don't lie. Stones don't know how.* Then, with the terrible directness of seven-year-olds everywhere: *Are you scared of the river?*

"I'm not scared of rivers," Quentin said, and was surprised to find it true. Rivers she understood. Rivers had banks, and banks could be mapped. It was everything on the far side of this one that made her palms sweat.

The river was wide and slow and the color of strong tea, and it did not make any sound. That was the first wrong thing. Rivers should sound like something—should whisper or roar or at least mutter to themselves. This one moved in perfect silence, a great dark ribbon sliding between reed banks, and the silence coming off it was thick enough to lean against. Quentin had heard of this water. Every mapmaker had, in the way that priests have heard of hell: as a caution, a rumor, a place that appeared on no chart because no chart could hold it. The River Unnamed. It flowed out of the mountain, the old accounts said, and back into it, and it carried away anything without a name to hold it to the world.

Wren had gone still at the bank. Not frightened—attentive, the way she went still before her prophecies came, her small face turning inward as though she were reading something written on the inside of her own skull.

"He's coming," she said aloud, in the trade-tongue, which she almost never did. "The boat man. He's sad today."

*How do you know?* Quentin signed, because her voice had gone strange in her throat.

Wren pointed at the water. Quentin looked, and saw nothing but the tea-dark current, and then, far downstream, a smudge that resolved into a flat-bottomed boat, and a figure standing at the stern with a long pole, coming toward them against the current without any apparent effort, as though the river were carrying him as a favor.

* * *

He was younger than Quentin expected, and that was the second wrong thing. In her imagination—built from guild gossip and the margins of forbidden maps—the ferryman of the Unnamed was ancient, hooded, faceless, a skeleton with a ledger. This man had a windburned face, a beard going grey at the chin, and eyes the same color as the water. He poled the boat to the bank and held it steady with one foot and looked at them both for a long moment.

"Cartographer," he said. Not a question. "I can smell the ink on you. It's in your blood now, that smell. It doesn't wash out."

"Then you know why I've come." Quentin kept her voice level. Beside her, Wren had taken her hand without being asked, the way she did, small fingers lacing through Quentin's with the confidence of someone who had decided, long ago and in some other place, that this particular adult was hers.

"I know where you're going." The ferryman's gaze moved to Wren and something in his face shifted—softened, then shuttered, like a window closing against weather. "And I know what's owed for the crossing. You'll have heard."

"I've heard stories."

"Stories are what people tell each other so they don't have to say the true thing." He stepped aside, gesturing at the boat's single bench. "The river takes memory. Not all of it. One piece, weighed and chosen. You pay, you cross. You don't pay—" he shrugged, "—and you can walk around the river. It's only four hundred miles to the fords, and the fords are watched by things I don't name on my own water. Your choice."

Quentin looked at the boat. Flat, black-tarred, with a lantern hanging at the prow that burned without flame, giving off a light like the memory of daylight. She thought about the road behind them, the erased milestones, the inn that had not been there when they walked back for it. She thought about Wren's pictures, the ones she drew and then hid under her cloak: the mountain with a door in its root, the long stairs down, and the one Quentin had made her stop drawing, the one with a figure that wore Quentin's coat, lying still, deep under stone.

"How is it chosen?" she asked. "The memory. Do I choose, or does the river?"

"You choose," the ferryman said. "That's the mercy of it. And the cruelty. The river doesn't want just anything. It wants weight. It'll know if you try to pay it with something light—what you ate on your ninth birthday, the name of a neighbor's dog. It'll refuse the coin and take the hand." He said it without drama, a man stating the fare. "You have to give it something that hurts to lose. That's the toll. That's always been the toll."

Wren tugged Quentin's sleeve. When Quentin knelt, the child signed close and quick, her fingers sharp with urgency: *Don't give it me. I know what you're thinking. Don't.*

"I wasn't," Quentin lied, because she had been, for one flickering instant—wondering whether a child's memories, seven years of them, might weigh enough, and hating herself with a thoroughness that felt almost cleansing. "I wasn't thinking that."

"You were." Wren's eyes were the grey of the river and just as unreadable. "But you stopped. That's the difference between you and the ones who sold me." She said it simply, without accusation, the way she said everything, and somehow that made it worse. Then she kissed Quentin's cheek, a quick fierce press of small lips, and went to sit in the boat, and began, with the unbothered patience of childhood, to fold a paper boat out of a scrap of the map-fragment they carried.

The ferryman watched the child with that same shuttered look. "She's not like other children," he said quietly.

"No."

"She remembers things that haven't happened."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something he'd long suspected. "Then she'll know what the crossing costs. They usually do." He turned to Quentin. "Sit down, cartographer. Pay your fare. The light's failing, and the river doesn't like to be crossed in the dark. Neither do I."

* * *

The middle of the river was the middle of nowhere, and Quentin meant that precisely. She had spent her whole life in places—had defined herself against them, had measured her worth in the accuracy with which she could fix a thing in space, this city here, that mountain there, this life at these coordinates. But the middle of the Unnamed was a place that refused to be placed. The banks had slid out of sight. The lantern's memory-light made a small warm room around the boat, and beyond it was not darkness exactly but *absence*, a nothing so complete it pressed on the eyes. There was no wind. There was no sound, still, except the soft knock of the pole against the boat's rim, and Wren's paper boat, which she set on the water, where it floated without drifting, obedient to nothing.

"Where does the river go?" Quentin asked, mostly to hear a voice.

"Out of the mountain and into it," the ferryman said. "It's the mountain's blood. Everything it takes, it carries home." He looked at her sidelong. "You're going to give it something good. I can tell. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face of someone deciding which finger to cut off." He poled, unhurried. "I'll tell you how it works, since the stories never do. You hold the memory in your mind—all of it, every corner, don't hide anything, the river tastes concealment like a fisherman tastes a hook. You hold it, and you let it go, and you say its weight. And then it's gone. Not dimmed. Not faded. Gone, like it never was. And here's the part nobody warns you about—" the boat rocked gently, though the water was still, "—you won't know what you lost. You'll know *that* you lost. There'll be a shape in you where it was, like a tooth-socket. You'll feel the edges of it on bad days. But the thing itself—" he snapped his fingers, and the sound was swallowed whole, "—gone. People pay and then spend the rest of their lives grieving without knowing for what. I've ferried ten thousand across. I've watched them all arrive on the far bank patting their pockets, checking for something they can't name."

"That's monstrous," Quentin said.

"It's a toll," the ferryman said. "Monstrous would be if it took without asking." He brought the pole around. "Choose now. We're at the deep place."

* * *

Here is what Quentin knew about herself, in the way that a woman knows the outside of her own house: she knew the streets she walked, the ledgers she kept, the careful grey life she had built at the Guild of Impossible Things, all small trades and smaller mercies, invisibility worn like a well-tailored coat. She knew she was good at her work and careful with her heart and that she flinched, in a way she had never examined, from certain things. Music boxes. The smell of plum brandy. Snow falling through lamplight. And lullabies—God, lullabies. She had crossed a tavern in Vell once, mid-winter, to beg the singer to stop, and had not been able to say why, and had seen her own stricken face reflected in a dark window and thought: *someone else's grief is living in me. Someone else left it here.*

She had always assumed it was a scar from the plague-winters. Everyone had scars from the plague-winters.

Now, sitting in a boat on a river with no name, holding the stolen vial of first-memory ink against her sternum like a second heart, she understood with sudden terrible clarity that she was about to make a choice with only half the information, and that the ferryman's toll was not a toll at all but a question: *what are you willing to lose, when you don't know what you have?*

She reached into herself the way she reached into her satchel, feeling past the top layers—the road, Wren's hand in hers, the Guildmaster's face when he refused her—and down into the cellar places, the ones she never opened. And there, in the dark, she found it: a warm shape, humming. A memory that was not one memory but a braid of them, wound tight as rope. A voice, low, half-singing. Hands. Snow through lamplight. The smell of plum brandy. A name—

The name was the center of it. She could feel the name at the braid's heart the way you can feel a stone at the center of a peach, and she reached for it, and it was *right there*, on the tip of her mind's tongue—

And Wren, without looking up from her paper boat, said, in the old tongue, very quietly: *Whatever you're holding—put it down. That one's not yours to pay. That one's yours to keep.*

Quentin froze. "What did you say?"

The child didn't answer. She never answered when it mattered.

The river waited. The ferryman waited, his pole stilled, his river-colored eyes patient as water. And Quentin understood that she had perhaps a minute, perhaps less, and that if she did not pay, they would walk four hundred miles around a river that flowed out of the mountain they needed to enter, and Wren's pictures would come true on the road instead of at the door, and none of it—none of it—would be her fault, which would be the worst consolation in the world.

She put the braided memory back. Carefully. The way you put down a sleeping child.

And she reached for the next thing.

It came easier than it should have. That was the horror of it, looking back: how smoothly her hand closed around a memory she had never catalogued, never guarded, because she hadn't known it was precious. A winter evening. A room with a fire. A woman's voice—*not the braided voice, another voice, or was it the same voice, she couldn't tell, the two were tangled like roots*—singing something low and looping, a lullaby in a language that was almost the old tongue but not quite, and a small weight against her chest, and the feeling—the feeling, God, the feeling, which had a name, which had always had a name, which was—

"Let it go," the ferryman said gently. "Say its weight."

Quentin let it go.

She said: "It weighs everything. It weighs the whole winter. It weighs—" but the sentence was already dissolving in her mouth, the words coming apart like sugar in water, and the river took it, took all of it, the fire and the voice and the small warm weight and the name of the feeling, and the silence where it had been opened inside her like a door into a cold room.

The boat moved. The far bank was suddenly there, reeds and grey shingle and, beyond them, the foothills, and above the foothills, for the first time, the mountain itself—vast, blue-black, wearing its snow like a crown of salt. The door in its root was too far away to see. But she could feel it, the way you feel a tooth-socket. The way you feel a missing thing.

Quentin patted her pockets.

There was nothing missing from her pockets. There was nothing missing from anything she could name. And yet she was crying—had been crying, apparently, for some time, her face wet and her throat raw—and she did not know why, and that not-knowing was the cruelest thing the river could have done, because grief needs an object the way a river needs banks, and hers had none, and so it simply spread, wide and slow and silent, filling every place in her that the memory had emptied.

"Cartographer." The ferryman had beached the boat and was looking at her with something that in another man might have been pity. "It gets quieter. The shape of it. In a year you won't notice it every day. Only on bad days. Only when someone sings."

Wren took her hand. Wren, who remembered forward, who had told her *that one's not yours to pay*, looked up at her with the river's grey eyes and did not say *I told you so*, and did not say *I'm sorry*, and did not explain. She only held Quentin's hand very tightly and walked with her up the shingle, and when, that night, in the shelter of the foothills, a nightbird began its looping evening song, Quentin flinched so hard she spilled the tea from her cup—and Wren was there instantly, small arms around her, holding on, humming nothing at all.

* * *

They made the foothills by dark and camped in the lee of a standing stone that was not on any map Quentin had ever drawn, because it had not been there when the maps were made. The mountain filled half the sky now. Its snowfields caught the last light and held it long after the valleys went dark, so that the peak seemed to burn coldly, a crown of veiled fire, and Quentin lay awake beneath it, patting her pockets, patting her memory, finding the shape of the loss and pressing her tongue against it like a sore tooth.

She tried to reconstruct the evening. She had been somewhere warm. There had been snow. There had been a voice. She built the scene the way she built maps, from inference and contour, and got exactly as far as anyone could get mapping a country they'd never seen: a shape, an outline, a blank space where the heart of it should be.

*Blank spaces are debts,* her first master had told her, forty years and one lifetime ago. *Every empty place on a map is a promise someone hasn't kept.*

She had a new blank space now. She could feel it owing.

Beside her, Wren slept with her back against the standing stone, and in the child's loose fingers, folded small enough to hide in a fist, was a scrap of paper Quentin hadn't seen her draw. Quentin eased it free and held it to the coals' light.

It was a picture of two women, drawn in Wren's sure, strange hand, sitting in a garden that had a wall around it and a gate that was open. One woman had Quentin's crooked nose. The other woman's face was left unfinished—just the outline, the features empty, a blank space in the middle of the picture.

And beneath it, in the old tongue, in Wren's careful child's letters, a single line:

*You will meet her again at the door, and she will not know you, and it will be the worst day, and it will be the beginning.*

Quentin sat very still, the paper trembling in her hand, the mountain burning above her, and somewhere inside her the shape of the loss ached like weather coming, and she thought: *Who? Who is she? Who did I pay away?*

The coals settled. The nightbird sang its looping song, and Quentin did not flinch this time, because there was nothing left in her for it to strike.

She folded the picture away, lay back against the cold stone, and watched the mountain's veiled crown burn against the stars until sleep took her—down, and down, and down, toward the door in its root.
