## 10. Beneath the Mountain's Skin

Wren had been dreaming of the door for as long as she could remember, which was longer than most people could remember anything.

It was not a door of wood or stone or iron. It was a door made of all the words that had never been said — every apology swallowed in a throat, every confession turned to ash on the tongue, every *I love you* that died unspoken in some dark room while its speaker looked out a window instead. They drifted together beneath the mountain like snow settling in a valley no one visited, and where they gathered they pressed against one another until they took shape: a tall archway of grey and silver, soft-edged, humming faintly, like a mouth about to speak.

Wren stood before it with her mittened hand in Quentin's, and she was not afraid.

She should have said something to explain this to the woman beside her. But Wren's words came out strange in the air above the ground — the old tongue fit poorly up there, like feet grown for water walking on stone — and besides, Quentin was busy being afraid enough for both of them. Wren could feel it coming off her in waves, the way heat came off a stove. The woman's jaw was set. Her eyes kept moving, measuring things. Measuring was what Quentin did instead of weeping, Wren had decided. She did it very well.

*The door knows you,* Wren signed, because her hands were honest even when her mouth was not. She had taught Quentin six signs on the road, clumsily, patiently, the way you teach a bird to trust a windowsill. *The door knows everyone. It just doesn't always open.*

Quentin looked down at her. In the blue light leaking from between the unsaid words, her face looked older than it had in the ferryman's warm kitchen, older than it had on the road, older maybe than it was. "What does it want?" she asked, and her voice came out smaller than usual.

Wren considered this seriously. Doors were serious business. She held up one finger — *wait* — and stepped forward alone, her boots crunching on frost that hadn't been there a moment ago, because the mountain made its own weather from other people's cold silences.

Up close, the door was beautiful. Words moved through it like fish under ice. Some were long and slow and heavy, words that had waited forty years to be said and would wait forty more. Some were small and quick, darting near the surface — *sorry*, *stay*, *please* — flashing and gone. And threaded all through them, deep in the middle where the oldest silences lived, Wren could see shapes that weren't words at all anymore, only pressure, only weight, the places where someone had needed so badly to speak that the not-speaking had become a solid thing.

She pressed her palm flat against the arch.

The door sighed.

It was not a sound exactly. It was the feeling of a held breath finally released after years, and it moved through Wren's arm and into her chest and settled there, warm, familiar, like a blanket that still remembered another body's shape. The words nearest her hand rearranged themselves. She couldn't read them — reading was a surface thing, a thing with letters — but she knew what they were doing. They were checking her. Turning her over the way you turn over a coin to see whose face is stamped on it.

And then, softly, in the language older than any kingdom, the door spoke to her.

*Little courier. You've come home ahead of schedule.*

*I brought her,* Wren answered, in the same tongue, the words rising in her throat easily here, easy as breathing underwater must be. *The cartographer. She doesn't know yet what she is.*

*They never do.* A ripple passed through the archway, almost like laughter, almost like grief. *The gate will take her name. Are you certain?*

Wren looked back at Quentin. The woman stood very straight, the way people do when they are frightened and refuse to show it, her satchel of stolen ink hugged to her chest like a child of her own. Her breath made small ghosts in the cold. She had fed Wren soup on the ferryman's boat without being asked. She had slept sitting up, between Wren and the door of the cabin, all night, every night, even though she must have been tired. She flinched at lullabies now — Wren had noticed, and noticed that she didn't know why she flinched, and thought that was the saddest thing she had ever seen, sadder than anything in the pictures she drew.

*She'll give it,* Wren said. *She gives things. That's what she's for.*

The door hummed, considering. Then the words at its center parted, slowly, the way a crowd parts for a funeral, and there was darkness beyond — but not empty darkness. Darkness with a warmth in it, far down, like a hearth seen through a keyhole. Like a whole city of hearths.

"Wait." Quentin's voice cracked on the word. She crossed the frost in three strides and knelt, taking Wren by the shoulders, and her hands were shaking. "Listen to me. Once we're inside, everything I've read says the same thing — names are currency down there. If anyone asks for yours, you say nothing. Nothing, Wren. You let me do the talking. Can you do that?"

Wren nodded solemnly. It wasn't a lie, exactly. She would say nothing.

Because the truth was a different shape than Quentin thought, and Wren had known its shape for a long time. She had drawn it, after all — she drew everything before it happened, that was her gift and her burden both, pictures arriving in her head already finished, waiting to be copied onto paper so that someone might finally look at them and understand. She had drawn the mountain road unwriting itself behind them. She had drawn the ferryman's outstretched hand. She had drawn Quentin kneeling in a hall of veils, and she had drawn what came after, and she had cried the night she drew that one, and burned it in the ferryman's stove, and drawn it again anyway, because true things insisted.

You couldn't warn someone away from their own story. Wren had tried. The pictures just got more insistent.

"I mean it," Quentin said. "Say nothing."

Wren reached up and patted the woman's cheek, twice, the way you pat a horse you're about to ask a great deal of. Quentin huffed something that was almost a laugh, which was what Wren had been aiming for. Fear sat differently in a body that was laughing.

Then she took Quentin's hand, and together they stepped through the door made of everything no one ever said.

* * *

Passing through was like being spoken.

That was the only way Wren could think of it afterward. The unsaid words closed around them, and each one touched her skin and told itself to her in a flash — a man in a southern city who never told his brother he forgave him; a queen who died with a whole speech in her chest; a girl on a hillside who meant to call after her friend and didn't, and the friend walked on into a war — thousands of them, tens of thousands, brushing past like moths, and each one left a little ache behind, a bruise of almost. Beside her she felt Quentin shudder again and again, and heard her whispering, "Gods, gods," under her breath, and Wren squeezed her hand harder, because the words hurt worse if you fought them.

And then their feet found stone, and the words let go, and they were through.

Wren had expected the dark to be dark. It wasn't. It was lit from below.

The whole Undercourt glowed the way coals glow — not bright, but patient, a light that had nowhere else to be and all the time in the world to be there. They stood on a ledge of black rock at the top of an enormous stair, and below them, carved into the mountain's hollow heart, the city spilled away in terraces: towers of pale bone-coloured stone, bridges like ribs, canals of slow silver water carrying boats with lanterns shaped like closed eyes. Everything was quiet, but it was the quiet of a place listening, not a place sleeping. Somewhere far off, a bell rang once, and the sound didn't fade so much as get absorbed, taken in politely, like a guest's coat at a door.

"It's real," Quentin breathed. "All the accounts called it legend. Half of them called it metaphor." Her cartographer's eyes were wide and moving, moving, drinking in angles and distances, and Wren saw her hand twitch toward her satchel — toward ink, toward paper — and then stop, deliberately, like a hand pulled back from a flame. Good, Wren thought. Drawing here would be like shouting in a library.

They descended the stair. The steps were worn smooth in the centers, worn by feet over more years than the surface world had kept records, and each step gave slightly underfoot, springy, like walking on the back of something vast and sleeping that didn't mind. Warm air rose around them smelling of stone and candlewax and, faintly, underneath everything, of apples. Wren didn't know why apples. She filed it away with everything else.

Other people began to appear.

They emerged from side passages and arched doorways, going about whatever business the Undercourt had — carrying jars, leading pale ox-like animals with too many joints, walking in pairs with their heads bent close. None of them seemed surprised to see a woman and a child descending the great stair. That was the eerie part, Wren thought, watching Quentin realize it. On the surface, strangers stared. Here, nobody stared. Here, everyone acted as though your arrival had been on a list, because it had.

The Undercourt folk dressed in greys and browns and deep reds, and their faces were ordinary faces, kind faces, tired faces — except that none of them spoke aloud. Their mouths stayed closed. When two of them met on a landing, they touched fingertips briefly and moved on, and Wren understood that the touch was the conversation, that meaning flowed through skin down here the way sound flowed through air above, and that the silence of the city was not emptiness but fullness, packed so tight it had gone quiet, like a book shut on a thousand stories.

Quentin's grip on her hand tightened. "Wren," she murmured, barely a breath. "Can you hear them? What they're saying?"

Wren shook her head. Not truly. Fingertips were for fingertips. But she caught moods the way you catch weather through a window — a low grumble of commerce from a pair hauling jars, a bright thread of gossip from two women on a bridge, and everywhere, everywhere, woven under it all, a hum of waiting. The whole city was waiting for something. Wren knew what. She had drawn it.

At the bottom of the stair, the way opened onto a wide plaza, and across the plaza stood the gate.

It was smaller than Wren expected, almost domestic — a doorway of dark wood standing free, framed in stone, with no wall attached to either side. You could walk around it. Several people were doing exactly that, passing it by without a glance, because the gate was only for those entering the Court proper, and everyone here had already entered long ago. Above the lintel, in letters of inlaid silver that shimmered like fish scales, ran a single line of the old tongue.

Wren translated it silently for herself: *Leave what names you carry. Take up what names you need.*

A figure sat beside the gate on a stool, knitting. It looked up as they approached. Its face was smooth as an egg, featureless except for two soft indentations where eyes might go, and its hands never stopped their clicking rhythm, needles flashing, drawing out a thread that came from nowhere and wound onto a spool that was already full.

"Oh no," Quentin whispered. "Oh, I know this. The ledgers mention a toll at the threshold. Names. They take your name at the gate." Her voice had gone thin. "If I lose my name down here, Wren — my name is how the guild finds me, how the maps find me, how *I* find me—"

But she was already walking forward, because there was no other way, and Wren trotted at her side, and the knitting figure rose smoothly to meet them, taller than it had seemed seated, much taller, its shadow falling over them both like a curtain drawn at evening.

It extended one long hand, palm up. Patient. Polite. The needles kept clicking somewhere inside its sleeve.

"You don't have to—" Quentin started.

The hand turned over. Waited.

Wren watched the struggle move through the woman beside her — watched her jaw work, watched her glance back up the stair they'd descended, watched her look down at Wren, really look, the way she'd been looking at her since the night on the guild doorstep when Wren had spoken three words of the dead language and Quentin's face had changed forever. Whatever Quentin was weighing, Wren already knew the outcome. She had drawn it. There was a picture, finished months ago in her head, of Quentin Sterling pressing her own name into a stranger's palm like a coin, like a key, like a wound.

Quentin exhaled. "Quentin Sterling," she said clearly, to the smooth-faced thing, as if announcing herself at a grand house. "Cartographer. Of the Guild of Impossible Things. My name is Quentin Sterling, and I'm giving it freely, and I want it noted that I expect it back."

The figure inclined its head — amused, perhaps, or perhaps simply courteous — and cupped both hands together, and something invisible passed from the woman's mouth into them. Wren saw it happen the way you see heat shimmer: a distortion, a gathering, a small bright weight settling into the creature's palms. It carried the name to the gate and pressed it into the wood, and the dark timber drank it, and somewhere deep in the grain a new ring formed around a tree that had never been a tree.

Quentin swayed. Caught herself. Blinked rapidly, twice, three times, like a woman surfacing from deep water.

"What — " She frowned. Touched her own sternum. "There was something I was going to — I had a reason, I wrote it down, I — " Her hands flew to her satchel, rifling, and produced a folded page covered in her own cramped handwriting, and she stared at it as though it were written by a stranger. "*Find the Sovereign. Trade for the child.*" She read it aloud slowly, tasting each word without recognizing any of them. Then she looked at Wren, and her expression was the worst thing Wren had ever seen: polite, wary, blank. The look you give a lost child at a market. "And you are...?"

For one heartbeat, terror — real terror, cold and total — went through Wren like winter water. *She's forgotten me. The name was the thread and I cut it and now—*

But no. No, that wasn't it. Wren made herself breathe. The name was gone; the person wasn't. Quentin's eyes were different, but her hands were the same hands that had fed her soup, and when Wren stepped closer, those hands came up automatically, steadying her, sheltering her, moving on their own ancient errand even as the mind behind them scrambled for a reason why.

"You're keeping me safe," Wren said aloud, in the old tongue, and then, remembering, in the surface words, clumsy and small. "Safe. You keep. Me."

Something flickered across Quentin's face — recognition of a deed if not of its history. "...Yes," she said slowly. "Yes, I suppose I am. Apparently." She looked down at the note again, then at the vast glowing city spread before them, and squared her shoulders with the stubbornness that was, Wren thought, the truest thing left in her. "Well. Someone paid a great deal to send us both here, whoever they were. Best not waste it."

The gatekeeper resumed its knitting. Beyond the free-standing door, the plaza opened onto avenues, and the avenues sloped gently downward, all of them, every street in the city bending toward the same distant center, where — far off, half-seen through the coal-glow, atop a terrace of white stone — Wren could make out the silhouette of a palace, and above the palace, hanging in the cavern air like a second moon, a crown of pale fire.

And before the palace gates, already waiting, already arranged in rows like courtiers, hung the veils.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Draped on nothing, stirring in a wind that did not exist, each one the size of a face, each one faintly, horribly, the colour of someone.

Quentin followed her gaze. Went very still.

"That's where we're going, isn't it," she said. Not a question. The note trembled a little in her hand. "To the one wearing the crown."

Wren took her hand — the hand that had given away its name and still knew how to hold on — and nodded, and together they walked down into the listening city, toward the veils, toward the throne, toward the picture Wren had drawn and burned and drawn again.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, the gatekeeper's needles paused for the first time in a century, and the smooth face turned, and watched them go.
