## 12. The Weight of a Soul

The scales of the Undercourt stood at the center of the veiled court like a heart that had been cut from some enormous body and left still beating.

They were not brass, as the guild's trade-scales were, nor any metal Quentin could name. They looked as though they had been grown rather than made—two pans suspended from a black rib of bone that arched out of the floor and vanished into the dark above, where no ceiling was visible. Each pan was wide enough to hold a sleeping child. The chains that held them were fine as hair and did not move at all, even when the Sovereign's breath stirred every other thing in the chamber: the veils, the candles' flames, the hem of Wren's coat.

*Put your soul upon it,* the Sovereign had said, in a voice like paper folded over paper. *And we will see what it weighs.*

Quentin had bargained before. She had haggled with river-things for safe passage and traded riddles with a market of ghosts beneath the autumn moon. She knew how to keep her voice level while her pulse tried to climb out through her throat. But standing before those scales, watching the empty pans hang in perfect, patient balance, she felt something she had never felt in thirty years of impossible commerce.

She felt *cheap*, somehow—like goods laid out on a table, waiting for an assessor's thumb.

She made herself look at the Sovereign instead. The veil was white, or had been white once; now it hung in folds stained by the faces pressed against its inner surface. Faces she knew. The merchant who had sold his daughter's laughter. The widow who had traded forty years of marriage for one perfect night. Every seller who had ever crossed this threshold wore their expression on the Sovereign's shroud forever—a gallery of transactions, each one a small damnation rendered in fabric.

Among them, near the shoulder, was a face she almost recognized. A young woman, laughing. The sight of it put a splinter under Quentin's ribs, some ache with no name attached, like reaching for a stair that wasn't there.

She looked away. There would be time to catalog that wound later. If there was a later.

"The terms," Quentin said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "You asked for a soul of equal weight to hers." She set her hand on Wren's shoulder. The child stood very straight, chin lifted, the way she'd stood since they came down through the door of unspoken words—as if this place remembered her and she was determined not to flinch at the embrace. "Then take mine. All of it. Not pieces, not payments, not installments. The whole account, closed."

The court went quiet in a way that was not silence. It was the quiet of a hundred ledgers being opened at once, of pens dipping into inkwells throughout the dark hall, of clerks Quentin could not see leaning forward to record what she was about to do.

The Sovereign's veil rippled.

"You offer yourself," it said. "Freely."

"Freely," Quentin agreed. "One condition. The girl walks out. Unclaimed, unowned, unentered. Her page torn from your books—not blanked, not archived. Torn. Burned. Forgotten by your ledgers so completely that no clerk can reconstruct her from the margins."

"And you will remain."

"I will remain." The words tasted like cold iron filings. "I'm a cartographer. I know what it is to be drawn into a map. I'm telling you I'll let myself be drawn into yours."

Wren's hand found hers and gripped hard. Quentin did not look down. If she looked down, she would see the child's face—the face that drew pictures of things that hadn't happened yet, including one picture Quentin had burned in the ferryman's stove—and her resolve would crack along its old fault lines. So she kept her eyes on the scales and waited for the Sovereign's verdict.

"Then let us weigh you," the Sovereign said.

*

They brought a stool. That was the detail that nearly undid her—not the bone arch, not the face-stained veil, but the small wooden stool, worn smooth in the middle, that a clerk placed beside the left-hand pan. It was the stool of someone who stepped up here often. Someone short. Someone who had been coming here for years.

*How many souls have climbed this little ladder?* Quentin thought, and then stopped thinking it, because the answer lived in the ledgers all around her, and she could feel the ledgers breathing.

She stepped up. She stepped off the stool and onto the pan, and the pan did not tip, did not groan, did not register her weight at all—it received her foot the way water receives a stone, without argument. She stood on the scale of souls in her salt-stained boots, and the chains above her sang one thin note, high and clear, like a struck wire.

Across from her, the empty pan dipped.

Not much. An inch, perhaps two—the way a pan dips when a sparrow lands on it. When a feather settles. When something light and partial and *fragmentary* is added where a whole should be.

The court made a sound. Not gasping. Worse than gasping. It was the sound of many throats closing at once, of a hundred veiled figures going rigid, and Quentin understood, with the particular clarity that horror grants, that whatever was happening had never happened before. In all the long history of this court, the scales had never refused.

"What is this?" the Sovereign said, and for the first time its voice lost its folded-paper smoothness. Something underneath it now. Something older. "What is this?"

The pan rose again. Dipped again. Rose. As if the scale were trying to get a reading on her and could not—as if she were a country whose borders shifted whenever anyone tried to survey her.

Quentin knew that feeling intimately. She had spent her whole life drawing coastlines that moved.

"She is already owned," said a voice from the darkness behind the clerks' row.

*

The speaker came forward slowly, and Quentin saw it was not a person but a function wearing a robe—a creature all elbows and ledger-corners, its face an open book with writing that rearranged itself as Quentin watched. An auditor. The court's memory given legs.

"The subject Quentin Sterling," the auditor said, and its voice was the sound of a finger running down a column of figures, "presents for weighing as a whole soul. The scales decline. Consultation of records indicates prior encumbrance." It turned its book-face toward her. Its eyes were columns of numbers. "Pieces of this soul were sold long ago. They are held in trust elsewhere in this court. One cannot pledge what one does not wholly possess."

The floor did not tilt. Quentin's knees tried to anyway.

"That's impossible," she said. Her voice sounded very far away, as if someone else were speaking it from the far end of a long corridor. "I've never—" She stopped. She thought of the guild's back-ledgers, the transaction from forty years past, the signature in her own handwriting that she could not remember signing. She thought of the ferryman's toll, and the way she had paid a memory without knowing which one, and afterward could not say why lullabies made her flinch. She thought of the young woman's laughing face stitched into the Sovereign's veil, and the splinter-ache beneath her ribs that had no story attached.

"No," she said, quieter. "No, I've—I've done this before. Haven't I. I've stood somewhere like this before."

"You have never stood here," the auditor corrected gently, terribly. "Your records show no prior visit. Your records show only sales. Three entries." It opened itself—a seam splitting down the center of the book-face, pages fanning outward. "Would you like to hear them?"

"No," said Quentin.

"Yes," said Wren, beside her, fierce and mute and speaking in the old tongue that only Quentin could half-hear, and the word landed in Quentin's ear like a stone into a well: *yes, yes, yes*, echoing up from depths.

The auditor took her silence for consent. Perhaps it was. She did not stop it.

"Entry the first," it read. "Winter of the plague-year. Sold by Quentin Sterling, aged nineteen years, of her own free will, under seal of survival: her memories of Ilse Vane. Consideration rendered: immunity through the dying season. The memories are held in the vaults below. Condition: intact. Condition: *grieved over nightly by the vault attendants, who request reassignment.*"

The splinter under Quentin's ribs became a blade.

Ilse Vane. The name went through her and found nothing to catch on—no face, no voice, no shared room with frost on the windows—and that nothing was worse than any wound, because wounds have edges and shapes, and this was pure absence, a hole in the world wearing the outline of a person. She heard herself make a small sound. She was nineteen in the memory she didn't have, feverish and starving in a plague-winter, and she had gone somewhere—somewhere like this, or exactly like this—and she had opened herself on a table and let them take—

*Her memories of Ilse Vane.* Not memories of a stranger. The grammar of the entry told her everything the absence couldn't. You did not sell *memories of Ilse Vane* unless Ilse Vane was the shape your whole heart had grown around.

"Entry the second," the auditor continued, merciless as arithmetic. "Sold by Quentin Sterling, same winter: her intention to return. Consideration: extension of immunity through the spring."

"You took my plan to come back for her," Quentin whispered. "I sold her twice."

"The second sale released the first from obligation," the auditor agreed. "It is common. Survivors rarely wish to remember what they did to survive."

"Entry the third."

The auditor paused. Its pages fluttered. And Quentin saw—she *saw*, in the flicker of that hesitation—that this entry troubled even the court's memory.

"Entry the third," it read at last. "Undated. Seller unknown. Purchaser unknown. Sold: portions unspecified of the soul of Quentin Sterling. Consideration: unknown. The entry predates our oldest ledger. It is written in a hand we cannot read."

"It's mine," Wren said.

Everyone turned. The child had stepped forward, and her strange eyes—eyes the color of deep water under ice—were fixed on the auditor's open pages. She spoke in her old tongue, and Quentin translated in her blood faster than thought: *the writing is mine. I wrote it before I was born. The court wrote me to keep the ledger balanced. Some of you was bought before you existed, cartographer. I am the receipt.*

The chamber seemed to lean inward. The candle-flames guttered in a wind that came from nowhere, or from underneath.

"That," said the Sovereign, and its folded-paper voice had frayed entirely now, "is not an entry that should exist."

"Neither is she," Quentin said.

And there it was—the horror and the hinge of it, arriving together like two sides of one coin spinning through the air. She was merchandise. She had been merchandise since before she was born; pieces of her had changed hands in winters she couldn't recall and wars she'd never heard named, and the woman she might have loved had been taken as payment for her survival, and she had stood in guild halls for thirty years calling herself a trader while never once suspecting she was also stock.

But.

But the scales had refused.

Thirty years among impossible things had taught Quentin one law above all others: *in any negotiation, find the thing the other party does not want you to notice, because that is always where the leverage lives.* The Sovereign had wanted her soul weighed whole and pledged whole. The scales had refused. Which meant that somewhere in this court—in these vaults, in these archives, in these breathing ledgers—there were pieces of Quentin Sterling sitting in trust like unclaimed parcels, and no bargain could be struck over her until the question of their ownership was resolved.

Which meant the bargain was not finished. Which meant there was still room to fight.

She stepped down off the pan. Her legs held. She was distantly proud of that.

"Then here are new terms," she said, and her voice carried in the dead air the way a struck bell carries through fog. "You cannot weigh what isn't whole. Fine. Then I'll go into your archives, and I'll find every piece of me you hold, and I'll learn who sold them and why and for what. And when I understand the full account—" she looked up at the veiled Sovereign, at the laughing girl's face pressed against the white cloth, "—when I understand *all* of it, then we'll talk about weights."

"You presume access," the Sovereign said. "The archives are not open to the living."

"Neither," said Quentin, "am I, anymore. Check your scales."

A pause. The chains hummed their thin note.

Somewhere in the dark behind the clerks' row, a door that should not have been open stood open, and cold air breathed out of it, smelling of dust and old ink and, faintly, impossibly, of lavender.

The Sovereign's veil settled, fold by fold, like a decision being reached.

"Go, then," it said. "Read your own history, cartographer. But know this: the last soul who went looking for her missing pieces in our vaults found them. Found them, and wished she hadn't, and has stood veiled at my shoulder ever since."

Quentin did not look at the veil. She did not need to. She already knew whose laugh was sewn into it.

She took Wren's hand.

"Stay close," she murmured, and the child nodded, and together they walked toward the open door and the dark that smelled like someone's memory of home.

Behind them, the scales swung slowly in the windless air, one pan heavy with a featherweight fragment, the other empty—and refusing, still refusing, to say which was which.

* * *

The stairs down into the vaults were cut from black stone that sweated moisture, and every tenth step had a name carved into it. Quentin read them as she descended: names of the sold, worn shallow by centuries of feet. Wren read them too, lips moving in the old tongue, and at the bottom of the stairs she stopped and pointed at the last step before the landing, where the carving was deepest and newest and wrong.

Quentin knelt. Ran her thumb along the grooves.

Two words, in handwriting she knew better than her own—because it *was* her own, the young version, the nineteen-year-old's eager slanted script:

**ILSE'S — Q.S.**

The lavender smell was stronger here. And below, at the end of a corridor lined with doors made of locked memories, something was humming a lullaby—the very tune, Quentin realized with a fresh, small horror, that had made her flinch at the ferryman's stove without knowing why.

Her feet began to walk toward it before she decided anything at all.
