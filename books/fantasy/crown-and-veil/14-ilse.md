## 14. Ilse

The vaults of the Undercourt were not rooms. They were the spaces between rooms—the silence behind a door, the pause before a name is spoken—hollowed out and shelved.

Quentin followed the archivist down stairs that did not agree with one another. The first flight was granite, the second was bone polished to the color of candlewax, and by the third she had stopped counting materials altogether, because the stairs had begun to be made of *distance*, each step placing her farther from herself in a way she could feel, like a tooth loosening in the jaw.

Wren had wanted to come. Wren always wanted to come. But a child's soul was already spoken for here, and the archivist—a tall absence wearing a robe of gray moths—had simply turned its hood toward her, and Wren had sat down on the cold floor and folded her arms and waited, which for Wren was a kind of screaming.

"She'll keep," the archivist said. Its voice sounded like pages turning. "Souls keep very well here. That is rather the whole point."

"I want to see my entry," Quentin said. "Ledger of the Sold. Nineteen years old. Plague-winter."

"We know why you came." The moths on its shoulders stirred, resettled. "Everyone who comes to the vaults knows why they came. It is only afterward that they forget, and then they come again, and again, until they stop coming. Would you like the ledger version or the living version?"

"What's the difference?"

"The ledger tells you what you paid." The archivist lifted a hand, and somewhere in the dark, an enormous lock considered itself and opened. "The living version lets you hold it."

Quentin thought of the entry she had found an hour ago—or a lifetime ago; time ran strangely below the mountain—her own young handwriting staring back at her from a page that should have been impossible: *Sold: the winter, and the woman in it. All of her. Every hour.*

She had stood there reading her own betrayal like a stranger's confession, and grief had come looking for purchase in her and found nothing to grip, because how could you mourn a person you could not remember? How could you grieve a face that had never existed?

"Both," she said. "I want both."

"No one wants both," the archivist said, but it opened the door anyway.

* * *

Here is what Quentin remembered, before the vaults gave it back:

Nothing.

Here is what she carried instead, in the place where the memory should have been:

A flinch at lullabies. An ache in the left hand, as if it had once held something and been made to let go. A habit, absurd and unexplained, of drawing gardens when her mind went idle—garden after garden in the margins of honest maps, walled gardens, gateless, full of flowers she never identified because naming them felt like stepping on graves.

She had shown these marginal gardens to other cartographers over the years. *Decoration,* they said. *You have a decorative instinct.* And she had agreed, because agreeing was easier than admitting that every time she drew one, some animal part of her brain leaned hard against a locked door.

* * *

The vault was cold in the specific way of places where warmth has been removed rather than merely absent. Shelves rose into darkness overhead, and on the shelves were vessels: bottles, jars, reliquaries, envelopes of grey vellum sealed with wax that was still faintly warm after decades, because memory does not cool. Some vessels were small as thimbles. Some were caged, and things moved inside them.

The archivist walked the aisle with the unhurried confidence of a creature that has never once been lost, and stopped.

"This one."

On the shelf, between a jar of someone's mother's voice and a stoppered flask that hummed a half-finished song, sat an envelope. Grey vellum. Wax seal pressed with the sigil of the Guild of Impossible Things—an open hand holding an open eye.

Quentin knew that seal. She had pressed it herself a thousand times since.

"Forty-one years ago," the archivist said. "Winter of the Gray Fever. Contents: three hundred and four hours. One person, entire. Sold under distress, price waived, buyer: this court." A pause, dry as dust settling. "Seller: one Quentin Sterling, aged nineteen, in exchange for passage through the quarantined city and enough health to walk out of it."

"Was it—" Her voice cracked and she started again. "Was it her choice? Mine?"

"They always say yes," the archivist said. "Whether it is true is a question we do not adjudicate. We are not a court of law. We are only a court."

It held out the envelope.

"You understand," it added, "that once you break the seal, the hours return to you. All of them. In order. They will feel like they are happening now, because to a memory, there is no other tense. You will live the winter again in the space between two breaths. Most people scream. It is permitted."

Quentin took the envelope. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything; her wrist knew the difference even if the scales upstairs would not.

"And if I refuse?" she asked. "If I leave it sealed?"

"Then you will go on drawing gardens in margins," the archivist said, "and flinching at lullabies, and mourning nothing, beautifully, forever. It is a mercy we offer no one else, cartographer. Consider taking it."

Quentin thought of Wren sitting in the dark corridor with her folded arms and her borrowed tongue, waiting for a woman whose soul could not balance a scale because pieces of it had been pawned off forty years ago by a girl desperate to survive.

"No," Quentin said. "Mercy is what got me here."

She broke the seal.

* * *

*The snow is falling in Vhalterre, and it falls gray, because the fever is in the air and everything the sky touches turns the color of ash. She is nineteen and she is dying, though slowly enough that dying still feels like an inconvenience rather than a fate. Her lungs rattle. Her hands shake so badly she cannot hold a pen, and she has just begun to understand that she might ever be good—might ever be more than good, might be the best the guild has seen in a century—and the unfairness of it is a fire in her chest hotter than the fever.*

*And there is a knock at the shutter.*

*"You're the mapmaker," says the woman in the doorway, stamping snow off her boots. She is older—twenty-five, twenty-six—with wind-burned cheeks and ink stains on her fingers and the particular insolent cheer of someone who has looked at the edge of the world and shrugged. "They told me in the low town. They said there's a guild girl up here drawing maps of places nobody's been, and I thought, well, either she's a fraud or she's the most interesting person in this rotten city."*

*"I'm dying," Quentin hears herself say. It comes out flat, a fact among facts.*

*"Yes," the woman agrees, cheerfully. "Terrible timing. I'm Ilse. I need a map drawn of somewhere that doesn't exist yet, and I've brought soup, and I don't take no for an answer from anyone with a rattling chest."*

*The soup is terrible. It tastes like boiled leather and regret. Quentin eats all of it.*

* * *

*Ilse comes back the next day. And the next. The plague shuts the city gate by gate, district by district, and Ilse keeps finding reasons to be inside the quarantine ring—smuggled letters, medicine runs, a sister of a friend of a cousin—and every evening she climbs the guild stairs with snow in her hair and something in her pockets, and every evening she talks while Quentin draws, too weak to do anything but draw, and too unwilling to ask her to stop.*

*Ilse is a surveyor. Quentin learns this on the fourth day. Not a guild surveyor—a road-builder's daughter who learned to read land the way other people learn to read faces, who can stand on a hill and tell you where the river used to run before anyone diverted it. She talks about watershed lines the way poets talk about lovers, and Quentin, feverish, laughing, coughing into her sleeve, thinks: oh. Oh, no. Not this. Anything but this.*

*"Draw me somewhere," Ilse demands, on the ninth night, when the coughing is worst. "Not from life. Somewhere that doesn't exist. Show me what your head looks like when I'm not in it."*

*"You're always in it," Quentin says, delirious, honest. "You've ruined it. It's very inconvenient."*

*Ilse goes very quiet. Then she crosses the sickroom, takes Quentin's face in her ink-stained hands, and kisses her like sealing a document—deliberate, binding, witnessed by the whole gray watching city.*

*"There," she says. "Now you're ruined too. We match."*

* * *

*Three hundred and four hours. The archivist was precise; the archivist was cruel without meaning to be, because precision is cruelty when applied to love. Quentin lives them all.*

*She lives the morning they map the garden together—on paper, in words, in the steam of bad soup—because Ilse says* every surveyor needs a home benchmark, *and neither of them owns land, so they build one out of language: a walled garden, gateless, with roses along the north wall because Ilse insists roses are underrated, and a plum tree in the center, and a bench, and a door in Quentin's imagination that will never close again once it is drawn.*

*She lives the afternoon Ilse teaches her to kiss with cold hands and warmer intentions. She lives the argument about projection methods that ends with both of them on the floor, breathless, defeated, delighted. She lives the night Ilse falls asleep mid-sentence about contour lines, head on Quentin's shoulder, and Quentin lies perfectly still for four hours because moving would wake her and waking her would end the best moment of her entire life.*

*And then she lives the last hours, and those are the ones the vault saves for last the way a knife is saved for last in a drawer.*

*The fever breaks in the city but not before it breaks Quentin nearly in two. The gates open for the recovered, but her lungs are shredded, her blood is thin as wash-water, and the physicians give her a week. Maybe two. And Ilse—Ilse has been listening at doors Ilse should not have been listening at, and she comes up the guild stairs white-faced and says the word* Undercourt *like spitting out glass.*

*"There's a door," she says. "Beneath the mountain. They buy things. Anything. Grief, years, names. And they pay in survival, Quentin. Real survival. Not hope—certainty."*

*"What do they cost?"*

*"Everything you can't afford to lose."*

*"Then I can't afford it."*

*"You can't afford* not *to," Ilse says, and her voice cracks down the middle, and Quentin sees—for the first and last time—that Ilse is terrified. "Listen to me. Listen. There's a cure under that mountain and it costs memories. Memories only. They eat the past and leave the body walking. So you sell them the winter. Sell them the fever. Sell them—" and here her voice fails completely, and she has to start again from nothing—"*sell them me. Sell me, and live."*

*"No."*

*"Quentin—"*

*"NO."*

*But the fever decides for her, that night, when she stops being able to breathe at all, when the world goes gray at the edges the way the snow has gone gray, when dying stops being an inconvenience and becomes a hallway with a door at the end. And in the morning, nineteen years old and ninety pounds and drowning on dry land, Quentin Sterling signs the ledger with a shaking hand.*

*Ilse holds the pen with her. Both their hands on the shaft. Neither willing to be the sole author.*

*"I'll find you," Ilse whispers against her hair as the wax cools on the seal. "After. Whatever they take, whatever's left—I'll find the rest of you. I swear it on every map neither of us has drawn yet."*

*The Undercourt takes three hundred and four hours.*

*It leaves a girl of nineteen alive in the snow, holding a pen she cannot remember why she was holding, standing beside a woman who is crying and whom she does not know.*

*"Are you all right?" Quentin asks her.*

*Ilse looks at her for a long, long time. Snow gathers on both their shoulders.*

*"I will be," she says.*

* * *

Quentin came back to the present screaming, as promised.

The archivist waited with the patience of furniture. When the sound finally tore itself down to whimpers, it said—not unkindly—"Now you know the weight. Shall we tell you the rest?"

"There's more?" Her voice was ruined gravel. Her hands were shaking around the empty envelope, and the ache in her left hand had become a bonfire, because now she knew what it had been holding. Ink-stained fingers. Cold hands. *There.*

"The seller signed," the archivist said. "So did a second party. Do you recall the transaction terms? The court does not only buy. Sometimes it trades upward. Someone purchased something, that same winter, from us. Something very large. With a price so steep we have never repeated the arrangement."

"What did they buy?"

"A heart," the archivist said. "Whole and entire. Voluntarily surrendered, so that the seller could remain here—in service, in residence, in perpetuity—to ensure that a certain surviving cartographer would one day be able to find her way beneath the mountain safely. Someone has spent forty years keeping these roads gentle for you, cartographer. Someone has spent forty years wearing a veil, because a sovereign without a heart is the only kind that can rule a place like this without going mad."

The shelf-lamps guttered, all at once, as if the whole vault inhaled.

Quentin stood very slowly. The empty envelope fell from her fingers and did not, quite, reach the floor.

"Take me to the Sovereign," she said. "Now. Take me to her right now."

The archivist's moths all lifted at once, a gray startled cloud, and resettled.

"You've never called the Sovereign *her* before," it observed. "None of them ever have. And yet you're certain."

Quentin was already running—up the stairs of distance, through the door of unspoken words, her ruined voice tearing itself hoarse around a name she had only owned for the space of two breaths and would now carry for the rest of her life, however long the Court allowed that to be.

"Ilse! ILSE!"

And far above, in the hall of veils, the Sovereign rose from its throne—and every face stitched into its robes, every seller and every sold, turned at once to watch her come.
