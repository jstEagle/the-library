## 17. Wren's Bargain

The Undercourt had a smell Wren had forgotten she remembered: cold stone and candle-tallow and underneath it something sweeter, like fruit going soft in a bowl no one would ever eat from. It was the smell of the place before it had a name anyone still used. It was the smell of home, if home could be a thing that forgot you.

She sat on the floor of the antechamber while the grown-ups argued beyond the veils, and she drew.

Her charcoal was a stub no longer than her thumb, taken from the ferryman's fire when Quentin wasn't looking. The paper was the back of one of Quentin's discarded drafts—a half-finished map of a road that had already unmade itself behind them, its inked landmarks now so much beautiful nonsense. Wren liked drawing over dead maps. Dead maps didn't argue with her about what they wanted to be.

She drew the mountain first, because everything here began and ended with the mountain. Then she drew the mountain's heart, and gave the heart a door, and gave the door a crack, thin as a hair, running corner to corner.

That part was true. She had seen it.

She pressed harder with the stub of charcoal until the crack went dark, and then, because the picture asked for it—pictures always asked; that was how they worked, they *asked*—she drew two small figures standing at the mouth of the door. One tall, one small. Holding hands.

She did not draw what came after. Not this time. Some pictures you made to warn people. Some pictures were just for you, kept folded up inside your chest where no one could weigh them or trade them or take them away.

"Wren."

Quentin's voice. Wren knew all of Quentin's voices by now—the cartographer voice, flat and careful as a ruled line; the guild-voice, clipped and official; the tired voice; and underneath all of them, rarely, the warm voice that came out sideways, like light under a door. This was the tired-warm one, which meant the arguing had stopped, which meant either good news or terrible news.

"Come sit," Quentin said. "There are things we need to talk about. Terms. You don't have to understand all of it, but—"

"I understand all of it," Wren said.

And then, because she was tired of being the child who sat on floors while adults decided her weight in souls, she said the thing she had been carrying since the surface, since the snow, since the knock on the guild door that had not been a knock at all:

"You have it backwards, you know. Both of you. Everyone does."

* * *

Ilse-as-Sovereign stood at the edge of the antechamber, veiled still, though the veil had gone translucent at the edges where Quentin's tears—or hers, it was impossible to say whose—had wetted it. The Sovereign's faces shifted beneath it like fish under ice. Wren watched them without fear. You couldn't be afraid of faces you'd helped choose.

"Backwards," the Sovereign repeated. Even through the veil, even worn smooth by centuries of bargaining, the voice caught on the word. Ilse's voice. Wren had heard it once, long ago, in a memory-vault she'd been too young to legally enter, and had never forgotten it. "Child, explain yourself."

Wren climbed onto the low stone bench so she could see both of them at once. Quentin perched beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. That helped. Quentin's shoulder was bony and always slightly cold and it was the most solid thing in the whole Undercourt.

"You think I got sold," Wren said. "Forty years ago. Before I was born, even—you sold a soul, and it went down here, and it waited, and eventually it became me. That's the story. That's what the ledgers say." She tapped Quentin's knee. "But ledgers only record what people paid. They never record why."

"The ledger records everything," the Sovereign said.

"No," Wren said. "The ledger records everything that *happened*. I'm telling you about the things that haven't happened yet." She held up her charcoal stub. "You've seen my pictures. Quentin has. The mountain falling down. The court going dark room by dark room, like candles going out. The doors that won't open anymore. The names wearing out until nobody can remember what anybody traded or why, and everything down here just—" she made a sound, a small collapsing noise, like a house settling wrong— "*stops*."

The veil went very still. All those borrowed faces stopped shifting at once, which was somehow worse than the moving.

"That is not common knowledge," the Sovereign said quietly.

"It's not knowledge at all yet," Wren said. "It hasn't happened. But I'm from the part of the court where things that haven't happened get born. Somebody has to make them. So they sent me."

"Sent you," Quentin said. Her hand had found Wren's shoulder without either of them deciding it should. "Sent you where? To do what?"

"To find you, obviously." Wren rolled her eyes, because honestly, even now, even here, adults required everything spelled out. "Do you know how many cartographers there are in the world? Dozens. Hundreds, maybe. And do you know how many of them swore an oath never to map this place?" She looked up at Quentin. "One. Exactly one. Because you're the only one who ever understood what an oath against mapping actually means."

"What does it mean?" Quentin asked. Her voice had gone careful. Ruled-line flat. But her fingers had tightened on Wren's shoulder, and Wren could feel the tremor in them, the fear coming through like heat off a stove.

"It means you saw us dying," Wren said, "and you couldn't bear to write it down. A blank space isn't a debt, whatever the guild taught you. A blank space is a *question*. And questions keep. They wait for someone brave enough to answer them." She spread her hands. "So I knocked on your door. Except I couldn't speak your language, because the court doesn't waste words on things it doesn't need yet, and I hadn't needed yours yet. So I spoke the old tongue. The one older than kingdoms. The one the mountain dreamed in before anyone built anything on top of it." She shrugged. "I figured a mapmaker would recognize a country when she heard one."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Down here silence had texture; it pooled in corners and hung from the vaulting like cobwebs made of nothing. Wren let it pool. She'd learned patience in the deep rooms, in the nursery of unborn hours, where the other children-of-the-court-to-be practiced becoming. She'd been the best at waiting. Waiting, the elders said, was just remembering forward slowly.

It was Quentin who broke it, of course. Quentin, who could never leave a blank space alone.

"The pictures," she said. "The ones you drew at the guild. My death beneath the mountain. I thought—" Her jaw worked. "I thought they were threats. Or curses. Something the court had put in you."

"They were warnings." Wren said it gently. Grown-ups needed gentleness more than children did; everyone knew that; children just weren't supposed to say it out loud. "A threat says: do this or else. A warning says: this is what happens if nothing changes. I showed you the mountain falling because I needed you scared enough to come down here. But I never showed you the ending, did I? Go on. Think. Did I ever draw you what happens after?"

Quentin's brow furrowed. Wren watched her count backward through every scrap of paper, every charcoal scrawl, every image the child had produced across a winter of strange companionship. Wren let her count. It was important that she arrive at the answer herself.

"There was always... a door," Quentin said slowly. "In the last drawings. A cracked door, and light behind it. And figures. Two figures, sometimes three." She turned to look at Wren properly. "I assumed the third figure was the Sovereign."

"One of them is," Wren said. "Sort of. Sort of not. Faces down here are complicated." She hopped off the bench and went to her satchel—the little one the ferryman had given her, patched with sailcloth—and drew out the drawing she'd been working on before they interrupted. The mountain with the hairline crack. The two figures at the door, holding hands.

"This one isn't finished," she said. "I can't finish it. I've tried forty times, on the surface, on the road, down here. The picture won't go any further. Do you know why?"

"No," Quentin said.

"Because endings aren't sent down from ahead," Wren said. "Endings get *chosen*. Backwards, by people standing exactly here, right now, in this ugly room with its stupid veils. Everything I could see from where I come from stops at this moment. Right here. You, and her, and me, and the choice." She held out the drawing. "Whatever happens next, none of us will have seen it coming. Isn't that wonderful?"

Neither of the grown-ups looked like they thought it was wonderful. Wren sighed. Adults had such a limited appetite for miracles.

* * *

"Why you?" Quentin asked later—they had migrated to a smaller chamber, one with an actual window, a slit of grey surface-light falling like a blade into the room. The Sovereign had withdrawn, pleading matters of state, but the veil's absence felt different now. Less like a monarch leaving and more like a woman unable to stand still and hear what came next. "Why were you chosen? Out of all the—not-children, the futures, whatever the court grows down here—why send you?"

Wren hugged her knees. The stone floor was cold through her dress and she liked that; cold meant the surface was somewhere above, reachable.

"Because I'm the one who was sold," she said. "Or—I'm what grew from the sale. Forty years ago somebody traded a soul to this court, and the court planted it, the way you plant anything you want to grow into something specific. Most trades rot. Most souls that get sold down here just... dissolve. Become wall. Become candle-fat. Become the sweet-rotten smell." She wrinkled her nose. "But this one took root. Because of who sold it. Because of how it was sold—it wasn't spite, it wasn't greed, it was—" She paused. "It was love, actually. Terrible, desperate, wrong-headed love, the kind that pays the wrong price for the right reasons. Souls sold in love don't dissolve. They *ferment*. They turn into possibility."

"And you grew from that," Quentin whispered.

"I grew from that." Wren nodded. "And here's the part nobody guessed, not the elders, not the ledgers, not even me for the longest time." She looked up, and met Quentin's eyes, and made herself say it plainly, because plainly was the kindest way. "The soul they planted was tangled up with yours before I ever existed. Your sale and mine share roots. When I was sent to the surface to fetch a cartographer, the elders thought they were sending their newest child on an errand. But the errand chose itself. I didn't come looking for any cartographer, Quentin Sterling. I came looking for *you*. The mountain has been trying to give you back something for forty years, and I'm what it could afford to send."

Quentin had gone white. Not fainting-white; thinking-white. Wren recognized it. It was the color of a person redrawing the entire map of the world in their head at speed.

"My handwriting," Quentin said. "On the old transaction. In the back-ledgers. I signed away a soul forty years ago and I don't remember doing it."

"You don't remember lots of things," Wren said, and immediately wished she'd phrased it differently, because Quentin flinched like she'd been struck. Wren scrambled on. "That's not an accusation! That's the point! Don't you see? The court didn't take your memories. Somebody *bought* them—took the debt on themselves, traded pieces of their own self to cover yours. That's what the entanglement is. That's what I am. I'm the change left over from a bargain between two people who loved each other enough to ruin themselves symmetrically." She threw up her hands. "Honestly, it's the most romantic thing I've ever heard, and neither of you will even talk about it!"

* * *

They talked about it, after that. Or tried to. Wren sat with her back against the wall and listened to the two of them circle each other like weather fronts, and she drew, and she let them.

This was the part she hadn't been able to see from ahead. This was the blank space. And watching it fill in—watching Quentin's fear curdle into resolve, watching the veil-less Sovereign's grief sharpen into something that might, given time and terrible effort, become hope—Wren understood at last why the elders had sent a child instead of a herald.

A herald delivers messages. A child makes people *change*.

"Then we redraw it," Quentin was saying, pacing now, her hands carving shapes in the air as though she were already drafting. "Not a payment. A remapping. New borders. A court that runs on something other than consumption. If the realm is dying because it eats souls, then we redraw it so it feeds on something else. On craft. On exchange freely given. On—" she faltered, and looked at the empty doorway where the Sovereign had gone, and finished anyway, quieter— "on whatever's left of what we used to be."

"The elders will refuse," Wren said. "The old borders are the old laws. Changing them unmakes everyone who was ever made by them."

"The elders refused to admit the mountain was cracking," Quentin said. "The elders can learn, or the elders can be redrawn along with everything else. I've spent my whole life believing blank spaces were debts. I'm done. From tonight, blank spaces are invitations."

Wren looked down at her unfinished drawing. The two figures at the cracked door, hand in hand. The darkness past the crack that wouldn't resolve into anything, because nothing had chosen it yet.

She picked up her charcoal stub. And slowly, carefully, feeling her way the way you feel your way down unfamiliar stairs in the dark, she added a third figure to the picture—small, standing just ahead of the others, pointing onward into the unwritten dark.

The picture didn't resist. The charcoal moved like it had been waiting for her.

Above them, far above, through miles of stone and snow and the long slow turning of the year toward spring, the mountain groaned—a single deep note, like a struck bell, like a door remembering it could open.

Wren capped her drawing, stood, and tugged Quentin's sleeve.

"Come on," she said. "We've got a court to convince, an archive to break into, and an ink to steal back. And Quentin?" She grinned up at her, gap-toothed, seven years old, older than any kingdom. "When the elders ask who authorized all this—tell them the future sent you."
