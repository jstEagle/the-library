## 20. Unmapped

Wren had learned, in the Court beneath the mountain, that the last stroke of any drawing was always the loudest.

She stood at the edge of the great chamber where the Sovereign's scales had hung for as long as the Court had been dying, and she watched Quentin Sterling finish her map. The woman's hands did not shake. Wren thought they should have. Every line Quentin drew cost her something—a memory first, then a name for a color, then the color itself, then the small sure knowledge of which hand she favored. The ink from the stolen vial drank it all down and gave back borders.

The chamber had been changing all night. Where there had been walls of pressed shadow and floors of traded names, there were now stone corridors with real doors, real hinges, real rooms behind them that no one had ever needed to buy their way into. The Undercourt was remembering how to be a place instead of a hunger.

But the price of the map was written in its own lines, and Wren could read it the way she read everything—backward, from the end toward the beginning.

Quentin was growing fainter.

Not fading like a candle fades—not dimming. Fading like a word spoken too many times, until the mouth making it forget what it meant. Her outline had begun to blur at the edges, soft as chalk in rain. When she paused to dip her pen, Wren could see straight through her wrist to the far wall of the new-made corridor, where torches burned in iron brackets because someone had remembered torches belonged in courts, and had drawn them so.

*She doesn't know,* Wren thought. *Or she knows and won't look.*

The child walked forward across a floor that still smelled faintly of old bargains, though the smell was going out of it, the way smoke goes out of a coat left in the sun. She carried nothing. In the Undercourt you never carried anything; everything you owned was already part of you or already sold.

"Quentin," she said.

The cartographer did not turn. "Nearly done, little wren." Her voice had changed too. It was still warm—it would be warm until the very last, Wren understood that now—but it had lost its anchor. It was a voice that belonged to no one. "There's a river here that has no source. I have to give it one."

"You don't have to give it yours."

That made her pause. The pen hovered above the vellum, and the vellum—which had grown enormous as the map grew, spreading across the floor and up the new walls, becoming not a picture of the Court but the Court's own skin—waited with the patience of paper.

"Wren." Quentin turned then, and Wren's chest went tight, because half the face looking down at her was already unmade. Not gone. Unwritten. Like a page in a book where the words had lifted off but the ghost-shapes of them remained, pressed into the fiber. "You know what happens when I stop."

"I know what happens when you don't stop either."

"Then let me finish the river."

Wren looked at the blank space where the river's source should go. It was a small emptiness, no bigger than a coin held at arm's length. But she had seen the pictures come through her hands before she ever came up the mountain—pictures drawn by a girl who remembers forward—and she knew that this last small space was not small at all. It was the size of a name. It was the size of every name.

*Quentin Sterling, cartographer, who maps realms that shouldn't exist.* That was what would go into the blank. And after it, everything else, poured out line by line until the Court had borders enough to hold itself together and the woman who drew them had nothing left to hold herself with.

"Finish the river," Wren said quietly. "I'll stay till the end. I promised."

Quentin smiled—the smile that had survived the whole long road, the one she'd worn over ferry-water and unwritten roads and the terrible bright halls of the Court of Veils. "You're seven years old. You shouldn't have to promise things like that."

"You're the one who told me promises are the only currency that keeps its value."

"So I did." The pen touched down.

Wren watched the river find its source. It rose out of a mountain's heart on the map, and beneath the map, in the true dark under the true stone, water moved where water had never moved before, and somewhere in the new corridors of the stabilizing Court a sound began that no one there had heard in forty years: running water, going somewhere, for no reason anyone had paid for.

And Quentin Sterling grew another shade fainter, and said, without sadness, "I think that's the last of my mother's lullaby. I can't remember if she sang high or low. It's all right. The river knows the tune now."

* * *

It took until dawn—or what the Undercourt now agreed to call dawn, a slow gold light seeping down through stone that had learned, in the last hours, to let light exist.

Wren sat against the wall and watched the map complete itself the way you watch a fire burn down: unable to look away, unable to help, understanding that the burning is also the warmth. Quentin worked through the last of herself with the steadiness of a woman settling accounts. She gave the Court its seasons—four lines, four curves, and suddenly the air moved with weather instead of with waiting. She gave it a gate that opened outward. She gave it a market square, and laughed once, low and strange, as she drew it: "Ilse always wanted to sell flowers in a market square. I never asked her why. Now there's a square, and I still don't remember why, but the square remembers wanting."

At the center of everything, where the veiled throne had stood, she drew a garden.

Wren saw her hesitate there. The pen hovered. Something crossed the thinning face—not a memory exactly, but the shape a memory leaves when it goes, the dent in the pillow after the sleeper rises.

"There was someone," Quentin murmured. "Wasn't there? There's a dent where someone used to be." She shook her head slowly. "It doesn't matter. The garden will hold it."

She drew the garden in eleven strokes. Wren counted. Eleven strokes, and in the middle of the dying Court a garden bloomed into being, roses and rosemary and a low stone bench worn smooth by sitting, and the scent of it rolled through every corridor at once, and in the vaults below—in the memory-vaults where the Court kept what it had bought—something stirred and knocked against its shelf like a moth against glass.

The pen stopped.

The vellum was full. There was no blank space left anywhere on the map, not one coin-sized gap, not one debt. The Undercourt lay bordered and bounded and *finished*, a realm that needed to consume nothing because it finally contained everything it was.

And Quentin Sterling stood up, set down the pen, and was not there.

Wren didn't mean she died. Wren had seen death coming since before she could speak, and this wasn't it. This was worse and better both. Quentin simply became difficult to look at, the way an afterimage is difficult to look at—there and not-there, present in the gold morning light of the made-dawn as a warmth, as a pressure on the air, as the sense of someone standing just beside you in an empty room.

On the floor, the map showed a small figure at the garden's edge, drawn in the same ink as everything else. A woman with ink-stained fingers, standing outside her own borders.

"She's on the map," Wren whispered. "She put herself *on the map.*"

The Court exhaled. Every door in every corridor opened at once, gently, like eyes waking. Somewhere far below, the great scales that had weighed souls for centuries tipped, found nothing left to weigh, and stilled forever.

* * *

They brought Ilse up from the vaults at midmorning.

Wren had gone down for her, because someone had to, and because Wren was the only soul in the whole reborn Court who owed no debts and therefore could walk anywhere. The memory-vaults were quieter now, most of their shelves empty—the Court had begun, on its own, in its first free hours, returning what it had bought. Small mercies, flowing downhill. Wren had passed clerks—former clerks, un-veiled, blinking in the new light—carrying jars of grief and bottles of borrowed shadow up the stairs, checking labels, knocking on the doors of the surface world to give things back.

Ilse had been harder. Ilse had been the Sovereign, and the Sovereign's memories were kept in the deepest vault, behind a door made of unspoken words that had gone silent and become, overnight, just a door.

Wren had found her kneeling in the empty vault room among scattered labels, holding one in both hands, turning it over and over. The label read: *Memories of Q.S., purchased in plague-winter, price of survival. Contents: laughter, salt bread, a future drawn in pencil. Condition: loved.*

"What does the Q stand for?" Ilse had asked. Her voice was wrecked and rusted from forty years behind a veil. She was tall and gray-eyed and her hands were shaking. "Child, what does it stand for?"

"It stands for Quentin," Wren had said. "Come and meet what's left of her."

Now Ilse stood at the edge of the finished map, at the border of the garden, staring down at the small inked figure with ink-stained fingers, and her face was doing something that had no name in any language, old or new.

"I know her," she said. "I know—I can almost—" She pressed her palm flat over the drawn figure, careful, so careful, as if touching a sleeping animal. "There was salt bread. We tore it in half on a bridge, and the gulls—" She stopped. Started again. "There was a plan. We drew it. A house with a garden, and a map table in the kitchen, and—" Her voice broke clean in two. "Why can't I keep it? Why does it slide?"

"Because she paid it away to live," Wren said gently. "And you paid yourself away so she could pay it. It's all tangled. Tangled trades don't come undone clean."

Ilse looked up at Wren then, and Wren saw the last fragment settle in the gray eyes like silt in water. "She's still here, isn't she. Not gone. Just—" 

"Just unmapped," Wren said. "No ledger anywhere holds her now. No contract, no debt, no entry. The Court can't see her. The Guild can't see her. Even the road she traveled won't remember her feet." Wren swallowed. "But the map is a kind of ledger too. And she's on it. So she isn't nowhere. She's just... only somewhere."

The warmth in the room shifted. Moved. Wren felt it pass close to her cheek, deliberate as a hand that couldn't quite remember how hands worked, and then drift toward Ilse and hover there, at the height of a forehead, the way a lover's hand hovers when it has forgotten permission.

Ilse closed her eyes. Tears ran straight down and off her jaw. "Stay," she said to the empty air. "Whatever you are now. Whatever's left. *Stay.*"

The warmth trembled. Pressed closer, for one heartbeat—two—close enough that Wren saw the fine hairs on Ilse's arm rise, felt the air take the temperature of a body that no longer had one.

Then it withdrew, slowly, and settled at the garden's edge, beside the bench, where a person might sit if a person had a person's edges anymore.

Ilse opened her eyes. She looked at Wren, and something in her face reorganized itself around a decision Wren was too young to read but old enough to recognize: the look of someone choosing to begin again from zero rather than mourn forever.

"Take me up the mountain, little wren," Ilse said. "Both of us. Whatever's left of her comes too."

* * *

The way home was longer than the way there, because the road had forgotten them.

The Road That Unwrites Itself had eaten every landmark Quentin and Wren had passed on the descent—the leaning mile-stone, the chapel with its bell full of bees, the ferryman's gray water—and now, with no Quentin to punish, it seemed confused about what it was for. They walked days that behaved like weeks. Wren led, because she remembered forward and could steer by things that hadn't happened yet: *the oak splits here, we go left of the lightning-scar; the ferryman will be waiting at the third crossing even though he owes us nothing now, because freed men get sentimental.*

He was waiting. He looked at Ilse a long moment—at the gray eyes he must have known behind the veil, once, in the old bad days—and then he looked at the space beside her where the air was warmer than it should have been, and he bowed, low, the way you bow to a grave you loved.

"No toll," he said roughly. "Not for her. Not ever again. Get in."

They crossed the gray water in silence. Halfway over, Ilse spoke without opening her eyes. "There was a lullaby," she said. "High or low?"

"High," said the ferryman.

"Low," Wren said at the same moment, and then, softer: "She couldn't remember either. That was the last thing the map took. The river knows the tune now. If you want it, you'd have to ask the river."

Ilse was quiet for the rest of the crossing. When they reached the far bank she stepped off, turned back once toward the mountain, and said, to no one, to the warm air, to the whole unmapped world: "I'll learn it from the water, then. However long it takes."

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things looked smaller from the outside than Wren had imagined, and older, and tired around its windows like an old dog. Its sign creaked in the wind. Through the leaded glass, lamplight moved—some clerk, some ledger, some ordinary impossible trade.

Wren stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"They won't know her," she said. "That's the whole point of what she did. No ledger holds her. The Guildmaster will look straight through her. The door might not even open." She glanced up at Ilse. "Are you ready for that? Walking into the place that sold you both, carrying a woman it can't see?"

"No," Ilse said honestly. Then she climbed the steps anyway, and Wren followed, and the warm air came with them, three travelers where the ledgers said none.

The door opened before they knocked.

An old clerk stood in the frame, quill behind his ear, blinking at them as if his eyes were arguing with each other. His gaze slid past Ilse twice. It caught on Wren and stuck—the mute child, the dead-tongue child, the child the ledgers said had been sold forty years ago and never returned.

"You," he breathed. "The entry—we closed the book on you—you're supposed to be—"

"Returned," Wren said, in her own tongue first and then, carefully, in the guild's common speech, tasting every word like a foreign fruit. "Returned. And I've brought guests."

"We have no record of—"

"No," Wren agreed. "You don't. That's rather the point." She stepped past him into the hall of ledgers, into the smell of dust and ink and small mercies, and turned in a slow circle, taking in the shelves where bottled grief glowed faintly amber, the case of borrowed shadows folded neat as linens, the great ledger chained to its lectern with its thousands of careful entries.

Every entry except one.

Somewhere in the warm air beside her, Wren felt the presence pause—felt it register the shelves, the ledgers, the whole architecture of a life spent trading in names—and felt it do something she had no word for yet, in any language, old or new.

Ilse felt it too. She put her hand out into the empty air, palm up, an offer with nothing in it.

"The ledgers are all wrong," she said softly, to the clerk, to the room, to the woman no page would ever hold again. "There should be an entry here. Quentin Sterling. There should be a whole shelf of her." Her fingers curled closed, holding the nothing. "So we'll start a new one."

The old clerk frowned. "A new ledger? For whom? What name do I write on the first line?"

And in the silence that followed—while the lamps guttered, while Wren held her breath, while the warm air leaned close between the two women who had paid everything to save each other and remembered almost none of it—Ilse smiled, slow and ruined and certain, and opened her mouth to answer.

Wren, who remembered forward, realized she didn't know what came next.

For the first time in her whole strange life, the future had gone beautifully, terrifyingly blank.
