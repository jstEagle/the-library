## 21. The Language Predating Kingdoms

Spring came to the city the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and not all at once, and never quite to every corner. The gutters ran with meltwater that tasted of iron and old snow. In the courtyard behind the Guild of Impossible Things, where the frost had held its white grip longest, the first green shoots pushed up through cracks in the flagstones like small stubborn arguments against the winter's verdict.

Quentin sat on the guild steps with a pencil in her hand and no contract in her sight.

This was, she was learning, a strange way to hold a pencil. For thirty years her hand had moved only under orders: commissions from merchants who wanted their trade routes drawn braver than they were, from magistrates who wanted rivers nudged to flatter their borders, from the Guild itself, whose ledgers demanded precision the way other institutions demanded piety. Her hand had always been an instrument of someone else's will. Now it hovered over the page like a bird unsure whether the cage door opening was freedom or a trick.

She drew a wren.

It was not a good wren. It was, in fact, barely a wren at all—more suggestion than bird, a few strokes of wing and a tail like a comma—but it was hers, uncommissioned, unbought, owed to no ledger anywhere. She looked at it for a long time. Then, carefully, she wrote nothing beneath it. No title. No name. Not even her own, because she did not have one to write.

*Unnamed but not empty*, Wren had said once, in the weeks after they climbed back into the light, when the child had appointed herself Quentin's translator-of-self. *You're like one of my words before I teach it to someone. It doesn't mean you're blank.*

The child was in the courtyard now, and the courtyard was full of children.

They came on market days at first—one or two of them, daring each other up the alley to the guild's crooked door, because word had spread the way word does about impossible things: sideways, half-disbelieved, irresistible. A mute child who spoke in a language older than any kingdom. Who could teach it, if you were patient, if you left your names at the threshold the way you'd leave muddy boots.

Now there were nine of them, sitting cross-legged in the spring mud, and Wren stood before them with the gravity of a much older soul and the dirt-smudged knees of exactly the child she was.

"Again," said the baker's daughter, a round-faced girl named Marta who had lost two front teeth and none of her ferocity.

Wren raised her hands. She still did not speak with her mouth—the Undercourt had taken that from her along with so much else, and though the healers said the silence might lift in time, Wren seemed in no hurry. Her hands moved instead, quick and certain as birds, shaping the old tongue in the air: the rising gesture that meant *dawn*, the falling one that meant *remember*, the circling motion that meant both *home* and *the place you return to*, which in the old tongue had never needed to be two separate words.

Nine small hands copied her, clumsy as fledglings.

*Dawn-remember-home.* Quentin watched from the steps and felt something turn over gently in her chest, some organ she had no name for anymore—she had traded most of her names, after all, and this one had gone into the ink with all the rest.

That was the thing about the map she had drawn beneath the mountain. Everyone assumed it had cost her memories. It had cost something stranger. She remembered her life the way one remembers a book read long ago: the shape of it, the turns of the plot, the fact of having wept at certain pages. But the names had gone out of it. When she thought of the plague-winter she had survived at nineteen, she remembered cold and hunger and the desperate arithmetic of survival, but the person she had sold her memories for stood in those recollections now only as a warmth, a presence, a pair of hands—never a face, never a name.

When she thought of Ilse—and she did think of Ilse, daily, helplessly, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth—she remembered everything except the woman herself.

* * *

"You're staring at your pencil again," said Master Corvane from the doorway.

The Guildmaster had aged years in the months since the solstice. The scandal of the stolen ink had faded—he had, with the guild's particular genius for self-preservation, reframed Quentin's transgression as a sanctioned expedition the moment it became clear she had returned alive and the Undercourt had stopped eating travelers—but the knowledge of what his ledgers contained, what his predecessors' signatures had sold and sold again, sat on him like a wet cloak he could not take off.

"It doesn't feel like mine," Quentin said.

"Whose would it be?"

"No one's. That's rather the point." She turned the pencil in her fingers. "I keep waiting for it to feel borrowed. Everything else does. These clothes." She plucked at her sleeve. "This chair. The guild itself. I remember earning them, but the memory has no weight, like coins cut from paper."

Corvane lowered himself onto the step beside her with the groan of a man whose joints had opinions. He smelled of pipe smoke and ledger-dust, that smell that had meant safety once, before she knew what safety bought and with whose coin.

"The archivists finished the reconciliation," he said. "All the entries touched by the remapping. Every soul the Court held is accounted free. Every debt written in the old borders reads *paid* in the new ones." He paused. "Except yours."

"I know."

"There's no line for you anywhere, Sterling." He caught himself, winced. "Forgive me. Habit."

"Don't apologize for it." She smiled, and found the smile came easier these days, worn smooth by use like a river stone. "It's rather lovely, actually. Being a clerical impossibility. I spent thirty years cataloguing them. Fair's fair."

Corvane studied her with eyes that had appraised ten thousand impossible trades and been fooled by perhaps nine thousand of them. "There are those in the College who say a person cannot exist off-ledger. That the world forgets such people. That by midsummer you'll have faded like a chalk line in rain."

"What do you say?"

He looked out at the courtyard, at Wren's hands shaping *home* in the air while nine children mangled it lovingly, at the green shoots breaking stone, at Quentin sitting solid in the morning sun casting a perfectly ordinary shadow.

"I say the world forgets what it holds," he said. "And I've checked three times this week. You're still here." He rose, groaning again. "The Council wants a report on the Undercourt's new borders. I've told them the cartographer is unavailable indefinitely. Indefinitely," he repeated, with the ghost of his old dry humor, "being a word I've decided to defend."

* * *

In the afternoons, Quentin drew.

Not maps. That was the vow she had made to herself on the road home, somewhere past the ferryman's crossing, watching the landmarks reassemble themselves behind them like actors returning for a curtain call—the hill that had erased itself rebuilding its own slopes, the village that had forgotten its own name remembering it aloud. Maps were contracts. Maps were debts. Every line she drew committed the world to something, and she had spent enough of herself committing the world.

So she drew other things. A kettle. The courtyard elm in four stages of budding. Marta's ferocious gap-toothed grin. The ferryman's boat, and the ferryman in it, dozing, his pole drifting, because she had paid him a memory once and he had repaid her since with crossings and with stories she half-recognized—stories of two women, he claimed, who used to ride his ferry years ago and laugh too loudly for the quiet water.

She drew the lullaby she couldn't explain. This was the strangest practice of her new life: sometimes, in the evenings, she would catch herself humming a melody with no origin, and when she tried to follow it inward she met only a flinch, a grief with no object attached, a sorrow shaped precisely like a person and containing no person at all. So she drew it instead—a slow curve, a repeated phrase, notes she didn't know how to notate and therefore invented, a private script for a song only she could hear.

Wren found her at it one afternoon and sat down beside her without asking, which was Wren's way, and studied the page for a long time.

"That's a mourning song," Wren signed at last. "Old court. We sang it when someone went behind the veil."

"Do you know why I know it?"

The child considered this with terrible seriousness, head tilted, mud on her chin. "Because someone taught it to you," she signed finally, "and then you forgot them, but your body kept the lesson." She tapped Quentin's chest, right over the heart. "Bodies are bad ledgers. They keep entries even when the master book says paid."

Then she took the pencil and added, below Quentin's invented notation, the true signs for the melody—the gestures that went with the song, the way the old tongue gave music hands as well as voice. *Dawn-remember-home*, over and over, which was apparently how the song ended, and Quentin felt her eyes sting and did not ask herself why, because there was no answer to be had, and because Wren leaned against her side just then, warm and solid and alive, and some prices should stay paid.

* * *

It was on a Thursday, in the hour when the market noise below the hill softened to a murmur, that the knock came at the guild door.

Quentin knew footsteps the way she used to know coastlines. Corvane's tread, heavy and deliberate. The apprentices', quick and scattered. Wren's, light as weather. And this—this was unfamiliar and yet not, a stride with a rhythm her heart recognized several seconds before her mind caught up, the way the body keeps its bad ledgers running long after the books are closed.

She opened the door.

The woman standing there was tall, with grey coming in at her temples like frost claiming high ground, and laugh-lines around her mouth that spoke of decades of laughing, and eyes the color of deep water over dark stone. She wore a traveling coat and carried a leather satchel, and she looked at Quentin with the expression of someone arriving at a house they have dreamed of many times and never seen awake.

"Forgive the intrusion," the woman said. Her voice did something to the air; Quentin felt it in her teeth. "I'm looking for—I'm not entirely sure. A cartographer, the ferryman said. Someone who draws things that aren't contracts."

"Yes," Quentin said, because it was true, and because her voice had gone strange and thin.

"My name is—" The woman stopped. Frowned. Started again, and Quentin watched, with a sensation like ice forming slowly down her spine, as the woman reached for her own name and found the shelf empty. "My name is," she said again, more quietly. "That's the second odd thing. The first is this."

She opened the satchel and drew out a map.

Quentin knew maps the way saints know scripture, and this one undid her in a single glance. It was drawn in ink that shimmered faintly, ink distilled from a first memory—she knew that shimmer, had fed it line by line into the remaking of a dying court. But the map was no cartography of mountain or border. It showed a garden. Two figures stood in it, rendered in loose confident strokes: one tall, one shorter, their hands joined between them. Around the garden grew things no garden grows—trees bearing lanterns instead of fruit, a hedge trimmed into the shape of a rising gesture, *dawn*, a sundial whose shadow pointed at neither sun nor hour but simply at the two women, always.

And in the lower corner, in a hand Quentin did not recognize and knew absolutely, was written a single line:

*For Q., who taught me that blank spaces are promises.*

"I found it in my satchel," the woman said. "Three days ago. I've been walking since. I don't know why I knew to walk here. I don't know why I've been drawing gardens for weeks, or why I wake up humming a song I can't name, or why—" She broke off, and pressed her lips together, and Quentin saw that this woman, who laughed in lines around her mouth, was fighting not to cry on a stranger's doorstep. "Or why standing here feels like coming home to a house I've never lived in."

Behind Quentin, in the courtyard, the children's lesson had gone silent. Wren stood among the frozen students, hands half-raised, watching the doorway with an expression far too old for her face.

Quentin looked at the map. At the two women. At the joined hands. At the warmth that poured off the page like heat from a hearth in a house she had never entered but somehow, impossibly, had built.

"I know the song," Quentin said.

The woman's breath caught.

"It's a mourning song. From the old court." Quentin stepped back from the door, holding it wide, the way one opens a gate to a garden. "But I think—it was never really about mourning. I think it ends with dawn-remember-home. I think it might be a courting song, actually. I've been humming it for forty years without knowing why."

The woman crossed the threshold as if pulled, and stopped close enough that Quentin could see her eyes properly, and in them was the same vertigo Quentin carried everywhere, the same grief-without-object, the same warmth shaped precisely like a person.

"This is going to sound mad," the woman whispered.

"I run a guild of impossible things," Quentin said. "Madness is our founding charter."

"I feel like I've loved you my whole life," the woman said, "and I can't remember a single day of it."

The courtyard held its breath. Somewhere in the old tongue's vast vocabulary—Wren would tell her later, signing it slowly, solemnly, teaching it the way you teach the most important words first—there was a single gesture for this exact moment: for two souls meeting across a price already paid, for recognition arriving without its reasons, for love returning home to find the door unlocked and the lamp lit and no memory of who had lit it.

Wren made the gesture, small, behind the woman's back, where only Quentin could see.

Quentin did not know the woman's name. Did not know her own. Knew only the weight of the map between them, the promise in its blank margins, and the extraordinary, terrifying, impossible fact of the door standing open.

"Come in," Quentin said. "Tell me everything. Or tell me nothing—we have time to draw it instead."
