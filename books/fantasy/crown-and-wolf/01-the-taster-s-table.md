## 1. The Taster's Table

The broth was wrong before I ever lifted the spoon.

I want to be clear about that, because people assume a taster's work is guesswork—some peasant girl sniffing at soup and hoping for the best. It isn't. It's reading. The way a scholar reads a page, I read a dish. The color of a reduction. The sheen of oil on stock that's been simmered too long or not long enough. The particular silence of a kitchen when something in it is afraid.

This broth was too clear. A good bone broth should murmur with its own history; it should have opinions. This one had been strained until it had nothing left to say, and things are usually strained like that to hide what they've swallowed.

"Again," said Master Corren, from his place by the hearth.

He didn't need to say it. He never needed to say anything, which was the point of him. Corren had trained me since I was nine years old, when my mother sold me to the Guild of the Palate for three silver marks and a promise I'd be fed better than she could feed me. He was sixty, dry as old bread, and he had survived thirty years of tasting by wanting nothing—not comfort, not company, not even, I suspected, his own life beyond its usefulness. Watching him was like watching a man live inside his own caution.

I lifted the spoon. The broth was pale gold and smelled of leek and thyme and, underneath, faintly, of nothing at all—which was itself a smell, if you'd been trained the way I'd been trained. Certain poisons announce themselves. Hemlock is green and grassy, sweet at the edges. Arsenic has an almond whisper if you're lucky and no whisper at all if you're not. The worst ones say nothing. The worst ones are polite.

I took a mouthful and held it on my tongue the way you hold your breath at a funeral.

Nothing. Warmth, salt, the soft lie of thyme. Then—there—a thinness at the back of the throat, like a door opening somewhere far away in the house of my body. Not poison exactly. Or poison wearing a coat I hadn't met yet.

I swallowed.

"Note," said Corren.

"Bitter finish," I said. "Late. Under the tongue. Could be foxglove prepared cold, but foxglove doesn't hide this well." I took another spoonful, deliberately, because the second taste tells you whether the first was a fluke or a guest. "Could be nothing."

"It is never nothing," Corren said. "That is the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone believes."

He was right, as it happened. By the time the envoy's party sat down to their midday meal at the Wayhouse of the Broken Antler—King Aldric's men passing through our valley on their slow circuit between garrisons—the broth had been remade twice under Corren's eye, and I had tasted every pot, every ladle, every sprig of herb that came within arm's reach of the kitchen. The envoy, a soft-bellied man named Serle with rings on six fingers, ate three bowls and complimented the cook, who wept a little with relief in the scullery afterward. Cooks always cry. Tasters don't get to.

That's the arrangement. The Vale of Ashenmoor sits in a fold of mountains where magic cannot go—has never gone, so far as anyone can remember—and because it cannot go there, neither can the poisoned charms and cursed wines and hexed delicacies that kill kings in more fashionable countries. But men are not magic. Men still carry powder and blade and bitterness into the vale, and King Aldric, who owns everything from the vale to the sea, likes his envoys fed and breathing. So the crown keeps tasters here. Keeps *me* here.

Here is how it works: I eat first. If I sicken, the dish dies with me and no one else does. If I don't, the dish goes out and I stand at the wall of the hall watching everyone eat, feeling the peculiar pride of a wall—useful, unthanked, expected to hold.

I have held for eleven years. My body is a ledger of small harms. Foxglove twice, hemlock once, a bad mussel season that nearly finished me at fourteen, and a hundred lesser guests whose names I never learned because poisoners don't sign their work. Each one taught me something. Each one left a little scar in me, most of them invisible, all of them permanent. People think immunity is a gift. It's a debt. You pay in advance, forever, for meals you'll never enjoy.

But I'll tell you the truest thing about the vale, the thing I only understood after I'd lived elsewhere for exactly zero days: it is quiet. Not silent—snow falls, rivers argue, Corren clears his throat—but quiet underneath, the way a room is quiet when the loudest person in it has finally gone home. There is no magic here. No charms humming in doorways, no blessings thickening the air, no curses coiling under floorboards waiting for a footfall. Other places, travelers say, you feel it like weather. Here, nothing. A great flat calm, a held breath that never has to be released.

I loved it the way you love a house you grew up in: without thinking, without gratitude, without imagining any other address.

Which is why, when the letter came, it felt less like news and more like a wound.

* * *

It arrived at dusk, with the snow starting—the heavy, deliberate kind that means business—and it arrived wrong. Letters to the guild come by courier, wax-sealed, ordinary. This one came by falcon, which we don't use in Ashenmoor because the passes are treacherous and birds are stupid about wind, and it bore a seal I'd only seen once before, pressed into black wax on the back of Corren's oldest ledger: the crowned stag of House Aldric himself.

Corren read it standing in the doorway with the snow settling on his shoulders like he couldn't feel it, which knowing him, he couldn't. His face did the thing his face does, which is nothing.

"Well?" I asked. I was mending a sleeve by the fire. My hands were steady. I remember noticing that, being proud of it even, the way you're proud of a dog that doesn't bark at thunder.

"The king requires a taster," he said. "For the capital. For the winter court." He looked at the letter again, though I'd already watched his eyes cross every line of it once. "Not a taster. *The* taster. They've asked for you by name."

Nobody knows my name. That's rather the architecture of the whole profession. Tasters are furniture that eats. We're listed in household rolls as initials, at best. "By name" is a sentence that shouldn't be able to end itself that way.

"That's absurd," I said. "There are forty of us in the vale."

"There were," Corren said. He folded the letter along its original creases, precise as surgery. "Two died at Harvestmas. Bad shellfish, officially. One retired to her sister's in Lowmarsh. And one—" he paused, and Corren did not pause, pausing was a luxury he had never permitted himself— "one was found at the bottom of the cistern beneath the guildhall, and no one will tell me why, and I have asked four times."

The fire cracked. Snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere in the kitchen below, the night cook dropped a pan and swore, and the sound rose up through the floorboards like a small animal escaping.

"You're sending me," I said.

"I'm giving you the choice," Corren said, "which I have never done in eleven years, which should tell you something." He set the letter on the table between us, face down, like a card he was ashamed of. "You could refuse. The king's writ bends a little in Ashenmoor; it always has. You could stay, keep tasting envoy broth until your tongue wears out, and die here at eighty the way I intend to die, quietly, of nothing worse than myself."

"And if I go?"

He was quiet long enough that the fire got loud.

"If you go," he said finally, "you will be in a city where magic works. Where the food can be spelled six ways before it reaches the table and where half the court would poison the king's shadow if they thought it would help them. You will be valuable in a way that furniture is never valuable, and things that are valuable get used, and things that get used get spent." He looked at me then, directly, which he almost never did, and his eyes were the pale gray of the sky over the pass in November. "I have buried four apprentices, Sable. Three of them I sent to the capital because they were good, and goodness gets requested."

The name landed strangely. He almost never used it either. We were a household of functions—he was Master, I was Girl, then Taster, then First Taster—and names were for people who expected to be remembered.

"I'm good," I said, because someone had to say something, and irony was the closest weapon to hand.

"You are the best I have made," Corren said, without any warmth whatsoever, which from him was practically a sonnet. "And now you know how that feels."

* * *

I didn't sleep, which surprised me, because I can sleep anywhere, anytime, through anything—it's part of the training, a taster who can't rest is a taster who makes mistakes, and mistakes are measured in other people's pulses. But that night I lay in my narrow bed under the eaves and listened to the storm lean against the house, and I did arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Four tasters gone in one season. A request by name from a king I had never seen, for a post in a city where the air itself was supposed to be full of working spells, spells that had never once touched me, that *couldn't* touch me, that slid off the vale like rain off slate. I tried to imagine magic the way travelers described it—as pressure, as color, as a sound just under hearing—and failed completely. It was like trying to imagine a new primary color. My mind kept sliding off the idea.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the snow, a question I couldn't put down:

Why me?

Not why the best taster—I could answer that, vanity being cheap. Why *me*, specifically, by name, from a king who did not know I existed. Someone had spoken my name into a room where it mattered. Someone had written it down. In eleven years I had worked very hard to be nobody, and somebody, somewhere, had undone that work with ink.

I got up before dawn, because lying still was worse than moving, and went down to the kitchen to make bread. This is the thing I do when the world misbehaves. Bread is honest. Flour, water, salt, yeast, heat—no hidden finishes, no late bitterness, nothing to read except effort. I kneaded in the dark before the ovens were lit, and the cold dough gave my hands something true to do, and I thought: *this is my life. This is enough. This has always been enough.*

Then the thought turned over, the way thoughts do when you press on them, and showed its belly: *and it has never once been chosen. It was sold to you for three silver marks, and you have called the cage comfortable ever since.*

I punched the dough down harder than it deserved.

* * *

Corren found me at first light, packing.

"You don't have to decide today."

"Yes, I do. The letter says within the fortnight, and the pass closes after that, and you know it does." I folded my second shirt the way Corren folds letters—precisely, because precision is the only inheritance he ever gave me and I meant to wear it visibly. "Also, you stood in the snow last night reading that letter, and you didn't come inside until you'd read it twice, and you've lied to me exactly never, so I'm going to ask you the real question and you're going to answer it or I'm going to stand here not packing until you do."

He waited. Waiting was another of his weapons; he had a whole armory of silences.

"Why me?" I said. "Truly. Not the best-taster answer. The true one."

Something moved behind his face—regret, maybe, or its poorer cousin, resignation. He reached into his coat and brought out a second letter. This one was older; the paper had gone the color of weak tea, and the seal on it had been broken years ago and broken carefully, by someone who knew how seals worked.

"This came eleven years ago," he said. "The week I bought you. I was instructed to train you well, keep you in the vale, and never, under any circumstance, let you taste for the crown outside Ashenmoor. Never let her leave the valley, Sable. Those were the words. Signed by the same office that signed yesterday's letter."

The kitchen was very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"So someone changed their mind," I said.

"Someone changed their mind," Corren agreed. "Or someone died. Or someone lost an argument they'd been winning for eleven years." He tucked the old letter back inside his coat, close to his chest, like a man carrying a coal. "Either way, the instruction is dead, and instructions that die are replaced by whoever is strongest in the room where they died. And you, girl, have just become the subject of a new instruction."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he said. "It isn't. It's the only honest thing I have." He picked up my bag from the table—heavy with bread, because I'd baked two loaves in the night out of pure nerves—and held it out to me. "Eleven years ago I made a bargain I didn't understand, and I've kept it because keeping things is all I know how to do. Now the bargain's broken from the other end, and all I can tell you is this: whatever waits for you in the capital wanted you in that vale, safe and blind, for eleven years. Ask yourself what it knows that we don't."

I took the bag. It was heavier than bread.

Outside, the storm had stopped sometime in the dark, and the valley lay white and still under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. The pass eastward was visible from the doorstep—a thin gray thread stitched up through the mountains, already filling in at the edges, already closing. Two days' travel, three if the snows came back. The road looked exactly like what it was: the only way out of the only home I'd ever known, narrowing while I watched.

I had tasted a thousand dishes for danger. I knew the discipline: take the mouthful, hold it, wait, swallow, note. Hold your fear on your tongue until it tells you what it is.

Standing on that doorstep, I finally understood that I had been doing it my whole life without knowing—that the vale itself was the mouthful, the safety was the holding, and somewhere far away, someone had decided it was time for me to swallow.

I stepped off the stone onto the snow.

Behind me, Corren said nothing, which from him was a blessing, a warning, and a farewell, all pressed into the same silence like flowers in a book.

The pass waited. I started walking.
