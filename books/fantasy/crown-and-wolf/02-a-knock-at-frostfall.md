## 2. A Knock at Frostfall

The snow came down like a verdict.

I watched it through the kitchen window of my cottage at Frostfall, the flakes thick and silent and utterly indifferent to my opinion of them, and I thought: *tomorrow I leave this place, and the world outside is going to try to kill me in ways the valley never has.*

The broth on the stove had nothing wrong with it. I'd checked. Belladonna would have turned the wild garlic bitter; hemlock gives off that faint sweetness I've spent twenty years learning to catch the way a hound catches a scent. Nothing. Just barley, marrow, thyme, and the particular sadness of a meal made for someone who outranks you. But I tasted it anyway — a full spoonful, held on my tongue for the count of thirty, then swallowed — because that was the discipline, and discipline was all I had ever been given to hold.

My name is Sable Kavanagh, and for twenty-two years I have been the royal food-taster of the Vale of Ashenmoor. Which sounds grander than it is. The king's envoy comes through twice a year with provisions for the garrison, and before anything touches his lordship's table, it passes through me first. I am, officially, a courtesy. Unofficially, I am the only person in the vale who poisons herself professionally and gets thanked for it.

Here's what nobody tells you about building immunity: it isn't courage. It's bookkeeping. A grain of arsenic in your morning tea. Two drops of foxglove tincture at midsummer. You keep the ledger, you honor the entries, and one day you realize you've spent your whole life making your body into a locked room where death keeps knocking and knocking and never gets let in. There's a pride in it, I suppose. There's also a loneliness so complete it stops registering as loneliness and starts registering as weather.

The vale helped. The Vale of Ashenmoor is magic-dead — has been for as long as anyone remembers, though nobody agrees on why. No charms work here. No curses. Priests perform their rites and get nothing back but echo, and they've learned to call that faith. For most people it's an absence they never notice, like a man born deaf doesn't miss music. For me it was the opposite of a locked room. It was the only unlocked room in the world. Nothing supernatural could reach me here, which meant all my dangers were honest ones: poison, cold, bad luck, other people. I could name every threat in my life. I liked that about the vale. I loved it, probably, though I'd have bitten my tongue before admitting it.

And tomorrow the letter said. *Come to the capital. By order of His Majesty's household.* Signed by a chancellor whose name I only knew from the wax seal.

I'd read it eleven times. The twelfth time, I gave up pretending I hadn't memorized it and put it under the salt cellar where I could stop looking at it.

*They know something,* I told myself. *Or they suspect something. Or it's routine — a taster recalled for the coronation season, nothing more.* Kings summon their servants for dull reasons far more often than interesting ones. That's practically the definition of a kingdom.

But kings also summon their servants for no reason at all, sometimes, and those are the ones you don't come back from.

I fed the fire another log and tried to think about packing. I was failing at it. My whole life fit into two trunks and I couldn't make myself open either one. Instead I stood at the window and watched the storm erase the road, and I thought about how strange it was that after twenty-two years of practicing for death in small doses, the thing I actually felt, staring at that summons, was not fear.

It was insult. As if the world were saying: *you did everything right, kept every ledger balanced, and now we're changing the terms anyway.*

The wind rose. Somewhere out in the dark, the pines along the ridge bent and groaned like old men getting up from chairs.

That was when someone knocked at my door.

* * *

Three things about that knock, in the order I noticed them:

First, it was small. Not the fist of a rider or the flat palm of a neighbor needing help with a calving cow. Small knuckles. Light, repeated, almost apologetic.

Second, it came from the front door, and nobody comes to Frostfall at night in a whiteout unless they're dead or lost, and the lost ones shout. This didn't shout.

Third — and this is the one I still can't explain properly — my scars ached when it landed. All of them. The burn along my left forearm from the aconite year. The thin white line at my throat where a jealous under-cook once decided the taster should taste something sharper than food. Every mark my body had ever taken in service of the king's table lit up at once, low and hot, like coals blown back to life.

I stood very still. Old habit. When you don't know what's coming, be furniture.

The knock came again. Then a third time, weaker, and then — silence, except the wind, and beneath the wind, something else. A sound I took at first for the draft under the door. A thin, wavering note, rising and falling.

Singing. Someone was singing at my doorstep in a blizzard, in a voice too small to belong to anyone with the sense to come indoors.

Every sensible instinct I owned said: *bar the door, wait for morning, whatever it is will freeze or wander off.* I want it noted that I heard those instincts clearly. I want it further noted that I ignored every one of them, crossed my own kitchen, and lifted the latch — because twenty years of tasting other people's meals teaches you one thing above all others: the worst thing in the dark is almost never as bad as the thing you imagine there.

Cold hit me like a wall of wet wool. Snow swirled into the doorway, and standing in it — hip-high in the drift that had built against my threshold — was a child.

A girl, I judged, six years old, maybe seven, though she was so thin the age was a guess. Barefoot. Barefoot, in a storm that had already claimed two toes from the cooper's son last winter. Her feet were blue-white and untouched by frostbite, which I noticed later, much later, after I'd stopped noticing everything else. She wore some kind of shift, grey, soaked through, hemmed with a pattern I didn't recognize — geometric, silver thread gone dark with age. Her hair was black and heavy with snow. And her eyes —

Her eyes were the color the sky gets at the exact moment the sun goes down behind mountains. That particular violet-grey that isn't any color at all but the memory of one. I have seen eyes like that exactly once since, and both times they made my chest hurt.

She looked up at me. She wasn't shivering.

"Inside," I said. "Now. Come on."

She didn't move. So I moved her — scooped her up, and God forgive me, my first coherent thought was *she weighs nothing, less than a winter lamb,* and my second was *her skin is warm. Warm as bathwater. In this cold.* I got the door shut with my heel, set her down by the fire, wrapped her in the good blanket, the one I'd been saving for the journey, and started rubbing warmth into her feet with both hands, working from long-ago field training that mostly consisted of *don't stop moving, don't let them sleep.*

She let me. She watched me the whole time with that twilight gaze, calm as a cat, and when I finally looked up from her feet to check her face, she opened her mouth and spoke.

Not the vale's tongue. Not the trade-speech of the lowlands. Not the clipped court dialect I'd heard from envoys. Something older. The vowels came round and deep, the consonants fell soft as ash. Three words, maybe four, pitched low, and the moment they left her mouth, three impossible things happened at once.

The fire burned green.

My scars sang.

And every hair on my arms stood up and pointed at the child like compass needles.

Let me be precise about the green, because precision is the only weapon I had left by then. Fire does not burn green. Copper salts will do it, certain woods, certain tricks — I know them all, I've tested dishes for less. This was none of those. The flames themselves turned the color of deep river ice, emerald shot through with white, and the light they threw went green too, so that my hands on the child's feet looked like a drowned woman's hands. The shadows on my walls leaned toward her. Toward her, against the draft, against physics.

And the singing in my scars — I have no better word. The old wounds woke, all of them, a chorus of heat, and underneath the heat was rhythm, and underneath the rhythm, faintly, horribly, was *meaning*. As if my body had been keeping accounts in a language I couldn't read, and someone had just spoken aloud in it, and every entry had sat up in its ledger and answered.

Because — understand this — the child was speaking the same tongue. Or near enough. The words she'd said weren't random. They slid into that ache in my scars like keys into locks I didn't know I carried.

Magic cannot enter the Vale of Ashenmoor. That is the first fact of my life, older than my name. Nothing charms, nothing curses, nothing burns green. Priests get echoes. Witches get headaches. Children are taught it as a blessing: *we live in the quiet place, where the world's madness cannot follow.*

And magic was standing in my kitchen. Barefoot. Wrapped in my good blanket. Watching me with eyes the color of endings.

I backed away until I hit the table, and the table dug its edge into my spine, and I gripped it because the floor seemed suddenly like an opinion rather than a fact.

"What are you?" I asked. Stupid question. Worse than stupid — rude, in every language including hers, whatever hers was. She tilted her head, considering me the way I considered suspect dishes: thoroughly, without malice, waiting for the truth to declare itself.

Then she spoke again, and this time I caught it — not understanding it, but *hearing* it, hearing the shape of it, and the shape was familiar the way a face in a dream is familiar. Round vowels. Falling cadence. Words that ended soft, that sounded like they'd been designed for lullabies and elegies and nothing in between.

"You're speaking Vhalenic," I said, and then clapped my hand over my own mouth, because I had no idea how I knew that word. It arrived in my mind the way a sneeze arrives — fully formed, unstoppable, from somewhere below thought. Vhalenic. High Vhalenic, whispered some cold little clerk in the back office of my skull. The dead tongue. The erased kingdom.

There is no kingdom of Vhalen. I want that understood. Not conquered, not fallen — *erased*. Maps don't show it. Histories skip from one dynasty to the next across a gap historians describe as "obscure." Ask anyone where Vhalen was and they'll look at you blankly, and then they'll frown, because there's a hole where the answer should be, a socket with no tooth in it, and most people find the frown unpleasant and change the subject.

Two hundred years ago, give or take, a country ceased. Not burned, not buried. Removed. And the language died with it, and the name survived only as a curse-word in border villages and a riddle scholars used to frighten students with.

And a barefoot child was speaking it fluently in my kitchen while my fire burned green and my scars recited poetry.

"No," I said aloud, to her, to the room, to the whole sensible arrangement of the universe. "No, this isn't possible. Nothing works here. Nothing *can* work here."

She regarded me with vast patience. Then she raised one small hand — fingers blue-tipped, nails neat, absurdly, heartbreakingly clean — and pointed at me.

At me. Past me, at the summons letter under the salt cellar, then back at my face.

And she said one more word.

One word, in that falling, ash-soft speech, and it went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor, and my knees nearly went, and I caught the table again, and this time I did sit down, hard, on the bench, because the word was — 

I can't write it. I've tried, these many nights, and the letters won't hold it. But I can tell you what it did. It sounded like my name and it was not my name. It sounded like *Kavanagh*, but older, deeper, the root under the tree instead of the branch. It sounded the way my grandmother used to hum when she thought I wasn't listening — that same falling melody, the same soft ending — and I had forgotten she ever hummed at all until this moment, standing in the doorway of my own memory, watching a child say a word that fit a lock inside me I had never known I carried.

The green fire flared. My scars blazed. Outside, the storm paused — actually paused, the wind holding its breath for the length of one syllable — and resumed.

The child lowered her hand. She pulled the blanket closer around her shoulders, a small, ordinary gesture, unbearably human, and looked at me with those twilight eyes, and waited.

The safe world ended tonight. I knew it the way you know a dish is poisoned — not by evidence, not by reason, but by the deep trained certainty of a lifetime's practice. Something had knocked on my door wearing the shape of a lost girl, and it had spoken the name of a country that doesn't exist, and it had called me by a name I'd never owned, and every bell in the cathedral of my skepticism was ringing at once.

I should have turned her out. I tell myself that sometimes, in the kind voice people use for their past selves. *You should have waited for dawn and taken her to Mother Ilsen like any foundling, done the ordinary thing.* And I did intend to — that was the plan, such as it was, huddled there on my bench with my heart slamming and the fire hissing green: feed her, warm her, and at first light carry her down the valley to the shrine-house where Mother Ilsen kept her books and her Latin and her unshakable conviction that the vale's silence was holy.

But plans require a world that behaves. And mine had just stopped.

So instead I did the only thing left. I ladled out a bowl of the envoy's broth — tested, harmless, the safest food in the valley by definition — and set it in front of a child who might be anything at all, and I sat across from her with my own bowl, because a taster never serves what she won't share.

"Eat," I said, in the vale's plain tongue. And then, because the word was still lodged in my chest like a fishbone, working its way toward my heart, I said it back to her. Badly. Accent mangled beyond recognition. But I said it.

Her whole face changed. Wonder — actual wonder, the kind adults spend their lives selling off piece by piece — opened across it like sunrise over the ridge. She set down her spoon. She reached across the table, took my scarred hand in both of her cold-warm ones, and pressed it, gently, the way you press a seal into wax.

Outside, the snow went on falling like a verdict I hadn't yet heard pronounced.

Tomorrow I was supposed to leave for the capital. Tomorrow the king's road would open, and the letter under the salt cellar would start ticking like a clock.

Tonight, in my kitchen at Frostfall, magic held my hand across a table and smiled at me with a child's mouth, and I understood — with the same certainty that had kept me alive through twenty years of small poisons — that whatever came next, I would not be facing it alone, and I would not be facing it sane, and the valley that had kept me safe my whole life had just failed me for the first time.

Somewhere out in the dark, the wind found the chimney and moaned down it, low and falling, soft as ash.

Almost like a word.
