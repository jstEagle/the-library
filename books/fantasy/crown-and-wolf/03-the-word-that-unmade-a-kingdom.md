## 3. The Word That Unmade a Kingdom

Mother Ilsen smelled of camphor and old paper, and she received us in the scriptorium of the Frostfall priory like a queen receiving an ambassador—which, it would turn out, was closer to the truth than either of us knew.

I had wrapped Wren in my spare cloak and carried her the four miles through what remained of the storm, because the child would not let go of my hand and I could not make myself pry her fingers loose. There is a particular stubbornness in a small hand that has decided you belong to it. I have refused kings their dishes. I could not refuse a child her grip.

The priory sat at the vale's edge like an old dog too loyal to move and too tired to bark. Inside, forty sisters copied out herbals and psalters in a silence so complete you could hear the ink drying. Mother Ilsen ruled over it from a wheeled chair by the great south window, her legs gone the way of her eyesight, though she claimed—loudly, to anyone who suggested otherwise—that her ears worked better than ever, which was why she heard everything and forgave nothing.

"You," she said, before I'd finished the doorway. "Sable Kavanagh. The taster. You've brought me a child in the middle of a storm, which means either charity or catastrophe, and your face says catastrophe wearing charity's coat."

"Good morning to you too, Mother."

"Don't 'good morning' me. Set her down. No—by the fire, not on my good chair. And you." A finger like a dried plum rose to point at me. "You've the look of a woman who has swallowed something that disagrees with her, and I don't mean at breakfast."

I set Wren down by the fire. The child knelt there, small and grave, watching the flames with the patience of someone waiting for a kettle. The green tinge from last night was gone, or I had dreamed it, or I had decided to have dreamed it. That was the arrangement I'd made with my own memory: we would not speak of it, and in exchange it would not insist.

"Her name is Wren," I said. "She turned up on my doorstep last night. She doesn't—she speaks, but not any tongue I know. And I know tongues. I've tasted in six languages."

"Six." Ilsen snorted. "The Vale of Ashenmoor, where a woman's education consists of learning the word for 'poison' in as many dialects as will have her." But her voice had changed. It had gone soft in the way that snow goes soft just before it takes a traveler's leg. "Bring her here. Let me hear her."

Wren came when I beckoned—came to me first, tucked herself against my side like a swallow against a cliff—and then, when Ilsen extended both hands, went to her with the unhesitating trust of the very young and the very old for one another. Two creatures at the edges of life, recognizing the weather.

Ilsen took the child's face in her palms. "Say something, little one. Anything. Tell me what you had for supper. Tell me the fire is pretty. Tell me the woman smells of iron and worry."

Wren's mouth opened. What came out was that language again—the one from my kitchen, the one that had made the fire burn green and my scars sing. It flowed and broke and flowed, syllables like water over stones, and I watched Mother Ilsen's face do something I had never seen it do in thirty years of her knowing me.

Her face went blank. Not empty—*emptied*, the way a room is emptied, everything of value carried out carefully by hand.

"Again," she whispered.

Wren said something else. Shorter. A question, maybe, by the lift of it.

"Where did you hear that word?" Ilsen's voice cracked like river ice. "Child. Where did you hear that word?"

"She's been speaking it since she arrived," I said. "No one taught her. She—"

"*Quiet.*" Ilsen never raised her voice. The whole priory knew this. She raised it now, and somewhere down the hall a novice dropped a stack of psalters. "Not you. Not yet. Let me think. Let me—" Her hands were shaking on the child's shoulders, and I saw that she was not looking at Wren at all. She was looking through her, at something two hundred years away. "I have spent my life on dead languages. Latin. Old Ashen. The trade-tongues of the coast. I have conjugated verbs for kingdoms that fell before my grandmother's grandmother was born, and I have never once—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, as if the loudness had frightened her. "I have never once heard a language that was not dead but *unmade*. There is a difference, girl. The dead can be exhumed. The unmade were never permitted to have existed."

My mouth had gone dry, and for once it had nothing to do with arsenic. "What language is it?"

Ilsen looked at me for a long moment. Then at Wren. Then at the fire, as if consulting it.

"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. What I am about to tell you is not written anywhere, because writing it was forbidden, and I know this because I was the one they asked to write it, forty years ago, and I refused, and it cost me a bishopric and the use of my legs, though the legs were probably just age." She arranged her blankets with the fussy dignity of a woman building a fortress out of wool. "Sit. And close the door. Not for secrecy. For *weight*. Some things should not be said in a draft."

I sat. Wren climbed into my lap without asking, the way she had been doing since midnight, as if we had negotiated terms I couldn't remember agreeing to. She was lighter than a child her size should be. I tried not to think about that. I was becoming an expert at not thinking about things.

"Two hundred and twelve years ago," Ilsen began, "there was a kingdom east of the Salt Flats, beyond the drowned coast, in a country so green that the maps had to invent a new shade for it. It was called Vhalen."

The word went through the room like a cold draft. Wren's head came up.

"That's not possible," I said. "There's nothing east of the Salt Flats. There's the coast, and then there's the sea, and the sea is the edge of everything. I've tasted for cartographers' banquets. I've seen the maps."

"You've seen *the maps*," Ilsen said, with a contempt that could have curdled milk at forty paces. "And who do you imagine drew them? Who do you imagine *un*drew them? Vhalen, girl. The kingdom of the glass towers and the moon-priests and the taster-queens. Vhalen, which was so beautiful and so proud that when the empire of Aldric's ancestors came for it, it did not fight. It did not surrender. It *chose not to be*. There were sorceries then that we cannot imagine, and the last of them was worked by a queen with no name left to her, and it did not break the kingdom. It *removed* it. From the land, from the memory, from the tongue. Every book that mentioned it burned itself. Every mind that remembered it forgot it in their sleep. Every map redrew its coastline as open sea. The language died in a single night, in every mouth that spoke it, and the mouths went on living and never knew what they had lost." She paused. "Except the words were not killed. They were *locked away*. And languages, girl, are like water. They find the crack."

The fire had gone green again. I watched it happen this time, watched the flames gutter that impossible color, and felt Wren's small weight grow heavier in my lap, as if the word *Vhalen* had added stones to her.

"You're telling me a fairy story," I said, because someone had to say it, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "You're telling me there was a whole country, and it's gone, and nobody noticed. Not one farmer. Not one fisherman. Two hundred years, and no grandmother's tale, no ruin, no—"

"No ruin," Ilsen agreed. "No tale. No grave. That is what erasure *means*. The empire wanted a kingdom gone, and the kingdom wanted itself gone more, and between the two of them they made a hole in the world so clean that even the hole was forgotten." Her eyes, clouded as they were, fixed on me with terrible precision. "And now a child walks out of a snowstorm speaking the tongue that was locked away, and the fire burns green, and you sit there telling me about maps."

"She could have learned it somewhere—"

"From whom? The dead?" Ilsen's hand found Wren's hair, stroked it once, twice. "This is High Vhalenic. The court tongue. The tongue of oaths and coronations. I know it from one source only: a fragment of a psalter that survived the burning because someone hid it inside the binding of an Ashen hymnal, and that fragment was shown to me in secret, forty years ago, by a man who wept while he read it, though he could not have told you why. I have spent forty years trying to forget it, because forgetting is safer, and I have failed, because I am a scholar before I am a coward. Barely." She laughed, a dry sound like a page turning. "And this child speaks it as a *mother tongue*. Not recited. Not studied. *Lived in.* Do you understand what I am telling you, Sable Kavanagh? This child is not speaking a dead language. This child is speaking a language that was never permitted to die. She is a message that has been walking for two hundred years, and she has just arrived."

The fire crackled. Down the hall, the novices had resumed their copying, pens scratching like small animals.

"Why her?" I asked. "Why a child? If—if something wanted to send a message out of a vanished kingdom, why not send an army, a prophet, a—"

"Because armies can be fought," Ilsen said, "and prophets disbelieved. But a child at your door in a snowstorm—" she looked at me, and her voice gentled into something almost cruel in its kindness, "—a child at *your* door, taster. Not the priory's. Not the king's. Yours. Ask yourself why. Ask yourself what you are, that the last word of an unmade kingdom walked through the snow to *you*."

"I'm a food-taster."

"You are a food-taster," she agreed. "In a valley with no magic, in a kingdom built on the ruins of a fear so old no one remembers what it was afraid of. You poison yourself in small doses, every day, for a king you've never met. Your mother did the same, and her mother before her, back and back, a lineage of women swallowing ruin so that others might eat safely." She leaned forward, and the blankets slid, and she did not seem to notice. "Girl. Who do you think invented that office?"

The question landed in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Kings keep tasters," I said. "It's an old custom. It's—"

"It is a Vhalenic custom," Ilsen said. "The taster-sworn. The women who stood between their queen and every poison in the world, who made of their own bodies a shield, who were honored above knights because any knight could die for the queen once, but a taster died for her daily, in small installments, for a whole life. When Vhalen was unmade, the office should have died with it. Instead it spread. The empire's kings adopted it, the way conquerors adopt everything they envy, and never asked themselves why the custom ached in their mouths like a borrowed word." Her hand trembled on Wren's head. "And the Vale of Ashenmoor, where magic cannot enter—did you never wonder why such a place exists? Who built the silence? A hole in the world, taster, and a valley with no magic in it, and a lineage of women trained to swallow harm. You were not placed in that valley by accident. You were *hidden* in it. Like a seed in a wall. Like a word inside a hymnal."

I wanted to laugh. I did laugh, a little, one dry bark of it, because the alternative was to be sick. "You're saying my whole life is a footnote to a country that never existed."

"I'm saying your whole life is a *dedication*," Ilsen said, "to a country that was never permitted to stop existing. And I think—" and here her voice did something I had never heard it do, something I would carry to my own grave: it *broke*, "—I think the country has noticed."

Wren stirred in my lap. She turned, and looked up at me, and her eyes in the green firelight were not a child's eyes. They were older than the priory, older than the maps, older than the forgetting. And she spoke.

One word.

It was not long. Three syllables, maybe four, shaped with a care that no child gives to language, each sound placed like a stone in a ring wall. And as she spoke it, every scar I have—the arsenic scars, the belladonna scar over my ribs, the little white constellation on my wrists where the first doses went in at age seven—every one of them woke up. Not painfully. *Attentively.* As if they had been waiting two hundred years to be addressed, and had just heard their names.

I knew the word. That was the horror of it. I had never heard it in my life, and I knew it the way you know your own hand in the dark. It was not a word in a language. It was a word that was *mine*, that had been mine before I was born, that had been carried down through women who swallowed poison and never told their daughters what for, and it meant—oh, it meant—

"Kavanagh," Ilsen whispered, translating, though no translation was needed. "Of Vhalen. It is not a surname, girl. It is a *title*. The taster-sworn. The shield-mouth. It means: *she who stands between.*" Her breath rattled. "You are not Sable Kavanagh, food-taster of the Vale. You are the last of the Kavanaghs of Vhalen, heir of the shield-line, and that child has just named you before witnesses, and the naming—" 

She stopped.

I turned. Mother Ilsen had gone very still in her chair, and her face—her sharp, impossible, unforgiving face—was doing something it had never done in all the years I had known her.

She was smiling. Not the wry, armored smile she used like a shield. A real one. The smile of a woman who has spent forty years guarding a secret and has just, at the very last, been permitted to put it down.

"Oh," she said, softly, to the fire, or to Wren, or to a kingdom two hundred years drowned. "*Oh.* So that's what it was for."

"Mother?" I reached for her hand. It was warm. It was already cooling. "Mother Ilsen—"

"Forty years I kept the fragment," she murmured. Her eyes had fixed on something past the window, past the snow, past the edge of every map I had ever tasted for. "Forty years I thought it was a curiosity. A dead psalm. And it was a *door*, and I stood in front of it my whole life, and I never—" The smile deepened. "The word. Say it again, little one. Say it to the—"

But Wren had already turned away, back into my arms, her face pressed against my neck, and Mother Ilsen's hand went slack in mine, and the fire burned green, and green, and green, and then ordinary gold, and the greatest linguist in the Vale of Ashenmoor died smiling in her chair with her work finished and her fortress of blankets undisturbed, as if she had merely stepped out of the room to consult a rarer book.

I sat there a long time. The novices found me at dusk, when they brought the Mother her evening broth, and I watched the cup pass from the sister's hands to the tray and thought, with a strange, floating calm: *someone should taste that. Someone always tastes it. That is the whole of my life. That has always been the whole of my life.*

Except it hadn't. It had been the shape of a life, the shell, the wall around the seed. And the seed had a name now, and the name was older than the kingdom that feared it, and it was *mine*, and I could feel it under my skin like a second heartbeat.

Wren tugged my sleeve. I looked down. She pointed one small finger toward the window—toward the east, where the storm was breaking apart over the Salt Flats, where the sea was supposed to be the edge of everything and was not—and she said, in the tongue that was never permitted to die, a sentence I understood without understanding, the way you understand falling:

*They are coming for you. We have to go.*

And far off, at the valley's mouth, I heard—faint, and wrong, and real—the sound of horses.
